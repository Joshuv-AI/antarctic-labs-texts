Urinary miR-574-5p as a candidate biomarker for early-stage lung adenocarcinoma: an exploratory case-control study
Authors: Ryota Nagashima, Masato Aragaki, Kanako C. Hatanaka, Yoshiki Shinomiya, Masaru Ushijima, Aki Fujiwara-Kuroda, Kazuto Ohtaka, Haruhiko Shiiya, Ryohei Chiba, Shinya Otsuka, Takumi Nakaya, Ritsu Omine, Tomoaki Kuji, Yutaka Hatanaka, Tatsuya Kato
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEarly detection of lung adenocarcinoma remains challenging despite advances in low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening. Urinary microRNAs (miRNAs) represent a non-invasive biomarker modality, but their clinical utility in early-stage disease remains unclear. This study aimed to identify urinary miRNA candidates associated with early-stage lung adenocarcinoma and to explore whether urinary miR-574-5p may provide complementary diagnostic information beyond established clinical risk factors.MethodsWe conducted an exploratory case-control study using urinary samples from the J-CAN cohort, a prospective biomarker study. Small RNA sequencing was performed to profile miRNAs. Candidate miRNAs were identified using edgeR and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Associations with lung cancer status were evaluated using multivariable Firth logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, and urinary creatinine. Model performance was assessed using the area under the curve (AUC), and internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling.ResultsA total of 77 participants (33 early-stage lung adenocarcinoma (stage 0–IA2) and 44 healthy controls) were included in the primary analysis. Among candidate miRNAs, miR-574-5p showed the most consistent overall signal. Urinary miR-574-5p expression was significantly decreased in patients with early-stage adenocarcinoma and remained independently associated with lung cancer status after adjustment [odds ratio (OR) 0.15, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.02–0.53, P<0.001]. The baseline clinical model yielded an AUC of 0.961, which increased to 0.988 with the addition of miR-574-5p (ΔAUC =0.027), although this increase was not statistically significant.ConclusionsUrinary miR-574-5p was decreased in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma and was independently associated with disease status. Although its incremental diagnostic value was modest, urinary miR-574-5p may provide limited complementary information beyond established clinical risk factors. Further validation in independent and prospective cohorts is warranted.

What is known and what is new?

• Liquid biopsy is increasingly used in oncology, but most studies have focused on blood-based analytes. Circulating miR-574-5p has been reported as a biomarker candidate in non-small cell lung cancer. However, the role of urinary miRNAs in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma remains insufficiently characterized.

• This exploratory case-control study profiled urinary miRNAs in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma and identified urinary miR-574-5p as a candidate biomarker. The decrease in urinary miR-574-5p was detectable from the adenocarcinoma in situ stage and remained associated with lung cancer status after adjustment for major clinical variables. The findings also suggest that urinary and circulating miR-574-5p may show compartment-specific dynamics.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Urinary miR-574-5p should not be interpreted as a stand-alone diagnostic test at this stage. Rather, it may serve as a complementary biomarker candidate for future evaluation alongside clinical factors and imaging-based screening. Further validation in independent prospective cohorts, with renal function data and matched urine, blood, and tissue samples, is needed before clinical implementation.

Introduction

Background

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide, largely because many cases are diagnosed at an advanced stage (1,2). Low-dose computed tomography (LDCT) screening has been shown to significantly reduce lung cancer mortality in high-risk populations and is currently the standard screening strategy (3,4). However, LDCT is associated with limitations, including high false-positive rates, overdiagnosis, and cumulative radiation exposure, which may lead to unnecessary invasive procedures and psychological burden (5,6). Therefore, complementary approaches that enable more refined risk stratification are needed to improve screening efficiency.

Rationale and knowledge gap

In this context, liquid biopsy has emerged as a non-invasive approach to capture tumor-related signals (7-9). While plasma-based analyses have been extensively studied, urine offers several advantages, including complete non-invasiveness, ease of repeated sampling, and suitability for large-scale population screening (10). Recent evidence suggests that molecular alterations originating from distant organs, including the lung, can be reflected in urine, supporting its role as a platform for systemic biomarker discovery (11,12).

MicroRNAs (miRNAs) are stable and detectable in body fluids and have been widely investigated as potential biomarkers in liquid biopsy (13). In particular, exosomal miRNAs have been investigated as potential biomarkers for early detection, disease subtype classification, prognosis, and therapy resistance in lung cancer (14). However, the clinical translation of miRNA-based biomarkers remains limited due to methodological challenges, including variability in pre-analytical processing, lack of standardization, small sample sizes, and insufficient control for confounding factors (15,16). Clinical variables such as age, sex, and smoking status are known to influence miRNA expression profiles (17,18), and failure to account for these factors may lead to overestimation of biomarker performance. These limitations likely contribute to the inconsistency observed across studies.

miR-574-5p has been reported as a circulating miRNA associated with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), with potential roles in early detection (19) and tumor progression (20). Although altered expression in blood has been consistently reported, it remains unclear how these systemic changes are reflected in urinary miRNA profiles. Accordingly, the clinical relevance of urinary miR-574-5p has not yet been established.

Objective

In this exploratory case-control study, we comprehensively profiled urinary miRNAs in patients with early-stage lung adenocarcinoma (stage 0–IA2) and healthy controls. We aimed to identify urinary miRNA candidates associated with early-stage lung adenocarcinoma and to evaluate whether miR-574-5p provides complementary diagnostic information beyond established clinical risk factors. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tlcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tlcr-2026-0600/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

This study was designed as an exploratory case-control study to identify urinary miRNA candidates that distinguish early-stage lung adenocarcinoma from healthy controls. A case-control design was chosen to efficiently capture rare early-stage cases, including adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS), and to detect initial biological signals.

Urine samples were obtained from participants enrolled in the J-CAN cohort, a prospective biomarker study conducted at Hokkaido University Hospital and affiliated institutions. Samples were collected between September 2023 and May 2025. For the cancer group, preoperative early-morning urine samples were obtained from patients who underwent surgery for pulmonary lesions clinically suspected of primary lung cancer at the Department of Thoracic Surgery, Hokkaido University Hospital. Final diagnoses and pathological stages were determined by histopathological examination of resected specimens. Healthy controls were recruited from healthcare workers, and early-morning urine samples were collected.

The initial sequencing dataset included 146 participants. After excluding cases with metastatic lung tumors, multiple primary lung cancers, benign diseases, and missing count data, 131 samples were retained for analysis. Among these, 104 participants, including 60 patients with lung adenocarcinoma and 44 healthy controls, comprised the adenocarcinoma cohort.

The primary analysis focused on early-stage lung adenocarcinoma (AIS–IA2, n=33) and healthy controls (n=44), yielding a total of 77 samples. All histopathological diagnoses were classified according to the 8th edition of the tumor-node-metastasis (TNM) classification for lung cancer (21). An extended cohort including all 60 adenocarcinoma cases and 44 healthy controls (n=104) was used for descriptive analysis of stage-dependent expression patterns.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Hokkaido University Hospital (approval No. 023-0130). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Extraction and library preparation

miRNA was extracted from urinary extracellular vesicles using the exoRNeasy Midi Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 2 mL of urine was filtered through a 0.8 µm Millex-AA filter (Merck Millipore, Burlington, MA, USA) to remove cells and debris prior to extraction. RNA quantity and quality were assessed using an Agilent Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA; Small RNA Kit). Extracted RNA was stored at −80 ℃ until further processing.

Small RNA libraries were prepared using the QIAseq miRNA UDI Library Kit (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany). Library size distribution was evaluated using an Agilent Bioanalyzer High Sensitivity DNA chip or TapeStation D1000 (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA).

Statistical analysis

Candidate screening

Candidate miRNAs were identified using two complementary statistical approaches. Differential expression analysis was performed using the quasi-likelihood framework in edgeR (estimateDisp, glmQLFit, glmQLFTest). In parallel, a nonparametric Wilcoxon rank-sum test was conducted.

Multiple testing was controlled using the Benjamini-Hochberg method to control the false discovery rate, and adjusted P values <0.1 were considered statistically significant. Candidate miRNAs were defined as the union of those identified by both methods and were limited to a maximum of 20 to reduce instability associated with evaluating a large number of candidates. To prioritize candidate miRNAs, each candidate was evaluated based on the change in area under the curve (AUC) (ΔAUC) in multivariable models and its selection frequency in bootstrap resampling to assess robustness of candidate selection.

Multivariable analysis

Associations between candidate miRNAs and lung cancer status were evaluated using multivariable logistic regression models adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, and log-transformed urinary creatinine. Urinary creatinine was not used to normalize miRNA expression values before differential expression analysis; instead, log-transformed urinary creatinine was included as a covariate in the multivariable regression models to account for urine concentration. Firth’s penalized likelihood method was applied to reduce small-sample bias and address potential separation. The primary multivariable model included five predictors for 33 cancer cases, corresponding to approximately 6.6 events per variable.

Model performance

Model performance was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis. The AUC was calculated for the baseline clinical model and the extended model including the selected candidate miRNA (miR-574-5p). Incremental value was evaluated as the ΔAUC. The difference between the baseline and extended ROC curves was assessed using DeLong’s test.

Internal validation

Internal validation was performed using bootstrap resampling with 1,000 iterations. For bootstrap A, candidate miRNAs retained after secondary screening were re-evaluated in each bootstrap resample. For each candidate, the baseline clinical model and the extended model including that miRNA were fitted, and ΔAUC was calculated within the bootstrap sample. Selection frequency was defined as the proportion of resamples in which each miRNA showed the largest ΔAUC. For bootstrap B, the final model including miR-574-5p was refitted in each bootstrap sample. Model performance was evaluated in both the bootstrap sample and the original dataset, and optimism was estimated as the mean difference in AUC between these two evaluations. The optimism-corrected AUC was calculated by subtracting the estimated optimism from the apparent AUC.

Statistical software

All analyses were performed in R version 4.5.2 using the edgeR, logistf, pROC, and tidyverse packages. Unless otherwise specified, a two-sided P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Study population

A total of 146 samples were included in the sequencing dataset. After excluding cases with metastatic lung tumors, synchronous multiple primary lung cancers, benign diseases, and those lacking count data, 131 samples remained for analysis. Restricting to lung adenocarcinoma yielded 60 cases and 44 healthy controls (n=104).

For the primary analysis, 33 patients with early-stage adenocarcinoma (stage 0–IA2) and 44 healthy controls (n=77) were included (Figure 1). Baseline characteristics are summarized in Table 1. Patients with lung cancer were significantly older (70.6 vs. 49.2 years, P<0.001) and more likely to have a smoking history (72.7% vs. 29.5%, P<0.001). The baseline clinical model including age, sex, smoking status, and log-transformed urinary creatinine showed a high discriminative performance for early-stage lung adenocarcinoma versus healthy controls (AUC, 0.961), indicating that clinical background variables strongly separated the two groups in this case-control dataset.

An extended cohort including all 60 adenocarcinoma cases was used for descriptive stage-wise analyses (Figure 1).

miRNA sequencing, preprocessing, and quality control

Small RNA sequencing identified 2,632 annotated miRNAs across all samples. Lowly expressed miRNAs were filtered using edgeR’s filterByExpr(), resulting in 63 miRNAs for downstream analyses. Expression values were normalized using the trimmed mean of M-values (TMM) method and transformed to log counts per million (CPM). No creatinine-based normalization was applied to miRNA expression values before differential expression analysis.

Quality control based on library size distribution and principal component analysis (PCA) showed no samples meeting predefined exclusion criteria (see Methods) (Figure S1).

Identification of candidate miRNAs

Candidate miRNAs were identified using two statistical approaches. Differential expression analysis with edgeR identified 20 miRNAs (adjusted P<0.1; Figure S2 and Table S1), while the Wilcoxon rank-sum test identified 11 miRNAs after multiple testing correction (Figure S3 and Table S1).

Final candidates were selected based on the union of both methods and restricted to a prespecified maximum of 20 to reduce instability associated with evaluating a large number of candidates (Table S1).

Each candidate was evaluated using ΔAUC in multivariable models and selection frequency in bootstrap A. Among the candidate miRNAs, miR-574-5p showed the largest ΔAUC and the highest selection frequency (357/1,000) (Table S2). However, this selection frequency was moderate, indicating uncertainty in the robustness of candidate prioritization.

Association between miR-574-5p and lung cancer status

In Firth penalized logistic regression adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, and urinary creatinine, miR-574-5p remained significantly associated with lung cancer [odds ratio (OR) 0.15 per one-unit increase in logCPM, 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.02–0.53, P<0.001] (Table 2; Figure 2). Lower expression was associated with the presence of lung cancer. In the control group, miR-574-5p expression did not differ significantly according to age (≥60 vs. <60 years) or smoking status (Figures S4,S5).

Model performance and internal validation

The baseline clinical model yielded an AUC of 0.961. Addition of miR-574-5p increased the AUC to 0.988 (ΔAUC =0.027) (Figure 5); however, this difference was not statistically significant by DeLong’s test (P=0.32). Thus, the incremental value of miR-574-5p should be interpreted as exploratory and modest rather than evidence of established clinical utility.

ROC curves comparing the baseline clinical model (age, sex, smoking status, and log-transformed urinary creatinine) and the extended model including miR-574-5p. The AUC and the change in AUC (ΔAUC) are shown. AUC, area under the curve; ROC, receiver operating characteristic.

In bootstrap A, miR-574-5p showed the highest selection frequency (357/1,000), although this frequency was moderate and the candidate was not selected in most bootstrap resamples (Figure S6). In bootstrap B, the final model including miR-574-5p had an apparent AUC of 0.988, with a mean optimism of 0.010 and an optimism-corrected AUC of 0.978 (Figure S7). Because bootstrap B was applied only to the final model after candidate selection, it did not capture uncertainty arising from the full screening and selection process.

Discussion

Key findings

In this exploratory case-control study, we identified urinary miR-574-5p as a candidate biomarker associated with early-stage lung adenocarcinoma. Urinary miR-574-5p expression was decreased in patients with early-stage adenocarcinoma compared with healthy controls, and this decrease was already detectable at the AIS stage. miR-574-5p remained independently associated with lung cancer status after adjustment for age, sex, smoking status, and urinary creatinine.

Adding miR-574-5p to the baseline clinical model resulted in a modest improvement in model performance (ΔAUC =0.027). Among the candidate miRNAs identified in this exploratory analysis, miR-574-5p showed the most consistent overall signal. Because the baseline clinical model already showed high discriminative performance and the increase in AUC was not statistically significant, the added value of miR-574-5p should be interpreted as exploratory and limited. These findings suggest that urinary miR-574-5p may provide modest complementary information in this case-control dataset, but they do not establish immediate clinical utility.

Strengths and limitations

A major strength of this study is its focus on very early-stage lung adenocarcinoma, including AIS. This enabled us to evaluate urinary miRNA alterations associated with the earliest detectable phase of lung tumorigenesis, rather than changes confined to more advanced disease. In addition, we adjusted for key clinical variables, including age, sex, smoking status, and urinary creatinine, to account for measured confounders (22). We also used Firth penalized logistic regression to mitigate small-sample bias (23) and performed bootstrap-based internal validation to estimate optimism in the final model (24). The study was reported with reference to the STROBE guideline for observational studies (25).

This study also has several limitations. First, because of its case-control design, the observed model performance may be overestimated relative to a true screening setting. Second, bootstrap-based internal validation was performed for the final model only and did not encompass the entire screening and candidate-selection process. Therefore, potential optimism related to model selection uncertainty may not have been fully captured (26). In addition, the selection frequency of miR-574-5p was 357/1,000, which indicates moderate rather than robust selection stability. This means that miR-574-5p was the leading candidate in this dataset, but the reproducibility of its prioritization remains uncertain and requires confirmation in independent cohorts. Third, external validation was not available, and the reproducibility and generalizability of these findings remain uncertain. Fourth, the sample size was modest, particularly for the early-stage adenocarcinoma group (n=33). The primary multivariable model included five predictors, corresponding to approximately 6.6 events per variable. Although Firth penalized logistic regression was used to reduce small-sample bias and separation-related instability, this events-per-variable ratio was below commonly recommended thresholds. Therefore, the multivariable estimates should be interpreted cautiously and require validation in larger cohorts.

A major limitation of this study is the comparability of the control group. The healthy controls were recruited from healthcare workers and were substantially younger and less likely to have a smoking history than the cancer group. In addition, the baseline clinical model alone achieved a high AUC of 0.961, indicating that clinical background variables strongly discriminated cases from controls in this case-control dataset. Although we adjusted for age, sex, smoking status, and urinary creatinine in the multivariable Firth regression model, residual confounding cannot be excluded. Therefore, the observed decrease in urinary miR-574-5p should be interpreted as an exploratory association in this dataset rather than definitive evidence of a tumor-specific signal. Notably, miR-574-5p expression did not differ significantly by age group or smoking status among healthy controls in exploratory analyses; however, these analyses do not eliminate the possibility of residual confounding. Finally, renal function data were unavailable, limiting the assessment of potential renal physiological influences on urinary miRNA levels.

Comparison with similar research

Previous studies have investigated miR-574-5p mainly as a circulating biomarker in NSCLC. Foss et al. reported serum miR-574-5p as a potential marker for early-stage NSCLC (19), and Zhou et al. described a functional association between miR-574-5p and tumor progression (20). More recently, Han et al. reported increased plasma exosomal miR-574-5p in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma, with decreased levels after tumor resection (27).

Our study differs from these reports in two important respects. First, we focused on urine rather than blood, thereby evaluating a distinct liquid biopsy compartment. Second, we focused specifically on very early-stage lung adenocarcinoma. Whereas prior blood-based studies generally reported increased circulating or plasma exosomal miR-574-5p, we observed decreased urinary miR-574-5p expression in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma. This directional discrepancy is important and limits direct cross-study interpretation. A compartment-specific difference between blood and urine is a plausible explanation, but this interpretation remains speculative because the present study did not include matched urine, blood, and tumor tissue samples. Therefore, urinary miR-574-5p should not be assumed to mirror circulating miR-574-5p, and the present findings should be interpreted as urine-specific exploratory observations requiring further validation.

Explanations of findings

A notable finding of this study is that reduced urinary miR-574-5p expression was already observed at the AIS stage. This suggests that alterations in extracellular miRNA dynamics may occur even in the earliest clinically and pathologically detectable phase of lung adenocarcinoma, and that the urinary signal may not simply reflect advanced tumor burden.

Several biological considerations may help explain the compartment-specific pattern observed in this study. Beyond their intracellular roles in gene regulation, miRNAs are involved in intercellular communication through extracellular vesicles and protein-bound complexes. Cells, including tumor cells, can release miRNAs via exosomes to influence both their local microenvironment and systemic signaling (28). In addition, prostaglandin E2 signaling has been reported to promote selective sorting of miR-574-5p into small extracellular vesicles (29). These observations provide a plausible, although unproven, biological context in which early lung tumorigenesis could influence extracellular miRNA profiles.

The biological origin of urinary miRNAs may also differ from that of circulating miRNAs. Many circulating miRNAs exist in association with extracellular vesicles or protein complexes, which may not simply pass through the glomerular filtration barrier because of their size and structural properties (30,31). In contrast, urinary miRNAs are thought to originate, at least in part, from extracellular vesicles released by nephron and urinary tract epithelial cells (32,33). Therefore, decreased urinary miR-574-5p may reflect compartment-specific urinary miRNA regulation associated with systemic environmental changes during early lung tumorigenesis, rather than direct release from the tumor itself.

These mechanistic interpretations remain hypothetical and should not be taken as evidence that urinary miR-574-5p reflects the same biological process as circulating miR-574-5p. Future studies using matched urine, blood, and tumor tissue samples will be necessary to clarify the origin, trafficking, and compartment-specific regulation of miR-574-5p in early lung adenocarcinoma.

Implications and actions needed

The present findings should not be interpreted as establishing a stand-alone diagnostic test or as justifying the addition of miR-574-5p to clinical decision-making in its current form. Given the high AUC of the baseline clinical model and the non-significant increase in AUC after adding miR-574-5p, the incremental value observed in this study should be regarded as exploratory and limited. Rather, the present results support further investigation of urinary miRNA profiling as a complementary biomarker approach in future cohorts. A potential clinical role would need to be evaluated in independent prospective studies with better-matched controls and a defined screening or imaging-based risk-stratification context, where the added value of urinary miR-574-5p can be assessed under clinically realistic conditions. Future studies should also incorporate renal function data, perform orthogonal validation using quantitative assays, and evaluate matched urine, blood, and tissue samples to clarify compartment-specific miRNA dynamics.

Conclusions

Urinary miR-574-5p was decreased in early-stage lung adenocarcinoma and remained independently associated with lung cancer status after adjustment for major clinical variables. These findings support the possibility that urinary miRNAs may reflect early biological alterations associated with lung tumorigenesis.

At the same time, the added diagnostic value was limited, and the current data support a complementary rather than stand-alone role for this biomarker. Further validation in prospective studies and independent cohorts will be necessary to establish its reproducibility and clinical utility.
