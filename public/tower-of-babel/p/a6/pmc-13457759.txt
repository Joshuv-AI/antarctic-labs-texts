Early diastolic dysfunction in patients with ischemia and no obstructive coronary artery disease: a prospective study on hybrid positron emission tomography-magnetic resonance imaging
Authors: Runze Wen, Qiang Xie, Ming Ni, Bo Pan, Xingxing Zhu, Xueer Meng, Dan Li, Xuemei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCoronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD) has been proposed to be one of the pathological mechanisms underlying heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) and left ventricular (LV) diastolic dysfunction. This study aimed to clarify the relationship between CMD and LV early diastolic function in patients with ischemia and no obstructive coronary artery disease (INOCA).MethodsConsecutive patients with INOCA treated at The First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China between August 2024 and March 2026 were prospectively recruited. LV strain rates, myocardial flow reserve (MFR), and peak filling rate (PFR) were acquired via positron emission tomography-magnetic resonance imaging (PET/MRI). Linear regression analysis was performed to identify independent associations between LV early diastolic strain rates and CMD.ResultsA total of 73 patients (mean age 55±10 years; 49.3% females) with INOCA were ultimately enrolled and divided into a CMD group and a non-CMD group based on global MFR assessment. Compared with the non-CMD group, the CMD group exhibited significantly lower radial peak early diastolic strain rate (PEDSR), circumferential PEDSR, and longitudinal PEDSR (all P values <0.001). However, no significant differences were observed in LV global strain rate parameters. Multivariate regression analysis revealed that the factors independently associated with global MFR were radial PEDSR [standardized β (95% CI) = −0.250 (−0.494 to −0.006); P=0.044] and circumferential PEDSR [standardized β (95% CI) = 0.464 (0.194 to 0.734); P=0.001]. Additionally, multivariate analysis confirmed CMD to be an independent predictor of all three directions of PEDSR (radial, circumferential, and longitudinal) (all P values <0.001). Notably, the independent predictors of radial PEDSR were PFR [standardized β (95% CI) = −0.356 (−0.523 to −0.190); P<0.001] and extracellular volume [standardized β (95% CI) = 0.289 (0.139 to 0.439); P<0.001].ConclusionsDespite normal LV ejection fraction in patients with INOCA, CMD was significantly associated with subclinical LV early diastolic dysfunction. These physiological findings provide a mechanistic basis for future prospective studies investigating the prognostic significance of these diastolic abnormalities in patients with INOCA.Keywords: Ischemia and no obstructive coronary artery (INOCA), early diastolic dysfunction, feature tracking, coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD)

Introduction

Ischemia and no obstructive coronary artery disease (INOCA) refers to the presence of myocardial ischemia in patients without obstructive coronary artery disease (CAD). Previous studies have indicated that among patients referred for invasive coronary angiography, 30–50% are diagnosed with INOCA regardless of whether noninvasive stress testing demonstrates ischemia (1,2). Although INOCA was once regarded as a benign condition, recent studies examining the long-term outcomes of patients with this disorder have reported a nearly fourfold higher incidence of mortality and major adverse cardiovascular events, including cardiovascular death, myocardial infarction, and heart failure with preserved ejection fraction (HFpEF) in these patients when compared to those without INOCA (3,4). Moreover, one possible underlying cause of angina in patients with INOCA is coronary microvascular dysfunction (CMD), a heterogeneous condition that impairs the structure and/or function of the coronary microcirculation. CMD can be broadly categorized into functional and structural subtypes: abnormal coronary flow reserve (CFR), which, in the setting of normal index of microcirculatory resistance (IMR), indicates functional CMD; and elevated IMR, which reflects structural microvascular damage.

A growing body of evidence suggests that CMD is linked to left ventricular (LV) diastolic dysfunction, with CMD being associated with an increased risk of developing HFpEF-related outcomes (5,6). However, the diagnostic accuracy of noninvasive testing for CMD remains suboptimal. A recent study demonstrated that conventional noninvasive stress tests have limited predictive value for invasively confirmed CMD, with approximately half of patients with positive stress tests exhibiting normal microvascular function (7). Although positron emission tomography (PET)-derived myocardial flow reserve (MFR) is the most widely validated noninvasive method for CMD assessment, it cannot reliably distinguish between functional and structural microvascular abnormalities. Furthermore, in the Women’s Ischemic Syndrome Evaluation (WISE) study, more than half of women with INOCA exhibited greater than expected diastolic dysfunction, and a higher rate of heart failure hospitalizations has been reported in this population (8,9). These data suggest that CMD may be mechanistically associated with diastolic dysfunction in individuals with INOCA and may act as a precursor to HFpEF. Clarifying the mechanisms underlying diastolic dysfunction in patients with INOCA is thus of critical importance for achieving a better understanding of disease progression and devising novel therapeutic strategies.

Cardiac magnetic resonance (CMR) is recognized as the reference standard for the assessment of cardiac chamber volumes; can characterize myocardial tissue, perfusion, and viability through a single integrated examination; and has demonstrated growing clinical utility in the diagnosis and monitoring of various cardiovascular diseases. The assessment of diastolic function with CMR has traditionally been challenging but is being increasingly embraced in clinical practice (10). Several studies have demonstrated the value of CMR for the assessment of diastolic function via the use of phase-contrast sequences and reported a strong correlation between CMR parameters and echocardiographic indices (11,12). The development of myocardial deformation imaging has allowed for the direct assessment of myocardial relaxation. CMR feature tracking (CMR-FT) has facilitated the measurement of strain and strain rates directly from routinely acquired cine images. Peak early diastolic strain rate (PEDSR) serves as a surrogate marker of myocardial stiffness, has been demonstrated to exhibit high consistency with echocardiographic indices in the diagnosis of diastolic dysfunction (9), and has been linked to adverse outcomes in patients with HFpEF (10). However, to our knowledge, no study has examined the prognostic value of CMR-derived strain parameters among individuals with or at risk of INOCA or examined the relationship among diastolic strain rates as a functional measure and PET-derived MFR.

To determine whether LV diastolic strain rates decrease with greater impairment of MFR, we compared subclinical diastolic strain rates between INOCA patients with and without CMD to determine the physiological association between LV early diastolic strain rate indices and CMD. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2026-0615/rc).

Methods

Study population

Study participants consisted of consecutive patients presenting with symptoms suggestive of myocardial ischemia, primarily chest pain, at The First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China between August 2024 and March 2026. This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments and was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of University of Science and Technology of China (No. 2024KYER-232). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants. Figure 1 presents a flowchart of patient inclusion in this study. Both men and women with suspected INOCA were enrolled in the study. INOCA was considered to be the presence of ischemic signs and symptoms but no obstructive CAD (<50% luminal stenosis in any coronary artery) as verified by computed tomography coronary angiography or invasive coronary angiography. Invasive coronary function testing was performed at the discretion of treating clinicians based on clinical indications and was not a mandatory requirement for study enrollment. The exclusion criteria were a history of myocardial infarction; prior coronary revascularization; and any conditions precluding the accurate and safe testing or subsequent follow-up, specifically, obstructive CAD (CAD) (defined as ≥50% luminal diameter stenosis in at least one major epicardial coronary artery), acute coronary syndrome, concurrent cardiogenic shock, any suspected or confirmed cardiomyopathies (e.g., dilated cardiomyopathy, hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, restrictive cardiomyopathy, tachycardia-induced cardiomyopathy, peripartum cardiomyopathy, and alcoholic cardiomyopathy), congenital heart disease, myocarditis, constrictive pericarditis, cardiac sarcoidosis, connective tissue diseases, severe renal impairment, chest pain of confirmed nonischemic origin (e.g., pneumonia and esophageal spasm), CMR contraindications (e.g., pacemakers, implanted electronic devices, and claustrophobia), incomplete clinical documentation, or suboptimal image quality.

Clinical data collection

Baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory data were collected retrospectively from electronic medical records for all enrolled patients. Variables included age, gender, body mass index (BMI), and traditional cardiovascular risk factors (smoking, diabetes, hypertension, and hyperlipidemia), medication use (antiplatelet agents, beta-blockers, calcium channel blockers, renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system inhibitors, statins, and oral hypoglycemic agents), and fasting laboratory parameters (lipid profile, estimated glomerular filtration rate, N-terminal prohormone brain natriuretic peptide, and glycated hemoglobin).

Hybrid PET-magnetic resonance (PET/MR) acquisition protocol

All patients underwent a simultaneous electrocardiogram (ECG)-gated cardiac PET/MR scan (Biograph mMR, Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany). Additionally, all patients were subjected to both resting and regadenoson-induced hyperemia stress perfusion imaging studies with 13N-ammonia. All participants were evaluated following an overnight fast and were instructed to refrain from caffeine-containing beverages and theophylline-containing medications for at least 12 hours prior to the study. CMR data were collected via a flexible anterior six-channel body transmission coil array and a posterior six-channel spine coil array, both mounted on the scanner bed.

Before myocardial perfusion imaging, localizer scans were performed, with subsequent attenuation correction imaging performed with standard Dixon-based maps. For resting PET/MRI, global myocardial perfusion was initially evaluated via an administration of 370 MBq of 13N-ammonia. Concurrently, a 10-minute list-mode PET acquisition was initiated a few seconds prior to 13N-ammonia injection. The 13N-ammonia was administered with a single peripheral intravenous (IV) cannula over 3 to 5 seconds, which was followed by a 10-mL saline flush. One hour later, a pharmacologic stress test was conducted, starting with the administration of regadenoson prior to the IV injection of a second dose of 13N-ammonia (370 MBq). Regadenoson (400 µg) was delivered as an IV bolus over less than 10 seconds through an antecubital cannula and followed by a subsequent 5-mL normal saline flush. Stress image acquisition was initiated a few seconds prior to the administration of the radiotracer. Dynamic PET images were generated via three-dimensional ordered-subset expectation maximization (3D-OSEM), 6 iterations, and 21 subsets, with a 5-mm postreconstruction smoothing filter. Dynamic frames were defined as 12×10 s, 2×30 s, 4×60 s, and 1×180 s, and the clinical two-point Dixon method was used for attenuation correction.

Briefly, balanced steady-state free precession (bSSFP) cine images were acquired in the two-, three-, and four-chamber long-axis images and in a stack of short-axis images covering the entire LV. The imaging parameters were as follows: field of view (FOV), 256×256 mm2; matrix size, 192×109; slice thickness, 8 mm; flip angle (FA), 67°; repetition time (TR), 2.8 ms; echo time (TE), 1.4 ms; and parallel imaging acceleration factor, 3.

Before the acquisition of resting perfusion images, midventricular short-axis T1 relaxation images were obtained during mid-diastole for the evaluation of native T1 values. After the administration of the first dose of 13N-ammonia, 0.075 mmol/kg of gadopentetate dimeglumine (Gd-DTPA, BeiLu Pharmaceutical Co. Ltd., Beijing, China) was administered, and postcontrast T1 images were obtained 15 minutes after the gadolinium injection for the calculation of extracellular volume (ECV). T1 measurements were obtained in three short-axis slices (basal, mid, and apical) via the shortened modified Look-Locker inversion recovery (shMOLLI) sequence, under breath-hold conditions and ECG gating. Native T1 mapping was performed with a bSSFP single-shot readout under the following scanning parameters: excitation FA, 35°; parallel imaging acceleration factor, 2; matrix size, 192×144; FOV, 340×255 mm2; slice thickness, 8 mm; TR, 2.58 ms; and TE, 1.07 ms. Postcontrast T1 imaging was performed with the same set of scanning parameters as those of native T1 mapping, with the exception of TR (2.5 ms) and TE (1.1 ms).

Postprocessing and imaging analysis

Perfusion images were analyzed by two experienced readers in standard cardiac imaging planes with the 17-segment model for the presence of myocardial ischemia, and they were kept blinded to the results of CMR quantification. Semiquantitative summed myocardial PET tracer uptake and quantitative myocardial blood flow (MBF) were derived from both stress and rest PET scans, which were analyzed with commercially available dedicated software package (syngo.via, Siemens Healthineers). MFR was computed as the ratio of stress MBF to resting MBF, and a global MFR <2.0 was considered abnormal (13). Based on global MFR values, study participants were divided into two groups for subsequent analysis: (I) a non-CMD group (high MFR ≥2.0; n=41) and (II) a CMD group (low MFR <2.0; n=32).

Myocardial perfusion was classified as normal tracer uptake, mild perfusion defect, moderate perfusion defect, or severe perfusion defect. Each myocardial segment was assigned a score based on a 5-point scoring system, with a score of 0 indicating normal radiotracer activity, 1 indicating mildly reduced activity, 2 indicating moderately reduced activity, 3 indicating severely reduced activity, and 4 indicating complete absence of radiotracer activity. For both rest and stress images, the segmental scores were totaled for the computation of the summed rest score and summed stress score (SSS). The scan results were deemed normal when the SSS was less than 4, mildly abnormal when it ranged from 4 to 7, moderately abnormal when it ranged from 8 to 11, and severely abnormal when it was 12 or higher.

LV volumetric and functional parameters were obtained through analysis performed by two CMR radiologists with at least 5 years of experience in CMR who remained blinded to participants’ demographic and clinical information. This analysis was performed with commercially available offline software (cvi42 version 5.11.2; Circle Cardiovascular Imaging, Inc., Calgary, AB, Canada). The endocardial and epicardial contours of the LV at end-diastole and end-systole were manually and carefully outlined layer by layer along the short-axis views from the cardiac apex to the base to obtain LV function parameters, including LV end-diastolic volume index (LVEDVI), LV end-systolic volume index (LVESVI), LV ejection fraction (LVEF), LV cardiac output (LVCO), and LV mass index (LVMI) (14). Papillary muscles were excluded from the quantification of LV mass. The ratio of LV mass to end-diastolic volume (LVM/V) was calculated as the LV remodeling index (LVRI). LV volume-time curves were employed to determine diastolic dysfunction parameters, including early peak filling rate (PFR) and time-to-PFR (T-PFR). PFR was defined as the maximum rate of change in the LV volume over a unit of time (Δvolume/Δphase), corresponding to the steepest slope during the diastolic phase of the cardiac cycle. A reduced PFR and a prolonged T-PFR were considered to indicate diastolic dysfunction.

In accordance with the American Heart Association (AHA) 17-segment model with the apex excluded, T1 values and ECV were quantified via postprocessing software, with the mean value being calculated three times per patient. The global value of each parameter was determined as the average of the 16 segmental values per patient. Care was taken to exclude border regions and papillary muscles during the measurement process. Venous blood samples were collected from all patients within 2 days before the PET/MR scan to obtain hematocrit (HCT) values. ECV was calculated with the following formula: ECV = (1 − HCT) × [(1/myocardial enhanced T1) − (1/myocardial native T1)] / [(1/blood pool enhanced T1) − (1/blood pool native T1)]. ECV values were recorded as percentages and subjected to statistical analysis.

LV global radial diastolic strain rate (RDSR), circumferential diastolic strain rate (CDSR), and longitudinal diastolic strain rate (LDSR) were quantified via CMR-FT, as previously reported (15). The endocardial and epicardial borders were manually delineated at end-diastole on short- and long-axis cine images before the feature-tracking algorithm was applied to all other cardiac phases. The end-diastolic phase served as the initiation point for strain tracking. Visual assessment was performed across all cardiac phases to verify the accuracy of tissue tracking, and manual corrections were implemented when required. PEDSR was defined as the first peak of the strain rate curve, which corresponded to the early passive filling phase of the LV. Participants with suboptimal tracking quality were excluded from the final analyses.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD) and tested for normality. Variables with normal and nonnormal distributions between groups were compared via the independent samples t test or nonparametric test (Mann-Whitney U test), respectively. Categorical variables are reported as percentages and were compared via the chi-squared test. The association between MFR and global strain rate parameters derived from CMR was assessed with the Spearman correlation coefficient. Following univariate analysis, all candidate variables with a P value <0.10 were included in the multivariate regression model. Additionally, variance inflation factor (VIF) testing was conducted to evaluate multicollinearity, and variables with VIF values exceeding 10 were excluded. Subsequently, the remaining variables were included in the multivariate regression models to identify the independent predictors of global MFR and PEDSR. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to assess both inter- and intraobserver reproducibility. A two-way random-effects model was adopted for the ICC analysis. ICC values were categorized as follows: excellent, greater than 0.90; good, >0.75–0.90; moderate, 0.50–0.75; and poor, less than 0.50 (16). A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted with SPSS software version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

Baseline characteristics

A total of 85 patients were screened, and 12 patients were excluded for the following reasons: severe claustrophobia (n=4), contrast allergy (n=2), metal implants (n=1), and insufficient image quality (n=5). Finally, 73 patients were included in the study. Of these, 32 (44%) patients (59.4% female; mean age 54±9 years) were assigned to the CMD group according to PET-derived MFR assessment. The remaining 41 (56%) patients (41.5% female; mean age 55±11 years) were assigned to the non-CMD group. Females (19/36, 52.8%) had a higher prevalence of CMD than did males (13/37, 35.1%). There were no significant differences in baseline demographics, cardiovascular risk factors, medications, or laboratory findings between the two groups (all P values >0.05). Relationship between LV diastolic function and CMD

Regarding basic LV functional parameters, the global MFR was lower in the CMD group than in the non-CMD group (1.72±0.38 vs. 3.07±0.72; P<0.001) while the mean resting MBF was higher (1.21±0.49 vs. 0.76±0.21 mL/g/min; P<0.001). Additionally, the PFR was significantly different between the CMD and non-CMD groups (303.5±53.8 vs. 348.5±55.7 mL/s; P=0.001). For LV global strain rate parameters, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two study groups (all P values >0.05).

Associations between CMD and PEDSR in all patients with INOCA

The correlation between global MFR and PEDSR parameters was further examined (Table 3). As illustrated in Figure 4, the global MFR was significantly correlated with all three directions of PEDSR (all P values <0.001), underscoring its key role in evaluating global cardiac function. In univariate regression models in which global MFR was a continuous independent variable and LV PEDSR parameters were continuous dependent variables, significant correlations were detected between global MFR and all three directions of PEDSR (all P values <0.001). Multivariate regression analysis in a model adjusted for known factors influencing LV diastolic function (age, gender, BMI, hypertension, smoking, and diabetes mellitus) also indicated that the factors with significant associations with global MFR were radial PEDSR [standardized β (95% CI) = −0.250 (−0.494 to −0.006); P=0.044] and circumferential PEDSR [standardized β (95% CI) = 0.464 (0.194 to 0.734); P=0.001].

Inter- and intraobserver variability

Inter- and intraobserver reproducibility was good to excellent for all three directions of PEDSR. For intraobserver assessments, the ICCs for radial PEDSR, circumferential PEDSR, and longitudinal PEDSR were 0.942 (95% CI: 0.892–0.969), 0.887 (95% CI: 0.793–0.939), and 0.873 (95% CI: 0.774–0.932), respectively; for interobserver assessments, the corresponding ICCs were 0.878 (95% CI: 0.786–0.935), 0.922 (95% CI: 0.857–0.954), and 0.832 (95% CI: 0.710–0.908).

Discussion

This study evaluated the physiological relationship between global MFR and subclinical LV diastolic function in patients with INOCA, with LV diastolic function being measured via PEDSR on a hybrid PET/MR device. The principal findings of the current study are as follows: (I) In patients with INOCA, those with lower MFR had a greater impairment of LV PEDSR compared to those with a higher MFR, despite both groups having similar global diastolic strain rates. (II) CMD was significantly associated with PEDSR in all three directions (radial, circumferential, and longitudinal) in both univariate and multivariate regression analyses, which were performed after adjustments we made for other clinical factors that affect cardiac function and deformation. (III) Multivariate analysis confirmed both PFR and ECV as independent predictors of radial PEDSR. Most notably, we observed a discrepancy between a significantly impaired PEDSR in all three myocardial directions and completely preserved global diastolic strain parameters in patients with CMD. This finding strongly suggests that PEDSR captures earlier subclinical myocardial relaxation abnormalities that precede the development of overt global diastolic dysfunction. Further, this constitutes evidence supporting the notion that LV diastolic dysfunction plays a pathogenic role in CMD, potentially through mechanisms including impaired coronary microvessel vasomotion and, in later disease stages, progressive fibrosis induced by chronic recurrent microvascular ischemia (17).

The LV wall comprises the endocardium, myocardium, and epicardium. The endocardium is the most susceptible to afterload and ischemia, with its function most easily compromised in various cardiac pathologies. LV circumferential function is primarily determined by the endocardial layer, while radial function is related to the myocardial layer (18). In our study, PEDSR parameters were all lower in the CMD group despite LVEF being preserved. Our findings, when viewed in conjunction with previous research, indicate that LV endocardial function and diastolic dysfunction are impaired in patients with suspected INOCA, thereby supporting the etiological associations between endocardial impairment, LV diastolic dysfunction, and CMD (15).

Consistent with our results, previous studies have shown that impaired MFR as indicated on noninvasive stress PET or Doppler echocardiography is independently associated with diastolic dysfunction and adverse cardiovascular events, including hospitalization for HFpEF. Moreover, an inverse relationship was identified between MFR and E/e’ in a large cohort of patients with HFpEF (19,20). In line with this, Mahmod et al. (21) reported that in a cohort of patients with HFpEF [N=27; mean age 72±7 years; 33% male (9/27)], CMR-derived circumferential PEDSR was significantly reduced compared to that in healthy controls. This parameter was also found to be positively correlated with myocardial steatosis and negatively correlated with peak exercise oxygen consumption. Notably, in another study (22) examining a cohort of patients with HFpEF [N=186; mean age 59±12 years; 59% male (109/186)], a reduced longitudinal PEDSR was linked to an elevated risk of hospitalization due to heart failure or all-cause mortality. However, there is conflicting evidence regarding systolic global longitudinal strain (GLS) in patients with CMD. A recent study by Merdler et al. (23) using gold-standard invasive coronary function testing to determine a CMD diagnosis (CFR <2.0 and IMR ≥25) found no significant differences in echocardiographic GLS or segmental strain between 21 patients with CMD and 38 patients with angina and no obstructive coronary artery disease (ANOCA) but without CMD. There are several possible explanations for these discrepant findings. First, GLS measures systolic myocardial function, while our study focused on diastolic strain rates, which appear to be more sensitive to early microvascular ischemia-induced myocardial damage. Second, CMR-FT offers superior spatial resolution compared to echocardiography, allowing for the more accurate detection of subtle, patchy regional abnormalities that may be missed by ultrasound. Finally, the timing of imaging relative to microvascular dysfunction may also contribute to these differences, as diastolic abnormalities typically precede systolic dysfunction in the natural history of CMD. In our study, global MFR was significantly correlated with PEDSR. However, in the analysis of global strain rates, an interesting observation was that non-CMD myocardial segments appeared to exhibit a compensatory effect, resulting in no decrease or even an increase in LV global CDSR and LDSR. This regional compensation effectively masks early diastolic impairment when only global parameters are evaluated, explaining why global strain rates remained normal despite significant regional PEDSR reductions. PEDSR, as a direct measure of regional myocardial relaxation velocity during the early diastolic phase, is inherently more sensitive than conventional global diastolic function parameters to subtle, patchy myocardial damage caused by coronary microvascular ischemia. Unlike global strain parameters that average function across the entire left ventricle, PEDSR can detect localized abnormalities even when adjacent segments are functioning normally. Another aspect differing from previous work is that our study was the first to use the radiation-free CMR-FT technique to assess LV diastolic function, which differs from the E/e’ and e’ indices applied in echocardiography. Furthermore, CMR-FT is capable of providing both global and segmental strain rate parameters, a feature that is particularly valuable in conditions associated with patchy myocardial fibrosis. Novel deep learning algorithms have facilitated the rapid and accurate fully automated segmentation of myocardial borders, thereby substantially enhancing workflow efficiency (24). However, the growing utilization of CMR across a wide range of guideline-recommended indications supports the integration of diastolic function assessment as a highly valuable practice. Employing CMR-FT for this purpose has the potential to eliminate the need for additional echocardiographic testing solely for the evaluation of diastolic function, enhance the diagnostic performance of CMR (e.g., in patients with HFpEF), and extend the indications for CMR, especially in patients with suboptimal acoustic windows. Despite these potential advantages, it is important to acknowledge that routine diastolic function assessment with CMR remains uncommon in current clinical practice. This is primarily due to the higher cost of CMR compared to echocardiography, which remains the first-line modality for diastolic function evaluation. Additionally, accurate CMR-FT analysis requires specialized training and expertise that may not be widely available in all clinical settings. Although these factors currently limit the immediate clinical translatability of our findings, they highlight important areas for future development, including the standardization of CMR diastolic assessment protocols and the implementation of fully automated postprocessing tools to lower expertise requirements and improve workflow efficiency.

At present, the pathophysiological mechanisms underlying the relationship between CMD and HFpEF remain unclear, raising the critical question as to whether CMD induces LV diastolic dysfunction and the subsequent development of HFpEF or, conversely, whether LV diastolic dysfunction detected in patients with HFpEF leads to secondary CMD. LV filling curves have been recognized as valuable quantitative tools for distinguishing volumetric filling alterations corresponding to varying severities of diastolic dysfunction. In the study by Mendoza et al. (25), diminished PFR and prolonged T-PFR were found to reflect a distinct aspect of global LV diastolic dysfunction, which is consistent with our results. However, Kong et al. (26) and Wei et al. (27) reported that in patients with INOCA, PFR and T-PFR exhibited no significant difference between the CMD group and the non-CMD group. This may be attributed to the fact that despite the presence of CMD, their study cohort, characterized by a lower incidence of comorbid conditions, might have been in the early stage of myocardial stiffness and in the early compensatory period. Additionally, a notable difference between their study cohort and ours was observed with respect to a larger proportion of older adult patients (mean age of 55 vs. 52 years) and the greater percentage of female participants (49.3% vs. 43.5%). Thus, the lack of decrease in PFR does not necessarily imply normal LV diastolic function. These findings have been consistently observed even in patients with HFpEF, and similar patterns were also reported for CMR-FT parameters (28). Thus, further investigations are needed to clarify the association between CMD and LV diastolic function in patient cohorts with differing grades of diastolic dysfunction.

Coronary blood flow occurs primarily in the diastolic phase, and it is conceivable that LV diastolic alterations may also independently contribute to CMD. In patients with INOCA who have LV diastolic dysfunction, MFR reduction may induce abnormal coronary microvascular perfusion, which coincides with the elevated metabolic demand resulting from pressure overload and increased ventricular wall thickness. Myocardial ischemia occurs in the setting of an imbalance between myocardial oxygen consumption (MVO2) supply and demand, and such ischemia is typically mostly or exclusively limited to the subendocardial region. In the WISE study, compared with control participants, study participants without obstructive CAD exhibited distinct alterations in systolic wave reflections and diastolic timing. These changes raise LV afterload and MVO2 demand, which may potentially impair coronary artery perfusion (29). This suggests that these alterations in cardiovascular function result in a detrimental imbalance between MVO2 supply and demand, which aggravates myocardial ischemia and chest pain.

Decreased coronary microvascular perfusion can also be affected by elevated LV filling pressure, which increases extravascular coronary resistance (30). Since the majority of coronary filling occurs during active LV relaxation, delayed LV relaxation can also lead to a decrease in hyperemic diastolic coronary flow (31,32). Another potential mechanism involves subclinical functional and structural changes secondary to chronic repetitive microvascular impairment. In our study, radial PEDSR, a marker of midwall LV diastolic function, showed a significant correlation with both PFR and ECV. It is important to recognize that global ECV measurements may lack sensitivity in detecting mild, patchy fibrosis that could still impair regional myocardial function. Therefore, coronary microvascular ischemia caused by CMD may induce myocardial injury, impair LV PEDSR, and ultimately lead to LV dysfunction. Notably, coronary microvascular endothelial dysfunction and elevated profibrotic cytokines are thought to contribute to a reduction in coronary microvascular density and enhanced myocardial fibrosis, ultimately resulting in the progression to HFpEF (19,33). However, our cross-sectional study design did not allow us to confirm this causal relationship, and the absence of significant global ECV differences in our cohort indicates that this fibrotic process might not have progressed to a detectable stage in most of the patients with early-stage INOCA. Moreover, a recent PET imaging study (19) established a significant correlation between CMD and diastolic dysfunction among patients with INOCA. These findings thus indicate that a core pathophysiological mechanism linked to HFpEF is LV diastolic dysfunction and increased myocardial stiffness secondary to coronary microvascular ischemia.

Although our study only demonstrated physiological associations between CMD and subclinical diastolic abnormalities, these findings should be viewed in the context of previous research that has established the prognostic significance of both CMD and diastolic dysfunction in patients with INOCA. Low MFR in patients with INOCA is associated with an unfavorable prognosis (34). Additionally, LV diastolic dysfunction itself is associated with a poor prognosis (35). Long-term follow-up data in patients with INOCA indicate a threefold increased risk of cardiac events compared to asymptomatic healthy controls, with heart failure hospitalization being the most common event (36,37). These findings suggest that CMD contributes to the development of HFpEF (38). It has also been proposed that the factors driving CMD also facilitate myocardial hypertrophy and fibrosis, which in turn contribute to diastolic dysfunction (39). However, it is important to emphasize that our study does not provide direct evidence linking the observed PEDSR abnormalities to clinical outcomes. The subclinical diastolic changes identified in our study may represent an intermediate phenotype in the progression from microvascular dysfunction to clinical heart failure, but this speculation remains to be confirmed through formal testing in large-scale prospective studies with long-term clinical follow-up.

Several limitations to this study should be acknowledged. First, a healthy cohort of participants was not incorporated as a control group. Second, although CMR-FT is a novel, rapid, and semiautomated approach for assessing myocardial function that requires no additional scans or sequences and shortens the postprocessing duration relative to conventional myocardial tissue tagging (40,41), it has inherent technical limitations. Specifically, the performance of its imaging algorithms relies on image quality, frames, and accurate delineation of endocardial borders. Furthermore, its temporal resolution is inferior to that of echocardiography, which may have implications for the evaluation of diastolic function-related strain rates. Echocardiography remains the first-line and most widely used modality for the clinical diagnosis of diastolic dysfunction. Although CMR-FT offers superior spatial resolution and reproducibility, it has not yet been integrated into standard clinical workflows for diastolic function assessment. Additionally, the feature tracking analyses were performed on resting CMR cine images, which precluded the establishment of direct associations between CMD-related ischemia and diastolic function. Third, we employed a single-center design with a relatively small sample size, and the patients were not followed up on. Overfitting risks were mitigated by restricting the number of covariates in each multivariate model to preserve statistical stability. Nevertheless, the limited sample size of these models suggests caution is needed in the interpretation of our findings. Therefore, further multicenter studies with larger sample sizes and extended follow-up periods are necessary to validate the results. Fourth, myocardial fibrosis as assessed by ECV cannot be confirmed by endomyocardial biopsy, which inevitably introduces a certain degree of ambiguity. Fifth, we did not include clinical endpoints or long-term follow-up data. As such, all the findings from our study should be interpreted strictly as physiological associations between coronary microvascular function and subclinical measures of myocardial relaxation. The clinical significance of these early diastolic changes remains to be determined, and future prospective studies with predefined clinical endpoints are required to establish their prognostic value and potential utility in risk stratification or therapeutic monitoring. Sixth, PET-derived global MFR was used to define CMD, which represents the most widely validated noninvasive method for this purpose. However, invasive intracoronary pressure and flow measurements remain the gold standard for definitive CMD diagnosis, and noninvasive MFR assessment may have inherent diagnostic limitations in certain patient subsets.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that patients with INOCA complicated with CMD had impaired LV early diastolic function. The LV myocardial perfusion parameter MFR was found to be significantly correlated with LV early diastolic function, which reflects the severity of early diastolic dysfunction. To further elucidate the relationship between CMD and LV early diastolic function, studies with a diversity of patient cohorts and varying grades of diastolic dysfunction should be conducted.
