Addressing the commercial determinants of unhealthy beverage exposure: a systems thinking approach
Authors: Megan R Winkler, Yeeli Mui, Alexis Smith, James Krieger
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe beverage industry plays an influential role in the US. Certain beverages, such as sugar-sweetened, have long been recognized as key contributors to poor population health with inequitable impacts. In response, multiple US initiatives have attempted to address these problems through public awareness campaigns and policies, such as taxes. Yet, the persistent and increasing reliance on unhealthy commercial products produced by the beverage industry suggests additional progress is needed. Leveraging a systems thinking approach, this study aimed to map out the complex forces limiting progress and driving inequitable impacts of unhealthy commercial beverages in the US to inform innovative thinking about future actions.MethodsWe facilitated a participatory group model building project to develop a systems model that depicts the main mechanisms underlying the increasing sales, marketing, and consumption of unhealthy commercial beverages. We convened 28 national experts from government, academia, advocacy, funding agencies, and community organizations for a series of workshops and interviews and supplemented their insights with other evidence (e.g., research, public records).ResultsThe final systems model makes visible the multiple interacting pathways through which industry protects their profits and maintains the status quo. Some are well known, such as normalizing product purchasing, influencing policymakers in their favor, and shaping public opinion to support their work. Others are less appreciated, such as how health and environmental consequences produced by beverage industry activities feedback into the system, ultimately leading to a greater reliance on beverage industry products and weakening political will for adopting equitable health promotion policies. While actions by advocates, public health departments, and others to address unhealthy beverages were identified, much of their impact is undermined by the large and influential control the beverage industry has on the system.ConclusionsGaining traction on addressing unhealthy commercial beverages is a complex problem with reinforcing cycles that strengthen the beverage industry’s power and influence. Background

Non-communicable diseases related to unhealthy diets continue to be the leading causes of premature death and disability around the globe [1, 2]. In the US, these diet-related diseases are not equally experienced, with greater burdens placed on socially-disadvantaged groups [3, 4]. Unhealthy commercial beverages, particularly sugar-sweetened beverages (SSBs), have long been recognized as a major contributor to these patterns [5–7]. Although the US has seen progress in SSB consumption since a peak in 2000, trends in recent years suggest progress has largely plateaued [8, 9]. Moreover, beverage marketing—while known to disproportionately target communities of color [10]— remains high and continues to increase overall [11, 12]. Further, beverage products with health consequences are increasingly understood to be more diverse than only SSBs. Growing evidence links non-sugar sweetened and low-calorie alternatives with poor metabolic health [13, 14], and plastic product containers are progressively recognized as vehicles for exposure to other potentially harmful chemicals, such as micro-plastics [15, 16]. At the same time, reports indicate that Americans are increasingly relying less on public sources of drinking water for hydration, with approximately 20% avoiding tap water altogether [17], while both the unit and dollar sales of non-alcohol commercial beverage products, including bottled water, are at record highs [18, 19].

Over the last two decades, multiple US initiatives have aimed to address some of these commercial beverage concerns, especially those related to SSBs and non-sugar-sweetened products. Some examples include public awareness campaigns by government [20] and advocacy [21] organizations about the health harms of SSBs. Others involve the enactment of policy levers [22], such as local SSB taxes [23], healthy default beverage laws, and other strategies (e.g., healthy checkout ordinance) [24] which, especially for SSB taxes, demonstrate effectiveness in reducing purchasing [25] and improving some health outcomes [26, 27]. Yet, to date, only 9 US jurisdictions have implemented a SSB tax [23], with the last one coming after a 6-year hiatus and is currently facing legal challenges [28, 29]. Further, such limited US policy momentum is coupled with ever-increasing beverage company profits [30, 31] and growing use of preemptive policies that block both local public health regulations (e.g., SSB taxes) and litigation against industry [32].

Taken together, these patterns indicate that the beverage industry has and continues to play a substantial and influential role in shaping how Americans hydrate in the US. While some industry adaptations could yield potential benefits for health and society, such as increased availability of seemingly more healthful options like unsweetened bottled water [18, 19], they may also generate unintended consequences that have not been fully scrutinized, including health and environmental harms associated with increased plastic bottle use [18]. Such dynamics highlight the value of applying a commercial determinants of health (CDOH) framework to better understand the beverage industry’s influence and power within the US. In particular, Gilmore and colleagues’ CDOH framework (2023) helps elucidate how commercial actors accumulate wealth and power through a range of explicit and implicit practices, often producing damaging externalities on population health and societal well‑being [33]. However, even corporate actors do not operate in silos; rather, they interact with governmental, economic, and social systems, creating complexity that must be considered when examining how observed outcomes are produced.

Complexity is a concept that is quite familiar to public health scholars and practitioners. For instance, poor nutritional health is commonly recognized as a complex problem resulting from multiple factors which interact across multiple scales—from upstream structural to downstream individual levels [3, 34–37]. However, the persistent growth in purchasing, consumption, and marketing of unhealthy commercial beverages, such as SSBs, despite decades of policy and consumer education work, suggests the complex problem of unhealthy beverages produced by the beverage industry may also benefit from being reconceptualized as a complex system [38, 39]. Complex systems not only consist of multiple interacting parts, but also produce patterns of general stability and predictability over time—such as those observed for unhealthy commercial beverages (e.g., increasing sales). Further, “high functioning” complex systems can resist change, making them highly challenging to alter, especially if the way the system operates to maintain the status quo is not fully known among those aiming to change it.

Systems science offers an important opportunity to understand complex systems [40–51]. Despite growing interest in ‘systems’ throughout public health, scientific tools that can meaningfully portray and visualize complex systems remain highly under-utilized [42]. Much of the science used to understand nutrition and public health challenges, and evaluate potential solutions, continues to rely on methods and linear thinking best suited for isolating singular effects, which are incapable of revealing how a system functions beyond the sum of its parts [38, 43]. Systems science, instead, facilitates a way to see complex systems as well as ideate on what actions could transform them [38, 44]. Part of this occurs because actions themselves are incorporated as part of the system, allowing users to understand how traditional interventions (e.g., public awareness campaigns, policies) and the system have adapted to one another over time [45]. It also provides opportunities to identify new actions as the visual tools enable users to think more operationally [43] and help identify leverage points that can transform the system given the relationships among system elements.

Given this, this project aimed to uncover the system that has been resistant to interventions aiming to address the high, increasing, and inequitable impacts of unhealthy commercial beverage purchasing, consumption, and marketing in the US. Leveraging a systems science approach, known as group model building, we elicited a multistakeholder understanding to map a collective conception of the system, its parts, and their relationships in a visual tool known as a causal loop diagram (CLD) [40, 41]. Through this work, we build upon theoretical work that demonstrates the potential of systems thinking approaches for unpacking and addressing complex problems [43–46] and also on practical applications focused on nutrition challenges [42, 47–50]. We do so by centering our attention on dynamics at both the national and local levels and explicitly starting with an orientation of the US unhealthy beverage problem as one inclusive of both the ‘supply’ (the beverage industry) and ‘demand’ (population purchasing) sides. We end by identifying an ambitious set of recommended actions informed by the participatory CLD, systems principles, and grounded in the CDOH framework [33, 52], which have the potential to transform the current system for population health and health equity.

Methods

Study design

The project was informed by community-based system dynamics and group model building (GMB) methods [40, 41, 53]. GMB is an approach that guides stakeholders through a series of activities to make mental models explicit and collectively synthesize these. The process involves having stakeholders identify key factors of the problem, their relationships, and eventually feedback loops that drive a system’s function and adaptability over time. Feedback loops describe relationships among factors in which a change in one variable eventually feeds back to influence that same variable, either directly or indirectly. As such, feedback loops are the mechanisms that drive system behavior, representing the internal structure of a system, and are central to explaining how a system functions over time [38, 41]. We conducted the project from August 2023-July 2024 over three phases and followed procedures used in prior GMB applications [42, 47–51]. Briefly, Phase 1 (August-December 2023) involved a two-day workshop with national stakeholders (n = 24) to gain a preliminary understanding of the system. Participants were purposively recruited using maximum variation sampling to represent a diverse range of perspectives grounded in public health that could also speak from local to national contexts. Specifically, participants represented local, state, and federal-levels of government (n = 6); national advocacy organizations (n = 7); academia (n = 7); and community-led organizations (e.g., food policy councils) (n = 4)—all of which were engaged in work to address unhealthy beverages. Participants were guided through a series of workshop activities (Table 2) that produced 4 initial CLDs across breakout groups. Following, the core modeling team completed a multi-stage process to synthesize breakout groups’ CLDs into one comprehensive model. This involved identifying commonalities among CLDs and resolving divergences through team discussions. Through this process, the team refined the model so that the most important feedback loops and variables were represented in the most parsimonious way. Phase 1 ended with a follow-up workshop to gain participant feedback on the initial synthesized model and hold an action idea discussion (Table 2). Starting with a list of potential actions generated during the first workshop, we asked participants to use the initial synthesized CLD to identify the most promising strategies.

In Phase 2 (January-March 2024), the core modeling team conducted consultations, through semi-structured interviews, with workshop participants (n = 6) who had specific expertise to clarify areas of the initial model identified by workshop participants as underdeveloped or inaccurate. We then revised the model and presented the revision for a second round of asynchronous feedback to all workshop participants.

Phase 3 (April-July 2024) consisted of a final round of data collection to complete areas of the CLD that workshop participants believed were important to include but uncertain how to fully incorporate. The model team reviewed other forms of written evidence (e.g., published literature, organizational reports) and conducted key informant interviews with individuals who did not participate in the workshop series but had relevant expertise to inform the model (n = 4). The model was then revised based on Phase 2 participant feedback and the new evidence to create the final model again prioritizing a parsimonious model to improve interpretability.

Additional details of methods are described in Appendix A and B. This study followed guidelines laid down in the Declaration of Helsinki, and all study procedures involving human participants were approved by the Emory University Institutional Review Board (Protocol STUDY00005972). Every human participant provided consent to participate prior to study participation.

Strengths and limitations

This study has certain limitations. Like all GMB processes, model results are dependent on who contributed, which shape the boundaries of the system and can lead to omission of other key variables or processes. While having a wide breadth of expertise and diverse experiences spanning local to national—especially for government health departments—allowed for broad representation of perspectives, doing so within a relatively small group may have limited the depth of insight into specific institutional or geographic contexts within the US. In addition, participants were recruited based on their professional engagement in efforts to address sugar‑ and non‑sugar‑sweetened unhealthy beverages. As a result, some domains and feedback loops of the system were not fully represented in the initial versions of the CLD (e.g., environmental consequences of industry manufacturing) as the expertise to finish them did not exist with this initial group of experts. We mitigated some of these concerns through use of outside key informant interviews and other forms of written evidence in Phase 3, yet results are still a product of the specific inputs. The GMB process was also challenging at times for the participants. Some struggled with identifying key feedback loops and following how variables influenced one another through indirect (versus direct) relationships and mechanisms. Some felt overwhelmed by the CLD and unclear how this could inform next actions. This challenge is not unique to our project as others have identified that using the behavior of a set of feedback loops to identify effective actions is difficult [38, 44, 50]. Finally, the CLD is a reflection of participant mental models at this point in time and it assumes the dynamics displayed will continue into the future; however, as other strategies to address industry power are tested and the behavior of sectors evolves, it will be important to modify the model to ensure it captures current conditions.

While our sampling strategy introduced certain limitations, it also conferred important strengths. Specifically, it facilitated multisectoral representation among participants, which is well suited to surfacing a wide range of perspectives and system dynamics and is appropriate for systems-mapping work. In addition, because many participants represented key national-level actors working on or studying unhealthy beverages in the US, the model was able to maintain our national focus rather than becoming overly context-specific. Another key strength of this study was the repeated interaction with workshop participants. This iterative engagement over a year allowed for deeper reflection by workshop participants and the model team than is typical of GMB applications, which commonly engage participants in one-time workshops. We believe this extended engagement enhanced depth of understanding of the system and, in part, offset the breadth associated with the wide‑ranging sampling strategy used in Phase 1. We also allowed the system boundaries to be broad resulting in a wider range of key mechanisms to be identified than prior work yet balanced this with a parsimony approach to synthesis to keep its broad dynamic patterns understandable.

Results

The final systems model (Fig. 1) included 31 variables (see Appendix C for definitions) and eight feedback loops that create cycles of reinforcing effects across multiple sectors over time. All loops were reinforcing, where change in one direction is compounded by more change (i.e., if they grow, they continue to grow or if they decay, they continue to deteriorate). Importantly, feedback loops and relationships between variables largely represent long-term relationships, often occurring over years and in some case decades. While the team started with framing the problem as unhealthy beverages (i.e., mainly SSBs and non-sugar sweetened beverages, See Appendix B), over the course of the project this was broadened to commercial beverages at-large to capture a wider range of health and environmental consequences from these products. Specifically, commercial beverages encompass all products produced by beverage manufacturers, including SSBs, non-sugar sweetened alternatives, and bottled water. As a result, the reference behavior of the system is the high and inequitable sales, marketing and impacts of unhealthy commercial beverages in the US, which continues to demonstrate persistent and incremental growth [18, 19]. Key sectors in the system are identified by colored arrows and include: (1) the beverage industry (orange arrows); (2) advocates (pink arrows); (3) climate (green arrows); (4) government agencies and governance (blue arrows); and (5) health and finance (black arrows). The light green overlay entitled Neoliberalism & Colonial Racial Capitalism represents key systems of oppression that intersect across the entire model to drive inequities throughout it. Normalization of commercial beverages [R1]

This feedback loop represents the market strategies the beverage industry uses to get consumers to purchase their products. Participants described how industry’s product formulation, their vast product profiles—from SSBs to non-sugar-sweetened products and bottled water—along with their ubiquitous media and retail marketing strategies (e.g., product placement, pricing promotion) have increasingly displaced non-commercialized water systems as sources of hydration (e.g., public water sources, Indigenous water systems). As one participant described in relation to the beverage industry’s approach in historically marginalized communities, “We are seeing all these trucks coming with unhealthy food [and beverages], and there’s no stop to them. When I go to the gas station, it’s like a candy store full of all different types of beverages…But [these beverages] have never been a part of our culture” and “I think [these beverages] is not who we are” (Community Organizer).

The persistent displacement over generations has led to the dominance of commercial beverage norms or shared beliefs about what are considered appropriate and expected sources of beverages in the US. These norms subsequently shape behavior and, when reinforced by environments that consistently cue commercial beverages over other hydration sources, result in habitual actions that bypass conscious evaluation; in some cases, leading to internalization of these norms (e.g., feel internal motivation to bring a purchased sports drink or plastic bottled water to an event rather than a refillable water bottle). Consequently, these norms and habits drive repeated and reinforced purchasing behaviors of commercial beverages that lead to beverage industry profits which enable further investment in influential market strategies that reinforce these norms.

Corporatocracy & the beverage industry [R2]

Beverage industry profits can also be used for industry strategies that aim to promote regulatory, ideologic, and cultural environments that support their market practices [52]. One well-known set of strategies targets government officials using lobbying tactics, political donations, and other approaches which can lead to a political system directed by corporate interests (i.e., corporatocracy). In the context of commercial beverages, this dynamic weakens political will to advance health promotion through policy levers such as taxation, which unlike in the US are gaining traction globally [23]. Instead, public policies at state and national levels often align with industry priorities (e.g., preemption laws that limit local jurisdictions’ ability to implement SSB taxes or restrictions on lawsuits alleging health harms from long‑term consumption [32])—resulting in additional industry power and influence. As one participant described in the context of potential reforms that could limit such industry political tactics, “If there’s… reform, policymakers won’t be scared or afraid to support [health promotion] things… then that leads to [related industry] policy enactment and enforcement (Academic).”

Industry shaping public opinion [R3]

The beverage industry also deploys tactics directed at improving relations and trust with the public. These include public relations (PR) and anti-regulation campaigns, community investments, and strategic alliances with public-trusting organizations and institutions (e.g., scientific organizations) that strengthen positive views of the beverage industry. As a result, public interest in beverage company regulation or policies (e.g., supporting a SSB tax) is diminished. As one participant described, “To me, all of these factors feed into ensuring industry continues to safeguard its ‘social license’ for business as usual… this [is the] ultimate objective behind building a positive public view of corporations (Advocate).” Through these reputational management strategies and dissuading public support for industry regulation, the industry can achieve other goals, such as reducing elected officials’ support for equitable health promotion policies and, as further discussed below, affect non-government organizations’ (NGO) support for corporate accountability strategies to address industry influence.

Inequitable health & the healthcare system [R4]

The health consequences of commercial beverage purchasing patterns are displayed in the feedback loop that first demonstrates that purchasing leads to consumption of not only sugar and non-sugar sweeteners, but also microplastics—all of which negatively affect health. These poor health outcomes then require treatments offered by the healthcare and pharmaceutical industries, which translate into revenues for these sectors. Much like the beverage industry, these revenues are used to influence policymakers to support the needs and interests of the health sector. In fact, 2023 data from OpenSecrets highlights that companies, physicians, and other health professionals in these industries collectively spent 25 times the amount on political tactics than the food and beverage industry spent [54, 55]. By advocating for resources to treat ever-growing levels of chronic disease in the US population, the health and pharmaceutical industries can further undermine a health promotion approach to policymaking. As one participant described, “There’s a lot of political discussion about health care, and it’s the health care system that is kind of…rewarded. The worse health gets, the more [policymakers] reward in terms of the ability to give treatment. So… what is the political will for– [treatment or prevention]? (Academic)”.

Environmental impacts from manufacturing and health care [R5 and R6]

In addition to health consequences, participants described numerous environmental impacts from these systems, such as increases in greenhouse gases, plastic pollution, and depletion of freshwater resources. Some of these are a product of beverage manufacturing, but as participants and research [56–61] also suggests these are a product of the healthcare system due to the growing demand for care to treat diet-related chronic disease and the energy and supply chain needs required to meet it. For example, while in its early stages, the links between healthcare sector and its impacts on climate change are increasingly studied [59]. Studies have found that hospital care (e.g., resource intensive activities such as surgical suites) [56, 62], pharmaceutical and medical products [56, 63], and transportation of supplies, patients, visitors and staff are among the primary mechanisms contributing to these environmental challenges [59]. Such environmental impacts can then lead to increases in water pollution, such as the abundance of harmful chemicals (e.g., PFAS from plastic bottles), as well as climate change and increases in water stress. Together, these have implications for the reliability and safety of the public water supply, as one participant described “we know climate change has really impacted the access to water (Community Organizer).” Participants frequently mentioned how this lack of access to safe, reliable drinking water leads to the public belief that commercial beverages are better than public water sources. As one participant described, “It’s challenging when water coming out of the tap is not looking or tasting good, and those families are finding safety and solace in bottled water manufacturers, because they have a lot of marketing dollars to make their product look good (Local Public Health Official).”

Government agencies (constrained) actions [R7]

To address the health and environmental impacts, government agencies have acted, including the work of water departments to address the safety and reliability of the water infrastructure and the work of public health departments to change norms and enact other policy, system, and environmental changes around unhealthy beverages and public water. However, participants also identified important barriers in moving these prevention-oriented actions forward when policymakers’ support for these are dampened by the influence of both the beverage and health sector industries. As one government participant described of recent industry lobbying against a proposed public health nutrition policy, “a company spent more [on lobbying against that one policy] than our whole [government] division receives in a year (National/State Public Health Official).” Beyond budgets, there may be other constraints, including confusion among government personnel over what is allowed in terms of policy advisement and education that does not place them in violation of lobbying statutes (see resources [64, 65] for distinctions). Constraints may also be set in other ways, as another participant described, there is “an underappreciation from non-government persons of our funding appropriation and autonomy…Funds get appropriated…[in] a very detailed appropriation bill… And, then we administer that.. Often the NGOs thinks we have more autonomy over the funding than we do (National/State Public Health Official).” The CLD partially represents this appropriation in the feedback loop through separate lines for the public health and water departments (Fig. 1), which may ultimately constrain cross-department coordination for similar hydration and health goals.

Advocating for corporate accountability [R8]

The last feedback loop represents how advocates are seeking greater corporate accountability. While advocates expressed the need for work that moves from talking about products to the companies that make the products, it was evident that a lack of resources and capacity limited the amount they could engage in this work. As one participant described, “What we need is long haul funding that can fund multiple campaigns over multiple years. And that’s just not there right now” (Advocate). Funders of advocacy work also recognized this gap, saying “Where I think we fall short is that we have been so focused on a “carrots” approach to industry involvement and done almost nothing [no funding] around the “sticks” … Industry accountability is something we haven’t figured out” (Advocate). Reasons for funders’ limited support of corporate accountability strategies included the funder’s investments in the health, pharmaceutical, and beverage industries, which may constrain how directly funders can challenge corporate behavior; limited public interest in corporate accountability, which can further reduce some funders’ appetite for acting as corporate watchdogs; and shifts in NGO leadership priorities away from obesity and diet concerns toward other issues. As one participant noted, funders “want to move on the next shiny object (Advocate).”

Neoliberalism & Colonial Racial Capitalism Overlay

Finally, the model shows the far-reaching role systems of oppression, including neoliberalism, racism, capitalism, and displacement of non-commercial (Indigenous) water systems, play across the CLD. The model team elected to not limit this to a single feedback loop as participants felt it was the only way to adequately display the pervasive ways these forces intersect across the entire system to drive inequities throughout it. For instance, as one participant reflected, “ I am pleased to see neoliberalism and colonial racial capitalism in the model. I think [it affects] all the causal loops because I don’t think public health, NGOs, or even consumers, are not touched by these deeply embedded systems that influence everything and really produce the inequitable health outcomes we see (Local Public Health Official).” Participants also shared how systems of oppression shaped industry’s behavior, such as use of targeted marketing, strategic alliances, and anti-regulation campaigns that portray SSB taxes as discriminatory (i.e., regressive).

Discussion

The systems model illustrates key pathways that have contributed to the persistently high and inequitable purchasing, consumption, and marketing of unhealthy beverages in the U.S. These pathways have created cycles of reinforcing effects enabling the beverage industry to have far-reaching influence on population health, equity, and the environment, while simultaneously restricting the important work of advocates and governments to promote health and mitigate industry harm – ultimately reinforcing the status quo. In the following sections, we first discuss three primary insights from the model and conclude with a proposed list of transformative actions to disrupt industry’s influence on the system.

Insights

The dominant and harmful role of the food and beverage industry is well-known in public health [43, 52, 66]. The systems approach makes this power visible by showing a fuller landscape of industry strategies used across multiple sectors and the consequences these have for our health and environments, which can feedback into the system to reinforce industry power [33]. The specific industry strategies highlighted in the CLD map on well to the CDOH framework [33, 52] that conceptualizes how industry uses different market and non-market strategies to stabilize cultural, political, and economic environments to their benefit. The model makes explicit how these strategies are intertwined, reinforcing industry control of the system and allowing them to act more nimbly in response to counteracting forces. For instance, any momentum in advocacy work to build public awareness of industry strategies may be swiftly met by the beverage industry amplifying their political strategies to influence policymakers [51] and PR campaigns to maintain favorable views among the public at-large.

Such nimble industry responses highlight another important model insight—much of the system operates in a way that reinforces the status quo of a ‘pathologic system.’ [33] The beverage industry’s direct tactics targeting consumers, policymakers, and the public block change, as do less commonly understood indirect pathways via other feedback loops. For instance, the adverse health outcomes associated with commercial beverages ultimately result in limited political support for disease prevention as it causes policymakers to focus limited resources towards treating ever-growing levels of chronic disease. Consequently, other connected feedback loops, like the work of government agencies, are limited in their counteraction strength as their budget size and appropriated funds depend on policymakers’ decision-making which is highly influenced by industry and public pressure to fund chronic disease treatment. As others have previously pointed out [43, 51, 67, 68], corporations have a significant influence within governments, and until limits are placed on this influence, little progress is likely to be made on correcting the numerous societal and health consequences produced by commercial entities.

Finally, we have received frequent feedback from participants and other audiences that the dynamics in our CLD are translatable to many other contexts. This includes how the beverage industry operates in countries beyond the US, which is especially salient given the growing global consumption of SSBs [69], increasing non-communicable chronic disease burden [1], and recent inaction to address it [70]. Others have commented on how feedback loops appear to represent dynamics driven by many other health- and planet-harming industries beyond food and beverage. The shared mechanisms that lead to industry power and corporate control call for a movement that both integrates a CDOH approach and is larger in scale than any single industry.

A transformative action agenda

A major goal of this project was to identify novel actions that could lead to greater traction on addressing unhealthy commercial beverages in the US. Given our results, it is evident that actions must address industry dominance over how the system operates and counter this influence. We describe four actions that are informed by recommendations for systems change [38, 46], participant ideas generated during the workshops, a CDOH perspective [52], and ultimately reflection by the model team upon how the CLD system operates. These are strategies that prioritize changing relationships within the system (e.g., modifying linkages between system elements, adding feedback) to change how the system behaves. They do not include important actions that focus on single variables or solutions (e.g., add a new SSB retail policy). As a participant noted, “There is a disconnect between [the CLD model] …and the policy and the action items [we generated] where I feel like… [the CLD] is a big picture. …But the action items are the same old thing we’ve been talking about for 20 years that’s not solving the issue. We need to change society” (Academic)—an interpretation echoed by others [71]. We offer this list with much humility as we know, “it’s one thing to understand how to fix a system and quite another to wade in and fix it” (p. 167) [38].

Enhance systems thinking abilities among decision-makers, advocates, and society at-large. Society continues to largely view problems and their solutions through direct cause and effect pathways that reduce problems into smaller digestible pieces. This dominant perspective at best diminishes and at worst ignores the complementary value a systems approach provides to understanding and resolving complex problems [38, 43, 44]. Yet, even when working with those willing to think about systems, challenges still arise, such as users’ limited ability to infer the system’s behavior, detect the most effective actions, and recognize how best to achieve change even when key leverage points are identified [44, 50]. Moreover, a limited capacity to think in and talk about systems can prevent interrogation of why and how systems were originally designed to result in the social hierarchies, deeply-engrained paradigms, and patterns of advantage and disadvantage that persist today.Together, this indicates a need to build capacity in systems thinking, especially among those with the most opportunity to change the system. Potential strategies could include making systems thinking competencies a more central part of education (from K-12 through advanced degrees). It could also involve increasing training among politicians, organizational leaders, practitioners, and advocates to apply the skills and ensure new policies and reforms dismantle systemic inequities rather than replicate discriminatory systems. Systems-oriented researchers and educators could also identify better dissemination strategies that translate systems insights into formats that are more readily usable and accessible by end users. We list this action first because widespread systems thinking and historical reflection has significant potential to facilitate implementation of the following actions and address a host of problems beyond the beverage industry.

Build power to counter industry influence by bolstering cross-sector, cross-field collaborations and networks. A key piece of advice in considering actions to change systems is “before you charge in to make things better, pay attention to the value of what’s already there [working in the system]” (p. 178) [38]. This often means looking at the forces and structures that are currently in the system that can be built upon to achieve a goal. For our CLD, this means looking at the important public health work of government agencies and advocates. Building stronger connections between the separate work of the public health nutrition and public water departments may be one initial strategy as both ultimately aim to support public water consumption. Accordingly, this may mean governments reconsidering unnecessary barriers (e.g., segregated appropriation budgets) and providing directives to engage in cross-department work.The CLD also suggests that public health nutrition advocates should build connections with a wider set of advocacy groups aiming to counter industry power and limit its negative environmental impacts. Such alliances could move the work forward by leveraging resources, sharing connections to media and policymakers, and coordinating action to galvanize corporate accountability work. Collective organizing is a key strategy to address corporate power [51, 72], but requires finding shared priorities and goals. For instance, public health nutrition may need to see beverages as harmful not only because of their nutritional composition but because of the micro-plastics leaching from the container, which are a priority of environmental advocates. This may also mean moving beyond health-specific policies, for instance policies regulating the retail or digital marketing of specific products (alcohol, sugar-sweetened beverages), to supporting a set of regulations that address corporate influence over policymakers (e.g., political campaign finance reform)—typically viewed “as someone else’s fight” (p. 302) [73]. United work is essential to build a collective movement capable of addressing industry power.

Disrupt the connection between industry and political will. The CLD indicates that one of the strongest leverage points in the model is disrupting the power industry has on lowering policymakers’ political will to implement health promotion policies. In fact, the system has significant potential to recalibrate in meaningful ways for health and health equity if the link between industry and policymakers can be severed or at least rules developed so the voice of industry is subordinate to that of the people or public health experts during policy development and implementation. Insist (uncompromisingly) on new system goals that prioritize health promotion and equity over corporate power. All systems have a goal that dictate the way structural elements, their relationships, and feedback loops are developed to conform to that goal. Many would characterize the goal depicted in our CLD as industry’s aim to maintain a status quo that favors their interests and guards them from uncertainty [38]. Our model shows how industry has developed and maintained relationships with the public and government to keep climate and chronic disease concerns and calls for corporate accountability at bay as it drives towards its goal.Yet, as goals are one of the highest places to intervene in a system, the deepest systems changes occur when goals are rewritten [38]. As one workshop participant expressed, changing the focus of the problem or the goal of the system “might lead us to a different understanding of both who to mobilize and how to mobilize them in order to bring about interventions that would change even the narrow [SSB] outcome[s]” (Academic). And we could not agree more. Such a reorientation might be achieved by centering alternative, marginalized models that work toward goals of disrupting corporate power (e.g., cooperative ownership [80, 81] and Indigenous models [82]), as well as simply illuminating the often-overlooked goals of systems and mechanisms that sustain them—as this project sought to do. Early adopters within the system who explicitly prioritize limiting corporate power and profit maximization could then help diffuse these goals more broadly, facilitating wider systemic change. If the system’s goal changes from industry dominance to equitable health promotion [51], profound changes will follow such as strengthened civic engagement and inclusive governance structures, investment in communities that have been marginalized, and dismantling long-standing systems of oppression.

Exemplar policies and actions to disrupt the connection between industry and political will

• Reform corporate spending in political campaigns. This could include:

• Revising what industry influence is allowed,

• How political contributions are disclosed, and

• Reconsidering the federal court decision in Citizens United v. Federal Election Commission that overturned previous restrictions on election spending from companies.

• Mandate disclosure of all past and present industry relationships, funding (e.g., current investments), and activities by all members of policy bodies—including official government employees and those in special advisory roles to policymakers.

• Increase transparency and disclosure of industry’s political activity and funding sources. Examples include:

• Mandate financial disclosure by nonprofit “think tanks” and trade associations of their industry and corporation funding.

• Mandate disclosure of lobbying activities by both companies and nonprofit “think tanks” that promote company priorities.

• Improve the quality of disclosure of the ‘revolving door’ between government and industry (e.g., ensure lobbyists disclose any prior government work).

• Have state attorney generals and others monitor corporate interference with elections and democracy.

• Mandate disclosure of all industry/government interactions (e.g., one-on-one or small group meetings between legislators and chief executive officers) by publicly providing meeting details and minutes (e.g., detailed contact logs).

• Establish regulations to govern industry/government interactions, such as following recommendations from the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control75 of only having interactions when it is necessary for governments to successfully regulate industry products and practices.

• Modify laws which provide corporations with the legal interpretation of having the same rights as individual citizens (e.g., protection of commercial speech under the First Amendment).

• Enhance lawmakers’ knowledge and understanding of industry’s unhealthy products, public health and environmental impacts, and tactics used to distract from their societal impacts.

• Train and build confidence among government employees at state and federal agencies on how to educate policymakers on health policy without lobbying.

• Ensure public health advocates and experts are present and have an adequate (potentially amplified) voice in all policy phases.

• Increase industry transparency and disclosure around research funding, philanthropy, donations to NGOs, and other activities used to influence public opinion (e.g., relationships with scientists and professional associations).

• Ban industry participation in public policy advisory groups, like the Dietary Guidelines for Americans.

• List names of chief executive officers from corporations or other corporate foundations involved in sponsoring and underwriting political commercials.

• Revive the Fairness Doctrine that proposed giving equal media time to corporations and advocacy and civil groups that will educate on public health policy issues.

• Avoid industry-government partnership narratives as a key principle of ‘good’ governance in government bills, documents, and reports.

• Investigate through public data sources whether peaks in industry lobbying correlate with the implementation of favorable industry policies or other forms of influence (e.g., political donations).

Conclusion

The systems model developed by this project illustrates key pathways that give the beverage industry power and influence which allows it to maintain the status quo of persistently high and inequitable purchasing, consumption, and marketing of unhealthy commercial beverages in the U.S. It shows how the work of advocates and governments to promote health and mitigate industry harm is constrained by industry power. Overall, it suggests that a focus on the commercial determinants of health, enhanced systems thinking, and cross-issue collective action around limiting industry influence on policy is needed to reduce corporate power and transform the system for health equity and chronic disease prevention.
