Suicidal Thoughts, Behaviors, and Mental Health Treatment Use in US Military Veterans
Authors: Ian C. Fischer, Brandon Nichter, Peter J. Na, Melanie L. Hill, John H. Krystal, Robert H. Pietrzak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
This cross-sectional study evaluates prevalence estimates, factors associated with, and treatment utilization among US military veterans with a history of suicidal thoughts and behaviors.

Introduction

Suicide remains a major public health problem among US military veterans and is a leading cause of death in this population.1 According to the most recent data released by the US Department of Veterans Affairs (VA), 2023 age-adjusted suicide rates were approximately double among female veterans compared with female nonveterans (103.1% higher) and 49.7% higher among male veterans compared with male nonveterans. Suicide deaths among veterans have, overall, been increasing since 2001, and more than 6000 veterans continue to die by suicide each year.1 These patterns point to the need for up-to-date population-based data that describe how suicide risk is distributed across the US veteran population and whether this risk varies by sociodemographic, military, and psychiatric characteristics. Indeed, it is well-established that suicidal thoughts and behaviors (STBs; suicidal ideation, suicide planning, and suicide attempts) are among the strongest predictors of suicide risk.2,3

Updated population-based estimates of STBs are important because prevalence estimates are time-bound, and the size, age distribution, and service-era composition of the US veteran population changes over time. For example, Gulf War–era veterans have surpassed Vietnam-era veterans to make up the largest segment of the living veteran population.4 Moreover, the total number of veterans are declining, and women and veterans from racial and ethnic minority groups are projected to account for increasing proportions of the US veteran population.4

Nationally representative data on STBs among US veterans have previously been provided by the National Health and Resilience in Veterans Study (NHRVS), a population-based survey designed to characterize mental health and related outcomes in the US veteran population.5 The most recent published NHRVS estimates, based on data collected in 2019 to 2020, indicated that 9.0% of US veterans reported past 2-week suicidal ideation, 7.3% reported lifetime suicide planning, and 3.9% reported a lifetime suicide attempt.6 Results further revealed that major depressive disorder (MDD), younger age, posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) emerged as the strongest factors associated with suicidal ideation and suicide plans, whereas MDD, younger age, alcohol use disorder, and ACEs were the strongest factors associated with suicide attempts. Despite the elevated clinical risk among veterans reporting suicidal ideation, only 35.5% were engaged in any form of current mental health treatment (ie, psychotherapy or pharmacotherapy use), with veterans who identified the VA as their primary source of health care being more than twice as likely as non-VA users to receive mental health treatment (54.7% vs 23.8%).

In the present study, we analyzed data from the most recent 2025 to 2026 wave of the NHRVS to address 3 primary aims. First, we characterized the prevalence of current and past-year suicidal ideation, lifetime suicide planning, and lifetime suicide attempts among US military veterans. Second, we identified sociodemographic, military, and psychiatric factors associated with STBs. Third, we examined mental health treatment utilization among veterans reporting STBs overall and stratified by primary utilization of VA health care services.

Methods

Sample

In this cross-sectional study, data were analyzed from the 2025 to 2026 National Health and Resilience in Veterans Study (NHRVS), a nationally representative survey of 2636 US military veterans conducted between December 16, 2025, and January 12, 2026. To date, the NHRVS has recruited 4 independent, cross-sectional samples of US veterans (2011, 2013, 2019-2020, and 2025-2026), each drawn from the same underlying sampling frame but comprising nonoverlapping participants. Thus, the NHRVS represents a series of independent cross-sectional samples at each wave, whereas its source population, Ipsos KnowledgePanel, is an ongoing, open cohort that continuously recruits members over time. The current sample was drawn from KnowledgePanel, the largest and longest-standing probability-based online panel of the US adult population (>50 000 members), which is designed to be nationally representative and covers approximately 98% of US households. Panel members are recruited using address-based sampling (ABS), with earlier dual-frame telephone methods, thereby including listed and unlisted numbers, cell-phone–only households, and households with and without internet access (with devices and connectivity provided as needed). Methodological considerations of bias were addressed using a total survey error framework. Coverage bias was minimized through ABS recruitment procedures, sampling bias through probability-based selection methods, and nonresponse bias through structured panel management protocols that included reminder contacts and respondent incentives. Poststratification weighting and trimming procedures were additionally used to reduce residual selection and nonresponse biases and limit the influence of extreme weights. Measurement error was minimized through rigorous questionnaire design and standardized survey administration procedures, and data processing error was reduced through validation checks and systematic data cleaning protocols. Although KnowledgePanel recruitment is ongoing, each NHRVS wave is independently sampled from this panel, and there is no overlap between the 2025 to 2026 sample and prior waves (including 2019-2020), supporting the validity of between-wave comparisons.

To permit generalizability to the US veteran population, poststratification weights were computed by the Ipsos statistical team using benchmark distributions from the August 2024 Current Population Survey Veterans Supplement, including age, sex, race and ethnicity, Census region, metropolitan status, education, household income, branch of service, and years of military service. Final weights were generated using iterative proportional fitting (raking). Veterans completed a 40-minute, anonymous, web-based survey, and all participants provided electronic informed consent. The study was approved by the Human Subjects Committee of the VA Connecticut Healthcare System and adhered to Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) and American Association for Public Opinion Research (AAPOR) reporting guidelines.

Measures

Table 1 displays all study measures.7,8,9,10,11,12,13 STB outcomes were past 2-week and past-year suicidal ideation, and lifetime suicide planning and attempts. Associated factors included sociodemographic (ie, age, sex, self-reported race and ethnicity [non-Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and non-Hispanic White], marital status, education, and household income), military (ie, branch, enlistment status, and number of deployments), and psychiatric characteristics (ie, lifetime MDD, PTSD, alcohol use disorder [AUD], and substance use disorder [SUD]; ACEs, cumulative trauma burden, concussion or traumatic brain injury (TBI), physical health problems, and disability level). Race and ethnicity were assessed in this study to describe the study sample and examine whether the prevalence of suicidal thoughts and behaviors varied across racial and ethnic groups. Mental health treatment utilization covered lifetime and current psychotherapeutic and psychopharmacologic treatment.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis proceeded in 6 steps. First, descriptive statistics were computed to estimate the prevalence of STBs. Second, Bonferroni-corrected 2-proportion z tests (α = .05/3 = .017) were used to compare 2025 to 2026 STB prevalence estimates to published 2019 to 2020 NHRVS estimates.6 Third, χ2 analyses were conducted to examine differences in STBs by age and sex. Fourth, multivariable logistic regression analyses were conducted to identify variables independently associated with ideation, planning, and attempts. Fifth, to quantify the proportional contribution of each independent variable to explained variance in each outcome, relative importance analyses were conducted. These analyses decompose the model R2 into nonnegative weights summing to 100% by constructing maximally correlated orthogonal variables from the original variables; this approach is robust to multicollinearity among variables and enables direct comparison of the unique explanatory contribution of each factor within a given model.14 Sixth, χ2 analyses were used to estimate the prevalence of mental health treatment utilization among veterans with STBs in the full sample and stratified by use of VA as their primary source of health care. All analyses were performed using SPSS v 29.0 (IBM).

Results

Sample Characteristics

Participants had a mean (SD [range]) age of 62.4 (16.2 [19-99] years). Of 2636 participants, 2349 (88.5%) were male; 244 (12.8%) were non-Hispanic Black, 151 (7.8%) were Hispanic, and 2134 (74.6%) were non-Hispanic White; 1814 (67.1%) were married; and 1426 (63.5%) had some college education or lower educational attainment. Most enlisted into the military (2042 participants [80.5%]), and 1069 (45.8%) served in the Army and 562 (20.6%) in the Navy. Overall, 660 (27.3%) reported using the VA as their primary source of health care

Prevalence of STBs

Table 2 presents the prevalence of STBs. The weighted prevalences of past 2-week and past-year suicidal ideation, lifetime suicide planning, and lifetime suicide attempt were 13.4% (95% CI, 11.7%-15.3%), 14.0% (95% CI, 12.3%-16.0%), 8.0% (95% CI, 6.7%-9.5%), and 4.9% (95% CI, 3.9%-6.3%), respectively. Relative to 2019 to 2020 estimates,6 the prevalence of past 2-week suicidal ideation increased from 9.0% to 13.4% (z = 5.69; P < .001), representing a 49% increase over 5 years in the general US veteran population. In contrast, the prevalence estimates of lifetime suicide planning and attempts were similar to prior estimates6: 7.3% to 8.0% (z = 1.06; P = .29) for planning and 3.9% to 4.9% (z = 1.97; P = .048) for attempts.

Prevalence of Suicidal Thoughts and Behaviors Among US Military Veterans

In the current 2025 to 2026 sample, the prevalence of past-year suicidal ideation differed significantly as a function of age, with veterans aged 18 to 44 years exhibiting substantially higher rates (29.9%; 95% CI, 23.4%-37.3%) relative to those aged 45 to 64 years (14.3%; 95% CI, 11.8%-17.3%), who, in turn, had higher rates relative to those aged 65 years or older (7.6%; 95% CI, 6.3%-9.2%). Lifetime suicide planning and attempts were more prevalent among veterans aged 18 to 44 years (16.5%; 95% CI, 11.6%-23.1% and 11.7%; 95% CI, 7.6%-17.7%, respectively) than among those aged 65 years or older (3.3%; 95% CI, 2.5%-4.4% and 1.7%; 95% CI, 1.1%-2.6%). Compared with male veterans, female veterans also exhibited higher prevalences of past-year suicidal ideation (20.0%; 95% CI, 14.5%-26.9% vs 13.3%; 95% CI, 11.5%-15.3%), lifetime planning (17.1%; 95% CI, 11.7%-24.3% vs 6.8%; 95% CI, 5.5%-8.3%), and lifetime attempts (11.1%; 95% CI, 7.0%-17.3% vs 4.1%; 95% CI, 3.1%-5.5%).

Factors Associated With STBs

Table 3 displays the complete results of multivariable logistic regression analyses examining factors associated with STBs. Younger veterans aged 18 to 44 years had greater odds of past 2-week ideation (OR, 2.65; 95% CI, 1.86-3.78), past-year ideation (OR, 2.66; 95% CI, 1.87-3.78), and lifetime suicide planning (OR, 2.11; 95% CI, 1.36-3.28) relative to those aged 65 years or older. Veterans aged 45 to 64 years also had greater odds of suicide planning (OR, 1.92; 95% CI, 1.28-2.88). Female veterans had greater odds of lifetime suicide planning (OR, 1.65; 95% CI, 1.14-2.39) and suicide attempts (OR, 2.32; 95% CI, 1.41-3.83) relative to male veterans. Veterans who were divorced or separated had greater odds of suicide planning (OR, 1.59; 95% CI, 1.07-2.38) and suicide attempts (OR, 1.75; 95% CI, 1.07-2.88), while those who were never married had greater odds of suicide attempts (OR, 2.81; 95% CI, 1.63-4.84) relative to those who were married or partnered.

Lifetime major depressive disorder (MDD) was associated with greater odds of all STBs, including past 2-week ideation (OR, 2.22; 95% CI, 1.63-3.02), past-year ideation (OR, 2.33; 95% CI, 1.72-3.16), lifetime suicide planning (OR, 2.61; 95% CI, 1.84-3.71), and suicide attempts (OR, 3.76; 95% CI, 2.31-6.10). Lifetime PTSD was associated with greater odds of past 2-week ideation (OR, 1.53; 95% CI, 1.09-2.13), past-year ideation (OR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.17-2.25), and suicide planning (OR, 2.61; 95% CI, 1.82-3.76). Greater ACEs were associated with higher odds of all STBs, including past 2-week ideation (OR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.04-1.16), past-year ideation (OR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.04-1.16), lifetime suicide planning (OR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.02-1.16), and suicide attempts (OR, 1.39; 95% CI, 1.29-1.51).

Additional factors associated with greater odds of STBs included higher disability levels for past 2-week ideation (OR, 1.10; 95% CI, 1.04-1.16), past-year ideation (OR, 1.09; 95% CI, 1.07-1.11), and suicide attempts (OR, 1.04; 95% CI, 1.02-1.07), as well as a history of concussion or TBI for past 2-week ideation (OR, 1.65; 95% CI, 1.13-2.42), past-year ideation (OR, 1.48; 95% CI, 1.04-2.18), and suicide attempts (OR, 1.70; 95% CI, 1.07-2.70). Veterans with a lifetime history of AUD had greater odds of past 2-week ideation (OR, 1.62; 95% CI, 1.01-2.61), as did those with 2 or more deployments (OR, 1.45; 95% CI, 1.01-2.01). In contrast, veterans who served in the Navy had lower odds of suicide attempts (OR, 0.46; 95% CI, 0.25-0.88), and those in other service branches had lower odds of past 2-week (OR, 0.41; 95% CI, 0.22-0.76) and past-year ideation (OR, 0.42; 95% CI, 0.23-0.78) relative to Army veterans.

Results of relative importance analyses indicated that disability level was the largest contributor to explained variance in both past 2-week suicidal ideation (36.6%) and past-year suicidal ideation (35.6%), followed by lifetime MDD (18.7% and 18.8%, respectively), lifetime PTSD (15.0% and 15.0%), younger age (11.3% and 11.3%), total ACEs (8.5% and 8.6%), and concussion or TBI (5.3% and 8.2%). For suicide planning, the largest contributors to explained variance were lifetime PTSD (34.7%), lifetime MDD (26.8%), younger age (17.0%), and total ACEs (12.8%). For suicide attempts, total ACEs accounted for the largest share of explained variance (53.6%), followed by lifetime MDD (18.0%), disability level (10.1%), concussion or TBI (8.3%), female sex (4.2%), and marital status (3.9%).

Mental Health Treatment Utilization Among Veterans With STBs

Table 4 presents mental health treatment utilization among US military veterans with a history of STBs. Mental health treatment utilization differed significantly between veterans who did and did not use the VA as their primary source of health care across nearly all STB groups. Among VA primary users, the prevalences of lifetime mental health treatment were 75.2% for veterans with past 2-week suicidal ideation, 75.3% for those with past-year suicidal ideation, 81.2% for those with lifetime suicide planning, and 90.4% for those with a lifetime suicide attempt, compared with 46.2%, 46.2%, 58.2%, and 81.4%, respectively, among non–primary VA users. The prevalences of current mental health treatment among VA primary users were 53.6%, 53.8%, 55.1%, and 65.3% for past 2-week ideation, past-year ideation, lifetime planning, and lifetime attempts, respectively, compared with 25.6%, 25.8%, 29.0%, and 36.8% among non–primary VA users. Current pharmacotherapy prevalences among VA primary users were 45.8%, 45.9%, 46.5%, and 63.6% across the 4 STB outcomes, compared with 25.1%, 25.2%, 27.5%, and 36.8% among non–primary VA users. Current psychotherapy prevalences among VA primary users were 38.6%, 38.8%, 38.6%, and 50.8%, compared with 13.3%, 13.0%, 6.0%, and 23.1% among non–primary VA users.

Discussion

Using data from the 2025 to 2026 NHRVS, this study provides the most up-to-date nationally representative estimates of the prevalence of STBs in US military veterans and identifies key sociodemographic, military, and psychiatric factors associated with them. Compared with estimates from the 2019 to 2020 NHRVS,6 the prevalence of past 2-week suicidal ideation increased nearly 50%, whereas the prevalence of lifetime suicide planning and attempts remained stable.

Consistent with our prior 2019 to 2020 NHRVS findings,3,5,15 after adjusting for covariates, younger age was associated with STBs. Compared with veterans aged 65 years or older, veterans aged 18 to 44 years had 2.66 (95% CI, 1.87-3.78) odds of past-year suicidal ideation and 2.11 (95% CI, 1.36-3.28) odds of lifetime suicide planning. These findings parallel national epidemiologic data from the VA, which indicate that suicide mortality rates are highest among younger veterans, particularly those aged 18 to 34 years.16 Several factors may account for higher levels of STBs reported by younger veterans, including greater psychiatric burden and elevated trauma exposure.17 In fact, veterans aged 18 to 44 are disproportionately from post September 11 cohorts, and Iraq and/or Afghanistan War veterans report significantly greater cumulative trauma burden and combat severity relative to earlier war eras.18 However, interpretation of service-era differences is complicated because these patterns may reflect both broader generational trends and military-specific experiences associated with different deployment eras and theaters of war. Additionally, the lower prevalence of STBs observed among older veterans may partly reflect survivorship bias, whereby individuals at highest suicide risk are less likely to survive into older age. This interpretation is consistent with US Department of Veterans Affairs data indicating that suicide rates are highest among younger veterans,1 suggesting cumulative mortality may reduce the proportion of high-risk individuals in older cohorts.

Female veterans also demonstrated elevated odds of STBs; relative to male veterans, they had 1.65 (95% CI, 1.14-2.39) higher odds of suicide planning and 2.32 (95% CI, 1.41-3.83) higher odds of a lifetime suicide attempt, despite VA national data showing that male veterans die by suicide at higher rates1 These demographic differences between STBs and suicide mortality may reflect greater lethality of suicide means by male veterans, given that firearms were used in approximately three-quarters of suicides among male veterans compared with less than half among female veterans,16 and that prior research has found that nearly 90% of individuals who die by firearm suicide do so on their first attempt.19 These age- and sex-based differences are consistent with other population-based studies of US military personnel,20 highlight the heterogeneity in the distribution of STBs across demographic subgroups, and underscore the importance of suicide risk assessment and monitoring among younger and female veterans.

Consistent with prior research,5,6 functional disability, lifetime MDD, PTSD, and a greater number of ACEs were among the strongest factors associated with STBs. Specifically, functional disability accounted for the largest proportion of the explained variance in ideation, PTSD and MDD accounted for the largest proportion of explained variance in planning, and ACEs accounted for the largest proportion of explained variance in attempts. These findings align with prior work documenting robust associations between functional impairment, psychiatric disorders, and early life adversity with STBs.2,21,22 Although the relative importance of these factors differed across STB outcomes, findings suggest that suicidal ideation, planning, and attempts share many risk factors in the veteran population, reinforcing the broad relevance of these domains across multiple forms of STBs.

Veterans with recent suicidal ideation who identified the VA as their primary source of health care were more than twice as likely to report current engagement in mental health treatment compared with veterans who primarily received care outside the VA system (53.8% vs 25.8%). This finding aligns with prior population-based data,23 and may reflect improved access to mental health services among primary VA users, given that limited access to mental health care is associated with increased suicide risk.24 Nevertheless, findings suggest that a substantial proportion of veterans reporting recent suicidal ideation are not currently engaged in mental health treatment. Collectively, these findings suggest that continued outreach and engagement remain priorities to increase coverage of care among veterans at increased suicide risk who receive care both within and outside the VA health care system.6

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting results of this study. First, the cross-sectional design precludes temporal or causal inference, and residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be excluded. Second, all measures were derived from self-report and may therefore be subject to bias or underreporting, particularly for sensitive outcomes such as STBs.25 Third, although each NHRVS wave was weighted to contemporaneous US veteran population benchmarks which could indicate methodological artifacts, the use of identical probability-based sampling, consistent weighting variables, and the same raking procedures across waves supports the validity of between-wave comparisons and suggests that the observed differences likely reflect temporal changes. Fourth, the repeated cross-sectional design and use of wave-specific poststratification weights precluded formal evaluation of age-period-cohort effects and direct comparison with nonveteran population trends. Moreover, the use of different temporal assessment windows across STB measures (eg, past 2-week ideation vs lifetime attempts) may complicate direct comparisons across outcomes and introduce differential recall bias. Additionally, although the NHRVS recruited a nationally representative sample of US military veterans, findings may not generalize to institutionalized veterans or other subgroups that were not captured by the survey sampling frame.

Conclusions

In this cross-sectional study of US military veterans from 2025 to 2026, we found a higher prevalence of past 2-week suicidal ideation among US military veterans relative to prior nationally representative estimates, as well as largely stable prevalence estimates of lifetime suicide planning and attempts. Using population benchmarks based on the most recent (August 2024) Veteran Supplement of the US Census Bureau Current Population Survey, these estimates corresponded to approximately 2.3 to 2.4 million veterans experiencing past 2-week or past- year suicidal ideation, 1.4 million reporting lifetime suicide planning, and 830 000 reporting lifetime suicide attempts. STBs were disproportionately concentrated among younger and female veterans, underscoring the importance of targeted suicide prevention efforts in these subpopulations. We also found that early-life adversities, psychiatric conditions, and functional 11 impairment were key factors associated with STBs in this population. Collectively, these findings underscore the importance of ongoing population-based surveillance to monitor changes in STBs, particularly given the observed increase in suicidal ideation, and the ongoing need to characterize how suicide risk and mental health service utilization are distributed across the US veteran population.
