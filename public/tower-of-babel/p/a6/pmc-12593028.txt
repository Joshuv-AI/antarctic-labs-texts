Characterization and Phenolic Profiling of Anticandidal Polycladia myrica and Its Mediated Iron Nanoparticles with the Evaluation of Their Antioxidant, Anti-Alzheimer, Catalytic Degradation, and Anticancer Activity via the P53 Pathway
Authors: Abeer I. M. EL-Sayed, Fatma Alzahraa Mokhtar, Mohammad Y. Alfaifi, Hanan Khalaf Anazi, Mofida E. M. Makhlof
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
As a potent reducing and capping agent, Polycladia myrica extract was used to create iron nanoparticles (FeNPs). UV–visible, Fourier transform infrared (FTIR), X-ray diffractive analysis (XRD), scanning electron microscopy (SEM), energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX), zeta potential, hydrodynamic analysis with the determination of polydispersity index (PDI), and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) were used to characterize the produced iron nanoparticles. By examining the peak at 320 nm, UV–visible spectroscopy confirmed the production of PFeNPs. Additionally, various in vitro biological assays were conducted, demonstrating significant therapeutic potential. The cytotoxic assay was performed using lung carcinoma cells (A-549) and normal human lung fibroblast (MRC-5) cell lines, which demonstrated promising safe results with IC50 = 225.91 ± 8.93 μg/mL and CC50 = 488.80 ± 17.23 μg/mL, respectively, due to the forced apoptosis caused by the accumulation of both ROS and p53 levels inside cancer cells. PFeNPs demonstrated weak α-amylase inhibition with a percent of 33.96 ± 3.12 and potent acetylcholinesterase inhibition with a percent of 71.42 ± 3.64. The anticandidal activity of FeNPs biofabricated from P. myrica against C. albicans was estimated. The inhibition zone diameters of PFeNPs and Nystatin reference drug (mean ± SD) were about 18.82 ± 0.87 and 19.74 ± 0.98, respectively. MIC was determined to be about 500 and 312.5 μg/mL for both our algal FeNPs and the standard used drug, respectively. These results were confirmed by scanning electron microscopy, which revealed lysis and bursting of the exterior cell surface, along with deformation and death of Candida albicans cells in treated Candida isolate. These results strongly suggested the possibility of introducing PFeNPs as a cotherapy for Candida infections in diabetic cancer patients.

Introduction

Nanosized particles (1–100 nm size), known as nanoparticles, link atomic or molecular structures and bulk materials. Nanoparticles exhibit distinct properties compared to those of their bulk counterparts. These characteristics render nanoparticles useful in a variety of scientific and technological domains. Due to their biological activities, as well as their diversity in chemical structure and behavior, iron nanoparticles, or FeNPs, have been the focus of attention over time for the treatment of many diseases. These particles come in many forms, such as iron hydroxides, iron oxides, zerovalent iron, and iron oxide-hydroxides. The iron oxide nanomaterials have peroxidase-like activities that augment reactive oxygen species scavenging activities and facilitate incorporation of other treatments. Other metal nanoparticles exhibit peroxidase-like activities as titanium oxide and gold nanoparticles that have a role in tumor management and Alzheimer’s disease management. ,

The traditional techniques for synthesizing such particles are uneconomical because they cannot regulate the size of particles in the nanoscale range and necessitate high temperatures and pressures. In contrast, chemical techniques are hazardous to the environment and also have limitations. Therefore, the development of sustainable methods for synthesizing biocompatible FeNPs is imperative. In an effort to address these issues, scientists are shifting toward “green” nanoparticle synthesis, which often occurs at room temperature and utilizes natural and renewable resources rather than chemical reagents and organic solvents. Instead of using chemical reagents for the biosynthesis of such particles with various morphologies and chemical formulas, bioactive substances from biological sources were employed in the green synthesis approaches. Since then, a variety of organisms have been involved in the green synthesis of FeNPs; nevertheless, the potential contribution of algae has received less attention, These organisms are frequently employed in the production of AgNPs, or silver nanoparticles. Although few papers exist regarding the production of nanoparticles based on iron, they are also abundant in bioactive substances that fight a variety of illnesses. These substances’ accessibility, security, and effectiveness make them useful for the environmentally friendly synthesis of FeNPs. The most common type of seaweed used to synthesize FeNPs is brown algae. To date, the biosynthesis of FeNPs was carried out using Padina pavonica, Dictyota dicotoma, Sargassum muticum, and Sargassun acinarium. It has been observed that the diameter of FeNPs biosynthesized by algae can range from 10 to 50 nm. Variations in the amounts of bioactive compounds from various algae species may be the cause of this discrepancy. Here, P. myrica-mediated ferric nanoparticles (PFeNPs), which were biosynthesized for the first time, were phycosynthesized and characterized by using various parameters. They were then tested as an antidiabetic agent because, according to the International Diabetes Federation (IDF), diabetes has been rising rapidly over the past few years, mostly in low- to middle-income countries, and recently is one of the main causes of premature death. As an agent to prevent Alzheimer’s, one of the leading causes of death, Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is typified by a gradual loss of particular populations of neurons as a result of a buildup of aggregated proteins inside neurons. AD is a degenerative brain illness marked by a reduction in understanding, memory, and cognitive function. Alzheimer’s disease (AD) causes a variety of structural and functional damage to particular brain regions, which reduces neuronal connections in those regions. Additionally, using methylene blue dye, the dye’s catalytic degradation ability of these nanoparticles was ascertained. This is because water pollution in the ecosystem has resulted from the inappropriate discharge of pharmaceutical pollutants and dyes produced by several industries. These effluents contain toxins that seep into water bodies, degrading the quality and stability of the global ecosystem and rendering potable water unsafe for human use. Herein, the activity of PFeNPs against Candida albicans ATCC10231 isolate was evaluated as the prevalence of infections caused by fungi has shot up over the past 40 years. Particularly in the substantial number of patients with immune-related issues and/or receiving treatment for severe underlying illnesses, Candida spp. contribute to over 70% of these types of infections. , The most prevalent fungal pathogens, Candida, are capable of causing a wide range of illnesses, from superficial to systemic candidiasis. There are over 17 distinct species of Candida known to be the etiological agents of human infection, which comprises a diverse collection of microorganisms. Nonetheless, of the various species of Candida, Candida albicans, Candida glabrata, and Candida tropicalis are responsible for over 90% of invasive infections. Over 60% of compromised hematologic patients will experience recurrence or chronic candidiasis as a result of long-term antibiotic therapy. A significant issue associated with fungal infections is the coexistence of bacteria, which exacerbates the inflammatory response and complicates the treatment. Compared to monomicrobial candidemia, concurrent bacteremia is associated with a worse prognosis even with antimicrobial therapy. As a result, the treatment of yeast infections often requires the use of combination therapy, which involves the use of additional medications from the antibiotic and steroid families. Unfavorable side effects frequently mount after this therapy. There are many different kinds of antibacterial medications in the market today, but there are not many that are effective against pathogenic fungi. According to recent reports, diseases caused by Candida are resistant to the antifungal drugs found recently in the market, especially azoles (where resistance has increased the most). Thus, one of the major challenges facing modern medicine is to find a novel drug to be more efficient. It is anticipated that the rapidly advancing field of nanotechnology will significantly alter medicine. Numerous studies have been conducted on specific classes of nanomaterials initially proposed for drug delivery. Different types of metallic nanoparticles that naturally possess antibacterial activities were tested. Studies on the effectiveness of algal iron nanoparticles against fungi, such as Candida, are, however, scarce.

Also the purpose of the subsequent work was to test the anticancer efficacy of PFeNPs, as p53 is the gene that is most frequently altered in human cancer, with the assessment of p53 levels. The most common changes found in human cancers so far are mutations and allele loss in the 17p gene, which produces the p53 protein. The p53 gene is regarded as a stress response gene because it causes cell cycle arrest or apoptosis in response to DNA damage, preserving the organism’s genetic stability. The p53 pathway is a convoluted and poorly understood chain of events that performs these functions. After being initially identified as an oncogene, p53 was found to be able to inhibit the growth of cells that undergo transformation. Research has additionally shown that the preservation of the nontumorigenic phenotype of cells depends on intact p53 activity. As a result, p53 is essential in preventing cancer growth because it is a tumor suppressor gene that triggers apoptosis. The anticancer activity of PFeNPs was evaluated in relation to the reactive oxygen species.

Materials and Methods

P. myrica Sampling and PFeNPs Preparation

According to Touliabah, P. myrica was collected, identified, washed, dried, and turned into powder, which was then used to prepare P. myrica aqueous extract (PAE) to be used in ferrous ammonium sulfate hexahydrate (FeH20N2O14S2) reduction into PFeNPs, which were prepared according to Bensy, by adding 25 mL of PAE to 25 mL of 0.1 M FeH20N2O14S2; then, the experiment was maintained for 120 h at 30 ± 2 °C. P. myrica extract functions as a reduction, stabilization, and decorative agent in the manufacture of iron nanoparticles. The resulting suspensions were centrifuged once more, cleaned three times with ethanol, and dried at 40 °C to produce nanoparticles.

Liquid Chromatography–Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS/MS)

The extract of P. myrica was examined using (LC-MS/MS). HSS T3 columns from Waters Corporation in Milford, MA, were used for chromatographic separation. A 0.5-m-long, 3.0-mm-diameter Phenomenex precolumn from Torrance, California was used. A column temperature of 40 °C was maintained. Sodium hydroxide adjusted the mobile phase (A) pH to 8. The mobile phase consisted of 1% methanol and 5 mM ammonium formate. We employed binary gradient elution. IDA was utilized in the experiment. MS and MS/MS data batches were prepared using Analyst-TF version 1.7.1. IDA simplified full-scan MS and MS/MS data gathering. Survey spectra were detailed and covered the mass-to-charge (m/z) range from 50 to 1000.

Characterization of PFeNPs

The greenly synthesized nanoparticles were first identified by measuring the absorbance using a UV–visible spectrum (Thermo Scientific Evolution TM 300) between 200 and 800 nm. After that, the sample was prepared for FTIR analysis, which was performed at a resolution of 4000–400 cm–1 to identify the active groups responsible for the reduction. After coating on a copper grid for five min under a mercury lamp, the nanoparticles’ shape and elemental composition were determined by SEM and EDX, respectively, with the same instrument (JEOL JSM 6490 LV, Japan). The crystallinity of PFeNPs was measured by using XRD spectra (XRD-6000, Shimadzu, Japan) with a LINXEYE detector. The diffractometer was operated at 30 kV and 10 mA current with 2.2 KW of Cu anode radiation (k = 1.54184 Å). Scherrer’s equation was used to calculate the average size of the nanoparticles D=Kλ/(β⁡cos⁡θ) where k is a constant = 0.9, λ is the wavelength of the X-ray (1.54060), and β is the width in radians of the peak due to the size effect, which is calculated by the following equation β=θ1−θ2×π/180

where θ is Bragg’s diffraction angle. The size and morphology of the PFeNPs were demonstrated using TEM (JEOL, JEM-2100, Japan) operating at 200 kV. The PFeNPs were diluted with deionized water and sonicated (Branson-Sonifier 250) for 10 min, then the PFeNPs were placed on carbon-coated copper grids to be dried for half an hour. The potential charges, PDI based on the particle dispersion, and hydrodynamic diameter of the PFeNPs were determined by sonicating the NP suspension (500 μg/mL) for 15 min, diluting it 10-fold, sonicating for 1–2 min, and transferring it to U-type tubes at 25 °C using the zeta sizer in the range of −200 to +200 mV (Zeta Plus, Brookhaven).

Antidiabetic Activity of PFeNPs

α-Amylase Enzyme (α-AE) Inhibition Test

Digestive enzymes called α-amylases (EC 3.2.1.1) are responsible for breaking down internal α-1,4-glycosidic bonds within starch to produce glucose, maltose, and maltotriose units. The pancreas and salivary glands are the primary sites of expression of human α-amylase. In humans, this enzyme is responsible for breaking down complex carbohydrates. Therefore, the inhibition of α-AE may be a viable approach to treating conditions such as diabetes, obesity, periodontal diseases, dental cavities, and problems associated with carbohydrate intake.

α-Amylases Kit Content

With a 96-well microplate (96-WMP), BioVision’s α-Amylase Inhibitor Screening Kit (BAISK) (www.biovision.com | tech@biovision.com) (Table ) was used to determine α-AE inhibition percent. The synthetic substrate in our kit is hydrolyzed by human α-AE, resulting in smaller fragments that carry the chromophore (pNP; OD = 405 nm). Additionally, the package includes a potent targeted inhibitor.

The PFeNPs were dissolved 100 times in water and then diluted to 3 times with α-AE assay buffer (α-AAB) and thoroughly mixed. Next, 50 μL of the diluted PFeNPs (3×) were added to 96-WMP. To prepare the inhibitor control [IC], 10 μL of reconstituted α-amylase inhibitor was added to 40 μL of α-AAB in the wells. The enzyme control [EC], background control [BC], and solvent control [SC] were created by adding 50 μL of α-AAB, 100 μL of α-AAB, and 50 μL of 3× solvent in α-AAB to the wells, respectively. To dilute the α-amylase stock, we added 490 μL of α-AAB to 10 μL of α-AE. Pipette up and down to mix thoroughly, then 50 μL of the diluted α-AE was added to PFeNPs, IC, EC, and SC wells, mixed, and incubated at room temperature for ten minutes. To ensure that it is protected from light, 490 μL of α-AAB was added to 10 μL of α-As to create the α-amylase substrate (α-AS). Next, 50 μL of diluted α-AS was added to the PFeNPs, IC, EC, BC, and SC wells, ensuring that no bubbles were introduced into the wells. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 405 nm, and the relative inhibition and activity % were calculated.

Anti-Alzheimer Activity

Inhibitory Activity of Acetylcholinesterase (AChE)

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurological illness that progresses irreversibly and causes memory loss, behavioral issues, personality changes, and a reduction in cognitive function. Accordingly, neuropathological research showed that AD was linked to acetylcholine (ACh) deficit in the brain. One of the most practical methods for treating AD symptoms could be the suppression of AChE, that is responsible for Ach breakdown. A modified 96-WMP assay, based on Ellman’s technique, was used to detect AChE activity, as modified by Rhee et al. The basis of this incredibly sensitive technique is the measurement of thiocholine synthesis during the hydrolysis of acetylthiocholine. Serial dilutions of PFeNPs (0–1000 μg/mL) were prepared using methanol, and then, 25 μL of ACh iodide was added to each well of a 96-well microplate. For 30 s, the absorbance was measured at 405 nm. The absorbance was measured four times every 5 min during incubation using a Microplate TS-800 reader (BioTek, Winooski) following the addition of 25 μL of AChE (0.22 U/mL). The percentage of AChE inhibition was determined by comparing the samples with the negative control; donepezil was utilized as a reference drug at the same concentrations.

Antimicrobial Activity

Anticandidal Activity Evaluation

The impact of an algal biofabricated iron nanoparticle was investigated using the well diffusion technique, with Nystatin oral suspension (100,000 IU/mL equivalent to 2.5 mg/mL) serving as a benchmark for antifungal activity, and the test was conducted in triplicate. Sabouraud–Dox Agar (SDA) was used to maintain the pathogenic Candida albicans ATCC10231, which was tested for antifungal activity. Using a sterile glass spreader, 100 μL of the C. albicans culture broth (CFU 3 × 103 cells/mL) was carefully distributed in different sterilized plates. A sterilized cork borer was used to make two wells, each measuring 6 mm in diameter. Incubation occurred for 5 days at 28 °C. One well was loaded with 100 μL of oral Nystatin suspension (reference drug), and the other with 100 μL of Fe nanoparticles alone with a concentration of 2.5 mg/mL. After that, inhibition zones were checked on them. ,

Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC)

The MIC of PFeNPs with Nystatin was examined using a microdilution assay according to Ericsson and Sherris. 100 μL of algal FeNPs with concentrations (1000–0.5 μg/mL) were used. Sabouraud dextrose broth (SDB) tubes inoculated with 100 μL of C. albicans ATCC10231, fungal spore suspension (3 × 103 CFU/mL), and a control tube were applied without treatment with PFeNPs. Fungal tubes were incubated for 5 days at 28 °C. MIC readings were determined as the minimum concentration of FeNPs that inhibited Candida growth. ,

SEM Micrograph

SEM was performed to prove the anticandidal effect exerted by P. myrica FeNPs. Small pieces of fresh Candida albicans ATCC10231 untreated and treated by our algal fabricated FeNPs were mixed with 4F1G (fixative, phosphate buffer solution) pH = 7.4 at 4 °C for 3 h for fixation. Specimens were then placed in 2% OsO4 in the buffer at 4 °C for 2 h, then all samples were washed using ethanol, then coated with gold up to a thickness of 400 Å in a sputter coating unit (JFC-1100 E).

Photocatalytic Degradation (PD) of the MB Dye by PFeNPs

PFeNPs and a 150 W UV lamp were used in the investigation of PD of methylene blue (MB) dye, adhering to a previously published protocol by many authors. , In these degradation tests, 100 mL of the 5 ppm MB solution was mixed with 25 mg of the PFeNPs photocatalyst in a 250 mL beaker, then let to sit at pH 12 for 30 min. To attain equilibrium, an adsorption–desorption experiment was carried out in complete darkness for almost half an hour before the light shone on the solution. After exposure to UV light for 30 min, 5 mL of the solution was centrifuged and taken to be analyzed at 650 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer. Degradation% was calculated as follows %degradation=(A0−At)A0×100 where “A 0” and “At ” were the initial and final absorption of the MB dye solution before and after the experiment, respectively.

Anticancer Activity against A-549 Cells (Lung Carcinoma Cell Line)

The American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Rockville, MD) provided the A-549 cells, which were grown on RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with 10% inactivated fetal calf serum and 50 μg/mL gentamycin (reference drug). A-549 cells were used with a concentration of 5 × 104 cell/well using 96-well tissue culture plates (96-WTCP), and then incubation occurred for 24 hours. After that, PFeNPs were introduced to 96-WTCP (three repetitions) in order to obtain eight concentrations of each chemical. Each 96-WTCP was subjected to six vehicle controls, each containing media or 0.5% DMSO. Following a 24 h incubation period, the MTT test was used to ascertain the number of viable cells. In short, the media was taken out of the 96-WTCP, then another fresh one (100 μL) was placed except for phenol red. Next, incubation occurred for 4 h at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Then, 50 μL of DMSO was added to each well with mixing, followed by another ten minutes of incubation. Optical density (OD) at 590 nm was determined using a microplate reader (SunRise, TECAN, Inc.). The percentage of viability was then computed as [(ODt/ODc)] × 100%, where ODt and ODc are the mean OD of PFeNPs treated and untreated ones, respectively. Evaluation of Cytotoxicity of PFeNPs Using the MRC-5 Cell Line

The normal human lung fibroblast cell lines, MRC-5, were supplied from ATCC, Rockville, MD, which were used in the cytotoxicity test. The MRC-5 were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), using 50 μg/mL of gentamycin as a reference drug. Subculturing occurred twice a week at 37 °C in a moist field with 5% CO2. To conduct a cytotoxicity experiment, 100 μL of growth media was used to seed 1 × 104 cells per well in 96-well plates. After 24 h of seeding, the cells were distributed into 96-well microplates (Falcon, NJ) using PFeNPs at concentrations ranging from 0 to 500 μg/mL, and then incubation was repeated for an additional day. DMSO and PFeNPs were not used in the culture of control cells and the MTT assay. , The 50% inhibitory concentration (IC50), the concentration required to cause toxic effects in 50% of intact cells, was estimated from graphical plots of the dose–response curve for each concentration using Graphpad Prism software (San Diego, CA.).

Microscopic Observation of MRC-5 and A-549 Cells

Following the completion of the cytotoxicity test, 96-WMP was turned over to extract the medium using phosphate-buffered saline (pH 7.2) for washing. Following this, mixing with 10% formalin for 15 min occurred and stained with 0.25% crystal violet for 20 min. After the stain was removed, rinsing occurs with deionized water, and then it is allowed to dry. An inverted microscope (CKX41; Olympus, Japan) fitted with a digital microscopy camera was used for the observation at 200× magnification.

Determination of Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS)

ROS, an indicator of oxidative stress, was measured in cancer cells using a 1.5 h solid-phase ELISA kit. This allowed researchers to gain insight into potential PFeNP toxicity pathways. Once the required number of coated wells has been secured in the holder, the corresponding well in the antibody-precoated Microtiter Plate was filled with 100 μL of Standards or PFeNPs, while for the blank control well, 100 μL of PBS (pH 7.0–7.2) was added. Next, 10 μL of balance solution was dispensed into 100 μL specimens and mixed thoroughly. After that, 50 μL of conjugate was added to each well (except the blank control well) and mixed thoroughly. Then, it was incubated for 60 min at 37 °C. Next, the plate was washed and let dry. Finally, 50 uL each of substrate A and 50 uL of substrate B were added to every well, including the blank control well. It was sealed and let stand for 10–15 min (avoiding sunlight). Stop solution was added to every well, even the control well that was left blank, and the test was done in triplicate. Finally, after thorough mixing, the O.D. was measured at 450 nm, and the standard curve, created between the O.D. and PFeNPs concentrations, was used to calculate the number of samples. Graph paper or statistical software can be used to draw the best fit curve, which creates a logit-log linear regression curve. ,

Estimation of the P53 Level

The human p53 ELISA Kit is an enzyme-linked immunosorbent test for the quantitative detection of human p53. The microwell strips were first thoroughly aspirated between washes, allowing about 10–15 s for each wash. After that, the excess wash buffer was wiped off with an absorbent towel. Next, 100 μL of PFeNPs was applied to each standard well, and 100 μL of the prepared standard was inserted into each well in duplicate. Then, we proceed with this process. Five times, two rows of standard human p53 dilutions, ranging from 50.00 to 0.78 U/mL, were created. 100 μL portions of the contents of the final microwells (G1 and G2) that were utilized were poured out. Then, both the blank and sample wells were filled with 100 μL of PFeNPs in duplicate. Next, the biotin-conjugate was prepared and each well filled with 50 μL of it, and incubation occurred at room temperature (18–25 °C) for two hours. After that, the adhesive film was removed, the wells were empty, washed three times, and each well was filled with 100 μL of diluted Streptavidin-HRP. After an hour of incubation, the wells were drained, covered, and then washed. After that, 100 μL of TMB substrate solution was added with the incubation for 10 min, avoiding direct light exposure. By the development of a deep blue color, the substrate reaction should be stopped using stop solution, and a test was done in triplicate. The ELISA reader can be used to monitor color development on the plate at 620 nm.

Results and Discussion

P. myrica LC-MS-MS

The analysis technique using LC-MS-MS in the negative ion mode identified 9 secondary metabolites in P. myrica extract. The identified metabolites belong to different classes. Organic and phenolic acid derivatives were the major metabolites represented in the form of dicarboxylic acid (succinic acid), β-hydroxy acid (d-(+)-malic acid), and cyclohexanecarboxylic acid (Shikimic acid). The second class of the identified compounds is the flavonoid derivatives in the form of glycosides and aglycones. The identified glycosides are kaempferol-3-O-(6-p-coumaroyl)-glucoside, delphinidin-3-O-(6″-O-α-rhamnopyranosyl-β-glucopyranoside), and isorhamnetin-3-O-glucoside. The identified aglycone was formononetin. Other identified compounds were a coumarin derivative (esculin) and indole-3-acetic acid derivatives (β-indoleacetic acid) (Table and Figure ).

PFeNPs Characterization

The seaweed’s aqueous extract contains an iron precursor, which caused the mixture’s color to abruptly change and result in a dark brown suspension. Iron NPs were present in the reaction mixture, as seen by the characteristic peak at 320 nm (Figure ), which is consistent with Bensy et al. They found iron nanoparticles between 275 and 325 nm that were mediated by Ulva. Figure shows the FTIR of PFeNPs, which indicates the existence of aliphatic nitro compounds, alcohol groups, methylene groups, aromatic C–H, venyl, carbonyl, and phenyl in the green synthetic iron NPs. There were signals at 3229.25 cm–1 (OH stretching). Since the iron NPs contained methylene and carbonyl groups, they showed strong, pointed peaks at approximately 3373 and 1625 cm–1. The FTIR data showed that the P. myrica extract component capped the PFeNPs. SEM results (Figure ) showed that the nanoparticle’s size was revealed to be between 26.23 and 32.19 nm by SEM pictures with nanospheres, and this agrees with AL-Kalifawi and Bensy as due to the magnetic attraction between the iron nanoparticles that appear in clusters. Using EDX, Fe atoms were found in the elemental composition analysis (mass%1.93 ± 0.13; atom% 0.55 ± 0.04; Figure ). In addition to other EDX peaks, including C, N, Al, Si, Mg, K, S, Ca, and O peaks, this investigation also indicated the presence of iron-containing nanostructures, suggesting that they were combined with a component of the P. myrica extract. To evaluate the phase and composition of the produced nanoparticles, we applied the XRD method. According to Figure , the characteristic peaks of nanoiron can be seen at 2θ = 13.241, 20.331, 35.079, 53.406, and 62.055°. The resulting nanoparticles appear to be crystalline in form, with a size of approximately 23.69 nm, as determined by the Scherrer equation, as indicated by the expanded diffraction peak. The TEM micrographs of PFeNPs are shown in Figure . The studied NPs had homogeneous sizes, and it was found that the particles were nearly spherical in shape. While some irregularly shaped particles were also noticed, the majority of the particles had regular shapes. Because of the agglomeration process connected to the van der Waals forces and magnetic attraction among FeNPs, these irregular shapes were examined. Additionally, the zeta potential of the iron nanoparticles was studied (Figure ). The findings indicated that the produced iron nanoparticles had a negative value of 123.6 ± 4.95 and a polydispersity index (PDI) value of 0.207, which were relatively stable, as a PDI value of less than 0.5 is regarded as appropriate for biological applications because it indicates how homogeneous the nanoparticles are. The lower the PDI, the more homogeneous the nanoparticles are. It is a marker of particle aggregation because a higher value indicates a polydisperse system. As shown in Figure B, the size distribution for the generated particles was centered on 169.2 nm, indicating the presence of PFeNPs that had a hydrodynamic diameter of 12.4 nm, with PDI (0.207), suggesting that PFeNPs had a polyisoprene standard.

Antidiabetic Activity of PFeNPs

Figure illustrates how PFeNPs can inhibit α-amylase enzyme. Under screening conditions, the tested concentrations acted as amylase inhibitors but had weak antidiabetic activity. The highest PFeNPs concentration (1000 μg/mL) caused an inhibition percent of 33.96 ± 3.12, which is very weak when compared to the inhibitory percent of Acarbose (a standard drug), which recorded 98.02 ± 0.24 at the same concentration with an IC50 = 12.31 ± 0.67 μg/mL. Bano also detected moderate inhibition for amylase enzyme using iron nanoparticles using Sesamum indicum Seeds Extract.

Anti-Alzheimer’s Activity of PFeNPs

One of the best treatments for Alzheimer’s disease (AD) now in the market is cholinesterase inhibitors, which can be administered at any stage of the illness. As illustrated in Figure , the present study examined the inhibitory response of acetylcholinesterase enzymes at dosages from 0 to 1000 μg/mL of PFeNPs. It was discovered that the enzyme’s inhibitory effects were dose-dependent. PFeNPs had remarkable activity at 1000 μg/mL, as they inhibited AChE by 71.42 ± 3.64%. Consequently, PFeNPs may find application in the targeted delivery of future Alzheimer’s therapies. Nonetheless, further investigation is required to enhance biocompatibility and examine the toxicity of PFeNPs in vivo. Furthermore, the anti-Alzheimer’s effect of FeNPs produced by P. myrica is not supported by any evidence found in the literature.

MB Catalytic Degradation

The experiment at pH 12 (basic conditions) is responsible for the promising removal percent, as Figure A,B demonstrated that MB dye was degraded using PFeNPs with a percent of 83.33%, as MB dye is a thiazine dye, with a cationic nature so it dissociated into negatively charged ions, especially at pH 12. As a result, there are more OH_ ions on the material’s surface. Increasing the amount of OH_ radicals as surface active sites, the percentage of MB deterioration increases and becomes complete. Furthermore, because PFeNPs are magnetic, they would have experienced electric-dipole moments, which facilitate the easy separation of positively and negatively charged ions, enabling the surface material to adsorb and degrade the dye. These findings demonstrated that the MB dye was completely and more easily broken down within the reaction time. Additionally, in basic conditions, several studies reported a deterioration of above 95% for MB. In a related work, Alshehri et al. found that when exposed to UV light, Fe nanoparticles quickly broke down more than 55% of Congo red. After 30 min of contact in the PFeNP-containing solution, the MB spectral band shifted from 650 to 600 nm. This shift in the absorption band’s location could be caused by the MB molecules protonating to generate an azonium ion on the molecular structure.

The inhibitory activity of PFeNPs against lung cancer cells in comparison to their cytotoxicity against MRC-5 was examined and is depicted in Figure A,B. At this dose, the cell survival rate was almost half that of the untreated cells. Additional findings on the dose-dependent cytotoxicity of PFeNPs for a 24 h incubation period verified that an increased dose over a specified duration can enhance the cytotoxic effect for both cell types. PFeNPs demonstrated a highly encouraging inhibitory percentage against A-549 (72.51% ± 1.57) at 500 μg/mL. The results showed that incubating A549 cells with iron nanoparticles for 24 h causes them to undergo apoptosis rather than necrosis, a type of cell death. It was discovered that the iron nanoparticle treatments caused morphological traits such as fragmentation, chromatin condensation, and cell shrinkage. The phenolic profiling of the P. myrica extract was performed using an LC-MS-MS analysis technique. This analysis identified phenolic acids and flavonoids as major compounds. P. myrica was rich in succinic acid, malic acid, and shikimic acid in addition to some flavonoids, such as kaempferol derivatives. The extract was able to act as both a reducing agent and a capping agent for iron nanoparticles formed through a green technology. The characteristics of PFeNPs were evaluated. The formed PFeNPs showed a spherical shape by using SEM and TEM analyses. The FTIR figure revealed relative peaks corresponding to the iron NPs, which showed promising and strong catalytic degradation, anti-Alzheimer activity, and anticancer activity against A-549 cells, via the accumulation of both p53 and ROS within the cancer cells, with high safety levels in normal cells. In contrast, such nanoparticles exhibit weak antidiabetic activity, but they can effectively treat Candida infections. PFeNPs also showed potential against Candida albicans ATCC10231 yeast pathogens.

Conclusion

The present investigation revealed that PFeNPs are low-cost capping and stabilizing nanoparticles maintaining promising biological properties; therefore, our PFeNPs could be introduced as a complementary therapy for cancer via selective cell death, as we clarify within the study, by accumulating ROS inside cancer cells and via trigger production of P53 and could be used as a promising anticandidal drug. And we can benefit from it to treat fungal infections in diabetic cancer patients but not for other diseases, as their effect was weak.
