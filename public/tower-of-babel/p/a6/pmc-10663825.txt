Achieving greater participation in agricultural production: A study of government workers in the Upper west region of Ghana
Authors: Peter Dery Bolang, Francis Nangbeviel Sanyare, George Ngmenter Gyader
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper assessed factors influencing participation in agricultural production by government workers in the Upper West Regions of Ghana. A cross-sectional survey design was employed, using purposive and simple random sampling techniques to select 400 respondents across four (4) municipalities in the Upper West Region (UWR) for interview. Data were analyzed using the Heckman two-stage model to determine the extent to which factors critical to the participation in agricultural production by government workers. Results show that the majority (85.2 %) of government workers participate in one form or the other in agricultural production. They are specifically involved in crop production that is; food, and cash crops production as well as animal production. The need to supplement family food needs and to earn extra income was seen to condition their choice to participate in agriculture. The first and second stage predicting factors are income, access to extension, knowledge in agriculture and government policies; these were found to be statistically significant at 1 %. Time availability was significant at 5 %, however, sex, age, household size, credit availability, access to credit, passion for agriculture, scarcity of food, market access and cost of food were all found to be statistically significant at 10 %. We recommend that MoFA and its development partners in agriculture, effectively use local media and community visits to create more awareness among government workers on agricultural programmes and policies, since extension and knowledge in agriculture influence participation in agriculture production as revealed by the study in the UWR.Keywords: Participation, Agriculture production, Government, Workers, Ghana

Introduction

There is a greater mobilization of global efforts in recent times by governments and development partners to meet the 2030 targets of the Agenda for Sustainable Development (ASD). In this regard, to operationalize these global efforts, dynamism and inclusiveness are paramount especially in the agriculture sector of the world economy [1]. The agriculture sector is key in propelling developing economies and regions such as Africa to realize both target one and two of the ASD targets that is: Reducing poverty and Zero hunger. The agriculture sector is said to have contributed 23 % to Africa's Gross Domestic Product (GDP) in 2019 [2], so concerted efforts must be made to sustain and increase the growth in agriculture. This is because 60 % of the world's arable land lies in the savannah of Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), and it is believed that if cultivated, it could feed the world's growing population. Coincidentally, the world recorded its 8 billionth baby in Ghana-Africa on November 15th, 2022; it therefore calls for greater participation in agricultural production especially those in SSA to feed the growing population (UN, 2020; Oxford Business Group [OBG], 2022). Leading advocates have called for massive changes in how people participate in food crops production, processing and consumption in challenging times of climate change and COVID 19, in order not to disrupt the world food supply chain [3,4]. It is argued that, changes in the agriculture sector could engender greater participation in agriculture production which may culminate in increased productivity and sustainable food production (OBG, 2022). This is crucial if the projected 50 % of world food production is to be met by 2050 from the 2012 level. In this regard, there is a clarion call for locally produced crops and food stuffs limited in carbon footprint but high nutritional value for the populace [[4], [5], [6]].

In Ghana, though agriculture is the third largest contributor to its GDP and the largest employer compared to other sectors, it is regarded as an informal activity and the participation in it is left in the hands of uneducated smallholder farmers (Ghana Statistical Service [7]; 2017). They constitute the major producers of Ghana's agricultural produce with farm sizes of less than two hectors per head, coupled with low adoption of technology due to inadequate credit. Agricultural technology comes with associated costs but due to the informal nature of Ghana's agriculture, financial institutions regard the smallholder farmers as high credit defaulters. Majority do not have proper documentation of their properties that can serve as collateral, and so banks and other financial institution rather convince and lend their credit to government workers. Over the years, in order to change the narrative and galvanize increased and effective participation, various specific agricultural policies and programmes have been designed by government and its development partners. These include: Women's Participation in Agriculture (WPA), Youth Participation in Agriculture (YPA), Physically Challenged Participation in Agriculture (PCPA), Planting for Food Jobs (PFJ), and Rearing for food and Export (RFE) [8]. The aim is to target identifiable cohorts which allow for policy and programme adjustment due to varied characteristics of the farmers in the agriculture sector.

The government of Ghana launched its maiden programme RFE in the Upper West Region in 2017. The region is endowed with flat arable and savannah grass land suitable for both food crop and animal production, yet it is one of the poorest and most food insecure regions in Ghana (Nkegbe, 2017; [[8], [9], [10]]. The success of the programme lies with effective and large-scale participation by the populace as this could lead to increased production not only of animals but also in crop production. This will raise the food ratio for families and improve the income status of people in the region and Ghana as a whole. The nature of the PFJ and RFE programmes has attracted some government workers in the formal economy to participate in agricultural production in the region [11]. Mostly however, literature on participation in agricultural production has focused on the illiterate smallholder farmers [12,13]. Others also argue along gender while others focus on age (women and youth in agriculture) [5,14]. However, the burgeoning literature has paid little attention to government workers who though work in the formal sector, have growing interest in participating in agricultural production. The aim of this study is three pronged. First to explore the characteristics of government workers who participate in agriculture; next, to understand the factors that contribute to their participation in agriculture production; and thirdly, to examine how their participation contributes to increased production and growth in income of participants in the Upper West Region of Ghana. The justification is that agriculture still maintains its position as a leading livelihood activity in the developing world. Thus, an increased participation in agriculture has a strong association with increased food production and income. Besides, agriculturally based programmes and policies are efforts of governments and their development partners at growing the food levels in order to reduce food insecurity and to meet the projected 50 % of world food production by 2050 [[4], [5], [6]]. In this regard, all available means must be exploited making this study appropriate.

Empirical review

Agriculture production

Empirically, agricultural production is conceptualized as the cultivation of plants and rearing of animals to produce goods to support or improve human life [15]. Agricultural production is categorized according to the type of crop being cultivated, land size, speed, and degree of mechanization, livestock combinations, and the distribution of farm produce. Another form of categorization is the output that is realized as a result of the different forms of agricultural practice; these include subsistence farming, commercial farming and mixed farming [12,16]. For instance, the chicken, eggs, feather and droppings are agricultural products that come from poultry keeping. Also, meat, hide and milk are outputs that come from keeping of livestock [17].

Identifiable cohorts’ participating in agriculture production

The literature has reported several categories of people in agricultural production; the first is the grouping of farmers according to the scale of production. This categorization groups farmers either as smallholder farmers or large-scale farmers. The majority of smallholders practice subsistence farming while large-scale farmers do commercial farming [15,17]. As [18]; noted, smallholder farmers are not able to afford farm machines and equipment compared to the large-scale farmers. They rely on farm hands for which they sometimes struggle to meet the daily wage rate payments. Scholars have suggested that stallholder farmers put their little resources together to form a common pool which could then enable them hire farm machines for their operation in order to lessen the burden on an individual farmer. Not surprisingly however, Anang, and Ayambila (2020) identify low productivity of smallholder farmers as the major cause of poverty and food insecurity among smallholder farmers in Ghana and Africa. The study noted that smallholder farmers rely on extension services from government, which most often than not hardly come timeously. While the large-scale holder can afford to employ extension officers on their farms.

In Egypt large scale expansion of land by fruit and vegetable commercial farmers enabled them to cash in high profits from European markets [19]. Another categorization that literature has is youth participation in agricultural production. Aaccording to Ref. [20] and Akinnagbe, and Ogundele (2019) the youths in any country are the greatest vital source for transformation of their natural resources and driver of economic growth if harnessed and properly integrated in the production system. They report that though the youth face a lot of challenges in their quest to participate in agricultural production, the majority of them exhibit positive attitude towards participation in agriculture in Nigeria. Similarly, [21]; report renewed interest of the Nigerian youth in participating in various agriculture related businesses and it is contributing to the reduction of youth unemployment in Nigeria. In the area of food security [22] found that youth participation in agricultural production was impacting significantly on households’ food security. Women in agricultural production are another crucial identifiable group that literature has copiously reported on. Women are the majority in contributing to agricultural labour and their participation in agricultural production contributes significantly to household food security and income. However, women face stiff limitations in accessing arable land and credit for agriculture due to cultural barriers [[23], [24], [25]].

Factors influencing participation in agricultural production

The factors influencing participation in agricultural production as reported in literature can be looked at along the lenses of methodology usage, context, and location. Researchers agree that age, sex, household size, income, education, marital status, credit and policies are factors that influence participation in agricultural production although their studies were conducted using different methods [16]; Twimasi, Jian & Acheampong, 2019 [26]; Dimelu, Umoren & Chah, 2020; [27]. However, arguing in specific context and location [28], found that membership of social organizations influences youth participation in agricultural production in Nigeria. In a study on the determinants of agricultural production among tertiary institution youths in Ghana, Twimasi, Jian and Acheampong (2019), isolate additional factors that influence youth participation in agricultural production. These include: perception of farm input price, access to credit, access to land, course of study of youth at the tertiary institution, gender composition of the youth, and youth perception of farm income. For [27]; in Ethiopia, other factors such as credit getting bureaucracy, lack of initial capital, fear of the group, risk and uncertainty, and lack of working place determine significantly youth participation in agriculture enterprise [16]. quantified the magnitude and direction effects of factors influencing groundnut production and market participation. The results also showed that mobile phone ownership, access to credit and access to information were influencing factors for participation in groundnut market commercialization in the UWR.

Furthermore [29], using binary logit model analyzed twelve independent variables in a study on membership of marketing cooperative and participation in the marketing of agricultural inputs in Ethiopia reported that age, education, landholding, change in standard of living due to joining of cooperative, membership in other cooperatives were predicting factors for participation in agricultural input marketing. In addition [30], also discovered that size of field and distance to market influence participation in agro-processing in the Gauteng province of South Africa.

A recent study by Ref. [26] argued that higher educational attainment has a stronger connotation with a person's ability to adopt new agricultural technology. In a study on women's access to land in the Upper East Region of Ghana, Bonye and Kpieta, (2012) noted massive marginalization of women in how family or clan lands are passed on to men to the detriment of women. Those that may want to buy land need leadership of men in many cases before lands are acquired. This situation discovered by the scholars limits the participation abilities of women in agricultural production in the study area. This suggests that people, particularly young people and women across contexts in Africa, face similar challenges in their quest to participate in agricultural production. Further, the socio-demographic and economic category of persons is a big predictor of who participate in agriculture in Africa.

Government workers in Ghana

In Ghana, workers are broadly grouped into two to reflect the duality of the Ghanaian economy; that is, the formal sector and the informal sector (GSS, 2013). The formal sector is well regulated, mechanized and to some extent digitized. Financially, the formal sector is relatively well resourced and workers in this sector are relatively open to varied financial outlays at their disposal. The informal sector on the other hand is characterized by poor regulation, poor modernization and primary operations. It is further characterized by poorly educated and uneducated people. Financially, the informal sector is under resourced, poorly remunerated and has a high-risk of credit default (GSS, 2013; 2017). The formal sector comprises of public and civil service and formal private businesses. The public sector, as part of the economy, is made up of both public utilities or corporations and public enterprises [7,31,32]. The public sector provides public goods and government services to the populace. Public corporations and companies are state-owned enterprises or publicly owned, self-financing business enterprises that supply various private products and services for sale and typically operate on a commercial basis [33]. Government workers are made up of public sector workers also called public servants and civil service workers or civil servants [7]. Ggovernment workers consist mostly of career managers recruited on professional merit rather than appointed or elected, whose institutional tenure usually survives political leadership transitions [9,34]. Others include hospital workers, educational workers, financial workers, security workers, among others. They comprise male and female, varied age groups and educational qualifications across the country.

Theoretical and conceptual frameworks

Participation in social science refers to a different method for the public to express opinions and use influence concerning economic and social decisions affecting individual welfare [35]. From the perspective of [36]; workers in various sectors exhibit some form of participation. They conclude that different benefits, real or perceived, account for their participation in economic activities. Several theories exist in the literature on the discourse of participation; some classify participation into low, medium, high and no participation in agriculture [37] This study adopts and modifies the views of [38] levels of participation theory. The scholars identified participation to be full, partial and non-participation. The strength of the theory lies in the way it is structured (full, partial, non-participation) and this assists easy conceptualization at first glance. The theory lends itself to modifications to suit the objectives of this study. For the purpose of this study government worker participation in agricultural production is conceptualized to be ‘participation’ or ‘non-participation’ However, it requires explanation of the components of the structure. ‘Participation’ here means a government worker in the study area is actively involved in agriculture, be it crop cultivation or animal rearing. On the other non-participation denotes a government worker in the study is not in any way involved in any form of agricultural production, that is crop or animal. Thus, owing to the benefits that may be derived in participating in agricultural production by government workers. Fig. 1, demonstrates how government worker participation or non-participation in agricultural production is influenced by demographic, social, cultural, political and economic factors. As indicated by Kondo, (2018) [39]; and [30] socio-economic factors have strong influence on participation in agriculture be it at the production or agro-processing level. These factors are assimilated by the government worker before a decision is made with respect to participation in agricultural production. Thus, if the decision is to participate in agricultural production it will lead to the participation outcome such as ensuring family food security since what may be produced will supplement what is bought from the market. It may also contribute to improving the family income status since excess produce and to some extent farm animals could be sold for extra income as depicted in Fig. 1. The other way round is the desire for food security and improved income, interacting with demographic, social, cultural, political, and economic factors culminate in the decision by the government worker to participate in agriculture production (see Fig. 1).

Methodology

Study area

The Upper West Region (UWR) is one of the sixteen administrative regions in Ghana situated in the North-Western part of the country. It lies between longitude 1 25″ W and 2 45″ W and latitudes 9 30″ N and 11 N. It is bordered to the South by the Savannah Region, to the North and west by Burkina Faso, to the east by the Upper East region. It covers a geographical area of 18.476 sq. km, which constitutes 12.7% of the total land area of Ghana. The Region has 5 Municipal Assemblies and 6 District Assemblies. The five Municipal assemblies are Wa, Nandom, Sissala East, Jirapa and Lawra Municipals. The District Assemblies are Lambussie, Nadowli-Kaleo, Sissala West, Wa East, Daffiama-Bussie-Issa and Wa West Districts. The Region has two marked seasons namely, the wet and dry seasons. The South-Western Monsoon winds from the Atlantic Ocean bring rains between May and September, whilst the North-Eastern Trade winds from the Sahara Desert bring the long dry season between October and April. Temperatures in the region can reach 41 °C in March and April just before the onset of the rainy season which begins in late March and ends in October. The mean annual rainfall varies between 840 mm and 1400 mm. One feature of the rainfall pattern is that it tends to occur in heavy downpours from June to September. The region is within the Guinea Savannah ecological zone. This zone is marked by short and fire-resistant trees interspersed with grass that shed their leaves in the dry season. Trees found in this zone include the baobab, kapok, parkia, and the economically valuable sheanut tree (GSS, 2014; [40]. Crops such as maize, beans, groundnut, soya beans, millet, bambara beans and yam are cultivated. Animals such as sheep, goats, cows and rabbits are kept. Guinea fowls, fowls, turkeys, and poultry birds also kept.

This study selected four (4) municipalities (see Fig. 2) through purposive sampling as a proxy for the UWR. They are; Wa Municipality, Jirapa Municipality, Lawra and Sissala East Municipalities. The justification is that they have large numbers of government workers [7].

Study design

A cross-sectional survey was adopted in this study The justification is that government workers which the study is about have similar characteristic as workers in the public sector. They are distributed all over the study area and a cross-sectional survey is appropriate to adopt [41]. The design also enabled the determination of important predicting variables of government workers participating in agricultural production in the UWR. It further permitted associations and inferences to be made from data collected and analyzed.

Targeted population, sample frame and method

The target population is all government workers who are in the selected municipalities. A sample frame was updated and computed for the selected areas based on [7] data for government workers in the UWR. The number of Government workers for the four municipalities is 10490, of this the Wa Municipality has the highest (5,235), the Jirapa Municipality has 2019 State government workers, followed closely by the Sissala East Municipality with 1770 and the Lawra Municipality harbouring the least (1,466) government workers. The study employed quantitative methods with simple random sampling techniques to select 400 respondents out of the 10,490 government workers for the study.

Sampling and data collection

Each municipality was divided into 4 sections and in each section, research assistant visited and screen prospective respondents. Upon the consent of a government worker, she/he was selected to respond to a survey questionnaire. The process went on till the sample size for that municipality was exhausted and then the team moved to the next municipality. In all 400 government workers were covered in the study area (see Table 1). The survey questionnaire sought information on demographic characteristics of respondents, participation in agriculture activities and influencing factors for the participation in agriculture.

Analytical Framework

The application of Heckman two-stage model

The Heckman Two-stage model was employed to predict the factors that may influence government workers to participate in agriculture production. The strength of this model over others is that, the method has the ability to predict the extent of participation.

Analysis of factors influencing government worker participation in agricultural production in the UWR of Ghana was achieved using the Heckman's two-stage selection procedure [43]. James Heckman has proposed an alternative to the Mutli-Lineal method, which is comparatively simple. This alternative consists of a two-step estimating procedure. In the first stage, a ‘participation equation’ attempts to capture factors affecting participation decisions. The second stage provides Heckit analysis that determines the level of participation. The first stage of the analysis focused on respondent's characteristic in participating in agricultural production in the UWR. Male dominance in economic is still exhibited in this study with 80.6 % (see Table 5). Education also has a bearing on participation in agriculture by government workers. The results also revealed that majority (87.7 %) of government workers who were married participate in agricultural production (see Table 5). The results further show that government workers whose monthly income is from 1600.00 and above were participating more 284 (87.7 %) in agricultural production as depicted in Table 5.

Factors influencing participation in agriculture by government workers

This section presents regression results of the factors that influenced government workers' participation in agricultural production and the extent of participation. Before the estimation of the model parameters, the issues of multicollinearity among the potential explanatory variables were tested. The Variance Inflation Factor (VIF) was used to test for continuous variables multicollinearity and Contingency Coefficient (CC) was used to test for dummy variables. Therefore, three variables, namely fertilizer subsidy, machine (tractor service) and seeds costs and availability were omitted due to their collinearity. The aforementioned variables all had association problems since their respective coefficients were very high (that is, more than 0.65). However, all other continuous variables had no issues of multicollinearity.

Probit results predicting government workers’ participation in agricultural production

From the results in Table 6, 18 explanatory variables were entered in the model for the first dependent variable (participation in agricultural production). Out of the 18 explanatory variables, 10 variables were found to have significantly determined government workers' participation in agricultural production. Specifically, income level, access to extension and knowledge of agricultural activities were found to be statistically significant at 1 %. However, sex of respondent, age of respondent, household size, credit availability, access to credit, passion for agricultural activities and scarcity of food items were all found to be statistically significant at 10 %.

Maximum likelihood of extent of participation in agricultural production by government workers

Discussion

The recent housing and population census revealed that women dominate the pupation of Ghana, with over 400,000 more women than men [45]. However, the public formal sector is generally dominated by men in all sections of the economy. This was evident from the study as majority 323 (80.8 %) of the respondents were men. This result is in agreement with [46]; who reported that men dominate the corporate world. Also, [47]; noted that men are given better chances in terms of recruitment in both the public and private formal sectors of the economy. Furthermore, the study revealed that the average age of state government worker is about 38 years suggesting that the public sector is dominated by the economically active age group. Similarly [14], reported that most young people see state government workers to have high prospects and thus coincide with their aspiration to career development. The private sector in Ghana is not buoyant enough to absorb young graduates from the tertiary institutions every year. Thus, young man or woman who intend to build a business career will have to find a public sector job to be credit worthy in order to raise the needed capital using the monthly income as guarantee. In the Ghanaian society, marriage is considered as one of the most important institutions, and cultural and social pressure is brought on singles to marry [48] hence, the high number (324) (81.0 %) of married public sector workers in the sample. They are more likely to work diligently as breadwinners for their families.

According to Ref. [49]; higher educational status of a person increases his or her ability to process and use information to achieve higher productivity. Here, the study revealed that only a few 7 (1.8 %) of state government workers had Middle/JHS education, while majority 373 (93.3 %) of the state government workers had tertiary education. The higher educational level of state government workers can be attributed to the high unemployment rate in the country as those with less educational qualification find it difficult securing jobs in the public sector. This finding is in line with [50]; who reported that most graduates in Africa go for further studies after a degree due to the non-availability of jobs in the system to improve upon their skills and qualification in order to get a high paying job.

From the study, the average household size was 5 persons per household. This finding is however higher than that of the national household average of 4 persons per household in Ghana [7]. Hence, the participation in agricultural production could help lessen the burden of catering for the high household size. Research findings of [39]; intimated that a large household size largely affects the capacity of that household to provide labour for economic activities.

In this study it has been found that, government workers' participation and extent of participation in agricultural production are influenced by sex, age, household size income, government policies, credit availability, credit access, access to extension, passion for agricultural activities, knowledge on agricultural activities, scarcity of food items, time availability, access to market, demand for food products and cost of food items. Furthermore, the sex of government workers was found to be significant at 10 % with a positive influence on the likelihood of the extent to which a government worker participates in agricultural production. Thus, male government workers are more likely to participate in any stream of agricultural production than a female state government worker. This finding is not surprising, taking into account the socio-cultural variables that are dominant in a patriarchal society like that of the UWR. Women in particular face stiffer limitations in accessing arable lands in Northern Ghana [[23], [24], [25],48]; Haile, Gebre & Workye, 2022). Research has shown that women who have the wherewithal to purchase landed properties even need the support of a male to vouch for them before access can be granted to them [23,51,52]. Thus, this may have adverse implications with regards to women's contribution to agricultural production and food security in developing societies [53].

At 10 % significance, older government workers are more likely to participate in agricultural production than the younger ones. Thus, age has a negative influence on the probability of a government worker participating in agricultural production. That is, holding other variables constant, a reduction in the age of a government worker by one year may lead to increased participation in agricultural production. This result confirms the literature positing that agriculture in developing countries is now left in the hands of the aged [14,29,54]. The findings suggest the need for a policy targeting youth participation in agriculture. Thus, both government policies (1 %) and socio-demographic variables of government workers strongly explain the extent to which they may participate in agricultural production holding other things constant. Furthermore, the household size significance at 10 % has a positive association with the probability of government workers participating in agricultural production. Government workers with a large household size are more likely to participate in agricultural production compared with those with a small household size. The results are expected because high household size comes with the responsibility of feeding the household [11,55].

In addition, at a 1 % significance level, the results show income has a positive influence on the probability of a government worker participating in agricultural production – government workers with high incomes are more likely to participate in agricultural production all other things being equal [56]. The results are encouraging, considering the fact that government workers are credit worthy [11]. Thus, their participation in agricultural production has the tendency to contribute to achieving the sustainable development goal target of zero hunger by 2030 (SDG 2). Moreover, government workers with extra income might prefer to invest in agricultural production as a means of increasing their fortunes. In contrast, the poor and vulnerable are mostly at the forefront of agricultural production in developing countries. However, this study revealed otherwise, the attribution of income being a positive predictor of government workers’ participation in agricultural production could be in line with them supporting [57] assumption of passion and happiness as a driving force for participation in agriculture.

In furtherance of the analysis, the results show that respondents that engage in agriculture for passion was significant at 10 % and has a positive influence on the probability of government workers participating in agricultural production. Thus, government workers who have the passion for agricultural activities are more likely to participate in agricultural production and the vice versa holding all other variables constant. The results imply that a significant measure to increase agricultural production is to scale up access to inputs as well as credit to make the sector attractive to not only farmers but workers in the formal sector. It has been argued that agricultural production is on the decline in developing society because the majority of the youth have lost interest in agriculture [40]. Although natural climatic effects such as drought, flooding and the outbreak of pests and diseases are largely highlighted as challenges facing agricultural production [12,58]. The low-level participation of the populace, the youth in particular, in agricultural production cannot be downplayed. In addition, the respondents’ knowledge about the various streams of agricultural activities (at 1 %) and the net benefits have the tendency to whip up passion in the participation in agricultural production by government workers in the UWR. Additionally, the time available to a government worker has the likelihood to influence participation in agriculture at a 5 % significant level. The findings are in consonance with previous scholarly findings that posit that government workers who spend less time at their formal work places are more likely to participate in agricultural production compared to those who spend the entire working hours at their public formal sector job (see Ref. [11].

Another determinant of government workers' participation in agricultural production was credit availability. This was found to be significant at 10 % and has a positive influence on the probability of government workers participating in agricultural production. The results encourage the debate on supporting farmers with credit in developing societies to increase production [[59], [60], [61]]. In addition, access to credit was found to be significant at 10 % and has a positive influence on the probability of government workers participating in agricultural production. Apart from the aforementioned determinants of government workers' participation in agricultural production, the results further show that access to extensive services significantly (at 1 %) and positively influence government workers' participation in agricultural production. Thus, the more a government worker is exposed to extension services, the more likely he/she would participate in agricultural production [62]. Thus, policy on expanding the scope of extension services in Ghana is encouraged. The results show that scarcity of food items in the local markets has the proclivity of increasing government workers’ participation in agriculture. The results show a significant (at 10 %) negative effect between food scarcity and participation in agricultural production. The results show that government workers are quite skeptical about the prevailing market condition for food items. Thus, they may be pushed to grow what they eat if their food needs are not met in the market. The aforesaid findings encourage the debate on rolling out policies that will make agriculture attractive in sub-Saharan Africa [63,64]; UN, 2020; OBG, 2022). Thus, although governments across sub-Saharan Africa have developed agricultural growth policies such as fertilizer subsidies, and credit support, among others, access to these facilities remains questionable.

Conclusions

The study shows that greater percentage of government workers participate in agricultural production in the UWR. The area of participation in agriculture includes food crops cultivation, cash crop and keeping of farm animals with majority at a subsistence level. The predicting variable for government workers to participate in any agricultural activity include sex, age, household size, income level, credit availability, access to credit, passion for agricultural activities, access to extension, knowledge of agricultural activities and scarcity of food items. However, the variables predicting the extent to which a government workers may participate in agricultural production are; government policies, credit availability and age. Others include; time availability sex, cost of food items and demand for food products. We recommend that MoFA and its development partners in agriculture effectively use local media and community visits to create more awareness among government workers on agricultural programmes and policies. Since extension services of MoFA and knowledge (awareness) in agriculture programmes influence participation in agricultural production as revealed by the study in the UWR. Government workers, if their potential is harnessed, could be blended with smallholderfarmers in the informal sector in order to raise production, reduce burden and reduction in food insecurity in the UWR.
