Computational identification of a marine derived dual inhibitor for type 2 diabetes mellitus using integrated in silico approaches
Authors: Arnob Biswas, Tamal Paul, Md.Iqbal Hossain, Bindu Shekhar Bahadur, Aminun Naher, Emon Mitra, Ananya Majumder, Ripu Chandra Das, Uma Shill, Nakul Chandra Bhowmick, Mehede Hassan Rubel, Dayanidhi Sarkar, Swagato Dutta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The multi-factorial metabolic disorder, type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is caused by inadequate insulin signaling and dysregulated incretin-mediated glucose homeostasis, resulting in complementary dual-target therapies. Using an integrated in-silico pipeline, it searched for marine compounds that act as dual inhibitors of PTP1B and DPP-4. After curation, 35,034 invalid entries were deleted from 74,507 marine compounds, followed by physicochemical filtering (MW 200–350 Da) to yield 4,500 compounds and Lipinski refinement to 3,395 ligands. PubChem mapping validated 2,230 compounds (name/CID-matched), while SwissADME and pkCSM toxicity filters selected 63 structure-based screening candidates. The highest docking affinities for human PTP1B (4I8N) and DPP-4 (5KBY) reached −8.7 and −9.0 kcal/mol, respectively, calculated using inhibitor-defined active-site grids in AutoDock Vina. Due to favorable docking and stable complex behavior in 200 ns GROMACS 2024.4 MD simulations, RMSD, RMSF, SASA, Rg, PCA, DCCM, and MM-GBSA analyses, CID_5281663 (1,3,5-trihydroxyxanthone) was chosen as the most consistent dual-target candidate. MM-GBSA calculations further supported stable binding, with total binding free energies of −5955.51 ± 54.24 and −8895.41 ± 202.85 for PTP1B and DPP-4 complexes, respectively. The top ligand’s frontier orbital properties were further examined using DFT (B3LYP/6–31+G(d,p)), and downstream biological relevance was contextualized using Reactome pathway mapping of the insulin and incretin signaling cascade. Overall, the combined screening and modelling methodology suggests 1,3,5-trihydroxyxanthone (CID_5281663) from soft coral Lobophytum microlobulatum as a promising marine-derived dual PTP1B/DPP-4 inhibitor candidate, warranting experimental validation for T2DM therapy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46463-4.

Introduction

Type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) is a chronic metabolic disorder characterized by persistent hyperglycemia arising from a combination of peripheral insulin resistance and progressive pancreatic β-cell dysfunction that limits compensatory insulin secretion1. Over time, chronic glucotoxicity, lipotoxicity, oxidative stress, and low-grade inflammation reinforce these defects and promote a self-perpetuating cycle of worsening glycemic control2. Clinically, T2DM is associated with major microvascular complications and macrovascular disease, making it a leading contributor to morbidity, mortality, and disability worldwide. The global prevalence of T2DM continues to increase, accounting for around 90% of all diabetes cases worldwide2. The International Diabetes Federation (IDF) Diabetes Atlas (11th edition, 2025) estimates that 589 million adults aged 20 to 79 years (11.1%) had diabetes in 2024. Driven by factors such as population growth, aging, and urbanization, the global prevalence of diabetes is projected to increase by 45%, reaching 853 million by 20503. The most significant increases are anticipated in Africa (142%) and the Middle East and North Africa (95%)4. Importantly, greater than 90% of people with diabetes have T2DM, largely driven by interacting socio-economic, demographic, environmental, and genetic determinants5. The rising prevalence of diabetes worldwide has intensified the search for innovative and more effective treatment options6.

Protein tyrosine phosphatase 1B (PTP1B) is a non-receptor phosphatase widely recognized as a key negative regulator of insulin signaling7. Mechanistically, insulin binding to the insulin receptor (IR) triggers IR autophosphorylation and downstream signaling through insulin receptor substrates (IRS proteins), activating the PI3K-Akt pathway that supports glucose uptake and suppresses hepatic gluconeogenesis8,9. PTP1B counteracts these processes by dephosphorylating IR and proximal signaling components, thereby attenuating insulin signal propagation and contributing to insulin resistance. PTP1B has long been considered an attractive antidiabetic target because of its proximal role in insulin signaling; however, the drug discovery path has been challenging, in part because the catalytic site is adapted for phosphotyrosine recognition, making many early inhibitors highly polar and raising selectivity concerns against related phosphatases10. Dipeptidyl peptidase‑4 (DPP‑4) is a serine exopeptidase that inactivates incretin hormones, most prominently GLP‑1 (glucagon-like peptide‑1) and GIP (glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide). Pharmacological DPP‑4 inhibition prolongs endogenous incretin activity, improving glycemic control with a relatively low intrinsic risk of hypoglycemia because the insulinotropic effect is glucose-dependent11,12. DPP-4 inhibitors, gliptins, are a clinically established drug class with a strong mechanistic foundation in incretin physiology. A major body of clinical evidence supports their glucose-lowering efficacy, oral bioavailability, and generally favorable tolerability profile, while acknowledging heterogeneity across agents in pharmacokinetics and clinical positioning13. Consequently, DPP-4 remains a validated target for glycemic control and a benchmark for evaluating novel DPP-4 inhibitory chemotypes, including natural products. Contemporary reviews emphasize ongoing innovation in allosteric inhibition, improved selectivity strategies, and the optimization of drug-like properties14.

Marine ecosystems represent one of the largest and least-exhaustively mapped reservoirs of biological and chemical diversity. Marine natural products frequently display distinct scaffolds, dense stereochemistry, and unusual functional groups shaped by unique ecological pressures and biosynthetic logic15. Marine-derived compounds have notably advanced research on PTP1B inhibition. For example, Phosphoeleganin, a marine-derived phosphorylated polyketide isolated from the Mediterranean ascidian Sidnyum elegans, has been identified as a dual inhibitor of PTP1B and aldose reductase, two emerging therapeutic targets in T2DM16. Similarly, Trodusquemine, an aminosterol originally isolated from the dogfish shark, has been investigated as an allosteric inhibitor of PTP1B in clinical trials17. Additionally, Bromophenol metabolites from marine red algae have been repeatedly investigated for PTP1B inhibition, including mechanistic and binding studies18. In vivo oriented work has also evaluated marine bromophenols for improvements in insulin signaling and glycemic phenotypes in experimental models19. Marine-derived protein hydrolysates and purified peptides, often sourced from fish by-products and collagen or gelatin, have demonstrated in vitro DPP-4 inhibitory activity. This validates marine peptide scaffolds as viable incretin-preserving agents for lead compounds or nutraceutical and functional food development20.

The aim of this study is to explore the potential of marine natural compounds as new T2DM therapeutic agents by targeting and inhibiting the activities of PTP1B and DPP-4. This approach is based on previous findings highlighting the potential of marine compounds as new anti-diabetic agents with better therapeutic effects on T2DM. This study may be among the first comprehensive assessments of marine natural compounds for dual-target inhibition, suggesting possible unique and effective approaches for T2DM treatment.

Method & material

Protein selection and preparation

Human target structures were retrieved from the Protein Data Bank and chosen for X-ray quality, human origin, and the presence of a co-crystallized inhibitor to define the binding site. PTP1B was modelled using PDB ID: 4I8N and DPP-4 using PDB ID: 5KBY; both are hydrolase/hydrolase-inhibitor complexes with no reported mutations21,22. Proteins were prepared by removing co-crystallized ligands and non-essential waters, adding hydrogens, assigning protonation states near physiological pH, and performing restrained energy minimisation to relieve local steric clashes while maintaining the experimental backbone. Binding sites were defined from the coordinates of the native co-crystallized inhibitors, and the resulting prepared receptors were used for downstream virtual screening of marine-derived compounds.

Ligand preparation

Ligands were sourced from the Marine Natural Products (MNPs) database (https://marinenp.scicloud.eu/#/home) by downloading the available compound dataset in CSV format. The library was then curated to generate an initial subset for drug-like screening. Compounds were filtered by molecular weight (MW) between 200–350 Da, targeting a size range generally compatible with oral small-molecule development and efficient sampling in docking workflows. In parallel, a physicochemical “drug-criteria” filter was applied using Lipinski-based constraints, retaining compounds that met the predefined Lipinski threshold (0 violations). After removing duplicates and entries with missing key descriptors, the resulting filtered ligand set was used for subsequent structure-based virtual screening against PTP1B and DPP-4 (PTP1B: https://www.rcsb.org/structure/4I8N; DPP-4: https://www.rcsb.org/structure/5KBY). Prior to docking, all ligands were subjected to geometry optimization to ensure energetically favorable conformations using the GROMACS energy minimization tool23. Initial 3D structures were generated from canonical SMILES and optimized using the MMFF94 force field to minimize steric strain and achieve a local energy minimum. The optimized geometries were subsequently converted to PDBQT format for docking. This step ensured that docking calculations were performed on structurally relaxed conformers rather than raw database geometries.

ADMET profiling

For ADMET profiling, each filtered marine compound was mapped to PubChem using its name, and the corresponding canonical SMILES were retrieved and compiled to generate a standardized ligand input set. Where multiple PubChem matches were identified, the entry with the most consistent structural identity (matching name/SMILES) was retained, and compounds lacking reliable identifiers were excluded from ADMET analysis. Drug-likeness and pharmacokinetic properties were then predicted using the SwissADME web platform (absorption, physicochemical descriptors, lipophilicity, solubility, and drug-likeness rules), while toxicity endpoints were estimated using pkCSM24,25. Compounds were prioritized based on favorable predicted ADME behavior and lower predicted toxicity risk, and the shortlisted molecules were advanced to the docking stage26,27.

Molecular docking

Molecular docking was performed using AutoDock Vina28 with the prepared receptors 4I8N.pdbqt (PTP1B) and 5KBY.pdbqt (DPP-4). For both targets, the binding region was defined as a rectangular grid box centered on the experimentally characterized active site (based on the co-crystallized inhibitor coordinates). Docking calculations were executed with an exhaustiveness of 8 to provide a balanced compromise between sampling depth and computational cost, and the best-ranked binding poses were retained for downstream analysis. For PTP1B (4I8N), the grid box was centered at (x, y, z) = (−7.3665, 46.4602, 49.6968) with dimensions 56.2732 × 58.5541 × 42.9245 Å. For DPP-4 (5KBY), the grid box centre was (49.7508, 31.4997, 18.4727) with dimensions 75.4849 × 71.2323 × 83.0665 Å. Docking outputs were ranked by Vina binding affinity scores, and top poses were visually inspected to confirm reasonable placement within the catalytic pocket and consistency with known ligand-binding interactions for each enzyme. To validate the docking protocol, the native co-crystallized inhibitors from 4I8N and 5KBY were re-docked into their respective binding sites using the same grid parameters and exhaustiveness settings. The reproduced poses were compared with the crystallographic conformations using RMSD analysis. An RMSD value below 2.0 Å was considered acceptable, confirming the reliability of the docking setup for predicting binding modes of screened ligands.

Molecular dynamics simulation

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were performed using GROMACS 2024.4 to evaluate the stability and interaction persistence of the top-ranked docked complexes. For each target (PTP1B and DPP-4), the four best-scoring ligands from docking were selected, and 200 ns production simulations were carried out for each protein-ligand system. All systems were parameterized using the CHARMM36 all-atom force field, ensuring consistent treatment of protein and ligand interactions throughout the trajectories24,25.

After system construction and initial energy minimization, each complex was equilibrated in two stages prior to the production run. First, NVT equilibration was performed for 100 ps at 310 K to stabilize the temperature while maintaining a constant simulation volume. This was followed by NPT equilibration for 100 ps at 310 K to allow the system density and pressure to relax under constant pressure conditions. Trajectory analyses were conducted to quantify structural stability and binding behavior over time. Global and local dynamics were assessed using RMSD and RMSF, while SASA and radius of gyration (Rg) were calculated to monitor solvent exposure and compactness, respectively. Protein-ligand interaction persistence was evaluated via hydrogen-bond (H-bond) analysis, and collective motions were characterized using principal component analysis (PCA) and dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) calculations. Binding energetics were further estimated using MM-GBSA calculations on the MD trajectories to obtain relative binding free-energy trends for prioritizing the most promising candidates.

Molecular orbital analysis

Frontier molecular orbital (FMO) analysis was performed using density functional theory (DFT) to further characterize the electronic properties of the most stable-performing ligand identified from the protein-ligand stability assessment. Geometry preparation and visualization were carried out in GaussView 6.0, and all quantum chemical calculations were executed in Gaussian 09W. The DFT computations were performed using the B3LYP functional with the 6–31+G (d, p) basis set, which provides a balanced description of valence electrons and polarization effects for drug-like organic molecules. From the converged DFT outputs, the HOMO (Highest Occupied Molecular Orbital) and LUMO (Lowest Unoccupied Molecular Orbital) energies were extracted, and the energy gap (ΔE = ELUMO - EHOMO) was calculated as an indicator of chemical reactivity and kinetic stability26,27. HOMO/LUMO orbital distributions were also inspected to identify electron-donating and electron-accepting regions that may contribute to intermolecular interactions within the binding site and to support comparative interpretation among shortlisted candidates.

Downstream pathway regulation

Downstream pathway analysis was conducted to contextualize the biological relevance of PTP1B and DPP-4 modulation in T2DM. The functional roles and signaling consequences of both targets were examined using KEGG and Reactome pathway databases to map their involvement in insulin signaling, incretin-mediated glucose regulation, and associated metabolic pathways29. Pathway enrichment and annotation were used to identify key nodes influenced by PTP1B and DPP-4 inhibition, including insulin receptor/IRS-PI3K-Akt signaling, glucose uptake, gluconeogenesis, and β-cell functional regulation. Based on the curated pathway information, downstream regulatory effects of target inhibition were systematically interpreted, focusing on changes in signal amplification, feedback control, and cross-talk with inflammatory and metabolic stress pathways30. This integrative pathway-level analysis was used to support the mechanistic rationale for dual targeting (PTP1B and DPP-4) and to link molecular-level inhibition with potential physiological outcomes relevant to glycemic homeostasis and T2DM management.

Results

Ligand selection and preparation

An initial library of 74,507 marine-derived compounds was obtained from the MNPs database. During preliminary curation, 35,034 entries lacking valid compound names or sufficient structural identifiers were excluded, yielding a refined dataset suitable for downstream processing. The remaining compounds were then filtered by molecular weight to retain drug-like small molecules, yielding 4,500 compounds within the predefined MW range 200–350 Da. Subsequent application of Lipinski-based drug-likeness criteria led to the removal of 1,105 compounds that violated the set threshold. As a result, 3,395 marine-derived ligands satisfied the initial physicochemical requirements and were advanced for comprehensive ADMET analysis, forming the final screening library for structure-based and simulation studies (Supplementary File 1).

ADMET analysis

A total of 3,395 filtered ligands were subjected to ADMET profiling. Compounds were first queried against PubChem using the available compound names, and 2,230 molecules were successfully retrieved with matching compound names and CIDs, confirming identity and enabling standardized structure collection (canonical SMILES). The PubChem-matched set was then evaluated in SwissADME, and compounds were prioritized using stringent pharmacokinetic and drug-likeness filters: high predicted gastrointestinal absorption, non-BBB permeant, and 0 Lipinski violations (re-checked). This step yielded 700 candidates with favorable ADME profiles. These 700 compounds were subsequently assessed for safety liabilities using pkCSM toxicity predictions. Molecules predicted to be Ames test positive, skin sensitizers, hepatotoxic, or hERG I and hERG II inhibitors were excluded (Table 1). Molecular docking and co-crystal validation

Docking of the screened marine-derived ligands against PTP1B (4I8N) and DPP-4 (5KBY) (Supplementary File 3) identified a clear set of high-affinity candidates for each target. To validate the docking protocol, the co-crystallized ligands of PTP1B and DPP-4 were re-docked using the same docking configuration. The obtained RMSD values were 1.893 Å and 2.134 Å, respectively. These results indicate satisfactory recovery of the experimental binding poses, with the PTP1B complex meeting the commonly used RMSD <2.0 Å benchmark, while the DPP-4 complex showed a marginally higher deviation [Supplementary File 4(Fig. S1)]. For PTP1B, the best docking scores clustered between −8.7 and −8.4 kcal/mol, led by 1,3,5-trihydroxyxanthone (CID_5281663; −8.7 kcal/mol) and 4-[2-(3,5-dihydroxyphenyl) ethenyl] benzene-1,2-diol (CID_4813; −8.7 kcal/mol). For DPP-4, the top-ranked ligand was 4-(3,5-dihydroxyphenyl)−5-methylbenzene-1,2-diol (CID_122182014) with the most favorable affinity (−9.0 kcal/mol), followed by Albidopyrone (CID_54740233; −8.8 kcal/mol), indicating stronger predicted binding in the DPP-4 pocket than most other compounds in the dataset.

Dynamic cross-correlation matrix (DCCM) analysis further quantified residue-residue motion coupling for the CID_5281663 complexes (Fig. 8C–D). Discussion

T2DM is driven by intertwined defects in insulin signaling and glucose-dependent hormone regulation, so single-target strategies often leave residual dysregulation. In this context, concurrently modulating PTP1B (to amplify insulin signaling) and DPP-4 (to preserve incretin activity) offers a mechanistically complementary approach for improving glycemic control and cardiovascular-metabolic risk37–40. PTP1B has long been recognized as an attractive anti-diabetic target, but remains challenging due to catalytic-site properties and selectivity constraints; contemporary strategies emphasize allosteric modulation and improved drug-like profiles41. Clinically, DPP-4 inhibition is a validated strategy for T2DM management, and ongoing medicinal chemistry continues to refine potency, selectivity, and pharmacokinetics of novel DPP-4 inhibitor scaffolds42. In parallel, marine and seafood protein hydrolysates and derived peptides have repeatedly demonstrated DPP-4 inhibitory potential, supporting marine resources as a plausible source of incretin-preserving chemotypes43.

In this study, a marine chemical space was systematically triaged using drug-likeness filtering (MW 200–350; Lipinski 0), in-silico ADMET profiling, and structure-based screening against human PTP1B and DPP-4. Top candidates were evaluated using 200 ns GROMACS MD simulations (RMSD, RMSF, SASA, Rg, PCA, and DCCM), along with MM-GBSA component analysis and DFT-based frontier-orbital profiling, to prioritize dynamically stable and energetically plausible inhibitors. The pipeline reduced 74,507 marine compounds to 63 ADMET and safety-filtered candidates, from which docking identified high-affinity hits for each target (PTP1B: −8.7 to −8.4 kcal/mol; DPP-4: −9.0 to −8.2 kcal/mol). Two ligands emerged as shared top candidates across both targets, with CID_5281663 (1,3,5-trihydroxyxanthone) showing the most consistent dual-target behavior across docking and MD stability metrics. For CID_5281663, MD analyses supported stable complex integrity across both proteins (stable backbone behavior, restrained RMSF outside flexible loops), while essential dynamics showed that the first three PCs captured 38.6% of motion for PTP1B (PC1 26.38%) and 70.2% for DPP-4 (PC1 46.39%), consistent with well-defined collective motion regimes rather than progressive destabilization. At the electronic-structure level, the post-MD DFT analysis showed an increased HOMO-LUMO gap magnitude for the ligand in both complexes (e.g., ~0.0627 to 0.1505 eV for 4I8N; ~0.0977 to 0.1471 eV for 5KBY), supporting increased electronic stabilization after binding-site relaxation.

These findings align with and extend prior work in several ways. Collectively, the studies summarised in “Supplementary File 5” demonstrate a consistent methodological trend in antidiabetic target discovery, where molecular docking is commonly used for initial hit identification and binding-mode rationalization, and is frequently strengthened by post-docking refinement, including molecular dynamics simulations and binding free-energy calculations. Importantly, several PTP1B and DPP-4-focused reports extend beyond computation by incorporating in vitro enzyme inhibition assays and, in some cases, cell-based functional validation and in vivo evaluation, thereby increasing confidence that the predicted interactions translate into measurable bioactivity42,44–49. However, this research demonstrates its uniqueness through significant scale-up via comprehensive ligand library screening, chemical novelty by investigating underutilized marine biodiversity, detailed ADMET analysis, and depth of dynamic validation by extending molecular dynamics (MD) simulations to 200 ns50.

Finally, the study has explicitly explored 1,3,5-trihydroxyxanthone from the soft coral Lobophytum microlobulatum (L. microlobulatum) dual targeting (DPP-4 and PTP1B) using docking, MD, MM-GBSA, and DFT, providing methodological precedent and supporting the rationale for dual-pathway modulation50. Lobophytum was also among the most reported genera found to produce bioactive secondary metabolites with a wide spectrum of pharmacological activity. Anti-inflammatory activity, neuroprotective effects in models of Parkinson’s disease, and anticancer potential of extracts and isolated compounds have previously been reported51–54. Metabolites from Lobophytum are also reported to have potent antimicrobial activities against Gram-positive bacteria, yeasts, and phytopathogenic fungi, and have significant antioxidant and antimalarial activities55,56. Moreover, L. microlobulatum has been reported to exhibit antiviral activity against chikungunya virus, antibacterial activity against Staphylococcus aureus, and antifungal activity against Cryptococcus neoformans57.

This study is primarily computational and does not yet include biochemical IC50/Ki validation or cellular confirmation of effects on insulin and incretin pathways. While docking scores and MD stability analyses provide valuable insights into plausible binding and dynamic retention, they do not directly correlate with potency. Additionally, a significant limitation of marine natural products is their isolation in small quantities, which are sufficient for structural analysis but inadequate for large-scale drug screening58. Another key limitation is that the current simulation setup does not fully account for the influence of DPP-4’s structural context, glycosylation state, or oligomeric environment on ligand binding59. Similarly, MD simulations of PTP1B with ligands fail to capture the full extent of its dynamic allosteric regulation, particularly the WPD-loop conformational shifts and helical bundle motions required for substrate access and catalysis60. Future research should focus on experimentally validating the top ligands (especially CID_5281663) through enzyme inhibition assays for both targets, along with selectivity profiling against related phosphatases and proteases. Furthermore, testing the mechanistic activity in cellular models is essential for further characterization and validation.

Conclusion

This study identifies CID_5281663 (1,3,5-trihydroxyxanthone) as a promising marine-derived dual inhibitor candidate of PTP1B and DPP-4, supported by favorable docking, long-timescale MD stability, coherent essential dynamics, and supportive MM-GBSA energy profiles. The integrated computational framework highlights marine chemical space as a valuable source of multitarget antidiabetic leads and provides a strong rationale for experimental validation and scaffold optimization toward next-generation therapeutics for T2DM.
