Zonal determinants of healthy aging: An exposomic analysis of determinants of healthy cardiovascular aging in Ohio
Authors: El Hussain Shamsa, Sai Rahul Ponnana, Santosh Siraspalli, Zhuo Chen, Jean-Eudes Dazard, Alice T Zhang, Robert Okyere, Tasveer Khawaja, Kanimozhi Sivanantham, Ian Neeland, Salil Deo, Sanjay Rajagopalan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEmerging evidence supports a role for social, behavioral, and environmental variables in explaining significant disparities in cardiovascular health and aging, beyond conventional risk factors.ObjectivesThis study sought to identify and characterize regional “Healthy Aging” zones by identifying census-tract clusters with high rates of healthy coronary artery calcium (CAC) scores as a precison marker of cardiovascular aging in older populations.MethodsIn this prospective cohort study, 31,544 older patients (age ≥ 65) across 1,141 census tracts in Ohio enrolled in a no-charge CAC screening program (CLARIFY registry) were grouped as “healthy” (CAC = 0, n = 7,932) and “non-optimal” aging (CAC > 0, n = 23,612). Cluster analysis was performed using Local Moran’s I on census-tracts and adjusted Z-scores of Local Moran’s I values were used to rank clusters from most significant “Non-optimal Aging” (low-low Moran’s I) to most significant “Healthy Aging” (high-high Moran’s I) zones. An eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) model followed by SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) analysis was used to determine the importance of 100 socioeconomic/environmental features in determining census-tract adjusted Z-scores.ResultsA total of 412 “High-High” and 356 “Low-Low” census-tracts were identified, which formed 4 distinct “Healthy Aging” clusters in northeast Ohio. “Healthy Aging” clusters were associated with less pollution, less noisy environments, lower rates of depression and disability, higher vegetation index, greater healthcare utilization, and greater economic/financial opportunity.ConclusionsOur analysis implies geospatial clustering of healthy cardiovascular aging and provides evidence of a “healthy” census-tract phenotype, supporting the importance of exposomic factors in determining cardiovascular health.Keywords: Coronary artery calcium (CAC) score, Healthy aging, Cardiovascular health, Spatial analysis, Exposome, Cardiovascular risk factors

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains the leading global cause of mortality and a major determinant of premature aging [1]. Although the past several decades have witnessed progress in reducing age-adjusted cardiovascular mortality rates, recent data suggest a plateauing of this trend, with persisting—and in some cases widening—disparities in cardiovascular outcomes across geographic, racial, and socioeconomic lines [2,3]. These observations highlight the urgent need for novel approaches that go beyond conventional clinical risk factor identification and management to encompass the broader environmental, social, and behavioral contexts in which individuals live and age [4,5].

One such paradigm is the exposome, defined as the totality of environmental (including social and physical environments), psychosocial, and lifestyle exposures encountered over a lifetime, and their interactions with genetic and biological variables or a time-place-person context [[4], [5], [6], [7], [8]]. Exposomic approaches emphasize upstream primordial determinants of health many of each are place based, including air pollution, green space, noise, access to healthcare, walkability, and psychosocial stressors, which have been increasingly linked to CVD risk and outcomes [9]. Many recently published statements on the exposome and cardiovascular health, highlight the relevance of identifying, measuring and mitigating adverse environmental exposures to reduce cardiovascular risk and promoting health equity [4,5,8,10,11].

To date, the majority of cardiovascular prevention has focused on identifying risk factors that increase disease burden. Far fewer studies have explored the inverse—factors associated with resistance to disease or the maintenance of cardiovascular health into older age [12]. Among the various biomarkers and investigations that have emerged, to predict the future risk for cardiovascular disease, coronary artery calcium score (CAC) is perhaps the best known tool for precision prediction of cardiovascular risk [13,14]. Importantly the negative predictive value of a zero coronary calcium score has been extensively investigated and reported. Multiple prior studies have demonstrated that individuals with a CAC score = 0 are at significantly lower risk for a future cardiovascular event [15,16]. Hence, CAC score = 0 in an elderly individual may be considered as a marker of cardiovascular resilience; studies have found that a near zero CAC score resulted in significantly enhanced biological aging compared to chronological age [17,18], and thus is used as a marker in our study for “healthy” arterial aging, though it is important to emphasize that these individuals remain susceptible to other non-calcific mechanisms of atherosclerotic and cardiovascular disease.

We hypothesize that spatial and exposomic factors, including area-level social, demographic, and environmental characteristics are common to patients with healthy aging. Hence, in this study, we used local indicators of spatial autocorrelation (LISA) to identify spatial clusters of “cardiovascular healthy aging” in northeast Ohio—regions with significantly higher-than-expected prevalence of CAC = 0 among elderly individuals (age ≥ 65)—followed by a machine learning approach to determine the nature of the exposomic features that best predict these geographic clusters of cardiovascular health. Our goal in ascertaining these factors contributing to healthy cardiovascular aging is to construct population-level phenotypes consisting of ideal characteristics of a healthy population, which could provide valuable information for future public health, environmental, and urban planning.

Methods

Data acquisition and pre-processing

CAC score data

Given that Medicare does not reimburse CAC testing in the state of Ohio, University Hospitals Health System (UHHS), one of Ohio’s largest healthcare networks, started a system-wide low-charge CAC program ($99 per test) in 2014 followed by piloting of no-charge CAC in June 2015 (fully implemented starting in January 2017) [19]. CAC scoring was offered to all men 45 or older and women age 55 or older with no history of cardiovascular disease, and with one or more risk factors for heart disease, including: dyslipidemia, hypertension, smoking, diabetes, family history of coronary artery disease, or a systemic inflammatory state. CAC testing was offered at 21 radiology locations within the system, geographically distributed throughout northeast Ohio. The protocols for CAC acquisition were standardized across the system and followed protocols recommended by Society for Cardiovascular Computed Tomography (SCCT) [20]. Scans were read at a centralized reading facility for quantification. Patient data were captured using electronic medical records and maintained in a prospective registry: the Community Calcium Scoring Assessment for Cardiovascular Risk Stratification (CLARIFY, ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04075162). Informed consent was waived by the University Hospitals institutional review board for entry into the registry. For the present study, we included all patients enrolled from January 1st, 2014 through September 6, 2024. Supplemental methods include a more detailed description of the CLARIFY registry.

Spatial, socioeconomic, health, demographic, and environmental data

All data was compiled into census-tract level data using shapefiles with boundaries obtained from the 2020 United States Census Bureau. A total of 100 census-tract level socioeconomic, health, demographic, and environmental variables were compiled from multiple sources, consisting of: 36 health variables [21] (including cardiovascular and metabolic diseases, other chronic diseases, and various social determinants of health); 25 socioeconomic variables used to measure social vulnerability indices [22]; 5 environmental burden variables [23]; 24 socioeconomic, employment, and census-tract design variables [24]; 4 variables measuring low food access [25]; averaged 2020 light pollution [26]; averaged 2022 annual rail, road, and aviation noise [27]; annual ambient fine-airborne particulate matter (PM2.5) [28], airborne nitrogen dioxide (NO2) [29], and airborne ozone [29] averaged over the years 2010-2016; normalized difference vegetation index (NDVI) averaged from 2010-2016 [30,31]. Variable definitions and sources are provided in Supplemental Table 1. Of the 768 census-tracts included in the final analysis, there was a total of 3.4% missingness in the above described 100 features—to preserve spatiality of variables, missing data was imputed using the mean of all 1st order bordering census-tracts with complete data. Further details on the above features are provided in the supplemental methods.

Creation of older age cohort

A total of 31,544 elderly patients, aged ≥ 65, living in Ohio with CAC scores as part of the CLARIFY registry were identified. These patients were spread across 1,141 census-tracts, the majority of which were in northeast Ohio. Patients were then separated into two groups: patients with CAC = 0 were grouped into the “healthy” aging cohort (n = 7,932), and all others with CAC > 0 were grouped into the “non-optimal” aging cohort (n = 23,612).

Spatial analysis

For any tract i, the standardized healthy ratio (SHR), which is the equivalent of the commonly used statistic standardized mortality ratio (SMR), is calculated as:

Where O(Healthy)i is the observed ratio of healthy patients within census-tract i, and E(Healthy) is the expected ratio of healthy patients. Cluster analyses were then performed to assess for spatial association of tracts with high SHR (e.g. high rates of healthy coronary arteries).

Given the large number of census-tracts relative to the total number of healthy patients, there was overdispersion of healthy patients throughout the census-tracts, resulting in skewed SHR values. This was resolved by smoothing SHR values using a local empirical Bayes shrinkage estimator with respect to 1st order neighboring census-tracts (Supplemental Fig. 1) to stabilize noisy values, as described by RJ Marshall [[32], [33], [34], [35]]. We will refer to the resultant values of the local empirical Bayes estimation as the smoothed SHR.

Cluster analysis was conducted using Local Moran’s I, a Local Indicator of Spatial Association (LISA) used to assess for local clusters of similar or dissimilar values (e.g. local spatial autocorrelation) amongst neighboring census-tracts [32,33,36]. For each census-tract, Local Moran’s I was calculated with respect to the smoothed SHR of neighboring census-tracts within 3rd order contiguity (Supplemental Fig. 1) in order to assess for clusters of “healthy aging” populations. The use of 3rd order contiguity allowed for detection of spatial autocorrelation at a broader scale and preservation of heterogeneity of clusters after smoothing SHR using 1st order neighbors. 52 isolated census-tracts lacking any neighbors were excluded from this analysis. Census-tract Local Moran’s I values can then be segregated into one of 4 cluster types (described in supplemental methods). We label high-high clusters, which are census-tracts with higher-than-average smoothed SHR surrounded by a neighborhood with higher-than-average smoothed SHR, as “Healthy Aging” geographical clusters where there are higher rates of “healthy” coronaries. “Non-optimal Aging” clusters, or low-low clusters, are census-tracts with lower-than-average smoothed SHR and surrounded by a neighborhood with lower-than-average smoothed SHR, and thus have lower rates of “healthy” coronaries. High-low and low-high census-tracts are considered spatial outliers as they are dissimilar to their neighbors, and thus were excluded as our study aims to assess what characteristics are shared amongst census-tracts in neighborhoods with similarly “Healthy” or “Non-optimal” aging populations.

As the value of Local Moran’s I depends only on the census-tracts within the 3rd order neighborhood, the magnitudes of Local Moran’s I values cannot be compared across different neighborhoods. Instead, Z-scores are commonly calculated for each I allowing for the assessment of the statistical significance of spatial autocorrelation [36]. We calculated the Z-score for each I using the spdep package in R. Z-scores of were then adjusted to appropriately rank them from most significant “Non-optimal Aging” to most significant “Healthy Aging” cluster, separated by 0, as visualized below:

We refer to this as the adjusted Z-score. Increasingly negative adjusted Z-score indicates increasingly significant “non-optimal aging” census-tract, while increasingly positive adjusted Z-score indicates increasingly significant “healthy aging” census-tract. Further details on smoothed SHR, Local Moran’s I, Z-score, and adjusted Z-score calculations and descriptions of cluster types are included in the supplemental methods.

Machine learning analysis

Given its robustness to overfitting and ability to handle complex, high-dimensional models, an eXtreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost) algorithm was used to assess for associations of the above described 100 census-tract level features with the significance of “healthy” or “non-optimal” aging of all census tracts [37]. This was performed using adjusted Z-score as the continuous dependent variable regressed against census-tract level features as the predictors. The final XGBoost model used was trained using a randomly selected training set of 80% of the total data, and validated using a random testing set of 20% of the final data. To interpret the final XGBoost regression model we applied SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) analysis, an approach commonly used in machine learning model interpretation which decomposes the model’s outcome predictions into the sum of predictor effects, thus allowing for the characterization and visualization of each predictor’s contribution to the model [38].

Sensitivity analyses

Local Moran’s I calculated using un-smoothed SHR showed similar census-tract clusters with Local Moran’s I p-value < 0.10 when compared to that calculated using smoothed SHR, however, as expected, there were significant irregularities in clusters with less significant Local Moran’s I. There was no significant difference in spatial clusters or final SHAP analysis results when Local Moran’s I was calculated using 2nd order neighbors as compared to 3rd order neighbors. XGBoost model validation was performed as above, ensuring model generalizability. Validation using 5- and 10-fold cross-validation as well as re-training the XGBoost model following feature pruning by using SHAP feature importance to select for important features did not significantly affect the final conclusions of our study. The above analyses were repeated using CAC = 10 as the cutoff for “healthy” versus “non-optimal” to control for possible noise in lower CAC values, but this resulted in no significant difference in spatial clusters of “Healthy Aging”/”Non-optimal Aging” zones, and thus the more strict CAC = 0 cutoff was used in the final results. Subgroup analyses for age (65-75 vs >75 years), sex (male vs female), race (white vs non-white), smoking (smoking history vs never smoker), and metabolic disease (history of diabetes, HTN, obesity, or DLD vs no metabolic disease) subgroups. Results of SHAP analysis did not change significantly though routine health maintenance (check-ups, cholesterol screening, CRC screening) was considerably more important in white and age > 75 groups. Locations of “Healthy Aging” zones did not change in most subgroups, except in the no metabolic disease subgroup due to very small sample size (n = 2,200).

Statistical software

R 4.4.1 and associated statistical packages were used for all statistical analyses and Fig. generation [39].

Results

Baseline characteristics

Of the 31,544 patients aged ≥ 65 years included in the study, 7,932 patients had CAC = 0 (“healthy” cohort) and 23,612 patients had CAC > 0 (“non-optimal” cohort). Median sample size per census-tract was 15 (SD = 34.1), and the median number of healthy patients per census-tract 4 (SD = 9.2). Compared to the “non-optimal” aging cohort, the “healthy” aging cohort was made up of significantly more females (Table 1). “Healthy” agers had lower rates of smoking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes mellitus, lower BMI, systolic blood pressures, lower baseline creatinine and hemoglobin A1c. There were higher rates of anti-hypertensive use in “non-optimal” agers, even amongst those with clinically diagnosed hypertension. “Healthy agers” had lower rates of statin use, even when diagnosed with dyslipidemia, while those with dyslipidemia had higher LDL and HDL. “Healthy agers” with diabetes mellitus had significantly lower hemoglobin A1c values and lower insulin dependence.

Clusters become much more apparent following Local Empirical Bayes smoothing of SHR (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, the distribution of smoothed SHR values is well centered around its mean of 0.246 in comparison to the highly skewed distribution of the raw SHR values (Supplemental Fig. 2).

Machine learning analysis of factors associated with clusters

The final XGBoost model fit the training data with root mean square error (RMSE) 0.41 and coefficient of determination (R [2]) 0.98, and showed fair generalizability, with good goodness of fit to the test data with RMSE 1.01 and R [2] of 0.68. Fig. 4 shows the feature importance and SHAP values for each of the top 30 most important features in the final XGBoost model fit to the full dataset. Fine (≤ 2.5 µm) airborne particulate matter (PM2.5) was found to be the most important feature in model prediction, with lower PM2.5 values being associated with increased census-tract “healthy aging.” Other environmental factors associated with “healthy” aging were lower diesel emissions (Diesel PM), lower airborne nitric oxide (NO2), higher airborne ozone (O3), lower transportation noise levels, and higher averaged annual vegetation index (NDVI). Health factors associated with census-tract “healthiness” were lower prevalence of depression, higher healthcare utilization (routine checkup), lower rates of overall disability (% disability), and lower rates of sleep deprivation. Demographic, socioeconomic, and infrastructural variables associated with census-tract “healthiness” were increased regional centrality indices (Worker Proximity, Regional Centrality_jobs, Regional Centrality_workers), higher African American percentage (% Black) and percentage of minorities (% Minority), and higher weekly wages (% High Wages). Other weaker/less clear associations were possibly lower mammogram use, higher job proximity, and higher social isolation with increased “healthiness” of census-tracts.

Discussion

Our study identified distinct geographic “Healthy Aging Zones” in northeast Ohio—areas where older adults (≥65) were significantly more likely to have a zero coronary artery calcium (CAC = 0) score, a marker of vascular resilience. Four major clusters of healthy aging were found, suggesting that cardiovascular health is not randomly distributed but highly structured by place-based factors. Using XGBoost and SHAP analyses, the study found that lower air pollution (specifically, PM₂.₅, NO₂, and diesel particulate matter), lower noise levels, and higher green vegetation (NDVI) were the most important environmental correlates of healthy aging. This supports the idea that cleaner, greener, and quieter environments foster vascular longevity. Census tracts with healthier cardiovascular aging also showed lower rates of depression and disability, higher healthcare utilization, greater economic opportunity (high wages, job centrality), and more racial diversity (higher % minority and Black populations). These findings emphasize that social connectedness, access, and opportunity—not merely wealth—contribute to healthy cardiovascular aging. Our findings provide evidence for an important influence of place-based contextual factors as important determinants of cardiovascular aging.

Fine particulate matter (PM2.5) emerged as the strongest predictor in the final model, with lower concentrations linked to “healthy aging.” This aligns with a substantial body of evidence showing that chronic exposure to PM₂.₅ is associated with aging and increased cardiovascular morbidity and mortality [40,41]. In addition to low PM2.5, lower measures of traffic-related pollutants, such as diesel emissions and nitric oxide (NO₂) levels, were also associated with healthier clusters, consistent with prior studies linking traffic emissions to adverse health outcomes and increased cardiovascular disease burden [42,43]. Interestingly, these findings were in the setting of median PM2.5 and NO2 levels significantly below maximum acceptable US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) in both “Healthy” and “Non-optimal Aging” census-tracts, highlighting the detriment of pollution levels at levels previously deemed to be acceptable. While averaged ozone (O3) levels showed a paradoxical positive association with “healthy” clusters, the effects of ozone on cardiovascular disease are complex and not well understood and there may be seasonal/spatial confounding or effect modification with co-pollutants such as NO2 and PM2.5 [44,45].

Another key feature in predicting census-tract “healthiness” was the prevalence of clinical depression, highlighting the importance of mental health and its related factors to cardiovascular health. This is further linked to our finding that “healthy aging” was associated with lower sleep deprivation, greater healthcare utilization, and lower rates of overall disability. These conclusions are consistent with previous studies which suggested that access to and utilization of preventive care may be critical to community-level health outcomes [46,47]. Green space exposure also encourages healthier, more active lifestyles and has been linked to reduced all-cause mortality and improved mental health [48,49], which is consistent with our finding that mean annual NDVI values were positively associated with “Healthy Aging” clusters.

Socioeconomic and demographic variables played a substantial role in cluster outcomes. Interestingly, higher proportions of African American and minority populations were associated with “healthy aging” in this model—possibly reflecting local resilience factors or inherent referral biases in our study design that offered the test albeit free and attracted a certain demographic profile. Higher weekly wages and enhanced infrastructural features and regional centrality also correlated with healthier clustering, supporting the well-established relationship between economic opportunity and community health [50].

Study limitations

To our knowledge this study is the largest such study to evaluate the distribution of healthy aging clusters, using coronary calcium as a cardiovascular marker of aging. Despite this, the study findings only assessed clusters with high-high and low-low Local Moran’s I, which excluded 321 census-tracts that were considered outliers. Though this is a valid approach to answer our study’s question, future studies on what characteristics result in high-low versus low-high clusters may provide interesting insights into resilience of specific populations to disease. Furthermore, the regional scope of our study was limited to northeast Ohio—this beckons for larger-scale studies that include geographically diverse and spatially vast regions. While CAC scoring is an important marker of coronary artery health has consistently been shown to be a strong predictor of cardiovascular morbidity and mortality, future steps will be to generalize our findings to other cardiovascular risk indices. Finally, although study participants were offered the study for free or low cost, the nature of the program may have inherent “referral” biases in favoring individuals self-interested in optimizing their health. Conclusions

This study found that healthy-aging geographic clusters were characterized by specific exposomic factors: lower rates of airborne pollution and emissions, enhanced mental health and its commonly associated factors such as healthcare utilization, better sleep, and greenspace exposure, and economic opportunity and enhanced infrastructure and regional centrality. These findings highlight important policy-level and clinically actionable changes to enhance biological aging of the general population and thus improve morbidity associated with chronological age. While many countries have enacted clean air policies to limit ambient air pollution levels, it is crucial to recognize there is no minimum target for air pollution as this study and others show significant detriments to health at levels well below federally deemed “acceptable” levels [51], highlighting the importance of continuing to reduce allowable pollution levels. Furthermore, a health-mindful infrastructure in city planning is a fundamental step in optimizing the exposome, including isolation of the general population from busy roadways to reduce pollution and noise exposure, emphasis on greenspace inclusion and upkeep, and prioritizing regional centrality. At the clinical level, it is important for providers to recognize exposomic risk factors as significant detriments to health and aging. This will allow providers to educate patients on avoidance strategies, such as re-routing commutes, exercising away from major roads, and use of indoor filtration systems or masks in areas of high ambient air pollution. Additionally, our findings suggest mental health as a key factor in enhancing biological aging, which emphasizes the importance of mental health check-ups at routine primary care visits. By promoting environments that incorporate these factors, clinicians, public health officials, policy-makers, and city planners can forward the effort to enhance healthy aging populations, thus reducing disease burden and cutting healthcare costs.
