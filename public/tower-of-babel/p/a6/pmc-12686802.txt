Translation and validation of the Chinese version of the adolescent and young adult psycho-oncology screening tool
Authors: Ning Qin, Juan Luo, Yuxuan Li, Huiyi Zhang, Yinglong Duan, Qinqin Cheng, Jianfei Xie, Andy SK Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveAdolescents and young adults (AYAs) with cancer suffer from a high prevalence of psychological distress, but validated, age-appropriate screening tools remain limited in China. The study aimed to translate and validate a Chinese version of the Adolescent and Young Adult Psycho-Oncology Screening Tool (AYA-POST).MethodsA methodological study was conducted, including forward and back translation, expert consultation, and a pilot testing. AYAs with cancer (n ​= ​301; 15–39 years at diagnosis; Mage ​= ​34.00 ± 5.99 years) across two hospitals in China from September 2024 to April 2025 were included. Measures included sociodemographic, clinical data, the AYA-POST (comprising the Distress Thermometer [DT] and AYA Needs Assessment [AYA-NA]), and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS).ResultsAll content validity indices were 1.0. Exploratory factor analysis yielded a four-factor solution explained 35% of the total variance, with a mean item complexity of 1.89. The DT correlated strongly with the HADS (r ​= ​0.533, P ​< ​0.001). A DT cut-off score of 4 showed optimal sensitivity (0.774; area under the curve ​= ​0.736). Cronbach's α was 0.754 for AYA-POST and ranged from 0.315 to 0.704 at domain level. The prevalence of psychological distress was 57.14% with a cut-off score of 4. The most frequent concerns were physical (83.72%), emotional (73.42%), and practical (66.78%).ConclusionsThe Chinese version of the AYA-POST demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability in AYAs with cancer. It can be a tool for stratified distress and psychosocial concerns management, with a DT cut-off score of 4 recommended for initial screening.Keywords: Adolescent and young adult, Cancer, Psycho-oncology, Psychological distress, Cross-cultural comparisons, Validation study

Introduction

Cancer is the second leading cause of death worldwide and the leading cause in China, with an increasing trend toward earlier age at onset.1 Among adolescent and young adults (AYAs, aged 15–39 years, as defined by the U.S. National Cancer Institute),2 it is the fourth leading cause of death and the tenth leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) globally.3 In 2022, over 1.3 million new cases and 377,000 deaths were reported in this group.4 Psychological distress (PD), considered the sixth vital sign in cancer care, after pulse, temperature, respiration, blood pressure and pain,5 is associated with tumor prognosis and recovery.6 The National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) defined PD as a multifactorial unpleasant experience that may involve psychological, social, spiritual, and/or physical components.7 It exists along a continuum, ranging from common emotional responses (such as sadness, fear) to severe and potentially disabling psychiatric conditions (such as depression, anxiety, social isolation, and spiritual crises). These experiences can occur at any stage of the cancer trajectory, from diagnosis to survivorship.7 Thus, precise evaluation and effective management of PD are critical for improving the quality of life of cancer patients.

Despite its critical importance, the current tools for assessing PD have notable limitations, particularly for AYAs with cancer. Several validated instruments are available to screen for PD in cancer patients,8 including the Distress Thermometer (DT, recommended by the NCCN),7 the Kessler PD scales (K6 and K10), the Brief Symptom Inventory-18 (BSI-18), and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS). However, most of these tools were initially developed for adult populations and may not fully capture the specific psychosocial support needs of AYAs with cancer. AYAs constitute a distinct subgroup with age-specific challenges, such as concerns about fertility, sexual health,9 long-term treatment side effects,10 financial hardship, disruptions to family and social roles,11 and difficulties returning to work.12,13 These challenges compound the psychosocial burden associated with cancer, making it essential to develop tools that address their specific needs.

Without timely identification and intervention, PD can develop into more serious mental health conditions,14,15 interrupt treatment, and further increase self injury or mortality risk.16,17 As a result, AYAs require tailored approaches to psychological assessment and support. Globally, AYAs with cancer experience elevated levels of PD, with a prevalence averaging 32%—a rate four times higher than that of older adults.3,18 In Asia, the prevalence is even higher, reaching 71% (95% CI: 52–84%).8 However, there is currently no specific screening tool available for the assessment of PD among AYAs with cancer in China.

To address this gap, Palmer et al.19 adapted the NCCN's DT (Version 1.2011) in 2014 to develop the Adolescent and Young Adult Psycho-Oncology Screening Tool (AYA-POST). AYA-POST consists of the DT and the AYA Needs Assessment (AYA-NA), a checklist identifying biological, psychological, and social concerns specific to AYAs with cancer. In 2021, Patterson et al.20 validated the AYA-POST among AYAs with cancer from 28 cancer centers across four countries, identified a DT score of 5 as the optimal clinical cut-off point, using the HADS as the criterion measure, with a sensitivity of 82% and a specificity of 75%. The validation demonstrated strong construct validity, with a significant correlation between DT and HADS-Total scores (r ​= ​0.65, P ​< ​0.001), as well as acceptable discriminant validity, as distressed participants endorsed significantly more concerns on the AYA-NA than non-distressed participants (67% vs. 20% reporting 10 or more concerns). Furthermore, in 2022, Patterson et al.21 evaluated its clinical utility and explored barriers to its implementation in health service systems, further confirming its value in triaging and tailoring care for AYAs with cancer. However, variations in optimal DT cut-off scores across regions raise questions about its applicability in specific cultural contexts.22 For example, a DT cut-off score of four is recommended for Asian cancer patients in a systematic review.23 And its suitability for Chinese AYAs remains unclear. Given the distinct sociocultural, linguistic, and health care system differences, a direct translation with cross-cultural adaptation may benefit the accuracy of distress detection and the response of psychosocial interventions. To date, no validated, age-specific, and culturally adapted screening tool exists for assessing PD in Chinese AYAs with cancer.

Therefore, this study aimed to translate and validate the Chinese version of the AYA-POST among AYAs with cancer. Specifically, this study sought to examine the validity and reliability, identify the optimal DT cut-off score using the HADS as the criterion, and examined the prevalence and correlates of PD, as well as specific endorsed concerns in Chinese AYAs with cancer.

Methods

Study design and setting

This methodological study was guided by the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) statement. The study was conducted at two university-affiliated tertiary hospitals in central and southern China from September 2024 to April 2025. One hospital was a general hospital with 2200 beds and 62 nursing units, whereas the other was a specialized oncology hospital designated as a National Cancer Regional Medical Center, comprising 2242 beds and 41 clinical departments.

Sample

A convenience sampling method was employed. Hospitalized patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (a) diagnosed with primary cancer for the first time between the ages of 15 and 39 years; (b) aware of their diagnosis; and (c) able to communicate and read Mandarin Chinese. Patients were excluded if they had severe cognitive impairment, a current diagnosis of a major psychiatric disorder, or were concurrently enrolled in other psychological intervention trials. Only hospitalized patients were included because those inpatient settings provided a stable clinical environment that facilitated consistent administration of the instrument, and adequate time for data collection. As this study was focused on translation and psychometric validation, a relatively controlled setting was preferred to minimize external variability. Additionally, discharged patients were not included because they often experience increased fatigue and require a period of recovery following treatment, which may limit their ability to complete self-reported questionnaires accurately and attentively.

Developing the Chinese version of the AYA-POST

Forward and back translation

AYA-POST, developed by Palmer et al.19 in 2014 and validated across multiple countries in 2021, consisting of the DT and the AYA-NA. The DT was a single-item visual analog scale (“thermometer”) ranging from 0 (“no distress”) to 10 (“extreme distress”) assessing overall PD over the past week. Patients indicated their distress level by circling a number, with higher scores indicating higher distress levels. The AYA-NA was a 51-item checklist of specific concerns that may contribute to the distress of AYAs with cancer. To be specific, 50 items were dichotomous items, and one item was an open-ended question. Scoring involved summing the number of “yes” responses within each domain and across the full checklist. Items were grouped into six domains: Practical, Family, Emotional, Physical, Social and Information concerns. Total concern counts and domain-specific frequencies could be calculated to identify key areas of unmet need. It was developed in collaboration with experienced clinical staff and AYAs with cancer, providing good content validity.21

This study was authorized by the original author of AYA-POST, Prof. Palmer. The translation process followed the standardized cross-cultural adaptation guidelines of Beaton et al.24 First, two bilingual native Chinese psycho-oncology researchers independently translated the original English version, generating two forward translations (T1 and T2), which were synthesized into a single version (T12) by a nursing scholar with expertise in cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric validation. Semantic discrepancies were discussed and resolved within the translation team, resulting in the preliminary Chinese version (V1). Next, two bilingual researchers, one professor at Western Sydney University and the other a research assistant with IELTS certification, who were blinded to the original and independently back-translated V1 into English (BT1 and BT2). These back-translations were reviewed and consolidated (BT12) by the research team, ensuring semantic and conceptual equivalence with the original version. The consolidated back-translation was then reviewed by Prof. Palmer to confirm its accuracy and cultural relevance. Based on the feedback, necessary revisions were made, yielding version 2 (V2), which includes 50 dichotomous items and one open-ended question for expert panel review.

Expert consultations

An expert consultation was conducted to review the Chinese Version 2, consisting of six interdisciplinary experts with PhDs: four with expertise in oncology nursing, one in psychology, and one in clinical oncology. And three out of six had experience in scale development and psychometric. The consultation reviewed each item of the AYA-POST and reached a consensus on any remaining discrepancies. The experts were asked to assess each item based on four aspects: relevance, semantic equivalence, cultural appropriateness, and clarity, drawing upon their professional knowledge and clinical experience. Two rounds of expert consultation were conducted. In the first round, 21 suggestions were collected. After discussions within the research team and consultation with clinical AYAs with cancer for feedback, 18 suggestions were adopted, resulting in 13 specific revisions. In the second round, one additional minor revision was made based on expert feedback, leading to the development of the Chinese Version 3 (V3) for pilot testing, which comprises 56 dichotomous items and one open-ended question.

Pilot testing

A pilot testing was conducted to assess the comprehensibility and clarity of the Chinese AYA-POST (V3). A convenience sample of 29 AYAs with cancer (age range: 17–40, Mage ​= ​32.93 ​± ​6.41 years) was recruited in October 2024. The Chinese version of the AYA-POST was administered via cognitive interviewing techniques. During the questionnaire completion process, trained investigators asked participants whether they understood the instructions and item content and whether the response scale was straightforward and easy to answer. The completion time was recorded, and patients were invited to provide feedback and suggestions on the tool. Finally, fifteen patients provided their suggestions, and a total of 14 revisions were made, resulting in a refined version - Chinese Version 4 (V4). And compared with the original version (50 dichotomous items and one open-ended question), the Chinese Version 4 has expanded to 56 dichotomous items with one open-ended question. For details about all the revisions of the AYA-POST tool, please refer to Supplementary File 1. For more specific details about the revisions in the pilot testing, please refer to Supplementary File 2. Moreover, the Chinese Version 4 (V4) and the original version of the AYA-POST tool were included together in the Supplementary File 3 for a clearer presentation of the contrast.

Cross-sectional study for psychometric validation

Measures

The questionnaire consisted of the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, the Chinese version of the AYA-POST (the Chinese Version 4, V4), and the HADS.

Sociodemographic and clinical characteristics

A self-designed questionnaire was used to collect sociodemographic and clinical characteristics, including age, gender, marital status, fertility status, education, employment status, monthly income, insurance status, residence, diagnosis duration, cancer stage, therapeutic methods, and cancer type, on the basis of variables commonly reported in previous literature.25, 26, 27

Chinese version of the AYA-POST (V4)

Prior to psychometric validation, the AYA-POST (V4) had undergone forward and backward translation, expert consultation, and pilot testing to ensure conceptual and linguistic equivalence. The use of the instrument at this stage aimed to collect data for psychometric validation.

Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)

The HADS, developed by Sigmond and Snaith28 in 1983, was a widely used instrument for assessing anxiety disorders and depression symptoms in hospital patients over the past month. A meta-analysis found that approximately three-quarters of DT validation studies used the HADS as a criterion.22 Similarly, Palmer et al.20 used the HADS to establish DT cut-off scores for the AYA-POST. Therefore, we also adopted the HADS as the criterion in this study. The HADS had been previously validated in Chinese cancer populations, demonstrating good psychometric properties (Cronbach's α for the total scale, anxiety subscale and depression subscale were 0.929, 0.874 and 0.874 respectively).29 The HADS consisted of 14 items, including two subscales: anxiety (HADS-A) and depression (HADS-D), with seven items each. Each item score ranged from 0 to 3, yielding subscale scores ranging from 0 to 21 and a total score ranging from 0 to 42, with higher scores indicating more severe symptoms. Previous research had recommended cut-off scores of ≥ 9 for HADS-A, ≥ 7 for HADS-D, and ≥ 15 for the HADS-Total to indicate clinically significant levels of anxiety, depression, and overall distress.30,31 In this study, the Cronbach's α coefficients for the HADS total scale and subscales were 0.858 (HADS-Total), 0.823 (HADS-A) and 0.723 (HADS-D) respectively.

Sample size

For exploratory factor analysis (EFA), it was widely recommended in psychometric studies that the sample size ranged between 5 and 10 times the number of items in the scale.31,32 Given that the AYA-NA scale contains 56 dichotomous items, the recommended minimum sample size is 280 participants. To account for a potential 5% invalid response rate, the target sample size was adjusted to 295 participants.

Data collection

Participants were recruited by four standardized trained investigators through the health care system during routine inpatient visits. Eligible AYAs were informed about the study purpose and procedures, and those who agreed to participate provided informed consent. Then, the participants were invited to complete self-report questionnaires online via the Wenjuanxing platform (www.wjx.cn) or on paper. Investigators were available to assist patients in reading or interpreting the questionnaires if any difficulties arose. To prevent missing data, most questionnaire items were set as mandatory fields, requiring participants to respond before submitting. The system also restricted multiple submissions from the same device or IP address to reduce the risk of duplicate responses.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted on SPSS 27.0 (SPSS Inc, Chicago, IL) and R version 4.4.1, with P ​< ​0.05 considered statistically significant. Missing data were eliminated. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the participants' characteristics. Continuous variables were presented as the means ​± ​standard deviation (SD), and categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). We evaluated content validity, construct validity, criterion-related validity, Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) analysis, internal consistency, split-half reliability.

Validity

The types of validity included content validity, construct validity, criterion-related validity, and ROC analysis. Content validity was evaluated based on all six experts' ratings on a four-point Likert scale. The item-level (I-CVI) and scale-level content validity indices (S-CVI/UA, S-CVI/Ave) were calculated, with thresholds of I-CVI > 0.78, S-CVI/UA ​> ​0.80, and S-CVI/Ave > 0.90 indicating satisfactory content validity.33 In this study, EFA was conducted on the AYA-NA scale. Given that the items are dichotomous, a tetrachoric correlation matrix was computed to reflect the underlying continuous relationships. Factor extraction was performed using the maximum likelihood (ML) method, followed by Oblimin oblique rotation to allow for correlations among factors. The number of factors was determined based on the Kaiser criterion (eigenvalues > 1), scree plot inspection, and the proportion of variance explained. According to previous studies on dichotomous scales, a cumulative explained variance of approximately 35% can be considered acceptable.34 Considering that the AYA-NA assesses multidimensional and complex distress, items may load on multiple factors. Therefore, conducting a confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) under the assumption that each item strictly corresponds to a single factor may not accurately reflect the underlying structure. Accordingly, this study primarily relied on EFA to explore the latent factor structure among items, providing a basis for subsequent scale refinement and potential CFA. Criterion-related validity was assessed through Spearman correlations analysis between AYA-POST scores and HADS total/subscale scores. ROC analysis was used to determine the optimal DT cut-off score for detecting distress (HADS score ≥ 15).20 The sensitivity, specificity, Youden's index and area under the curve (AUC) were computed. The screening accuracy was considered acceptable with sensitivity and specificity ≥ 0.70.35 The optimal DT cut-off score gained through the ROC analysis was used to calculate the proportion indicate the prevalence of PD in this sample.

Reliability

The type of reliability included the internal consistency reliability and split-half reliability of the Chinese version of the AYA-POST and its domains, via Cronbach's α and Spearman-Brown coefficients (according to an odd–even item division approach), respectively. Values were 0.70 and above indicated acceptable reliability.36, 37, 38 For dichotomous items, internal consistency reliability was evaluated using Cronbach's α coefficients, which is equivalent to KR-20 for binary data.

Associations of psychological distress

Chi-square analyses were conducted to explore associations between PD and sociodemographic or clinical characteristics, as well as to examine group differences in item and domain level responses between distressed and non-distressed participants. Bonferroni correction was applied to adjust for multiple comparisons (α ​= ​0.05/50 ​= ​0.001).

Most participants were married (71.76%) and had children (69.10%). The most frequent primary cancer types were cervical (20.60%), breast (20.27%), and ovarian (16.28%) cancer. Validity results

The item “Alcohol or drug abuse” had an endorsement rate of 0%, indicating that it was not applicable or culturally relevant in the Chinese medical context and was therefore removed from the scale. Regarding content validity, the results from two rounds of expert's consultation revealed that all content validity indices met the highest thresholds: the I-CVI, S-CVI/UA, and S-CVI/Ave were all 1.0, suggesting excellent content validity. For the criterion-related validity, the mean total HADS score among participants was 12.35 ​± ​7.16, with mean scores of 6.39 ​± ​3.87 for HADS-A and 5.96 ​± ​4.04 for HADS-D.

EFA was conducted on the 55 dichotomous items (removing the item ‘Alcohol or drug abuse’) of the AYA-NA scale, yielding a four-factor solution that explained 35% of the total variance, with a mean item complexity of 1.89. Although solutions with 3–6 factors were examined, the four-factor model was deemed optimal based on its superior interpretability and stability. The four-factor structure effectively captured the multidimensionality of the AYA-NA scale while accounting for the interrelated nature of distress dimensions: ML1 (Physical and Information distress), ML2 (Familial and Emotional distress), ML3 (Cross-domain distress), and ML4 (Physical distress). Among these, ML2 contributed the largest proportion of variance (12%), followed by ML3 (9%), ML1 (8%), and ML4 (6%). Supplementary Table S1 presents the standardized factor loadings, communalities, and uniqueness values for each item. ML1 (“Moderate Physical-Emotional Distress”) was associated with moderate loadings in the Physical (0.24) and Emotional (0.20) domains, reflecting a combination of bodily and emotional distress. ML2 (“Prominent Emotional Distress”) demonstrated the highest loading in the Emotional domain (0.47), with secondary contributions from the Physical (0.32) and Information (0.26) domains, emphasizing the centrality of emotional distress. ML3 (“Family-Centered Multidimensional Distress”) was strongly linked to the Family domain (0.55) and showed notable associations with Practical (0.36) and Social (0.26) domains, highlighting the interplay of familial, practical, and social challenges. Lastly, ML4 (“Moderate Social-Physical Distress”) primarily captured distress related to the Physical (0.28) and Social (0.22) domains, indicating moderate associations with bodily and social concerns. Supplementary Fig. S1 illustrates the correspondence between the AYA-NA domains and the four-factor EFA results. Supplementary Fig. S2 displays the scree plots obtained through parallel analysis for the AYA-NA scale.

Spearman correlations analyses revealed a strong positive correlation between the DT scores and HADS-Total scores (r ​= ​0.533, P ​< ​0.001). Significant positive correlations were also observed between the DT scores and HADS-A scores (r ​= ​0.549, P ​< ​0.001) and between the DT scores and HADS-D scores (r ​= ​0.446, P ​< ​0.001).

Reliability results

The Cronbach's α coefficients for the Chinese versions of the AYA-POST and AYA-NA were 0.754 and 0.806 respectively, indicating acceptable internal consistency. The domain-level Cronbach's α coefficients of the AYA-NA were 0.464 (Practical), 0.606 (Family), 0.627 (Emotional), 0.315 (Social), 0.704 (Physical) and 0.462 (Information), suggesting variable reliability across the domains. In the Social domain, the item–total correlation analysis of five items with the overall subscale ranged from 0.318 to 0.795. Deleting each of these items, the Cronbach's α coefficients ranged from 0.198 to 0.286 (Supplementary Table S2), indicating that removing any item caused a reduction in the reliability coefficient of the Social domain. The Spearman-Brown coefficients according to an odd–even item division approach for split-half reliability were 0.642 for AYA-POST and 0.822 for AYA-NA, with domain-specific coefficients ranging from 0.405 to 0.735. Details of the Cronbach's α and Spearman-Brown coefficients for each domain were presented in Table 3.

Rates and associations of psychological distress

With a DT cut-off score of 4, the overall prevalence of PD was 57.14%. A higher prevalence of PD was observed among AYAs with cancer who were married (68.02%; χ2 ​= ​2.767, P ​= ​0.096), had children (65.12%; χ2 ​= ​2.988, P ​= ​0.084), had lower income levels (< 5000 CNY; 68.02%; χ2 ​= ​6.693, P ​= ​0.082), and had insurance (90.12%; χ2 ​= ​5.226, P ​= ​0.022). The sociodemographic and clinical characteristics of the participants in the PD and non-PD groups, classified according to the DT cut-off of 4, were presented in Supplementary Table S3. The domain-specific and item-level endorsement rates among the participants in the PD group were presented in Supplementary Table S4.

Endorsement of the AYA-NA

Nearly all AYA participants (97.00%) endorsed at least one concern on the AYA-NA. Domain-level and item-specific endorsement rates for the AYA-NA item of all the participants are presented in Table 4. The most prevalent concerns were related to Physical (83.72%), Emotional (73.42%) and Practical concerns (66.78%). Among the individual items, the highest endorsement rates were observed for “Medical expenses” (49.17%), “Anxiety” (38.87%), “Hair loss” (34.55%), “Work or career” (26.91%), “Eating or appetite” (26.25%) and “Sleeping difficulty” (25.58%). And the lowest endorsement rates were observed for the less five concerns were “Alcohol or drug abuse” (0.00%), “Painful sexual activity” (1.66%), “Worry about boy/girlfriend” (2.33%), “Other family members” (2.66%), “Loss of faith or belief” (2.66%), “Feeling excluded from treatment decisions” (2.66%). Additionally, the sociodemographic and clinical characteristics associated with high-frequency items are presented in Supplementary Table S5. AYAs with cancer who were unemployed or had lower income were more likely to report distress about medical expenses. Those diagnosed within one month to one year, at stage II, and receiving chemotherapy were more likely to report concerns about hair loss.

Discussion

Main findings

This study is the first to report the cross-cultural adaptation and psychometric validation of the Chinese version of the AYA-POST, which demonstrated acceptable reliability and validity as a cancer-specific screening tool for PD among AYAs with cancer. To enhance its clinical utility, an optimal cut-off score for DT was identified, with a threshold of four yielding a sensitivity of 0.774 and an AUC of 0.736, indicating better sensitivity than the cut-off of 5 and suggesting improved suitability for routine clinical screening. The AYA-NA was also validated for identifying specific psychosocial concerns. Our findings revealed a high prevalence of PD and notable domain-specific concerns across the sample and among subgroups. Notably, substantial interindividual variability was found in the concerns endorsed, underscoring the heterogeneity of psychosocial needs within this population.

This study demonstrated good content validity for the AYA-NA scale, with all indices exceeding established thresholds. EFA results supported construct validity, identifying a four-factor structure that explained 35% of the total variance: Moderate Physical-Emotional Distress, Prominent Emotional Distress, Family-Centered Multidimensional Distress and Moderate Social-Physical Distress. The mean item complexity of 1.89 suggested that items loaded significantly on two factors, reflecting the interconnected nature of distress dimensions. While the four-factor structure does not fully align with the original six-domain framework, it highlights meaningful relationships, such as the integration of physical and emotional, as well as social and physical distress. The Family-Centered Multidimensional Distress factor further underscores the complexity and interrelatedness of psychosocial factors among AYAs with cancer, consistent with previous findings.39 Future studies could use network analysis to identify core distress dimensions, which may facilitate more targeted and effective interventions. Moderate to strong positive correlations between the DT scores and the HADS-Total score (r ​= ​0.533), HADS-A score (r ​= ​0.549), and HADS-D score (r ​= ​0.446) support the construct validity. Notably, the correlation between the DT and HADS-A score was higher than that with HADS-D score, which is consistent with previous findings,20 suggesting that anxiety may be a more prominent component in this population and warrants greater attention in future interventions.

The ROC analysis revealed distinct diagnostic performances for DT cut-off scores of 4 and 5. While the Youden's J was marginally higher at 5 (0.360 vs. 0.328), the sensitivity at 4 was notably superior (0.774 vs. 0.704), making it more appropriate for early clinical screening, especially when high-risk identification was prioritized. Similarly, Recklitis et al.40 reported that a cut-off score of 4 most closely matched the recommended diagnostic criteria compared with a gold-standard structured psychiatric interview among AYAs with cancer. Furthermore, systematic reviews and meta-analyses evidence supports the widespread use of a score of 4 for self-reported distress among the cancer population41 and highlight its optimal balance of sensitivity and specificity for Asian cancer patients.23 While DT cut-off scores of 4 demonstrated optimal sensitivity and was preferred in this study, it is important to note that different thresholds may be appropriate depending on the intended use. For screening purposes, higher sensitivity is often prioritized to ensure patients with PD are not missed. In contrast, DT cut-off scores of 5, with higher specificity, may be more suitable for diagnostic applications to reduce false positives, especially in settings with limited mental health resources or when a confirmatory assessment is available.

For reliability, this study also demonstrated that the Chinese version of the AYA-POST exhibited acceptable internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α exceeding 0.70. The Spearman–Brown coefficient was slightly below 0.70, possibly because of the continuous variable item DT.37,38 However, the specific AYA-NA checklists had an acceptable Cronbach's α of 0.806 and a Spearman–Brown coefficient of 0.822. Notably, the item “Alcohol or drug abuse” had a 0% endorsement rate, suggesting cultural or contextual irrelevance in China, which may reflect the low alcohol consumption and prevalence of substance abuse and strict prescription policies. Furthermore, when this item was removed, the Cronbach's α of the scale hadn't changed, indicating a negligible effect on internal consistency. However, reliability across the subdomains of the AYA-NA was variable. In particular, the Social domain showed poor internal consistency, with a Cronbach's α of 0.315 and a Spearman–Brown coefficient of 0.405. The item–total correlation analysis of the Social domain indicated weak correlations of three items (r ​< ​0.4)42 and strong correlations of two items (r ​≥ ​0.4) with the overall subscale. This implies these items reflect distinct aspects of social well-being. Moreover, removing any item led to a reduction in the reliability coefficient. This implies, despite their weak representation of the underlying construct, each item contributes uniquely to the overall reliability and should not be removed without careful consideration. The relatively low reliability of the Social domain may reflect unaddressed aspects of social interactions within this construct, highlighting the need to refine or expand the item pool to better reflect its multidimensional nature. For instance, in the Chinese context, young people increasingly rely on online social media as a primary avenue for social interactions, social support, and maintaining relationships.43 To improve the Social domain, it is essential to broaden the item pool to capture diverse, age-specific social concerns. Incorporating qualitative research, such as exploring how cancer impacts online social media use, could further optimize the content and enhance the psychometric reliability and robustness of the Social domain.44

This study revealed that the prevalence of PD among AYAs with cancer was 57.14%, which was higher than the report by Wang et al., in 2022 (21.7%),45 but lower than findings from reported in 2021 (83.4%)46 and 2017 (89.1%).47 Several factors may explain these discrepancies. First, evidence suggests that female patients and those with gynecologic or breast cancer are more likely to experience higher PD, as supported by systematic reviews.48 While Wang's study predominantly included patients with head and neck cancer (64.1%), our sample included mainly gynecologic and breast cancer patients, which may account for the higher levels. Second, the lower PD rate in AYAs with cancer compared to earlier studies may reflect improvements in health literacy, psychosocial awareness, and supportive care availability among AYAs in recent years.49

The domain of physical, emotional and practical concerns were the most reported concerns among Chinese AYAs with cancer, which is consistent with findings from a recent cross-sectional study in China.45 Notably, within the Practical domain, the most highly endorsed item was “Medical expenses”. This may reflect challenges treatment-related employment disruption, and financial burden.50,51 For younger AYAs (aged 15–25 years), caregivers often assume partial or full responsibility for treatment costs despite this group beginning to develop financial independence.52 Another frequently reported item was “Hair loss”, a common and distressing side effect of cancer treatment. It has been associated with a high risk of PD, significant psychosocial impairment, and disruption of functional status and social relationships, especially for women.53,54 While social concerns were relatively mild in this study (22.92%), contrasting with the findings reported by Patterson et al.20 in AYAs aged 15–25 years (69.4%). One possible explanation for this discrepancy is the age distribution of the participants. In this study, over 80% of the participants were over 30, whereas Patterson's sample primarily comprised younger individuals. Younger AYAs with cancer may be more vulnerable to social challenges such as educational disruption, peer relationship difficulties, and identity development conflicts.55 In contrast, older AYAs with cancer may have already established more stable social roles, leading to lower perceived social disruption.20 Furthermore, analysis of the item-level differences between the PD and non-PD groups revealed that the Emotional domain and the item “Extreme exhaustion or tiredness” from the Physical domain were more frequently endorsed in the PD group. This finding is consistent with prior research indicating that fatigue is not only a common symptom among AYAs with cancer but also significantly impairs their quality of life. It compromises their ability to meet work demands and fulfill social and familial roles, often leading to frustration and PD.11,56

Implications for nursing practice and research

The validated Chinese version of the AYA-POST provides health care professionals with an efficient tool to identify PD and specific psychosocial concerns among AYAs with cancer. By integrating the DT and AYA-NA, clinical staff can adopt a stratified approach to distress management: the DT serves as a quick screening tool to identify AYAs with significant distress (DT ​≥ ​4), while the AYA-NA pinpoints specific areas of concern, such as emotional or social difficulties. This targeted assessment facilitates problem-specific interventions. For modifiable factors (e.g., emotional distress), problem-oriented support programs can be implemented to address specific psychosocial needs, while non-modifiable concerns (e.g., practical challenges) can be addressed through evidence-based therapies like Acceptance and Commitment Therapy. These interventions aim to improve psychosocial well-being and enhance overall quality of life. Additionally, integrating the AYA-POST into hospital information systems can enable standardized workflows and AI-assisted decision-making, allowing for early identification, efficient referrals, and continuous monitoring. Insights from the tool also guide resource allocation and optimize multidisciplinary care planning, ensuring AYAs’ unique needs are met comprehensively.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, external validity may be limited by sample characteristics, as most participants were female with breast or gynecologic cancers, restricting the generalizability to the broader population ​of AYAs with cancer. A related reason is that this study was conducted in adult general hospitals and specialized oncology hospitals. Gynecologic oncology is a key department in two hospitals, with more than three wards, which made it easier to reach and access this specific population during the actual recruitment process. Second, while the ROC-derived DT cut-off score of 4 showed acceptable sensitivity, its suboptimal specificity may lead to increased false positives. In resource-constrained settings, a higher threshold (e.g., 5) may enhance screening efficiency. Third, although the AYA-POST demonstrated overall acceptable reliability, internal consistency varied by domain. Notably, the Social and Information domain showed relatively low internal consistency. For the Social domain, it may be that the scale fails to identify the socialization needs of Chinese AYA cancer patients. For the Information domain, it's reported that cancer patients are less likely to actively participate in medical decision-making, instead following the schedule made by professionals in China.57 Although all were categorized as Information domain, the entries reflect different types of information contents. This indicates the need to conduct content analysis or interviews to explore any ambiguities or redundant meanings, and to provide a basis for the revalidation of culture. It is also be considered whether the broad domain could be divided into more specific sub-domain. Finally, the use of the HADS as a reference for PD, without confirmation via structured psychiatric interviews, may limit the construct validity of the derived cut-off. Future studies should incorporate diagnostic interviews alongside psychometric tools to improve the validation of screening thresholds.

Conclusions

This study conducted a rigorous cross-cultural adaptation and validation of the Chinese version of the AYA-POST among AYAs with cancer, which demonstrated acceptable validity and reliability. A DT cut-off score of four indicated optimal screening performance. The AYA-NA was also validated as a suitable tool for identifying specific psychosocial concerns. These findings highlight the prevalence of PD and the key concerns among AYAs with cancer. The availability of this validated tool supports early identification of unmet needs and informs targeted clinical care and service development.
