Pan-cancer analysis of the immunological and oncogenic roles of ATAD2 with verification in papillary thyroid carcinoma
Authors: Zhecheng Li, Yaxin Zhao, Peng Huang, Zhaoyi Wu, Dengjie Ouyang, Adolphus Osei Nyarko, Lei Ai, Zhejia Zhang, Shi Chang
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractATAD2 (ATPase Family AAA Domain-Containing 2) is highly expressed across varies tumor types, yet its common roles in tumor progression and immune interaction remain unclear. We analyzed the expression and alteration profiles of ATAD2, along with its diagnostic and prognostic role in pan-cancer, utilizing TCGA, GTEx, CPTAC, HPA, and cBioPortal databases. Furthermore, we examined the relationship between ATAD2 and immune infiltration utilizing single-cell sequencing data and TCGA database. Additionally, the expression and oncogenic functions of ATAD2 were verified in papillary thyroid carcinoma (PTC) through MTT, wound-healing, transwell, and flow cytometry assays. Our results revealed significant overexpression of ATAD2 in most cancers, strongly associated with poor prognosis. Amplification was the most frequent alteration type of ATAD2, with its mutation correlating with improved overall survival. ATAD2 was positively correlated with multiple inhibitory immune checkpoints and closely associated with the immunosuppressive microenvironment, particularly in PTC. In vitro experiments demonstrated that ATAD2 promoted the proliferation, migration, and invasion of PTC cells by activating the PI3K-AKT pathway and modulating the G1/S cell cycle checkpoint. Collectively, ATAD2 holds promise as a biomarker for pan-cancer diagnosis and prognosis, as well as a predictor of immunotherapeutic responsiveness and a therapeutic target to enhance the efficacy of existing anti-tumor immune therapies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73274-2.Keywords: ATAD2, Pan-cancer, Papillary thyroid carcinoma, Therapeutic target, Tumor immune microenvironmentSubject terms: Cancer microenvironment, Tumour biomarkers, Tumour immunology, Thyroid cancer, Diagnostic markers, Predictive markers, Prognostic markers, Oncogenes, Oncogenes, Tumour biomarkers, Tumour immunology, Cancer, Thyroid cancer, Biomarkers

Introduction

Although cancers are still the leading cause of death, straining global healthcare resources1, progress in immunotherapies and targeted drugs offer breakthrough potential in cancer treatment and has largely improved cancer prognosis2–4. In the clinical realm, despite advancements in immunotherapy and targeted drug therapies, a notable subset of patients exhibits resistance or non-responsiveness5,6. Thus, there exists a critical imperative to delineate suitable biomarkers that can accurately discern individuals who are primed for favorable responses to these treatments. Furthermore, such biomarkers hold promise as actionable targets, with the potential to augment the efficacy of current therapeutic protocols7,8.

With the rise of bioinformatics, comprehensive databases enable multi-perspective analyses across various cancers. Meanwhile, these resources provide invaluable insights into pan-cancer biomarkers that hold promise for the timely detection and therapeutic intervention of malignancies9. Furthermore, these methodologies have unveiled the elusive molecular underpinnings of carcinogenesis while elucidating the intricate tapestry of interactions dictating the tumor immune microenvironment10–12. Previous studies have already unearthed the tumor-specific TE chimeric antigens13, the discernment of T cell associations with immune resistance14, and the identification of DNA methylation links to tumor heterogeneity15, all through comprehensive pan-cancer analysis.

ATAD2, short for ATPase Family AAA Domain-Containing 2, is an intriguing protein characterized by an AAA + ATPase domain and membership in the bromodomain protein family16. Research has unveiled the multifaceted role of ATAD2 as an epigenetic decoder, transcription factor, and coactivator, participating in a wide array of biological processes, including DNA replication, transcriptional regulation, protein synthesis, and post-translational modifications17. Numerous scientific investigations have reported its close association with cancer staging and prognostic outcomes, exemplified notably in gastric cancer18, hepatocellular carcinoma18, and renal cell carcinoma19, underscores its pivotal role in oncological contexts. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that the majority of prior research endeavors have primarily centered on delineating the functionalities of ATAD2 within discrete cancer types. There is a discernible paucity of comprehensive studies elucidating the overarching role of ATAD2 across general neoplastic contexts, particularly concerning its implications within the tumor immune microenvironment.

In this study, we performed the first pan-cancer analysis of ATAD2 using public database resources, combined with in vitro experimental validation in PTC. We investigated the potential molecular mechanisms by which ATAD2 promotes tumorigenesis by elucidating its gene expression, genetic alterations, oncogenic function, and association with prognosis and immune infiltration in a variety of cancers. The current findings highlight ATAD2 as a promising biomarker for prognostic assessment and immune infiltration across diverse cancer types, while also elucidating its role in promoting tumor progression through multiple mechanisms in PTC.

Methods

Ethics statement

The protocols and the utilization of patient tissue samples in this study were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committees of the Xiangya Hospital. The patients/participants provided their written informed consent to participate in this study. All experiments were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Gene and protein expression analysis of ATAD2

For gene expression analysis of ATAD2, we employed data from the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) combined with the GTEx project. For insights into the protein-level expression of ATAD2 across diverse cells and tissues, we accessed The Human Protein Atlas (HPA) database (https://www.proteinatlas.org/ ) and the Clinical Tumor Proteomics Analysis Consortium (CPTAC) dataset via the UALCAN portal (http://ualcan.path.uab.edu/analysis-prot.html)20. To augment our analysis with pathological staging information, we utilized the “Pathological Stage Plot” module of the GEPIA2 (Gene Expression Profiling Interactive Analysis, version 2) web server (http://gepia2.cancer-pku.cn/#analysis). This allowed us to generate violin plots depicting ATAD2 expression variations at different pathological stages20.

Survival prognostic analysis

To assess survival prognostics, we acquired overall survival (OS) and disease-free survival (DFS) significance plots for all TCGA tumor types through the “Survival Mapping” module of GEPIA21. These groups were classified into high and low expression categories using a 50% threshold. For analysis of recurrence-free survival (RFS) across multiple tumor types, we integrated various GEO datasets using the Kaplan-Meier plotter (http://kmplot.com/analysis/). The diverse tumor cases were stratified into two groups, employing the “automatic selection of the best critical value”. The PROC package and timeROC package in R software were used to draw the ROC curve of diagnosis. The Survival and Survminer packages were used to establish the nomogram model analysis and calibration curves, as well as to perform survival analysis comparing the BRAF-mutant and BRAF-wildtype subgroups within the THCA samples.

Genetic alteration analysis

We utilized the cBioPortal web (https://www.cbioportal.org/) to do genetic alteration analysis22. Firstly, we selected the “Cancer Types Summary” module. Then entering “ATAD2” for queries, the alteration frequency, mutation type, and copy number alteration (CNA) of ATAD2 in the TCGA database were obtained; Secondly, we chose the “Mutations” module to show the mutated site as well as the three-dimensional structure. Finally, the “Comparison” module was chosen to tell the differences in survival and disease progression between cancer cases with and without ATAD2 genetic alteration in the TCGA database. Kaplan–Meier plots with log-rank P-value were used to demonstrate the above.

The Sangerbox tool (http://sangerbox.com)23 was used to investigate the correlation between ATAD2 expression and tumor mutational burden (TMB) or microsatellite instability (MSI) in different tumors of TCGA. Additionally, gene mutation landscapes were plotted for both high and low ATAD2 expression groups, allowing for a comparison of the mutation frequencies of individual genes between the two groups. Spearman’s rank correlation test was performed, and the P-value and partial correlation (cor) value were obtained.

Relationship between ATAD2 expression and immunity

We obtained associations between ATAD2 and immunomodulatory genes including major histocompatibility complexes (MHC), immunostimulants, chemokines and chemokine receptors and immune checkpoints from the Sangerbox tool. The Tumor Immunity Estimation Resource (TIMER) (http://cistrome.org/TIMER/) was employed to analyze the correlation between ATAD2 and tumor-infiltrating immune cells in pan-cancer, while the association between ATAD2 and various immune cell subtypes in thyroid carcinoma (THCA) was assessed using ssGSEA analysis. TISCH (http://tisch.comp-genomics.org/home/) was utilized to unveil the expression pattern of ATAD2 across major lineage cell types in THCA at the single-cell level.

Protein-protein interaction analysis

The STRING website (https://string-db.org/) was used to obtain the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of ATAD2. Subsequently, the top 100 ATAD2-associated target genes across all tumors and normal tissues within TCGA were screened employing the “similar gene detection” function of GEPIA2.

The screened genes above were enriched by Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses using R software packages including “GOplot”24, “ggplot2”, “org.Hs.eg.db”, and “clusterProfiler”25.

Gene set enrichment analysis

We downloaded the expression matrix of THCA patients from the TCGA database and classified them into high and low expression groups based on the expression levels of ATAD2. These groups were categorized using a 25% threshold. We employed the R software package DESeq2 for differential analysis and explored the KEGG and REACTOME pathways of ATAD2 through gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA). GSEA was conducted using the R package ClusterProfiler and visualized using the enrichplot and ggplot2 packages.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Thirty PTCs and paracancerous normal tissues were collected from the Department of Thyroid Surgery, Xiangya Hospital, Central South University for immunohistochemical staining. The slides were subjected to xylene dewaxing, gradient alcohol hydration, antigen repair with antigen repair solution, blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% hydrogen peroxide, followed by non-specific staining with goat serum. Subsequently, ATAD2 antibody was added to the slides and left overnight at 4 °C. The next day, the secondary antibody was added for half an hour at room temperature, then the color was developed with DAB for 2 min and observed under a microscope. A semi-quantitative score of ATAD2 protein expression was performed, equal to the intensity of expression multiplied by the area of expression. The intensity of expression was scored from 0 to 3, indicating negative, weak staining, moderate staining, and strong staining. Expression zone scores were from 0 to 4 for < 5%, 6–25%, 26–50%, 51–75%, and > 75%, respectively.

Cell culture

The human PTC cell lines TPC-1, K1, and B-CPAP, were provided by the Institute of Medical Sciences at Xiangya Hospital. The IHH-4 and KTC-1 cell lines were purchased from Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences (Shanghai, China). These cell lines were cultivated in RPMI 1640 (VivaCell Biosciences) medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (Procell) and placed in an incubator at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2. Cells in the logarithmic growth phase were selected for further experiments.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA was extracted from cells with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). Reverse transcription was performed using an Advantage RT-for-PCR Kit (Takara, Dalian, China). RT-qPCR analysis was done using SYBR®Green Real time PCR Master Mix assay kit (Takara) in a 7300 Real-Time PCR system (Applied Biosystems Inc, Foster City, CA). 2 − ΔΔCt value was calculated to perform the quantitative analysis. The primer sequences are listed in Table S1.

Western blotting (WB)

The protein sample was cracked with 4 ℃ cracking buffer solution RIPA (Pierce, Rockford, IL) supplemented with protease inhibitors for 30 min. The sample was centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 15 min. The supernatant was taken, protein concentration was measured using a BCA protein assay (Thermo Scientific, Rockford, IL). After mixing with 5 × sample buffer solution, it was separated on SDS-polyacrylamide gels and transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Belford, MA). Then these membranes were blocked with 5% skim milk at room temperature for 1 h, and incubated with diluted primary antibodies at 4 °C overnight. After 3 washes with TBST, add secondary antibody (1:2000) and incubate for 1 h at room temperature, then 3 washes with TBST. Color development by enhanced chemiluminescence. The antibodies are listed in Table S2.

Cell transfection

The overexpression vectors and knockdown shRNA were purchased from GeneChem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The shRNA sequences were used as follows: sh-Control: TTCTCCGAACGTGTCACG; shATAD2-1: GCTAGAAACATCGTTCAAA; shATAD2-2: GCTGCTAAGCCTCCTATAT; shATAD2-3: GCCTACACCCTCACTTGTT. The transfections were performed according to the manufacturer’s instructions (lipo2000, thermos fisher). After transfection for 24 h, the cell was cultured in a fresh medium for two days, then treated by 3ug/mL puromycin until it establishes stable cell lines.

Cell cycle analysis

A total of 1 × 106 cells were inoculated in each well of a 6-well plate. After collecting the cells, the plates were incubated with 1 ml of DNA staining solution and 10 µl of osmotic stabilization solution for 30 min at room temperature. Subsequently, the samples were immediately detected by flow cytometry.

Colony formation assay

Five hundred cells were spread per well in 6-well plates and incubated. After two weeks, those plates were washed three times with PBS, and the colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet. Only colonies containing more than 50 cells were counted.

Wound healing assay

We spread 5 × 105 cells into 6-well plates, and Mitomycin C (10 µg/mL) was used to suppress cell proliferation during the assay26. We used a 10 µl pipette tip to scratch the confluent cell monolayers to make the wounds and removed cellular debris by washing with PBS. After incubating in a serum-free medium for 24 h, we used an inverted microscope TE-2000 S (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) to observe the wound closure rate and capture images.

MTT assay

An equal number of cells were added to a 96-well plate and kept in an incubator at 37 °C for 48–72 h in a humidified, 5% CO2 atmosphere. After adding 20 µL of CellTiter 96® AQueous One Solution Reagent into each well, cells were incubated for 4 h under the same condition. Then, 100 µL DMSO (Solarbio) was added into the supernatants and the 490 nm absorbance was measured.

Transwell invasion assay

We used a 24-well transwell plate with 8-µm polyethylene terephthalate membrane filters (Corning Costar Corp, Corning, NY) to do this assay. Firstly, we filled the upper chamber with 100 µl of diluted Matrigel (1:20 dilution) and incubated it for 2 h at 37 °C. Next, we added 200 µl of serum-free medium containing 1 × 105 cells and 500 µl medium with 10% FBS (Procell) into the upper and lower chambers, respectively. After 24 h of incubation, we washed the plates three times using PBS to remove the noninvasive cells. Finally, cells that invaded the bottom of the chamber were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet for 30 min. An inverted microscope TE-2000 S (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan) was used to observe five randomly chosen fields (magnification, ×200) per well and capture images.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis and diagrams were made through SPSS 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 8. The R software (R-4.2.2, 64-bit) was used in this research. All results were shown as the mean ± SD from at least three independent experiments. Quantitative data were compared using the Student t-test; categorical data were analyzed by the Fisher exact test. A two-tailed P value of less than 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

ATAD2 expression is upregulated in pan-cancer tissues

Utilizing TCGA and GTEx databases, we observed a consistent upregulation of ATAD2 expression in 32 malignant tumor types compared to normal tissues. These tumors encompassed THCA, breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), UCEC, and many others (Fig. 1A). The protein expression level of ATAD2 from HPA and CPTAC database was consistent with the mRNA results (Fig. 1B-C and Figure S1A). Further exploration using the HPA database to investigate ATAD2 mRNA expression in various cancer cell lines corroborated these findings, consistently showing heightened ATAD2 expression across diverse cancer types (Figure S1B). Collectively, these observations underscore the potential pro-carcinogenic role of ATAD2 in tumorigenesis.

Proportion of different tumor tissues with high expression of ATAD2 protein. (C) Protein expression level of ATAD2 from HPA. *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

The prognostic and diagnostic value of ATAD2

To comprehensively assess the prognostic significance of ATAD2 in cancer, we conducted survival analyses focusing on Overall Survival (OS), Disease-Free Survival (DFS), and Recurrence-Free Survival (RFS) across a diverse range of cancer types. In the high-expression ATAD2 group, we observed significantly lower OS rates in patients with adrenocortical carcinoma (ACC) (p < 0.001), kidney renal papillary cell carcinoma (KIRP) (p = 0.003), lower grade glioma (LGG) (p < 0.001), LUAD (p = 0.0056), and UCEC (p = 0.045) (Fig. 2A). Similarly, high ATAD2 expression was associated with shorter DFS in ACC (p < 0.001), bladder urothelial carcinoma (BLCA) (p = 0.034), KIRP (p = 0.0055), LGG (p < 0.001), and uveal melanoma (UVM) (p = 0.028) (Fig. 2B). Moreover, in cancers such as THCA (p = 0.0023), UCEC (p < 0.001), PDAC (p = 0.016), LUAD (p = 0.0043) and liver hepatocellular carcinoma (LIHC) (p = 0.023), elevated ATAD2 expression correlated with poorer RFS (Fig. 2C). The Supplementary Figs. 2 and 3 provide comprehensive prognostic data on ATAD2 across various cancer types. Notably, elevated ATAD2 expression is linked to improved OS in thymoma (THYM). The observed differences may be attributed to the underlying heterogeneity among the distinct cancer types under investigation.

The prognostic value of ATAD2 in pan-cancer. (A) Overall survival (B) Disease-free survival between high and low expression of ATAD2 group from the GEPIA2 database. (C) Recurrence-free survival curves between high and low expression of ATAD2 groups in some cancer types by Kaplan Meier methodology. (D) The correlation between ATAD2 expression and cancer stage.

Furthermore, we conducted Cox regression analyses, which revealed that high expression of ATAD2 was associated with poorer OS in patients across a spectrum of cancers including LUAD (HR = 1.31), UCEC (HR = 1.25), BLCA (HR = 1.17), pancreatic adenocarcinoma (PAAD) (HR = 1.71), KIRP (HR = 2.61), LIHC (HR = 1.46), sarcoma (SARC) (HR = 1.24), Mesothelioma (MESO) (HR = 1.89), kidney renal clear cell carcinoma (KIRC) (HR = 1.54), LGG (HR = 1.6), kidney chromophobe (KICH) (HR = 2.04), ACC (HR = 2.08), and UVM (HR = 2.58) (all p < 0.05) (Figure S4). The relationship between ATAD2 expression and pathological stages was also explored through GEPIA2 platform. The results revealed positive associations between ATAD2 expression and the grading of several cancers, including ACC, KICH, KIRC, KIRP, LUAD (all p < 0.05) (Fig. 2D).

To further investigate the diagnostic value of ATAD2, we conducted ROC curves in pan-cancer. Results showed that ATAD2 could be regarded as a diagnostic marker in glioblastoma multiforme (GBM) (AUC = 0.958), colon adenocarcinoma (COAD) (AUC = 0.943), BLCA (AUC = 0.863), BRCA (AUC = 0.886), UCEC (AUC = 0.803), THCA (AUC = 0.795), skin cutaneous melanoma (SKCM) (AUC = 0.776), ovarian cancer (OV) (AUC = 0.741) (Fig. 3A). Next, we combined the expression level of ATAD2 with the clinical features, such as age, gender, stages to construct a nomogram model and calibration analysis in THCA. The results showed that high level of ATAD2 contributed significantly to poorer PFI in THCA (Fig. 3B) and the calibrated 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival prediction curves demonstrated that the nomogram models had high accuracy in predicting the PFI of THCA (Fig. 3C). These findings highlight ATAD2 as a potential prognostic and diagnostic biomarker with clinical relevance across a diverse array of cancer types, particularly in PTC.

(B) Nomogram model, integrating clinicopathologic factors and ATAD2 level to predict survival probability at 1-, 3- and 5-years in THCA. (C) Calibration curves were used to evaluate the nomogram model in THCA at 1-year, 3-year, and 5-year.

The genetic alterations of ATAD2 in pan-cancer

Genetic alterations of specific genes can profoundly impact tumorigenesis, immune responses, and overall prognosis within the context of cancer27. Our analysis revealed that amplifications constituted the most prevalent form of genetic alterations in ATAD2, closely followed by mutations and deep deletions. Notably, OV, esophageal carcinoma (ESCA), UCEC, and LIHC were among the prominent human tumors displaying relatively high frequencies of genetic alterations in ATAD2, ranging from approximately 15–25%. Moreover, it was observed that OV, BRCA, UVM, and ACC predominantly exhibited genetic alterations in the form of “amplification”. In contrast, other malignancies, including UCEC, BLCA, SKCM, and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC), featured a combination of “mutations” alongside “amplification” as their primary modes of ATAD2 genetic alteration (Fig. 4A). Our specific focus was on the various forms of ATAD2 mutations, with missense and truncation mutations emerging as the predominant types (Fig. 4B). For instance, sites like R1072C* and R1072H*, contributed by patients with UCEC, cervical squamous cell carcinoma and endocervical adenocarcinoma (CESC), and stomach adenocarcinoma (STAD), are exemplified by a spatial structure representation in Fig. 4C.

(C) The mutation site R1072C* and R1072H* in the 3D structure of ATAD2. (D) The potential correlation between mutation status and overall, disease-specific, disease-free and progression-free survival of UCEC conduct by the cBioPortal tool. Subsequently, we conducted an in-depth analysis to assess the potential clinical implications of these genetic alterations, focusing on UCEC, as the cancer with the highest probability of mutation in ATAD2. Interestingly, our findings indicated that patients with genetic alterations in ATAD2 had a better OS (p = 0.0463), but this observation was not significant for patients with PFS, DSS, and DFS (all p > 0.05) (Fig. 4D).

TMB and MSI serve as predictive markers for assessing tumor responses to immunotherapy28. Our analysis revealed strong associations between ATAD2 expression and both MSI and TMB across different cancer types. Notably, ATAD2 exhibited negative correlations with TMB in cancers like LUAD, prostate adenocarcinoma (PRAD), UCEC, KIRP, cholangiocarcinoma (CHOL), and KICH (Fig. 4E). Conversely, positive associations were found, such as ATAD2 being positively correlated with MSI in uterine carcinosarcoma (UCS), GBM, LUSC, and KIRC, while displaying negative correlations in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBC) and TGCT (Fig. 4F).

The correlation between ATAD2 expression and immune-related characteristics in pan-cancer

The tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) constitutes a complex, integrated system that arises from the dynamic interaction between tumor cells, immune cells, fibroblasts, and non-cellular components, including chemokines and cytokines29.Initially, utilizing the TIMER database, we noted a positive correlation between the expression level of ATAD2 and the infiltration of CD4 + T cells in a majority of cancers, especially in THCA, UVM, and PCPG. Furthermore, a strong correlation was also observed between ATAD2 and CD8 + T cells in THYM, UVM, and PCPG (Fig. 5A). Additionally, in cancers such as THCA, UCS, and ACC, the infiltration of CAFs was positively correlated with ATAD2 expression (Fig. 5B). Further detailed scatter plots analyzed through a single algorithm are depicted in Figure S5.

Correlations between ATAD2 expression and TIME. Relationship between ATAD2 expression and immune cell infiltration (A), Cancer-associated fibroblasts (B), inhibitory immune checkpoint genes (C) and MHC (D). *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

Immune checkpoints have emerged as vital mechanisms for tumor cells to evade recognition and attack from immune cells30,31. Our findings illustrated a positive association between ATAD2 and inhibitory immune checkpoints in a wide array of cancer types, including THCA, ACC, ESCA, and UCS. Remarkably, ATAD2 expression exhibited a significant connection with nearly all immune checkpoints in OV (Fig. 5C). Moreover, we conducted an analysis of the relationship between ATAD2 expression and major histocompatibility complexes (MHC). Analogous to the immune checkpoint results, a robust correlation was identified between ATAD2 expression and MHC, including HLA-DMB, HLA-A, and HLA-B displayed significant correlations with ATAD2 expression across multiple tumor types, hinting at the potential influence of ATAD2 on tumor immunogenicity and immune evasion phenomena (Fig. 5D).

The immunomodulatory role of ATAD2 in PTC

We further analyzed the correlation between ATAD2 and immune cells in PTC. The TCGA-THCA results suggested that the expression level of ATAD2 was significantly correlated with T helper cell infiltration, especially Tcm with Th2 cells, but negatively correlated with plasmacytoid Dendritic Cell (pDC)32, cytotoxic cell and NK cell33 infiltration, which might closely related to the immunosuppressive microenvironment of PTC (Fig. 6A-B). To further explore the expression of ATAD2 among different cell types at the single-cell level, we analyzed the single-cell sequencing dataset, THCA_GSE148673. The result suggested that ATAD2 was mainly expressed not only in tumor cells but also in exhausted CD8 + T cells (CD8Tex) and proliferating T cells (Tprolif) (Fig. 6C), further suggesting that ATAD2 is strongly associated with tumor immune resistance and low response rates to immunotherapy34. In summary, the poor prognosis observed in PTC with high ATAD2 expression may be associated with its suppressive tumor immune microenvironment, making ATAD2 a potential new immunotherapy target.

Functional enrichment analysis of ATAD2

We screened proteins interacting with ATAD2, explored genes associated with ATAD2 expression, and performed functional enrichment analysis to learn more about the molecular mechanisms of ATAD2 in pan-cancer. We used the STRING tool to obtain the protein-protein interaction (PPI) network of ATAD2 (Fig. 7A). Then we used the GEPIA2 tool to screen the top 100 genes associated with ATAD2 and selected the top six genes to create a scatter plot of ATAD2 association. The expression of ATAD2 had a strong positive correlation with the expression of CCNE2 (R = 0.74), DSCC1 (R = 0.72), MCM4 (R = 0.73), MTBP (R = 0.81), TAF2 (R = 0.76), and TBC1D31 (R = 0.77) (Fig. 7B). The heatmap showed significant positive correlation between these six genes and ATAD2 in pan-cancer (Figure S6).

(B) Scatter plot showing the positive correlation betweenATAD2expression and the expression of (CCNE2, DSCC1, MCM4, MTBP, TAF2, and TBC1D31) as determined by GEPIA2. (C-D) KEGG/GO analysis based on ATAD2 interacted genes. (E) The Volcano plot of single gene analysis of ATAD2 in THCA patients. Based on the previously screened related and interacting genes, we performed GO and KEGG pathway analyses. KEGG data indicated that “alcoholism”, “cell cycle”, “neutrophil extracellular trap formation”, “systemic lupus erythematosus” and “viral carcinogenesis” were the five most highly enriched pathways (Fig. 7C). In addition, GO analysis indicated that biological processes associated with antigen processing and expression, innate immune response and cell cycle were preferentially activated (Fig. 7D).

We further employed the KEGG and Reactome datasets to carry out the GSEA analysis based on the differentially expressed genes in the ATAD2 high and low expression groups (Fig. 7E). This analysis aimed to identify key biological pathways that are enriched in THCA patients exhibiting high ATAD2 expression levels. The results revealed that ATAD2 is associated with pathways known to drive cancer growth, such as the cell cycle, PI3K, and MAPK pathways (Fig. 7F-H). As a result, ATAD2 may not only be involved in regulating the tumor immune microenvironment in PTC but also simultaneously promote tumor proliferation and invasion.

ATAD2 promotes the malignant phenotype in PTC

In view of our previous findings that ATAD2 expression was significantly upregulated and strongly associated with poor prognosis in PTC, we further investigated the function of ATAD2 in PTC. First, we observed upregulation of ATAD2 in tumor tissues compared to normal adjacent tissues (Fig. 8A). Then, we assessed the mRNA and protein expression levels of ATAD2 in PTC cell lines. ATAD2 expression was up-regulated in all PTC cell lines compared to normal cells and was significantly up-regulated in the more aggressive cell line IHH-4 compared to the less aggressive cell line TPC-1 (Fig. 8B and Figure S7A).

The biological functions of ATAD2 in PTC cell lines. (A) Immunohistochemical results and immunohistochemical staining scores of ATAD2 in patients with PTC and paraneoplastic tissue. (B) The expression level of ATAD2 in different PTC cell lines. (C) MTT assay and (D) colony formation in TPC-1 and IIH-4 cells. The effect of ATAD2 on cell migration (E) and invasive capacity (F). *p < 0.05; **p < 0.01; ***p < 0.001.

Subsequently, we induced ectopic expression of ATAD2 in the TPC-1 cell line and transfected IHH-4 cells with three ATAD2 shRNA knockdown vectors. The efficiency of ATAD2 expression in these cells was then assessed by WB and RT-qPCR techniques (Figure S7B-C). MTT (Fig. 8C) and colony formation assays (Fig. 8D) showed that the ability of cell proliferation was inhibited when ATAD2 expression was decreased, while results were opposite when ATAD2 expression was increased. Furthermore, our findings revealed that the migratory and invasive capabilities of PTC cells were attenuated following the knockdown of ATAD2 in both wound healing assays (Fig. 8E) and transwell invasion assays (Fig. 8F). Conversely, overexpression of ATAD2 resulted in an enhancement of these cellular functions. These observations suggest that ATAD2 plays a pivotal role in regulating the proliferative, migratory and invasive behaviors of PTC cells, thereby contributing to the aggressive nature of PTC.

ATAD2 regulates the PTC cell cycle through the PI3K-AKT pathway and is upregulated in BRAF-mutated PTC

Previous findings indicated that differentially expressed genes associated with ATAD2 were enriched in cell cycle and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways, as revealed by KEGG and GSEA analyses. To further elucidate the potential mechanism by which ATAD2 contributes to PTC malignancy, we examined its effect on cell cycle regulation using flow cytometry. The results demonstrated that ATAD2 knockdown induced G0/G1 phase arrest, whereas overexpression of ATAD2 reversed this effect (Fig. 9A).

The PI3K-AKT pathway is pivotal in carcinogenesis, tumor progression, and metastasis, as corroborated by numerous studies35. This pathway exerts its influence on cell cycle regulation by activating CDK4, thus facilitating cell entry into S phase and DNA synthesis36. Subsequently, correlation analysis based on TCGA-THCA data unveiled a positive association between ATAD2 expression and cell cycle-related proteins, including CDK2 (r = 0.70), CCNE2 (r = 0.70), CCND1 (r = 0.61) and CDK4 (r = 0.58) (Fig. 9B). Further cytological experiments corroborated that the knockdown of ATAD2 suppressed the activation of the PI3K-AKT pathway and downregulated the expression levels of CDK2, CDK4, CCND1 and CCNE2 at both the mRNA (Fig. 9C) and protein (Fig. 9D-E) levels. Conversely, overexpression of ATAD2 exhibited opposing outcomes. These results suggest that ATAD2 may regulate the PI3K-AKT pathway and subsequently modulate cell cycle checkpoint molecules, such as CDK2, CDK4, CCND1 and CCNE2, thereby affecting the G1/S phase transition and ultimately influencing the malignancy of PTC cells.

BRAF mutations, the predominant genetic driver in PTC, are the most frequently observed mutations37,38. Abnormal PI3K-AKT signaling is closely linked to resistance to BRAF inhibitors39,40. Given ATAD2’s regulatory role in the PI3K pathway in PTC, we further investigated the association between ATAD2 and BRAF mutations in this context. We first performed a mutation landscape analysis on patients with high and low ATAD2 expression levels. The results revealed that BRAF mutation, the most frequent genetic alteration, occurred at a significantly higher rate in PTC patients with high ATAD2 expression compared to those with low ATAD2 expression (Fig. 9F). Additionally, ATAD2 expression was significantly higher in BRAF-mutated PTC patients than in BRAF-wildtype patients (Fig. 9G). However, survival analysis showed no significant difference in OS and DFS between high and low ATAD2 expression levels in both BRAF-mutated and BRAF-wildtype cohorts (Figure S8).

Discussion

As the pioneering comprehensive pan-cancer analysis of ATAD2, this study has unveiled its consistent oncogenic propensity across a spectrum of malignancies, implying its potential as a versatile diagnostic and prognostic biomarker. We have delineated its ability to expedite cancer progression via modulation of the tumor immune microenvironment, and specifically elucidated its close association with suppressive tumor immune microenvironment in PTC. We have validated the oncogenic role of ATAD2 in PTC, elucidating its role in fueling cellular proliferation, migration, and invasion. Consequently, ATAD2 emerges as a novel pan-cancer biomarker with implications for early diagnosis and multifaceted therapeutic interventions, including targeted and immunotherapies.

ATAD2 effectively modulates the transcriptional activity of pivotal oncogenes, including c-Myc, E2F, and the androgen receptor18,41. Subsequently, it actively engages in orchestrating the oncogenic signaling pathways across a spectrum of malignant tumors, thus assuming a paramount role in the intricate process of carcinogenesis. For instance, in the context of ESCC, ATAD2 exhibits a significant correlation with clinical staging. It interacts with augmented C/EBPβ to facilitate metastasis through the TGF-β1/Smad3 signaling pathway42. Moreover, studies have revealed that the silencing of ATAD2 can influence the stability of the MYBL2 protein, thereby inducing mitotic arrest and subsequently impacting OV cell proliferation43. Within the domain of LUAD, ATAD2 assumes a pivotal role in regulating cell proliferation, tumorigenicity, and migration, primarily through the modulation of the PI3K/AKT pathway44. Our comprehensive study revealed that ATAD2 consistently exhibited upregulation in mRNA and protein levels across a broad spectrum of cancer tissues and cell lines when compared to their normal counterparts. Additionally, we discerned a significant correlation between high ATAD2 expression and unfavorable prognosis in several cancer types, including THCA, ACC, KIRP, LUAD, and UCEC. We also emphasized that ATAD2 can serve as a diagnostic biomarker for various cancers, including PTC. Collectively, our analysis suggested that ATAD2 holds significant promise as a potential prognostic and diagnostic biomarker for pan-cancer and it may exert a promoting role in cancer progression across diverse malignancies.

Genetic alterations within tumor cells confer them with a biologically advantageous growth potential and can lead to immune evasion, as well as suboptimal responses to therapeutic interventions27,45. In our current investigation, we have uncovered that ATAD2 mutations are prevalent across various cancer types, with amplification and mutation being the predominant modes of ATAD2 alteration. This observation aligns with a study by Liu et al., which reported that gene copy number amplification predominantly accounted for heightened ATAD2 expression in OV, a condition correlated with advanced disease progression and a less favorable prognosis46. Intriguingly, our survival analysis unveiled a noteworthy association between ATAD2 mutation and a more favorable prognosis in UCEC. This phenomenon could potentially stem from the structural alterations within ATAD2, resulting in the loss of its regulatory influence over other oncogenic factors. Nonetheless, further in-depth investigations are warranted to elucidate the underlying molecular mechanisms through which ATAD2 mutations impact cancer prognoses. These findings offer new insights into the relationship between ATAD2 mutations and prognosis, suggesting that the mutation status of ATAD2 might potentially serve as crucial biomarkers for prognosis assessment.

TIME serves as a dynamic stromal milieu crucial for tumor growth, wielding a decisive influence on tumor survival and progression47. Reshaping the TIME holds promise for advancing cancer immunotherapy48. An increasing body of evidence underscores the intricate network of information exchange between tumor-infiltrating immune cells and cancer cells, with the potential to either impede or bolster tumor development49. Our investigations have unveiled a compelling correlation between ATAD2 expression and the infiltration levels of diverse immune cell types including CD8+T, CD4+T cells, and CAFs populations within various tumor contexts. CAFs represent a heterogeneous group of cells originating from various sources50. They interact with tumor-infiltrating immune cells within the TIME through the secretion of diverse cytokines and growth factors, thereby facilitating cancer cells in evading immune system detection51. Specifically, we found a significant positive correlation between the expression levels of ATAD2 in PTC and the infiltration levels of T helper cells, especially Th2 cells, which typically exert pro-tumorigenic effects52,53. However, ATAD2 exhibited a negative correlation with the infiltration levels of anti-tumorigenic pDCs, cytotoxic cells, and NK cells, thereby fostering an immunosuppressive microenvironment that enables tumor cells to evade immune surveillance54 Moreover, our investigation delved into the analysis of single-cell sequencing data, uncovering a notable elevation of ATAD2 expression within both CD8Tex and Tprolif cell subsets, which states signifies a condition of T-cell depletion typified by compromised effector function, limited proliferative capacity, and attenuated responsiveness to tumor antigenic cues. Notably, this state represents a significant factor contributing to the observed low response rates to immunotherapy55,56. This emphasizes the critical role of ATAD2 in regulating cellular states associated with diminished efficacy of immunotherapeutic interventions.

After gaining insight into the correlation between ATAD2 and immune cells within the TIME, our focus shifted towards the regulation of immune-related molecules. The findings unequivocally demonstrate a strong, positive correlation between ATAD2 expression and immune checkpoint genes and MHC-associated genes across most cancer types, underscoring the significant impact of ATAD2 on the immune system. Currently, ICIs represent a highly effective avenue in cancer immunotherapy57. Critical targets for ICIs in the treatment of diverse cancers are the genes encoding immune checkpoint proteins58. While the role of ATAD2 in immune responses has been relatively underexplored, Wang et al. demonstrated a significant correlation between ATAD2 and inhibitory immune checkpoints, such as B7-H4, PD-L1, and CMTM6, in oral squamous cell carcinoma, suggesting that ATAD2 may contribute to immunosuppressive conditions within the tumor microenvironment59. Notably, ATAD2 displayed a noteworthy association with the expression of several MHC-related genes in various tumors. Of particular relevance, MHC class II tumor antigens constitute a pivotal component of antitumor immunity, exclusively activating CD4+ T cells within the tumor microenvironment. This activation, in turn, can stimulate CD8+ cytotoxic T cells in the tumor microenvironment, unleashing an effective antitumor immune response60. Furthermore, TMB and MSI have gained widespread recognition as predictive markers for immune checkpoint inhibitor responsiveness61, offering guidance for precision medicine-driven immunotherapy strategies62. Encouragingly, we have unearthed a significant correlation between ATAD2 and TMB/MSI in a diverse array of tumors. Consequently, the measurement of ATAD2 expression may emerge as a potential approach for gauging treatment responses in antitumor immunotherapy.

While our findings substantiate the contribution of ATAD2 to tumorigenesis and disease progression, the possible common molecular mechanisms governing these processes remain elusive. Therefore, we employed KEGG and GSEA analyses to uncover the key biological processes associated with differentially expressed genes related to ATAD2. Our findings revealed that cell cycle regulation and the PI3K-AKT signaling pathway were the most enriched pathways. Our investigations in PTC cell lines further indicated that ATAD2 may possess the capacity to regulate the G1/S phase transition in the cell cycle via the PI3K-AKT pathway, consequently influencing the proliferation of PTC cells. These discoveries echo earlier research indicating that ATAD2 regulates the PI3K-AKT pathway to promote malignant phenotypes in lung adenocarcinoma and ovarian cancer44,63. Moreover, ATAD2 has been shown to govern cell cycle progression by modulating the expression of pivotal proteins within the Rb-E2F1 signaling pathway, as well as key transcription factors like E2F and MYC64.

Furthermore, BRAF mutation is the most prevalent genetic alteration in PTC65,66 We also found a significant association between ATAD2 expression and BRAF genotype. Previous studies have indicated that BRAF mutations in PTC may synergize with the oncogene STRA6 to activate downstream signaling pathways, thereby promoting cancer progression67. In addition, there is evidence that the BRAF gene may be able to drive PI3K/AKT activation, which in turn promotes a malignant phenotype in cancer68. In our study, we observed that BRAF mutation occurred at a significantly higher rate in PTC patients with high ATAD2 expression, and ATAD2 levels were significantly elevated in BRAF-mutated PTC patients compared to BRAF-wildtype patients. This leads us to speculate that ATAD2 may synergize with BRAF mutations to activate the PI3K-AKT pathway, promoting tumor progression in PTC. However, survival analysis did not show a significant difference in outcomes, suggesting that the precise mechanisms of how ATAD2 and BRAF mutations interact to regulate downstream signaling pathways warrant further investigation.

Collectively, these insights underscore that the regulation of the cell cycle and immune response may represent common mechanisms through which ATAD2 promotes the progression of various cancers. Nevertheless, it is imperative to underscore the need for further comprehensive investigations to substantiate this hypothesis.

Conclusion

ATAD2 exhibits high expression across various cancers and is closely correlated with poor prognosis and immune infiltration. Its significant promotion of tumor progression in PTC occurs through multiple mechanisms. As a result, ATAD2 holds promise as a biomarker for pan-cancer diagnosis and prognosis, as well as a predictor of immunotherapeutic responsiveness. Additionally, it may serve as a therapeutic target to enhance the efficacy of existing anti-tumor immune therapies.
