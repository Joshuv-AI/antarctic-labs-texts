Preoperative ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 PET/CT-derived liver fibrosis quantification independently predicts post-hepatectomy liver failure: a histologically validated study
Authors: Qiuzhi Chen, Yu Wang, Lei Ou, Chunmei Guo, Bo Li, Xiaoliang Chen, Yuanyuan Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeAccurate preoperative risk stratification for post-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF) is crucial. This study aimed to investigate whether, in patients deemed eligible for safe resection by conventional clinical criteria, ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 PET/CT-based quantitative assessment of liver fibrosis could serve as a non-invasive predictor for PHLF.MethodsIn this retrospective study, 107 patients with liver cancer underwent preoperative ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 PET/CT. Quantitative parameters, including liver SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak, and liver-to-aorta SUV ratios (TBRs), were measured. These imaging metrics were correlated with histopathological fibrosis stage (S0-S4) and analyzed alongside clinical and surgical variables to identify independent predictors of PHLF.ResultsPHLF occurred in 30 (28.0%) patients. All PET parameters significantly increased with advancing fibrosis stage (all P < 0.05). Multivariable logistic regression identified TBRmax (odds ratio [OR] = 4.590, 95% CI 1.439 to 14.637, P = 0.010), prothrombin time (OR = 1.566, 95% CI 1.009 to 2.428, P = 0.045), and major hepatectomy (OR = 2.857, 95% CI 1.065 to 7.665, P = 0.037) as independent predictors of PHLF. A nomogram incorporating these three variables demonstrated good predictive performance, with an area under the curve of 0.780, satisfactory calibration, and positive net benefit on decision curve analysis.ConclusionPreoperative ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 PET/CT offers a histologically confirmed, non-invasive method to quantify liver fibrosis. This hypothesis-generating model offers a promising approach for preoperative risk stratification, warranting further validation in independent cohorts.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00259-026-07946-8.

Introduction

Liver cancer ranks as the sixth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality globally [1, 2], with China accounting for nearly half of all new cases and deaths worldwide [3]. For patients with resectable hepatocellular carcinoma and preserved liver function, international guidelines recommend hepatic resection as the first-line curative treatment [4, 5]. However, hepatectomy is associated with various complications, among which post-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF) represents a severe complication with high mortality, ranging from 52 to 68% [6, 7].

Given the high mortality associated with PHLF, effective prevention is critical. Current approaches include clinical scoring systems (Child-Turcotte-Pugh [Child–Pugh], Model for End-Stage Liver Disease [MELD]), imaging evaluation of cirrhosis and portal hypertension, functional tests (indocyanine green [ICG] clearance, technetium-99 m galactosyl human serum albumin [⁹⁹ᵐTc-GSA] scintigraphy), and liver volumetry [8]. However, many of these methods are technically complex or suboptimal for specifically predicting PHLF [9]. The albumin-bilirubin score (ALBI) provides a simple, objective assessment of liver function and predicts PHLF [10, 11], but lacks surgery-related factors (resection extent, fibrosis, portal hypertension), potentially underestimating risk in cirrhotic patients [12]. While assessment of the future liver remnant (FLR) is a cornerstone in preventing PHLF, volumetry alone primarily reflects liver "quantity" rather than functional "quality" [13]. This approach may overlook high-risk patients with adequate FLR volume but impaired function due to underlying cirrhosis or chemotherapy-induced liver injury [14, 15]. Thus, while FLR volumetry assesses liver quantity and ICG clearance evaluates functional reserve, neither directly quantifies the fibrotic burden that determines functional quality. Liver elastography enables non-invasive fibrosis assessment but is influenced by obesity, ascites, and operator experience, as it provides an indirect measure of stiffness rather than a direct molecular visualization of fibrogenesis [16, 17]. Despite these limitations, elastography remains a widely accepted non-invasive standard for fibrosis staging in many clinical guidelines. Portal vein diameter widening, an indirect sign of portal hypertension closely associated with fibrosis and cirrhosis [18, 19], may alter hepatic hemodynamics and reduce functional reserve, potentially increasing PHLF risk [20]. However, these indirect anatomical or functional markers cannot directly quantify the activity of the fibrogenic process itself. Therefore, a non-invasive imaging modality that accurately evaluates both liver fibrosis severity and functional reserve could address the shortcomings of existing methods and provide a novel dimension for predicting PHLF.

In recent years, research has increasingly focused on liver fibrosis as a critical determinant of hepatic functional reserve. A study by Zhang et al. reported that all patients who developed fatal liver failure exhibited severe fibrosis, reinforcing its role as a key risk factor [21]. Histopathology remains the reference standard for fibrosis staging, but its routine application is limited by sampling variability, cost, and complication risks [22]. Non-invasive indices such as fibrosis-4 index (FIB-4) and aspartate aminotransferase-to-platelet ratio index (APRI) can indicate fibrosis but tend to underestimate its stage and progression [23, 24]. Thus, reliable non-invasive methods for assessing liver fibrosis severity are needed to enhance risk stratification.

Gallium-68 (⁶⁸Ga) fibroblast activation protein inhibitor (FAPI) positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) can non-invasively stage liver fibrosis by quantifying activated hepatic stellate-cell fibroblast activation protein (FAP) expression [25, 26]. However, whether this quantitative fibrosis burden can forecast postoperative outcome remains unclear. We hypothesized that ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 PET/CT-derived liver uptake values could serve as a novel imaging biomarker for preoperative risk stratification in patients scheduled for hepatectomy.

Therefore, this study aims to: (1) correlate ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 uptake with histopathological fibrosis stage; (2) determine whether preoperative fibrosis burden independently predicts PHLF; and (3) build a nomogram for PHLF risk stratification.

Materials and methods

Study design and population

This single-center retrospective study was approved by the ethics committee at our institution (approval No. CZLL2025-074–001), and patient written informed consent was waived. Patients who underwent whole-body ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 PET/CT examination between October 2020 and December 2024 were enrolled. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pathological diagnosis of liver cancer, including hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and cholangiocarcinoma; (2) age between 18 and 85 years; (3) underwent partial hepatectomy within one week after the ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 PET/CT examination; (4) no other systemic malignancies; (5) no manifestations of extrahepatic biliary obstruction. Exclusion criteria included: (1) absence of liver fibrosis staging in the postoperative pathological report (n = 11); (2) severe motion artifacts in the images (n = 3); (3) moderate-to-severe fatty liver (n = 5); (4) active hepatitis (n = 2). A total of 107 patients were ultimately included in the analysis. Patients with moderate-to-severe fatty liver or active hepatitis were excluded to avoid inflammation confounders on FAPI uptake. All patients underwent standardized preoperative assessment including ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 PET/CT, ICG clearance, and routine labs. According to the Makuuchi criteria (Tokyo criteria [27–29]), patients undergoing major hepatectomy (≥ 3 Couinaud segments) had CT-based three-dimensional reconstruction to measure FLR, ensuring safe volume based on integrated assessment of ICG testing and underlying liver condition [30, 31]. For early-stage small tumors with well-preserved liver function, FLR was deemed sufficient based on preoperative imaging and intraoperative confirmation, and was not routinely measured. All patients were confirmed eligible for hepatectomy through multidisciplinary tumor board discussion.

Data collection

Data collected from medical records included etiological factors, such as hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection, alcohol consumption, and other causes. Preoperative variables comprised age, sex, height, weight, history of diabetes, hypertension, and heart disease, as well as complete blood count, liver function, and coagulation profile. Intraoperative parameters, including blood loss, hepatic inflow occlusion status, and extent of liver resection were also recorded. Major hepatectomy was defined as resection of ≥ 3 Couinaud segments, while minor hepatectomy was defined as resection of < 3 Couinaud segments [32].

The patient's preoperative parameters were used to calculate body mass index (BMI), APRI, FIB-4, and ALBI. The formula for BMI is weight (kg) divided by the square of height (m2). The formula for APRI is [aspartate aminotransferase (AST) level/upper limit of normal] × 100/platelet count (10⁹/L). The formula for FIB-4 is (age × AST)/(platelet count × √alanine aminotransferase (ALT). The formula for ALBI is (log10 total bilirubin (TBil) [μmol/L] × 0.66)-(albumin [g/L] × 0.085). Liver function was assessed using the Child–Pugh classification [33] includes five parameters: TBil, serum albumin, prothrombin time (PT), ascites, and hepatic encephalopathy.

PHLF was diagnosed and graded (grades A, B, and C) according to the diagnostic criteria and grading system proposed by the International Study Group on Liver Surgery (ISGLS) [34]. Liver fibrosis was staged according to the Scheuer classification system [35] (S0-S4). Two experienced pathologists (each with 8 years of experience), blinded to all clinical information, independently evaluated and staged all pathological specimens. Disagreements were settled by consensus after a collaborative review.

Preparation and quality control of ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04

The FAPI precursor (chelator 1,4,7,10-tetraazacyclododecane-1,4,7,10-tetraacetic acid, DOTA) was purchased from MCE (MedChemExpress, USA). Radiolabeling to synthesize ⁶⁸Ga-DOTA-FAPI-04 was carried out following standard procedures. Quality control of the final product was performed using high-performance liquid chromatography, confirming compliance with the following specifications: radiochemical purity > 95%, and a sterile, pyrogen-free injectable formulation.

PET/CT image acquisition and reconstruction

No special patient preparation was required prior to the examination. Based on body weight, ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 was administered intravenously at a dose of 1.85 MBq/kg. Following injection, patients rested quietly for 40–60 min, after which they were instructed to empty their bladder. Image acquisition was performed using a GE Discovery 710 PET/CT scanner. A low-dose CT scan was first conducted from the vertex of the skull to the upper-middle third of the thigh, with the following parameters: tube voltage 120 kV, tube current 100 mA, slice thickness 5 mm. Subsequently, PET data were acquired in three-dimensional mode, covering 6–8 bed positions, each with an acquisition time of 3.0–3.5 min. CT data were used for attenuation correction, and PET images were reconstructed using the ordered-subset expectation maximization (OSEM) iterative algorithm with 2 iterations, 24 subsets, and standard filter. Of note, partial-volume correction was not applied in this study.

FAPI uptake measurement

Tracer uptake was quantified using maximum, mean, and peak standardized uptake values (SUVmax, SUVmean, SUVpeak). All measurements were performed on the MedEx system, which automatically carried out image reconstruction, attenuation correction, and time correction, while retaining the original pixel units (Bq/mL) for subsequent quantitative calculations. The SUV calculation model employed the total body weight-normalized SUV value (SUVtbw). A circular region of interest (ROI) with an area of approximately 900 mm2 was placed in homogeneous liver parenchyma, avoiding all focal lesions, and was automatically fitted into a three-dimensional volume of interest (VOI) by the software to measure background hepatic activity. Concurrently, another circular ROI with an area of approximately 100 mm2 was positioned centrally within the lumen at the origin of the thoracic aorta to measure blood pool activity (Fig. 1). Portal vein diameter was measured at the midpoint of the main portal vein on CT images. The TBR (liver-to-aorta SUV ratio) was then calculated as the target-to-background ratio. To assess reproducibility in the presence of parenchymal heterogeneity, additional ROIs were placed in a second independent liver segment in a randomly selected subset of 50 patients. The inter-segment agreement was excellent (ICC > 0.92), confirming robust reproducibility. Two nuclear-medicine physicians (each with > 5 years of PET experience), blinded to clinical data, histological findings, and PHLF outcomes, independently drew liver and aorta ROIs. One of the physicians repeated the measurements 30 days later to assess intra-reader reproducibility. Intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC, two-way random-effects model, absolute agreement) was calculated to evaluate both inter-reader and intra-reader agreement. Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS software version 23.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY USA) and R software (version 4.2.3). Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), as appropriate. Categorical variables are described as numbers and percentages. For comparisons between patients with and without PHLF, continuous variables (e.g., liver function parameters, complete blood count, and SUV values, etc.) were analyzed using the independent samples t-test or Mann–Whitney U test, depending on the data distribution and variance homogeneity. Categorical variables were compared using the Chi-square test. Differences in SUV values across the three stage hepatic fibrosis grades were assessed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis test, followed by post-hoc analyses where applicable. To identify independent predictors of PHLF and construct a predictive nomogram, univariate logistic regression analysis was first performed. Variables with significance in the univariate analysis were assessed for collinearity using variance inflation factor (VIF), and those with severe collinearity were excluded before being entered into multivariable logistic regression analysis. Subsequently, stepwise regression was applied to variables that remained significant in the multivariable analysis to select predictors for the final nomogram. Meanwhile, to explore the incremental value of serological markers, additional extended models incorporating APRI, FIB-4, and ALBI were constructed for comparison. Bootstrap resampling (1000 repetitions) was performed for internal validation. In each bootstrap sample, the model was refitted and its performance was evaluated in both the bootstrap sample (apparent performance) and the original dataset (test performance). The optimism for each performance metric (e.g., C-statistic, calibration slope, calibration intercept) was calculated as the difference between apparent and test performance. The average optimism across the 1000 repetitions was subtracted from the apparent performance of the original model to obtain optimism-corrected performance estimates. The calibration curve was generated by averaging the predicted probabilities from the 1000 bootstrap samples, which were then compared with the observed event rates in the original data using locally weighted smoothing (LOESS). A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. To explore whether the predictive performance of TBRmax varies across fibrosis stages, exploratory subgroup analyses stratified by fibrosis grade (S0-2 vs. S3-4) were performed.

Results

Patient demographics

Among the 128 initially eligible cases based on the inclusion criteria, 21 were excluded, resulting in 107 patients ultimately enrolled in the study. Among them, 30 patients (28.0%) developed PHLF, while 77 patients (72.0%) did not. Regarding the severity of PHLF, 25 of the affected patients (83.3%) were classified as grade A, 4 (13.3%) as grade B, and 1 (3.4%) as grade C. HBV-related liver disease was present in 87 cases (81.3%), HCV-related liver disease in 3 cases (2.8%), alcoholic liver disease in 2 cases (1.9%), and unknown etiology in 15 cases (14.0%). Preoperative Child–Pugh classification: 103 (96.3%) patients were classified as grade A, and 4 (3.7%) as grade B. Among the grade B patients, 3 developed PHLF, the remaining patient was classified as postoperative liver dysfunction (not meeting ISGLS criteria). Postoperative pathological diagnoses confirmed 104 cases (97.2%) of hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and 3 cases (2.8%) of intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma. A total of 12 patients (11.2%) had previously undergone liver tumor-related treatments, including 2 cases of partial hepatectomy, with the remainder having received interventional therapy, immunotherapy, or comprehensive therapy. The intra- and inter-observer ICCs for SUV and TBR values were greater than 0.93, indicating excellent agreement.

Compared with patients without PHLF, PHLF patients had significantly longer PT [11.85 (11.20, 13.15) sec vs. 11.60 (10.80, 12.25) sec, P = 0.05] and higher TBil [17.71 (13.55, 22.91) μmol/L vs. 14.20 (10.63, 18.11) μmol/L, P = 0.030]. However, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in terms of preoperative clinical characteristics, including sex, age, BMI, International Normalized Ratio (INR), comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, heart disease), complete blood count, or other liver function parameters (all P > 0.05). Analysis of intraoperative characteristics showed that the proportion of patients undergoing major hepatectomy was significantly higher in the PHLF group (18/30 vs. 23/77, P = 0.004), whereas no statistically significant differences were found between the groups in intraoperative blood loss or hepatic inflow occlusion. Additionally, no significant differences were noted in APRI, ALBI and FIB-4 scores between the groups. Notably, hepatic uptake parameters (including liver SUV and TBR values) on ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI PET/CT imaging were all significantly higher in the PHLF group compared to the without PHLF group (all P < 0.05) Nomogram for predicting PHLF

Multivariable analysis identified TBRmax, PT, and extent of hepatectomy as independent prognostic factors for PHLF, and all these variables were included in the subsequent nomogram construction. Meanwhile, to explore the incremental value of serological markers, additional extended models incorporating APRI, FIB-4, and ALBI were constructed for comparison. A baseline clinical model was first constructed incorporating extent of hepatectomy and PT, yielding an AUC of 0.724. Adding TBRmax to this clinical model created a combined nuclear medicine-clinical model, which improved the AUC to 0.780 with good calibration (calibration slope 0.9877, intercept 0.0034), (Fig. 2). When PT was replaced by INR in the combined model (TBRmax + extent of hepatectomy + INR), the AUC was 0.743, suggesting that the PT-based model outperformed the INR-based model in this cohort. Stratified analyses showed numerically greater improvements for the S3-4 subgroup (sensitivity: 0.667 → 0.905; PPV: 0.300 → 0.613), although the interaction was not statistically significant (P = 0.879); see Supplementary Table 3 and Supplementary Fig. 1. To assess the incremental value of serological markers, APRI, FIB-4, and ALBI were each added to the combined model, resulting in AUCs of 0.780, 0.782, and 0.786, respectively, showing no substantial improvement in performance compared to the nuclear medicine-clinical model. Discussion

In this histologically validated cohort of 107 patients with liver cancer (predominantly HBV-related HCC), we demonstrated that preoperative ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 PET/CT-derived parameters not only correlated strongly with pathological fibrosis stage but also independently predicted PHLF. To our knowledge, the potential of preoperative ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 PET/CT to simultaneously stage liver fibrosis and predict PHLF has not been well studied.

Compared with the S0-2 group, both S3 and S4 groups showed significantly higher liver SUV and TBR values (all P < 0.05), while no difference was observed between S3 and S4 groups (P > 0.05). This pattern underscores the role of ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 PET as a biomarker of active fibrogenesis, as the transition from S3 (active stellate cell-driven matrix production) to S4 (mature scar stabilization) is accompanied by reduced fibrogenic activity [36–38]. Thus, FAPI signal reflects fibroblast activation rather than absolute matrix deposition. This explains why SUV values rise markedly from S2 to S3 despite only modest collagen increase. In summary, ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 PET not only stages liver fibrosis but also non-invasively identifies the bridging fibrosis stage—a phase of active fibrogenesis that may represent a critical window for anti-fibrotic intervention, although longitudinal studies are needed to confirm its clinical utility.

This study confirms that TBRmax, PT and extent of hepatectomy are independent predictors of PHLF. Previous studies have identified prolonged PT-INR as an independent predictor of PHLF [39]. Li B et al. reported that the incidence of grade B/C PHLF increased progressively with the number of resected segments, and resection of ≥ 4 segments was confirmed as an independent predictor of PHLF (OR = 2.13) [40]. In patients with normal liver parenchyma, resection of ≥ 70% of liver volume significantly increases the risk of PHLF [15], whereas in patients with chronic liver disease, the safe resection limit should be kept below 60% or even 40% [41, 42]. These varying thresholds suggest that liver functional reserve may be influenced by not only the quantity of remnant parenchyma but also its underlying quality, particularly the severity of fibrosis. Notably, in our predominantly Child–Pugh A cohort, the model incorporating PT (AUC = 0.780) outperformed that with INR (AUC = 0.743), which may be attributable to the narrower distribution of INR values and the finer granularity of PT for risk discrimination.

Previous studies have established the utility of serum-based scores such as APRI, FIB-4, and ALBI in predicting PHLF [43–45]. However, in the present study, adding APRI, FIB-4, or ALBI to the core model (TBRmax + PT + extent of hepatectomy) resulted in only marginal improvements in discrimination (AUC 0.780, 0.782, and 0.786, respectively), and none emerged as independent predictors in univariate analysis. Several factors may explain this observation. First, over half of our cohort had mild fibrosis (S0-2), where FAPI PET/CT—as a direct biomarker of active fibrogenesis—may outperform serum scores. Second, methodological factors likely contributed: AST and ALT, core components of APRI and FIB-4, correlate closely with PT, potentially attenuating their independent contribution; our lower AST upper limit of normal (35 U/L vs. 40 U/L) may also affect score calculations. Third, regarding ALBI, its modest incremental value likely reflects the characteristics of our highly selected surgical population (96.3% Child–Pugh A), where the discriminative ability of ALBI is inherently constrained. Furthermore, albumin in ALBI is inherently correlated with PT, and bilirubin's predictive information may be partially captured by TBRmax and PT. Thus, in a model already containing PT and TBRmax, the additional information provided by ALBI is limited. It should be emphasized that these findings should not be interpreted as negating the clinical value of these serological markers; in settings where ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI PET/CT is unavailable, they remain practical tools for assessing liver fibrosis and functional reserve.

To contextualize the performance of our model, we conducted a preliminary comparison with PHLF prediction models reported in the literature. Previous work has demonstrated that models based on ALBI and APRI have reported AUCs of approximately 0.70–0.80 [43, 46], while models incorporating indocyanine green retention rate at 15 min (ICG-R15) and FLR have achieved AUCs ranging from 0.75 to 0.82 [47]. The final model in our study (TBRmax + extent of hepatectomy + PT) achieved an AUC of 0.78, which is comparable to well-performing models reported in the literature.

Based on the following considerations, we selected this three-variable model as optimal despite other models achieving similar AUCs. First, adhering to the principle of parsimony, this model maintains good discriminative performance while incorporating fewer variables, facilitating clinical implementation. Second, it highlights the core innovation of this study by retaining TBRmax—a novel biomarker derived from ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI PET/CT. Finally, it avoids information overlap, as adding ALBI to a model already containing PT may introduce redundant information rather than providing independent incremental value. In summary, balancing predictive performance, clinical utility, and innovation, we selected TBRmax, PT, and extent of hepatectomy as the constituent variables for the final nomogram.

This complementary relationship may extend more broadly to established functional assessment methods. ICG clearance testing, ⁹⁹ᵐTc-GSA scintigraphy, and ⁹⁹ᵐTc-mebrofenin single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) primarily evaluate hepatic function through dynamic uptake or metabolic processes, reflecting functional reserve. In contrast, FAPI PET/CT offers a distinct dimension by directly visualizing FAP expression—a molecular marker of active fibrogenesis. In our analysis, extent of hepatectomy—a clinical parameter closely correlated with FLR—was identified as an independent predictor of PHLF, underscoring the enduring importance of resected volume in risk assessment. However, we acknowledge that relying on resection extent as a proxy for FLR has inherent limitations, as it cannot distinguish whether a sufficient FLR volume is functionally healthy or severely compromised. This distinction is particularly critical for S3‑S4 patients, in whom the safe FLR threshold is substantially higher than for healthy livers; even an adequate FLR volume may harbor advanced fibrosis that impairs functional reserve. FAPI PET/CT directly addresses this gap by positioning liver fibrosis as a "quality" indicator of hepatic functional reserve. By quantifying FAP expression in activated hepatic stellate cells, it captures the pathological burden of fibrosis—a key determinant of postoperative functional recovery—thereby complementing the traditional "quantity" dimension provided by FLR volumetry. This principle of complementarity—wherein emerging biomarkers augment rather than supplant established tools—consistently informs our interpretation of the findings across different domains of preoperative assessment.

Elastography and FAPI PET/CT differ fundamentally in their underlying principles and may serve as complementary tools. Elastography indirectly reflects fibrosis by measuring liver tissue stiffness, whereas FAPI PET/CT directly targets activated hepatic stellate cells by quantifying FAP expression. These two techniques assess liver fibrosis from biomechanical and molecular imaging perspectives, respectively, and theoretically hold promising complementary potential. Elastography is not routinely performed in all centers due to operator dependence, technical limitations, and variable cut-off values. In our center, it was not part of the standard preoperative workflow. FAPI PET/CT directly quantifies fibroblast activation rather than tissue stiffness. Future head-to-head studies are warranted.

The low number of grade B/C PHLF events (n = 5) in this study may reflect our center's comprehensive perioperative management, including strict preoperative selection (Makuuchi criteria), meticulous surgical techniques, and routine postoperative hepatoprotective therapy. The inclusion of all PHLF grades in our primary analysis is justified on two grounds. First, grade A PHLF, while clinically mild, serves as an early warning signal of borderline functional reserve; its identification can optimize perioperative management. Second, all PHLF grades share a common pathological basis, impaired hepatic reserve, and a predictor effectively identifying grade A events should theoretically also capture higher-risk populations. To confirm this for severe outcomes (grade B/C) will require larger studies.

Limitations

The main limitations of this study include: (1) Single-center, small-sample design (n = 107; S3-4 subgroup n = 52). This limits generalizability and statistical power; the non-significant interaction between TBRmax and fibrosis stage is likely attributable to the latter. The cohort was predominantly Asian, HBV-related HCC, with few cholangiocarcinoma cases. Thus, model performance in other etiologies (e.g., metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease [MASLD], HCV), cancer types, ethnicities, or centers using different protocols remains unknown. (2) Retrospective design, with inherent selection and information biases. (3) Lack of separate validation cohort. Bootstrap resampling (optimism-corrected AUC = 0.780) suggests robustness but does not substitute for true internal or external validation. Separately, PT outperformed INR in our model, which may limit comparability with INR-based studies. (4) Exclusion of moderate-to-severe steatosis. This is a major limitation for generalizability to Western populations where MASLD is prevalent. Steatosis was excluded to avoid confounding with FAPI uptake, yet steatosis and fibrosis often coexist in practice. Therefore, our findings may not apply to patients with significant steatosis. (5) limited staging granularity (S0-2 combined) and surrogate markers (portal vein diameter for portal hypertension; no systematic FLR measurement). FLR was measured only for major hepatectomy. Notably, this limitation also highlights why FLR measurement is complementary to FAPI: FAPI may quantify functional quality when FLR quantity is adequate but advanced fibrosis impairs function. (6) No elastography comparison. This study does not compare FAPI PET/CT with elastography-based fibrosis staging. (7) Predominance of grade A PHLF (25/30, 83.3%). While grade A PHLF provides early warning of borderline functional reserve, the ability of TBRmax to predict clinically significant (grade B/C) PHLF could not be reliably assessed due to the limited number of such events. Larger studies with sufficient grade B/C events are needed.

To address these limitations, we plan multicenter, larger-scale, prospective studies to: (1) perform internal/external validation; (2) include diverse etiologies, steatosis grades, tumor types, and ethnicities; (3) analyze S0-4 subgroups to validate the observed numerical differences; (4) systematically collect FLR for quantity-quality assessment; and (5) perform head-to-head comparisons with elastography, ⁹⁹ᵐTc-mebrofenin SPECT, and ICG clearance.

Conclusion

By simultaneously mapping hepatic fibrosis, ⁶⁸Ga-FAPI-04 PET/CT-derived TBRmax provides a promising non-invasive tool for preoperative PHLF risk stratification, particularly for patients already undergoing PET/CT for tumor staging or those at high risk (e.g., advanced fibrosis). However, given the single-center derivation of these findings, this hypothesis-generating model requires rigorous prospective validation in independent, multicenter populations before clinical application.
