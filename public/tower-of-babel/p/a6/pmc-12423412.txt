In-situ study on grain refinement mechanism of Al-13Cu alloys modified by high entropy alloy particles and ultrasonic treatment☆
Authors: Yuanzheng Cao, Xiaojuan Shang, Guangkai Yang, Jie Wang, Yiwang Jia, Qibin Liu, Yun Che, Sanquan Men, Xiang Li, Yuliang Zhao, Da Shu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study explores the grain refinement mechanisms of primary α-Al during solidification of Al-13 wt% Cu alloy modified by FeCoCrNiAl high-entropy alloy (HEA) particles via ultrasonic treatment. In situ X-ray radiography combined with machine learning-based computer vision techniques was employed to systematically analyze the nucleation and growth behaviors of α-Al grains. Results indicate a significant grain refinement effect associated with increased HEA particle content and higher cooling rates. Specifically, the addition of 2.0 wt% HEA particles reduce the primary α-Al grain size notably, achieving approximately 213 μm at a cooling rate of 1.0 K/s. At a cooling rate of 0.2 K/s, raising the HEA content from 0.5 to 2.0 wt% markedly increases the maximum nucleation rate (from 8.6 to 30.3 crystals/mm3/s), shortens the time required for grain growth cessation (from 18 s to 10 s). Similarly, at a fixed HEA content (1.0 wt%), increasing the cooling rate from 0.2 K/s to 1.0 K/s enhances the maximum nucleation rate (from 25.7 to 93.9 crystals/mm3/s) and reduces the grain growth cessation time (from 15 s to 9 s). Grain size distributions consistently exhibit normal distribution patterns. The addition of HEA particles significantly inhibits grain growth and reduces solute enrichment during solidification. These findings demonstrate that FeCoCrNiAl HEA particles facilitate grain refinement by effectively promoting heterogeneous nucleation and inhibiting grain growth.Keywords: Power ultrasound, Aluminum matrix composite, Ultrasonic treatment, High entropy alloy particles, X-ray radiography, Solidification

Introduction

High mechanical performance, including strength and hardness, significantly contributes to extending the service life of engineered components and products. Grain refinement is among the most effective approaches for improving mechanical properties [[1], [2], [3]]. Advanced manufacturing techniques, such as incorporating foreign particles into metallic matrices and applying secondary treatments, have been developed to produce ultrafine grain structures [4]. These high-performance materials are especially sought after in industries such as aerospace, marine, military, and automotive, where material properties critically determine component reliability and longevity.

Aluminum matrix composites (AMCs) have become increasingly preferred in these fields due to their distinct advantages over traditional materials, including reduced weight and improved resistance to corrosion and wear [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10]]. Previous studies has investigated various ceramic reinforcements to achieve grain refinement and improve mechanical performance [[11], [12], [13]]. For example, Li et al. [14] utilized TiB2 nano-ceramic particles to refine aluminum alloys, reducing their microstructural sensitivity. Their study elucidated the mechanisms of microstructure evolution during solidification, thereby guiding the production of high-performance Al alloy cast billets. Seetharam et al. [15,16] investigated AMCs reinforced with B4C ceramic particles, analyzing strain behavior using mathematical modeling. Successfully introducing AlN-TiN/Al particles into pure aluminum led to notable enhancements in tensile strength, yield strength, and microhardness [17,18]. However, this modification also resulted in a reduction in elongation.

However, despite their advantages, ceramic particles present several notable limitations. Specifically, ceramic reinforcements often negatively impact the plasticity and formability of materials, primarily attributable to the substantial disparities in their thermal expansion properties relative to metallic matrices [19]. Weak interfacial bonding at ceramic–metal interfaces frequently leads to crack initiation and subsequent failure, diminishing composite reliability. To overcome these shortcomings, HEAs—a new category of metallic alloys consisting of five or more main elements in almost equal molar proportions—have emerged as promising alternatives to traditional ceramic reinforcements [20]. Owing to these desirable attributes, HEA particles emerge as optimal reinforcement candidates for aluminum matrix composites. HEAs exhibit a range of superior qualities, such as elevated mechanical strength, thermal constancy, and resistance to abrasion [21,22]. Ultrasonic melt processing (USMP) and casting techniques enable uniform dispersion of HEA particles in an aluminum matrix, significantly enhancing grain refinement and mechanical properties while avoiding interfacial issues common with ceramic materials [[23], [24], [25], [26], [27]]. USMP enhances ingot integrity and quality through two primary mechanisms: cavitation-induced bubble collapse disrupts dendritic structures and intermetallic phases, while acoustic streaming promotes uniform solute distribution, leading to a more homogeneous spatial arrangement of microstructural features [[28], [29], [30], [31], [32]]. Additionally, as metallic reinforcements, HEAs also offer improved compatibility with the aluminum matrix, leading to better bonding and more uniform microstructures [33,34].

Recent studies have consistently shown that incorporating HEA particles into aluminum alloys can significantly refine the grain structure and enhance microstructural homogeneity. For instance, Pandey et al. [35] introduced Al4CrFeMnTi0.25 HEA nanoparticles into Al-7150 via a dual-sonic sequential stir-casting method, achieving remarkable grain refinement (grain size reduced from 108.7 μm to 23.2 μm). Similarly, Luo et al. [36] observed grain refinement (124.4 μm to 38.2 μm) in AA1050 composites reinforced with Al0.5CoCrFeNi HEA particles using stir casting. Additionally, adding AlCoCrFeNiTi HEA particles has been shown to promote a transition in α-Al grain morphology from columnar to equiaxed, contributing to microstructural uniformity [37].

These studies collectively corroborate that HEA particles are capable of markedly augmenting the mechanical performance of AMCs, especially through the mechanism of grain refinement [[38], [39], [40]]. While the microstructural benefits of HEA reinforcements have been widely documented, instantaneous observations of grain formation and development during the solidification process remain limited [41]. This lack of detailed mechanistic insight hinders the optimization strategies for AMC processing. To address this gap, in-situ solidification techniques have recently emerged as powerful tools for directly observing microstructural evolution during solidification [[42], [43], [44], [45]]. In particular, in-situ X-ray imaging facilitates the immediate observation of grain nucleation and expansion, offering novel perspectives on the grain refinement process in AMCs mediated by HEA particles [[46], [47], [48], [49]].

In this study, Ultrasonic treatment was employed to achieve uniform dispersion of FeCoCrNiAl HEA particles within the Al-13 wt% Cu melt. Subsequently, the treated samples underwent in situ X-ray radiography to investigate the solidification process, capturing real-time insights into the nucleation and growth mechanisms of α-Al grains. we utilize in-situ X-ray imaging coupled with sophisticated artificial intelligence-driven image analysis methods to examine the influence of FeCoCrNiAl HEA particle reinforcements on grain initiation and development during the solidification of Al-13 wt% Cu alloys. Specifically, we examine the variations in grain morphology, nucleation events, grain-growth kinetics, and solute redistribution throughout solidification. By leveraging advanced image analysis algorithms, we precisely track individual grain nucleation and growth events, offering a comprehensive, quantitative understanding of how HEA particles refine microstructures during solidification.

Materials and experimental methods

Sample Preparation

The raw materials consisted of high-purity aluminum (99.99 %), copper (99.9 %), and Equiatomic FeCoCrNiAl HEA particles. Fig. 1(a) presents SEM images and elemental distribution of the FeCoCrNiAl particles. From the electron microscope image and EDS mapping, it is evident that the HEA particles exhibit a uniformly spherical morphology, with each element distributed evenly across the particles. In Fig. 1(b), a detailed view of selected particles from the rectangular frame of Fig. 1(a) is shown, clearly indicating that all components are present in nearly equal atomic ratios, with no noticeable segregation. The dimensional dispersion of the HEA particles was evaluated based on SEM imagery, with more than 300 particles assessed to guarantee precision. The dispersion closely adheres to a lognormal distribution. The data in Fig. 1(c) highlight that the particle sizes are uniformly distributed in a logarithmic pattern, with dimensions spanning from 0.9 to 30.8 μm, and an average diameter of 10.1 μm. Additionally, the melting point of the HEA particles (∼1000 °C) greatly exceeds the processing temperatures used for ultrasonic melt treatment and solidification [33,37]. Thus, the HEA particles remain unmelted and thermally stable throughout the process (Fig. 2). Although the particles are too fine to be distinctly visible in processed X-ray images like Fig. 5, their presence is confirmed by SEM/EDS analysis and is reflected in the enhanced nucleation and grain refinement observed. Furthermore, these particles’ high thermal stability and low solubility in aluminum, along with their other favorable properties, underpin their effectiveness as grain refiners [50].

Ultrasonic melt processing

To fabricate the composite samples, high-purity aluminum and copper were initially loaded into an alumina crucible and melted in an inert argon environment within a resistance furnace. Once the mixture reached a molten state, it was maintained at 760 °C for a duration of 20 min. Thereafter, HEA particles that had been pre-conditioned (heated to 300 °C for a period of 2 h and enveloped in aluminum foil) were introduced into the molten alloy. Ultrasonic melt processing (frequency: 20 kHz, power: 1500 W) was then applied for 5 min to disperse the HEA particles uniformly within the melt, effectively breaking any particle agglomerations. A diagrammatic representation of the ultrasonic dispersion process is depicted in Fig. 3(a). Following ultrasonic treatment, the molten material was poured into a conventional Reynolds golf T-mold.

(a) Diagram depicting the ultrasonic treatment process; (b) Schematic representation of the experimental setup for synchrotron X-ray radiography.

Fig. 2 presents the SEM morphology and corresponding EDS elemental distribution of the HEA particle reinforced composite. In Fig. 2(a) (secondary-electron mode), bright particles are visible within the Al matrix. EDS maps (Fig. 2(b)–(f)) show these particles contain high concentrations of Cr, Fe, Co, and Ni (with a corresponding local depletion of Al), confirming they are the introduced AlCoCrFeNi HEA particles. The images demonstrate that the HEA particles are uniformly embedded in the Al matrix with no obvious agglomeration or debonding, thanks to the USMP treatment. In summary, the ultrasound-assisted dispersion yielded a consistent, well-integrated distribution of HEA particles throughout the matrix.

Sheet-shaped specimens (approximately 15 × 10 × 1 mm) were excised from cylindrical samples via wire electrical discharge machining. The surfaces were subsequently polished and refined with sandpaper to achieve a final dimensional extent approximately 200 μm. This was succeeded by ultrasonic cleaning at anhydrous ethanol for a duration of 10 min. Anyone specimen interposed between two high-purity (99.6 %) Al2O3 ceramic plates (20 mm × 15 mm × 0.1 mm) using a sealing mixture composed of silica sol and alumina particles.

Radiography image acquisition and processing

In-situ X-ray radiography was performed at the BL13HB beamline of the Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF). To attain pronounced absorption contrast, monochromatic X-rays at 18 keV were produced via a dual-crystal monochromating device. The solidification dynamics were captured employing a scientific CMOS sensor integrated with an Optique Peter X-ray imaging system, as depicted in Fig. 3(b).

Prior to solidification testing, specimens were elevated to about 50 K above their liquidus temperature and dwelled for a period of 10 min to ensure melt equilibration. Solidification was thereafter conducted under quasi-isothermal circumstances at regulated cooling rates of 0.2, 0.5, and 1.0 K/s via a synchronized shutdown of both heaters, thereby preserving uniform thermal gradients. The region of interest (ROI) for imaging was consistently centered within each sample.

Radiographic frames were acquired at 5 frames per second, providing a spatial field of view (FOV) of 3900 μm × 3900 μm and an effective pixel resolution of 3.25 μm following image processing. The overall analyzed volume was obtained by multiplying the projected scan area by the 200 μm thickness of the sample.

Machine learning

The “You Only Look Once” version 8 (YOLOv8) deep learning algorithm was employed to analyze the grain nucleation and growth processes captured by in situ X-ray radiography. YOLOv8 is based on convolutional neural networks (CNNs) and enables efficient real-time object detection by concurrently performing classification, localization, and bounding box regression. The input image is divided into a grid of smaller cells, each responsible for identifying the presence of target objects. Through successive convolution and pooling operations, the network extracts increasingly abstract features, reducing spatial resolution while enhancing channel depth—from layer P1 to P5, as illustrated in Fig. 4(a).

Computer vision algorithm principle based on YOLOv8.

The output of YOLOv8 consists of predicted bounding box coordinates, object classes, and confidence scores for each detected object. To eliminate redundant overlapping predictions, a non-maximum suppression (NMS) algorithm is applied, retaining only the bounding box with the highest confidence score [51].

Due to its robust performance, YOLOv8 has found broad application across multiple domains, including high-precision analysis of medical images (e.g., X-ray, CT, and MRI). Its effectiveness in grayscale image processing and single-object recognition makes it particularly well-suited for analyzing solidification image sequences. Consequently, YOLOv8 offers significant advantages for the automated and quantitative analysis of grain evolution in real-time radiographic imaging.

In this study, YOLOv8 was utilized to quantitatively evaluate the formation and expansion of primary α-Al grains. As illustrated in Fig. 4(d), the computer vision algorithm identifies each grain and assigns it a unique ID, which remains consistent throughout the grain’s entire growth process. Each identified grain is enclosed within a bounding box associated with its ID. The area of each grain in every frame is then approximated by calculating the area of the inscribed circle within its bounding box, and the resulting data are used for subsequent analysis. This Computer vision algorithm enables the automatic identification of grains in images such as Fig. 4(b), with the detection results exemplified in Fig. 4(c). A detailed visualization of the grain recognition process is provided in the Supplementary video (Movie S2).

Results

Effect of HEA contents

Effect of HEA content on nucleation

Fig. 6 delineates the image sequences of in situ X-radiography experiments of Al-13Cu alloys and their counterparts infused with incremental HEA particle additions (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 wt%; all subsequent values are in weight percent), subjected to a fixed cooling rate of 0.2 K/s. The earliest frames (t = 0) denote the onset of visible nucleation within the captured observation area. After processing the images from Fig. 5(b)–(d) using Eqs. (2), (3), we obtained the solute concentration distribution maps shown in Fig. 6. Due to low X-ray absorption, grains with lower solute concentrations appear blue. As Fig. 6(a) display, in absence of HEA particles, only three coarse grains were observed within the FOV, and the grain size exceeded 1 mm. Fig. 5(b)–(d) shows the solidification sequence of Al-13Cu alloys at a 0.2 K/s cooling rate with various HEA particle contents (0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 wt%). The number of grains observed in the FOV is 173, 321, and 359 for HEA particle contents of 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 wt%, respectively. The microstructural imagery reveals a pronounced elevation in grain density alongside a progressive diminution in individual grain scale as the loading fraction of HEA particulates increases. Specifically, grain counts within the observed FOV rose dramatically from 161 to 360 and further to 393 as the HEA addition increased from 0.5 to 1.0 and then to 2.0 wt%. These findings confirm that HEA inclusions serve as efficient nucleation catalysts, significantly intensifying grain refinement. As their concentration rises, the initial α-Al structures exhibit a progressive shift toward a compact, spheroidized equiaxed configuration. The red circular region in Fig. 6(d) indicates a gas bubble formed during solidification, which causes a local data anomaly—an unavoidable issue in synchrotron radiography [52]. To minimize the impact of such bubbles on the solute concentration analysis, the affected regions were cropped out using MATLAB’s imcrop function [44]. A top-to-bottom temperature gradient exists during solidification due to the equipment reasons. However, since all experiments were conducted under the same controlled cooling rate and thermal boundary conditions, this inherent temperature gradient was consistent across all sample groups and treated as a constant background factor.

As displayed in Fig. 7(a), the number of grains in the solidification sequence with different HEA contents increases rapidly in the first 20 s and then tends to be stable. Additionally, increasing HEA particle content from 0.5 to 2.0 wt% enhanced the maximum nucleation rate from 8.6 to 30.3 crystals/mm3/s and increased the maximum grain density from 62.9 to 152.4 crystals/mm3, highlighting the effectiveness of HEA particles in promoting nucleation.

Effect of HEA content on growth

To further investigate the influence of HEA particle additions on grain growth kinetics, solidification sequence data were processed using computer vision algorithms. The area change of each grain is tracked via its identification box across frames, and the final bounding box size is used to approximate the grain size. The inscribed circle of the bounding box represents the area of the labeled grain. For each HEA concentration, over 125 representative grains from different stages of solidification were selected and analyzed. Fig. 8(a)–(c) present the dynamic variation in grain size over the course of growth, with color mapping used to indicate the chronological sequence of nucleation.

Changes of grain area with time when cooling rate is 0.2 K/s at different HEA particle additions (a) 0.5 wt%, (b) 1.0 wt%, and (c) 2.0 wt%; Distribution of diameter in with time when cooling rate is 0.5 K/s at different HEA particle additions (d) 0.5 wt%, (e) 1.0 wt%, and (f) 2.0 wt%.

In each sequence, grains that nucleate earlier tend to grow for longer durations and achieve larger final sizes, as they experience less spatial competition. Conversely, grains that nucleate later are constrained by surrounding grains, resulting in shorter growth periods and smaller final sizes. This spatial interaction reflects classic impingement effects in grain growth.

The data reveal that augmenting the HEA particle content contributes not only to a diminution in average grain scale but also to a more rapid arrest of grain coarsening dynamics. Specifically, the average time required for grains to reach their maximum size decreases significantly—from approximately 18 s at 0.5 wt% HEA to about 10 s at 2.0 wt%. Furthermore, the grain area evolution curves become progressively smoother and more consistent with higher HEA content, indicating a more uniform and stable growth environment facilitated by enhanced nucleation efficiency and spatial saturation. Quantitative assessment of final grain size distributions (Fig. 8(d)–(f)) reinforces this trend, revealing systematic refinement with successive increments in HEA particle dosage. Average grain size reduced from 320 μm (0.5 wt%) to 253 μm (1.0 wt%) and ultimately to 244 μm (2.0 wt%).

Further insights into grain growth dynamics at different cooling rates were obtained using computer vision analyses of selected grains. Additionally, smoother growth trajectories at higher cooling rates indicated more uniform and stable grain growth processes.

Discussion

Effect of HEA particles on grain growth

The addition of HEA particles significantly inhibits grain growth during solidification. As clearly shown in Fig. 13(a), increasing the HEA particle content markedly reduces the grain growth rate. An inset binary mask highlights grain morphologies for different HEA additions, and the statistical analysis of these areas provides the data for Fig. 13(a). The images demonstrate that as HEA content increases, grain shapes become progressively more spherical and uniform.

(a) Grain area in the early nucleation stage at a cooling rate of 0.2 K/s; (b) The relationship between the area growth rate of all grains and time during the solidification process; The binary mask to distinguish different HEA particle additions (c) 0, (d) 0.5, (e) 1.0, (f) 2.0 wt%.

Quantitatively, at 4 s after nucleation, the average grain area in the alloy without HEA particles is approximately 46 × 103 µm2. Incorporating HEA particulates at concentrations of 0.5, 1.0, and 2.0 wt% results in a sharp decline in typical grain area, decreasing to 10.8 × 103 µm2, 4.2 × 103 µm2, and 3.1 × 103 µm2, accordingly. Thus, the average grain area is diminished by nearly 15-fold as the HEA concentration escalates from zero to 2.0 wt%, underscoring the pronounced grain-growth retardation effect imparted by HEA particles.

Fig. 13(b) shows the evolution of total grain area over time for various HEA loadings, based on the frame-by-frame area measurements of all grains. Maintaining a steady cooling gradient of 0.2 K/s, the apex rate of grain expansion sharply declines from 100.8 × 103 µm2/s at zero HEA content to only 1.12 × 103 µm2/s at 2.0 wt% HEA. These results clearly confirm that incorporating HEA particles effectively suppresses grain growth, playing a key role in achieving refined grain structures. Furthermore, due to the confined dimensions of the observation window, when the unrefined aluminum alloy solidifies, oversized grains partially protruding beyond the FOV margins are omitted from the statistical evaluation. Consequently, derived grain growth velocity underestimates actual value, although it still maintains a degree of reliability.

Fig. 13(c)–(f) presents the binary masks of grains grown at 4th second under the 0.2 K/s cooling rate for different HEA particle additions. Note that the cropped area sizes were adjusted for measurement convenience: 210 × 210 pixels in Fig. 13(c), and 100 × 100 pixels in Fig. 13(d), (e), and (f).

These results clearly demonstrate that HEA particle additions effectively mitigate solute enrichment during grain growth, contributing significantly to microstructural refinement in Al-Cu alloys.

Grain refinement mechanism of HEA particles

Integrating FeCoCrNiAl HEA particles through ultrasonic treatment into an Al-Cu melt refines the microstructure through two complementary actions: heterogeneous nucleation and growth inhibition. As reported by Yang et al. [33,55], a diffusion layer, ∼200 nm thick, forms at the Al/HEA interface during ultrasonic dispersion. High-resolution TEM combined with SAED shows that this layer consists mainly of an Fe-Ni phase with a face-centered-cubic (FCC) lattice that matches the α-Al matrix. This interfacial layer also suppresses detrimental interfacial reactions while maintaining strong metallurgical bonding, allowing effective load transfer and further stabilising the newly formed grains. Furthermore, the application of USMP facilitates the uniform dispersion of HEA particles throughout the melt, allowing a greater number of particles to act as nucleation substrates and thereby significantly enhancing grain refinement [[56], [57], [58]]. Our in-situ observations reveal that introducing HEA particles via ultrasonic treatment decreases solute buildup in the liquid and fosters a more homogeneous solute distribution among dendrites (Fig. 14), while also enhancing the nucleation rate and increasing grain count (Fig. 7). Consequently, the promotion of nucleation by HEA particles effectively reduces the grain size.

Not every particle in the melt becomes a nucleation site. Extensive studies have shown that second-phase particles, including HEA nanoparticles, can impede grain boundary migration through mechanisms such as Zener pinning, solute drag, and interfacial energy modification. HEA particles that remain in the liquid (illustrated as Sphere 2 in Fig. 15) obstruct the advancing solid–liquid interface and hinder grain-boundary migration [59]. Their high hardness and low solubility create a physical barrier that shortens the duration of dendritic growth and encourages the formation of finer, more equiaxed grains. Fig. 15 schematically summarizes these dual roles: particles that act as nucleation sites (Sphere 1) increase the number of grains, while non-nucleating particles (Sphere 2) limit the subsequent growth of grains. Together, an increase in nucleation site density coupled with a decrease in growth kinetics leads to significantly refined and more equiaxed grain structures. This dual action explains the pronounced grain size reduction and morphological transition from dendritic to equiaxed observed as HEA content rises.

Conclusions

In-situ synchrotron X-ray radiography, plus quantitative image analysis, clarifies how FeCoCrNiAl HEA particles through ultrasonic treatment refine the microstructure of Al-Cu alloys. The principal findings are:

Ultrasonic dispersion of HEA particles markedly refines primary α-Al grains. As the HEA particles content increase from 0.5 % to 2.0 %, At 0.2 K/s, the grain density increases from 62.9 to 152.4 crystals/mm3 and the average grain size falls from 328 µm to 243 µm. This is because the HEA particles provide heterogeneous nucleation sites during solidification, thereby reducing the size of grains.

In each sequence, grains that nucleate earlier tend to grow for longer durations and achieve larger final sizes, as they experience less spatial competition. Conversely, grains that nucleate later are constrained by surrounding grains, resulting in shorter growth periods and smaller final sizes. With a fixed cooling rate of 0.2 K/s, raising the HEA content advances the time at which grain growth halts—from ≈18 s (0.5 wt%) to ≈10 s (2.0 wt%). At a constant 1.0 wt% HEA, increasing the cooling rate shortens the growth-cessation time from ≈15 s (0.2 K/s) to ≈9 s (1.0 K/s) and yields finer grains.

The addition of HEA particles significantly inhibits grain growth and reduces solute enrichment during solidification. At 0.2 K s−1, the maximum grain-area growth rate declines from 100.8 × 103 µm2/s (no HEA) to 1.12 × 103 µm2/s (2.0 wt% HEA), while grain morphology shifts from dendritic to equiaxed. FeCoCrNiAl particles refine α-Al grains by (i) providing potent heterogeneous-nucleation substrates that raise the nucleation rate and (ii) lowering interfacial solute buildup, which curtails subsequent grain growth. These dual effects—nucleation promotion and growth suppression—should extend to other HEA/Al systems, offering a strategy for microstructural control in Al-based alloys.
