Effects of melatonin on advanced glycation end products, inflammation, and oxidative stress in peritoneal dialysis patients: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Mina Movahedian, Hadi Tabibi, Shahnaz Atabak, Mehdi Hedayati, Iraj Najafi, Shiva Seyrafian, Mohammad-Hossein Rouhani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvanced glycation end products, systemic and vascular inflammation, and oxidative stress are risk factors for cardiovascular disease in peritoneal dialysis (PD) patients. No studies have examined the effects of melatonin on these risk factors in PD patients. This study was a randomized clinical trial. Forty-four PD patients were randomly assigned to either the melatonin or the placebo group. Participants in the melatonin group received 5 mg melatonin for 10 weeks, while the placebo group received a corresponding placebo. In this study, serum pentosidine, carboxymethyl lysine (CML), high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), soluble intercellular adhesion molecule type 1 (sICAM-1), malondialdehyde (MDA), and glucose were measured. Serum MDA showed a significant reduction in the melatonin group (P = 0.001), and the reduction was significant compared to the placebo group (P = 0.03). Serum hs-CRP decreased in the melatonin group, and this reduction was significant compared to the placebo group (P = 0.04). Significant reductions were observed in serum sICAM-1 (P = 0.02) and pentosidine (P = 0.03) in the melatonin group. Serum CML and glucose did not show significant changes within each group. This study indicates that nightly administration of 5 mg melatonin reduces MDA, hs-CRP, sICAM-1 and pentosidine, which are cardiovascular risk factors in PD patients. Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of mortality among individuals with chronic kidney disease, particularly those undergoing peritoneal dialysis (PD)1. Approximately 40–50% of deaths in dialysis patients can be attributed to CVD2, and the prevalence of CVD in these patients is 10–100 times higher than that in the general population1. Some of the major risk factors for CVD in PD patients include systemic and vascular inflammation3,4, oxidative stress5,6, and advanced glycation end products (AGEs)7,8. Additionally, these risk factors can lead to peritoneal membrane fibrosis and impair ultrafiltration function9–11.

Melatonin (N-acetyl-5-methoxytryptamine) is a neurohormone synthesized from the amino acid tryptophan and secreted by the pineal gland12. This compound is also found in various dietary sources, including eggs, fish, poultry, red meat, nuts, seeds, and legumes13. Melatonin is essential for regulating circadian rhythms and the sleep–wake cycle, improving sleep efficiency and alleviating insomnia12,13. In recent years, researchers have investigated other effects of melatonin, including its cardiometabolic effects. Some studies in nonuremic individuals have shown that melatonin administration can reduce serum concentrations of fasting glucose14,15, insulin14,15, malondialdehyde (MDA), a biomarker of oxidative stress14,16, and high-sensitivity.

C-reactive protein (hs-CRP), a marker of systemic inflammation14,16. Furthermore, a few studies on patients with kidney disease indicated that melatonin decreased serum MDA17–19, hs-CRP19, and tumor necrosis factor-α18. However, to our knowledge, no studies have examined these effects in PD patients. Additionally, the impact of melatonin on serum AGEs and vascular inflammation markers has not been evaluated in human studies. Therefore, the present study was designed to investigate the effects of melatonin on serum AGEs, systemic and vascular inflammation, and oxidative stress in PD patients.

Methods

Study design

This study was a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled parallel trial performed between. May 2024 to September 2024. The study adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all patients at the outset. The Ethics Committee of the National Nutrition and Food Technology Research Institute of Iran approved the study protocol (Ethical Code: IR.SBMU.NNFTRI.REC.1402.034). Furthermore, the clinical trial protocol is registered on the ClinicalTrials.gov website, under the identification number NCT06096558 (23/10/2023).

Sample size

The estimated minimum sample size for each group was 20, based on a power (1–β) of 80% and α = 0.05, for a two-arm parallel study using two tailed testing to detect a difference of 2.8 mg/L in serum hs-CRP concentration. The pooled standard deviation used for this calculation was 10.06 mg/L, obtained from the study by Ostadmohammadi et al.19.

Study setting & selection criteria

A total of 44 patients (25 males and 19 females) with chronic kidney failure undergoing continuous ambulatory peritoneal dialysis (CAPD), aged 19 to 75 years, were recruited from four peritoneal dialysis centers in Iran. The inclusion criteria for this study were individuals aged 18 years and older who had been undergoing CAPD for at least six months. Patients with inflammatory or infectious diseases, such as peritonitis, autoimmune disorders, and liver diseases, as well as those receiving non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, glucocorticoids, estrogen, sedatives, melatonin supplements, or those who consume alcohol were excluded. In addition, none of the patients performed any specific exercises.

Randomization

After stratification based on diabetes status, patients were randomly assigned to either a melatonin or placebo group using block randomization with a block size of four and a 1:1 allocation ratio. The blocks were then randomly selected using simple random sampling to determine the group assignments for all participants. A trained dietitian executed the block randomization process.

Blinding and intervention

Participants in the melatonin group were given a 5 mg melatonin tablet one hour before bedtime for 10 weeks, while those in the placebo group received a corresponding placebo tablet containing starch each night. In our study, a trained dietitian implemented the blinding process, ensuring that both patients and researchers were unaware of the group allocations. The melatonin tablets and placebos were produced by Daana Pharmaceutical Company in Tabriz, Iran, and Barij Essence Pharmaceutical Company in Kashan, Iran, respectively. Participants were instructed to maintain their usual dietary habits, physical activity levels, and medication regimens.

Outcome assessment

Biochemical assessment

At baseline and at the end of the 10th week, 7 mL of blood was collected from each patient after a 12- to 14-h fasting period. The blood samples were kept at room temperature (20–25 °C) for 15 min. Following clotting, the samples were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 10 min. The serum samples were subsequently divided into small aliquots and stored at − 80 °C until they were used. In the present study, the primary outcomes were serum hs-CRP, soluble intercellular adhesion molecule type 1 (sICAM-1), MDA, pentosidine, carboxymethyl lysine (CML), and glucose. Serum concentrations of hs-CRP, sICAM-1, pentosidine, and CML were determined by enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits (ZellBio GmbH, Ulm, Germany). Intra-assay coefficients of variation (CVs) for serum hs-CRP, sICAM-1, pentosidine, and CML were 5.1, 5.9, 6.2, and 6.3%, respectively. Serum MDA was measured using a colorimetry method by commercial kits (ZellBio GmbH, Ulm, Germany) with an intra-assay CV of 5.5%. Serum concentrations of creatinine, urea, and glucose were assessed using various colorimetry methods by commercial kits (Delta Darman, Tehran, Iran), with the aid of a Pictus 700 autoanalyzer (Diatron, Budapest, Hungary). Intra-assay CVs for serum creatinine, urea, and glucose were less than 3%.

Anthropometric and dietary assessments

Patients were weighed at baseline and at the end of week 10. Additionally, their dietary intakes were assessed using a 3 days dietary recall (comprising two weekdays and one weekend day) at baseline and at the end of weeks 5 and 10. The analysis of patients’ diets was performed using Diet Analysis Nutritionist IV software (Version 3.5.2, N Squared Computing, San Bruno, CA, USA).

Dialysis parameters assessment

Dialysis adequacy, quantified as total Kt/V per week, was assessed for each patient based on urine urea concentration, 24 h urine volume, dialysate urea concentration, 24 h dialysate drain volume, blood urea concentration, weight, height, and age20. This assessment was performed using a Kt/V calculator. Additionally, each patient underwent the peritoneal equilibration test for glucose, based on a 2-L 4.25% dextrose dwell with dialysate samples at 0 and 4 hours21. The ratio of dialysate glucose concentration at time 4 to that at time 0 (D4/D0) was determined, and then the percent of glucose absorbed from the dialysate was calculated using the formula 1—D4/D021. Also, the ratio of dialysate to plasma (D/P) creatinine was determined, and peritoneal transport status was classified based on D/P creatinine values as follows: high (0.81–1.03), high average (0.65–0.80), low average (0.50–0.64), and low (0.34–0.49) 22.

Compliance

In order to assess patient compliance, each participant received a predetermined number of tablets along with instructions to return any unused tablets at the end of the study. Compliance for each patient was evaluated based on the number of returned tablets. The overall compliance rate among our patients exceeded 90%.

Statistical analyses

All statistical analyses were performed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS, Inc., Chicago, IL) for Windows version 23.0. A chi-squared test was conducted to compare categorical variables between the two groups. Since all quantitative parameters displayed normal distributions according to the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test, we utilized a t-test and a paired t-test to compare parameters between and within groups, respectively. Dietary parameters were measured three times throughout the study, so we used analysis of variance for repeated measurements to compare the data at these time points. The results are presented as mean ± standard error, and differences were considered statistically significant at P ≤ 0.05.

Results

Of the 46 PD patients initially recruited, one individual from each group was excluded due to peritonitis (Fig. 1). The underlying causes of kidney failure among the participating patients were as follows: hypertension (39%), diabetes mellitus (23%), trauma (9%), polycystic kidney disease (2%), nephrolithiasis (2%), and other or unknown causes (25%). No adverse events were reported during this investigation.

Effects of melatonin on oxidative stress, and systemic inflammation

At the end of week 10, serum MDA concentration in the melatonin group showed a significant decline in comparison to baseline (P = 0.001). In contrast, the placebo group did not exhibit any statistically significant changes (Table 3). The reduction in serum MDA in the melatonin group was also statistically significant when compared to the placebo group (P = 0.03; Table 3).

During the study, serum hs-CRP levels decreased in the melatonin group, and this reduction was significant in comparison with the placebo group (P = 0.04). In contrast, serum hs-CRP in the placebo group increased significantly at the end of week 10 when compared to baseline (P = 0.03; Table 3).

Effects of melatonin on vascular inflammation, AGEs, and fasting glucose

Serum concentrations of sICAM-1 (P = 0.02) and pentosidine (P = 0.03) decreased in the melatonin group at the end of week 10 compared to baseline. In contrast, no statistically significant changes were observed in the placebo group (Table 3). Furthermore, serum concentrations of sICAM-1 (P = 0.005) and pentosidine (P = 0.01) decreased in the melatonin group at week 10 compared to the placebo group (Table 3). However, the mean changes in serum sICAM-1 and pentosidine in the melatonin group were not statistically significant in comparison to the placebo group (Table 3).

Serum concentrations of CML and fasting glucose did not exhibit significant changes within each group throughout the study (Table 3).

Discussion

Oxidative stress is a prevalent complication in PD patients and can contribute to CVD, peritoneal membrane fibrosis, and ultrafiltration failure6,11. In dialysis patients, oxidative stress arises from various factors, including increased elimination of water-soluble antioxidants like vitamin C, impaired clearance of oxidant compounds due to kidney failure, the bioincompatibility of PD solutions, and pre-existing conditions such as diabetes mellitus. PD solutions contain high concentrations of glucose, reactive carbonyl species, elevated osmolality, and low pH6,11,23. The bioincompatibility of PD solutions can activate peritoneal cells, especially mesothelial cells, leading to an increase in the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS)6,11. In the present study, serum MDA, an oxidative stress marker, showed a statistically significant reduction of 17% in the melatonin group compared to the placebo group. Consistent with this finding, most prior research on nonuremic patients has shown that melatonin supplementation significantly decreases serum MDA concentrations14,17,24–27. However, a few studies in nonuremic individuals have indicated that melatonin does not significantly affect serum MDA28,29. No research was found in the existing literature regarding the effect of melatonin on serum MDA concentration in PD patients for comparison with the findings of our study. Nevertheless, two studies on kidney transplant recipients and hemodialysis patients have indicated that that melatonin supplementation reduces serum MDA levels18,19. Melatonin is known to function as an antioxidant by scavenging free radicals, thereby reducing oxidative stress30.

Inflammation is a prevalent complication among PD patients31,32, occurring in 12–65% of these individuals32. Systemic inflammation in PD patients arises from several factors, including the bioincompatibility of PD solutions, insufficient removal of inflammatory cytokines due to kidney failure, increased production of inflammatory cytokines due to the accumulation of compounds such as AGEs, and occurrences of peritonitis31,32. Systemic inflammation in PD patients is associated with a higher risk of CVD, peritoneal membrane fibrosis, ultrafiltration failure, protein-energy malnutrition, and increased mortality rates31,32. In our study, we observed a significant decrease of 11% in serum hs-CRP, a marker of systemic inflammation, in the melatonin group compared to the placebo group during the study. We found no research in this field in PD patients to compare with our study results. Consistent with our findings, Ostadmohammadi et al.19 reported that melatonin administration significantly reduced serum hs-CRP in hemodialysis patients. Similar studies on nonuremic patients have also demonstrated that melatonin can decrease serum hs-CRP14,16,28,29,33–36. However, some researchers have reported that melatonin has no effect on serum hs-CRP26,37. Oxidative stress, mainly ROS, has been shown to activate and translocate nuclear factor-κB (NF-κB) into the nucleus by activating the inhibitor of NF-κB (I-κB) kinase38,39. Then, NF-κB binds to NF-κB -dependent genes, such as inflammatory cytokines, and induces their expression38. Inflammatory cytokines, especially interleukin-6, can increase CRP synthesis in the liver40. Therefore, melatonin as an antioxidant reduces oxidative stress, and prevents systemic inflammation41.

Serum concentrations of vascular inflammation markers, such as sICAM-1, are elevated in PD patients3,42. These markers play a central role in the development and progression of atherosclerosis and are associated with an increased risk of cardiovascular mortality3,42. In the present study, we observed a significant reduction of 7% in serum sICAM-1 levels in the melatonin group at the end of week 10 compared to baseline. To our knowledge, no previous studies have investigated the effects of melatonin on serum markers of vascular inflammation to compare with the results of our study. However, in an in vitro study, Li et al.43 demonstrated that melatonin decreased the expression of the vascular cell adhesion molecule-1 gene in mouse aortic smooth muscle cells. It is well recognized that oxidative stress activates NF-κB, leading to the expression of genes associated with vascular inflammation38. Therefore, melatonin, as an antioxidant, may play a role in preventing vascular inflammation41.

In PD patients, AGEs such as pentosidine and CML accumulate in blood and tissues44. This accumulation is attributed to decreased clearance of AGEs resulting from kidney failure, the presence of reactive carbonyl compounds in glucose-containing PD solutions, glucose absorption from PD solutions, and oxidative stress44,45. The increased levels of AGEs in PD patients may contribute to CVD, peritoneal membrane fibrosis, and ultrafiltration failure44,46. In the present study, serum pentosidine concentration in the melatonin group was significantly reduced by 5%. In addition, serum CML decreased in the melatonin group during the study, but this reduction was not significant. To date, no human studies have examined the effects of melatonin on serum AGEs for comparison with our findings. However, two animal studies have demonstrated that melatonin reduces serum AGEs47,48. In our study, there were no significant differences in dialysis adequacy or the percentage of glucose absorbed from the PD solution between the two groups at baseline and at the end of week 10. Also, serum fasting glucose did not show significant changes within each group during the study. Therefore, the observed decrease in serum pentosidine concentration in the melatonin group cannot be attributed to alterations in dialysis efficiency, glucose absorption from PD solutions, or blood glucose concentration. However, as previously mentioned, our study found that melatonin reduces oxidative stress, suggesting that the decrease in serum pentosidine concentration may be related to this effect. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that antioxidants, including melatonin, can decrease the formation of AGEs44.

A clinically significant reduction in traditional risk factors for CVD, such as overweight, hypercholesterolemia, and hypertension, ranges from 5 to 10% or more 49–51. Although there are no specific studies evaluating the clinical significance of reductions in non-traditional risk factors for CVD, including MDA, hs-CRP, sICAM-1, and pentosidine, we can apply the clinical significance observed in traditional risk factors to these non-traditional factors. Therefore, the reductions of 5% to 17% observed in this study may be considered clinically significant for these non-traditional risk factors.

A limitation of our study was the lack of measurement of serum melatonin concentration.

In conclusion this study indicates that the nightly administration of 5 mg melatonin supplement significantly reduces serum concentrations of MDA, hs-CRP, sICAM-1 and pentosidine, which are risk factors for CVD and peritoneal membrane fibrosis in PD patients.
