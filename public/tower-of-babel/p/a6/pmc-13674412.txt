Harnessing microbial metabolism and nutrition to target the gut–mammary axis for better livestock production and health

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe gut‐mammary axis orchestrates bidirectional crosstalk linking intestinal homeostasis and mammary function through integrated neural, endocrine, and immune pathways. Dysregulation driven by gut microbiota dysbiosis or metabolic dysfunction compromises lactation performance and induces mastitis via pathogen translocation and epigenetic reprogramming. This review systematically discusses the molecular mechanisms underlying microbial metabolite‐mediated mammary development and lactation efficiency, evaluates the impact of gut‐derived pathogens on mammary barrier integrity and inflammatory cascades, explores maternal offspring microbiome transmission for neonatal immunity programming, and proposes precise nutritional strategies targeting microbial immune metabolic networks. Our synthesis of previous research findings aimed to optimize animal productivity, prevent mammary gland disorders, and provide insights for the advancement of the livestock industry.

INTRODUCTION

Recently, the introduction of the gut–brain, gut–lung, gut–liver, and gut–kidney axes has triggered research into the mechanisms underlying these interactions [1, 2, 3, 4]. Unlike these anatomically connected axes, the gut–mammary axis represents a functional rather than an anatomical conduit that integrates intestinal and mammary functions through systemic circulation, neuroendocrine signaling, and immune cell trafficking. This distinction is critical because the mammary gland lacks direct anatomical connections with the gastrointestinal tract; instead, communication occurs via blood‐borne microbial metabolites, hormones, and immune cells that migrate from the gut‐associated lymphoid tissues to the mammary gland. The gut–mammary axis has been studied across multiple mammalian species, serving distinct roles in the discovery to application pipeline. Rodents (mice and rats) are the most widely used models for mechanistic studies owing to their genetic tractability, short reproductive cycles, and well‐characterized immune systems. However, their gastrointestinal tract is simple, and they possess five pairs of mammary glands with relatively low milk output. Pigs serve as an important translational bridge between rodents and production livestock. They share physiological similarities with humans and other large mammals, have a monogastric digestive system similar to that of rodents, and their mammary gland architecture and lactation demand more closely resemble those of production animals. Ruminants are the primary target species for production‐oriented applications. Their forestomach enables extensive microbial fermentation that profoundly alters the metabolism of dietary compounds before they reach the mammary glands. Ruminants also have the highest lactation demand and a distinct mammary gland structure. Understanding these anatomical and physiological differences is essential for interpreting cross‐species data and translating mechanistic insights from model organisms into effective livestock management interventions.

The “gut‐mammary axis” constitutes a complex regulatory network connecting the intestine and mammary glands, which has attracted considerable attention because of its key role in animal production and health. It dynamically integrates intestinal and mammary functions through multidimensional pathways, such as neural signaling, endocrine regulation, and immune interactions. First, the gut microbiome and its metabolites are transported to the mammary gland via the circulatory system, thereby directly regulating milk component synthesis, inflammatory responses, and pathogen defenses. In addition, hormones regulating the mammary glands (e.g., prolactin) and immunologically active substances (e.g., secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA) can regulate intestinal barrier function and microbiome homeostasis through a feedback mechanism [5, 6, 7]. These findings expand the theoretical framework that represents the pathogenesis of mammary disease and also provide a scientific basis for optimizing gut‐mammary axis function through nutritional interventions (e.g., probiotics and functional metabolites). In this review, we systematically discuss the neural, endocrine, and immune regulatory mechanisms underlying the gut‐mammary axis. We further explore its role in regulating animal performance through nutrient uptake, metabolic reprogramming, and immune homeostasis maintenance. This synthesis aims to provide theoretical support for the precise management of mammary gland health and sustainable livestock production.

While previous reviews have provided valuable insights into the connection between the gut microbiota and mammary glands, several important knowledge gaps remain. First, most existing literature focuses on human or rodent models, with limited systematic integration of findings across livestock species (e.g., dairy cows, sows, and goats) that are central to animal production. Second, the mechanistic crosstalk among the neural, endocrine, and immune pathways has been discussed separately, but a holistic framework that captures the synergistic regulation of the gut‐mammary axis is lacking. Third, although nutritional interventions (e.g., probiotics, prebiotics, and functional metabolites) have shown promise, a comprehensive synthesis of their specific effects on lactation performance, mammary health, and offspring development in livestock has never been conducted. Fourth, the bidirectional nature of this axis, particularly how mammary‐derived factors feedback to modulate intestinal function, remains underexplored in production settings. This review addresses these gaps by providing a species focused synthesis that prioritizes relevant evidence from livestock, integrating the neuro endocrine immune network into a unified conceptual framework, and critically evaluating precise nutritional strategies that target the gut–mammary axis. Furthermore, we highlight the translational potential of this axis for improving animal productivity and health, with particular emphasis on maternal offspring microbiome transmission and sustainable livestock production.

Unlike previous reviews that focused mainly on human or rodent models or isolated mechanisms, this review offers three unique contributions: a species focused synthesis of livestock relevant evidence, a direct link between neuro endocrine immune pathways and practical nutritional strategies, and a critical assessment of translational barriers such as species specific physiology and industrial scalability. Within this framework, the term precision nutrition refers to targeted strategies that optimize the gut–mammary axis to improve lactation, mammary health, and offspring development. These strategies include stage specific maternal nutrition, microbiome guided supplementation, metabolite based interventions, and farm level decision support systems using real time biomarkers. The following sections critically evaluate each approach, focusing on species specific feasibility and translational barriers.

PHYSIOLOGICAL BASIS OF THE GUT–MAMMARY AXIS IN ANIMALS

Gut health affects mammary health

The mammalian intestinal tract, which is the longest digestive organ in animals, has a complex multifunctional structure. The inner wall is lined with folds and villi that substantially increase the surface area to facilitate nutrient absorption. The mucosal layer of the intestinal tract is rich in various digestive enzymes and nutrient absorption related transport proteins that efficiently break down food into small molecules entering the bloodstream or lymphatic system through active transport or passive diffusion [8]. Moreover, the intestinal tract plays a crucial role in immune defense. The intestinal mucosal barrier, gut microbiota, and gut associated lymphoid tissues collectively form a robust line of defence that protects the host from external pathogens [9].

The gut microbiota comprises complex communities of bacteria, fungi, viruses, and archaea residing in animal intestines [10]. They provide energy to the host by breaking down dietary fibers to produce short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), synthesize essential nutrients such as vitamins K and B, protect host health by regulating the immune response, and maintain intestinal barrier integrity. They also participate in metabolic regulation, neural signaling, and systemic inflammatory homeostasis [11, 12, 13]. Therefore, intestinal dysbiosis may contribute to the onset and progression of mammary diseases. Disruptions of the intestinal microbiome might simultaneously or subsequently affect the probiotic environment, which can cause probiotics to lose their “dominant” role in the tumor environment. This negatively affects the control or inhibition of mammary tumor cell growth and even accelerates tumor cell growth and promotes angiogenesis, indirectly contributing to tumor metastasis [14, 15, 16]. Although mammary cancer is not a primary production issue in livestock, the underlying signaling pathways are evolutionarily conserved and directly regulate lactation performance, mammary epithelial cell turnover, and inflammatory responses, thereby providing mechanistic insights applicable to mastitis prevention and milk production. In addition, intestinal dysbiosis undermines the integrity of the intestinal barrier and promotes the migration of specific pathogenic bacteria through the circulatory system to the mammary glands. He et al. [17] demonstrated that enterogenic Aeromonas species can migrate from the intestines to the mammary glands and may contribute to the development of mastitis in mice. In contrast, gut health and balanced microbiota exert positive effects on mammary health. According to recent studies, probiotic supplementation (e.g., Lactobacillus suis R389 and Lactobacillus reuteri) protects against mammary tumor development in mouse models [18, 19]. In addition, Kassayova et al. [20] showed that Lactobacillus plantarum LS/07 or dietary fiber supplementation ameliorated mammary carcinogenesis in rats. These findings indicate that regulating intestinal health can positively impact mammary health. Although the endpoints used in rodent mammary tumor studies are not directly applicable to livestock, these experiments reveal conserved immunomodulatory pathways (e.g., NF‐κB, STAT3) that also regulate mammary gland inflammation and immune defense.

Mammary function affects intestinal function

The mammary gland, a unique organ in mammals, undergoes development and lactation, and is regulated by numerous hormones and growth factors. During animal growth and development, the mammary gland undergoes multiple morphological and functional changes, gradually developing from an undifferentiated state at the embryonic stage to a mature gland capable of lactation [21]. Lactation involves the uptake, synthesis, and secretion of blood‐derived nutrients by mammary epithelial cells, a process regulated by hormones such as prolactin and insulin‐like growth factor [22]. As a major secretory organ, the mammary gland's lactation function is regulated by intestinal nutrient supply, and it also exerts feedback effects on intestinal nutrient absorption and metabolism [23]. During lactation in particular, the high nutrient demand of the mammary glands stimulates the intestines to enhance nutrient absorption efficiency [24]. Mammary milk is the “bridge” between the mother's mammary glands and the infant's intestines and provides nutrients and immune components that are crucial for defense against bacterial infections. Immunoglobulins and antimicrobial peptides in milk enhance the mucosal barrier function of the newborn intestine and promote colonization by beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus spp., thereby fostering a healthy gut microbiome [25, 26, 27].

MECHANISMS OF GUT–MAMMARY AXIS REGULATION IN ANIMALS

Connecting pathways of the gut–mammary axis: Neurologic, endocrine, and immune

The nervous, endocrine, and immune systems interact dynamically to integrate the functions of the gut and mammary glands [28]. First, the neural pathway primarily transmits sensory information from the gut to the central nervous system (CNS) via the vagus nerve (VN). Subsequently, the CNS regulates mammary gland development and lactation through pathways, such as the hypothalamic pituitary axis [29]. Acetylcholine released from VN endings directly acts on cholinergic receptors in mammary epithelial cells to promote milk protein synthesis [30]. Simultaneously, the hypothalamic pituitary axis dynamically adapts to mammary gland lactation demands (e.g., suckling stimulation in young animals) by regulating the prolactin secretion cycle [31]. These components of the endocrine pathway link the gut to the mammary glands through hormones that circulate in the blood. For example, hormones secreted by the intestines, such as glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) and peptide YY (PYY), promote the efficient utilization of glucose by mammary epithelial cells by lowering blood glucose and insulin levels, which, in turn, affect mammary gland development and lactation function. Pituitary hormones, such as prolactin, also act on the intestinal tract to regulate intestinal digestion, absorption, and immune function [32, 33]. The immune system regulates inflammation, barrier repair, and immune memory signals between the gut and mammary glands through the directional migration of intestinal microbial metabolites (e.g., SCFAs), immune cells (e.g., memory B cells), and the remote interactions of immune molecules (e.g., sIgA and IL‐10) [34]. All of these immune interactions collectively maintain physiological homeostasis and improve animal performance.

Mechanisms of neuromodulation

The VN, an important part of the autonomic nervous system, serves as a bridge between the gut and the CNS of mammals (Figure 1). The VN senses intestinal stimuli (e.g., nutrient absorption and changes in the gut microbiota) and transmits this information to the brain via neural reflexes, influencing mammary gland development and lactation [35]. The VN regulates the proliferation, differentiation, and secretory activity of mammary epithelial cells via neurotransmitters, such as acetylcholine and norepinephrine. This mechanism enables the mammary glands to respond adaptively to the overall needs of the organism and to environmental changes [36]. In addition to long‐distance vagal regulation, localized neural reflexes and regulatory mechanisms link the gut and mammary glands. These localized neural networks include nerve endings, plexuses, and neurons connecting the gut to the mammary glands [37]. These networks detect local environmental changes. For instance, increased intramammary pressure is detected by local nerve endings, which trigger nerve reflexes that promote mammary duct contraction and milk ejection [38]. Similarly, when nutrients are abundant in the gut, local neural networks enhance nutrient absorption and delivery to support lactation by regulating intestinal muscle contraction, relaxation (motility), and digestive enzyme secretion [39]. Local neurological reflexes and regulatory mechanisms help maintain the dynamic balance between the gut and mammary glands, and adapt to external environments. During lactation, suckling by pups stimulates the mammary glands as the demand for milk increases, which rapidly increases the amount of milk produced to meet the needs of the pups through localized nerve reflexes [40]. Simultaneously, the intestines boost their digestive and absorptive functions to ensure that the mammary glands are supplied with sufficient nutrients to support lactation [41]. This synergy allows animals to maintain efficient lactation in complex environments.

Endocrine regulatory mechanisms

Hormones play a crucial role in regulating the gut–mammary axis, and various hormones transmit messages between the gut and mammary glands through the bloodstream, coordinating the physiological functions of both organs (Figure 2). For example, GLP‐1 not only promotes glucose metabolism by enhancing insulin sensitivity but also acts on mammary epithelial cells to inhibit mTORC1 activity by activating the AMPK signaling pathway in human breast cancer cell lines. The AMPK signaling pathway, in turn, blocks mammary cancer cell cycle progression, reducing mammary cancer cell proliferation and migration, and is associated with a lower incidence of mammary cancer in experimental models [42, 43, 44]. While mammary carcinoma is not a primary concern in livestock production, studies highlight the GLP‐1‐AMPK‐mTORC1 signaling axis, which regulates milk protein synthesis and mammary gland metabolism (see Section 4.1). This suggests that gut hormones simultaneously regulate gut metabolism and mammary gland function via the endocrine pathway and that the hormones secreted by the intestines indirectly regulate the lactation function of the mammary glands by influencing appetite, energy metabolism, and other functions [45]. For example, GLP‐1 enhances insulin secretion and sensitivity and promotes glucose utilization and storage, thus increasing nutrient uptake efficiency and utilization by mammary epithelial cells [46]. In murine mammary epithelial cells, Peptide YY (PYY) inhibits the NF‐κB pathway by binding to the mammary Y2 receptor to alleviate the mammary inflammatory response. Concurrently, it indirectly reduces mammary lipid synthesis load and affects the oxidative stability of mammary lipids by reducing energy intake through central appetite regulation [47, 48, 49]. In contrast, in humans and rodents, mammary‐derived leptin is a multi‐targeted regulator of intestinal mucosal barrier integrity, immune homeostasis, and microbiota host interactions through autocrine and paracrine pathways. An imbalance in leptin signaling pathways is associated with the pathogenesis of inflammatory bowel disease and metabolic syndrome, providing a new theoretical basis for targeted intervention [50, 51, 52, 53]. In neonatal mice, prolactin secreted by the pituitary gland promotes milk synthesis and acts on the intestines through blood circulation, regulating the expression of glucose transporter proteins in intestinal epithelial cells and affecting the efficiency of intestinal absorption of carbohydrates [54]. Studies indicate that endocrine regulation of the gut–mammary axis achieves a dynamic balance between gut and mammary functions through hormone‐mediated metabolic reprogramming, signaling pathway intervention, and immune metabolic interactions. Additionally, the multi‐targeted and bi‐directional regulation of the gut–mammary axis provides novel molecular targets and intervention strategies for the prevention and treatment of metabolic diseases and mammary cancer.

Notably, most of the endocrine mechanisms described above, particularly those involving GLP‐1, PYY, and leptin, were originally characterized in rodent or human models under non‐lactating or disease conditions. Whether these pathways operate with identical efficiency and regulatory dynamics in ruminants during metabolic stress under lactation remains largely unexplored. Ruminants possess a distinct digestive physiology (rumen fermentation) and face extreme nutrient demands that may alter hormone secretion patterns, receptor expression, or downstream signaling thresholds. Therefore, while the core signaling pathways (e.g., AMPK‐mTORC1, STAT5, and NF‐κB) are evolutionarily conserved, their quantitative contribution to lactation performance and mammary health in livestock requires direct validation in target species under production‐relevant conditions.

Mechanisms of immunoregulation

Intestinal immune cells and molecules regulate local intestinal immune responses as well as the immune function of the mammary gland through the gut‐mammary axis (Figure 3). Intestinal immunogenesis depends on several factors, the most important being the relations between the immunogen and the intestinal immune system, and the influence of the intestinal environment [55]. For example, probiotics in the gut can reduce the incidence of intestinal inflammatory reactions and systemic immune responses by regulating intestinal microbiome balance, which is conducive to mammary gland health and the maintenance of lactation functions [56]. Milk also contains various immunologically active substances (e.g., immunoglobulins and lactoferrin) that enhance offspring immunity and promote growth and development [57]. Recently, modulation of beneficial gut microbiota abundance and diversity has been found to enhance immune regulation and ameliorate diffuse mammary hyperplasia in mice [58]. Therefore, the gut–mammary axis exerts an important influence on the immune function of animals. The immune pathways and regulatory mechanisms of the gut–mammary gland axis are shown in Figure 3. The gut to mammary immunization pathway begins with the intestinal epithelium, through which immunoglobulin A (IgA) enters the circulatory system and migrates to mammary tissues [7]. Memory B cells in the intestinal epithelium migrate through the circulatory system to mammary tissues [59]. In mammary tissues, secretory IgA (sIgA) facilitates immune cell migration through C–C chemokine receptor 10 (CCR10) interactions [60, 61]. sIgA cells secrete sIgA into milk via polyimmunoglobulin receptors on mammary epithelial cells [62], thus converting maternal intestinal immunity into passive neonatal immune protection, a process that is synergistically regulated by intestinal microbiome metabolites, immune signals, and the neuroendocrine system. Furthermore, modulating the composition of gut microbiota can enhance immune regulatory functions and improve mammary hyperplasia in experimental models. These findings are discussed in Section 4.2, drawing on the original literature.

Gut barrier immunity and microbial interactions

The gut microbiota is one of the largest microecosystems in the animal body, which is closely associated with the host immune system. Intestinal microbiota are involved in the regulation of immune function and inflammation by modifying the integrity of the intestinal mucosal barrier, activating and differentiating immune cells, and producing immune molecules [63]. Research indicates that dysbiosis of the human gut microbiota can lead to the disruption of intestinal barrier function and increased permeability. This triggers systemic inflammation and subclinical endotoxinemia and promotes the migration of pathogenic bacteria from the gut to the mammary glands, thereby inducing mastitis [64, 65]. Interactions between the intestinal microbiome and the mucosal immune system lead to an increase in potentially pathogenic gram negative intestinal bacteria and their associated metabolic changes, disrupting the intestinal epithelial immune barrier and increasing susceptibility to infection [66]. These processes affect gut health and directly or indirectly affect mammary gland health and function through the gut‐mammary axis. Probiotics can reduce intestinal inflammation and systemic immune responses by balancing the intestinal microbiome, thereby contributing to the maintenance of mammary gland health and lactation [67]. Therefore, gut microecology affects mammary health. In summary, the intestinal barrier serves as the first line of defense, integrating the physical, chemical, and immunological functions that are dynamically regulated by the gut microbiota. Disruption of this barrier not only compromises gut health but also predisposes the mammary gland to infection via the gut‐mammary axis. In addition to immune regulation, the gut microbiota influence mammary epithelial cell metabolism and milk protein synthesis. Specifically, intestinal microorganisms modulate the expression of milk protein genes and the activity of related transcription factors, thereby affecting the quantity and composition of milk proteins synthesized. These mechanisms, which involve microbial metabolite signaling and nutrient partitioning, are discussed in detail with supporting evidence in Section 4.1.1.

Immune cell trafficking along the gut‐mammary axis

The transmission of immune cells and molecules in the gut‐mammary axis is an important part of the immunoregulatory mechanism; they transmit information and play a regulatory role between the gut and mammary glands through blood or lymphatic circulation [68]. Macrophages, dendritic cells, and other immune cells phagocytose and process pathogens or antigenic substances in the intestinal tract and then enter the lymphatic or blood circulation to migrate to the mammary tissue, activating local immune cells in the mammary gland and triggering localized immune responses through antigen presentation and cytokine secretion [69]. Additionally, immune molecules, such as cytokines and chemokines, can influence the inflammatory response and lactation function of the mammary gland by regulating the activation and migration of immune cells [70]. These trafficking processes for immune cells and molecules defend against pathogen attack and the spread of infection and participate in regulating mammary physiological balance and productivity [71]. In addition, the inflammatory response is a defense mechanism of the body against stimuli such as injury or infection [72]. A moderate inflammatory response is essential for pathogen clearance and tissue repair within the gut–mammary axis [73]. However, excessive inflammation can damage the mammary tissues and affect lactation performance [74]. In summary, the directional migration of immune cells, particularly IgA+ plasma cells and memory B cells, from gut‐associated lymphoid tissues to the mammary gland is orchestrated by chemokine receptors such as CCR10 and their ligands. This trafficking mechanism ensures that maternal intestinal immunity is effectively transferred to the mammary gland and subsequently to their offspring via milk, thereby providing passive immune protection during early life.

REGULATION OF THE GUT–MAMMARY AXIS IN ANIMAL PRODUCTION

Modulation of the gut–mammary axis affects animal milk performance

Influence of gut–mammary axis regulation on milk proteins

Interactions between the gut microbial community and the mammary glands offer significant promise in regulating milk quality and composition. As discussed in Section 3.3.1, the gut microbiota influences mammary functions through multiple pathways. Recent research indicates that gut microbiota can influence dairy cow lactation performance via the gut–mammary axis [75]. Intestinal microorganisms influence the metabolic processes of mammary cells and alter the expression levels of protein genes (e.g., αS1‐casein (CSN1S1) and β‐lactoglobulin (BLG) genes), thus affecting the amount of milk protein synthesized [76]. Moreover, gut microbes regulate the expression of transcription factors associated with protein synthesis in mammary cells, resulting in an increase in protein synthesis or changes in the ratio of their isoforms [77]. For instance, a controlled dose response trial in lactating dairy cows demonstrated that dietary inulin supplementation at a level of 200 g/d per cow significantly increased milk yield, milk protein content, and lactose production rate, while also modulating ruminal propionate concentrations. In the same dose finding study, inulin inclusion rates ranging from 100 to 400 g/d per cow consistently elevated milk protein and lactose yields, and the most pronounced effect on milk protein was observed at the 200 g/d dose [78, 79, 80]. These quantitative data provide a dose‐related reference for translating inulin supplementation into practical dairy feeding strategies. For inulin, although a 200 g/d dosage consistently improved milk protein content, the magnitude of the protein response varied by approximately 0.1–0.3 percentage points depending on basal diet and lactation stage. The positive effects of inulin on milk protein synthesis can be primarily attributed to the increased production of propionic acid, which acts as a key glucogenic precursor. Propionic acid elevates insulin levels through a dual mechanism: directly stimulating insulin secretion from β‐cells while alleviating the inhibitory effect of free fatty acids and non‐esterified fatty acids on insulin secretion. The resultant hyperinsulinemia is thought to activate the insulin/signal transducer and activator of transcription 5 (STAT5) signaling pathway in mammary cells, leading to phosphorylation of STAT5 transcription factors, which may enhance the efficiency of milk protein synthesis [75, 76]. Another study showed that SCFAs produced by Vibrio butyricus spp. in the intestinal tract can migrate to mammary tissues through blood circulation, where butyric acid indirectly regulates the epigenetic modification of lactoprotein genes (e.g., CSN1S1, CSN2) in mammary cells through the inhibition of histone deacetylases (HDACs) and activation of the peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) nuclear receptor and transcriptional activity, thereby promoting milk protein synthesis in cows [81, 82]. Alterations of the gut microbial community can affect the efficiency of amino acid uptake and utilization by mammary cells, which in turn affect whey protein synthesis [83, 84]. Supplementation with leucine at 1.5 mM (equivalent to 10 μg per mammary gland daily) significantly upregulated whey protein (β‐lactoglobulin) and milk fat synthesis genes (FASN, ACC) in bovine mammary epithelial cells by activating the mTOR/SREBP1 pathway [85]. Moreover, when the intestinal microecological balance is maintained, a stable immune environment promotes the secretion of high‐quality milk proteins by mammary cells [86]. For example, selenium, copper, and calcium supplementation enhance the abundance of Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium, which produce SCFAs that attenuate mammary oxidative stress via Nrf2 signaling. This cascade restores immune homeostasis, promoting the synthesis of high‐quality milk proteins in dairy cows [87, 88].

Effect of gut‐mammary axis regulation on milk fat

Gut microbes can modulate the activity of fatty acid synthase in mammary cells, thereby altering the ratio of saturated to unsaturated fatty acids, and thus milk fat composition [89]. For example, in human models, lactic acid bacteria, through their expression of linoleic acid isomerase, converted dietary linoleic acid (LA) to cis‐9, trans‐11‐conjugated linoleic acid (c9, t11‐CLA), significantly elevating the CLA content of milk fat [90]. In a controlled feeding trial with lactating dairy cows, supplementation with fermented feed containing Lactobacillus acidophilus increased milk CLA content by 20%–30% [91]. CLA is an unsaturated fatty acid with various physiological functions in improving milk quality [92]. Some probiotics promote CLA production. For example, Lactococcus lactis generates CLA isomers by fermenting linoleic acid, increasing the percentage of c9,t11‐CLA in milk fat under optimized conditions [93]. Bifidobacteria also synthesize CLA through a similar pathway and regulate lipid metabolism in infants via mammary milk transmission [94, 95]. Triglycerides are the major form of milk fat, and intestinal microorganisms can alter triglyceride synthesis and secretion by affecting related signaling pathways, such as the peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor (PPAR) signaling pathway, in mammary cells [96, 97]. Notably, the synergy between gut microbes and host metabolic pathways is not limited to gene expression regulation, but may also indirectly optimize the environment for milk fat synthesis by improving the metabolic status of the host. Yohe et al. [98] found that supplementation with Saccharomyces cerevisiae maintains the microecological balance of the digestive tract, improves the host's metabolic state, thereby increasing milk production and milk fat content in cows.

Influence of gut‐mammary axis modulation on other milk components

Lactose synthesis is relatively stable; however, the regulation of mammary cell metabolism by intestinal microorganisms can indirectly affect lactose synthesis [99]. For example, microbe‐regulated shifts in energy metabolism can affect the activity of key enzymes involved in lactose synthesis [100]. Some gut microbes can synthesize vitamins, such as vitamins K and B [101]. Consequently, these microbes can indirectly influence vitamin levels in milk by modulating their availability or synthesis in the gut of cows. For example, probiotics such as Bifidobacteria in the bovine gut synthesize vitamin B12, and changes in their abundance in the gut affect the level of vitamin B12 in milk [102, 103]. However, the quantitative contribution of intestinally synthesized B12 to the vitamin B12 content in milk remains poorly defined. Additionally, intestinal microorganisms can affect the mineral content of milk. Some gut microorganisms produce organic acids that lower the intestinal pH, promoting the dissolution and absorption of minerals such as calcium and phosphorus. These absorbed minerals are then transported via the bloodstream to mammary cells, ultimately influencing their concentration in milk [104].

Modulation of milk performance by gut‐mammary axis strategies

In livestock production, strategic adjustments to feeding management can effectively modulate the gut–mammary axis and gut health, thereby improving milk yield and quality [105, 106]. To compare the efficacy of different regulatory strategies, we summarized the key research findings in Table 1, which summarizes the effects of various regulatory strategies targeting the gut–mammary axis. These strategies improve milk quality through multiple pathways, including targeting the intestinal/mammary microenvironment (e.g., inulin and Lactobacillus salivarius), whereas other strategies enhance nutrient substrate utilization (e.g., LPL and SO + SSL) [107, 108]. Future development of precision nutrition strategies targeting the gut–mammary axis should prioritize SCFA‐based formulations to enhance STAT5‐mediated milk protein synthesis, engineered probiotic communities for species‐specific microbiota modulation, and real‐time monitoring of immune metabolism biomarkers to dynamically create interventions [109, 110, 111, 112]. These comprehensive approaches translate previous research into customized solutions for livestock production systems.

Impact of gut–mammary axis regulation on mammary gland health

Somatic cell counts in milk reflect the functional and immune status of the mammary glands, and gut microorganisms can regulate and improve somatic cell counts in dairy cow milk [113, 114]. Stabilization of the gut microbial community can regulate the expression of mammary epithelial cell‐associated proteins, enhance pathogen recognition and clearance, and modulate inflammatory signaling pathways, thereby controlling somatic cell count [115, 116, 117, 118]. For instance, in a mouse model of mastitis induced by Staphylococcus aureus, Lactobacillus rhamnosus significantly reduced somatic cell counts in milk by activating phagocytic activity in mammary macrophages and upregulating the expression of the anti‐inflammatory factor IL‐10 [119]. Butyric acid produced by Clostridium butyricum decreases somatic cell counts in cows with subclinical mastitis by inhibiting the TLR4/NF‐κB pathway [120, 121]. Moreover, microorganisms play a role in protein and fat metabolism to ensure cow nutrition and mammary gland health, and in regulating the secretion of stress hormones and protecting the intestinal barrier to mitigate the effects of stress, thereby reducing somatic cell counts [122, 123, 124]. Beneficial microorganisms enhance macrophage and neutrophil activity, regulate the secretion of anti‐inflammatory and pro‐inflammatory factors, and facilitate the normalization of somatic cell counts in milk [125, 126, 127]. For example, dietary supplementation with SCFAs reduces somatic cell counts and alleviate LPS‐induced inflammatory responses in dairy cows; however, effect sizes vary across studies [128]. The underlying mechanism involves inhibition of the NLRP3 signaling pathway, as detailed in Section 3.3.1. For instance, Bifidobacteria reduce somatic cell counts in milk by activating dendritic cells in the cow gut and promoting the differentiation of regulatory T cells (Tregs), thereby suppressing excessive inflammatory responses in the mammary glands [129]. Bifidobacterium bifidum inhibits excessive inflammatory responses in the mammary glands of dairy cows by activating intestinal dendritic cells, promoting differentiation of Tregs, and reducing somatic cell counts [130]. Bacillus subtilis reduces the risk of mammary infection by directly inhibiting the proliferation of Streptococcus agalactiae through the secretion of antimicrobial peptides, resulting in lower somatic cell counts in dairy cows [131, 132]. In addition, nutritional modulation can promote intestinal microecological balance and reduce mammary inflammatory responses via the gut‐mammary axis pathway. Notably, probiotic preparations (e.g., Lactobacillus casei Zhang and Lactobacillus plantarum P‐8) can promote cow health by maintaining intestinal microbiome homeostasis, increasing milk production, improving milk quality, and significantly reducing somatic cell counts in milk [133, 134].

Based on the delivery mechanism by which modulation of the gut‐mammary axis improves mammary health, the following application strategies have been popularized in animal husbandry (Table 2). Early warning models for mastitis can be constructed by monitoring dynamic changes in the intestinal microbiome. He et al. [14] found that intestinal microbiome disorders can induce mastitis in mice through the activation of the calcium‐ROS‐AMPK‐mTOR pathway, and that the elevated abundance of bacteria can serve as a high‐risk signal. In practice, dairy farms can screen for marker microorganisms in cow manure and combine machine learning algorithms to predict the probability of mastitis occurrence (By incorporating machine learning algorithms to predict the likelihood of mastitis, the reported prediction accuracy ranges from 94% to 98%.) and subsequently regulate the gut–mammary axis in a targeted manner to reduce the incidence rate and somatic cell count. Pilot studies of these technologies have been conducted in European and American ranches and may reduce economic losses by an average of approximately 12%–15% [142]. Collectively, interventions targeting the gut‐mammary axis enhance mammary health through three synergistic mechanisms: microbial metabolite signaling (e.g., SCFAs), immunomodulation (e.g., secretory IgA trafficking), and direct antimicrobial activity [135, 136]. Although probiotic efficacy in both ruminants and monogastrics is of interest, rumen‐specific challenges, such as maintaining probiotic viability in acidic environments, require tailored delivery systems [137]. Plant extracts have emerged as potent antibiotic alternatives by concurrently modulating multiple targets, underscoring the importance of integrating precision nutrition with scalable technologies for rapid translational impact [138]. Importantly, many mechanistic insights underlying these interventions originate from rodent or human studies [139, 140, 141]. Although conserved pathways have been confirmed in livestock, species‐specific differences in the gastrointestinal anatomy (rumen vs. monogastric), metabolic rates, and microbial ecology may alter the efficacy and optimal dosage of these interventions. Therefore, extrapolation from model organisms to production animals requires validation in the target species.

Vertical transmission along the gut–mammary axis improves offspring performance and health

Effect of animal milk on the intestinal health of young animals

The colostrum is rich in immune‐related factors, and immunoglobulins are key components. Colostrum contains relatively high levels of immunoglobulin A (IgA), immunoglobulin G (IgG), and immunoglobulin M (IgM), which account for 70%–80% of the total protein in colostrum [143]. These immunoglobulins can be absorbed directly into the circulation by the intestines of the offspring, providing passive immunity during immune system development [144]. IgG can bind to antigens on the surface of pathogens, activate the complement system, and promote phagocytosis and clearance of pathogens by phagocytes, thus providing immediate immune protection to the offspring [145]. Donald et al. [146] found that milk secretory immunoglobulin A(sIgA) enhanced passive immunity in piglets by modulating the intestinal microbiota. In addition, lysozymes in milk can hydrolyze peptidoglycan found in the bacterial cell wall, destroying bacterial structure, killing the bacteria directly, reducing the chances of offsprings coming into contact with pathogens, and enhancing their immune defense ability [147, 148].

Milk plays an important role in promoting intestinal immune system development in offsprings. Lactoferrin is an important component of milk that promotes the proliferation and differentiation of intestinal epithelial cells and contributes to the maturation of the intestinal mucosal barrier [149]. A mature intestinal mucosal barrier effectively prevents pathogen invasion and reduces the risk of infection. Growth factors in animal milk, such as Epidermal Growth Factor (EGF) and Insulin‐like Growth Factor (IGF), stimulate the development of intestinal‐associated lymphoid tissues in offspring [150, 151, 152]. EGF promotes the expression of receptors on the surface of intestinal epithelial cells, strengthens intercellular junctions, and improves the intestinal barrier function [153, 154, 155]. In contrast, IGF can stimulate the proliferation and differentiation of intestinal lymphocytes and increase the activity and number of intestinal immune cells, thus enhancing intestinal immune function and promoting intestinal development [156]. The current challenges of artificial milk replacements in addressing intestinal developmental disorders and immune deficiencies in neonates necessitate functional biomimetic strategies based on bioactive components that are naturally present in maternal milk [157, 158, 159, 160, 161]. Modulation of maternal gut–mammary axis improves offspring health

Offspring health can be improved by regulating the maternal gut–mammary axis (Figure 4). Animal milk secreted by the mammary glands contains specific microorganisms, such as lactic acid bacteria, which colonize the intestinal tract of young animals during lactation and shape the structure of their early intestinal microbiome [162]. Research has found that bifidobacteria in human breast milk can regulate the immune balance of Th17 cells in the intestines of young animals, thereby reducing the risk of inflammation [163]. This indicates that milk components from animals exert metabolic regulatory effects on the intestinal microbiota. Additionally, active immune substances in milk, such as lactoferrin and secretory immunoglobulin A (sIgA), inhibit pathogen colonization, maintain intestinal barrier function in young animals, and enhance nutrient absorption efficiency [164, 165, 166]. In the targeted optimization of milk components, adding lactoferrin or sIgA enhancers to the diet of lactating animals can increase the content of immune active substances in milk, significantly promoting intestinal barrier development and increasing the growth and weight gain of young animals [167]. Donald et al. [148] confirmed that animal milk sIgA influences immune development and prevents asthma by regulating the intestinal microbiota in mice. In terms of microbial vertical transfer interventions, maternal supplementation with Lactobacillus probiotics during pregnancy can colonize the intestines of young animals through the “maternal intestine mammary gland milk” pathway, shaping their early microbiota structure and reducing the risk of pathogen infections [168]. A meta‐analysis of randomized controlled trials and related studies revealed that maternal supplementation with probiotics can achieve strain‐specific vertical transmission to offspring through the gut–mammary axis, significantly increasing the abundance of specific probiotics (such as Lactobacillus) in mammary milk and potentially reducing pathogen colonization. Concurrently, the colonization of beneficial bacteria such as Bifidobacterium in the infant gut, and through the regulation of mammary milk components and immune factors, synergistically inhibits the proliferation of pathogenic bacteria, improves the immune system and gut microbiota of offspring, and reduces the incidence of infectious diseases, allergies, and necrotizing enterocolitis in offspring [169, 170, 171]. Wang et al. [172] found that feeding sows Lactobacillus reuteri regulates and accelerates the maturation of the early fecal microbiota composition of piglets, thereby improving their intestinal health. Additionally, adding lactoferrin or sIgA enhancers to the diet of sows can significantly increase the levels of immunologically active substances in animal milk, thereby promoting the development of the intestinal barrier in young animals and increasing their physical growth and weight gain [173, 174].

The efficiency of maternal to offspring microbial transmission via the gut–mammary axis is not uniform but is influenced by several factors. Maternal diet plays a critical role: dietary fiber and prebiotics (e.g., inulin and fructooligosaccharides) enhance the abundance of beneficial bacteria, such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, in the maternal gut, thereby increasing their availability for translocation to the mammary glands [135, 170]. Lactation stage also modulates transmission, as colostrum typically contains higher microbial diversity and load than mature milk, suggesting a window of enhanced transfer in the early postnatal period. Additionally, maternal stress, antibiotic use, and parity may affect the gut microbiota composition and, consequently, the pool of vertically transmitted microbes.

In addition to immediate health benefits, the vertical transmission of specific maternal microbes has been linked to long‐term productive traits in the offspring. In piglets, early colonization with Lactobacillus reuteri via maternal supplementation reduced post‐weaning diarrhea, and improved the average daily gain and feed conversion ratio during the growing phase [172]. In dairy calves, the composition of the initial gut microbiota, shaped by colostrum‐derived microbes, correlates with subsequent weight gain and age at first estrus [162]. Moreover, retrospective studies suggest that the early life gut microbial profile may influence lifetime milk production efficiency, possibly through the programming of metabolic pathways and immune setpoints. These findings underscore the potential of targeting the maternal gut‐mammary axis as a long‐term strategy for the genetic and nutritional improvement of livestock productivity, complementing conventional breeding programs. Thus, offspring health can be managed by regulating the maternal gut–mammary axis.

Species‐specific considerations in gut–mammary axis research

The gut–mammary axis has been investigated across multiple species, each serving distinct roles in the research continuum from mechanistic discovery to translational application. However, direct extrapolation of the findings between species requires careful consideration of anatomical, physiological, and microbiological differences (Figure 5).

Species‐specific characteristics of the gut–mammary axis in rodents, pigs, and ruminants. Key species‐specific features of the gut–mammary axis across three animal categories are summarized. Rodents are characterized by short‐chain fatty acid (SCFA) profiles dominated by acetic and butyric acid (butyrate acts as a histone deacetylase inhibitor), lack a rumen, and require validation in larger animals. Pigs share a human‐like gastrointestinal tract with hindgut fermentation and a balanced SCFA ratio; gastric acid degradation necessitates enteric protection, and once validated, they serve as a translational bridge to ruminants. Ruminants exhibit low butyric acid, high probiotic/metabolite loss (60%–90%) due to rumen degradation, making rumen‐protected formulations essential; cost‐effectiveness is the primary focus, and results directly inform production practices.

Rodent models (mice and rats) are invaluable for elucidating fundamental mechanisms, including immune cell trafficking, epigenetic regulation, and microbial metabolite signaling. The advantages of using rodents include genetic tractability, precise experimental control, and short reproductive cycles. However, rodent models have inherent limitations; they lack a rumen ecosystem, have different mammary gland architectures, and do not fully mimic the metabolic demands of high‐producing livestock. Therefore, findings from rodent studies should be considered as a mechanistic proof of concept that requires validation in target livestock species. To bridge this gap, monogastric livestock serve as physiologically relevant intermediates.

Monogastric livestock (pigs) share a close gastrointestinal anatomy with humans and rodents, making them valuable for translational studies that aim to provide mechanistic insights from rodent to large animal applications. Pigs are particularly relevant for studying maternal offspring microbiome transmission and neonatal intestinal development, as their physiology mimics that of human infants more closely than that of rodents. However, the ultimate target for production‐oriented applications is ruminants, which pose the greatest challenge for intervention delivery.

Ruminants (dairy cows, goats, and sheep) are the primary targets for production‐oriented applications. The rumen introduces unique complexities, including extensive microbial fermentation, biohydrogenation of unsaturated fatty acids, and metabolic interactions that can either enhance or attenuate the effects of dietary interventions. For example, probiotic efficacy in ruminants is constrained by survival in an acidic ruminal environment, necessitating rumen‐protected formulations or post‐ruminal delivery strategies. Similarly, SCFA profiles differ substantially between ruminants (acetate and propionate dominance) and monogastrics (butyrate enrichment), which may influence downstream signaling via G protein‐coupled receptor 41/43 (GPR41/43) receptors. These species‐specific traits highlight the fact that no single model can fully recapitulate the gut–mammary axis of production animals. A tiered approach, starting with rodent mechanisms, moving to pig validation, and finally to ruminant applications, is essential for successful translation.

FUTURE PERSPECTIVES

New discoveries in the regulatory mechanisms of the gut–mammary axis

With the recent rapid development of molecular biology, bioinformatics, and systems biology, scientists have gained a deeper understanding of the regulatory mechanisms underlying the gut–mammary axis. Epigenetic modifications, including DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non‐coding RNAs, have emerged as key mediators of the long‐term effects of gut microbiota on mammary gland function. Accumulating evidence indicates that traditional neural, endocrine, and immune regulatory pathways play an important role in the interaction between the gut and mammary axis, as do new regulatory factors, such as epigenetic modifications (e.g., DNA methylation and histone modifications) and gut microbiota metabolites [175, 176]. The oral administration of berberine increases the expression of miRNA‐145‐5p and miRNA‐34a‐5p in mouse mammary cells and reduces the formation of mammary glands in mouse mammary tumors [177]. In addition to miRNAs, long non‐coding RNAs (lncRNAs) have emerged as critical regulators of mammary gland development and function. The H19 lncRNA, for example, is an imprinted gene that controls mammary epithelial cell proliferation, apoptosis, and differentiation. Its expression is tightly regulated by DNA methylation at the H19/IGF2 locus and modulated by gut microbiota‐derived metabolites and inflammatory signals [178, 179]. H19 dysregulation has been linked to aberrant mammary tissue remodeling and increased susceptibility to mastitis, suggesting that lncRNAs may serve as a mechanistic link between gut microbial ecology and mammary health. In addition, Ivanova et al [178]. demonstrated that gut microbiota‐derived metabolites and immune signals remotely regulate the DNA methylation of mammary genes in female mice. For instance, SCFAs such as butyrate can inhibit HDAC activity, leading to histone hyperacetylation and altered gene expression, whereas folate and other methyl donors from microbial synthesis influence DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) activity and DNA methylation patterns. Specifically, the methylation dynamics at the imprinted IGF2R locus form a bidirectional network that influences mammary development, lactation, and inflammatory responses. Hypermethylation of the IGF2R promoter has been associated with reduced expression and impaired mammary epithelial cell turnover during involution. H19 encodes a long non‐coding RNA that regulates cell proliferation and apoptosis. Its methylation status is sensitive to gut microbial signals, and dysregulation may predispose mammary tissue to aberrant remodeling. Specifically, methylation dynamics at the imprinted IGF2R locus form a bidirectional network that influences mammary development, lactation, and inflammatory responses. For example, gut microbiota imbalance may interfere with the proliferation and apoptotic balance of mammary epithelial cells by inhibiting the demethylation of the H19 gene (imprinted gene), thereby potentially increasing the risk of breast cancer in humans, ruminants, and monogastric animals, though such pathology is rare in livestock [180]. These findings indicate the complexity and diversity of gut–mammary axis regulation and provide new directions and ideas for future research.

Research progress on intestine microbiota and mammary gland health

Recent research on the association between the gut microbiota and mammary health has revealed the multidimensional role of the gut–mammary axis in regulating the occurrence and development of mammary diseases through metabolic, immune, and epigenetic mechanisms. Mechanistic studies show that the human gut microbiota promotes the development of estrogen receptor‐positive mammary cancer through estrogen recycling mediated by β‐glucuronidase [180]. These cancer‐derived findings are used solely to illustrate conserved signaling mechanisms; the same pathways regulate lactation and mastitis in livestock. In contrast, metabolites such as SCFAs enhance mammary barrier function and alleviate inflammatory responses by inhibiting HDACs and activating GPR43 receptors [181]. Although estrogen‐dependent mammary pathology is not a major production issue, this mechanism underscores the importance of gut microbial metabolism in shaping the hormonal environment that influences mammary function and inflammation. Epigenetic evidence has shown that inflammatory signals associated with mastitis can reduce DNA methylation by regulating DNA methyltransferase (DNMT) activity and inducing DNA methylation reprogramming in the promoter region of the lactoprotein gene, thereby inhibiting lactation function in dairy cows [182, 183]. The human maternal gut microbiota influences the methylation patterns of genes related to mammary development in the offspring through mammary milk microbial metabolites, suggesting the potential for intergenerational regulation [184, 185]. In practical applications, strategies such as probiotic intervention (e.g., Lactobacillus rhamnosus) [186], dietary fiber supplementation [187], and microbiota transplantation [188] have demonstrated the potential to regulate the mammary immune microenvironment in dairy cows, pregnant mice, sows, and humans, thereby enhancing chemotherapy sensitivity. However, the species‐specific effects of the gut microbiota, spatiotemporal dynamics of host microbiota interactions, and standardized intervention protocols require in‐depth analysis through multi‐omics integration studies to provide new targets for the precise prevention and treatment of mammary diseases [189]. In addition, the gut microbiota can produce various biologically active metabolites, such as SCFAs and bile acids, which can influence the proliferation, differentiation, and apoptosis of mammary epithelial cells, thereby exerting a significant impact on mammary gland health [190]. Zhao et al. [191] found that bile acids produced by the gut microbiota of mice reduced Staphylococcus aureus‐induced mastitis. Future studies should elucidate the species‐specific mechanisms of gut microbiota‐derived metabolites in mammary pathogenesis, develop targeted delivery systems for microbial therapeutics, and validate clinical biomarkers such as fecal Aeromonas abundance coupled with serum sIgA to enable the precise prevention of mastitis across livestock species.

Potential application of the gut–mammary axis in nutritional intervention and health management

The regulatory mechanisms of the gut–mammary axis provide new strategies and directions for nutritional intervention and health management. First, mammary gland development and milk performance in animals can be significantly improved by regulating intestinal health [192]. For example, the addition of probiotics and prebiotics, which are intestinal regulators, can improve the intestinal microenvironment and increase nutrient absorption efficiency [193]. Second, nutritional interventions, such as amino acids, fatty acids, and minerals, can meet the nutritional needs of mammary development and lactation [194]. Simultaneously, the adoption of precise feeding management measures can reduce stress responses and disease incidence in animals, thereby promoting mammary gland health [195]. In addition, by monitoring physiological indicators in the intestines and mammary glands and analyzing changes in related metabolic products, early warnings and precise interventions can be provided to monitor the health status of the animals [196]. Research has shown that Stenotrophomonas maltophilia can increase the permeability of the blood‐milk barrier by activating the calcium ROS‐AMPK‐mTOR autophagy pathway, thereby inducing mastitis in mice. This also demonstrates that targeted regulation of the gut‐mammary axis may be an effective method for treating mastitis [197]. Javier de Andrés et al. [198] found that Lactobacillus salivarius PS2 significantly reduced the incidence of mastitis in human females. These potential applications provide strong support for improving animal production performance and ensuring the health of both animals and humans. Extending this concept to ruminant production, recent longitudinal studies on dairy cows have identified rumen microbiota‐derived alanine as a key metabolite linking gut microbial ecology to host energy metabolism and lactation performance [199]. Specifically, alanine deficiency due to rumen dysbiosis was associated with ketosis and reduced milk protein content, whereas alanine supplementation alleviated ketogenesis in vitro, reinforcing the potential of targeting specific microbial metabolites as precise nutritional strategies for metabolic health management in livestock [200, 201].

Despite the promise of gut–mammary axis targeted interventions in controlled studies, commercial translation faces practical barriers. Cost effectiveness remains paramount, as production and delivery costs must be justified by on‐farm returns, especially in ruminants where rumen degradation necessitates expensive protected formulations. Scalability is hindered by the need for a sophisticated manufacturing infrastructure to maintain probiotic viability. Standardization is complicated by protocol variability and inconsistent active ingredient concentrations in plant extracts, necessitating industry‐wide consensus through meta‐analyses and multicenter trials. Regulatory approval processes are lengthy and costly and producer acceptance depends on clear returns on investment, ease of use, and compatibility with existing farm systems. Addressing these challenges requires collaborative efforts among researchers, feed manufacturers, veterinarians, and producers focusing on cost‐benefit analyses, scalable rumen‐protected formulations, standardized protocols, and commercial‐scale demonstration projects.

CONCLUSION

The gut‐mammary axis represents a functional bidirectional network that integrates intestinal and mammary physiology through circulating microbial metabolites, neuroendocrine signals, and immune cell trafficking. This review systematically presented evidence indicating that this axis operates through three interconnected layers. First, microbial metabolites modulate mammary epithelial cell function via HDAC inhibition, GPR41/43 activation, and STAT5/PPARγ signaling. Second, immune cell homing, particularly CCR10‐mediated trafficking of IgA+ plasma cells from gut‐associated lymphoid tissues to the mammary glands, establishes a direct link between maternal intestinal immunity and neonatal passive protection. Third, neuroendocrine mediators coordinate nutrient partitioning and metabolic adaptation to support lactation. The translational value of these mechanistic insights lies in their application in livestock production. Targeted nutritional interventions, including probiotic and prebiotic supplementation, SCFAs formulations, and plant‐derived bioactive compounds, have demonstrated efficacy in enhancing milk protein and fat synthesis, reducing somatic cell count, and mitigating mastitis risk in dairy cows, sows, and goats. Moreover, modulation of the maternal gut–mammary axis during gestation and lactation offers a powerful strategy for programming offspring gut microbiota and immune development, thereby improving neonatal health and reducing reliance on antibiotics.

Despite these advances, several critical challenges remain. First, species‐specific differences in the gastrointestinal anatomy, microbial ecology, and metabolic demands necessitate the development of tailored intervention strategies rather than a one‐size‐fits‐all approach. Second, most mechanistic evidence has been derived from rodent models, which, although invaluable for pathway discovery, do not fully recapitulate the physiological complexity of high‐producing livestock. Third, the cost‐effectiveness and scalability of precision nutrition interventions in commercial production systems remain largely unvalidated. Fourth, the bidirectional nature of the gut‐mammary axis, particularly how mammary‐derived factors affect intestinal function, remains underexplored.

We propose three directions for future research. First, multiomics integration (metagenomics, metabolomics, and epigenomics) across species should be leveraged to identify evolutionarily conserved regulatory nodes that can serve as universal targets for interventions. Second, next‐generation delivery systems, such as rumen‐protected formulations, encapsulation technologies, and maternal dietary programming, should be developed to overcome species‐specific barriers to probiotic and metabolite efficacy. Third, real‐time biomarker panels should be validated to provide early warning of mammary inflammation and monitoring of intervention responses in precision farming systems. By bridging the mechanistic understanding with species‐adapted nutritional strategies, the gut–mammary axis holds transformative potential for sustainable livestock production. Realizing this potential requires close collaboration between nutritionists, microbiologists, immunologists, and animal scientists to translate laboratory discoveries into cost‐effective and scalable solutions that enhance animal health, productivity, and welfare.
