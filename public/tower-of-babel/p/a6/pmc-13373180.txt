Next-generation osteosarcoma models for precision medicine
Authors: Mohamed Saqawa, Miguel Vieira Coelho, Marta Lourenço, Claire Villette, David Barata, Lorenzo Apolloni, Silvia Panseri, A. S. Silva-Barroso, Rafaela Seabra, Diana Pacheco, Nuno Alves, Paula Faria, Bernard Staumont, Liesbet Geris, Carlos Mota, Monica Montesi, Tatiana M. Fernandes Patrício
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Osteosarcoma (OS) is a highly aggressive bone malignancy that predominantly affects adolescents and young adults. Despite the progress in conventional treatment approaches, such as surgery and chemotherapy, patient outcomes remain poor due to OS metastatic potential, chemoresistance and frequent recurrence. Although recent advancements in novel therapeutic strategies, such as molecular inhibitors, gene-based interventions, alongside immuno- and radiotherapies, have emerged in recent years, OS is still not well understood due to its complexity and heterogeneity. Tissue engineering and predictive preclinical models offer a good toolbox to study OS and produce patient-tailored therapeutic protocols. This review discusses the recent development of tissue engineering-based strategies and OS mimicking preclinical models like 3D in vitro models, organ-on-chip technologies, and predictive computational (in silico) models, ranging from mechanistic (white-box) to data-driven (black-box) and hybrid (grey-box) approaches. Based on these recent developments, researchers can better replicate the native OS tumour microenvironment, which opens the doors to a more representative high-throughput drug screening tools and patient-tailored treatment strategies.

Introduction

Osteosarcoma (OS) is a highly aggressive primary bone malignancy characterized by marked biological heterogeneity and complex interactions within the tumour microenvironment (TME). Despite advances in surgery and chemotherapy, patient outcomes remain poor, particularly in metastatic and recurrent disease, highlighting the need for more predictive and personalized therapeutic strategies. To address this challenge, a wide range of experimental and computational models have been developed to better understand OS biology and treatment response. OS is characterized by bone destruction and functional loss and its bimodal age distribution, affecting both children and adolescents, as well as older adults1–5. Several cancer predisposition syndromes (e.g. Li–Fraumeni syndrome, hereditary retinoblastoma, Diamond–Blackfan anaemia) and primary DNA helicase disorders (Rothmund–Thomson syndrome, RAPADILINO syndrome, Bloom syndrome, Werner syndrome) are OS risk factors, with germline mutations found in 18–28% of OS patients, particularly in younger individuals4,6,7. These inherited alterations may predispose to malignant transformation by compromising genomic stability and disrupting the normal differentiation programme of osteoblast-lineage cells.

Bone homeostasis (also known as bone remodeling) is maintained through the coordinated activity of osteoblasts (OBs), which form bone and osteoclasts (OCs), which resorb it5–10. Their interaction is primarily regulated by the RANK/RANKL/OPG axis, whereby RANKL expressed by osteoblasts promotes osteoclast differentiation, while osteoprotegerin (OPG) acts as a decoy receptor that inhibits this process8,10–14. Disruption of this balance contributes to pathological bone remodelling and is implicated in OS progression4,7,8,13,15–17. Tumour-derived cytokines and signalling pathways further amplify osteoclast activation and support a tumour-promoting microenvironment. For example, activation of the parathyroid hormone (PTH)/parathyroid hormone-related peptide (PTHrP) pathway enhances Transforming Growth Factor-beta (TGF-β) signalling, increasing RANKL expression and osteoclast-mediated bone destruction. The origin of OS is still poorly understood18; however, many studies suggest that pre-OB and OB-lineage cells may represent the cells of origin of osteosarcoma and that malignant transformation is favoured when normal osteogenic differentiation is disrupted19,20. During endochondral bone formation, mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) differentiate into chondrocytes and subsequently into osteogenic progenitors and osteoblasts; interference with this tightly regulated programme can therefore contribute to OS initiation. In this context, altered expression of key regulators such as p53 and RUNX2 may promote aberrant osteoblastogenesis, while also affecting cell-cycle control, apoptosis and lineage commitment19,21.

As OS is a multifactorial disease, the bone microenvironment actively contributes to OS progression through reciprocal crosstalk between tumour cells, MSCs, osteoblasts, osteoclasts and immune cells21. Tumour-derived and stromal cytokines, including SDF-1, MIF and TGF-β, can recruit MSCs to the tumour site and reprogramme them toward tumour-supportive phenotypes, including cancer-associated fibroblast-like cells. Moreover, immune-mediated bone remodelling is regulated by the RANKL/M-CSF axis, which promotes osteoclast differentiation and bone resorption, thereby reinforcing pathological bone turnover. Together, these alterations in osteoblast differentiation, osteoclast activation and cytokine signalling create a permissive microenvironment that supports OS growth, local bone destruction and metastatic progression19,21.

The mechanisms outlined above are selected to frame the biological rationale for the OS treatment and models discussed below; however, a broader and more detailed discussion of OS biology and pathogenesis is available in the existing literature19,20,22.

Current OS treatments, combining surgery and chemotherapy, achieve long term survival in over 60% of patients with localized disease, but for metastatic cases, which account for more than 85% of cases, this type of therapy remains limited1,7,16,17,23,24. OS can metastasize to various organs, most commonly the lungs and, less frequently, to bone or lymph nodes, with metastatic cells undergoing a series of critical steps to colonize secondary sites and form clinically detectable lesions25. In bone-to-bone metastasis, this process occurs via hematogenous spread, where cancer cells detach from the primary bone, enter circulation and resist anoikis (detachment-induced cell death) through molecular adaptations26. They are guided to distant bone sites by chemokine signaling and interactions with the bone marrow (BM) niche, where they then interact with MSCs, promoting a tumour supportive microenvironment through growth factor secretion26. Hypoxia and acidity also contribute to the metastatic behavior, with cancer cells disrupting normal bone remodeling by activating osteoclasts and subsequently releasing growth factors that sustain tumour growth26. OS metastatic cases consist of 74% with lung-only metastases, 9% with bone-only metastases and 8% with both. Five-year event free survival is below 40% for patients with a primary lesion and single lung nodules and under 20% for multiple nodules or metastases detected at other sites1,7,17. Novel advanced therapies

Current scientific effort focuses on discovering new therapeutic targets, developing less toxic chemotherapy drugs with innovative approaches to overcome the limitations of existing therapies and to provide personalized therapeutics for OS (Table 2)1,27–32. Gene therapy shows promise but faces challenges in developing efficient and nontoxic gene delivery systems against OS cells28,33. Chimeric Antigen Receptor (CAR) T-cell therapy, for instance, uses viral vectors to modify patient’s T-cells with a CAR gene that enables OS-specific antigenic recognition, combining gene therapy with immunotherapy in an immune-driven anti-tumour mechanism. Another example is oncolytic virotherapy, an in vivo strategy where genetically engineered viruses selectively infect and kill OS cells while sparing healthy tissue, causing oncolysis and spreading to neighbouring tumour cells. This stimulates a strong anti-tumour immune response and, although in early clinical development for OS, is considered for combination with other therapies. In suicide gene therapy, a non-human gene is delivered into tumour cells, which then produces an enzyme that converts a non-toxic ‘prodrug’ into a potent toxin that kills the cells34,35. In tumour suppressor gene replacement, the goal is to reintroduce a healthy, functional copy of Tumour Protein 53 (TP53), frequently mutated in OS, into the cancer cells, aiming to restore their natural ability to undergo programmed cell death (apoptosis)36. While conceptually powerful, both methods have been profoundly hindered by the challenge of achieving widespread and efficient gene delivery in vivo and they remain largely in the preclinical research phase. OS is characterized as an immunologically ‘cold tumour’, due to its inherently immunosuppressive tumour microenvironment (TME) and low infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes37. Additionally, the typically low tumour mutational burden in OS, which results in fewer neoantigens available for immune recognition, together with the intratumoural heterogeneity, complicates the development of effective targeted immunotherapies38–40. Currently, integrating different immunotherapies or combining them with conventional treatments, are considered the most promising strategies for improving outcomes in OS, though these approaches largely remain in the clinical validation phase. Anti- human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) CAR T-cells have shown success in preclinical and early clinical studies with anti-tumour activity, including proliferation and the release of effector cytokines such as Interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) and Interleukin-2 (IL-2), leading to OS cell lysis41–43. Research has also expanded to other targets like receptor tyrosine kinases44–46, disialoganglioside 2 (GD2)47–49, immune checkpoint molecule B7 Homolog 3 (B7-H3)49–53 and surface proteins54–56. CAR T-cell OS therapy, however, faces challenges like antigen escape (tumour heterogeneity leads to the loss of the target antigen), the risk of on-target, off-tumour toxicity against healthy tissues and poor T-cell trafficking and persistence within the TME. For this, next generation ‘armored’ CARs are being engineered to resist immunosuppression, through multi-antigen targeting to prevent escape and regional delivery (direct injection), to maximize local efficacy and safety. An alternative approach targets the innate immune system, exemplified by Liposomal Muramyl Tripeptide Phosphatidyl Ethanolamine (L-MTP-PE), also known as Mifamurtide, a liposomal immunomodulator that activates macrophages and monocytes to produce pro-inflammatory cytokines like Tumour Necrosis Factor-alpha (TNF-α) and Interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β), boosting their ability to attack malignant cells. Approved in the European Union as Mepact® since 2009 for the treatment of OS, Mifamurtide is administered as an adjuvant therapy after surgery and alongside post-operative chemotherapy57–59 Conventional therapy may also enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy. Chemotherapy can induce immunogenic cell death, whereas radiotherapy may improve antigen presentation and occasionally induce systemic immune responses61–63. Likewise, combining different immunotherapeutic modalities, including checkpoint inhibitors and CAR T-cell therapy, represents a promising strategy to overcome tumour-induced immunosuppression64,65.

Combination therapy is hindered by the increased risk of cumulative and unpredictable toxicity and requires careful patient monitoring, extensive clinical trials to optimize sequencing, dosage and drug combinations and ultimately the development of personalized regimens tailored to each patient’s genetic and immunological tumour profile to maximize efficacy while minimizing risks.

To bridge these translational gaps, next-generation preclinical models are being established to more faithfully recapitulate the biological, genetic and immunological complexity of human OS. Three-dimensional (3D) tumour spheroids and patient-derived organoid cultures retain the native tumour cytoarchitecture and cellular heterogeneity, offering physiologically relevant platforms to interrogate therapeutic penetration, resistance mechanisms and combinatorial treatment efficacy. The following section delineates the spectrum of advanced experimental models currently leveraged to investigate OS pathophysiology and therapeutic response.

Advanced models to study OS treatments

OS is a highly complex and heterogeneous tumour composed of cancer cells, stromal and immune components, embedded within a dynamic microenvironment defined by cell–cell and cell–extracellular matrix interactions, chemical gradients and mechanical cues66. OS tumours display marked spatial heterogeneity, with highly mineralized regions containing few tumour cells and less mineralized areas enriched in proliferating cells67. This complexity, together with intra- and intertumour heterogeneity, makes OS particularly challenging to study14. No single model can fully recapitulate all aspects of OS biology; therefore, complementary models are required to provide a comprehensive understanding of the disease. Besides their role in studying tumour biology, advanced models, particularly 3D in vitro models, have important translational applications by preserving key microenvironmental features and enabling more physiologically relevant assessment of drug response and resistance. Current in vitro models include two-dimensional (2D) cell cultures and 3D systems such as spheroids organoids and organ-on-a-chip platforms, while in vivo studies primarily rely on xenograft and transgenic mouse models66,68.

2D cell cultures

Conventional 2D cell cultures still represent the most used model for in vitro studies, because of their availability, cost-effectiveness, facility of manipulation and high-throughput options69. For these reasons they have been extensively used as initial screening models to investigate mechanisms of cancer biology and for drug screening tests70. It has been shown that, upon appropriate selection and controlled culture conditions, established OS cell lines can adequately represent the characteristics of the tumour they are originating from at the genetic level71,72 and, partially, at the proteomic level73.

One of the first established OS cell lines is Uppsala 2 (U2)-OS, which was first cultured in 1964 from a biopsy of a moderately differentiated OS obtained from the tibia of a 15-yearold Caucasian girl and was extensively characterized in vitro74. A second one, human OS (HOS) cell line, was first derived in 1970 from the distal femur of a 13-year-old Caucasian girl75 and has been genetically altered to increase its tumourigenic potential: using the chemical agent N-methyl-N-nitro-N-nitroguanidine (MNNG), the MNNG-HOS cell line was established76 and another cell line, 143B-HOS, was developed as a result of Ki-ras oncogene transformation of HOS74. Another widely used cell line, Sarcoma osteogenic 2 (SAOS-2), was derived from a primary OS in an 11-year-old Caucasian girl77 and, together with U2-OS, this non-transformed cell line is among the best characterized OS lines74,78. MG-63 is another well characterized cell line derived from a 14-year-old male79. MG-63 provide an appropriate model to study integrin subunit expression and cell adhesion, as well as production of osteocalcin80. Moreover, this cell line is currently used to generate 3D spheroids to model OS characteristics and to study drug responses81,82. Over time, additional cell lines have been established and characterized at the genomic, epigenomic and proteomic levels72,73,83.

Despite their widespread use, 2D cultures possess important limitations. Long-term culture and serial passaging promote genetic drift and selection, resulting in reduced heterogeneity. Moreover, growth on rigid, planar substrates alters cell morphology and signalling and fails to reproduce the interactions between tumour cells, stromal cells, ECM and other components of the TME66,68,84. For these reasons, in recent years the attention has been focused on the design and bioengineering of 3D models which can provide suitable platforms to closely mimic the complex biological interplay within the in vivo TME in and in vitro set up.

Spheroids and organoids

Spheroids and organoids are different types of cell-based 3D in vitro models, which differ in their structural organization, biological complexity and fidelity to the tumour of origin.

Spheroids are cellular aggregates derived from the self-assembly of cells suspended in a culture medium or a matrix that mimics the ECM, such as collagen, Matrigel or chitosan85,86, offering a tunable microenvironment in terms of both biochemical signaling and mechanical properties. These models enable the culture of cells in a 3D format, which promotes ECM deposition and increased cellular compactness mimicking the in vivo tissues. Moreover, spheroids replicate in vivo tumour architecture by developing oxygen and nutrient gradients from the periphery to the core66. Once spheroids reach a big enough size (around 500 µm diameter87), it forms distinct cellular zones, including a proliferative outer layer, a quiescent middle layer and a necrotic core, thereby reflecting the proliferative heterogeneity of solid tumours84. Accordingly, spheroids may be particularly suitable for modelling proliferative gradients and hypoxic cores, as well as poorly mineralized, high-cellularity tumour regions.

Spheroids models could be prepared by different techniques. For example, the hanging drop technique where drops of cell suspension are applied on a flat surface of a culture vessel which is then placed upside down, resulting in gravity force acting on the cells to promote cell aggregation84,88. Another widely used approach for generating spheroids is the liquid overlay technique, a method in which cell adhesion to surfaces is prevented by coating the surface of the culture plate with non-adherent materials like agarose, poly(2-hydroxyethyl methacrylate) (poly-HEMA) or by employing commercially available non-adhesive plates. In this setup cell aggregation is promoted, leading to the formation of spheroids within 1-3 days89.

In addition, to its relative ease of production, low cost and high reportability, OS cell spheroids showed increased resistance to conventional antitumour drugs (i.e. doxorubicin, cisplatin, taxol and taurolidine) compared to 2D cultures, as demonstrated by an increase of the Half-Maximal Inhibitory Concentration (IC50) values81,90. A study by Belisario et al. analysed the regulation of ATP Binding Cassette (ABC) Transporters B1(ABCB1) and A1 (ABCA1) in OS, which are responsible for drug efflux, causing the reduction of drugs intracellular accumulation and toxicity91. It was shown that 3D spheroids of U2-OS and SaOS-2 and 2D doxorubicin-resistant sublines, had higher levels of ABCB1 transporter, which is involved in doxorubicin efflux, compared to their corresponding parental cell lines grown in standard 2D conditions, thus impairing chemoresistance to drugs91.

Furthermore, spheroids could be used to replicate the heterogenous cell population within tumours. OS, together with most tumours, is composed of highly heterogeneous cell populations that comprise ‘cancer stem cells’ (CSC)92. CSC exhibit both stem cell properties and tumour characteristics, such as the ability to initiate tumours, dormancy, recurrence and metastasis. Consequently, CSC respond differently to anti-tumour treatments compared to non-CSC tumour cells, displaying a more resistant drug phenotype and playing a crucial role in treatment failure93–95. In this way, this OS spheroid model provide a more memetic representation of the OS which could be used for the preclinical testing of candidate therapies. In a notable study, Ozturk et al. developed a CSC tumouroid model using cluster of differentiation 133 positive (CD133+) cells isolated from the SaOS-2 OS cell line and cultured in agar molds96. Their results showed that the stem cell phenotype is maintained for longer in 3D cultures, allowing for a more relevant screening and targeting efficiency in pharmaceutical testing96.

Overall, spheroids models have been shown to better correlate with in vivo studies regarding sensitivity and tolerance to chemotherapeutics, resulting in higher IC50 values than 2D monolayer cell cultures97,98. In fact, 3D spheroids could offer a simple, high-throughput and reproducible model that exhibits key features from natural tissue, namely its compaction and the production of ECM.

Organoids, on the other hand, are defined as 3D in vitro stem cell-derived cultures that recapitulate the cellular variety, architectural organization and function of their in vivo normal tissue counterparts and with the ability to self-organize and self-renew99. Over the years, organoids of several tissues have been established, such as brain100, liver101, skin102, intestine103 and pancreas104, among others68,105. Compared with spheroids, organoids are generally more complex and are often derived from stem cells or patient-derived material, enabling a greater degree of structural organization and biological heterogeneity. Nonetheless, organoids may also share key features with spheroids, such as oxygen and nutrient gradients and spatial cellular zonation. Organoids can also be engineered to resemble a tumoral tissue and used to study cancer biology and drug response. These cancer organoids or tumoroids can be generated by genetic engineering of organoids derived from healthy patient tissues or from induced pluripotent stem cells68 or from adult stem cells of patient-derived tissues (patient-derived organoids or PDOs)68,106,107. To date, tumor organoids are mainly derived from epithelial tumors and techniques for generating non-epithelial organoids, like OS, still need further research108. An exception was presented by He et al. who successfully established a patient-derived organoid culture system from lung metastatic OS109. Their system was able to mimic the TME and at the same time maintained the phenotypical and molecular attributes of the tumor of origin109. Importantly, these OS organoids preserved key immunological features of the original tumour, including similar distributions of T lymphocytes within the organoid microenvironment compared with patient tumour tissues. Thus, organoids may be particularly valuable for capturing tumour heterogeneity, patient-specific architecture and stem-like subpopulations. Moreover, related organoid-based co-culture studies in other tumour types have demonstrated that incorporation of immune cell populations, such as natural killer cells, can recapitulate tumour–immune interactions and functional immune phenotypes observed in primary tumours, which make organoid models a possible candidate for screening potential immune therapy. In an observational clinical trial, an organoid-based platform has been applied to sarcoma biopsies and resections to evaluate chemosensitivity in vitro (NCT06064682). In this study, organoids generated from more than 120 sarcoma specimens displayed patient-specific growth behavior and subtype-specific histopathology, and drug sensitivity profiles correlated with clinical variables such as diagnostic subtype, patient age, lesion type, prior treatment history and disease trajectory for a subset of screened compounds. This type of approach illustrates how patient-derived organoids may support personalised treatment selection, help avoid ineffective therapies and provide actionable preclinical data to inform translational research and therapy prioritisation.

Scaffold-based and bioprinted models

Scaffold-based culture models are already successfully applied to study OS because they can mimic the complex microenvironment of bone specifically the mineralized components of osteosarcoma lesions. Both natural and synthetic-based biomaterial scaffolds can be engineered with macro- and microstructures that resemble the trabecular bone and thus are ideal to study bone mineralization110. Specifically, collagen and hydroxyapatite-based scaffolds are among the most used biomaterials for OS 3D cell cultures, given their natural presence in the in vivo tissue84.

In a study by Pavlou et al., the generation of 3D OS models named ‘tumouroids’ was described, which were geometrically compartmentalized to reproduce the bone cancer mass and its surroundings111. The 3D matrix was supplemented with the bone-marrow proteins laminin and fibronectin and NuOss® bone granules. The authors observed an increased invasion of OS cell aggregates of MG63 and 143B cells from within the bone-like matrix into the surrounding acellular BM-like ECM. The presence of bone granules influenced gene expression and changed the cellular responses to doxorubicin, reproducing key issues of the in vivo treatment, such as drug penetrance and doxorubicin-induced bone toxicity111.

A different approach in scaffold-based cultures consists of the formation of spheroids in the first place, which are then placed into scaffolds once formed. An example of this method is given by Bassi et al.82, which developed biomimetic scaffolds obtained by a biomineralization process of Mg-doped hydroxyapatite (MgHA) on self-assembling collagen fibers (MgHA/Coll) and porous hydroxyapatite-based scaffolds82. These scaffolds were then co-cultured with enriched CSC obtained by sarcosphere-forming cultures of MG-63 and SaOS-2 cell lines. The results highlighted that CSC were more induced to maintain their stemness features in both MgHA/Coll and hydroxyapatite biomaterials-based co-cultures, in comparison to 2D culture. The proposed model recapitulates the stem cell niche composition and architecture of native tissue and offers a drug-screening model that is closer to the in vivo microenvironment82.

Additionally, ceramic materials have been employed as scaffolds in OS in vitro models. In a recent study, Menshikh et al. fabricated a 3D-printed Beta-tricalcium phosphate (β-TCP) scaffold and used it to establish a tri-culture preclinical system112. First, primary BM–derived MSCs were seeded on the β-TCP scaffold; next, U2-OS, human BM MSCs, Y201 spheroids (in a 3:1 ratio) and human dermal microvascular endothelial cells were introduced to the scaffold. All cells were seeded in a 1:1 mixture of culture medium and 5 mg/mL collagen to facilitate attachment to the β-TCP scaffold. To mimic physiological stimuli, the authors compared static culture with dynamic perfusion in a bioreactor. Dynamic perfusion increased metabolic activity and induced extensive MSC-driven ECM deposition and spheroid integration. Gene expression profiling showed upregulation of angiogenesis markers and tumour-associated regulators in the tri-culture versus spheroid-only controls, evidence of a more in vivo-like tumour phenotype. Finally, when treated with doxorubicin, the tri-culture constructs accumulated significantly less drug in their spheroids compared to only spheroids seeded on the scaffold, demonstrating the model’s enhanced recapitulation of ECM- and cell-mediated drug-barrier effects112, thus it could be used a more representative screaning platform for potential therapies in clinical setting.

More recently, bioprinting emerged as a promising approach for recreating the spatial and temporal complexity of native tissues, mostly sourcing bioinks (i.e. cells and biomaterials) for the highly controlled deposition of cell-laden hydrogels constructs. In this regard, various works have been recently published aiming to develop bioprinted bone models, with a particular focus on extrusion-based and inkjet bioprinting techniques113,114. For example, extrusion bioprinting allows the deposition of highly viscous bioinks and supports the fabrication of mechanically stable constructs, making it suitable for bone tissue engineering. Inkjet bioprinting offers high resolution and speed but is limited to low-viscosity bioinks, which may restrict its use in load-bearing applications. Alternative methods, such as microfluidic bioprinting and digital light processing (DLP), have also emerged. Microfluidic bioprinting enables precise control over cell and material flow, while DLP offers high-resolution patterning through light-based polymerization, ideal for recreating fine structural details113–115.

In summary, scaffold-based OS models leverage the unique biochemical and biophysical cues of both natural and synthetic biomaterials to recreate key aspects of the bone TME, specifically the mineralized components. This enables a versatile platform to study tumour invasion, stem-cell niches, drug delivery and angiogenesis. Additionally, tumouroids and spheroid-seeded scaffolds and 3D-printed and bioprinted hydrogels further bridge in vitro cultures and in vivo validation, offering versatile tools for mechanistic research and preclinical screening.

Organs-on-a-chip

Organ-on-a-chip (OoC) technology is also a promising tool that is rapidly evolving and highly innovative to mimic in vitro the human organs' physiology and pathology through controlled and physiologically relevant microenvironments68,116. OoCs models are based on cell culture devices, often incorporating microfluidic features, that host multiple cell types hierarchically organized to form 3D tissues and/or organs. These devices allow precise control of crucial parameters such as vasculature-like perfusion, heterotypic cellular interactions, chemical gradient flows and mechanical forces117,118.

Over the years, different systems for bone-on-chip have presented key aspects of bone complex structure and physiology. Bone-on-chip devices have also been leveraged to recapitulate in vivo functional bone tissues and offer innovative approaches to serve as predictive models for testing drug efficacy and safety. On most of these systems, the patient-sourced BM MSCs are a preferred tissue for bioengineering of highly hierarchical constructs119,120.

These models range from simple tissue models to platforms incorporating multiple tissue types, vascularized and served by media perfusion120–122. Through complex chip geometries and different scaffold fabrication methods or scaffold integration, it is also being explored to bring further mimicry and spatial tissue organization123,124. For example, Galván-Chacón et al.124 used a 2PP-based scaffold derived from a non-contrast computed tomography scan to resemble the trabecular bone microstructure under a biomimetic thin calcium phosphate coating, leading to human MSCs differentiation and large ECM production over 3 weeks.

Therefore, the use of microphysiological systems is currently being set as an alternative to establish complex cultures of hematopoietic stem and progenitor cell compartments and the malignancies that can develop in the human BM120,125. In 2020, Chou et al. presented an on-chip model of haematopoietic dysfunction the BM chip may serve as a human-specific alternative to animal testing for the study of BM pathophysiologies. These systems presented a rich and heterogeneous population of cell types, comprising a fluidic channel filled with a fibrin gel in which cluster of differentiation 34 positive cells and BM-derived stromal cells are co-cultured, being surrounded by human vascular endothelium and perfused with culture medium on a parallel channel separated by a porous membrane126.

An example of application of Tumour-on-a-chip in OS modelling is given by Lu and colleagues, who developed a micro-OS through 3D printing using a bioink composed of decellularized OS ECM loaded with extracellular vesicles from human BMSCs and MG63 cells127. This micro-OS was further combined with a microfluidic system and integrated into a multistage biomimetic OS-on-a-chip that includes a built-in recirculating perfusion system. The authors were able to integrate BM niches, cell-cell and cell-ECM crosstalks and microcirculation into the model, thus providing an accurate representation of OS characteristics and a potential platform for drug screening127.

Additionally, organ-on-a-chip is one of the best models to be used for simulating the OS-immune cell interactions. For instance, a recent study from Jaiswal et al. provided an immunocompetent tri-culture osteosarcoma-on-a-chip (iTC-OS) model. This system was developed using a porous 3D silk fibroin–hydroxyapatite scaffold seeded with MG-63 cells, tumour-associated macrophages (TAMs) and endothelial cells, thereby partially recapitulating the cellular complexity of the osteosarcoma tumour microenvironment. When integrated into a microfluidic bioreactor, the model enabled dynamic perfusion conditions that simulate intra-tumoural mechanical stress gradients, further enhancing physiological relevance. Importantly, this platform allowed the investigation of OS–immune interactions, particularly macrophage polarization within the tumour microenvironment. Pharmacological targeting of TAM-associated pathways (CSF1R and PI3Kγ) using pexidartinib and tenalisib demonstrated the ability to induce TAM reprogramming from a pro-tumour M2-like phenotype towards an anti-tumour M1-like phenotype, as confirmed by cytokine profiling and gene expression analysis. This iTC-OS-on-a-chip model therefore provides a physiologically relevant platform for studying OS–immune interactions and for preclinical screening of immunomodulatory. Indeed, the possibility to combine a bone-on-a-chip co-cultures including OB, osteocytes, OC and immune cells in a complex ECM environment that can represent normal bone and OS pathological conditions opens great opportunities to study the crosstalk between cell types and inter-organ relationships128.

Since no single model fully reproduces the complexity of osteosarcoma, complementary approaches are required to capture different aspects of tumour biology and therapeutic response. Advanced 3D models, enhanced by co-culture strategies and incorporation of native extracellular matrix components, aim to better recreate the tumour microenvironment and improve drug screening capabilities84. Despite significant progress, in vitro systems remain unable to fully capture the multiscale and dynamic nature of OS. Computational modelling for osteosarcoma

The complex interactions between tumour cells and the bone microenvironment make osteosarcoma and other bone malignancies particularly challenging to study. In recent years, computational (in silico) models have emerged as valuable tools to investigate these processes, generate hypotheses and potentially guide therapeutic strategies129. These models integrate biochemical factors and biophysical processes to simulate and predict cancer progression, from onset to metastasis, including response to treatment130. Their predictive power depends on the current understanding of biological interactions, the quantity and quality of available data and model design choices131.

Subcellular modelling includes the modelling of Signal transduction networks. For instance, Sanchez-Diaz et al. used such an approach to identify OS survival-associated microRNAs after analysing high-throughput RT-qPCR quantitative expression from 27 OS patients132.

At the cellular level, models simulate processes such as proliferation, migration and cell death based on experimentally derived parameters. Romero Rodriguez et al., for instance, developed an in silico model describing proliferation and migration in 21 OS cell lines133.

At the multicellular level, models incorporate cell–cell and cell–matrix interactions, nutrient diffusion and spatial heterogeneity to reproduce tumour growth and invasion dynamics145134,135. White-box models: unpacking the mechanisms

White-box models, also known as mechanistic, hypothesis-driven or physics-based models, are constructed based on fundamental knowledge of the underlying biological and physical processes129. These models aim to explicitly represent the known mechanisms driving cancer progression.

Physics-based models often focus on biophysical processes such as the diffusion of nutrients and chemicals, mechanical stresses on tumours and cells and fluid flow in the circulatory system130,136. These mathematical models can be used to explore therapeutic interventions, drug delivery and interactions between cancer and the immune system. For example ordinary differential equations (ODEs) and partial differential equations (PDEs) are a fundamental basis for continuum tumour models137–139. They respectively describe the temporal or spatio-temporal evolution of quantities like cell density and chemical concentrations and are typically used to model processes like tumour growth, the diffusion of nutrients, oxygen and waste products and the response of cells to chemical gradients140–142. Using reaction-diffusion PDEs, Araujo and colleagues143 described concentration maps of TGF-B, RANK-L and bone matrix-derived factors over time in bone metastasis, considering specific cell type secretion (e.g. OB precursors secreting RANK-L), tissue-dependent diffusion coefficient and decay of the compound over time. Hu and colleagues144 used similar formulations to simulate diffusion of cell populations within the OS microenvironment. Another example of a white-box approach involves modelling mechanical forces. Although mechanical forces are of tremendous importance in bone physiology and remodelling and have been extensively modelled in studies on healthy bone145–148, consideration of mechanics in in silico modelling of bone cancer in general (Fig. 3a)149,150 and OS in particular144, are scarce. They range in scope from finite element modelling of the strength of a full metastatic human vertebrae150, to considerations of individual cell deformation149. Hu and colleagues144 used finite element modelling of fluid circulation within OS tumour modelled as a poroelastic material to extend their cell population diffusion models and include fluid-induced cell transport through the tumour.

In addition to the continuous formulations described so far, mechanistic models also include the discrete formalisms referred to as agent-based models, describing discrete behaviour of individual key players, most often the biological cells139,143,151. In their model, Araujo and colleagues143 considered individual cells of six different phenotypes (MSCs, pre-OBs, OBs, pre-OCs, OCs and prostate cancer cells) able to divide, differentiate and die. In a hybrid formulation, their agent-based module was coupled to the PDE system describing diffusion of biological factors as individual cells either acting as sources of the factors or modulating their behaviour in response to the computed biochemical microenvironment.

Black-box models: learning from data

In contrast to white-box models, black-box models or data-driven models are built primarily from experimental or clinical data, often without explicit representation of the underlying biological mechanisms129. These models seek to identify patterns, correlations or predictive relationships within the data152,153 and many of them draw from Artificial Intelligence (AI) approaches.

For instance, Consalvo and colleagues154 trained a deep neural network model to automatically detect pathological lesions on paediatric bone radiographs and differentiate them between Ewing sarcoma and acute osteomyelitis. Training supervision was provided by diagnosis confirmation through histopathological examination as a reference standard.

Zhao and colleagues155 analysed gene expression data from a large cohort of OS samples confronted to donor follow-up information and identified prognosis-associated genes using Cox regression analysis (Fig. 3c). They then used a combination of machine learning (ML) algorithms LASSO (least absolute shrinkage and selection operator) and RSF (random survival forest) to build a seven gene signature allowing stratification of patients between low and high risk of poor prognosis.

Relying on a similar approach, Zhou and colleagues156 used a bootstrapping strategy to select bone metastasis-related genes from a merged dataset of over eight hundred breast cancer samples (Fig. 3d). Then, they performed enrichment analysis to detect dysregulated pathways involved in bone metastasis based on these selected genes. High metastasis development risk scores were computed for each dysregulated pathway and combined to predict overall bone metastasis risk.

Grey-box models: combining knowledge and data

Many in silico models in cancer biology fall somewhere between the purely mechanistic and purely data-driven extremes. These are often referred to as grey-box, hybrid or semi-mechanistic129,157. These approaches combine aspects of both white-box (mechanistic principles) and black-box (data-driven components) modelling. In this framework, white-box models are fully mechanistic and explicitly encode biological or physical laws, whereas black-box models rely primarily on data to learn patterns with limited mechanistic structure. Grey-box models occupy the intermediate space by retaining an interpretable mechanistic core while using empirical data to estimate, calibrate or personalize model parameters. This is particularly relevant for complex phenomena like OS development, which involves processes across molecular, cellular, tissue and even organ levels. Multiscale models, which integrate different scales of biological organization or physical processes, often consider some level of empirical or phenomenological prior knowledge to mitigate their complexity.

Clear examples of such semi-mechanistic models are mixed-effects population models, where an experimental/clinical outcome measured in a large number of samples/individuals is captured by a mechanistic mathematical equation defining a structural model, coupled to a probabilistic model describing observed variability around the structural model. Challenges and future directions in integrating in vitro and in silico models of osteosarcoma

In silico models, ranging from mechanistic white-box approaches to data-driven black-box methods and integrated grey-box frameworks, are becoming indispensable tools for studying biomedical questions. By providing time and cost-effective ways to investigate complex biophysical processes and integrate vast amounts of biological data, they are key to enhancing our understanding of such complex questions. This is of particular interest in the multifactorial context of OS, which involves dynamic interactions between heterogenous populations of tumour cells, OBs, OCs, immune cells, stromal cells and the ECM, influenced by intricate signalling networks. However, except for promising examples highlighted in the previous sections, computational modelling of OS and other bone malignancies remains scarce.

For a better in silico model integration, we envision a cyclic in vitro–in silico information loop for OS that is based on the data extracted from biomimetic OS cultures, which would better parameterize the in silico models, whose output would in turn refine experimental design. For example, one could measure proliferation rates, invasion speeds and drug responses in a baseline 3D OS model, fit a computational model (ODE or hybrid) to these data and then simulate scenarios that are difficult to replicate in vitro, such as adding different cell types with different cell culture media conditions or changing material stiffness. Moreover, incorporating patient-specific samples into the in silico modelling would overcome some of the limitations of in vitro models, including the primary cells short life span and enable precision medicine workflows161,162. For instance, genomic and proteomic profiling of patient-specific samples with their drug response data could be integrated into the model (via multi-omics data) to tailor predictions and optimize treatment protocol. For example, Ramzy et al. proposed a personalized treatment approach for colorectal cancer based on the integration of ex vivo patient-derived organoid efficacy testing with mathematical modelling163.

In an effort to push forward, Rejniak and colleagues164 proposed, for instance, a Virtual Clinical Trial approach to predict OS chemoresistance by combining mechanistic simulations with advanced image analysis. In the development phase of their proposed framework, pre-treatment biopsies and post-treatment resection tissues would be imaged and individual cellular morphological and immunochemical features quantified. Based on the hypothesis that remaining cells after treatment are chemoresistant, a data-driven model would be trained to identify the features that are characteristic of the chemoresistant cells. A (semi)-mechanistic computational model predicting chemo response based on pre-treatment biopsy-specific features of chemosensitive and chemoresistant cells would then be developed and calibrated against post-treatment resection tissue data. As a result, in the translation phase of this framework, pre-treatment data could be used as input to the computational model to predict patient-specific chemoresistance and thus inform OS grading and therapeutic decisions.

Such multi-modal grey-box frameworks that bridge the gaps between molecular events, cellular behaviour and tissue-level processes hold great promise for understanding the different aspects of OS development and support advancement in therapeutic strategies. Efforts are also needed to translate these complex models into tools that can inform clinical decision-making, requiring rigorous verification and validation processes165.

Given the inherent heterogeneity of data sources, standardizing the inputs that feed in silico models is essential to move computational predictions from proof-of-concept to reproducible, clinically relevant tools. Currently, many models are parameterized based on literature sources that use different cell lines, animal strains and experimental conditions. Consequently, when aggregated, they may represent no single biological reality166. Closing this gap requires harmonized experimental protocols and metadata that include standardized sample handling, assay readouts, imaging acquisition parameters, units and quality-control thresholds and clear provenance records and ontologies for multi-omics preprocessing. Without input harmonization, model validation, transferability between labs and pooling of patient-derived organoid or clinical datasets remain severely constrained—hindering reproducibility, slowing discovery and preventing reliable translation into precision-medicine workflows.

Parallel to input harmonization is the need to standardize the in silico models themselves. Shared formats, minimal metadata, semantic annotation and benchmark datasets make results reproducible and models interoperable across platforms and laboratories, reducing redundant effort and accelerating validation167. A lot of effort was directed to address these challenges, including recent initiatives such as EU-STANDS4PM168 and EDITH169. For example, EU-STANDS4PM was a European project that aimed to evaluate data integration and in silico modelling aiming to develop standards, recommendations and guidelines for personalized medicine170. Such standardization guidelines will accelerate validation and trust, which are critical steps if multi-modal grey-box frameworks (linking omics, imaging and tissue-scale mechanics) are to inform patient-specific therapy. Related efforts, such as the Avicenna Alliance’s work on Good Simulation Practice, have further contributed to defining consensus-based principles for the development, evaluation and use of in silico trials171. However, standards succeed only if widely accepted and implemented across researchers, clinicians and software tools170. Additionally, the early engagement with regulatory and governance bodies helps ensure standards can be made certifiable without over-standardization that could limit the flexibility in this fast-moving field170.

In this context, a more explicit integration between in vitro and in silico approaches is required to fully exploit their complementary potential, bridging in vitro and in silico modelling in osteosarcoma.

A key opportunity in osteosarcoma research lies in establishing a tighter and more explicit integration between in vitro experimental systems and in silico computational models. While both approaches aim to capture the complexity of the tumour microenvironment, they often evolve in parallel rather than in synergy. Computational models typically represent tumour cells alongside key stromal and microenvironmental components, including osteoblasts, osteoclasts, mesenchymal stem cells, immune populations and extracellular matrix interactions, often incorporating biochemical gradients and mechanical cues. In parallel, advanced in vitro systems, such as co-culture spheroids, scaffold-based tumouroids and organ-on-chip platforms, are increasingly incorporating similar cellular and structural components. However, a systematic correspondence between computational variables and experimentally measurable parameters remains limited.

To enable effective cross-talk between these approaches, in vitro models should be designed with computational parameterization in mind. This includes standardized quantification of proliferation rates, migration and invasion dynamics and drug-response profiles, as well as controlled characterization of extracellular matrix composition, stiffness and spatial organization. Moreover, incorporating spatially and temporally resolved measurements, such as oxygen and nutrient gradients or cell distribution dynamics, would provide critical inputs for mechanistic and hybrid models. In this context, harmonized experimental protocols and metadata reporting are essential to ensure reproducibility and facilitate model calibration across laboratories.

Despite recent progress, current in vitro models still lack several features required to serve as robust validation platforms for in silico predictions. These include limited integration of immune and vascular components, insufficient temporal resolution of dynamic processes and the absence of standardized multi-omics datasets linked to functional readouts. Addressing these limitations will be essential to transform engineered tumour models into reliable systems for validating computationally derived hypotheses.

Conversely, in silico models can actively inform the design of in vitro experiments by identifying critical system parameters, such as cell-type composition, spatial organization or dominant signalling pathways driving tumour progression and treatment response. Model-based predictions can thus guide the selection of relevant co-culture configurations, the tuning of microenvironmental conditions and the prioritization of experimentally testable hypotheses. Ultimately, establishing a bidirectional and iterative in vitro–in silico workflow will be crucial to advance osteosarcoma research, enabling more predictive, reproducible and clinically translatable modelling frameworks.

Conclusion and outlook

OS is a therapeutic challenge due to its high metastatic potential, chemoresistance and high recurrence rates and although current multimodal treatment protocols combining surgery and chemotherapy have improved overall patient survival rates, outcomes for metastatic and recurrent OS remain stagnant. This review summarized recent therapeutic advances, including gene therapy, immunotherapy and other novel targeted and personalized therapies, whose clinical application is still limited by challenges regarding targeting mechanisms, toxicity control and patient stratification. This review also discussed the latest developments in OS modelling, highlighting a wide range of 2D and 3D in vitro systems. These include both scaffold-free and scaffold-based platforms, each contributing uniquely to mimicking the OS’s TME. Given their individual strengths and limitations, current evidence suggests that the most effective in vitro OS models are those that integrate multiple approaches and include co-cultures of various cell types to better capture OS complexity and heterogeneity. However, challenges related to standardization, scalability and the integration of vascular and immune components still hinder the broader implementation of most in vitro models. In addition, in silico modelling was discussed, ranging from mechanistic white-box systems (TME dynamics) to data-driven black models (patterns from omics and clinical data) and hybrid grey-box models. These models are promising for linking patient-specific data to predictive computational simulations. Future research should also focus on validating these models clinically, incorporating real-time patient data and ensuring accessibility to non-specialists through more user-friendly platforms. Future progress in OS treatment will require a multidisciplinary therapeutic approach that supplements the currently available therapeutic modalities with computational and biological modelling for clinically translatable and personalized OS therapies. Additionally, given OS heterogeneity, the integration of 3D in vitro and in silico models within a clinically relevant framework has the potential to customize the treatment for the patient’s needs and ultimately improve patient outcome and quality of life.
