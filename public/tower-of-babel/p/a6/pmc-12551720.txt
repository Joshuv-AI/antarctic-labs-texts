Anderson’s negative-U chemistry in amorphous silicon nitride: A complex system approach
Authors: Woon Ih Choi, Bokyeom Kim, Uihui Kwon, Yong-Hee Cho, Won-Joon Son, Hawoong Jeong, Richard Dronskowski, Dae Sin Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
P. W. Anderson introduced a negative-U (i.e., attractive electron-electron interaction) to describe the scarcity of singly occupied spin states in amorphous materials. To uncover the underlying chemistry behind this phenomenon, we combined structural sampling with quantum-chemical analysis of amorphous silicon nitride (a-SiNx) based on DFT. Our analysis demonstrates that coordination defects act as charge traps, with a density on the order of 1021 per cubic centimeter. These defects render singly occupied electronic states energetically less favorable than paired or fully emptied states, primarily due to electron donor-acceptor interactions. Furthermore, excess charge trapping occurs through chemical bond reorganization, which disrupts the balance of existing electron and hole traps. In addition, we found that in Si-rich a-SiNx, various Si─Si bonding networks exhibit power-law–like size distribution, where larger networks are associated with deeper trap levels. These findings explain why a-SiNx has been used as a charge storage layer in the charge trap flash memory.

INTRODUCTION

A negative-U parameter implies that electrons behave as if there were an attractive interaction, favoring the formation of fully occupied and fully emptied states over two singly occupied spin states. This parameter has been introduced in the Hamiltonian by P. W. Anderson to describe the experimentally observed scarcity of single-spin states in amorphous materials compared to their prevalence predicted by theoretical models (1–3). Therefore, amorphous materials characterized by a substantially reduced proportion of single spins, leading to a predominance of positively or negatively charged defects, became known as negative-U materials. Through continued research, amorphous silicon nitride (a-SiNx) has also been identified as a negative-U material (4–6). This material has been also used as a charge storage layer in flash memory, where its stable charge-trapping properties serve as the key mechanism for storing vast amounts of digital information in modern devices (7, 8). From an engineering perspective, this technology has progressed remarkably, facilitating digital information storage at increasingly lower costs. The precise atomic-level mechanism, however, remains a subject of debate. In device-level modeling, such as technology computer-aided design (TCAD) simulations, it is still incorrectly assumed that silicon dangling bonds (Si-DBs) act as charge trap sites (9). On the other hand, Anderson’s argument is predominantly phenomenological and relatively abstract, seemingly designed to enhance the model’s versatility across a broad spectrum of material classes, such as amorphous oxides and polymers (10, 11). The theoretical identification of negative-U behavior in crystalline As2Se3 represented an important milestone (3), as it was the earliest such case and led to extensive research on defects in crystalline materials (12). Over the years, defective structures in other crystalline materials have been identified as showing negative-U behavior (13–16). One should bear in mind, however, that negative-U was originally introduced to explain the electronic structure of amorphous materials. The key lies in the complex structure of amorphous materials, which continues to make the underlying chemistry responsible for negative-U behavior a topic of ongoing debate. For the specific case of a-SiNx, various defective features have been experimentally observed or are believed to play substantial roles in charge trapping. These include Si-DBs (17), N dangling bonds (N-DBs) (18, 19), N+ centers (20), coordination defects (CDs) (21–23), Si─Si bonds (24–26), strained Si─N bonds (27), and even Si─H bonds (28–30). On top of these structural features, chemical bond reorganization (20), polaron formation (29), and localized floating states (31) are also suggested as underlying mechanism of charge trapping. All of these structural features or mechanisms may be present, at least in principle. A comprehensive understanding of charge trapping in this material, however, depends on their relative abundance and the underlying principles governing their behavior. To address these questions from the perspective of negative-U behavior, we conducted a comprehensive analysis of a statistically meaningful set of amorphous structures in this study.

RESULTS

Identification of unusual structural features

In any amorphous structure generated theoretically via a melt-quench process, a noticeable number of atoms with unusual coordination numbers, as well as Si─Si bonds, can be observed. Hence, Si-rich a-SiNx can be regarded as a material characterized by various CDs and connected networks of Si─Si bonds within a hosting matrix of Si3N4 (refer to fig. S1 for an atomistic description). Therefore, as schematically presented in Fig. 1 (A and B), defective features contributing to the generation of trap states in a-SiNx fall into two categories, dubbed as either CDs or Si─Si bonding networks (SBNs).

Atypical atomic configurations are identified in Si-rich a-SiNx.

(A) CDs and (B) SBNs within a Si3N4-based host featuring fourfold Si and threefold N coordination.

To practically identify the key player atoms in amorphous structures, determining whether chemical bonds are present between atomic pairs is critically important. Although bonding analysis using Wannier functions is also possible (32), we chose to use Local Orbital Basis Suite Toward Electronic-Structure Reconstruction (LOBSTER) in this study because its quantitative and automated approach, based on Slater-type atomic orbitals, offers high efficiency for large-scale statistical sampling of amorphous structures. Based on the bonding analysis obtained from LOBSTER, Figure 2A shows seemingly disproportional relationship between interatomic distances and the strength of chemical bonds. As shown in Fig. 2B, however, it turns out that interatomic distances are unreliable descriptors for making such determinations (22). This also confirms that integrated crystal orbital bond index (ICOBI) (33) values (equivalent with quantum-chemical bond orders) is appropriate. Although atoms may be structurally close to each other, they may not necessarily form chemical bonds if atomic-orbital interference stays small. At the same time, relatively robust chemical bonds (ICOBI ≈ 0.6, little more than half a bond) do exist even at distances up to 2.9 Å, particularly for Si─Si pairs. This well-known finding reflects the importance of the long-ranged Si 3p valence orbital and the lowered sp mixing compared to the carbon atom. Therefore, to systematically analyze both CDs and Si─Si bonds, all results of the delocalized plane-wave calculations were unitarily transformed into an equivalent but local representation using atomic-orbital basis sets (34–36). Subsequently, the presence or absence of covalent bonding between pairs of atoms within a 3-Å distance was determined on the basis of ICOBI values, using a threshold of 0.30 (approximately one-third of a single bond). The reason for this ICOBI criterion is obvious because the ICOBI distribution in Fig. 2A indicates the presence of two hotspots of bonding, nonbonding, and a gap region where ICOBI data are sparse. In principle, although we can select any ICOBI corresponding to the gap region, a value of 0.30 enables the identification of CD configuration satisfying charge neutrality across plenty of amorphous samples. Although a bond criterion of 0.30 was used for the neutral charge state, reducing it to 0.20 allowed us to further identify CDs induced by excess charge (see fig. S5).

Given the frequent references in the literature to Si-DBs, it is crucial to examine the CDs to verify or falsify this model. As depicted in Fig. 2C, regardless of the N:Si ratio, both N and Si atoms are clearly more often overcoordinated than undercoordinated. Among the five different types of CDs, fourfold coordinated nitrogen (dubbed Nover) has been confirmed by x-ray photoelectron spectroscopy analysis to exist at a ratio of approximately 2 to 3% of nitrogen (37), and our computational results confirm this with a similar proportion of overcoordinated nitrogen (20). Therefore, it can be inferred that other CDs (besides Nover) also exist in proportions similar to those obtained from amorphous structures sampled here. It needs to be emphasized that such overcoordinated atoms serve as charge centers capable of donating and accepting an electron. For example, Nover (fourfold coordinated N) can donate one electron to become a positively charged cation, which functions as a hole trap, whereas most of Siover (fivefold coordinated Si) can accept one electron to become a negatively charged anion, thereby acting as an electron trap (38). We also found that, although usually rare but found in Si-richer samples, when Siover forms four or more bonds with Si atoms, its charge state shifts to +1 instead of −1. Most of the Siover, however, has a negative charge. Therefore, because of the attractive Coulombic interaction between the two, it was often observed that both Nover and Siover exist in high concentrations and tend to form direct bonds between multiple numbers of overcoordinated atoms in some samples (see fig. S2A), implying the presence of both electron-donating and electron-accepting pairs. Charge-transfer relationships arise not only from overcoordinated but also from undercoordinated atoms. As shown in Fig. 2C, undercoordinated atoms have distinct charge states: Nunder and Siunder can become energetically stabilized either as fully occupied states with two electrons or as completely empty states after donating an electron, even before any excess charge is introduced into the system. In amorphous samples, direct bonding between Nunder and Siunder is also observed as schematically sketched in fig. S2B. In this case, oppositely charged CDs form a direct bond. The strong ionic interaction amplifies orbital hybridization and drives a large level splitting, resulting in the main projected density of states (PDOS) peaks of N and Si being separated by almost 6 eV (figs. S2, C to E).

In contrast, the long-standing model based on Si “dangling bond” (DB) relies on a single defective state of undercoordinated, neutral Si involving singly occupied Si 3p orbitals. While the term “dangling bond (DB)” has historically been used somewhat loosely to include both neutral and charged states (4) for the interpretation of experiments, here, we refer to the DB strictly as the neutral configuration with a single unpaired electron. In terms of computational modeling, the charged configurations should be treated as distinct defect states, as their electronic and spin characteristics differ. According to the Si-DB model, singly occupied Si-DBs are assumed to become doubly occupied after capturing an externally supplied electron or emptied after capturing an injected hole. However, our analysis shows that charge storage involves a two-electron process associated with the complete filling or emptying of defect levels that originate from atypical bonding configurations, rather than single unpaired electrons. Consistently, electron spin resonance (ESR) measurements reveal that the concentration of spin states is two to four orders of magnitude lower than the electrically stored charge (17), thereby challenging the validity of the Si-DB model. The fascinating ESR experimental finding has been phenomenologically interpreted with a negative-U effect (attractive electron-electron interaction) at the charge center (5, 6, 39), but our study reveals that this behavior is a consequence of charge transfer involving other CDs.

As marked in Fig. 2C, each CD has its own charge state. Hover indicates a hydrogen atom simultaneously bonded to two Si atoms, a charge state to be understood in the same way as the charge state of an M-H-M (M = metal atom) moiety in some hydrides, also reported in the context of crystalline silicon and oxygen-deficient silica (40–43). Because of the bonding flexibility of the Si atoms, the CDs require further categorization. Their detailed statistics in terms of various coordination numbers and charges are provided in Fig. 2C. Although their concentration is on the order of 1019 cm−3, we find sixfold and twofold coordinations with charges of −2 and 0, respectively. Such CDs have also been identified in earlier studies of other molecular and bulk systems (44–46).

Charge neutrality sum rule in CDs

Among the four different coordination numbers of the Si atom, particular attention should be given to the two oppositely charged states (+1 and −1) associated with threefold coordinated Si. We found that the sum rule of CDs holds under charge neutrality, allowing the charge state to be inferred even without examining the PDOS of threefold Si. For visualizing the charge sum rule in our sampled structures, we plotted the number of electron donors (hole traps) versus acceptors (electron traps) with a two-dimensional (2D) Gaussian function (Fig. 3A) without taking into account Siunder. The thick blue diagonal region indicates that those amorphous samples satisfy the sum rule even without considering Siunder. These structures (i) do not include any Siunder atoms, (ii) contain an even number of oppositely charged Siunder atoms that compensate each other, or (iii) include charge-neutral twofold coordinated Si atoms. Because all structural samples satisfy the charge neutrality sum rule, in the plot, the region just above the diagonal (upper left) corresponds to Siunder donating electrons (hole trap), whereas the region just below the diagonal (lower right) corresponds to Siunder accepting electrons (electron trap). If we plot the same data only with the structures containing Siunder (see Fig. 3B), then the off-diagonal region exhibits stronger intensity than the diagonal region, highlighting the electron donor/acceptor role of Siunder. Figure 3 (C to E) exemplarily shows that, depending on the surrounding charge-defect configuration and coordination numbers, Siunder could have three different charge states. To clearly present the configurations of charge defects in the amorphous structure, the numbers of under- and overcoordinated atoms are shown using empty and filled bars, respectively, along with their associated charge contributions. Bars pointing upward and downward represent overcoordination and undercoordination, respectively. To exemplify, the configuration of Fig. 3C has one Nunder and one Siunder, and since Nunder is the electron acceptor (electron trap), Siunder must be the electron donor (hole trap) as confirmed by the Siunder PDOS. Because the configuration in Fig. 3D has two Nover donating two electrons (two hole traps), in addition to one electron acceptor Nunder (electron trap), Siunder needs to accept one more electron for charge neutrality. This is also confirmed by the PDOS of Siunder. The configuration in Fig. 3E has one Nover and Nunder pair which donates and accepts one electron and satisfies charge neutrality. Therefore, it can be inferred that twofold coordinated Siunder is a charge neutral CD. The PDOS confirms that twofold coordinated Siunder generates four levels by coupling with surrounding states, two below the Fermi level and the other two above, to be interpreted as orbitals of occupied Si 3s and unoccupied Si 3p character, indicating an overall charge-neutral configuration. As shown in fig. S3, these levels further hybridize with nearby states of similar energy—the Si 3s level is coupled with N 2p orbitals and the Si 3p level with either other Si 3p orbitals or the π-bonding states of Si─Si bonds.

Building on insights from the previous study (20), we assumed the validity of a two-electron process and, accordingly, generated amorphous structures via molecular dynamic simulations without spin polarization. We further examined this assumption by performing spin-polarized quenching to see whether Si-DBs and their associated spin states could survive the process.

For systems with an N/Si ratio of 1.2, we performed quenching simulations on 100 samples without hydrogen and another 100 samples with ~5% hydrogen. In the hydrogen-free case, using a 2-fs time step, the systems were quenched from 3000 to 0 K over 9000 steps. In all but six samples, the spin states vanished throughout quenching. For the remaining six samples, extending the quenching duration to 15,000 or 30,000 steps resulted in complete spin annihilation in the quenched structures. Figure 4 (A to C) shows one such representative case, where spin polarization mainly emerges at the temperature higher than 2000 K as long as the quenching rate is slow enough.

Si-DB spins are unstable and naturally vanish during quenching when the rate is sufficiently slow.

(A to C) In hydrogen-free structures, spin states can survive under rapid quenching conditions, but spin only emerges at a high temperature of >2000 K, when the quenching rate is reduced. (D to F) In structures containing ~5% hydrogen, even under more rapid quenching, Si-DBs with single spin states are eliminated in the course of quenching.

In the amorphous samples with hydrogen, we expected that even more rapid quenching rate would annihilate spin polarization due to the passivating role of hydrogen. When quenched over 3000 steps (1-fs time step, hydrogen assigned the mass of tritium to stabilize dynamics), spin states disappeared in all but two samples. In those two cases, further extending the quenching time likewise led to complete spin elimination throughout quenching as shown in Fig. 4 (D to F). While previous studies have reported the emergence of negative-U behavior in amorphous silicon nitride by deliberately constructing odd-electron configurations with persistent dangling bonds (47), our spin-polarized molecular dynamic simulations show that such spin-polarized states are not preserved during quenching. Therefore, such findings should be interpreted with caution, especially given the possible influence of artificially constrained or nonequilibrium spin configurations.

This consistent annihilation of Si-DB spins during quenching is attributed to their inherent energetic instability, which drives the system toward alternative CD configurations. For an effective demonstration of this energetic preference and the instability of dangling bonds, we intentionally generated the latter species by removing hydrogen atoms from the as-sampled structure. This homolytic bond cleavage leaves unpaired electrons in singly occupied atomic orbitals (dangling bonds) at each atomic site. For this purpose, we chose one structural sample without CD. In case the structure was relaxed after single (neutral) H atom removal either from Si─H or N─H, as shown in Fig. 5 (A to C), the system always had a single spin (1 μB) given one unpaired electron remaining in the atomic orbital, a natural consequence of a system with odd number of electrons. The relative total energy in Fig. 5D shows that a dangling bond on Si is energetically more stable than on N, because the higher electronegativity of nitrogen makes its dangling bond less favorable. For the 15 different types of two H-atom removal, relative energies and magnetic moments are presented in Fig. 5E. The charge-defect configuration of a fully relaxed structure is presented with a histogram consisting of filled (overcoordination) and empty bars (undercoordination). With the exception of four cases that ended up with two Si-DBs, most structures reconfigure their charge-defect configuration through structural relaxation. As given by the filled circles in Fig. 5E, various pairs of dangling bonds geometrically relax upon energetic gain into a state without a dangling bond. The configuration in Fig. 5G has two Si-DB states generated by two Siunder (energy zero configuration). However, other configurations in Fig. 5 (F, H, and I) do not have dangling bonds, because they form the energetically preferred charged donor-acceptor pairs. Therefore, we were able to identify the same number of electron and hole traps. Those CDs also directly or indirectly generate defect states in the bandgap as marked in Fig. 5 (F, H, and I) (see fig. S4 for the details).

As shown in Fig. 2C, a considerable number of charge defects around 3 to 4 × 1021/cm3 already exist in the form of charge traps and satisfy charge neutrality. Therefore, trapping of excess charge from outside occurs by altering the numbers of positive (cationic) and negative (anionic) charge traps, resulting in a nonzero net charge. Our previous research provided a detailed analysis of a specific case where the trapping of two electrons is accompanied by the reorganization of chemical bonds (20). In this process, a fourfold coordinated, positively charged, cationic nitrogen changes to a threefold configuration, resulting in the formation of a fivefold coordinated, negatively charged, anionic silicon. Further analysis of charge trapping in various sample structures has confirmed that chemical bond reorganization is the fundamental mechanism enabling stable charge trapping. The processes through which this occurs differ a lot, however. Both the types of charge traps (positive and negative) and the corresponding pathways for bond reorganization are highly diverse. For instance, trapping two electrons might involve two existing positive charge traps to disappear, the creation of two additional negative charge traps, or even the formation of two positive charge traps alongside four additional negative charge traps. In some cases, the process can be as simple as a positively charged Siunder maintaining its coordination number while becoming negatively charged upon receiving two electrons. Although there were instances where localized charge traps did not form and electrons occupy delocalized bands, in more than 80% of the sample structures, charge trapping occurred with changes in CD configuration. As shown in fig. S5, the delocalization of excess charge can be tracked through the reorganization energy, which takes relatively smaller values compared to those associated with charge trapping. Given the limitation of amorphous cell size, our modeling examined an excess electron or hole trap density of 2 × 1021 cm−3, whereas experiments typically observe excess charge trapping below 1020 cm−3, charge trap mechanisms with some of the highest reorganization energies are likely to dominate the charge trapping process (see fig. S5). For the details of the charge neutrality sum rule and CD configuration change upon trapping of two electrons and two holes, see tables S1 and S2 and fig. S5 in the Supplementary Materials.

Si chain and power-law behavior of SBNs

The other important ingredient participating in generating and stabilizing defect states is Si─Si bonding. The stoichiometry of the Si-rich charge-trap layer always dictates a certain number of Si─Si bonds. For example, in the case of N:Si = 1.2, our simulation cell has 50 Si and 60 N atoms. Assuming regular coordination, this requires 4 × 50 = 200 bonds for Si and 3 × 60 = 180 bonds for N atoms. Given a higher priority to the energetically more stable Si─N bond, ideally, there could be 180 Si─N bonds, so the remaining 20 bonds for Si can be satisfied with 10 Si─Si bonds. Attention must be paid to the spatial Si─Si connectivity since a large number of Si─Si bonds connected with each other generate deep trap levels. As shown in Fig. 6 (A and B), this can be easily demonstrated with a molecular Si chain passivated with NH2. By generating a N-Si3 moiety at the tail of molecular Si chain and using the N 1s level of the central N atom as the energy reference, we can indirectly examine how highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO) and lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) levels of Si chain might shift from either side of the band edge when they are embedded in bulk SiNx. Contrary to the bare Si chain that generates an occupied deep level possibly ending up with stable hole trapping (represented as the HOMO-level upshift as the Si chain becomes longer), the Si chain directly bonded to the fourfold coordinated N contributes to generating an unoccupied deep level which can lead to electron trapping. The difference between these two cases can be understood in terms of the bonding characteristics of the involved orbitals. As shown in Fig. 6A, the HOMO spanned by Si 3p orbitals has σ bonding character, while the LUMO is of the π bonding type between the same orbitals. This characteristic behavior of Si atoms is also observed in the SBN found in actual amorphous bulk materials. For example, we presented one SBNs bonded with fourfold N (N+ center) and the PDOS on each Si atoms belonging to it (Fig. 6C). The energy window corresponds to the bandgap region of crystalline Si3N4 roughly estimated with the H density of state belonging to N─H and Si─H bonds and also pCOHP (project crystal orbital Hamilton population) of fourfold Si and threefold N pairs. We can recognize that a total of six states is generated in the bandgap, even without any Siunder. Considering further filling of near band-edge regions with other defective states such as short Si─Si chains, we can still conclude that at least three or four states are generated in the bandgap as a result of the interaction between SBN (size 6) and Nover.

To figure out the distribution of the concentration with respect to the size of SBNs, we collected those data for each stoichiometry. Unexpectedly, upon increasing the SBN size, the density decreases following a power law (48). Since the seminal work by Barabási et al. (48), it is known that many complex systems exhibit this behavior. Therefore, the amorphous material SiNx:H adopts the same complex system nature that statistical physics and network science have been dealing with (49, 50). We also note that there is a certain cutoff size beyond which the formation of larger SBNs is suppressed (see orange data points and arrows in Fig. 7, showing suppressed formation of large-size SBNs), and the cutoff size decreases with an increasing H content. The small cutoff could lead to the formation of shallow electronic levels decreasing the probability of hole trapping by depopulation of the level, since its depth depends on the size of the Si chain (see Fig. 6A). From the perspective of optimizing device performance, two compositional tuning directions appear promising: One is toward Si-richer compositions, i.e., a decreasing N:Si ratio, and the other one is a decreasing H content. As shown in Fig. 7D, a smaller N:Si ratio of 1.115 makes the power-law’s exponent (γ) decrease slightly and also increase its cutoff size, so the power law extends up to about 12. Since the criterion for a power law in statistical physics is that the scaling behavior is maintained over an interval of at least one decade (Δx ≥ 10) on the x axis, this satisfies the rigorous definition of a power law.

We speculate that the fundamental mechanism behind the power-law distribution is related to the HOMO-LUMO gap of SBNs or Si─Si chains in general. As molecules with smaller HOMO-LUMO gaps, that is, smaller chemical hardnesses (51) exhibit higher reactivity, larger SBNs are expected to have lower energy barriers for chemical reaction, thereby facilitating their growth.

As shown in Fig. 7, increasing the Si content leads to a decrease in the power-law exponent, indicating a higher probability of forming larger SBNs. Notably, compositions richer in Si than x = 1.115 highlight the particular significance of the N/Si = 1.0 case. Given that each Si atom can form four bonds and each N atom three, this composition corresponds to an average of half a Si─Si bond per Si atom. By conceptualizing each Si atom as a “node” and each Si─Si bond as a “link,” the system can be modeled as a 3D network. In the conventional Erdős-Rényi (ER) random network framework, the emergence of a giant component occurs when the average number of links per node reaches 0.5. Unlike conventional network models, however, in which nodes can form unlimited connections, atoms are subject to strict chemical valency constraints. Therefore, rather than characterizing the network by the number of connections, we focus on the number of Si─Si bonds connected—that is, the size of each SBN cluster—and analyze their size distribution. This approach reveals a clear power-law behavior. Given that N/Si = 1.0 coincides with the critical connectivity threshold in the ER model, it is plausible that this composition facilitates the emergence of a broad power-law regime in the SBN size distribution.

Because of the limited size of unit cells tractable in density functional theory (DFT) simulations, it is challenging to directly observe large SBNs within a 116-atom cell. To address this, we generated 100 amorphous structures via melt-quench simulations at N/Si = 1.0 with ~5% hydrogen content and then expanded the simulation cells to 2 × 2 × 2. Following a 1-ps melting and a 3-ps quenching of expanded structures, we then analyzed the resulting SBN size distributions. Because of computational cost, only 50 of these structures were examined in detail. As shown in Fig. 7E, the size distribution of SBNs follows a power-law with an exponent of 1.69, extending up to a size of about 28.

In Fig. 7E, we also observe increasing scatter in the log-log plot for larger SBN sizes. This deviation suggests that as SBNs grow, they evolve beyond linear chains into more branched and morphologically diverse structures. As a result, their physical extent and geometric complexity may influence further growth, beyond simple considerations of chemical hardness. Our simulations revealed SBNs as large as size 40, and we anticipate that by modeling larger unit cells and expanding the sample size with N/Si = 1.0, the power-law regime could be further extended.

DISCUSSION

Our systematic DFT-based sampling of hundreds of amorphous SiNx structures with varying compositions demonstrates that charge trapping is most consistently and reasonably explained in terms of chemical bond reorganization (20) involving CDs. Within this framework, each type of CD has its own characteristic charge state and concentration, and charge trapping arises from defect reconfiguration rather than from isolated defect states. This perspective provides a unified interpretation of diverse trapping behaviors observed across different stoichiometries.

This view also clarifies the long-standing debate surrounding the Si-DB model. From a rigorous computational chemistry standpoint, singly occupied Si-DBs are energetically unstable and occur only at very low concentrations, making them unlikely to play a dominant role in charge trapping. Instead, Si-DBs readily reconfigure into more stable CDs, and the transformations among these defects govern the observed trapping processes. Therefore, we propose that the coordination defect–based model presented here offers a more robust and physically grounded alternative to the traditional Si-DB picture. The negative-U features long discussed in the literature should therefore be understood not as an intrinsic property of threefold Si only but as a phenomenological consequence of charge exchange between threefold Si and nearby CDs. This interpretation highlights that negative-U behavior emerges as a natural consequence of coordination defect chemistry, rather than solely from Si-DBs.

Our discovery of SBNs and their size distribution provides a particularly important perspective, showing that amorphous SiNx must be understood as a complex system in which SBNs not only interact with diverse CDs to give rise to charge-trapping phenomena but also form the physical backbone that governs charge transport. The robust power-law behavior near N/Si = 1.0 stoichiometry is of particular importance for the application of amorphous SiNx in resistive switching and neuromorphic devices (52, 53). Among various models proposed to describe charge transport, the space charge limited current (SCLC) model offers the best agreement with experimental behavior (54). Notably, one variation of the classical SCLC model assumes that high-resistance states correspond to smaller filaments, whereas low-resistance states correspond to larger filaments. This concept of a percolating conduction network is consistent with our findings, where the structural entities responsible for transport are the large-size SBNs rather than the Si-DBs traditionally assumed (55).

The emergence of power-law behavior near this critical composition is a hallmark of complex systems and network science, underscoring that amorphous SiNx must be understood as more than a collection of isolated defects. Our findings suggest that the intrinsic behavior of amorphous materials, in general, should be viewed through the lens of complex systems, where charge trapping and transport emerge from the cooperative interplay of diverse local motifs and extended networks. This perspective offers an alternative framework for interpreting the long-debated negative-U behavior in SiNx and for guiding the design of functional amorphous materials in memory and neuromorphic applications.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Sampling of amorphous structures

To analyze the statistics based on the N:Si ratio and H atom content, we computationally generated 100 amorphous structures for each stoichiometry using ab initio molecular dynamics (AIMD) with Vienna Ab initio Simulation Package (VASP) (56). In this process, we disabled spin polarization, as our previous study indicates that the charge trapping mechanism is a two-electron process. On purpose, we enabled spin polarization when intentionally detaching H atoms from Si or N, however, and also when examining whether singly occupied spin states survive the quenching process. While performing AIMD melt-quench, we often detected the formation of N2 molecules, which is undesirable for the statistics as well as for the electronic trap analysis. Therefore, among the amorphous structures generated for the previous study, we chose four such structures without N2 molecules. After fine tuning for targeted stoichiometry by adding, deleting, or substituting one element with another, we further ran 30 ps of AIMD at 3000°K to generate structures for use in the subsequent melt-quench process. The unit cell for amorphous silicon nitride [(a-SiNx:H) (x = 1.200, H ≈ 5%)] contains 50 Si, 60 N, and 6 H atoms. For x = 1.115, two N atoms were replaced with Si, keeping the total number of atoms the same. In the case of x = 1.333, the amorphous structures contain 48 Si, 64 N, and 6 H atoms, while for x = 1.000, the unit cell contains 54 Si, 54 N, and 6 H atoms. As reported in the literature, H atoms from the precursor can remain in a-SiNx; therefore, we generated samples both with and without H atoms. Since it takes some time to stabilize the modified or edited starting geometry, the initial 6 ps of data was discarded. Then, one structure was selected every 1 ps to be used as the initial structure for the subsequent melt-quench process, hence preparing a total of 25 initial structures for the following melt-quench. Since we started with four different samples, eventually we would have 100 different amorphous samples for one stoichiometry. During melt-quench, to minimize memory effects, AIMD was conducted at 5000 K for 1 ps under the canonical (NVT) ensemble in which the number of atoms (N), system volume (V), and temperature (T) were kept constant, followed by melting at 3000 K for 10 ps and then quenching down to 0 K for 60 ps. Since NPT molecular dynamics, in which the number of atoms (N), pressure (P), and temperature (T) were kept constant, was employed for the sampling of initial structures, each starting structure has a different cell shape and volume. Therefore, although we performed relatively short time (1 ps) of premelting at 5000 K (NVT), this would help randomize the atomistic structure with the size and shape of the unit cell fixed. Except for this premelting at 5000 K (NVT), the volume and shape of the unit cell were allowed to change over time (NPT). This leads to the Gaussian type of density distribution in the amorphous samples as shown in Fig. 8 (A and B). Moreover, as shown in Fig. 8C, the average mass densities of different stoichiometry follow the trend found in the experiment (57). These data also show that the H content makes the mass density of a-SiNx:H lower.

The mass density of amorphous a-SiNx:H samples follows a Gaussian distribution, with the mean values tracking the experimental trend across different stoichiometries.

The mass density of sampled structures exhibits a Gaussian distribution for both N:Si ratios of (A) 1.200 and (B) 1.000, each containing ~5 atomic % H (bin size = 0.04 g cm−3). (C) The modeling results closely follow the experimentally measured density trend (57), although higher H concentrations yield slightly lower densities.

To accelerate AIMD, the Γ-only k-point sampling scheme was applied, and the electronic convergence criterion was set to 1 × 10−4 eV. For the subsequent structural relaxation and analysis, however, the final structure derived from quenching was relaxed using a 2 by 2 by 2 k-points sampling grid and an electronic convergence criterion of 1 × 10−7 eV. A plane wave basis set with a cutoff energy of 400 eV was used throughout the modeling. For a more reliable description of the bandgap, electric charge, and polarization, the meta-generalized gradient approximation (meta-GGA) regularized- restored strongly constrained and appropriately normed (r2SCAN) (58, 59) exchange-correlation functional was used throughout the calculations. For the bandgaps of the two lowest-energy phases of crystalline Si3N4, hexagonal P63/m (α phase) and trigonal P31c (β p1hase), the r2SCAN functional yields 5.05 and 5.38 eV, respectively. Compared to those of the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) functional (4.30 and 4.70 eV) with lower computational cost, r2SCAN practically coincides with the experimentally measured optical gaps of 5.0 to 5.2 eV (α phase) and 5.4 to 5.8 eV (β phase) (60).

To derive statistical figures, we only selected data from amorphous structures where N2 molecules were absent. Although the melt-quench process was carefully designed, 0 to 16% of all amorphous structures contained N2 molecules, perfectly mirroring the reducing conditions since 945 kJ mol−1 is released upon forming the N≡N triple bond. Also, a substantial increase in entropy occurs at high temperatures due to the release of gas molecules.

To validate the amorphous structures, we also calculated the structure factor S(Q) and compared it with the experimentally obtained profile (fig. S6) (61). For this comparison, we used H-free a-SiNx (x = 1.115). Although the exact stoichiometry can vary with the deposition method, our modeling matches the experimental data well in the low-Q region up to Q ≈ 6 Å−1 and reproduces the overall profile in the high-Q region. The remaining mismatch at high Q is likely due to impurities, such as oxygen, which are not included in the model.

Chemical bonding analysis

It is very important to realize that simple interatomic distances are unreliable descriptors in determining bonding in amorphous structures since they lack atomic-orbital interference information. Therefore, to assess the strength of chemical bonds quantitatively, we used the ICOBI (quantum-chemical bond order from the wave function). To get these values, one fully reconstructs the plane-wave results by the use of an all-electron atomic orbital basis, which was achieved using the LOBSTER tool (34, 62). This tool also provided additional valuable information such as atomic charges (Löwdin charges, also from the wave function), crystal orbital Hamilton populations (COHP), and accurate PDOS to local atomic orbitals.

Upon plotting ICOBI versus interatomic distance for all atomic pairs below a distance of 3 Å (Fig. 2), different bondings are distinguishable. For the plot in Fig. 2 (A and B), we assigned 2D Gaussian for each dataset and displayed the total summation to show the relative data frequency. In particular, the fourth root was applied to simultaneously show the highest peak area and the single data point. Therefore, a ratio of 1:5 in the intensity color map corresponds to 1:625.
