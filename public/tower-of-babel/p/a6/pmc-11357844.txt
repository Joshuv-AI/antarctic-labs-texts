LSWH100: A handshape dataset for Brazilian sign language (Libras) using SignWriting
Authors: Vicente Coelho Lobo-Neto, Helio Pedrini
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper presents Libras SignWriting Handshape (LSWH100), a new handshape dataset focused on Sign Language Recognition. The dataset includes 144,000 synthetic images of a realistic human hand, covering 100 distinct handshape classes used in Brazilian Sign Language (Libras). Handshapes are named using the convention from SignWriting, a writing system for sign languages. The dataset contains annotations for classification, detection, segmentation, depth estimation, and 3D hand keypoints. Images include indoor and outdoor scenes during different times of day, centered on a single hand that can change size, 3D rotation, and skin tone. We generated these images using Blender, a free and open-source 3D creation software. This is a challenging dataset that can be further explored. With a focus on sign language, this dataset has the potential to advance sign language recognition systems, positively impacting those who rely on sign language for communication.Keywords: Computer vision, Handshape recognition, Hand configuration recognition, Hand keypoints

Background

Hands are exceptionally mobile, presenting numerous challenges for research areas focused on hands, such as handshape estimation and human–computer interaction [[1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]].

Sign Language Recognition (SLR) [[8], [9], [10], [11], [12], [13], [14], [15]] is particularly impacted by this mobility. The goal of SLR research is to develop systems that can automatically recognize and understand human sign language. One significant challenge in this field is the vast variability of handshapes that occur during the signing process. Yet, this area lacks handshape datasets containing a high number of standardized classes.

The limitations of the datasets can be caused by the difficulty and high cost of reliably collecting and annotating data. Using synthetic rather than natural data has been proposed to overcome this and create large-scale reliable datasets. With synthetic data, an unlimited amount of data can be generated while maintaining control over data variability and ensuring the accuracy of annotations.

Now, we will briefly describe some other related datasets.

RWTH-PHOENIX-weather handshapes

This is a well-established dataset [16] for hand shape recognition. It is based on the German sign language performed during the daily news and weather forecasts on a television station. The dataset contains 3359 annotated images and 17 GB of additional unlabeled images.

The images have a resolution of 92×132 pixels and were captured with a plain background. They feature various individuals performing signs and primarily focus on the hand region, including parts of the arm and body.

The provided annotations include class-only information and are divided into 60 classes. Among these, 45 classes have annotated images, while the remaining 15 classes do not have annotated images.

LSA16 handshapes

This dataset [17] is a collection of handshape images captured in a controlled laboratory environment. The images were captured indoors with artificial lighting and were segmented to focus only in the hand region.

The segmentation process was based on a plain background and fluorescent-colored gloves. The dataset comprises 800 images with a resolution of 640×480 pixels, and it was collected from 10 subjects performing 16 different handshapes.

Irish sign language hand-shape

This dataset [18] focuses on hand images used for fingerspelling in Irish Sign Language. The dataset was created by recording 6 individuals performing 23 handshapes in a controlled indoor environment. It consists of 468 videos, resulting in a total of 58,114 frames captured against a plain background.

The videos were recorded at a frame rate of 30 frames per second and had a resolution of 640×480 pixels, using an Apple iPhone 7. The lighting conditions varied across the videos as they were recorded at different times and on different days.

Japanese sign language fingerspelling

This dataset [19] includes 41 handshapes captured from images of 10 subjects. Variations were introduced by adjusting the movements, rotation, and hand pose of the actors.

A total of 8000 grayscale images of 32×32 size were created by recording 5000 images, followed by segmentation, augmentation, and conversion to grayscale, resulting in a comprehensive dataset for Japanese Sign Language fingerspelling research.

Data Description

This section describes relevant information about the proposed dataset.

Content

This dataset focuses on handshapes specifically for sign language. Handshape, also known as hand configuration, refers to the specific shape that hands take when producing signs.

The dataset contains a collection of handshapes. Each handshape is identified by a code, such as “S150”. A list of all the codes and a text description can be found in the dataset main folder.

SignWriting

The handshape naming and hierarchical classification utilized in this study were derived from the SignWriting system. SignWriting [20], created by Valerie Sutton and formally described by Steve Slevinski, is a logographic system designed for writing sign language words.

It employs a collection of symbols to represent handshapes, hand movements, and facial expressions. By combining these symbols, it becomes possible to depict a wide range of signs in most sign languages.

Handshape selection

Our dataset includes 100 handshapes from a collection of 240 used in the Brazilian Sign Language (Libras). To select the handshapes, we first ranked them by their occurrence in words in Libras, based on SignPuddle data.

SignPuddle is an online database system for creating, sharing, and searching sign language symbols and signs. It was developed as part of the SignWriting movement. An important point about SignPuddle is that, as a collaborative tool, the data is created by both institutions and independent contributors. This leads to variations in the number of signs categorized for each language. Additionally, people from different countries may prioritize cataloging signs that are most relevant to their social context and aspects of the specific sign language they use.

For Libras, there are currently over 52,000 cataloged signs. For each of these signs, we extracted only the part referring to the handshapes, discarding the parts that indicate movement, directionality, facial expression, and other aspects. Having the handshapes of all the signs cataloged, we ranked them by their frequency.

After ranking the handshapes, we selected the top 100, which represent the most common handshapes. The other handshapes appear in just a few words. We observed that these signs result in a coverage of 97 % of words in Libras. Coverage, in this case, indicates the percentage of signs in the language that use only the selected handshapes. If a sign uses a handshape from the list and others that are not on the list, we consider that this sign would not be fully covered and is not included in this percentage.

Although the collaborative aspect of SignPuddle makes it difficult to make direct comparisons between different languages, we also performed this ranking process to calculate the frequency of occurrence of the 100 most used Libras signs in other languages. In other words, instead of selecting the top 100 signs for each language, we calculated how much the top 100 Libras handshapes cover the signs of other languages. This analysis takes into account only the total number of signs and not just signs with the same meaning.

We evaluated American, German, and Korean sign languages. They have approximately 12,000, 24,000, and 32,000 signs, respectively. The coverage of the top 100 Libras signs in relation to the signs of these languages was respectively 96 %, 98 %, and 92 % for American, German, and Korean sign languages.

From these numbers, it is possible to verify that the work for adding the remaining 140 classes would not generate as much value for the dataset as the first 100 classes. That is the reason why we did not include the 240 classes in this version of the dataset. We are considering adding more classes in future versions of the dataset.

Synthetic data

Creating a new dataset can be costly and requires collaboration among many individuals. Considering the numerous possible handshapes and their unique characteristics, we opted to develop a synthetic dataset. Synthetic datasets utilize computer-generated data instead of capturing real-world data, as is commonly done in traditional datasets.

To generate the data, we chose the software Blender for its versatility and powerful capabilities. This tool enabled us to create a wide range of scenarios under various conditions.

Variety

Aiming to add more variation to the data, we constructed the dataset considering some elements of data augmentation. This is important to enhance the realism and applicability of the dataset.

The variations started by changing the basic properties of the hand, such as size, rotation, and position within the image. We also incorporated diverse backgrounds and lighting conditions, including indoor and outdoor scenes as well as morning, afternoon, and night settings.

Using image generation software enables the use of advanced augmentation techniques in addition to traditional ones. Our dataset includes two uncommon augmentations: 3D rotation and varying skin tones. The former allows seeing different angles of the hand, while the latter aims to reduce racial bias in the dataset. We used a diverse range of hand skin tones, including white, yellow, and black shades.

Statistics

The current version of the LSWH100 dataset contains 100 handshapes. Each handshape is depicted from four different perspectives: front, back, left, and right. The dataset consists of 48,000 RGB images, divided into three sets: training (400 images per class), validation (40 images per class), and testing (40 images per class).

Additionally to RGB, each perspective includes segmentation masks and depth images, resulting in a total of 144,000 images. Some sample images are illustrated in the following Figure.

Annotations

In the synthetic generation of datasets using Blender, it is possible to generate annotations alongside the images. This eliminates the need for manual annotation and ensures the accuracy of the provided information. We employed the COCO (Common Objects in Context) [21] format for this dataset annotations.

It includes essential details such as the class, bounding box, segmentation mask, and 21 keypoints representing the hand pose. These keypoints align with those used in human pose estimation tools such as OpenPose and MediaPipe. To visualize the combined annotations, refer to the following Figure.

Depth images show the distance between the hand and the viewpoint. In this dataset, the darkness of an image corresponds to its distance from the viewpoint, with darker shades indicating greater distance.

Instance segmentation images assign each pixel in the image to either the background or a specific class within the dataset. Given that each image in this dataset features only one hand, the instance segmentation images will display solely two colors: black for the background and a distinct color unique to each dataset class, representing the hand pixels.

Each folder, alongside the images, contains JSON files with annotations tailored to its directory. Specifically, the test and val folders each house a single annotation file. Conversely, within the train folders, there will be one annotation file for the entire folder and additional individual files assigned to each subdirectory.

Finally, the folders containing the images will also include a subdirectory containing metadata information uniquely related to the images in that folder.

Mesh preparation

Meshes are one of the fundamental objects used in Blender. Other examples are lights and cameras. A mesh is composed of vertices, edges, and faces. A set of meshes compose a three-dimensional object (Fig. 4).

Diagram of the steps to build LSWH100.

Due to our limited experience with three-dimensional modeling, we utilized an existing mesh available on the Internet1 as a starting point for our dataset construction. This mesh was shared by a user named Super Dasil on the Blender Artists forum. Although it is no longer available for download, similar paid and free meshes can be found on the same forum and other websites.

Using this mesh as the base made the construction of the dataset easier. However, the mesh was static, lacking rigging. The rigging allows moving meshes by adding “bones” and skeletal animation information. Mesh movement was essential for structuring the hand configurations. To address this, we followed tutorials available on the internet2 to add the rigging. The following Figure illustrates the final result of the rigging.

Once the preparation was completed, we proceeded to incorporate the hand configurations into Blender. In the context of machine learning, each configuration can be considered as a separate class within the dataset. To create each class, we positioned the mesh using the bones that were added during the rigging step. Our goal was to align the mesh with the images available in SignBank.3

To achieve this, we leveraged Blenderʼs action manager, which enabled us to assign different finger positions to the same mesh by utilizing keyframes for temporal placement. By utilizing this feature, we were able to create classes with distinct finger configurations while using the same underlying mesh (Fig. 5).

Visualization of bones added to the base mesh.

Scene preparation

Having the mesh, it is necessary to add other elements to prepare the scene, such as lights, cameras, and backgrounds.

To illuminate the mesh appropriately without causing any shadows that could hinder the visualization of the hand signals, we strategically placed point lights in key positions. According to the Blender documentation,4 point lights radiate light uniformly in all directions, simulating the behavior of a light bulb. Although not used in this dataset, other lightning types are available in Blender: spotlight, area light, and sunlight.

For the camera setup, we utilized perspective lens5 in Blender, which simulates the distortions observed in the real world based on the objectʼs distance from the camera. Other lens types available in Blender include orthographic and panoramic lenses. We positioned the camera in front of the hand mesh, with the hand centered in the view.

The background also plays an important role in the scene. In Blender, we took advantage of high dynamic range images (HDRI) to create a realistic background. HDRI images capture a wide range of lighting conditions in a scene, which will affect the object shadows and visibility. We obtained the HDRIs from Poly Haven,6 a repository of public domain resources for three-dimensional illustrations.

The selected images represented different scenarios, including indoor and outdoor environments, as well as day and night settings, allowing us to simulate realistic backgrounds for the renderings.

Image annotation

One of the challenges in constructing datasets is the annotation phase, which can be time-consuming and error-prone. In the synthetic construction of datasets using Blender, annotations can be generated automatically along with the images, eliminating the need for manual annotation and ensuring accurate annotation information. This is achieved using Python codes.

In this work, we employed a Blender addon called zpy developed for automatic annotation. This addon facilitates the creation of annotations in the COCO format, which includes class labels, bounding boxes, and segmentation masks. More details about the COCO format can be found on the dataset page.7

In addition to the annotations already generated by the extension, we extended the code to also generate annotations for 21 hand keypoints. These keypoints represent the hand joints and follow the pattern of human pose estimation works such as OpenPose and MediaPipe. The locations of these keypoints can be seen in Fig. 2.

We divided the data into three subsets: training, validation, and testing. This is a common practice in machine learning tasks.

Distinct HDRI backgrounds were employed for each subset. Specifically, testing images were ensured to have different backgrounds than the training images. This practice is useful for reducing the potential training biases that may arise from using similar backgrounds for both training and testing purposes.

The images capture the hand from four different perspectives: front, back, left, and right sides. Each image is randomly rotated within a range of ±40° along the x and y axes, as well as ±70° along the z axis.

We generated 100, 10, and 10 RGB images with a resolution of 500×500 pixels for each class (handshape) in the training, validation, and test subsets, respectively. With a total of 100 classes shown from 4 different sides, the RGB component of the dataset comprises 40,000 training images, 4000 validation images, and 4000 test images.

For each RGB image, two additional images are generated: a depth map and a segmentation mask. Refer to Fig. 1 for examples. Considering these images, the dataset totals 144,000 images.

Limitations

One of the limitations of the dataset is that it only focuses on static handshapes, and other aspects of sign language, such as hand and body movements, are not included. However, this limitation is not unique to this dataset and is a common challenge in sign language recognition. Future work could focus on developing datasets that include these other aspects of sign language.

Another limitation of the dataset is that it only includes a limited number of handshapes, considering all available in SignWriting. While the current handshapes cover more than 90 % of the words for the Brazilian, American, German and Korean sign languages, future work could focus on expanding the dataset to include the remaining handshapes.

While having limitations, this dataset significantly contributes to gesture recognition, particularly for human-machine interaction and assistive technologies. Its emphasis on sign language is valuable for advancing this field and enhancing sign language recognition systems. This potential improvement could greatly impact the lives of sign language users who rely on it as their primary communication method.
