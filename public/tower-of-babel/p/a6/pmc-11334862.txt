Thermo- economic feasibility of solar-assisted regeneration in post-combustion carbon capture: A diglycolamine-based case
Authors: Farzin Hosseinifard, Milad Hosseinpour, Mohsen Salimi, Majid Amidpour
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
The solvent regeneration in the post-combustion carbon capture process usually relies on steam from the power plant steam cycle. This heat duty is one of the challenges of energy consumption in PCC (Post-combustion Carbon Capture). However, this practice results in a significant energy penalty, leading to a substantial reduction in the capacity of the Power Plant, estimated to be between 19.5 and 40 %. This paper investigate the techno-economic feasibility of a solar-assisted regeneration process for the PCC industrial scale with diglycolamine solvent. The study aims to assess the impact of system configuration modifications, such as LVC (Lean Vapor Compression), SPCC (Solar Post-combustion Carbon Capture), and combinations of trough or compound solar collectors with LVC, on energy efficiency and overall plant performance. With 3E analysis for SPCC configuration results show that this configuration. However, reducing energy consumption and energy penalty factor, exhibits a decrease in exergy and exergoeconomic efficiency compared to the other configurations in terms of exergy and exergoeconomic aspects. However, the LVC + SCSS (Solar Combined Separator-Stripper) configuration demonstrates the best performance across the 3E aspects, resulting in a reduction energy penalty to 12.2 % and improvements of 38 % and 4.2 % in exergy and exergoeconomic factors, respectively.

Introduction

Adverse effects of climate change, including changes in weather patterns, sea level rise, wildlife, agriculture, and human health, are becoming more apparent due to the ongoing utilization of fossil fuels [1,2]. To address this issue, international efforts have been made as part of the Paris Agreement [3] to restrict the increase in global temperatures to either 1.5 °C or 2 °C [4] above the levels observed during the pre-industrial era. Achieving this objective involves not only decreasing carbon emissions but also reducing them to zero [5]. To meet the climate targets, experts suggest eliminating 10 Gt of carbon dioxide per year by 2050 and 20 Gt after that [6]. The industrialization of cities and societies leads to irreversible issues that have adverse effects on the environment and human health. CCS is an effective tool for reducing carbon emissions from industrial fossil fuel combustion [7,8]. Various techniques have been explored to reduce emissions, including improving efficiency [9], using renewable energy sources [10,11], and employing CCS (Carbon Capture and Storage) methods. CCS technology can reduce about 20 % of carbon emissions [12], making it a vital component of net-zero emissions by 2050 [13]. Industries such as steel, cement, and power generation can benefit from carbon capture to mitigate their high carbon emissions [14,15]. In particular, CCS is critical for blue hydrogen production and thermal power plants that still rely on fossil fuels. Implementing carbon capture technology in these sectors is essential for minimizing the social and economic impacts of transitioning to a low-carbon future. Post-combustion carbon capture technology is well-suited for existing industrial facilities and power plants that burn fossil fuels, making it a cost-effective option for carbon capture [16]. The concentration of CO2 and the mass flow of flue gas from the exhaust of a power plant can indirectly affect the capital cost, so in the study by udara et al. They simulated a real industrial case of a carbon capture unit for a natural gas-fired power plant with a flue gas flow rate of 793.9 kg/s, including 3.72 % mole CO2 in the exhaust stream. The results showed that the absorber column diameter is 15 m and the stripper column diameter is 12 m [17]. One significant challenge in carbon capture technology is the high energy consumption associated with using power plants, which can range from 15 to 32 % depending on various conditions, such as fuel type and industrial electricity consumption. This may not be appealing to industries looking to implement carbon capture [[18], [19], [20], [21]]. One solution to reduce the energy penalty factor is to utilize solar energy to directly or indirectly power the PCC unit(SA-PCC) [22]. SA-PCC utilizes solar collectors to provide thermal energy, reducing the electricity or steam usage from the power plant and increasing power plant capacity. Two methods for solar repowering include individual reboilers and recirculation methods, each with its trade-offs [22]. Implementing these approaches involves significant investments in solar fields, impacting cost [23]. Renewable energies, such as solar energy, require flexible resources like energy storage due to their inherent instability [24]. Integrating solar thermal energy into carbon capture offers environmental, economic, and efficiency benefits to the system [25]. Two prominent solar collector technologies, parabolic trough collector [26] and parabolic compound collector [27], provide distinct benefits when implemented in this specific situation.

Parabolic trough collectors are well established and known for efficiently concentrate sunlight onto a receiver tube efficiently [28,29]. Comparing the performance of parabolic trough collectors with compound parabolic collectors at high temperatures, as well as evaluating other solar collectors for integration into the reboiler of a PCC, can have a positive economic impact on the plant [30]. Compound parabolic collectors, with their distinct design optimized for capturing sunlight at steep angles, complement trough collectors [31]. By utilizing both collector types, a combined approach can maximize energy collection throughout the day. This results in increased thermal efficiency and prolonged capture operation hours, leading to enhanced economic viability and reduced greenhouse gas emissions. Using a thermal collector via a reboiler in PCC is an effective method to capture CO2 from flue gas emissions. The carbon capture involves stripping CO2 from the solvent using a solar thermal collector [22,32].

Scope, objective, and structure

The objective of this study is to optimize the 3E configuration for post-combustion carbon capture by utilizing solar thermal energy to power the PCC process. The study focuses on the flue gas from the Abadan Power Plant in Iran. Abadan city, located in the southeast region of Iran, has a high potential for solar energy. The Abadan Power Plant is the largest in Iran, and implementing PCC at this scale is a significant challenge. Relying solely on conventional configurations for this purpose is not economical and results in low energy consumption efficiency. Novelty

The use of PCC to significantly reduce carbon emissions is beneficial. However, the energy penalty and energy consumption in this industrial process are very high. Therefore, integrating conventional PCC configurations directly or indirectly with solar energy remains a gap in research [[32], [33], [34], [35], [36], [37]]. It is better to first reduce energy consumption in the reboiler through various PCC configurations and modify conventional configurations to minimize the energy penalty. Secondly, utilizing solar energy to compare various scenarios in different configurations using thermoeconomic analysis for each configuration can be beneficial. Solar energy can assist in both direct and indirect carbon capture and can optimize the capturing process. It is true that using solar energy is free in terms of fuel, but it incurs significant investment and operational costs. Therefore, it is better to optimize the process before integrating solar energy into it. In this study, we optimized the configuration by incorporating heat pump applications first and then directly using solar energy in conjunction with PCC. In specific configurations, such as LVC + SPCC, solar energy is integrated into the capturing process (not as energy consumption) alongside the optimized PCC configuration (LVC configuration). In this setup, the rich amine first enters the compound collector, and any CO2 not captured initially goes to the stripper column. This approach significantly reduces energy consumption in the reboiler indirectly and enhances other aspects of energy, economy, and environment (3E). We investigated SPCC, LVC + SCR (Solar Combined Reboiler), LVC + SCS (Solar Combined Stripper), and LVC + SCSS configurations. Furthermore, we delved into finding the optimal combination of these configurations. The LVC + SCSS configuration is a novel approach in post-combustion capture as it utilizes the total capacity of solar thermal energy, especially the parabolic compound collector, for CO2 capture.

Methodology

PCC process

In the PCC process for exhaust power plants, flue gas is continuously directed to a capture plant where amine, such as diglycolamine, selectively absorbs carbon dioxide (CO2) from the gas stream. The resulting rich amine solution is then sent to a stripper column for regeneration, where a reboiler provides the necessary heat. In the reboiler, the amine solution is heated, causing the CO2 to desorb and creating a concentrated CO2 stream. This regenerated amine solution is then recirculated for reuse in the absorption process. At the same time the concentrated CO2 can be compressed and transported for storage or utilization, effectively minimizing carbon emissions from the power plant. The continuous and cyclical nature of this process demonstrates its potential for reducing greenhouse gas emissions and mitigating the environmental impact of fossil fuel combustion [38]. Fig. 1 presents the PCC process from the flue gas exhaust Abadan power plant.Fig. 1Conventional Configuration of PCC.Fig. 1

Conventional Configuration of PCC.

After pre-treatment (not shown in Fig. 1) [39], the flue gas stream from the power plant, initially at 40 °C and 100 kPa, is directed to an absorber column, designed as a packed column. In this column, a solvent, potentially DGA, selectively absorbs carbon dioxide (CO2) from the flue gas. The cleaned gas, containing less than 8.3 kg/s of CO2, is vented into the atmosphere, while the lean solvent enters the pump for increasing pressure, after the pump and reaching the pressure stream to the operational pressure stripper column for increasing temperature using a heat exchanger. The stripper column, also a packed column, operates at a higher temperature (127 °C) and pressure (250 kPa) for efficient CO2 desorption from the solvent. The CO2 captured at the top of the stripper column amounts to 77.77 kg/s, demonstrating the systems effectiveness in removing and concentrating carbon dioxide from the flue gas. The cyclic process involving absorption, pressurization, heating, and stripping contributes to the continuous and sustainable capture of CO2, making it a viable solution for reducing carbon emissions from power plants.

SA-PCC configurations

A CPC (Compound Parabolic Collector) type of solar collector with a curved reflector surface focusing sunshine on an absorber tube. The reflector surface is designed to capture and concentrate sunlight over a wide range of incident angles, making CPC highly efficient in capturing solar energy even when the sun is not directly overhead. The receiver tube, located at the focal line of the reflector, absorbs the concentrated sunlight and transfers the heat to a working fluid for heating.

On the other hand, a trough parabolic collector is a solar thermal collector that uses a parabolic-shaped reflector. The reflector is typically a long, curved mirror that tracks the sun movement throughout the day to maximize sunlight capture. The receiver tube, placed along the focal line, absorbs the concentrated sunlight and transfers the heat to a working fluid. This heat can be used for various applications, including electricity production and industrial processes.

Compound parabolic collectors and trough parabolic collectors are effective in capturing solar energy and converting it into useable heat. However, they differ in design and operational characteristics. CPC is known for their ability to capturing sunlight from a wide range of angles, making them suitable for applications where the sun's position varies throughout the day. Trough parabolic collectors are particularly effective in capturing direct sunlight and are commonly used in large-scale solar thermal industries.

Compound parabolic collectors differ from trough parabolic collectors in the arrangement of their components. In compound parabolic collectors, a glass cover is typically placed on top to trap and retain heat. Below the glass, a pipe or tube allows the heat transfer fluid to flow and absorb the captured solar energy. In contrast, trough parabolic collectors lack a glass cover and a pipe below them. Instead, they feature a curved reflective surface that concentrates irradiance in one tube positioned at the focal point of the parabolic shape. This receiver tube holds the heat transfer fluid, which absorbs the concentrated solar energy (see Fig. 3).Fig. 3Shape of collectors used in this study (a)TPC, (b)CPC.Fig. 3

Shape of collectors used in this study (a)TPC, (b)CPC.

In this paper, we explore the potential of these two solar collector technologies within post-combustion carbon capture systems. We used CPC before the stripper column where a low temperature difference is required, and the solvent enters the collector pipe. We used TPC (Trough Parabolic Collector) in combination with the reboiler. The overall configuration, when combined with solar energy, can store thermal energy for up to 12 h using TES (Thermal Energy Storage).

.Energy penalty

Carbon capture and storage (CCS) is a process used to reduce the amount of carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from power plants and other industrial facilities. One method of CCS involves using steam and electricity to capture and store CO2, but this process comes with an energy penalty [47]. The energy penalty in carbon capture with steam and electricity occurs because the capture process requires additional energy to separate and capture the CO2 from the flue gas emitted by the power plant. The energy penalty factor refers to the additional energy consumed, directly or indirectly, from the power plant by the carbon capture unit or other energy systems. Fig. 7 illustrates this by showing a red line labeled "steam production by power plant unit," which indicates the steam consumed indirectly by the carbon capture unit. Additionally, the energy used in the form of electricity comes directly from the power plant's output, with the remainder going to the grid. This increased energy requirement results in a decrease in the overall efficiency of the power plant, as more fuel or electricity is needed to maintain the same power output level. When steam is used for carbon capture, the power plant must divert some of the steam generated to the capture process, reducing the steam available for power generation, which can decrease the overall power output of the plant. Similarly, using electricity for carbon capture requires additional electricity to operate the capture equipment, which can reduce the net power output of the plant. Exergy

All thermodynamic studies employ exergy analysis to evaluate energy performance. Exergy analysis provides crucial metrics for assessing system performance and allows for a comprehensive evaluation of the system's energy conversion capabilities. The detailed assessment of energy efficiency is made possible by the established conditions and assumptions in energy analysis, leading to more informed decision-making.(6)Ψk=EP,kEF,k=1−ED,k+Eloss,kEF,k(7)Ψk,Plant=EP,PlantEF,Plant=1−ED,Plant+Eloss,PlantEF,Plant(8)ED,k=EF,k−Eloss,k−EP,kInthisstudy,EF,k denotes the exergy of the fuel stream entering the component or plant through a control volume, which may encompass one or more material or energy streams, such as electricity directly or steam indirectly. Various factors influence the increase or decrease of these parameters, such as differences in temperature or pressure compared to reference conditions. The unit of measurement for these parameters in this research is in megawatts (MW).

Ψk represents the exergy efficiency, Exergy efficiency refers to the ratio of the actual useful work obtained from a process to the maximum potential work that could be obtained from the available energy. In other words, it measures how effectively a system is converting available energy into useful work. When exergy efficiency is increased, the system is reducing waste and utilizing energy more effectively. This can be achieved by minimizing exergy destruction, which is the irreversible loss of available work within a system. Exergy destruction occurs due to irreversible, inefficiencies, and losses in the energy conversion process. By improving exergy efficiency, a system can reduce waste and optimize its energy utilization. This can have significant benefits, including increased sustainability, reduced resource consumption, and improved overall performance of energy conversion processes. In essence, increasing exergy efficiency means that a greater proportion of the available energy is being converted into useful work, while minimizing losses and waste. This is a key goal in the design and operation of energy systems, as it contributes to more sustainable and efficient energy utilization. In the equation (8), which is used for calculating the exergy destruction for each component in the system, the difference in product from fuel represents the remaining part as either waste or exergy destruction. In many implementations, there are no losses, but in the absorption column, there are also losses due to the vent of clean gas into the atmosphere.

Conclusion

PCC is in a high-energy consumption sector in industrial applications, making the high energy penalty in PCC an important challenge. Our study focuses on reducing the energy penalty for the Abadan power plant as a case study to decrease carbon emissions in this region with increasing sustainability for plant. This involves changing the configuration of the conventional PCC setup and optimizing this configuration using 3E analysis to enhance exergy and exergoeconomic parameters. To reduce energy penalty and improve 3E, we employ a configuration combined with solar thermal energy, such as LVC + SCS, LVC + SCSS, LVC + SCR. The results show that this LVC + SCSS is the best configuration in terms of 3E because in this configuration, the amount of energy consumption is provided by solar energy, and a percentage of the capturing process is done in solar compound collectors. This allows the rich solvent to enter the collector directly and proceed to the stripper column. The results indicate that in this configuration, the energy penalty is reduced by 12.2 %. Additionally, the exergy-based analysis shows an increase in exergy efficiency to 73 %, and the exergoeconomic factor for the PCC plant increases to 7.38 %. In addition to the suggested improvements, it is also important to consider the potential for utilizing waste heat from the power plant for the carbon capture process. By integrating waste heat recovery systems, the overall energy efficiency of the carbon capture process can be further improved, leading to cost savings and reduced environmental impact. Furthermore, exploring the potential for utilizing novel materials and technologies for the carbon capture process, such as advanced absorbents and membranes, could also lead to significant improvements in efficiency and cost-effectiveness. Additionally, it is important to consider the potential for carbon capture utilization and storage (CCUS) technologies, which can provide economic opportunities for utilizing captured CO2 in industrial processes or for enhanced oil recovery, further enhancing the economic viability of carbon capture systems. Overall, by continuing to explore and implement these various strategies and technologies, the efficiency and economic viability of post-combustion carbon capture systems can be significantly improved, leading to a more sustainable and environmentally friendly industrial sector.
