The phospholipid profile of T cells shapes ACSL4 dependency and ferroptosis sensitivity of naive, effector, and memory T cells
Authors: Gariné Magarditchian, Ivan Berest, Aikaterini Ziogou, Mai Matsushita, Michelle Reid, Alaa Othman, Manfred Kopf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceFerroptosis, a regulated cell death program induced by phospholipid (PL) peroxidation, has been extensively studied in cancer cells as a therapeutic strategy; however, its characterization in primary T cells remains poorly understood. Here, combining CRISPR screening and lipidomic analysis, we demonstrate that in vivo effector T cells are vulnerable to ferroptosis independently of the main in vitro regulator ACSL4, owing to their unique PL profile enriched in polyunsaturated fatty acid (PUFA-PLs). In contrast, naive and memory T cells share a PL landscape poor in PUFA-PLs, rendering them ferroptosis-resistant. These findings revealed that the PL profile is key to regulating ferroptosis in T cells, resolving discrepancies between in vitro and in vivo conditions, and helping define strategies to modulate ferroptosis.

Ferroptosis is a form of regulated cell death characterized by iron-dependent accumulation of phospholipid (PL) peroxides in the presence of reactive oxygen species (ROS), ultimately leading to plasma membrane damage and necrotic death (1, 2). Due to its relevance in various pathologies, including cancer (3, 4), ischemia/reperfusion injury (5, 6), and neurodegenerative diseases (7), tremendous effort has been made to decipher the underlying mechanisms regulating ferroptosis (8). Owing to its capacity to initiate lipid peroxidation, iron is at the center of the pathway. Indeed, an imbalance in intracellular iron levels regulates ferroptosis, with iron overload promoting it and iron chelators serving as effective inhibitors (1, 9).

To cope with increasing levels of lipid peroxides, cells possess antioxidant mechanisms, with glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) serving as the primary gatekeeper of ferroptosis. GPX4 catalyzes the reduction of PL peroxides into their alcohol derivatives at the expense of its cofactor, glutathione (GSH), thereby suppressing ferroptosis (10). Indeed, chemical inhibition, using RAS-selective lethal 3 (RSL3), or genetic deletion of Gpx4 quickly leads to the accumulation of lipid peroxides, ultimately causing cell death in many cell types (11, 12). Overall, ferroptosis occurs in environments where iron-dependent membrane lipid peroxides accumulate and cannot be detoxified, for instance, in the absence of GPX4. The peroxidation of fatty acid chains in PLs, the predominant lipid class of cellular membranes, specifically induces ferroptosis. Due to their chemical properties, polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) have a higher propensity to be peroxidized and were found to be the predominant substrate in cells undergoing ferroptosis (13). Indeed, treatment with PUFAs or PUFA-containing PLs (PUFA-PLs) has been shown to induce ferroptosis, whereas supplementation with exogenous monounsaturated fatty acids (MUFAs) inhibits it (14–16). The relevance of lipid composition was further highlighted by the discovery of a crucial inducer of ferroptosis, acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), which enriches membranes with PUFA-PLs (17–19). Correspondingly, ACSL4 deficiency inhibited ferroptosis in various cell types, including mouse embryonic fibroblasts and several cancer cell lines (17, 18), suggesting that the PL profile of cells influences their vulnerability to ferroptosis.

Because ferroptosis relies on the intrinsic properties of cells, such as iron levels and lipid composition, different cell types have varying susceptibility to ferroptosis. Among lymphocytes, effector CD8+ and CD4+ T cells are highly susceptible to ferroptosis, while GPX4 is dispensable in follicular B (B2) cells (20, 21). Moreover, myeloid cells, including neutrophils, monocytes, inflammatory macrophages, and dendritic cells (DCs), are relatively ferroptosis-resistant (22, 23). This was further corroborated by a systematic comparison of the susceptibility of different immune cells to GPX4 inhibition, which revealed that, in contrast to T and B cells, monocytes and neutrophils are resistant to ferroptosis (15). Interestingly, the sensitivity of immune cells to ferroptosis correlated with the abundance of PUFA-PLs, as neutrophils had a low and T cells a high PUFA-PLs profile. Although T cells exhibit the highest sensitivity to ferroptosis among immune cells, the absence of GPX4 affects various T cell subsets differently. Indeed, in vitro cultured T cells or effector T cells after viral or parasitic infections in vivo, heavily relied on the presence of GPX4, while in contrast, secondary effector CD8+ T cells did not undergo ferroptosis in the absence of GPX4 (20). More recently, a study on regulatory T cells (Tregs) revealed that GPX4-deficient Tregs fail to maintain immune tolerance, even though their viability is not compromised (24). Finally, GPX4 was shown to be essential for the survival of T follicular helper cells (Tfh) (25). However, factors explaining this differential susceptibility remain unknown.

Studies attempting to elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying ferroptosis have mainly focused on cell lines and cancer cells. The regulation of ferroptosis at different stages of T cell responses remains largely unknown. Here, using a genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen in activated primary T cells, we show that they exclusively rely on ACSL4 to promote ferroptosis in vitro. A follow-up screen with a lower proferroptotic pressure reveals that other iron- (i.e., Ireb2 and Tfrc) and lipid metabolism-associated genes (i.e., Stk11 and Dhcr7) only partially contribute to ferroptosis sensitivity. We show that ACSL4 is essential only upon T cell activation in vitro, and effector CD8+ T cells lacking GPX4 undergo ferroptosis in an ACSL4-independent manner in vivo. The ACSL4 dependency observed is due to the intrinsic higher saturated fatty acid (SFA)- and MUFA-PLs profile of T cells cultured in vitro, and treatment with a PUFA, docosahexaenoic acid (C22:6, DHA), promotes ferroptosis independently of ACSL4. Finally, by comparing the PL profile of naive, effector, and memory CD8+ T cells, we reveal that the abundance of PUFA-PLs correlates with their susceptibility to ferroptosis. Specifically, unlike effector T cells, the decreased PUFA-PLs content of naive and memory T cells renders them less prone to lipid peroxidation. This study unveils the central role of the PL composition of T cells in regulating not only ACSL4 dependence but also ferroptosis sensitivity.

Results

ACSL4 Is Essential to Induce Ferroptosis in T Cells In Vitro.

The underlying molecular mechanism promoting ferroptosis in T cells is still poorly characterized. To identify factors that induce ferroptosis in primary mouse T cells, we performed an in vitro genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screen. To this end, in vitro activated T cells were treated with RSL3, an inhibitor of GPX4 (12), which induced rapid cell death in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1A). The initiation of cell death after 4 h correlated with a RSL3 concentration-dependent increase in lipid peroxidation, as measured by BODIPY™C11581/591 staining, indicating that RSL3 is a potent inducer of ferroptosis in T cells (Fig. 1B). To further confirm the specific induction of ferroptosis, RSL3-induced cell death and lipid peroxidation were prevented by the ferroptosis inhibitor ferrostatin-1 (Fer-1) (1) (Fig. 1 C and D).

Next, T cells isolated from the spleen of R26Cas9-EGFP mice (26) were activated and transduced with a genome-wide single-guide RNA (sgRNA) library (27) targeting 18,424 genes. Then, cells were treated with RSL3 or DMSO for 24 h and live efficiently transduced (as determined by expression of blue fluorescent protein, BFP+) cells were sorted (Fig. 1E). Quality control of the amplified library revealed a normal distribution of sgRNAs (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). First, a stringent screen was performed in duplicate using a highly lethal concentration of 500 nM RSL3 and revealed the proferroptotic gene Acsl4 as the only enriched gene in RSL3-treated cells in comparison to DMSO-treated control (Fig. 1F). To note, the five Acsl4-targeting sgRNAs were enriched in both replicates, except for sg2, of the screen (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B). The role of ACSL4 in promoting ferroptosis was further validated on a single knockout level, where loss of ACSL4 mediated by each of the five sgRNA (SI Appendix, Fig. S1C) led to a significant rescue of the viability upon RSL3 treatment, compared to cells transduced with a nontargeting (NT) sgRNA control (Fig. 1G). The relevance of Acsl4 in inducing ferroptosis is consistent with previous studies and screens across different cell types, which highlighted its role as a potent enricher of PUFA-PLs in cell membranes (17, 18, 28–31). However, we reveal here on a genome-wide scale that primary T cells rely exclusively on Acsl4 to induce ferroptosis.

Iron- and Lipid Metabolism–Associated Genes Modulate the Susceptibility of T Cells to Ferroptosis.

In order to identify genes that alter the susceptibility of T cells to ferroptosis beyond Acsl4 in vitro, a second screen was performed using a lower concentration of RSL3 (Fig. 1E). In the second more permissive screen, twenty-two genes, including the top hit Acsl4, were significantly enriched in RSL3-treated compared to DMSO-treated T cells (Fig. 2A). Metascape analysis (32) revealed that those genes are associated with iron and lipid metabolism, highlighting that these pathways contribute to ferroptosis sensitivity (Fig. 2B). The RSL3 dose-dependent relevance of some of the hits of the screen was further validated by individual knockouts (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B).

Ireb2, which encodes a regulator of iron metabolism, and Tfrc, which encodes the transferrin receptor, both modulate iron import and contribute to increased intracellular iron levels, thereby promoting the induction of ferroptosis (1, 9). In contrast to Acsl4 deficiency, which presented a significant resistance to ferroptosis even at high RSL3 concentrations (Fig. 2C), RSL3 titration on Ireb2 and Tfrc knockouts revealed that, although those genes partially contribute to ferroptosis at low concentrations, their relevance diminishes as RSL3 concentration increases (Fig. 2 D and E), corroborating the results obtained from the two genome-wide screens. Next, to assess the effect of iron overload, wild-type T cells were treated with increasing concentrations of iron (III) ammonium citrate. High concentration of iron induced lipid peroxidation, while viability was only partially affected, suggesting that iron overload primes T cells to ferroptosis (Fig. 2 F and G). Overall, these data indicate that an imbalance in iron metabolism can affect the susceptibility of T cells to undergo ferroptosis.

Similar results were obtained with the lipid metabolism–related genes tested, namely Stk11 and Dhcr7, which were previously identified as proferroptotic (30, 31, 33). Indeed, knockout of Stk11 or Dhcr7 slightly inhibited ferroptosis at low RSL3 concentrations but failed to do so at higher concentrations (SI Appendix, Fig. S2 C and D). In summary, these data indicate that Acsl4 is the primary inducer of ferroptosis. However, other genes involved in iron and lipid metabolism also influence the susceptibility of T cells to undergo ferroptosis.

T Cell Ferroptosis Depends on ASCL4 In Vitro but not In Vivo.

GPX4 is considered the key gatekeeper of ferroptosis through its ability to reduce lipid peroxides directly (5, 12). GPX4 was deleted by treating T cells isolated from the spleen of Gpx4fl/fl R26creERT2/+ mice with 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT) in vitro for 72 h (Fig. 3A). Concomitant with the deletion of Gpx4, T cells exhibited a significant decrease in viability, which was further worsened upon longer treatment (Fig. 3B), as well as a substantial increase in lipid peroxidation, indicative of ferroptosis (Fig. 3C). To evaluate the role of ACSL4 in loss of GPX4-induced ferroptosis, Gpx4fl/fl R26creERT2/+ mice were crossed to R26Cas9-EGFP mice to generate Gpx4fl/fl R26creERT2/+; Cas9/+ mice and T cells were isolated and cultured. Similar to T cells treated with RSL3, loss of GPX4-mediated ferroptosis was rescued by the deletion of Acsl4 (Fig. 3D). Notably, absence of ACSL4 and GPX4 was confirmed at a protein level (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A) and similar data were obtained by looking at CD8+ T cells specifically (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). Collectively, these results reveal that ACSL4 is a crucial factor in in vitro activated T cells undergoing loss of GPX4-induced ferroptosis.

Given the relevance of ACSL4 in inducing ferroptosis in vitro, we next aimed to assess its role in vivo. To do so, we transduced CD8+ T cells isolated from Gpx4fl/fl R26creERT2/+; Cas9/+ P14 mice, which have transgenic T cells expressing a specific T cell receptor that recognizes gp33-41 from lymphocytic choriomeningitis virus (LCMV), with Acsl4-targeting or NT sgRNAs and transferred the cells to TCRβ−/− recipient mice, lacking endogenous T cells. These mice were subsequently infected with LCMV WE to induce the potent activation and proliferation of the transferred virus-specific CD8+ T cells, followed by tamoxifen (TAM) injections to induce Gpx4 deletion specifically in the transferred CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3E). Efficient knockout of ACSL4 in the transferred Gpx4fl/flR26Cas9/+ CD8+ T cells transduced with Acsl4-targeting sgRNA was confirmed 8 d after transfer (Fig. 3F). As previously shown (20), CD8+ T cells lacking Gpx4 underwent ferroptosis in vivo, and they completely failed to expand upon LCMV infection (Fig. 3G). Surprisingly, in contrast to what was observed in vitro, knocking out ACSL4 did not rescue the loss of GPX4-mediated ferroptosis (Fig. 3G). Notably, loss of ACSL4 did not affect the viability of T cells in the GPX4-sufficient controls. Additionally, none of the other hits tested rescued loss of GPX4-induced ferroptosis in vivo (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C). Overall, these data suggest that even though ACSL4 is a critical inducer of ferroptosis in vitro, an ACSL4-independent mechanism governs the initiation of ferroptosis in vivo.

The Lipid Composition Determines Whether T Cell Ferroptosis Depends on ACSL4, in Both In Vitro and In Vivo Settings.

To decipher the discrepancy between the role of ACSL4 in inducing ferroptosis in vitro and in vivo, we hypothesized that the environment affects the lipid composition of CD8+ T cells, which in turn influences their dependency on ACSL4. To test this, untargeted lipidomics analysis was performed on ACSL4-deficient (i.e., transduced with Acsl4-targeting sgRNA) and ACSL4-sufficient (i.e., transduced with NT sgRNA) CD8+ T cells, which were either cultured in vitro or transferred into recipient mice in vivo (Fig. 4A). We focused on PLs, the main lipid subset detected, which are central to ferroptosis. In line with the function of ACSL4 in the biosynthesis of long PUFA-PLs (15, 18, 19), ACSL4-sufficient CD8+ T cells were enriched in PLs containing four or more double bonds compared to ACSL4-deficient CD8+ T cells in vitro as well as in vivo (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 A and B).

Analysis of the differential lipid profile revealed that the PL composition is predominantly affected by the environment, rather than by the presence of ACSL4. Indeed, principal component analysis (PCA) of the lipidome clustered more closely ACSL4-deficient and sufficient cells in vitro (sg1_Acsl4 in vitro and sg1_NT in vitro) than ACSL4-deficient cells in vitro and in vivo (sg1_Acsl4 in vitro and sg1_Acsl4 in vivo) or than ACSL4-sufficient cells in vitro and in vivo (sg1_NT in vitro and sg1_NT in vivo) (Fig. 4B). Additionally, the number of significantly differentially abundant PLs was lower when comparing ACSL4-deficient and -sufficient cells than when comparing in vitro and in vivo conditions (Fig. 4C). In general, the in vitro environment shifted the lipid composition toward a PL profile abundant in SFA- and MUFA-PLs. In contrast, all PLs species significantly enriched in vivo contained PUFAs (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S4C). The relative abundance of PLs was estimated based on the peak area of each lipid.

Upon closer examination of which membrane lipid species could confer the resistance to ferroptosis, phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) 18:0_22:6 was consistently found to be increased in ferroptosis-sensitive subsets, namely ACSL4-deficient T cells in vivo (Fig. 4D) and ACSL4-sufficient T cells in vitro (Fig. 4E) when compared to the ferroptosis-resistant subset (i.e., ACSL4-deficient T cells in vitro). In addition to being significantly enriched in ferroptosis-sensitive subsets, PE 18:0_22:6 was found to be highly abundant, as calculated based on the area (Fig. 4 D and E). To test whether this difference drove ACSL4 dependency in vitro, cells were treated with docosahexaenoic acid (C22:6, DHA) to increase its content. Treatment with DHA rendered ACSL4-deficient T cells susceptible to loss of GPX4-induced ferroptosis in vitro, indicating that the lipid profile of a cell governs ACSL4 dependency (Fig. 4F). We next supplemented wild-type CD8+ T cells with DHA in vitro to assess whether this renders them also more susceptible to ferroptosis. Upon DHA treatment, lipid peroxidation dramatically increased (Fig. 4G), while viability was only partially affected (Fig. 4H), suggesting that in the presence of GPX4, cells can cope with DHA-induced lipid peroxidation. All these features were reversed by Fer-1, confirming that DHA treatment primes T cells to ferroptosis (Fig. 4 G and H). To evaluate the role of ACSL4 in GPX4-independent ferroptosis, we treated ACSL4-deficient T cells with DHA, which, as we showed, sensitized the cells to ferroptosis. DHA treatment reduced viability in an ACSL4-independent manner (SI Appendix, Fig. S4D), indicating that ACSL4 does not play a role in GPX4-independent ferroptosis.

These data demonstrate that the environment affects the overall cellular PL composition and that in vitro T cells have a lipid profile enriched in SFA- and MUFAs-PLs. Therefore, to undergo ferroptosis in vitro, T cells rely on ACSL4 to incorporate more PUFAs. However, a PUFA-rich environment, as observed in vivo or upon DHA treatment in vitro, renders T cells susceptible to ferroptosis independently of ACSL4. Overall, this indicates that the molecular pathway driving ferroptosis depends on the PL landscape of the cell.

Compared to Effector CD8+ T Cells, Naive and Memory CD8+ T Cells Have Lower PUFA-PL Content, Making Them Resistant to Ferroptosis.

While effector CD8+ T cells in an acute response to LCMV WE completely rely on GPX4 to survive (20) (Fig. 3G), naive CD8+ T cells showed only a partial dependency on GPX4 (20, 21). Upon deletion of GPX4 in the memory phase (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A), the number and percentage of virus-specific memory CD8+ T cells remain unaffected (Fig. 5A). Moreover, their effector function during a secondary infection was shown not to be altered in the absence of GPX4 (20). Because we found that the PL profile is critical in regulating ferroptosis, we hypothesized that the PL composition of different CD8+ T cell subsets affects their susceptibility to ferroptosis. Thus, we compared the lipid landscape of naive CD8+ P14 T cells as well as effector and memory CD8+ P14 T cells following an acute LCMV WE infection (Fig. 5B). Upon activation, the overall PL profile of effector T cells changed dramatically (Fig. 5C). Interestingly, overtime, the PL landscape of memory T cells returned to a composition resembling naive T cells (Fig. 5C). Additionally, PCA of the lipidome revealed that naive and memory T cells are more similar than effector cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). Looking at the fatty acid chains composition, many PUFA-PLs species, including the ones containing linoleic (C18:2), eicosadienoic (C20:2), arachidonic (C20:4), or adrenic acid (C22:4), were found to be enriched in effector T cells when compared to naive T cells (Fig. 5D). More specifically, all the highly abundant PUFA-PL species (e.g., PE 18:1_20:2, PEO 16:1_22:4, and PC 18:0_18:2), as calculated based on the area, were enriched in effector T cells compared to naive. Similarly, compared to memory T cells, the PL profile of effector T cells was enriched in PUFA-PLs (Fig. 5E). Moreover, species with more than one total number of unsaturation were decreased in naive and memory T cells when compared to effector T cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S5 C and D). Overall, these data suggest that the lipid profile of effector T cells, which is enriched in abundant PUFA-PLs, renders them more susceptible to lipid peroxidation and ferroptosis. On the other hand, naive and memory T cells have a similar lipid composition with reduced level of the abundant PUFA-PLs, making them less susceptible to ferroptosis.

Discussion

In an oxidative environment, such as during T cell activation (34), T cells become susceptible to iron-dependent lipid peroxidation, which can ultimately induce ferroptosis in the absence of antioxidant mechanisms. Indeed, effector T cells heavily rely on the presence of GPX4 to reduce PL peroxides and thereby prevent ferroptosis (20). However, factors regulating the induction of ferroptosis in T cells beyond GPX4 are poorly characterized. Here, we show that the PL composition of T cells defines not only the molecular mechanism inducing ferroptosis but also the susceptibility of naive, effector, and memory T cells to it.

To gain insight into the underlying molecular mechanisms inducing ferroptosis in T cells, two genome-wide CRISPR-Cas9 knockout screens were performed in primary T cells. The results from both screens highlighted a hierarchy between ACSL4, which is essential for inducing ferroptosis in T cells, and other iron- and lipid metabolism–related genes that only slightly affect their susceptibility. Indeed, by lowering the concentration of RSL3, the level of GPX4 inhibition was reduced, allowing the characterization of genes that only partially modulate ferroptosis sensitivity. Many of the genes identified in the low RSL3 screen, including Ireb2 (1), Tfrc (9), Dhcr7 (30, 31), and Stk11 (33), have been identified in different cell lines as important inducers of ferroptosis. However, these genes are not critical for ferroptosis of activated T cells, and the respective knockouts only partially prevented ferroptosis induced by a low concentration of RSL3. Interestingly, the relevance of Ireb2 was shown to vary across different cell lines, and silencing of the gene inhibited ferroptosis at various levels (1). This variability among cell types was further highlighted in a study comparing the results of different CRISPR screens, demonstrating that the genetic regulation of ferroptosis depends on both the cell type and the ferroptotic trigger (35). Together, these results suggest that the contribution of certain genes is cell type dependent and that intrinsic features of cells, such as iron levels or lipid composition, can modulate the molecular pathway promoting ferroptosis.

ACSL4 was identified as an essential inducer of ferroptosis in various cell types, including T cells in vitro (17, 18, 36). Surprisingly, ferroptosis was found to be induced independently of ACSL4 in vivo, and ACSL4-deficient T cells underwent loss of GPX4-induced ferroptosis, highlighting the importance of the environment in this process. In vitro cultured T cells were enriched in SFA- and MUFA-PLs, and upon GPX4 deletion, they relied on ACSL4 to incorporate PUFA-PLs and undergo ferroptosis. On the contrary, in vivo effector T cells were intrinsically enriched in PUFA-PLs. Although ACSL4 knockout reduced the PUFA-PLs content, this reduction was insufficient to protect the cells, suggesting that the abundance of PUFA-PLs is critical. These results reveal that the environment influences the PL composition of T cells, which in turn defines ACSL4 dependency. Indeed, treating T cells in vitro with DHA abrogated their ACSL4 dependency. Overall, these results suggest that ACSL4 is essential for inducing ferroptosis in contexts where PUFA-PLs are low.

GPX4-sufficient T cells cultured in the presence of DHA exhibited increased lipid peroxidation, indicating that they were sensitized to ferroptosis. Similar results were obtained across various cancer cell lines sensitized to ferroptosis by adding PUFAs, including DHA (16, 37). Notably, neutrophils contain inherently low levels of PUFA-PLs and resist ferroptosis. However, upon treatment with DHA and arachidonic acid (C20:4), they undergo RSL3-induced ferroptosis (15). Neutrophils resist ferroptosis during bacterial infection (22), but tumor-infiltrating neutrophils can undergo spontaneous ferroptosis (38), emphasizing that the environment influences their ferroptosis sensitivity. Because mammalian cells are unable to synthesize ω-6 and ω-3 PUFAs de novo, they rely on the uptake and the availability of these PUFAs to build their membrane. Overall, this provides further evidence that lipid availability is crucial and that an environment rich in PUFAs, particularly DHA, renders T cells more susceptible to ferroptosis. The presence of specific lipids in the tumor environment has also been shown to shape the functionality and to induce ferroptosis in tumor-infiltrating T cells (39, 40). In a cell, PUFAs can be taken up either through simple diffusion or via specialized PUFA transporters. For instance, Mfsd2a has been identified as a DHA transporter in the brain (41). However, the relevance of PUFA transporters, including Mfsd2a, in ferroptotic T cells in vivo warrants further investigation.

The lipid landscape of immune cells is a feature defining their identity and is closely related to their biological function. In the context of ferroptosis, myeloid cells, including neutrophils, monocytes, inflammatory macrophages (M1), and DCs, are relatively ferroptosis-resistant (22). However, under specific conditions, myeloid cells, including neutrophils in the tumor environment (38) or alternatively activated macrophages (M2) (22, 23), become sensitive to ferroptosis, suggesting that their susceptibility depends on the environmental context. The sensitivity of lymphoid cells to ferroptosis varies across different subsets and stages of activation. Indeed, while B1 cells, marginal zone B cells, Tfh cells, effector CD8+, and CD4+ T cells heavily rely on GPX4 to survive, follicular B2 cells, Tregs, memory, and secondary effector CD8+ T cells are resistant to ferroptosis (20, 21, 24, 25). The susceptibility of immune cells to ferroptosis was related to their PUFA-PLs content. Indeed, neutrophils and monocytes were shown to exhibit a low PUFA-PL profile, whereas T cells and B cells displayed a high PUFA-PL content (15). Moreover, increasing the MUFA content in T cells protected them from ferroptosis, while PUFA treatment rendered neutrophils susceptible, suggesting that ferroptosis susceptibility and PUFA content are closely associated. Compared to effector CD8+ T cells, naive and memory CD8+ T cells rely less on GPX4 and exhibit greater resistance to ferroptosis (20). Through lipidomic analysis, we revealed that these subsets exhibit a differential abundance in PUFA-PLs that correlates with their susceptibility to ferroptosis. Indeed, in contrast to naive and memory CD8+ T cells, effector CD8+ T cells were enriched in abundant PUFA-PLs, suggesting that the differential PL composition of T cell subsets underpins their susceptibility to ferroptosis. However, whether the differential susceptibility observed in other T cell subsets, including Tfh and Tregs, can be explained by their PL composition remains to be determined.

Upon activation, T cells undergo rapid proliferation, accompanied by morphological, transcriptomic, epigenetic, and metabolic changes, to become effector T cells. Here, we show that activation is accompanied by important changes in the PL landscape. Interestingly, these changes were reversed, and memory CD8+ T cells harbored a lipid composition similar to that in naive T cells. PLs are the main component of cellular membranes, and the fatty acid composition of PL bilayers affects their biochemical function (42). Membranes enriched in PUFAs are more flexible and deform more easily (43), which might support the formation of the immunological synapse upon activation. Whether and how higher levels of PUFA-PLs affect the effector function of CD8+ T cells warrants further investigation. Highly proliferative cells use lipids present in the environment to fuel the synthesis of membranes, suggesting that effector T cells are in a PUFA-enriched environment. Nevertheless, the mechanism promoting these changes and allowing memory CD8+ T cells to return to a PL profile low in PUFA-PLs remains to be further investigated. Similar to memory CD8+ T cells, GPX4 was shown to be dispensable for an efficient response during a secondary viral infection (20). However, whether the PL profile of effector CD8+ T cells during a recall response remains low in PUFA-PLs and protects them, as observed in the memory stage, remains unknown. Deciphering the mechanism that promotes the shift in PUFA content observed between effector and memory T cells is essential for understanding how secondary effector CD8+ T cells survive in the absence of GPX4.

Many genes have been linked to ferroptosis, but their relevance seems to be context- and cell type–dependent, suggesting that the molecular mechanism promoting ferroptosis relies on intrinsic features of the cell. In summary, we demonstrate that the propensity of T cells to undergo ferroptosis and the molecular mechanisms promoting it depend on their PUFA-PL composition, which is specific to each cell type but can also be influenced by environmental factors. Inducing ferroptosis in therapy-resistant cancer cells, which heavily rely on GPX4, has been investigated as a potential therapeutic strategy (3, 4, 44). However, understanding the factors that promote the high vulnerability of T cells to ferroptosis will help define strategies that specifically target cancer cells.

Methods

Mice.

Gpx4fl/fl mice (11) (originally provided by M. Conrad, Helmholtz, Munich, Germany, and backcrossed to C57BL/6J for more than eight generations) were crossed to R26CreERT2/+ mice (45) to generate Gpx4fl/fl R26creERT2/+ mice. P14 mice (46) were crossed to Gpx4fl/fl R26creERT2/+ mice to generate Gpx4fl/fl R26creERT2/+ P14 mice, which were further crossed to R26Cas9-EGFP (26) mice to obtain Gpx4fl/fl R26creERT2/+; Cas9/+ P14 mice. TCRβ−/− (47) (JAX 2118), R26Cas9-EGFP (26) (JAX 24858) as well as CD45.1 P14 (46) (JAX 4694) mice were also used. Wild-type mice were either C57BL/6J (CD45.2, JAX 664) or B6 CD45.1 (JAX 2014) or an intercross (CD45.1/CD45.2 C57BL/6mice). Further details on mouse housing are available in SI Appendix.

Cloning for Single Knockout and Retrovirus Production.

For the generation of single knockouts, sgRNAs selected from the Teichmann library (Addgene #104861) or NT sgRNAs from the Brie library (48) were inserted in the pMSCV-U6sgRNA(BbsI)-PGKpuro2ABFP plasmid (Addgene #102796) according to the protocol (27). Sequences of the sgRNAs and a list of the plasmids used are available in SI Appendix, Tables S2 and S3. Details on the cloning strategy are available in SI Appendix. Retrovirus was produced using HEK293T cells (ATCC). Further details on retroviral production are available in SI Appendix.

Genome-Wide Library Amplification and Retrovirus Production for CRISPR Screens.

The retroviral Mouse Genome-wide CRISPR Knockout Library was a gift from Sarah Teichmann (Addgene #104861; http://n2t.net/addgene:104861; RRID:Addgene_104861) and was first amplified, and the quality of the amplification was checked (27). The library targets 18,424 genes with 90,230 sgRNAs. The amplification of the library was performed as previously described (49), and further details can be found in SI Appendix. Retrovirus was produced using HEK293T cells, and further details on the production are available in SI Appendix.

Splenic Cell Extraction and T Cell Magnetic Sorting.

Mice were killed by CO2 asphyxiation, and spleens were collected and pressed through a 70 μm cell strainer (Corning) to extract splenic cells. The resulting cell suspension was centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min. Erythrocytes were lysed by incubation with an ammonium–chloride–potassium (ACK, homemade) buffer for 5 min at room temperature. Splenic cells were resuspended in PBS (Gibco) plus 2% FBS and 2 mM EDTA for further magnetic enrichment or staining.

CD90.2+ or CD8+ positive enrichment was achieved using the magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) system following the manufacturer’s instructions (Miltenyi Biotec). Single cell suspensions were incubated with CD90.2 or CD8 mouse MicroBeads (Miltenyi Biotec) for 20 min at 4 °C in PBS plus 2% FBS and 2 mM EDTA. After washing, cells were passed through the LS column (Miltenyi Biotec) in the magnetic MACS separator.

T Cell Culture In Vitro.

MACS-sorted CD90.2+ or CD8+ T cells were counted, and 106 cells/mL were seeded in a suspension flat-bottom 24-well plate (Sarstedt) in T cell medium, namely IMDM plus GlutaMAX™ complemented with 10% FBS, 50 μM 2-mercaptoethanol, and 50 U/mL penicillin/streptomycin. Unless stated differently, T cells were activated for 24 h with 2 μg/mL soluble αCD3ε (BioLegend, clone 145-2C11), 2 μg/mL soluble αCD28 (BioLegend, clone 37.51), and 20 ng/mL mouse recombinant IL-2 (BioLegend). Following activation, fresh T cell medium with 20 ng/mL mouse recombinant IL-2 was supplemented daily, and cells were cultured for several days at 37 °C, 5% CO2. Further details on RSL3, iron, 4-hydroxytamoxifen (4-OHT), and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) treatments are available in SI Appendix.

T Cell Transduction.

For an efficient transduction, 106 T cells/mL were seeded and activated in suspension in a flat-bottom 24-well plate using T cell medium with 2 μg/mL soluble αCD3ε, 2 μg/mL soluble αCD28, and 20 ng/mL mouse recombinant IL-2 for a maximum of 24 h. Next, transduction was performed through 2 h of spin infection at 32 °C at 1,000 g in transduction medium containing up to 400 μL of retroviral supernatant, 20 ng/mL mouse recombinant IL-2, 1 μg/mL αCD28, and 8 μg/mL polybrene (hexadimethrine bromide, Sigma-Aldrich) in T cell medium. Different treatments were started at least 24 h post transduction.

When T cells isolated from R26Cas9-EGFP were used, transduced cells were selected by the addition of 3 mg/mL puromycin dihydrochloride (Sigma) in the T cell medium.

CRISPR Screening.

Approximately 3.5*107 CD90+ T cells were isolated from the spleens of four to five R26Cas9-EGFP mice and were seeded in T cell medium in suspension flat-bottom 24-well plates at a concentration of 2*106 cells/mL with 2 μg/mL soluble αCD3ε, 2 μg/mL soluble αCD28, and 20 ng/mL mouse recombinant IL-2 for activation. Cells were transduced as described at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.3 to 0.4, calculated based on a transduction efficiency of 30 to 40%, followed by puromycin selection (3 mg/mL puromycin dihydrochloride) and cultured for a few days (3 d for high RSL3 screen and 6 d for low RSL3 screen). Subsequently, a total of 4.5*107 cells per condition were seeded in suspension flat-bottom 24-well plates and treated with either 500 nM (high RSL3 screen) or 100 nM (low RSL3 screen) and DMSO. After 24 h of treatment, cells were harvested and sorted by fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) based on viability (SYTOX™Red−, [Invitrogen]) and blue fluorescent protein expression (BFP+), followed by genomic DNA extraction using the Puregene Cell Kit (Qiagen) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Both screens were performed in duplicates. Amplification of the sgRNA sequences and sequencing were performed as described in the SI Appendix. For downstream analysis, reads were demultiplexed using bcl2fastq (Illumina) and analyzed by MAGeCK v0.5.9.4 (50) to identify significantly (fdr < 0.05) enriched genes. Significantly enriched genes were used for Metascape (32) overrepresentation enrichment analysis. Sequences of sequencing primers are available in SI Appendix, Table S2.

Western Blot.

Further details on the methods used to analyze the levels of GPX4, ACSL4, and β-ACTIN are available in SI Appendix.

In Vivo T Cell Transfer and LCMV Infection.

For the transfer of transduced cells, splenic CD8+ T cells were isolated, activated for 24 h, and transduced as described. After spin infection, cells were kept in the transduction medium for four additional hours at 37 °C, 5% CO2, and 24 h after transduction, cells were harvested, washed with PBS, and 105 cells were transferred intravenously into TCRβ−/−. On d1 post transfer, mice were infected with LCMV WE 500 pfu intravenously, and tamoxifen was injected as described on d4 and d5. On d8, mice were killed by CO2 asphyxiation, and splenic cells were stained and analyzed as described.

To delete GPX4 in the memory phase, Gpx4fl/fl R26creERT2/+ mice were infected with LCMV WE 500 pfu intravenously. On days 38 and 39 post infection, mice were treated with tamoxifen as described, and T cells were stained and analyzed as described below on d45 post infection.

Tamoxifen Administration In Vivo.

Mice were treated as mentioned on two consecutive days with intraperitoneal injections of 2 mg of tamoxifen (Sigma-Aldrich) dissolved in 100 μL of corn oil (Sigma-Aldrich).

Flow Cytometry.

Viability and BODIPY™581/891 C11 stainings of in vitro cultured cells were performed as detailed in the SI Appendix. For in vivo stainings, splenic cell suspensions were stained as described in SI Appendix. Samples were acquired on a BD FACSymphony™ A5 SE cell analyzer, gating strategies are shown in SI Appendix. Figures and antibodies used are listed in SI Appendix, Table S1. For FACS, cells were not fixed.

Lipidomics Analysis.

Splenic CD8+ T cells were MACS-sorted from R26Cas9 /Cas9 P14 mice, activated for 24 h, and transduced as described. Some of the transduced cells were kept in culture in vitro and T cell medium with 20 ng/mL mouse recombinant IL-2 was supplemented daily from d0 to d8. Cells were also transferred on d0 in TCRβ−/− recipient that were subsequently infected on d1 with LCMV WE 500 pfu as described. On d8, in vitro and in vivo cells were harvested and stained in PBS plus 0.5% fatty acid–free BSA (Sigma-Aldrich) plus 2 mM EDTA and SYTOX™Red−, CD19−, TCRβ+, CD8+, Vα2+, BFP+ cells were sorted.

To generate effector and memory CD8+ T cell populations, splenic CD8+ T cells were isolated from P14 mice, and 105 cells were transferred intravenously in CD45.1/CD45.2 recipients. On d1 post transfer, mice were infected with LCMV WE 500 pfu. From naive mice, on d8 post transfer for effector cells or on d50 post transfer for memory cells, cells were harvested and stained in PBS plus 0.5% fatty acid–free BSA plus 2 mM EDTA, and SYTOX™Red-, CD19-, TCRβ+, CD8+, Vα2+, CD45.1+, CD45.2− cells were sorted.

The 5*105 sorted cells were centrifuged at 400 g for 5 min and the lipidomic analysis was performed by the Functional Genomic Centre Zurich. Further details on lipid extraction and LC–MS are available in SI Appendix. Downstream analysis was done using lipidR v2.14.1Bioconductor package (51) in R v4.3.2. We standardized lipid names using LIPID MAPS nomenclature and merged lipid isomers to the unique names. For the total chain saturation enrichment plots, we used a significance cutoff of adjusted P-value < 0.1. To highlight differences in lipid differential abundance, we used ComplexHeatmap v2.16.0 R package (52).
