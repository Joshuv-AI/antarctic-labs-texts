Familial Cerebral Cavernous Malformations : A Clinical Series and Literature Review
Authors: Huseyin Dogu, Ali Osman Mucuoglu, Abdulkerim Gokoglu, Roya Gasimli, Emre Tepeli, Hidayet Akdemir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveFamilial cerebral cavernous malformation (FCCM) is a genetically inherited condition involving the collection of abnormal slow-flow venous capillaries with no cerebral parenchyma in between. In this case series, we review the clinical, radiological, pathological, and genetic findings of seven blood relatives diagnosed with FCCM and discuss their treatment in light of the different presentations. MethodsThe patients with FCCMs were assessed in our neurosurgery clinic between April 2016 and October 2024. All patients underwent detailed clinical evaluation, radiological imaging, histopathological examination, and genetic testing. Functional outcomes were evaluated using the Karnofsky performance scale (KPS). ResultsFive of the seven patients were symptomatic, while the remaining two were asymptomatic carriers. On radiological examination, hemorrhagic type I lesions were detected in symptomatic cases, whereas the asymptomatic carriers had non-hemorrhagic type 4 lesions. Genetic testing revealed a heterozygous pathogenic mutation in the CCM1 gene in one of the symptomatic patients. The three symptomatic cases underwent surgery for complete resection of the lesions; no additional neurological deficit or residual lesion was detected postoperatively (postoperative KPS score, 100). Histopathological examination revealed benign cavernous angioma in all cases. Over the postoperative follow-up, no seizures were detected in patients who underwent surgery due to refractory epilepsy. ConclusionAdvances in molecular genetic testing have allowed for prompt diagnosis and timely management of patients with FCCMs. Surgical treatment is an effective option in symptomatic cases with progressive neurological deficits and refractory epilepsy. Regular neurological monitoring and radiological assessment are recommended in symptomatic cases and asymptomatic carriers.

INTRODUCTION

A cerebral cavernous malformation (CCM) is a collection of abnormally dilated, slow-flow vascular canals with no cerebral parenchyma in between vascular structures [19,39,41]. Vascular malformations of the central nervous system (CNS) are seen in 3–7% of the general population, with the most common being arteriovenous malformations, followed by CCMs or cerebral cavernomas [4,42]. Based on genetic and clinical features, CCMs are classified into two major groups – familial (FCCMs) and sporadic (SCCMs), with the sporadic form accounting for 80% of all CCM cases (prevalence : FCC=1 : 10000; SCC=1 : 100–1 : 200) [19]. Ninety percent of patients with SCCs have a single lesion, while 10% have multiple lesions. In contrast, FCCMs show an autosomal dominant inheritance, with 90% of patients showing multiple lesions [5,17,27,34,35].

FCCM is clinically diagnosed when one or more family members of an individual with a cavernoma are found to have similar lesions or when genetic testing identifies a mutation in either of the KRIT1 (CCM1), CCM2 or PDCD10 genes [12]. Approximately 50% of FCCMs remain asymptomatic throughout life, whereas the other 50% develop symptoms due to recurrent bleeding or an increase in lesion size. Previous studies have reported a 2.8–16.5% incidence of intracranial hemorrhage with FCCMs [40]. These lesions can be rarely seen in organs other than the CNS, including the skin, retina, liver, or spine [6,8,10,29,38].

Approximately 25% of cases with FCCMs and SCCMs are identified in the pediatric population, with a significant proportion of these malformations located in critical regions, including the brainstem and basal ganglia [30,36]. Although SCCMs are found in infants and children, symptoms usually appear in the second to fifth decades of life in general in cases with FCCMs [12]. A study including patients who underwent genetic testing reported that the time of symptom onset was below 15 years of age in 20% of cases [9].

The findings of the neurological examination vary according to the location of the lesion, the most common findings being headaches, epileptic seizures, focal neurological deficits, and cranial nerve paralysis. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) plays an important role in the diagnosis and radiological assessment. Zabramski classified cerebral cavernoma into four types based on MRI sequences : types I, II, III, and IV. Type I is the most common form of FCCM which is recognized by its characteristic radiological image of subacute hemorrhages within or around the lesion on the cerebral susceptibility weighted imaging (SWI) or the spinal gradient echo (GE) sequences [41].

FCCM is inherited in an autosomal dominant pattern with incomplete penetrance. The gene mutationss associated with FCCM (CCM1 [KRIT1], CCM2, and PDCD10) are located on chromosomes 7q21.2, 7p13, and 3q26.1, respectively; these genes encode proteins that regulate the formation of junctions between vascular endothelial cells [4,5,27,28,41,42].

In this study, we review the clinical, radiological, histopathological, and genetic results of seven patients diagnosed with FCCM and examine the treatment options employed in these patients. We also assess the characteristics of their family members with symptomatic FCCM who were found to carry the mutation and discuss these findings in light of the existing literature.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

The study was approved by the local ethics committee (approval number : E-22686390-050.99-56876). We retrospectively reviewed seven cases of FCCMs who were assessed by a senior surgeon (H.A.) and treated at the Department of Neurosurgery. Detailed physical and neurological examinations were performed for all patients referred to our hospital. Clinical symptoms and functional recovery were monitored using the Karnofsky performance scale (KPS) [16].

For the radiological evaluation, routine MRI sequence plus SWI for the brain and the GE sequence for the spinal cord were used. The lesions were classified based on the radiological (using SWI, GE, T1, and T2 MRI sequences) and histopathological criteria defined by Zabramski et al. [41].

Case 1

A 32-year-old female underwent brain surgery due to a left temporal cavernoma associated with resistant epileptic seizures diagnosed at the age of 22 years at another center (Fig. 1A). In the molecular genetic analysis, a heterozygous pathogenic mutation (NM_004912.3 [KRIT1] : c.1052_1053ins353) was detected in the KRIT1 gene. Postoperatively, the patient was followed up regularly in outpatient clinics and taking levetiracetam (500 mg twice daily) for 10 years. The postoperative MRI studies conducted between 2014 and 2024 revealed a significant increase in the size (2.7×2.6 cm) of a newly developed lesion with recurrent hemorrhages located in the left frontal region between multiple type I cavernoma in the brain. The new lesion in the left frontal region became symptomatic; eventually, a second craniotomy was recommended to the patient, for which she presented to our clinic (Fig. 1A). On July 15, 2024, we conducted a complete physical examination during the outpatient control visit, which revealed no abnormal findings. No neurological deficits other than persistent headache and forgetfulness were detected in the neurological examination; the KPS score was assessed as 80. An electroencephalogram (EEG) revealed abnormal discharges in the left temporal region. On July 17, 2024, the patient underwent a second surgery—left frontal craniotomy—at our clinic; en bloc cavernoma resection was performed using microsurgical techniques. The histopathological examination confirmed the lesion as a cavernous angioma (Fig. 1B). The early postoperative period was uneventful. At the three-month follow-up, the neurological examination was normal without neurological deficits or epileptic seizures. Annual clinical and radiological follow-up was recommended.

Case 2

A 14-year-old male presented with sudden-onset headache, vomiting, altered consciousness, dysphasic speech, and acute visual disturbances to the pediatric neurology emergency department of another center on September 16, 2024. Detailed anamnesis revealed that he was the son of case 1 and the mother had undergone two brain surgeries due to FCCM 2 months ago. Thus, the patient was immediately admitted to the intensive care unit for further investigation and follow-up. An emergency MRI of the brain revealed a hemorrhagic type I cavernoma in the mesencephalon (Fig. 2A). The patient was given medical treatment to regulate the intracranial pressure. An outpatient visit was scheduled after 2 months to perform genetic testing. During this visit (on November 17, 2024), we noticed that the patient’s clinical symptoms had improved; the followup MRI also demonstrated resorption in the hematoma of the lesion. However, a neurological examination revealed mild dysarthria and headache. The KPS score was assessed as 80. Another control visit was scheduled after 3 months.

Case 3

Case 3 was the 49-year-old father of case 1 and the grandfather of case 2. He was evaluated at our outpatient clinic on July 15, 2024. No abnormalities were detected on physical examination; however, the neurological examination revealed that the patient occasionally needed analgesics for headaches, but there were no neurological deficits. The KPS score was assessed as 90. The cranial MRI studies, particularly the SWI sequence, revealed multiple cavernomas in the brain and cerebellum (Fig. 2B). The patient was identified as an FCCM carrier; accordingly, molecular genetic analysis and periodical outpatient follow-up were recommended.

Case 4

We also evaluated the 49-year-old brother of case 3 and the uncle of cases 1 and 2 at our outpatient clinic. He had previously undergone spinal stabilization and decompression surgery at another center at the age of 38 years due to an acute pathological T11 vertebral compression fracture caused by atypical vertebral hemangioma. There were no significant complaints; the physical examination was also unremarkable apart from a dorsal skin incision scar from the previous surgery. However, in the neurological examination, we found that he had longstanding headaches, pain in both arms, and paresthesia in the hands; no objective neurological deficits were detected. The KPS was assessed as 80. The cervical and thoracic MRI studies showed instrumentation from the prior decompression and stabilization procedure. However, multiple CCMs were detected in the cranial diffusion MRI study. A cervical MRI was performed on July 13, 2024, which showed two small non-hemorrhagic type IV cavernomas within the cervical cord on the GE sequences (Fig. 2C). The patient was prescribed 600 mg gabapentin twice daily as medical treatment and recommended regular outpatient follow-up. At the 3-month control visit, significant improvement was observed in the arm pain and paresthesia in the hands. He was further to attend regular outpatient control visits and undergo molecular genetic analysis because of the cerebral and spinal findings consistent with FCCM.

Case 5

The 14-year-old son of case 4 was evaluated in our outpatient clinic. Detailed history revealed that his 3-year-old sister had died earlier due to recurrent bleeding from a CCM before any intervention. The boy had also presented to a neurology clinic 3 months ago complaining of sudden onset headache, dizziness, vomiting, imbalance, and loss of vision. Multiple cavernomas were detected in imaging studies, the largest measuring 3.5×3.4 cm in the left occipital region. Surgical treatment was recommended to the patient for this symptomatic, hemorrhagic type I cavernoma (Fig. 3A). The patient presented to our clinic for surgical treatment on March 15, 2024. Notably, a dark brown to black cutaneous cavernoma was identified on the posterolateral region of the left thigh during this visit (Fig. 3B). In the neurological examination, headaches, ataxia, diplopia, and heteronymous hemianopsia in the right eye were noted; the KPS score was assessed as 70. On March 18, 2024, a left occipital craniotomy was performed and the cavernoma was completely excised using microsurgical techniques (Fig. 3C). Histopathological examination confirmed the specimen was a benign cavernous angioma. The early postoperative period was uneventful. A month after the surgery, the visual field symptoms had improved, and no residual cavernoma was observed in the surgical area on the follow-up brain MRI (Fig. 3D). At the 3-month follow-up visit, the KPS score was assessed as 100, and no neurological deficits were detected. Annual control visits and molecular genetic testing were recommended to the patient.

Case 6

A 50-year-old woman, the sister of case 4, was also evaluated in our clinic. She underwent stereotactic radiotherapy at 35 years of age (February 27, 2015) at another center for hemorrhagic type I cavernoma in the right pons, following which she developed hemiplegia and had to receive physical therapy. The patient went through regular clinical and radiological followups between 2019 and 2024. In the most recent MRI study conducted on September 3, 2024, a small residual cavernoma (10 mm in diameter) with minor bleeding was detected in the ventral pons following radiotherapy, which was confirmed to be stable (Fig. 4). In the physical examination, the patient reported using analgesic medications for many years due to chronic headaches and antihypertensive drugs for systemic hypertension. In the neurological examination, she had clumsiness, nystagmus, ataxia, and mild hemiparesis in the left arm and leg on cerebellar testing; the KPS score was assessed as 70. A senior neuroradiologist performed the radiological assessment which confirmed no signs of recurrent bleeding (September 14, 2024). The patient was recommended regular neurological and radiological follow-ups for the residual FCCM, and to visit the emergency department in case of acute onset of any new neurological symptoms. It was emphasized that antihypertensive medications should be taken regularly and that she should avoid nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), aspirin, and other anticoagulant agents. Molecular genetic testing was also recommended.

Case 7

The 45-year-old sister of case 6 was also evaluated in our clinic. In her anamnesis, she reported presenting to the emergency department of another neurology clinic with a generalized seizure at 37 years of age. She was hospitalized for further evaluation because of the family history of FCCMs in first-degree relatives. The brain MRI study conducted during hospitalization revealed a large, hemorrhagic type I cavernoma among multiple CCMs, which was considered to be symptomatic; in addition, left frontal epileptic discharges were detected on EEG. Accordingly, antiepileptic therapy was initiated and regular control visits were recommended. However, the antiepileptic therapy was rearranged because of the increased frequency of seizures. After consultation with the relevant departments, surgical treatment was recommended for the left frontal, hemorrhagic type I cavernoma. Subsequently, the patient presented to our clinic for surgical treatment. In the initial assessment performed on June 6, 2016, no abnormal physical or systemic finding was detected. In the neurological examination, no neurological deficit other than headache and seizure were detected; the KPS was assessed as 80. The EEG revealed left frontal epileptic discharges, while a hemorrhagic type I cavernoma (4×3 cm) was detected in the left frontal region on the MRI study (Fig. 5A). On June 17, 2016, the patient underwent a left frontal craniotomy in our clinic. The hemorrhagic cavernoma was resected completely using microsurgery techniques. The early postoperative period was uneventful, and the neurological examination was considered normal at the first-month postoperative control visit with no signs of seizures or neurological deficit. Histopathological examination confirmed the specimen as a benign cerebral cavernous angioma (Fig. 5B). On a follow-up visit 8 years after the surgery (September 8, 2024), the patient showed no neurological deficit, a KPS score of 100, and no seizures or the need for an antiepileptic. The radiological findings were also unremarkable. Regular control visits and molecular genetic testing were recommended to the patient.

DISCUSSION

FCCMs are reported to occur in approximately one in 5000 cases to one in 10000 cases in the general population. However, exome sequencing studies suggest a higher frequency, ranging between one in 3300 to one in 3800 individuals [37]. FCCMs were first described in 1928 by Kufs H; subsequent research demonstrated that this condition follows an autosomal dominant inheritance pattern [41].

It was reported that individuals with FCCMs have a 50% chance of passing the mutation to their offspring [27]. A genetic study reported the following distribution of the causative genes within FCCM carrier families : 40% for CCM1 (KRIT1), 20% for CCM2, and 40% for CCM3 [11]. Toldo et al. [38] stated that CCM1 (KRIT1) is the gene responsible for FCCMs in 70% of carrier families. Similarly, in our study, a heterozygous pathogenic mutation in CCM1 (KRIT1) was detected in case 1 in the molecular genetic testing performed after the first surgery.

FCCMs can occur at any age and in both sexes and are more prevalent in the Hispanic populations in the southwestern region of México and the United States [3,11,14,21,31,32,37]. In this study, the cases presented belong to a large Turkish family; some of the family members are living in Turkey, while the majority of members, who were FCCM carriers or patients, were living abroad.

Anatomically, CCMs show significant variability according to their location. In the supratentorial region, they can be superficially (lobar, subcortical, or in critical or non-critical areas) or deeply (thalamic, basal ganglia, intraventricular, or periventricular) located. In the infratentorial region, they are typically found in the cerebellar hemispheres or in different anatomic locations such as the brainstem (mesencephalon, pons, or medulla oblongata) [27].

About 70–86% of FCCMs are located in the supratentorial compartment while 24–30% are found in the infratentorial compartment, 50% of which are located in critical regions such as the brainstem [7]. In our study, one of the three cases treated surgically underwent two separate surgical interventions at a 10-year interval due to lesions located in the left temporal and left frontal regions. The remaining two cases had lesions in the left occipital and left frontal regions, respectively.

FCCMs generally present as multiple lesions – supratentorial and infratentorial lesions can be detected on MRI using SWI sequences, whereas spinal lesions are identified using GE sequences. In case 4, multiple cavernomas were observed in the brain and cerebellum on the cranial SWI sequences. In addition, two cavernoma were detected within the spinal cord on the GE sequences. Supratentorial FCCMs can cause headaches clinically based on their location, size, and the amount of hemorrhage in particular, but the majority of lesions are silent and usually remain asymptomatic. However, in cases where the cavernoma is located in the temporal and frontal regions, the individual may present to the emergency department with epileptic seizures caused by hemosiderin and iron accumulation in the gliotic tissue resulting from repeated hemorrhages.

Infratentorial cavernomas, particularly those located in the brainstem, manifest with acute neurological symptoms and findings, including nausea, vomiting, vision loss, altered consciousness, and cranial nerve palsy. These patients generally seek immediate medical attention with these symptoms. Furthermore, 20–50% of the FCCMs are incidentally detected during neuroimaging studies (MRI or computed tomography scan) ordered for another reason [18].

A previous study reported that the annual risk for bleeding from supratentorial CCMs ranged from 1.6% to 4.6% while the 5-year cumulative risk for bleeding was 15.8% [37]. However, the bleeding rate for infratentorial brainstem CCMs was reported to be as high as 60% [1,25,26]. Zabramski et al. [41] reported a 25–32% incidence of hemorrhage occurring within or around type I CCMs. Because FCCMs are dynamic conditions, both outpatient neurological examination and radiological MRI studies are essential to determine which of the multiple lesions are clinically symptomatic. In the present case series, we defined four distinct types of CCMs based on radiological (SWI, GE, T1, and T2-weighted MRI sequences) and histopathological findings.

In the literature, there are a relatively limited number of studies. Alalfi et al. [2] reviewed 75 patients with FCCMs (60% females; mean age, 41±16 years) in whom large or symptomatic lesions were located in the supratentorial region; 27 of these patients were asymptomatic while the remaining were symptomatic at the time of diagnosis. During the 9.9-year follow-up, the authors reported a prospectively estimated bleeding rate of 4%, while the de novo seizure rate was 1.2%. In addition, 64% of the patients experienced at least one symptomatic bleeding, while 32% experienced at least one seizure. Further, 38% of the patients underwent at least one surgical intervention, while 5.3% received stereotactic radiotherapy. In the final follow-up visit, 83% of patients survived independently with an modified Rankin scale score of ≤2. Likewise, Geraldo et al. [13] reviewed 41 pediatric cases (53.7% male), of which 15 cases (36.3%) presented with symptomatic bleeding. The authors estimated the 5-year bleeding rate as 5.0%; for symptomatic bleeding, the 1-, 2-, and 5-year cumulative risk was found to be 7.3%, 14.6%, and 17.1%, respectively. Furthermore, the risk for bleeding was found to be higher in children with a history of previous bleeding (33.3%), CCM2 genotype (33.3%), and positive family history (20.7%) [13].

From the histopathological perspective, CCMs are well-defined lesions; they are typically described as well-circumscribed vascular structures of varying sizes with multiple nodules and a popcorn-like appearance. In all three cases treated surgically in our study, histopathological examination reported vascular proliferation compatible with benign cavernous angioma and dystrophic calcification foci. Typically, a conservative approach, surgery, and stereotactic radiotherapy are used to manage FCCMs. Those with small and asymptomatic lesions are generally managed conservatively with regular follow-ups; additionally, antimigraine medications can be used to alleviate headaches. However, narcotic analgesics should be avoided owing to the risk of dependency and rebound effects. In addition, the use of NSAIDs, such as ibuprofen, naproxen, and aspirin, should be avoided as these medications are known to increase the risk of hemorrhage in cavernomas. Surgical treatment should be employed in symptomatic patients with large solitary lesions who have refractory epileptic seizures or have developed focal neurological deficits due to the mass effect caused by repeated bleeding [15,20,22,23,33].

Stereotactic radiotherapy is preferred in symptomatic lesions located in critical regions, particularly in the ventral brainstem (mesencephalon, pons, and medulla oblongata). However, surgical treatment is recommended for hemorrhagic and symptomatic lesions located in the dorsal pons and medulla oblongata. Additionally, repeat surgical intervention is recommended in patients with recurrent hemorrhages from residual lesions after the index surgery or stereotactic radiotherapy or in symptomatic patients [24]. In FCCM cases treated surgically because of refractory epilepsy, one of the most significant prognostic factors influencing improvement in epilepsy treatment is achieving complete resection of the lesion and removing the surrounding gliotic tissue as much as possible depending on the location of the lesion [25].

CONCLUSION

In the past two decades, advancements in molecular genetic testing for hereditary diseases have significantly contributed to the management and treatment of FCCMs by identifying family- specific pathogenic variants. As FCCM is a dynamic disease, asymptomatic family members across all age groups should also undergo molecular genetic testing to detect specific pathogenic variants. FCCM carriers and patients require regular neurological follow-ups and MRI studies if new clinical symptoms occur. In our study, a patient with the CCM1 (KRIT1) heterozygous pathogenic mutation was monitored for 10 years with regular MRI studies, which revealed the progression of a type I cavernoma located in the frontal region by recurrent hemorrhages.

When planning treatment for FCCM cases, surgical intervention should be considered in the presence of progressive neurological deficits, refractory epilepsy, or newly emerging focal neurological deficits; in case of minor symptoms, such as isolated headaches, conservative management is preferred. Furthermore, in case the lesion is located in critical areas with high morbidity and mortality risks for surgical intervention, stereotactic radiosurgery may be considered. Asymptomatic carriers should be managed with regular outpatient follow-ups and periodic monitoring.

Limitations

The retrospective nature and parameters used in our study limit the generalizability of the data obtained. In addition, our dataset encompasses a relatively small population; larger prospective studies with long-term follow-up are needed. These studies will help better understand the relationship between genetic and clinical findings and optimize the treatment approach. Furthermore, in this study, we reported seven patients from the same family with rare familial cavernomatosis. Due to the rarity of this condition, our cases are limited to these individuals. For this reason, we have presented our series as an original article.
