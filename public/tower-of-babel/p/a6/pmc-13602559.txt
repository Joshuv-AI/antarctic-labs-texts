Characterization of mitochondrial genomes from three medicinal species of rutaceae and comparative analysis within the family: insights into evolution
Authors: Tianying Ren, Jing Wang, Lijun Mu, Yingxin Ma, Yichen Huang, Xuekun Wang, Pan Li, Lusha Ji
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRutaceae encompasses numerous medicinal plants with unclear genetic relationships and mitochondrial genome (mitogenome) characteristics, which hinders understanding of their evolutionary adaptation and medicinal trait development. Here, we aimed to investigate the conserved and divergent features of mitogenomes among closely related medicinal genera of Rutaceae, and to explore how mitogenomic variations correlate with their phylogenetic positions. Citrus aurantium, Toddalia asiatica, and Zanthoxylum nitidum (Roxb.) DC are medicinally valuable but understudied Rutaceae species belonging to different genera. We sequenced, assembled, and annotated their complete mitogenomes: C. aurantium (504,387 bp, 45.17% GC content) contained 62 genes (36 protein-coding genes (PCGs), 3 rRNAs, and 23 tRNAs), T. asiatica (566,784 bp, 45.37% GC content) harbored 58 genes (35 PCGs, 3 rRNAs, and 20 tRNAs), and Z. nitidum (539,013 bp, 45.48% GC content) possessed 61 genes (34 PCGs, 3 rRNAs, and 24 tRNAs). Comparative analyses with 12 published Rutaceae mitogenomes showed that the three species exhibited the highest number of RNA editing sites in PCGs among the examined species. Evaluation of selective pressure and gene clusters revealed fewer differences between C. aurantium and C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan than with the other two species, while C. sinensis displayed greater dissimilarities with other Citrus species (notably, C. maxima and C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan were conspecific and should have the least divergence). Phylogenetic trees constructed using 24 conserved mitochondrial PCGs clarified evolutionary relationships: C. maxima was closest to C. sinensis, followed by C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan and C. aurantium formed an unexpected separate clade, highlighting the need for more Rutaceae species’ mitogenome in future phylogenetic analyses. This study expands the resource pool in Rutaceae mitogenomic resources, provides insights into features that may be associated with ecological adaptation, and offers valuable genomic data for future phylogenetic and comparative studies of Rutaceae medicinal plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13078-y.Keywords: Rutaceae, Mitochondrial genome, Gene clusters, Selective pressure, Phylogeny

Introduction

The Rutaceae family encompasses a diverse assemblage of medicinal plants with a long-standing history of application in traditional medicine, among which Citrus aurantium, Toddalia asiatica, and Zanthoxylum nitidum (Roxb.) DC stand out for their prominent pharmacological value [1–3]. The essential oil of C. aurantium, rich in aromatic compounds, not only serves as a safe flavoring and solvent but also significantly inhibits colon cancer cell proliferation [4], while robust cellular antioxidant activity of its extracts highlights potential for development as natural antioxidants and antiseptics [3]. T. asiatica, a traditional medicinal plant in tropical regions, is documented for its efficacy in treating malaria, rheumatism, and gastrointestinal disorders [5]; modern studies confirm its antimicrobial and analgesic properties, further revealing that its extracts can inhibit the growth and metastasis of cancer cells, as well as have cardiovascular protective effect [1]. Z. nitidum, as a classic herb, has been used for thousands of years as a detoxifying, analgesic, and hemostatic agent for relieving toothache, sore throat, stomachache, and snakebites [6]; its alkaloid components (e.g., nitidine and chelerythrine) exhibit potent anti-tumor activities by inducing cell apoptosis and inhibiting proliferation in various cancer cell lines, with nitidine chloride [7, 8].

Despite their recognized medicinal importance, the genetic underpinnings of their ecological adaptation and pharmacological trait development remain poorly understood, particularly regarding the mitochondrial genome (mitogenome). Mitochondria, as intricate intracellular organelles, have long been acknowledged as the “cellular powerhouses” due to their vital role in oxidative energy metabolism [9]. Serving as the central metabolic hub, mitochondria swiftly adjust to varying environmental signals and metabolic changes to meet cells’ bioenergetic demands, a phenomenon termed “mitochondrial plasticity” [10]. Owing to this high plasticity, mitochondria form a dynamic network of signaling organelles that play pivotal multifunctional roles in cell metabolism, proliferation, and survival [11].

Plant mitogenomes are notably distinct from their animal counterparts: they are typically over 1000 times larger and structurally more complex, primarily attributed to frequent recombination events [12]. The landscape of large plant mitogenomes is shaped by multiple factors, such as a diverse gene repertoire containing numerous introns, the capacity to acquire and incorporate intracellular transfer sequences from chloroplasts and the nucleus, and frequent horizontal gene transfer from external sources [13–15]. To systematically assess the current state of Rutaceae mitogenomic resources, we conducted a comprehensive retrieval of publicly available, complete mitogenome assemblies from authoritative databases (NCBI GenBank and EMBL Nucleotide Archive) up to 30 September 2025, using the search strategy: “Rutaceae“[Organism] AND “mitochondrial genome“[Title], with explicit exclusion of partial sequences (e.g., single-gene fragments), unpublished drafts, and chloroplast/nuclear genome contaminants. This retrieval identified only 8 mitogenomic records, all corresponding to 4 species within the Citrus genus (C. sinensis, C. clementina, C. reticulata, and C. grandis), including 2–3 assembly versions per species (e.g., 3 distinct assemblies for C. sinensis differing in contiguity) [3]. Notably, recent studies on Rutaceae mitogenomes (e.g., Chen et al. 2023 on plastid-mitochondrial gene transfer in Z. bungeanum; Sun et al. 2022 on fruit development in C. grandis) have also highlighted the scarcity of complete mitogenomes for non-Citrus medicinal genera and the lack of research on mitogenomic links to secondary metabolism (e.g., respiratory chain genes regulating alkaloid synthesis) [16, 17]. No complete mitogenomes of C. aurantium, T. asiatica, or Z. nitidum were found in these databases, and their absence was further confirmed by a comprehensive search of all updates through 15 October 2025.

A striking imbalance exists between Citrus nuclear and mitogenome data (≈ 50 nuclear vs. 8 mitogenomes). This primarily stems from prioritized research on nuclear genomes (focusing on fruit quality, disease resistance, etc.) and technical challenges in plant mitogenomics, plant mitogenomes, featuring large size, complex structural variations, and frequent horizontal gene transfer, are laborious to assemble and annotate. Such data paucity has created three critical knowledge gaps in Rutaceae medicinal plant biology. First, a comparative mitogenomic framework integrating structural and functional traits for closely related medicinal genera is lacking, leaving conserved genetic features and genus-specific adaptive variations unclear. Second, the evolutionary and functional links between mitogenomic variations and key traits (e.g., T. asiatica’s drought tolerance and Z. nitidum’s nitidine synthesis) remain unknown. Third, taxonomic controversies (e.g., C. aurantium’s placement in Citrus) persist due to limited markers, while the resolving potential of mitochondrial conserved PCGs remains untapped.

To address these gaps, we generated high-quality mitogenomes of C. aurantium, T. asiatica, and Z. nitidum via next-generation sequencing (NGS) combined with long-read technology, followed by meticulous assembly and annotation. This study focused on three key questions: (1) Genus-specific differences in mitochondrial gene content (especially plastid-derived tRNAs), repeat composition, and RNA editing site distribution; (2) Whether a phylogeny based on 24 conserved mitochondrial PCGs can resolve controversies like C. aurantium’s taxonomy; (3) Whether species-specific mitochondrial traits (e.g., Z. nitidum’s partial matR fragment) correlate with their ecological adaptations.

We anticipate our findings will fill critical gaps in Rutaceae mitogenomic resources, shed light on the evolutionary relationships of medicinal plants, the complex evolutionary relationships of medicinal plants, and provide a valuable mitogenomic dataset for phylogenetic studies within Rutaceae.

Materials and methods

Plant materials

The samples were collected from South China Botanical Garden, Tianhe District, Guangzhou, Guangdong Province (N23°11′12″, E113°22′50″), and the collected specimen was stored in the Herbarium of Liaocheng University (LCU Herbarium, Liaocheng, Shandong, China). The taxonomist responsible for formal morphological identification of all three Rutaceaemedicinal plants is from the biology department of the same university. The corresponding unique deposition voucher numbers for three plant samples are CA20220701LP, TA20220701LP, and ZN20220701LP.

For Illumina sequencing, genomic DNA was isolated via a modified CTAB method [18]. Codon usage analysis, selective pressure analysis, RNA editing sites prediction, and identification of repeat sequences

Codon usage analysis: Codon usage frequency and relative synonymous codon usage (RSCU) of all PCGs were analyzed using CodonW v1.4.4 (http://codonw.sourceforge.net/).

Selective pressure analysis: To assess PCG selective pressure, nonsynonymous (Ka) and synonymous (Ks) substitution rates were calculated for each PCG among C. aurantium, T. asiatica, Z. nitidum, and four reference plants (selected by taxonomic affinity and data availability: two Rutaceae species C. sinensis (GenBank ID: MG736621) and C. maxima (MN946544); two model outgroups Arabidopsis thaliana (NC_037304) and Oryza sativa (NC_011033). Orthologous gene pairs were aligned and formatted with ParaAT2.0 (default parameters) [19]. Ka, Ks, and Ka/Ks values were computed via KaKs_Calculator v2.0 (YN method) [20], with Fisher’s exact test (P < 0.05) to determine significant deviation from 1: Ka/Ks > 1 (P < 0.05)= positive selection; Ka/Ks < 1 (P < 0.05)= purifying selection; P ≥ 0.05 = neutral evolution. Identification of repeat sequences: Three repeat types (SSRs, tandem repeats, dispersed repeats) were detected with literature-supported parameters: (1) SSRs via MISA-web (https://webblast.ipk-gatersleben.de/misa/): 1–6 nucleotide motifs, thresholds (mono ≥ 8, di ≥ 4, tri-hexa ≥ 3), 100 bp minimum interval [22]; (2) tandem repeats via Tandem Repeats Finder (https://tandem.bu.edu/trf/trf.html): alignment parameters match = 2, mismatch = 7, indel = 7 (plant mitogenome default); (3) dispersed repeats via REPuter (http://bibiserv.techfak.uni-bielefeld.de/reputer): ≥ 30 bp, ≥ 90% global identity, E-value≤ 1e-10, no overlaps; counted across 6 size intervals (30–49, 50–69, 70–99, 100–149, 150–199, ≥ 200 bp).

Phylogenetic analysis

To construct phylogenetic trees, the mitogenome sequences of the selected species (C. aurantium, T. asiatica, Z. nitidum, C. maxima, C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan, C. unshiu, and C. sinensis) were uploaded to the NCBI BioProject database, with the respective BioProject accession numbers: PRJNA1013489 (for C. aurantium), PRJNA1013192 (for T. asiatica), and PRJNA1013133 (for Z. nitidum). The specific links to the BioProject entries are as follows: C. aurantium (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA1013489), T. asiatica (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA1013192), Z. nitidum (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/bioproject/PRJNA1013133). This upload was conducted to ensure data accessibility, reproducibility of phylogenetic analyses, and seamless integration with published Rutaceae mitogenome data (e.g., C. maxima MN946544 and C. sinensis MG736621) for accurate sequence alignment.

To identify homologous PCGs, 24 PCGs were selected from these sequences. We aligned the homologous PCGs using MAFFT v7.429 with the FFT-NS-2 strategy and a model finder to select GTR + F + R2 [23, 24]. The phylogenetic tree was constructed using IQTREE v1.6, with 1000 bootstrap replicates and maximum likelihood methods [25].

Results

Genomic features and gene content of mitochondrial genomes

The complete sizes of the mitochondrial genomes of Citrus aurantium, Toddalia asiatica, and Zanthoxylum nitidum were 504,387 bp, 566,784 bp, and 539,013 bp, respectively, with respective GC contents of 45.17%, 45.37%, and 45.48%. The cumulative length of PCGs in C. aurantium, T. asiatica, and Z. nitidum amounted to 32,352 bp, 31,227 bp, and 29,799 bp, respectively. Among these genomes, C. aurantium harbored a total of 62 genes, including 36 PCGs, 3 rRNA genes, and 23 tRNA genes. T. asiatica possesses 58 genes, consisting of 35 PCGs, 3 rRNA genes, and 20 tRNA genes. Z. nitidum contains 61 genes, comprising 34 PCGs, 3 rRNA genes, and 24 tRNA genes (Table 1).

Gene content differences and plastid origin of tRNAs

Genus-specific differences were observed in tRNA content: Citrus species (including C. aurantium) had two copies of trnM, while T. asiatica and Z. nitidum had only one. Additionally, Z. nitidum uniquely contained a partial matR gene fragment, which was absent in both Citrus and Toddalia. To explore the potential plastid-derived origin of genes contributing to the observed gene number variations (especially tRNA genes), we conducted sequence similarity analysis between tRNA genes of the three species’ mitogenomes and published plastid tRNA sequences of Rutaceae species (e.g., C. sinensis plastid genome, NC_008334; Z. bungeanum plastid genome, NC_060110) using BLASTn (e-value cutoff 1e-10). The results showed that four tRNA genes (trnI-CAU, trnV-GAC, trnW-CCA, and trnY-GUA) in C. aurantium, three tRNA genes (trnI-CAU, trnV-GAC, and trnY-GUA) in T. asiatica, and four tRNA genes (trnI-CAU, trnV-GAC, trnW-CCA, and trnM-CAT) in Z. nitidum shared ≥ 98% sequence identity with corresponding plastid tRNAs. This indicated that these tRNA genes in the mitogenomes were possibly derived from plastids via intracellular gene transfer (IGT), a common phenomenon in plant mitochondria [26, 27].

Among the PCGs, all were present as single copies in C. aurantium, except for atp1 and rps12, which were present in duplicate. In Z. nitidum, atp9 and rpl16 also had two copies. Regarding the RNA genes, there were three rRNA genes (rrn5, rrn18, and rrn26), each with a single copy. In terms of tRNA genes, trnM, trnI, trnP, and trnS had two or four copies, with trnM-CAT being present in four copies in C. aurantium. T. asiatica had two or three copies of trnM, trnK, and trnS, while Z. nitidum had two or four copies of trnM, trnI, trnK, trnQ, trnS, and trnN. Furthermore, we compared the distribution of PCGs among C. aurantium, T. asiatica, Z. nitidum, C. maxima, C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan, C. unshiu, and C. sinensis. We found that all three species (C. aurantium, T. asiatica, and Z. nitidum) lacked the genes rps2, rps8, rps11, and rps13. Additionally, C. aurantium lost four additional genes (rpl2, rps1, rps14, and rps19), T. asiatica lost three additional genes (rps7, rps19, and sdh3), and Z. nitidum lost six additional genes (rpl2, rpl5, rps7, rps10, rps14, and sdh3) (Fig. 1, Table S2).

Codon usage and RNA editing prediction in PCGs

In the mitogenomes of the three plants, the majority of PCGs employed ATG as the start codon (Table S3). However, there were exceptions: rps10 in T. asiatica, as well as nad1 and nad4L in all three species, initiated with ACG; C. aurantium’s rps12 began with AGT; C. aurantium’s mttB, rps10, and sdh3, T. asiatica’s rps4, and Z. nitidum’s ccmFN started with TTG; T. asiatica’s mttB started with GCG; Z. nitidum’s mttB started with CTC; Z. nitidum’s rpl16 started with GTG; Z. nitidum’s rps1 started with TCA; Z. nitidum’s rps19 started with TCC, and Z. nitidum’s rps4 started with TTA. Regarding stop codons in PCGs, three types were identified: (1) TAA in genes atp6, atp8, atp9, cox1, nad1, nad2, nad3, nad4L, nad5, nad6, nad9, rpl10, rpl16, rpl5, and rps4 in all three plants; atp1, ccmFN, rps7, and sdh4 in C. aurantium; rps1 in T. asiatica; atp4 and rps1 in Z. nitidum; (2) TAG in genes cox2, matR, mttB, nad7, and rps3 in all three plants; ccmFC in C. aurantium and Z. nitidum; atp4 in T. asiatica and C. aurantium; rps19 in C. aurantium, rps14 in T. asiatica, and rps12 in Z. nitidum; and (3) TGA in other genes.

As presented in Table S3, codon usage analysis revealed that leucine (Leu) was the most frequently utilized amino acid residue, while cysteine (Cys) was the least utilized in the mitochondrial proteins of C. aurantium, T. asiatica, and Z. nitidum. Furthermore, based on the results of the RSCU analysis of the three plants (Figure S1), among the first codons (blue color) of all 23 amino acids, seven codons had RSCU values lower than 1, two codons had RSCU values of 1, and the remaining codons had RSCU values higher than 1. However, among the second codons of 21 amino acids, six codons had RSCU values higher than 1, while the others had RSCU values lower than 1. Only nine amino acids had a third codon, and eight had a fourth codon. Among the fourth codons, only one RSCU value was higher than 1, and among the third codons, only one RSCU value was lower than 1.

The codon usage in four species within the genus Citrus were also analyzed. Figure 2 illustrates that leucine was the most frequently utilized amino acid residue and cysteine the least frequent across all seven examined species. The consistent bias toward AT-rich codons (e.g., TAA stop codon dominance) in all three species was consistent with the overall AT-rich nature of plant mitogenomes (45.17–45.48% GC content in this study) and reflected evolutionary constraints on codon-anticodon pairing efficiency. Additionally, the shared codon usage bias among Citrus species (C. aurantium, C. maxima, and C. sinensis) (Fig. 2) further supported their close phylogenetic relationship, complementing the findings from phylogenetic analysis.

Repeat sequences analysis

In this study, we conducted an analysis of the mitogenomes of C. aurantium, T. asiatica, and Z. nitidum to identify simple sequence repeats (SSRs), tandem repeats, and dispersed repeats. A total of 470, 492, and 412 intact SSRs were detected in the whole genome of the three species, encompassing six types of repeats: mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-, and hexa-nucleotide repeats (Table S4). As expected, the number of SSRs showed a negative correlation with complexity, with mononucleotide repeats being the most abundant type. In C. aurantium and T. asiatica, the number of mononucleotide repeats reached 198 and 204, respectively. Conversely, in Z. nitidum, dinucleotide repeats were the most prevalent, accounting for 170 SSRs, followed by mononucleotide repeats (155 SSRs). Together, these two types of SSRs represented 78.94%, 78.05%, and 78.88% of the total SSRs in C. aurantium, T. asiatica, and Z. nitidum, respectively, while the remaining four types accounted for 21.06%, 21.95%, and 21.12% (Table 3).

In addition to SSRs and dispersed repeats, we also identified tandem repeats in the mitochondrial genomes of C. aurantium, T. asiatica, and Z. nitidum. Specifically, we detected 23 tandem repeats with lengths ranging from 5 to 40 bp in C. aurantium, 27 tandem repeats with lengths ranging from 9 to 45 bp in T. asiatica, and 18 tandem repeats with lengths ranging from 12 to 45 bp in Z. nitidum (Table S5). The presence of dispersed repeat sequences is known to have a significant impact on mitogenomes. Furthermore, long repetitive sequences have proven to be valuable markers in studies related to plant evolution, comparative genomics, and phylogenetics. These repeats exhibited a wide range of lengths, from 30 bp in C. aurantium to 16,949 bp in C. maxima (Fig. 4; Table 4). Interestingly, the majority of the repeats across all species fell within the range of 30–49 bp in length. The number of repeats varied among the species, with the smallest number ranging from a large repeat exceeding 1 kb in length in all species except for C. sinensis, which had the smallest number of repeats ranging from 150 to 199 bp. The total number of large repeats (> 1 kb) ranged from 1,225 bp in Z. nitidum to 16,949 bp in C. maxima, including two in C. aurantium, one in C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan, one in C. maxima, seven in C. sinensis, and two in Z. nitidum (Table S5). Despite the common occurrence of dispersed repeats in the seven angiosperm mitogenomes analyzed in this study, they were not well conserved across species, even within the same genus.

Selective pressure analysis

To investigate the selective pressure indicated by the value of Ka/Ks on these genes within seven species, the values of Ka/Ks were calculated. Ks represents the estimated number of synonymous substitutions per synonymous site, reflecting the rate of neutral amino acid evolution. Ka is the number of non-synonymous substitutions per non-synonymous site. Under neutral protein level evolution, Ka should be equal to Ks, resulting in a Ka/Ks ratio of 1. A Ka/Ks ratio < 1 indicates purifying or stabilizing selection, which acts against change, while a ratio > 1 suggests positive or Darwinian selection, driving change.

Ka/Ks analysis on 30 shared mitochondrial PCGs across the seven species were performed. As shown in Table 4, we found that between C. aurantium and T. asiatica, 25 PCGs had non-zero Ka/Ks values. The same was observed for 25 PCGs between C. aurantium and Z. nitidum, seven PCGs between C. aurantium and C. maxima, five PCGs between C. aurantium and C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan (the fewest among all interspecific comparisons involving C. aurantium), eight PCGs between C. aurantium and C. sinensis, and seven PCGs between C. aurantium and C. unshiu. Similarly, we identified 25 PCGs between T. asiatica and C. maxima, 24 PCGs between T. asiatica and C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan, 23 PCGs between T. asiatica and C. sinensis, 26 PCGs between T. asiatica and C. unshiu, and 14 PCGs between T. asiatica and Z. nitidum. Lastly, we discovered 26 PCGs between Z. nitidum and C. maxima, 25 PCGs between Z. nitidum and C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan, 23 PCGs between Z. nitidum and C. sinensis, and 27 PCGs between Z. nitidum and C. unshiu.

Notably, the minimal number of PCGs with nonzero Ka/Ks values (5) between C. aurantium and C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan indicates that the two taxa share the most similar molecular evolution rates among all compared pairs involving C. aurantium. This pattern typically reflects a closer evolutionary relationship, as fewer genes showing substitution rate differences suggest more recent divergence or stronger functional constraint on shared PCGs. In contrast, the high number of PCGs with nonzero Ka/Ks values (25) between C. aurantium and T. asiatica/Z. nitidum further confirms their distant phylogenetic affinity, consistent with their genus-level taxonomic distinction.

Based on the Ka/Ks values, we identified genes with ratios greater than one, indicating positive selection. In C. aurantium, the gene nad5 exhibited positive selection compared to C. maxima, C. sinensis, and C. unshiu. Additionally, nad1 showed positive selection compared to C. sinensis, rps4 showed positive selection compared to T. asiatica and Z. nitidum, and nad7 and rpl5 showed positive selection compared to T. asiatica. Furthermore, rpl10 and ccmFN showed positive selection when compared to Z. nitidum (Fig. 5; Table 4).

Gene clusters and phylogenetic analysis

To investigate the conservation of mitochondrial gene organization, we analyzed 11 gene clusters across seven Rutaceae species (C. aurantium, T. asiatica, Z. nitidum, C. maxima, C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan, C. unshiu and C. sinensis) using data from Table S6. Six clusters ((rpl5)-rps4-nad6, nad1-rpl10, rps12-nad3, rrn18-rrn5, nad4L-atp4, and (atp8)-cox3-sdh4) were highly conserved across all species, while five others (atp9-nad5, rps3-rpl16, rps10-cox1, nad1-matR-nad7, and nad2-sdh3) showed species-specific losses: atp9-nad5 was absent in C. aurantium, C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan, and C. sinensis; rps3-rpl16 and rps10-cox1 were lost only in Z. nitidum; nad1-matR-nad7 was missing in C. sinensis; and nad2-sdh3 was absent in C. sinensis, T. asiatica, and Z. nitidum. Notably, Z. nitidum and C. sinensis lost the most clusters, while C. maxima retained all 11, even its cultivar C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan lacked atp9-nad5, and the remaining species (C. aurantium, T. asiatica, and C. unshiu) each lost only one cluster, reflecting minimal interspecific differences within the family but key taxon-specific variations.

Plant mitogenomes exhibit high structural fluidity due to repeat-mediated homologous recombination, sequence duplications, genome expansion/shrinkage, and foreign DNA incorporation—traits well-documented in green plants from charophytic algae to angiosperms. This fluidity often fragments ancestral ribosomal gene clusters into smaller units (≤ four genes), yet functionally critical clusters remain conserved in large-scale phylogenetic analyses. Gene organization also correlates with evolutionary distance: closely related species share similar gene order and clusters, while family-level differences emerge from genome recombination. Based on our gene cluster data, we inferred that C. sinensis and Z. nitidum were likely distantly related to other species, and C. aurantium shared a closer relationship with C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan than with wild C. maxima.

We constructed a maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic tree using the seven species’ mitogenomes (Fig. 6), with 100% bootstrap support for all nodes confirming reliability. The tree showed C. maxima is closer to C. unshiu than to C. sinensis—consistent with prior chloroplast and nuclear DNA studies [28]. However, C. aurantium did not cluster with the C. sinensis-C. unshiu-C. maxima clade, forming an independent branch instead. Additionally, T. asiatica was closer to Z. nitidum than to any Citrus species, which contradicted inferences from gene cluster analysis.

The phylogenetic tree of seven Rutaceae species

To resolve this inconsistency, we integrated findings from the selective pressure analysis: C. aurantium and C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan had only five PCGs with nonzero Ka/Ks values—far fewer than the 25 PCGs observed between C. aurantium and T. asiatica/Z. nitidum, and fewer than the 7–8 PCGs between C. aurantium and other Citrus species (C. maxima, C. sinensis, and C. unshiu). This minimal molecular evolution rate difference between C. aurantium and C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan aligns with their close relationship inferred from gene cluster conservation, yet contradicts the phylogenetic tree topology where C. aurantium forms a separate clade. We hypothesize this discrepancy may stem from structural variations in the mitogenome of C. aurantium, such as the unique 1,200 bp repeat in the nad7 upstream region (Fig. 7). This repeat sequence could mediate homologous recombination, altering genome rearrangement patterns and introducing phylogenetic signals that diverge from those of coding regions. To validate this hypothesis, future analyses should integrate nuclear genome data, as nuclear-mitochondrial interactions may further clarify the evolutionary position of C. aurantium within Citrus.

This inconsistency likely stems from differing analytical scopes: gene cluster analysis only considers the presence/absence of specific clusters, while phylogenetic analysis incorporates the entire mitogenome sequence. Intraspecific differences between C. maxima and its cultivar (C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan) may also influence tree topology. To further validate Citrus-specific traits, we compared C. aurantium with four congeneric species (C. sinensis, C. maxima, C. reticulata, and C. grandis): their mitogenomes ranged from 460 to 520 kb (C. aurantium was smallest at 462,891 bp), all shared 30 PCGs, and C. aurantium uniquely had a 1,200 bp repeat in the nad7 upstream region (Fig. 7)—a feature potentially regulating gene expression during fruit ripening [16]. Collectively, these findings highlight complex evolutionary relationships within Citrus and Rutaceae, emphasizing the need for more comprehensive analyses.

Discussion

Mitogenomic conservation and divergence in Rutaceae

Codon usage bias reflects conserved energy metabolism functions

The codon usage analysis further enriches our understanding of Rutaceae mitogenomic divergence and functional adaptation. The consistent preference for Leu-encoding codons (UUA and UUG) across all seven species confirms the conservation of mitochondrial energy metabolism functions, as Leu is critical for the structural stability of respiratory chain complexes. The AT-rich codon bias (reflected in TAA stop codon dominance) aligns with the GC content of the three species’ mitogenomes (45.17–45.48%) and is a common feature of plant mitochondria, shaped by a balance between mutation pressure and natural selection acting at the level of translation [29].

Dispersed repeats drive structural evolution of Rutaceae mitogenomes

Dispersed repeats play a key role in plant mitogenome evolution by mediating homologous recombination, a major driver of structural rearrangements [30, 31]. In this study, the high length variability of dispersed repeats across species (Fig. 4) is associated with gene order disruption (e.g., C. sinensis, with the most large repeats, exhibits extensive gene cluster losses) and genome size differences (e.g., Z. nitidum harbors more short repeats than C. aurantium) [28, 31]. Additionally, their low conservation even among congeneric taxa provides valuable genetic markers for phylogenetic studies, complementing PCG-based signals. Dispersed repeats flanking acquired sequences (e.g., the partial matR fragment in Z. nitidum, Figure S2) suggest their role in integrating foreign DNA [32], suggesting their involvement in structural evolution of Rutaceae mitogenomes.

Plastid-derived tRNAs underpin gene number variation among genera

Gene number variation among C. aurantium (62 genes), T. asiatica (58), and Z. nitidum (61) primarily reflects differences in plastid-derived tRNA genes, not ribosomal PCGs. Four, three, and four tRNA genes, respectively, are of plastid origin (≥ 98% identity with Rutaceae plastid tRNAs), supporting intracellular gene transfer as a key driver of tRNA variation in angiosperms [33]. Ribosomal PCGs showed no plastid signals, confirming they do not contribute. The association between plastid-derived tRNA number and habitat humidity (C. aurantium > Z. nitidum > T. asiatica) hints at a potential role in environmental adaptation.

SSR distribution reveals genus-specific adaptive evolutionary patterns

SSR analysis revealed distinct repeat preferences: mononucleotide repeats dominated in C. aurantium (198 copies) and T. asiatica (204 copies), while Z. nitidum showed unique dominance of dinucleotide repeats (170 copies). The prevalence of more mutable mononucleotide repeats in the two species occupying stable habitats (warm-humid and arid, respectively) aligns with the role of SSRs in generating adaptive genetic variation. Notably, genome size and SSR content are decoupled in Rutaceae. Despite C. aurantium has the smallest mitogenome, it contains more SSRs than Z. nitidum. This result suggests that recombination intensity, rather than genome expansion, drives SSR accumulation in angiosperms [17]. This inference is consistent with the high recombination rate inferred from two large dispersed repeats (> 1 kb) in C. aurantium (Table 3).

Species-specific gene losses represent adaptive evolutionary trade-offs

Core PCG conservation and genus-specific structural variation in Rutaceae

Rutaceae mitogenomes share 24 core PCGs essential for energy homeostasis (Table S7). This conservation pattern aligns with other angiosperms [16]. In contrast, genus-specific variations occur in tRNA copy number, repeat dynamics, and genome size [31]. Z. nitidum harbors a partial matR fragment flanked by dispersed repeats (Figure S2), also reported in drought-tolerant Reynoutria japonica [35], suggesting a potential role in stress tolerance pending transcriptomic validation. Cultivated Citrus species retain two trnM copies, possibly enhancing translational efficiency, while T. asiatica and Z. nitidum have one. Notably, Rutaceae exhibits a decoupling of genome size and SSR content, contrasting with the positive correlation reported in Rosaceae [36, 37, 38]. This suggests that recombination intensity may play a greater role than genome expansion in shaping SSR content within this family, consistent with hypotheses proposed for angiosperm mitochondrial evolution [17].

Phylogenetic significance of mitochondrial markers

Conserved mitochondrial PCGs resolve intergeneric phylogenetic relationships

The mitochondrial PCG phylogeny (Fig. 6) clarified evolutionary relationships within Rutaceae: T. asiatica and Z. nitidum formed a well-supported sister clade, while C. aurantium clustered with other Citrus species. Notably, it clarified the long-ambiguous relationship between Toddalia and Zanthoxylum, which previous studies with limited markers failed to resolve. The congruence between mitochondrial signals and morphology supports PCGs as reliable markers for Rutaceae systematics, consistent with their utility in other plant groups (e.g., Papilionoidinae and Stemon).

RNA editing sites act as fine-scale phylogenetic markers

RNA editing analysis supported phylogenetic relationships within Rutaceae. The three focal species exhibited more editing sites (C. aurantium: 344; T. asiatica: 355; Z. nitidum: 347) than other Rutaceae (324–343). These differences may reflect distinct evolutionary trajectories. Within Citrus, C. aurantium shared an editing profile with C. maxima cultivar Hirado Buntan (high ccmB editing) but differed from C. sinensis (high ccmFN editing), consistent with its distinct phylogenetic position (Fig. 6). For intergeneric relationships, conserved editing in nad1 and nad7 (> 20 sites each) further supported the T. asiatica-Z. nitidum sister relationship. Thus, RNA editing sites provide fine-scale markers complementing core PCGs.

Limitations and future directions

Key limitations of the present study

While our family-level analysis revealed key patterns of Rutaceae mitogenome evolution, several limitations should be noted. First, taxonomic sampling was limited to three genera (Citrus, Toddalia, and Zanthoxylum), excluding other medicinally important genera (e.g., Phellodendron and Evodia). Second, intraspecific diversity was not assessed. Sequencing different geographic populations (e.g., South China vs. Southeast Asia Z. nitidum) could link genetic variation to habitat adaptation. Third, multi-omics integration was lacking. Transcriptomic and metabolomic data could may clarify links between mitochondrial genes and medicinal compound biosynthesis.

Future research directions

Future work should address these limitations: (1) expand taxonomic sampling to include more medicinal Rutaceae genera (e.g., Phellodendron amurense and Evodia rutaecarpa). (2) sequence multiple geographic populations of Z. nitidum to assess intraspecific variation and adaptive traits. (3) integrate multi-omics data (e.g., drought stress experiments and medicinal compound analysis) to explore functional implications. (4) validate species-specific tandem repeats (e.g., in rps1 of T. asiatica) as molecular markers. (5) incorporate chloroplast genome data to resolve phylogenetic conflicts. These approaches will advance understanding of Rutaceae mitogenome evolution and its relevance to medicinal plant resource conservation.

Conclusions

In this study, we successfully assembled and annotated the complete mitochondrial genomes of Citrus aurantium, Toddalia asiatica, and Zanthoxylum nitidum, three medicinally important Rutaceae species from distinct genera. We conducted a systematic comparative mitogenomic analysis of these three species and other reported Rutaceae taxa, encompassing genomic characterization, codon usage patterns, repeat sequence distribution, selective pressure, gene cluster conservation and phylogenetic inference based on mitochondrial PCGs. This study complements the current Rutaceae mitogenomic resource pool by providing high-quality mitochondrial genome data for three understudied medicinal species, clarifies the conserved structural and functional features as well as genus-specific divergent characteristics of Rutaceae mitochondrial genomes, and offers a robust mitochondrial genomic dataset for subsequent phylogenetic and comparative genomic studies of the Rutaceae family.
