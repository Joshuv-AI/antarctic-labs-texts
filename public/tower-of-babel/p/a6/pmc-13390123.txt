Toward simultaneous pseudo-space reconstruction and cell-type deconvolution of single-cell spatial transcriptome using SpaDicer
Authors: Junming Zhang, Shi Yin, Lingxi Xu, Chen Wu, Hui Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummarySingle-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) and spatial transcriptomics (ST) offer complementary insights into tissue heterogeneity. Each modality is characterized by inherent trade-offs: scRNA-seq offers high resolution but lacks spatial context, while ST retains spatial information but compromises resolution. Here, we introduce SpaDicer, an end-to-end deep-learning framework designed to bridge these gaps by unifying pseudo-spatial reconstruction of single cells and cell-type deconvolution of ST spots. SpaDicer employs cross-domain feature disentanglement to extract domain-invariant feature encoding both cellular identity and spatial localization. An adaptive loss weighting strategy harmonizes multiple objectives across data reconstruction, cell-type deconvolution, and spatial regression tasks. We evaluated SpaDicer on two simulated datasets and on five real-world datasets covering a wide range of human tissue contexts, demonstrating consistent improvement over state-of-the-art methods in reconstructing cellular spatial localization and resolving cell-type composition. Through a unified multi-task learning framework, SpaDicer provides a powerful tool for advancing spatially informed biological discovery.Keywords: single-cell RNA sequencing, spatial transcriptome, cell-type deconvolution, pseudo-space reconstruction, feature disentanglement

Introduction

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) enables unbiased profiling of transcriptome of single cells and has become a mainstream approach for unraveling cellular compositions in complex tissues.1 Despite scRNA-seq providing valuable opportunity to dissect tissue heterogeneity, it requires dissociation of tissues into single-cell suspensions, which inevitably results in the loss of spatial context. Spatial localization is critical for understanding cell states and functions within complex tissues.2,3,4 The spatial transcriptome (ST) has emerged as a powerful technology for capturing the spatial patterns of gene expressions in diseased and healthy tissues.5,6,7,8,9,10 Current ST technology can be primarily categorized into two major types: (1) imaging-based protocols, such as MERFISH11 and seqFISH+,12 and (2) sequencing-based protocols, such as STARmap and 10× Visium.13 However, the imaging-based protocols are often limited in the number of genes assayed at once, whereas sequencing-based protocols aggregate transcriptomic signals from multiple cells—often 10 or more—within a single spot,14 resulting in reduced resolution and sensitivity for single-cell detection.

In recent years, some methods have been developed to utilize the precise cell-type signatures obtained from scRNA-seq to inform cell-type deconvolution in ST data.15,16,17,18,19,20 For instance, Cell2location20 integrates scRNA-seq data with ST using a Bayesian framework to infer the spatial distribution of cell types while leveraging prior annotations for improved accuracy. SpatialDWLS18 accurately deconvolves spatial transcriptomics data by leveraging scRNA-seq references with a robust weighted least-squares approach. CARD21 is a spatially informed deconvolution method that integrates scRNA-seq with ST data by modeling spatial correlations in cell-type compositions across tissue locations to enhance deconvolution accuracy and enable high-resolution spatial mapping. Tangram2 is designed to learn spatially resolved single-cell transcriptomes and to address the deconvolution challenges inherent to ST data. On the other hand, a pioneer method scSpace22 has been proposed to infer single-cell spatial locations, termed as pseudo-spatial reconstruction.2,22,23 It leverages transfer component analysis (TCA)24 to reconstruct cellular spatial associations and identify spatially heterogeneous subpopulations from single-cell data using ST data as references. Similarly, despite these advancements, existing methods fail to fully exploit the complementary potentials of scRNA-seq and ST to simultaneously enhance the performance of both cell-type deconvolution and pseudo-spatial reconstruction tasks.

In this paper, we propose SpaDicer, a cross-domain feature disentanglement framework for the joint analysis of scRNA-seq and ST data. Our method employs the domain separation network25 to extract common features across domains that encapsulate both cell-type identity and spatial position information. We ensure that the common features are disentangled from domain-specific confounders by imposing orthogonality constraints. By integrating pseudo-spatial reconstruction for single cells and cell-type deconvolution for ST spots in a unified multi-task learning framework, SpaDicer allows mutual supervision between these two domains, thereby maximizing the utility of their complementary information. Extensive experiments conducted on multiple simulated and real-world datasets demonstrated that SpaDicer consistently outperforms current state-of-the-art methods on both tasks, enhancing the interpretability and practical application of scRNA-seq and ST data in spatially informed biological research. To our best knowledge, SpaDicer is the first unified framework for jointly modeling both pseudo-spatial reconstruction and cell-type deconvolution. By effectively leveraging the complementary information inherent in scRNA-seq and ST data, our approach achieves pronounced mutual reinforcement in performance across both tasks.

Results

SpaDicer framework

SpaDicer is a deep-learning model that integrates feature decoupling and multi-task learning to effectively extract task-relevant features from both scRNA-seq and ST data (Figure 1). Specifically, SpaDicer employs a domain separation network to disentangle shared feature from domain-specific confounding factors. The shared encoder is used to capture feature representing both cell identity and spatial localization, while private encoders retain domain-specific feature by imposing orthogonality constraints. The multi-task learning architecture jointly optimizes three objectives: data reconstruction, cell-type classification, and spatial coordinate regression. To enhance model robustness, an adaptive weighting strategy dynamically balances these training objectives during training phase. Once trained, the predictors leverage the extracted features to infer pseudo-spatial coordinates of individual cells and deconvolve cell-type compositions for each spot.

To evaluate the performance, we employed a range of metrics for specific tasks. For pseudo-space reconstruction, we calculated pairwise distances between all cells based on their predicted and actual spatial coordinates, and then the Pearson correlation coefficient (PCC) between the predicted and actual pairwise distances was calculated to assess the overall spatial reconstruction accuracy. We also evaluated the performance along each dimension by separately computing the PCC for x axis and y axis coordinates. For the cell-type deconvolution task, model performance was evaluated by comparing the predicted and actual proportions of various cell types within each spot. Three metrics were employed to provide a comprehensive assessment: the PCC, the structural similarity index measure (SSIM), and the root-mean-square error (RMSE). For the cell-type multi-class classification task, we report the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve, as well as Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) and enrichment ratio (ER) (see STAR Methods). These metrics collectively provide a multi-faceted evaluation of our model’s predictive accuracy across tasks.

Benchmarking on two simulated mouse cortex datasets

For performance evaluation, multi-cell-per-spot ST datasets with known cell compositions for each spot were required. For this purpose, we used the ST data from mouse cortex and mouse visual cortex datasets established by Li et al.15 These datasets were derived by gridding single-cell resolution ST data using the coarse-graining procedure introduced by SpatialDWLS.18 The cortex dataset (dataset 4 in original study) consists of 72 spots, with each spot representing a 500 × 500 pixel region. The visual cortex dataset (dataset 10 in original study) contains 189 spots, each spanning a 750 × 750 pixel region. To simulate the gene expression profile of spots containing multiple cells, the expression values of all cells within each spot are aggregated. Since both datasets are single-cell-resolution ST data, the spatial positions of individual cells are inherently defined, which allows us to evaluate SpaDicer’s performance in pseudo-space reconstruction. To facilitate performance evaluation on cell-type deconvolution task, we clustered cells in the mouse visual cortex dataset and annotated each cluster based on well-documented marker genes. This process identified four types of excitatory neurons (L2/3, L4, L5, and L6), as well as oligodendrocytes (olig), astrocytes (astro), and endothelial cells (endo). Cell types with relatively low abundance were grouped into a single category labeled as other type. The proportions of each cell type within each spot were calculated and used as the ground truth for evaluating the cell-type deconvolution task.

To benchmark the performance of SpaDicer on the pseudo-space reconstruction task, we compared it against the representative baseline method scSpace.22 To ensure an objective evaluation, both methods were independently run for 50 times on both dataset, and the average results were reported as the final performance metrics. In terms of the pairwise distance-based PCC metric, SpaDicer consistently outperformed scSpace on both datasets, achieving substantially higher PCC values (0.51 vs. 0.32 and 0.75 vs. 0.43, respectively). The statistical significance of SpaDicer’s performance advantage was confirmed by the Wilcoxon rank-sum test,26 yielding p values of 1.8e−4 for both datasets. To further examine spatial reconstruction capability, we separately evaluated the PCC metric regarding the x and y coordinates. Across both datasets, SpaDicer demonstrated superior performance over scSpace in both dimensions (Figures 2A and 2B). Notably, both methods exhibited markedly higher accuracy along y axis compared to x axis, with the x axis showing greater variances across datasets. To investigate the directional discrepancy, we visualized the spatial distributions of cells using both the actual and predicted coordinates from two mouse cortex datasets (Figures 2C and S1). The results revealed clear stratification of cell types along the y axis, whereas variations along the x axis were relatively limited. Kernel density estimation (KDE) of the actual cell-type distribution (Figures 2D and 2E) highlighted more pronounced differences along the y axis, whereas variation along the x axis is subtle. This anisotropic spatial organization likely offers stronger positional cues along the y axis, thereby enabling our method to infer spatial localization in that dimension with greater accuracy (Figures 2F and 2G).

For the cell-type deconvolution task, we benchmarked SpaDicer against 10 previously published methods, including Cell2location,20 DestVI,27 DSTG,28 RCTD,19 Seurat,29 Tangram,23 SPOTlight,17 Stereoscope,30 STRIDE,31 and SpatialDWLS.18 The results showed that SpaDicer outperformed all other methods in both PCC and SSIM metrics, achieving a mean PCC of 0.79 and a mean SSIM of 0.69, with all p values below 0.001 (Figure 2H). For RMSE metric, while some methods obtained slightly lower mean RMSE values than SpaDicer, the difference between the top-performing methods Cell2location and SpaDicer was not statistically significant (0.15 vs. 0.16, p value = 0.3). The findings indicated that SpaDicer’s RMSE performance was comparable to current state-of-the-art method. Furthermore, we designated the cell type with the highest proportion in each spot as the primary cell type, resulting in eight distinct cell types. SpaDicer was then evaluated on this multi-class classification task. The ROC curves on both datasets confirmed SpaDicer’s robust performance, with micro- and macro-average AUCs of 0.96 and 0.97, respectively, for the visual cortex dataset and 0.96 and 0.95, respectively, for the cortex dataset (Figures 2I and 2J). These results underscore that SpaDicer effectively captures cell-type proportions and achieves superior performance in cell-type deconvolution tasks.

Layer-resolved spatial reconstruction and deconvolution in human DLPFC

We further collected ST data of the human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) profiled by 10× Visium and STARmap32 to examine SpaDicer’s performance. As a critical region of the cerebral cortex, the DLPFC comprises six distinct layers (layers 1–6), each specialized in functions such as information reception, processing, and feedback. Additionally, the white matter (WM) layer supports interregional communication within the brain by transmitting signals critical for cognitive processes like working memory (Figures 3A–3F). This intricate hierarchical structure presents a stringent benchmark for evaluating spatial domain restoration and cell-type deconvolution tasks. We analyzed three tissue sections (no. 151674–151676 from original study) derived from a single donor. These sections contained 3,635, 3,566, and 3,431 spots, respectively, with an average of approximately 3.3 cells per spot. Each spot provided both gene expression profiles and the spatial positional information of the cells within the spot. For objective evaluation, we adopted a hold-out cross-validation strategy, where two sections were iteratively used for training and the remaining section for validation. During training, the ST data from the training set served as the spatial reference, while spot coordinates in the test section were withheld to reconstruct a pseudo-spatial map. For cell-type deconvolution task, SpaDicer estimated the probability of each spot belonging to a specific hierarchical category, thereby enabling the assignment of spots to their respective cortical layers. During data preprocessing, we used Seurat v.3 to identify the top 8,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) in each section. The intersection of HVGs across the three sections yielded 2,027 HVGs, which served as input for subsequent analyses.

We initially evaluated SpaDicer’s performance in reconstructing pseudo-spatial coordinates. Taking section no.151674 as an example, we visually compared the true and predicted spatial localization of spots and observed that SpaDicer effectively restored their spatial coordinates (Figure 3A). Quantitative assessment was conducted by calculating the pairwise-distance PCC between the predicted and ground-truth coordinates, where SpaDicer achieved a higher PCC compared to scSpace (0.58 vs. 0.52) (Figure 3B). To evaluate generalizability, we applied SpaDicer to the other two sections by hold-out validations. Consistently, SpaDicer outperformed scSpace in reconstructing spatial localizations of spots (Figures S2A, S2B, S3A, and S3B). Given the critical importance of capturing spatial landscape of gene expression, we conducted differential expression analysis to identify biomarker genes specific to each cortical layer and visualized their spatial expression landscape. The results revealed a strong concordance between the actual and predicted spatial landscape (Figures 3D and 3E; Figures S2D, S2E,S3D, and S3E), further underscoring SpaDicer’s robust capability in reconstructing spatial transcriptomic patterns.

To further evaluate SpaDicer’s performance in the cell-type deconvolution task, we treated each cortical layer as a distinct cell type, thereby formulating the problem as a seven-class classification challenge. SpaDicer demonstrated exceptional performance in this task (Figure 3C; Figures S2C and S3C). For example, in section no. 151674, SpaDicer achieved a macro-average AUC of 0.89 and a micro-average AUC of 0.90. Particularly notable was its near-perfect classification of the WM layer, with AUC values reaching 0.99 across all three sections. The only exception was observed in layer 4 of section 151676, where the AUC dropped slightly below 0.8. To visually examine the accuracy of spot-level cell-type classification, we compared the true spatial localization of seven cortical layers with the predicted layers for each spot (Figures 3F and 3G; Figures S2F, S2G,S3F, and S3G). These comparisons confirmed a high degree of concordance between the predicted and ground-truth spatial distributions of spots, further highlighting SpaDicer’s robust capability in spatially resolved cell-type deconvolution.

We are also interested in the effectiveness of feature disentanglement and the biological interpretability of the common features. For this purpose, we generated the UMAP visualization for both common and private features of the DLPFC dataset (Figure S4A). It can be found that the common and private features are clearly separated, with the private features from the two domains exhibiting distinct clustering, while the common features from both domains are mixed. This result provides strong evidence supporting the effectiveness of our model’s feature disentanglement. Meanwhile, we observed that the common features are prominently clustered according to cell identity and gradually form a layered structure from left to right (Figure S4B). This outcome is exactly consistent with our speculation, as the common features are intended to encode not only cell identity but also spatial localization information of individual cells. To illustrate the model interpretability, we explored the genes or pathways that contribute most to specific task by computing SHAP values for input genes to quantify their contributions to cell-type convolution task. As expected, we found that MOBP, a gene known to be specifically highly expressed in oligodendrocytes, ranked among the top contributors (Figure S4C). Moreover, functional enrichment analysis of the top 100 genes revealed significant involvement in biological processes related to nervous system development, including myelination, axon ensheathment, and ensheathment of neurons (Figure S4D). These findings indicate that our method effectively captures genes that are critical for both cell-type identification and spatial localization.

Spatial reconstruction and layer deconvolution of solid tumors

Given the high molecular heterogeneity inherent in solid tumor tissues, reconstructing pseudo-space topology and deconvoluting constituent cell types present formidable challenges. To further benchmark SpaDicer, we applied it to two human tumor datasets: cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC)33 and HER2-positive breast cancer (BC-HER2+).34 For cSCC dataset, three 10× Visium sections (r1, r2, and r3) from patient P2 (666, 649, and 638 spots, respectively) were profiled. We selected the top 8,000 highly variable genes (HVGs) per section and used their intersection (3,106 HVGs) as input. Similarly, for BC-HER2+ dataset, three sections (A3, A4, and A5) from patient A (359, 344, and 332 spots) yielded 2,746 intersecting HVGs. To generate matched single-cell and spatial profiles from the same tissue, we treated one ST section as a pseudo-scRNA-seq dataset, performed unsupervised clustering to define “cell-type” labels, and then employed a leave-one-section-out cross-validation scheme: two sections for training and the held-out section for testing.

We first evaluated SpaDicer’s performance on the cSCC dataset. Visual inspection showed that SpaDicer effectively recapitulated spot spatial coordinates (Figure 4A; Figures S5A and S6A). On section r2, SpaDicer reconstructed spot coordinates with a pairwise-distance PCC of 0.56 against true Visium coordinates, exceeding scSpace’s PCC of 0.53 (Figures 4A and 4B). Notably, SpaDicer exhibited higher accuracy in recovering spot coordinates along the y axis compared to the x axis (Figures 4D–4F). In the hold-out cross-validation setting, SpaDicer consistently outperformed scSpace across r1 and r3 sections as well (Figures S5A, S5B, S6A, and S6B). For deconvolution task, spots were clustered into five histological layers (Figure 4G), thereby formulating a five-class classification task. Experimental results confirmed that SpaDicer excelled in this task (Figure 4C; Figures S5C and S6C). To further illustrate its efficacy, we visualized the true and predicted spatial distributions of each layer across the three sections (Figures 4F and 4G; Figures S5F, S5G, S6F, and S6G) and observed that the predicted layer distributions closely matched the ground truth across all sections. Similarly, we computed SHAP values of input genes to quantify their contribution to specific task. From the top 20 genes most relevant to the spatial reconstruction task (Figure S7A), we found that the top-ranked gene KRT2 is selectively expressed in squamous epithelial cells and highly upregulated in cutaneous spindle cell melanoma (see the human protein atlas). Functional enrichment analysis of top 100 genes further highlighted strong associations with biological processes central to epidermal biology, including epidermis development, skin development, and keratinocyte differentiation (Figure S7B). These findings underscore the ability of our approach to uncover biologically meaningful signals, demonstrating that the common features not only capture task-relevant genes but also reveal interpretable molecular determinants of spatial organization.

We further evaluated SpaDicer’s performance on the BC-HER2+ dataset. Across slices A3–A5, SpaDicer effectively reconstructed the spatial localization of spots and consistently outperformed scSpace (Figures S8, S9, and S10). Interestingly, unlike the observations from the cSCC dataset, SpaDicer achieved substantially better performance in recovering spot coordinates along x axis compared to the y axis, with PCCs of 0.857 and 0.175, respectively. A closer examination revealed pronounced variations in cell-type distributions along the x axis, which likely provided stronger positional cues, thereby enhancing the model’s ability to capture spatial information along this dimension. For cell-type deconvolution task, SpaDicer classified spots into four layers, with an average AUC exceeding 0.96 across all slices, highlighting its robustness and precision in cell-type classification.

Generalizability evaluation on paired and unpaired tumor samples

To assess SpaDicer’s generalizability, we evaluated its performance using real scRNA-seq and ST data. Specifically, we utilized paired scRNA-seq and ST datasets from a human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) patient A.35 The scRNA-seq and ST data were generated from the same tumor specimen. After data preprocessing, the PDAC-A ST section retained 426 spots, while the scRNA-seq dataset retained 1,926 cells. Following gene filtering, 3,556 HVGs were retained. Based on cell-type annotations and spatial distributions, cancer clone A and cancer clone B cells were predominantly enriched in the cancer region, whereas ductal-major histocompatibility complex (MHC) class II, ductal-CRISP3, and ductal-terminal cells were mainly localized within the duct epithelium region. In contrast, acinar cells were clustered in the pancreatic region (Figure S11A). Inspired by LETSmix,36 we evaluated model performance using Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) and the enrichment ratio (ER). JSD quantifies the similarity between the estimated cell-type proportions in ST spots and the ground truth (typically derived from matched reference scRNA-seq data), thereby assessing the accuracy of deconvolution at the global compositional level. Lower JSD values indicate higher performance. ER evaluates whether a specific cell type is significantly enriched within its expected spatial regions, reflecting the accuracy of spatial localization, and higher ER values indicate superior performance.

We first evaluated SpaDicer’s performance using the paired scRNA-seq and ST samples of PDAC-A patient. For pseudo-space reconstruction, SpaDicer effectively recapitulated the spot spatial coordinates (Figure S11B). Next, we evaluate its performance of cell-type deconvolution against three representative methods, GraphST, Cell2Location, and Tangram. SpaDicer consistently achieved the lowest Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) and the highest enrichment score (ER) among all methods (Figures S11C and S11D), demonstrating its superior accuracy in cell-type deconvolution. Spatial visualization further confirmed these results, as SpaDicer produced predictions that were most consistent with the ground-truth regional annotations, exhibiting well-defined spatial boundaries and minimal noise (Figures S11E–S11H). While GraphST showed comparable but slightly inferior performance, Cell2Location and Tangram suffered from varying degrees of cell-type mixing in multiple regions. Notably, acinar cells exhibited relatively lower deconvolution accuracy, likely due to the severe imbalance between the number of cell types in the scRNA-seq reference (20 cell types) and the limited number of acinar-associated spots (13) in the ST data.

We further conducted validation experiments using unpaired scRNA-seq and ST samples to demonstrate our model generalizability. Specifically, we utilized data from two human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) patients, denoted as PDAC-A and PDAC-B.35 To construct an unpaired setting, we trained our model using ST data from PDAC-A and scRNA-seq data from PDAC-B, and then predicted the cell-type proportions of each PDAC-A ST spot. After preprocessing, the ST section from PDAC-A retained 426 spots, while the scRNA-seq dataset from PDAC-B retained 1,733 cells. Following gene selection, 3,319 HVGs were shared between the two datasets. Using cell types derived from the scRNA-seq data of PDAC-B, three spatial regions—cancer, duct epithelium, and pancreatic—were annotated in the ST section of PDAC-A (Figures 5A and 5B), corresponding to three representative cell types: cancer clone A, ductal-MHC class II, and acinar cells, respectively. The results show that our method achieved the lowest Jensen-Shannon divergence (JSD) and the highest enrichment score (ER) (Figures 5C and 5D), substantially outperforming all competing algorithms in terms of deconvolution accuracy. Spatial visualization (Figures 5E–5H) further demonstrated that SpaDicer’s predictions are most consistent with the ground-truth regional annotations in terms of spatial structure, markedly surpassing the other three competing methods. Collectively, the unpaired cross-sample experiments indicate that, despite being trained on scRNA-seq data from a different patient, SpaDicer can accurately recover the correspondence between cell types and spatial regions, highlighting its strong cross-patient generalizability.

High-resolution spatial mapping of embryonic cardiomyocyte subtypes

To further demonstrate SpaDicer’s versatility in spatial reconstruction and cell-type deconvolution, we used the scRNA-seq and ST data of human embryonic heart samples generated by Asp et al.37 The human heart embryos at 4.5–5, 6.5, 6.5–7, and 9 post-conception weeks (PCWs)—staged by clinical ultrasound and anatomical landmarks—were harvested. Three dorsal-ventral sections (4.5–5, 6.5, and 9 PCWs) underwent ST, producing an aggregate dataset of 3,115 spots (∼ 30 cells per spot), while the 6.5–7 PCW section was profiled by scRNA-seq, yielding 3,777 cells. Based on 69 curated marker genes, we performed clustering to annotate major cardiac cell types. We focused on three cardiomyocyte subtypes—atrial, ventricular, and MYOZ2-enriched—whose transcriptional and spatial heterogeneity mirrors divergent developmental origins and specialized electrophysiological, contractile, and secretory functions.37 By mapping expression of subtype-specific genes (MYH7 for ventricular, MYH6 for atrial, and MYOZ2 for MYOZ2-enriched cells), we manually delineated their spatial distributions (Figure 6A): ventricular cells localized predominantly to the lower-left region, atrial cells to the upper-right, and MYOZ2-enriched cells centrally. In this experiment, ST data provided the spatial scaffold for reconstructing pseudo-spatial positions of scRNA-seq cells, while scRNA-seq-derived identities guided cell-type deconvolution of ST spots.

(C) Boxplots of standardized pairwise distances among three cardiomyocyte subtypes, illustrating that atrial cells are significantly more spatially separated from ventricular cells (left, p value = 3e−16, Wilcoxon rank-sum test) than to MYOZ2-enriched cells (right, p value = 5.9e−7, Wilcoxon rank-sum test).

Our experimental results demonstrate that SpaDicer’s reconstructed pseudo-space faithfully recapitulates the embryonic cardiac architecture and the spatial topology of distinct cardiomyocyte subpopulations. Specifically, atrial and ventricular cardiomyocytes form two clearly segregated clusters that reflect their native localization within the atria and ventricles, respectively. MYOZ2-enriched cardiomyocytes occupy an intermediate region between these clusters, precisely as observed in the empirical ST maps (Figures 6A and 6B). To rigorously quantify their spatial relationships, we computed normalized pairwise distances from each cardiomyocyte subtype to both ventricular and atrial clusters (Figure 6C). This analysis revealed that atrial cardiomyocytes reside significantly farther from the ventricular population than do MYOZ2-enriched cells, in full agreement with their actual localizations in real tissue.

Because the true cellular composition of each ST spot is unknown, we assessed deconvolution accuracy by comparing predicted cell-type proportions with assignments based on marker-gene expression. We benchmarked SpaDicer against three leading methods—Cell2location,20 Tangram,23 and GraphST38—and visualized results by coloring each spot according to its inferred fractions of atrial, ventricular, and MYOZ2-enriched cardiomyocytes (Figures 6D–6G). It can be seen that SpaDicer’s predictions closely matched the marker-based annotations (Figure 6D). In contrast, Cell2location returned almost uniform proportions across all spots, yielding minimal discriminative power between subtypes (Figure 6E). Tangram and GraphST captured the spatial gradients of the three cardiomyocyte populations but displayed substantial quantitative deviations from the ground truth (Figures 6F and 6G). Furthermore, we plotted kernel density estimates (KDEs) of inferred proportions of spots along the x, y, and diagonal axes (Figure S12), which confirmed that atrial and ventricular cardiomyocytes occupy distinct domains, with MYOZ2-enriched cells localized intermediately—fully consistent with empirical ST observations.

Discussion

In this study, we have introduced SpaDicer, the first unified deep-learning framework to jointly model pseudo-space reconstruction of single cells and cell-type deconvolution of ST spots. By leveraging cross-domain feature disentanglement and multi-task learning, SpaDicer not only surpasses existing pseudo-space reconstruction methods such as scSpace22 but also outperforms a broad array of dedicated deconvolution tools including Cell2location,20 SpatialDWLS,18 Tangram,23 SPOTlight,17 and others (Figures 2H and 5D–5G). Therefore, the primary contribution of our method lie in the integrative analysis of scRNA-seq and ST data for simultaneous pseudo-space reconstruction and cell-type deconvolution of ST data. The extensive experiments demonstrated that our method effectively leverages the complementarity between scRNA-seq and ST data to enhance overall model performance.

Compared to optimization-based alignment methods such as scSpace,22 Tangram,23 and deep-learning approaches like CytoSPACE,2 SpaDicer achieves higher spatial correlation on simulated data and consistent gains on real tissues, particularly along axes with stronger spatial cues. At the same time, SpaDicer’s deconvolution accuracy exceeds that of Bayesian frameworks (Cell2location20), weighted least-squares models (SpatialDWLS18), regression-based methods (SPOTlight17), and graph-based algorithms (GraphST38), while achieving comparable RMSE to the best performer (Cell2location20) without sacrificing classification AUC. This broad superiority underscores the benefit of learning a shared representation that captures both transcriptomic identity and spatial context, rather than treating the two domains independently.

A main contribution of SpaDicer lies in its ability to learn transferable cell-type representations from unpaired scRNA-seq data, thereby improving deconvolution performance in ST data even when matched reference samples are unavailable. Unlike methods that rely heavily on patient-specific pairing between scRNA-seq and ST data, SpaDicer captures intrinsic transcriptional signatures that characterize each cell type and aligns them with spatial expression patterns through its cross-modal modeling strategy. This enables the model to generalize across samples and patients, effectively recovering the correspondence between cell-type identities and spatial regions despite inter-sample heterogeneity. As demonstrated in our unpaired cross-patient experiments, SpaDicer maintains high deconvolution accuracy when trained on scRNA-seq data from a different individual, indicating that the learned representations encode robust biological features rather than dataset-specific patterns. So, we believe that SpaDicer provides a practical solution for real-world applications where paired datasets are scarce, while simultaneously enhancing ST spot deconvolution accuracy through improved cell-type feature transferability.

In conclusion, SpaDicer demonstrates that a unified, multi-task deep-learning paradigm can substantially elevate both pseudo-space mapping and cell-type deconvolution, offering a versatile tool for dissecting tissue architecture. As spatial profiling technologies move toward higher-throughput and three-dimensional resolution, and as richer metadata (e.g., epigenetic marks and protein stains) become available, the SpaDicer framework can be extended and adapted to integrate these modalities—paving the way for truly comprehensive, spatially informed single-cell atlas.

Limitations of the study

While SpaDicer demonstrates competitive performance across multiple benchmarks, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, pseudo-space reconstruction is mainly benchmarked against scSpace, which limits the strength of evidence for its reconstruction advantage. Second, an ST section from tumor tissue is treated as pseudo-scRNA-seq, and unsupervised clusters are used as cell-type labels. Although this design probes cross-section prediction, it departs from typical deconvolution settings that rely on bona fide scRNA-seq references and may partially capture regional or layer structure rather than true cell-type composition. Third, interpreting softmax outputs directly as spot-level cell-type proportions does not explicitly account for key ST generative factors (e.g., cell-type-specific RNA content, capture efficiency, gene-specific biases, and uncertainty in total cell numbers), potentially yielding biased or overly optimistic proportion estimates across tissues or platforms. Finally, the method assumes paired scRNA-seq and ST data from the same biological specimen, which constrains training-set scale and diversity and may reduce robustness when generalizing to unpaired or highly heterogeneous datasets.

Method details

Data source and preprocessing

All datasets analyzed in this study were sourced from publicly available repositories and widely chosen for spatial transcriptomics methods validation by virtue of their rigorous quality controls. Two simulated mouse cortex benchmarks—seqFISH+ (“dataset 4”) and STARmap (“dataset 10”)—were obtained from.15 Moreover, five independent real-world datasets were also incorporated: 1) human dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) profiled by 10x Visium and STARmap32; 2) cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (cSCC) Visium data33; and 3) HER2-positive breast cancer (BC-HER2+) Visium data34; 4) human embryonic heart scRNA-seq and matched 10x Visium spatial transcriptomics spanning 4.5–9 post-conception weeks37; 5) Human pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) scRNA-seq and ST data derived from two patients.35

For two simulated datasets, human embryonic heart and PDAC dataset, we first performed preprocessing of the scRNA-seq and ST data, including the removal of duplicated gene names and samples with missing gene expression values. We then applied the Seurat v329 highly variable gene selection method implemented in Scanpy (v1.11.1)40 to both scRNA-seq and ST data, selecting the top 2,000 highly variable genes (HVGs). The selected HVGs were subsequently normalized and log-transformed. Finally, to ensure consistent feature representations across modalities, we took the union of HVGs from the two modality datasets as the final input genes.

For the DLPFC, cSCC, and BC-HER2+ datasets, the top 8,000 HVGs were determined per section, and only genes present in all slices were retained. Count matrices were then normalized to 10,000 counts per cell or spot and subsequently log-transformed via log1p function. For downstream classification tasks, DLPFC spots were annotated based on histologically defined cortical layers, whereas carcinoma and breast cancer spots were clustered into spatial regions. Clustering was performed using principal component analysis (PCA) with 30 principal components, followed by K-means clustering.41

SpaDicer framework

We propose SpaDicer, a cross-domain feature disentanglement framework to infer cell types and spatial localization (Figure S13). This framework employs a domain separation network25 to extract shared features by a common encoder (Ec) and domain-specific features by two private encoders (Eps for scRNA-seq and Ept for ST). The orthogonal constraints are imposed to ensure the disentanglement between shared and private features, effectively isolating cell type and spatial localization information from confounding factors.

During training phase, the single-cell domain predictor (Ps) classifies cell types for individual cells, while the ST domain predictor (Pt) estimates spatial coordinates for spots. As such, the shared encoder Ec learns to capture common features encoding both cell type and spatial location information. In inference phase, the features extracted by Ec from the single-cell and ST domains are exchanged and input into the corresponding predictors. This enables our model to simultaneously infer pseudo-spatial coordinates for single cells and estimate the cell type composition of ST spots.

Training and inference

The model was trained for 200 epochs using an initial learning rate of 1×10−3, which decayed by a factor of 0.95 per epoch. A batch size of 128 was employed for the single-cell domain, while a batch size of 16 was used for the spatial transcriptomics domain. Once the training phase finished, the shared encoder Ec can extract both cell type and spatial localization information from expression profiles, so that the predictors Ps and Pt utilize the encoded feature to predict cell type composition and spatial localization, respectively.

During the inference phase, the features extracted by Ec from the single-cell and ST domains are exchanged and fed into corresponding predictor to predict pseudo-spatial coordinates and cell-type compositions, respectively. Specifically, the feature extracted from single-cell domain is fed into the predictor Pt to infer the pseudo-spatial coordinates of individual cells. Similarly, the feature extracted from ST domain is fed into the predictor Ps to estimate the cell type composition for each spot. The softmax scores output by Ps are regraded as the proportions of each cell type within a spot.

Clustering analysis

For the ST data of cancer tissue, we utilized the Scanpy package (version 1.9.1) to perform principal component analysis (PCA) on the filtered highly variable genes. The first 30 principal components were selected for downstream analysis. K-Means clustering was then applied to the selected principal components to perform clustering analysis, and category labels were assigned to each spot based on the clustering results.

Differential expression analysis

Differential expression analysis was conducted using the ‘rank_genes_groups’ function in the Scanpy package (version 1.9.1). This statistical analysis is performed based on t of he differences of gene expression levels between distinct cell types. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was employed to check statistical significance.

Benchmarking competing methods

For the pseudo-space reconstruction task, we compared SpaDicer’s performance with the pioneer method scSpace.22 The source code for scSpace was obtained from its GitHub repository (https://github.com/ZJUFanLab/scSpace). Performance evaluations were conducted on the same benchmark datasets, on which the training and testing was consistent with the protocol used for our method. The hyperparameters for scSpace were configured as follows: activation = ‘sigmoid’, learning rate = 0.001, and epoch_num = 200.

For the cell-type deconvolution task, we benchmarked our method against ten competing methods on the two simulated mouse cortex datasets, including Cell2location,20 DestVI,27 DSTG,28 RCTD,19 Seurat,29 Tangram,23 SPOTlight,17 Stereoscope,30 STRIDE31 and SpatialDWLS.18 The performance of these competing methods on these two simulated datasets was directly derived from the comprehensive bechmarking study reported by Li et al.15 For objective performance evaluation, our method was evaluated using the same protocol used in this bechmarking study.

Quantification and statistical analysis

It is worth noting that all methods were trained and tested on the same workstation. Moreover, the partition of training and testing sets is uniform across all methods, thereby enabling an objective performance comparison. The predictive performance of the pseudo-space reconstruction task is evaluated using multiple performance metrics, including Pearson Correlation Coefficient (PCC), Structural Similarity Index Measure(SSIM), and Root-Mean-Square Error (RMSE). For the cell type deconvolution task, the evaluation metrics include Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic Curve (AUROC), Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD), and Enrichment Ratio (ER). The Wilcoxon rank-sum test is employed to evaluate the statistical significance of differences between groups. A p-value of less than 0.05 is considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses and performance metrics are calculated using SciPy Python package.
