Healthcare accessibility and subjective well-being in Xizang: evidence from a high-altitude region of China
Authors: Chaofan Li, Xiaoyan Wu, Yongzhu Lacuo, Liyong Lu, Gang Chen, Zhaxi Dawa, Shunping Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveAchieving universal access to healthcare is a specific target of Sustainable Development Goal 3 which seeks to promote health and well-being for all. However, the subjective well-being (SWB) in Xizang and its relationship with healthcare accessibility are inadequately understood. This study aims to assess the magnitude of SWB and examine its relationship with healthcare accessibility in Xizang, China.MethodsData were obtained from the 2018 Sixth National Health Service Survey in Xizang. The SWB was measured using a revised version of the Personal Well-Being Index (PWI). Healthcare accessibility was assessed based on geographical accessibility, while affordability and availability indicators were included as control variables. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the basic characteristic of participants. After controlling for sex, age, marital status, occupation, education, household income per capita and chronic disease, multiple regression models were used to examine the relationship between SWB and geographical accessibility.ResultsThe PWI score among the 2113 participants in 2018 was 69.04 (SD = 14.97), and the overall life satisfaction score was 7.00 (SD = 2.04). After adjusting for control variables, participants with good geographical accessibility reported significantly higher SWB (β = 1.75, 95% CI: 0.05–3.47).ConclusionThe SWB in Xizang fell within the normative range of China, and better geographical accessibility to healthcare was associated with higher SWB. Future research should delve into potential mechanisms between access to healthcare and SWB.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-025-13597-6.

Introduction

Subjective well-being (SWB) is gaining increasing attention from both scholars and policy makers [1]. It is increasingly advocated as an important criterion for evaluating of public policy and healthcare programs [2, 3]. Goal 3 of Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) set by United Nations in 2015 calls all countries to take actions to “ensure healthy lives and promote well-being for all at all ages” [4]. This goal included 13 concrete targets, such as crucial target 3, “achieving universal health coverage (UHC), including financial risk protection and access to quality healthcare without financial risk for all.” Progress toward achieving the SDGs, as reported by De Neve, has made an enormous contribution to promoting human well-being [5]. Specially, health is a critical factor driving SWB globally and regionally [5–7].

However, regional disparities still pose a challenge to achieving UHC and improving SWB for all [8]. Moreover, recent research emphasizes the significance of measuring well-being at the regional level to guide policy decisions tailored to the specific needs of participants, highlighting important differences in well-being status across different regions [9, 10]. The Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) also advocates for regional well-being assessments to inform policy prioritization based on localized factors [11].

The Xizang, an autonomous region in southwest of China, is the largest and highest plateau region globally, with an average elevation higher than 4,000 m. The natural environment in Xizang is challenging, marked by low atmospheric oxygen pressure, harsh cold weather, and limited resource [12]. The harsh natural environment has historically constrained social and economic rapid development in Xizang, and impaired the participants’ health, such as a high prevalence of chronic mountain sickness and Kaschin-Beck disease [13]. According to satisfaction of goals theories, SWB is determined by the extent that populations’ needs, desires and goals are satisfied [14]. Participants at high altitude areas faced harsh nature experiments and had various basic needs, for example, shelter, food, health and education. The SWB of Xizang participants is tightly related with the extent of fulfillment of their basic needs. To evaluate the magnitude of SWB in Xizang and examine its determinants at the regional level will be of significant importance to identify the specific needs of participants and make tailored policy.

During past years, the Chinese government has invested significant funds to improve living conditions, transportation, education, especially health system to enhance the well-being of Xizang’s participants [15]. These investments have yielded improvements in population health and universal access to essential health services in Xizang. The life expectancy of participants in Xizang has risen from 35.5 years in 1951 to 72.2 years in 2021, while maternal and infant mortality rates have declined to 48 per 100,000 and 7.6 per 1,000, respectively [16]. The universal health coverage index, an indicator tracking progress toward UHC, has increased from 45 in 2003 to 65 in 2018, signifying advancements in healthcare access and financial protection [8]. Notably, the density of physicians, nurses, and ward beds reached 2.90, 2.13 and 5.37 per 1,000 in 2021, respectively. The household out-of-pocket payments (OOP) only accounted for 7.16% of total health expenditure in 2020, much lower than the national level (27.65%) [17].

Despite Xizang’s considerable achievements in improving health status and universal access to healthcare, limited research has been conducted on the status of SWB among Xizang participants. Existing studies on SWB in China often draw samples from provinces with larger populations, neglecting the unique characteristics of Xizang’s environment and population [9, 18–21]. Webb investigated the SWB among ethnic Zang with a small sample (n = 102) in Qinghai province, and Chen examined relationship between SWB and living arrangements among the elderly ethnic Zang in Gansu province [22, 23]. However, both two studies were not conducted in Xizang province.

This study aims to fill this gap by investigating the SWB status in Xizang, considering the region’s harsh environment and limited health resources. Moreover, it seeks to examine the relationship between SWB and healthcare accessibility, providing valuable insights into the intersection of health and well-being.

Methods

Data source

This cross-sectional data was obtained from the sixth wave of National Health Service Surveys (NHSS) conducted in 2018 in Xizang, China. The NHSS is a nationally representative survey conducted every five years since 1993 across mainland China. Its comprehensive questionnaire covers various domains, including household characteristics, demographic and socioeconomic status, health status, healthcare access and utilization, medical expenditure, and enrollment in health insurance schemes. The survey employs a multi-stage stratified random sampling method, ensuring representation at the national level. All members of the selected households are included in the questionnaire survey. Previous studies provide detailed information on the four sampling stages of the NHSS [24].

For this study, a subset of 2292 participants was randomly selected to complete the SWB questionnaire. After excluding 28 observations from individuals under 18 years old, 57 non-Xizang residents, and 94 cases with missing or erroneous values in key variables, the final sample included 2,113 participants. This rigorous selection process aimed to ensure data integrity and the inclusion of a representative sample for analysis.

Measurement of SWB

The assessment of SWB in this study employed the globally recognized and cross-culturally valid questionnaire known as the Personal Well-being Index (PWI). Developed by Cummins in 2001, the PWI has undergone translations into more than 20 languages, attesting to its widespread applicability [25]. Comprising eight items, the PWI encompasses one item gauging satisfaction with life, and seven items evaluating satisfaction across various domains of personal life. These domains include standard of living, health, achievements in life, personal relationships, personal safety, community connectedness, and future security. Participants provided responses on a Likert scale indicating the degree of satisfaction, ranging from 0 to 10 for each item. The PWI score, indicative of satisfaction with different aspects of personal life, was then standardized into the Percentage of Scale Maximum (%SM), with values ranging from 0 to 100 [26]. The reliability and validity of the Chinese-translated version of PWI, performed by Xing, have been established [27]. Furthermore, Webb’s study affirmed the reliability and validity of PWI specifically among the Xizang population [23].

It is noteworthy that, for the purpose of this survey, the item related to the personal safety was excluded based on two considerations: first, we posited that there was no discernible connection between healthcare accessibility and satisfaction with safety; second, prior research indicated a weak association between feelings of safety and SWB and satisfaction on life as a whole [23, 28]. As Shu hypothesized, there was potential relationship between healthcare access and satisfaction on overall life and domains of life, such as personal life, interpersonal life and public life [29]. Thus, the questionnaire for SWB in this survey incorporated one item assessing overall life satisfaction and six life domain items. Refer to Appendix Table 1 for a display of the PWI items, while the results section provides details on the reliability and validity assessments.

Accessibility to healthcare

Geographical accessibility was gauged by travel time and distance from the patient’s location to the nearest health facility, a widely acknowledged metric in healthcare accessibility research [30]. Participants were asked, “How long does it take from your home to the nearest health facility by the fastest way?” The answers were categorized into two groups: 0) >15 min and 1) ≤ 15 min.

According to previous studies, healthcare availability and affordability were also associated with well-being [31, 32]. Thus, we included healthcare availability and affordability as control variables. Availability of healthcare resources was measured using the density of physicians per 1,000 population at the city level, as reported in the Statistical Yearbook of Xizang in 2019 [33]. This metric aligns with established practices in assessing healthcare availability [34].

Healthcare affordability, a critical aspect of access, pertains to the ability to pay for necessary healthcare without encountering impoverishment or catastrophic spending [35, 36]. Affordability is influenced by both population characteristics and health system characteristics, such as health insurance coverage [37].

Control variables

To address potential confounding effects, control variables associated with both access to basic healthcare and SWB were incorporated into the model. Prior empirical studies and SWB reviews have identified demographics, education, marital status, occupation, economic status, and health status as influential factors for SWB [2, 14, 18, 27, 38–40]. Consequently, these factors were included as control variables. Demographic variables comprised sex and age (continuous variable in years). Education was categorized into four levels: illiterate, primary school, middle school, and high school. Marital status was classified into three categories: unmarried, married, and divorced, widowed, or other. Occupation included five groups: unemployed, employed, farmer, self-employed, and other. Economic status was measured by per capita household income (Chinese Yuan, RMB) and log-transformed for inclusion into regression model. Household annual income was obtained from the survey question: “What was the total income of your household in the previous year (2017) in Chinese Yuan? Please include wages, business income, property income, and transfer income from all sources, after deducting personal income tax, social security contributions, support payments, and interest expenses. Exclude income from the sale of assets, loans, inheritances, or one-time gifts.” Per capita household income was calculated by dividing the reported total household annual income by the number of household members, and then dividing by 1,000 to express the value in units of 1,000 RMB. Health status was assessed by the presence of a chronic disease rather than self-rated health status. Chronic disease was determined by the question, “Has any chronic disease been diagnosed by a physician in the past six months, such as hypertension, diabetes, or other chronic illnesses?” Participants’ answers were dichotomized into 0 for “No” and 1 for “Yes.”

Statistical analysis

Data were presented as frequency and percentage for categorical variables and mean with standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables. Differences in PWI scores across binary variables were examined using the T-test, and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was employed to assess differences in PWI scores across multiple categorical variables. Variance inflation factors (VIF) were utilized to detect multicollinearity among independent variables.

To account for correlations between observations from the same household, cluster-robust standard errors were applied. Multiple linear regression models were used to examine the association between SWB and geographical accessibility, adjusting for all control variables including affordability and availability.

Reliability of the PWI was evaluated using Cronbach’s α, with values ≥ 0.70 considered acceptable. Construct validity was examined by Pearson’s correlation coefficients between each domain item and the global life satisfaction item, and exploratory factor analysis (EFA) was conducted to assess factor loadings and total explained variance. Significance was defined as a two-sided P value < 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using Stata (version 15.0).

Results

Participants’ characteristics

Table 1 provides an overview of the basic characteristics of the 2113 participants. More than half were female (54.80%), with an average age of 45.96 ± 14.15 years. Illiterate individuals constituted the majority (57.03%), and over 80% were married. The majority were engaged in farming or grazing (60%), with a per capita household income of 17.17 ± 28.37 thousand yuan (RMB). Urban participants comprised 21.53%, while the majority (78.47%) resided in rural areas. Additionally, 55.37% of participants were diagnosed with chronic diseases. Basic health insurance covered 99.34% of participants, and supplementary health insurance covered 72.60%. In terms of healthcare accessibility, around 77% could reach the nearest health facility within 15 min.

Reliability and validity of PWI

Table 3 illustrates the reliability and validity of the PWI. The Cronbach’s α of PWI was 0.81, and the α values of the six items ranged from 0.75 to 0.80. For validity, the Pearson’s correlation coefficient between each item and “Satisfaction with life as a whole” was significantly positive at the 0.001 level, ranging from 0.31 to 0.73. The factor loading of each item ranged from 0.64 to 0.81, explaining 51.27% of the variance.

Scores of PWI

Descriptive statistics and univariate analysis of PWI scores are reported in Table 1. The PWI score for all sampled participants was 69.04 (SD = 14.97). Univariate analysis revealed no significant gender difference in PWI scores, while significant differences were observed across educational levels, marital status, occupation, and region of residence at the 0.05 level. Additionally, healthy participants had slightly higher PWI scores (71.68) than those diagnosed with chronic diseases (66.92), a significant difference at the 0.001 level. Participants within a 15-minute travel time to the nearest health facility reported higher PWI scores (69.65) than those with travel times exceeding 15 min (66.99). Similarly, participants with supplementary health insurance coverage reported higher PWI scores (69.48) than those without (67.88).

Association between accessibility to healthcare and PWI scores

Table 4 presents the results of the multiple regression model. After controlling for sex, age, education, marital status, occupation, income, chronic disease, basic health insurance, supplementary health insurance, physicians per 1,000 population and region of residence, healthcare accessibility was significantly positively associated with PWI scores. Participants who could reach a health facility within 15 min scored 1.75 points higher on PWI (95% CI: 0.05–3.47) than the reference group. Furthermore, the density of physicians per 1,000 population was also significantly positively associated with PWI scores (β = 2.19, 95% CI: 0.83–3.53). However, no significant relationship was found between PWI scores and either basic health insurance coverage (β = 1.92, 95% CI: -4.48–8.32) or supplementary health insurance (β = 0.02, 95% CI: -1.71–1.75). All variance inflation factors were less than 2.55.

Table 5 reports the association between health accessibility and six life domains of the PWI, after controlling for multiple covariates. Compared to the reference group, participants with good geographical accessibility reported higher satisfaction with standard of living (β = 0.36) and future security (β = 0.33), all significant at the 0.01 level. Participants enrolling in basic health insurance evaluated higher satisfaction with the standard of living (β = 1.65, P < 0.05) but lower satisfaction with personal relationships (β=-1.18, P < 0.001) compared to those without basic health insurance coverage. Participants with supplementary health insurance coverage reported higher satisfaction with community connectedness (β = 0.40, P < 0.01) than the reference group. Regarding the availability of healthcare, the density of physicians was significantly positively correlated with satisfaction with achievement (β = 0.25, P < 0.01), personal relationships (β = 0.41, P < 0.001), community connectedness (β = 0.22, P < 0.05), and future security (β = 0.32, P < 0.001).

Discussion

Summary of findings

To improve well-being among residents in Xizang’s high altitude region, this cross-sectional study assessed the magnitude of SWB using the PWI and examined its relationship with healthcare accessibility. The study found a high PWI score of 69.04 in 2018, positively correlated with geographical accessibility to healthcare. These results underscored the importance of healthcare accessibility as a significant contributor to well-being. The findings not only emphasized the need for monitoring healthcare accessibility and SWB in Xizang for progress toward SDGs but also provided valuable policy implications for other regions facing challenging environments and poor living conditions to enhance participants’ well-being.

Magnitude of SWB in Xizang

The observed PWI score among Xizang residents in 2018 was 69.04. However, the score was incomparable with other studies, because the safety domain was excluded. The overall life satisfaction in this study (7.00) was slightly higher than Webb’s finding (6.73) in 2009 23. Xizang residents also reported higher satisfaction on all domains of life than the urban citizens did [41]. The high level of life satisfaction in harsh circumstances could be attributed to improved living conditions, meeting basic physiological and health needs. The satisfaction of goals theory suggests that people’s satisfaction with life correlates with the fulfillment of basic needs and achievement of goals [14]. Despite the challenging natural environment, residents expressed satisfaction with improved living standards, education, work, economy, healthcare, social security, and the environment over the past decades [16]. Additionally, the influence of Buddhism on well-being was evident, with studies showing a positive association between Buddhist faith and SWB among Xizang residents [22, 23].

Healthcare accessibility and SWB

The study revealed that good geographical accessibility to healthcare significantly enhanced associated with higher SWB. This finding aligns with prior research demonstrating a positive relationship between healthcare infrastructure and SWB in China [9, 42]. Improved accessibility not only increased satisfaction with health but also had spillover effects on other domains of SWB, particularly standard of living and future security. These results underscore the importance of enhancing healthcare accessibility to meet the health needs of the population and promote overall well-being.

Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration. Firstly, this cross-sectional design impedes the observation of SWB trends over time or the inference of causal relationships between affordability for healthcare and SWB. Secondly, the NHSS questionnaire did not include religion, which is prevalent among Xizang residents and a potentially significant factor for SWB. Thirdly, the PWI questionnaire in this study excludes items of safety and the score of PWI is incomparable with other studies. Fourthly, the bidirectional relationship between health status and SWB poses challenges in this cross-sectional study to fully examine the complex mechanisms between healthcare access, health status, and SWB.

Conclusion

In summary, this study conducted a comprehensive analysis of SWB in Xizang, and examined its association with healthcare accessibility. Our findings indicate that the residents reported a high level of satisfaction on overall life and domain-specific satisfaction. Furthermore, the study demonstrated the pivotal role of favorable healthcare accessibility in fostering heightened overall SWB and influencing its diverse domains. Future research endeavors should adopt longitudinal study designs to unravel the potential mechanisms shaping the relationship between healthcare accessibility and SWB.
