Metabolic reprogramming in diffuse intrinsic pontine gliomas (DIPG): dual inhibition of mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation and lactate metabolism to enhance anti-tumor and radiosensitizing effects in DIPG cells
Authors: Han Shen, Quy-Susan Huynh, Faiqa Mudassar, Cecilia Chang, Brian Gloss, Prunella Ing, Shiyong Ma, Harriet Gee, Eric Hau, Kristina M Cook
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiffuse midline gliomas (DMGs), including diffuse intrinsic pontine gliomas (DIPGs), are universally fatal pediatric brain tumors with no effective treatments. DIPG tumors actively utilize mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS). Inhibition of Complex I (a core OXPHOS component) by phenformin radiosensitizes DIPG in vitro and in vivo. However, phenformin’s clinical application is limited by its risk of lactic acidosis. We investigated whether co-administration of the pyruvate-dehydrogenase-kinase (PDK) inhibitor dichloroacetate (DCA) can mitigate phenformin-induced acidosis while enhancing its anti-tumor activity.MethodsPatient-derived DIPG cells (SU-DIPG17, HSJD-DIPG007, SU-DIPG-VI) were treated with phenformin (0.625 mM), DCA (25 mM) or both. Mitochondrial and glycolytic flux (oxygen consumption rate (OCR) / extracellular acidification rate (ECAR)), ATP production, Reactive Oxygen Species (ROS), cell-cycle and apoptosis were quantified alongside RNA-seq and Liquid Chromatography-Tandem Mass Spectrometry (LC-MS)/MS metabolomics. Hypoxia was measured in neurospheres using fluorescence. Radiosensitization was assessed by γ-H2AX foci and clonogenic survival. In vivo, HSJD-DIPG007 orthotopic xenografts received phenformin (125 mg/kg/day) and DCA (250 mg/kg/day) by oral gavage for 4 weeks, alone or with focal brain-stem irradiation (2 Gy × 10); tumor immunohistochemistry and survival were recorded.ResultsDCA alone shifted glucose metabolism from glycolysis to oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS), reducing ECAR and intracellular lactate. When combined with phenformin, DCA significantly suppressed phenformin-induced glycolysis and ECAR while further reducing ATP. In vitro, this combination induced synergistic cell-cycle arrest, apoptosis, and ROS-induced DNA damage. Multi-omics integration revealed coordinated repression of glycolytic/ hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) programs and diversion of glucose into redox-supportive pentose-phosphate pathways. Hypoxic staining confirmed reduced hypoxia and HIF-1α in 3D neurospheroids. The combination produced additional in vitro radiosensitization, with the phenformin + DCA + radiation triple regimen achieving the greatest γ-H2AX persistence and clonogenic kill. In mice, the triple combination schedule incurred systemic toxicity, reflected from weight loss, and did not extend survival over 4 weeks treatment.ConclusionsDCA effectively counteracts phenformin-induced lactic acidosis in vitro yet heightens metabolic stress and radiosensitization in DIPG cells, providing proof-of-concept that a carefully chosen metabolic partner can boost tumor control. However, weight loss in vivo limited the length of treatment schedule and optimizing dose and schedule, or selecting safer mitochondrial inhibitors with PDK blockade, will be essential next steps before determining in vivo efficacy of this metabolic strategy.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40170-025-00411-4.Keywords: Metabolism, Mitochondria, Lactate, Oxidative phosphorylation, Diffuse intrinsic pontine gliomas, DIPG, Diffuse midline gliomas, DMG, Radiation, Phenformin, Dichloroacetate

Introduction

DMGs, including DIPGs, are lethal pediatric brain tumors driven by the H3K27M mutation. We use the term DIPG here to align with the naming of the cell lines studied. As the second most common malignant childhood brain tumor, DIPG responds only transiently to radiotherapy; nearly all recur within 12 months, and 90% of patients die within two years [1]. These dismal outcomes highlight the need for novel therapies that enhance radiotherapy or other potential treatments and improve survival.

One of the key drivers of radioresistance across all cancers is tumor hypoxia, which blunts radiation-induced DNA damage. Hypoxic cells require 2–3 times more radiation to achieve the same level of killing as well-oxygenated cells [2, 3]. While DIPGs have not been definitively determined to be hypoxic, clinical samples exhibit features consistent with hypoxia, including elevated citrate [4], which is associated with poor perfusion, and increased hypoxia-related transcriptional signatures [5]. An emerging therapeutic strategy to overcome hypoxia‐mediated resistance is to exploit the cancer cell’s reliance on mitochondrial OXPHOS. OXPHOS has a high demand for oxygen and without sufficient supply, can create localized hypoxia. We and others have shown that DIPG cells use high rates of both OXPHOS and glycolysis for ATP production in vitro, and that inhibiting OXPHOS alleviates neurosphere hypoxia and enhances radiotherapy efficacy both in vitro and in vivo [5, 6]. Similar OXPHOS-targeted approaches, with or without radiation, have shown preclinical benefit in other cancers [7–11].

Mitochondria are central hubs for energy generation, metabolic flux, and signaling. Therefore, disrupting their function offers multiple anticancer vulnerabilities. Cancers have been found to have mitochondrial variations when compared to their normal tissue counterpart, including alterations to mitochondrial number, size, and overall metabolic activity [12, 13], reduced mitochondrial DNA quantity [14, 15] and differential OXPHOS gene expression [5, 16]. For example, increased Complex I activity promotes kidney cancer metastasis [17], while chronic myeloid leukemia stem cells rely on OXPHOS for survival [18]. More specifically, in DIPG, tumors show decreased mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) [15], but paradoxically DIPG cell cultures exhibit increased mitochondrial metabolism [5]. Patient-derived DIPG tumor samples display elevated expression of nuclear-encoded OXPHOS-related genes, which correlates with a higher hypoxia score [5]. These observations suggest nuclear gene expression might compensate for mtDNA loss, producing a hyper-metabolic, oxygen-consuming phenotype that strengthens the case for therapeutically targeting OXPHOS.

Phenformin, a biguanide and complex I inhibitor, effectively reduces OXPHOS in DIPG cells, alleviates hypoxia in spheroid models, enhances radiosensitivity both in vitro and in vivo, and prolongs survival in DIPG mouse models [5]. However, its clinical utility is limited due to a risk of lactic acidosis, a well-documented rare but toxic side effect (one study estimated the incidence at approximately one case per 6,500 patient-years of therapy [19]), that led to its market withdrawal. Lactate is a direct consequence of the glycolytic increase that follows mitochondrial ATP depletion [20]. Similar effects have emerged with next-generation Complex I inhibitors such as IACS-010759, which produced neuro- and metabolic toxicities in early-phase trials despite encouraging pre-clinical activity [21, 22]. However, the safety profile of metformin (a less-potent biguanide) suggests that toxicity might be a function of the depth of respiratory chain inhibition, the dosing or the off-target effects rather than the OXPHOS target itself [23, 24].

Tumor metabolism offers several therapeutic targets beyond OXPHOS, and glycolysis is a key pathway that supports rapid cancer growth. We reasoned that inhibiting both glycolysis and OXPHOS could weaken DIPG cells, which may be less able to adapt metabolically than normal tissues. Lowering glycolytic activity should also reduce lactate production and therefore lessen the risk of phenformin-induced lactic acidosis. To redirect pyruvate away from lactate formation and toward mitochondrial oxidation, we employed DCA, an orally available inhibitor of PDKs. DCA reactivates the pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) complex, increases oxidative decarboxylation, and lowers blood lactate in several pre-clinical and clinical studies [25–27]. DCA has been used safely in patients with congenital mitochondrial disorders [28] and a very small number of case histories has shown that DCA may effectively lower lactate production induced by phenformin in patients [29]. We hypothesized that adding DCA to phenformin could curb systemic acidosis when it occurs while maintaining, or even enhancing, its anti-tumor and radiosensitizing effects in DIPG.

Here, we test this dual-metabolic targeting approach using metabolomics, bulk RNA-seq, extracellular flux, and radiobiological assays across a panel of patient-derived DIPG cultures and in an orthotopic xenograft model. We show that in vitro, DCA normalizes phenformin-driven lactate accumulation, reprograms central-carbon flux towards redox-supportive pathways, amplifies DNA-damage signaling and, increases radiosensitivity. Initial in vivo studies highlight the challenges of triple combination, combining two metabolic drugs with radiation, and underscore the need for dosing optimization or safer metabolic inhibitors before clinical translation.

Materials and methods

Cell cultures and chemicals

Patient-derived DIPG cell lines were grown as before [5] in Advanced DMEM/F12 (Gibco) mixed with Neurobasal-A medium (Gibco; 1:1) supplemented with 1x Glutamax, Non-Essential Amino Acids, HEPES, Sodium Pyruvate and Antibiotic-Antimycotic, B27 (1×), human FGF (20 ng/mL), human EGF (20 ng/mL), PDGF-AA (10 ng/mL), PDGF-BB (10 ng/mL) and Heparin (2 ng/mL). Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma. Cells were authenticated using short tandem repeat analysis. Sodium DCA and phenformin hydrochloride were purchased from Sigma. For all experiments referencing the phenformin and DCA combination where no concentration is specified, the concentrations are: 25 mM DCA and 0.625 mM phenformin.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation following drug treatment was evaluated using the MTS cell proliferation assay according to the supplier’s instructions (Promega). Cells were seeded in 96-well plates for 24 h, followed by adding indicated drug treatments for 72 h. Seeding densities: HSJD-DIPG007 and SU-DIPG17: 3,000 cells/well; SU-DIPG-VI, HSJD-DIPG012, and HSJD-DIPG013: 7,000 cells/well. After the addition of the MTS reagent, absorbance was measured using a 560-nm excitation/590-nm emission with a SpectraMax microplate reader (Molecular Devices). Medium only wells were used as blanks. Combination index (CI) was calculated from CI equation algorithm using CompuSyn software. CI = 1, < 1, and > 1 indicates additive effect, synergism and antagonism, respectively.

Colony formation assay

For the 2D liquid colony forming assay, SU-DIPG17 cells were seeded in triplicate into 6-well plates at a density of 250 cells/well and allowed to adhere overnight. Cells were then exposed to drug treatment for 24 h. Radiation (2–6 Gy, single fraction) was delivered using an X-RAD 320 irradiator, 18 h after initiation of drug exposure. Following treatment, cells were incubated for 2–4 weeks at 37 °C with 5% CO₂, with media replenished every 3 days. Colonies were fixed and stained with 0.5% crystal violet in 25% (v/v) methanol, and visible colonies were counted. For the 3D soft agar colony forming assay, HSJD-DIPG007 cells were seeded into 6-well plates and cultured for 3 days to allow neurosphere formation, followed by drug treatment and irradiation under the same schedule as described for SU-DIPG17. Soft agar assays were performed by first preparing a bottom layer of 0.3 mL complete medium containing 0.5% agar in 24-well flat-bottom plates. A top layer consisting of 1,000 cells suspended in 0.33% agar in 0.3 mL complete medium was then added. Sterile water was placed in the peripheral wells to minimize evaporation. Colonies were grown for 2–4 weeks, then incubated with Thiazolyl Blue Tetrazolium Bromide (MTT, Sigma; 5 mg/mL) solution for 1 h prior to imaging. Colonies were counted, and numbers recorded. For both assays, the surviving fraction was calculated as the ratio of colonies formed in irradiated wells to those in untreated controls. Plating efficiency was defined as the number of colonies formed divided by the number of cells seeded, normalized to the average plating efficiency of untreated samples. For combined drug and radiation treatments, surviving fractions were normalized to drug-only controls. Survival curves were generated by fitting data to a linear-quadratic model (Y = exp−[A*X+B*X2]) using GraphPad Prism with a least-squares (ordinary) fit. All conditions were tested in technical triplicates, and each experiment was independently repeated at least twice.

Western blotting

For γ-H2AX detection, cells were seeded in 6-well plates at 3 × 105 cells per well, incubated for 24 h, treated with drug for 18 h, then followed by a single 10 Gy dose of radiation delivered using the X-RAD 320. Cells were harvested 30 min post-irradiation. For cleaved caspase-3 analysis, cells were treated with drug for 18 h, exposed to 10 Gy irradiation, and collected after a total of 24 h of drug treatment. Following the indicated treatments, cells were lysed in RIPA buffer (150 mM NaCl, 1% Triton X-100, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% SDS, 50 mM Tris, pH 8.0) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Pierce). Lysates were sonicated, clarified by centrifugation (14,000 rpm, 5 min, 4 °C), and protein concentrations were determined using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (Pierce). Equal amounts of protein (30 µg per sample) were resolved on Bolt 4–12% Bis-Tris gels (Thermo Fisher Scientific) under reducing conditions using the Mini Gel Tank system, and subsequently transferred to membranes using either the iBlot 2 Dry Transfer System (Thermo Fisher Scientific, #NW0412AIB2) or the Bio-Rad Trans-Blot Semi-Dry Transfer Cell. Proteins of interest were detected using the following primary antibodies purchased from Cell Signaling Technology: cleaved Poly (ADP-ribose) polymerase (PARP), cleaved Caspase-3 (#9664S), p-AMPK (#2531S), phospho-γ-H2AX (#9718S), MCL-1 (#4572S), p-BRCA1 (#9009), p-CHK2 (#2197), HIF-1a (Abcam, #ab51608), HK2 (#2867S), PDK-1 (aka PDHK1, #3820S), LDHA (#3582S), c-MYC, GAPDH #2118S), a-tubulin (#2148S), b-actin (#4967S).

Cell cycle analysis

3 × 105 cells per well were seeded in 6-well plates followed by drug treatments as indicated. After treatments, cells were harvested and fixed in cold 70% v/v ethanol for at least 2 h. Fixed cells were washed with PBS and stained in the dark with a solution containing propidium iodide (10 µg/mL), Triton X-100 (0.1%) and RNAse (100 µg/mL) for 20 min at room temperature. DNA content was analyzed using a BD FACSCantoTM II flow cytometer. Forward scatter and side scatter were measured to identify single cells. Pulse processing was used to exclude cell doublets from the analysis. At least 10,000 events were acquired per sample. Flow cytometry data were analyzed using FlowJo.

Extracellular flux assay

The OCR and ECAR of DIPG cells were determined using the XF Extracellular Flux Analyzer (Agilent Technologies). Plates were coated with laminin (Sigma) to allow cells to attach. DIPG cells were seeded at a density of 50,000 cells per well and allowed to attach overnight in regular culture media. On the following assay day, cells were washed twice with fresh XF assay media containing 10 mM glucose, 1 mM pyruvate and 2 mM L-glutamine. Setup for assay was according to manufacturer’s instructions. The cartridge was loaded to dispense drugs (in Port A) and metabolic inhibitors (in Port B and C) from XF Real-Time ATP rate assay kits. The final concentrations of metabolic inhibitors are: 1.5 µM Oligomycin in Port B and 0.5 µM Rotenone + Antimycin A in Port C.

ROS production assays

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates, followed by drug treatments as indicated for 1 h (mitochondrial ROS) or 3 h (cytosolic ROS). MitoSOX Red (Invitrogen) and dihydroethidium (DHE) (Invitrogen) were used to measure the level of mitochondrial and cytosol ROS production respectively according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After staining, single cell suspensions were prepared, washed with PBS (for DHE) or Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) (for MitoSOX Red) and resuspended in 0.5 mL of the same buffer. Sytox Blue (Invitrogen; 1 µM) was added to counter-stain for non-viable cells. DHE and MitoSOX Red fluorescence were analyzed using flow cytometer and data analysis was performed using FlowJo.

Apoptosis assay

Apoptosis was quantified by Annexin-V-FLUOS Staining Kit (Roche) according to the manufacturer’s instructions using flow cytometry. 3 × 105 cells per well were seeded in the 6-well plates for 24 h followed by adding drugs for 24 h treatment. Cells were stained with a solution containing both Annexin-V and PI for 30 min, followed by flow cytometry analysis. As a second confirmation of apoptosis, Mcl-1, cleaved PARP and caspase 3 were detected by western blotting.

Clinical transcriptomics datasets

To analyze mRNA expression of specific genes related to the protein targets of DCA, we used the pediatric cBioportal tool [30, 31] to download mRNA expression (similar to [5]), from the Open Pediatric Cancer (OpenPedCan) Project v15 as follows: for non-tumor samples there were 246 caudate (BG: basal ganglia, GTEx), and 255 brain cortex (GTEx) samples. For tumor samples there were: 10 brainstem glioma DIPGs and 220 DMG-H3K27M samples. For cell lines we downloaded RNA data from the Childhood Cancer Model Atlas (CCMA) database [32].

RNA sequencing and bioinformatics

Cells were plated in 6-well plates with 3 replicates each at a seeding density of 3 × 105 cells per well in 2 mL. 24 hafter seeding, cells were treated with drugs as described in figures. Cells were collected 24 h post-treatment and RNA was extracted (RNeasy mini handbook Qiagen). High-quality total RNA (RIN > 8) was used for library preparation with the Illumina TruSeq Stranded mRNA Library Preparation Kit. Indexed libraries were sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using an SP flow cell, generating single-end 100 bp reads. Sequencing quality was assessed with FastQC (Babraham Bioinformatics). Illumina adapter sequences and low-quality reads (removed if shorter than 20 base pairs) were trimmed using Trim Galore (Babraham Bioinformatics: www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/). Reads were aligned to the human genome (hg38) using STAR, with ENSEMBL gene annotations serving as a reference. Gene-level read counts were obtained with HTSeq. All analyses were conducted in the R Statistical Environment using the tidyverse package. Read counts were background-corrected and normalized for library size with edgeR, and differential gene expression (DGE) was determined using the threshold of ≥ 2-fold change with a false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05. Reactome pathway annotations (adjusted p < 0.05) were assigned using the clusterProfiler package.

PCR microarray

Cells were plated in 6-well plates with 3 replicates each at a seeding density of 3 × 105 cells per well. After 24 h incubation, cells were treated with vehicle control or drugs for 24 h, followed by cell pellet collection and RNA extraction (RNeasy mini handbook, Qiagen). RNA transcripts were reverse-transcribed using the RT2 first strand kit manual (Qiagen #330404). Following cDNA synthesis, the template was mixed with the RT2 SYBR Green qPCR Mastermix (Qiagen #330501). Equal volumes of sample were aliquoted to each well of the 384 well plate format provided by the RT² Profiler PCR Array Human Hypoxia Signaling Pathway Plus (Qiagen # PAHS-032Y) and RT² Profiler PCR Array Human Glucose Metabolism (Qiagen # PAHS-006Z) followed by running the real-time PCR cycling program according to the manual provided.

Metabolomics

Method conducted similar to [5]. Five replicate dishes were seeded at 2 × 106 cells per 100 mm² well. The next day, drugs were added and metabolites were harvested 24 h later. For extraction, SU-DIPG17 monolayers were removed from the incubator, briefly rinsed with ice-cold PBS, and extracted immediately. HSJD-DIPG007 neurospheres were removed from the incubator, briefly centrifuged, rinsed with ice-cold PBS, centrifuged again, and extracted immediately. For normalization, one of the five dishes per group was used solely for cell counting and then discarded. The remaining four replicate dishes per group were extracted with pre-chilled extraction buffer (1:1, v/v, Optima® LC/MS Methanol [Fisher Chemical]: Optima® LC/MS Water [Fisher Chemical]). Subsequently, 1 mL chloroform was added, the samples were briefly vortexed, and incubated on ice for 10 min. After centrifugation, the aqueous phase was transferred to fresh microfuge tubes. Samples were dried, and pellets rehydrated with water and an acetonitrile mixture for targeted metabolomics profiling on a TSQ Altis™ Triple Quadrupole MS (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Three-dimensional neurospheroid assay

Cells were plated in U-bottom plates and cultured until they reached the desired spheroid size (approximately ≥ 250 μm). Neurospheroids were then treated with drugs for 24 h before being stained for hypoxia using the Image-iT™ Green Hypoxia Reagent (5 mM). Nuclei were counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (1 mg/mL). Imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (Olympus FV 1000) with a 405 nm filter for Hoechst and a 488 nm filter for the Image-iT™ Green Hypoxia Reagent.

Confocal microscopy

Cover glass chambers were pre-coated with 200 µL of 1% laminin for at least 3 h. On day 1, chambers were washed with sterile PBS, air-dried, and seeded with 50,000 DIPG cells in 500 µL of complete medium, followed by overnight incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO₂. On day 2, freshly prepared drugs were added to the respective chambers in a final volume of 500 µL. After 18 h of drug treatment, cells were irradiated with a single 10 Gy fraction using the X-RAD 320. Thirty min post-irradiation, cells were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min, washed once with PBS, and blocked with 200 µL of 10% donkey serum in PBS for 1 h. Cells were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibody against phospho-Histone H2A.X (Ser139) (Rabbit mAb, Cell Signaling Technology). On day 3, cells were washed with PBS and incubated for 1 h at 4 °C with secondary antibody (goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488, 1:300 in 2% BSA). After three PBS washes (500 µL each), cells were stained with DAPI (1:1000) for 15 min, followed by a final PBS wash. ProLong Diamond antifade mountant (Thermo Fisher Scientific) was added prior to imaging. Confocal images were acquired using an Olympus FV1000 microscope.

Tumor engraftment in mice and in vivo efficacy test

Orthotopic DIPG models were established in 8-week-old female athymic nude mice (Balb/c) under a protocol approved by the Northern Sydney Local Health District Animal Ethics Committee (RESP19/034). Mice received stereotactic injections of 2 × 105 HSJD-DIPG007 cells into the 4th ventricle/pons (coordinates: 6 mm posterior, 0.5 mm lateral, 3.5 mm below bregma). Animals were randomized into four groups: vehicle control, radiotherapy (RT) alone, DCA + phenformin, and RT + DCA + phenformin. DCA (250 mg/kg/day) and phenformin (125 mg/kg/day) were administered by oral gavage on a 5-days-on/2-days-off schedule for 4 weeks; controls received distilled water. RT (20 Gy total; 2 Gy/fraction) was delivered to the brainstem using a small animal radiation research platform (SARRP) from week 2, concurrently with drug treatment for 2 weeks. One mouse per group was sacrificed after 4 weeks of treatment for immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining of Ki-67 and H3K27M; remaining mice were euthanized upon neurological symptoms or ≥ 20% weight loss. Survival was recorded for Kaplan–Meier analysis, and brains collected for H3K27M IHC.

IHC

DIPG tumor cells and their proliferation were estimated after H&E staining and by immunostaining for anti-Histone H3 (mutated K27M) (abcam #ab190631) and anti-Ki67 antibodies (abcam #ab16667). Entire mouse brains with tumors were isolated and were fixed in formalin, processed and embedded in paraffin and 4 μm sections were cut and mounted on Superfrost Plus slides. The following procedure was performed on the Leica Bond RX Platform. Sections were deparaffinized in Bond Dewax Solution (Leica), and Heat-Induced Epitope Retrieval (HIER) was performed for 30 min with ER2 (EDTA pH9) at 100 °C. Sections were stained using the Leica BOND Polymer Refine Detection System, 5 min peroxidase block, 1 h primary antibody incubation (H3K27M 1:1000 and Ki67 1:500), 15 min Polymer (Anti-rabbit Poly-HRP IgG) followed by 10 min DAB. Slides were counterstained with Hematoxylin (Shandon Instant Hematoxylin Kit) and cover slipped. The H3K27M and Ki67 positive staining of tumor cells were quantified by calculating the percentage of positive staining area per field in 3 “hot-spots” 40× magnification fields per slide/mouse. For the mouse brains that were collected 4 weeks after treatment, one mouse from each arm was quantified. For the mouse brains that were collected from the endpoint, three mice from each arm were quantified.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism. Data are presented as the mean ± SD and all experiments were repeated at least twice in triplicate. Direct comparison between two groups was examined using an unpaired two-tailed student t test. ANOVA was conducted for multigroup comparisons followed by a post-hoc Dunnett’s test (groups compared with one control group) or post-hoc Tukey’s test (to identify differences among subgroups) where appropriate. P values < 0.05 (two-sided) were considered statistically significant.

Results

DCA decreases the percentage of ATP from glycolysis and increases ATP from oxidative metabolism while reducing ECAR and increasing OCR in DIPG cells

We initially selected the SU-DIPG17 cell line to test DCA, as these cells are very metabolically active and use high rates of both glycolysis and mitochondrial metabolism to produce ATP and support their growth [5]. HSJD-DIPG007 cells were selected as a secondary cell line based on overall high metabolic activity, with high rates of glycolysis and moderately high mitochondrial activity [5]. Conversely, SU-DIPG-VI was used as a third comparison as this cell line has relatively high rates of mitochondrial metabolism with moderate to low basal glycolysis [5]. This enabled us to test the metabolic response in a range of DIPG metabolic phenotypes. Consistent with its known mechanism of action, DCA shifted glucose metabolism from anerobic glycolysis towards OXPHOS in SU-DIPG17 (Fig. 1A), HSJD-DIPG007 (Fig. 1B), and SU-DIPG-VI (Fig. 1C) as measured by the increase in the OCR (a measure of mitochondrial metabolism) and decrease in the ECAR (a measure of lactate production). DCA increased the percentage of total ATP coming from OXPHOS and decreased the percentage of ATP stemming from glycolysis in all three cell lines (Fig. 1D), which led to an increase in the ATP Rate Index (Fig. 1E).

PDK and PDH genes are expressed across DIPG cell lines and patient tumors

DCA inhibits PDKs and one preprint found that DCA is most effective when PDK2 is abundant and the poorly-inhibited isoform PDK3 is low [33], so we next profiled expression of the four PDK isoforms and PDHA1 and PDHB, which encode the catalytic α- and β-subunits of the PDH complex in our three DIPG lines to determine basal expression of these genes and determine if expression impacted response to DCA. We compared the DIPG cell lines to neuro stem cells (NSCs) using the Childhood Cancer Model Atlas (CCMA) database [32]. We also examined publicly available patient datasets available through OpenPedCan v15 [30, 31] to identify expression levels in tumors. Row-wise z-scores of cell line expression revealed PDK2 expression across NSCs, HSJD-DIPG007 and SU-DIPG-VI, with lowest expression in SU-DIPG17, whereas PDK3 was comparatively low in SU-DIPG-VI but moderately elevated in SU-DIPG17 (Fig. 1F). PDHA1 and PDHB were expressed at levels similar to, or higher than, NSCs, indicating that the PDH complex is not limiting. Principal-component analysis separated the cell lines along PC1 (77.5% variance), driven largely by differential PDK3/PDK4 loading, with NSCs clustering distantly (Fig. 1G). Thus, SU-DIPG-VI, characterized by a favorable high-PDK2/low-PDK3 profile, theoretically sits at the “sensitive” end of the metabolic spectrum. However, extracellular flux data (Fig. 1A-E), highlights that despite differences in target expression, all cell lines responded to DCA treatment.

Across the pediatric DIPG/DMG cohort every gene member of the PDH–PDK axis was expressed (Fig. 1H). PDHA1 and PDHB, were present at levels similar to, or slightly higher than, those seen in caudate and hypothalamic controls, indicating that the core enzymatic target of PDK is transcriptionally available in patient tumors. The four PDK isoforms showed broader, partially overlapping distributions. Median expressions of PDK2 and PDK4 trended higher than control tissue, whereas the median for PDK1 and PDK3 was higher in tumors with a wide range of expressions depending on the tumor. Taken together, the transcriptional landscape of PDH and PDK in our cell lines and patient tumors satisfies the required target profile [33], with sufficient PDH and PDK2 present (Fig. 1L). This molecular context explains the shift from glycolysis to OXPHOS that we observed in Extracellular Flux assays (Fig. 1A-E) and predicts that most DIPG/DMGs should remain susceptible to DCA treatment.

The phenformin and DCA combination reduces OCR in DIPG cells without inducing an increase in ECAR or intracellular lactate

In contrast to DCA, phenformin alone causes a large decrease in OCR in all cell lines (Fig. 1I and K). The ECAR response to phenformin varied across cell lines. Susceptibility to phenformin-associated lactic acidosis varies markedly between patients for reasons that remain poorly defined; although robust epidemiological data are scarce, an early survey estimated the incidence at roughly one case per 6,500 patient-years of therapy [19]. Only SU-DIPG-VI increased ECAR significantly in response to phenformin, while the other two cell lines maintained similar ECAR. This may be due to SU-DIPG17 and HSJD-DIPG007 already using a larger proportion of glycolysis in basal conditions to produce total ATP than SU-DIPG-VI (Fig. 1D and [5]). When phenformin was combined with DCA, ECAR decreased in SU-DIPG17 cells compared to both control and phenformin alone (Fig. 1I). In contrast, ECAR remained unchanged across control, phenformin, and combination treatment groups in HSJD-DIPG007 cells (Fig. 1J). In SU-DIPG-VI cells (Fig. 1K), ECAR in the combination group was similar to control and significantly lower than in the phenformin-alone group. These differences likely reflect variations in the basal metabolic profiles of the cell lines, including their relative rates of glycolytic versus mitochondrial ATP production [5]. Importantly though, the combination treatment significantly reduced OCR compared to control or DCA alone in all three cell lines (Fig. 1I and K).

To directly quantify intracellular lactate following treatment with phenformin, DCA or the combination, we used LC-MS/MS-targeted metabolomics in SU-DIPG17 and HSJD-DIPG007 cells. Cells were harvested at 24 and 48 h post-treatment to assess lactate levels. As expected, phenformin significantly increased intracellular lactate at both time points in both cell lines (Fig. 1M and N). In contrast, DCA treatment alone did not lead to any increase in lactate. When cells were treated with the phenformin-DCA combination, intracellular lactate levels were reduced compared to phenformin alone at both time points in both cell lines, indicating that DCA reduces phenformin-induced lactate accumulation in DIPG cells. There were some differences observed between ECAR and intracellular lactate, and the modest ECAR changes seen with phenformin in SU-DIPG17 and HSJD-DIPG007 likely reflect that ECAR reports net extracellular proton flux from multiple sources, whereas LC–MS/MS specifically quantifies intracellular lactate. Suppression of mitochondrial CO₂ production and/or altered lactate efflux may minimize a rise in ECAR despite a clear rise in intracellular lactate.

The combination of phenformin-DCA reduces ATP, metabolic homeostasis and proliferation in DIPG cells in vitro

Having established that DCA, when given alone to DIPG cells, inhibits production of lactate by enhancing flux through OXPHOS, and when combined, reduces phenformin-induced lactate intracellularly in vitro, we next asked whether this metabolic combination drug strategy translates into broader bioenergetic stress in tumor cells and if it reduces viability of DIPG cells. We hypothesized that the addition of DCA might enhance phenformin efficacy by simultaneously reducing lactic acidosis and exacerbating tumor metabolic stress. We therefore initially assessed the impact of phenformin and DCA, alone and in combination, on glycolytic and mitochondrial ATP production rates in vitro.

Immediately following treatment, the combination of phenformin and DCA caused the largest reduction in total ATP production compared to control or single-agent (SU-DIPG17 in Fig. 2A, HSJD-DIPG007 in Fig. 2B). Extending the treatment to 24 h, prior to running the Extracellular Flux assay, further decreased ATP production rates in both cell lines (Fig. 2A and B), consistent with a sustained disruption of metabolic homeostasis. The prolonged treatment reduced all apparent mitochondrial ATP production in phenformin only and combination groups. AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) is a key sensor of cellular energy depletion, and it responds to low intracellular ATP. AMPK is known to be activated by phenformin in DIPG cells, increasing phospho-AMPK [5]. Therefore, we assessed AMPK phosphorylation, total AMPK and cell viability in our three DIPG cell lines under combined treatment. We found that phospho-AMPK substantially increased in HSJD-DIPG-007 and SU-DIPG17 under combination treatment but there was little change in SU-DIPG-VI, which had higher basal phospho-AMPK than the other two cell lines (Fig. 2C). To study this further, we conducted a time point analysis of SU-DIPG-VI cells treated with control, single drug or combination at 24 h and 48 h (Fig. 2E) and compared to the response in SU-DIPG17 (Fig. 2D). At 24 h post-treatment, the control SU-DIPG-VI demonstrated substantial phospho-AMPK and there was no difference between control and any of the single drug or combination, indicating AMPK activation even in basal conditions. However, at 48 h, at least compared to 24 h, there was no phospho-AMPK for control, DCA alone or phenformin alone but there was phospho-AMPK in the combination group (Fig. 2E), indicating a robust energy stress response was sustained at 48 h.

To assess whether drug-induced energetic stress translated into reduced viability, we performed an MTS assay 24 h after treating multiple DIPG cell lines with phenformin, DCA, or their combination. Consistent with the observed metabolic stress, the combination of phenformin and DCA significantly reduced the proliferation of DIPG cell lines in a dose-dependent manner across multiple DIPG cell lines and more potently than either drug alone (Fig. 2G). Synergism between the two drugs was also confirmed by the CI (Fig. 2F). This suggests that DCA intensifies phenformin-induced energetic vulnerabilities, leading to cell death in DIPG cells.

Phenformin and DCA combination reduces hypoxia, HIF-1α, and downstream hypoxia markers in DIPG neurospheres

Because our previous work showed that phenformin alone decreases hypoxia in neurospheres [5], we next tested if the phenformin and DCA combination would exert similar effects. Cell lines HSJD-DIPG007 and SU-DIPG-VI grow as neurospheres, forming hypoxic cores in vitro [34], while SU-DIPG17 grows as a monolayer and therefore grows in a more normoxic environment, enabling us to compare the effects of different levels of oxygen on treatment response. Initially, HSJD-DIPG007 and SU-DIPG-VI neurospheres were treated with vehicle control, single agents or combination. Hypoxia was measured using a fluorescent dye (green) which reacts below 5% oxygen (v/v). The combination treatment significantly reduced fluorescence intensity, indicating a reduction in hypoxia (Fig. 3A and B).

To assess the impact on hypoxia signaling, we examined levels of HIF-1α and downstream transcriptional activity. In HSJD-DIPG007 hypoxic neurospheres, the combination treatment reduced HIF-1α protein levels (Fig. 3C). We observed a similar prominent reduction of HIF-1α in SU-DIPG-VI neurospheres (Fig. 3D). We wondered if the combination treatment would have similar effects in monolayer cell cultures which do not possess severely hypoxic cores. Despite growing in higher oxygen conditions, SU-DIPG17 monolayer cells display substantial HIF-1α protein (Fig. 3E), possibly achieved through overexpression or other genetic alterations that can increase HIF-1α in cancer cells. When monolayer SU-DIPG17 cells are grown in a hypoxic atmosphere, HIF-1α further increases beyond basal normoxic levels (Fig. 3E). DCA and phenformin decrease HIF-1α to varying degrees depending on whether cells are grown in normoxia or hypoxia. The combination treatment of phenformin and DCA effectively reduced HIF-1α expression under both monolayer and neurosphere conditions.

To determine whether the observed reductions in HIF-1α protein altered gene expression of hypoxia pathways and known HIF targets including glycolytic enzymes, we employed RT² Profiler PCR Arrays in HSJD-DIPG007 cells after phenformin-DCA combination treatment. The arrays capture changes in two interrelated pathways central to the known mechanisms of phenformin and DCA: HIF-mediated hypoxic adaptation and glucose metabolism. Following 48 h of phenformin-DCA combination treatment, our analysis revealed several upregulated genes (≥ 4-fold cutoff) within glucose metabolism including those involved in gluconeogenesis (PCK2), the pentose-phosphate pathway (G6PD), and ATP synthesis in glycolysis (PKLR) (Fig. 3F). Conversely, most downregulated genes (≥ 4-fold cutoff) span core glycolysis genes (HK2, TPI1, ENO1, PGK1/2, PGM2), pyruvate gateway control (PDHA1, DLAT plus inhibitors PDK1/3), multiple steps of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle (IDH3B, FH, SUCLG2), and glycogen / gluconeogenic turnover (GYS1, AGL, PHKG2, G6PC/3), while the PRPS1/2 genes curtail ribose-phosphate supply for nucleotide synthesis (Fig. 3F). The gene-expression data show a coordinated downregulation of glucose-fueled central carbon metabolism and anaplerosis. These transcriptional changes mirror the phenformin–DCA inhibition of glycolysis and mitochondrial respiration, via direct enzymatic effects and downstream transcriptional suppression.

In the hypoxia signaling array, the most strongly upregulated gene was HMOX1, indicative of oxidative stress (Fig. 3G). Other upregulated (≥ 4-fold) genes further reinforce vascular remodeling and redox/stress-adaptation programs (PGF, IER3, PIM1, ANGPTL4, NDRG1, VEGFA, GBE1, SERPINE1, BTG1). Several canonical HIF target genes that drive glycolysis and pH control (CA9, PGAM1, PGK1, LDHA, TPI1) were markedly repressed, which is consistent with the decrease in HIF-1α seen in Fig. 3C. Genes involved in IGF signaling (IGFBP3), coagulation (F3) and DNA-maintenance/stress responses (BLM, MAP3K1, RUVBL2, APEX1) were also decreased. Classic HIF-responsive genes are highlighted in purple in Fig. 3F and G; while some are induced, the majority of those that decline belong to glycolytic and acid-base regulation. We found decreased LDHA protein (Fig. 3C) when HSJD-DIPG007 neurospheres or SU-DIPG-VI neurospheres (Fig. 3D) were treated with phenformin and DCA, confirming a decrease reflected at both mRNA and protein levels.

The combined phenformin and DCA treatment alters metabolism directly and indirectly in DIPG cells

Phenformin and DCA individually influence cellular metabolism. Phenformin inhibits OXPHOS, which can lead to a compensatory increase in glycolysis, while DCA inhibits glycolysis which may promote alternative metabolism pathways to increase OXPHOS. We observed coordinated transcriptional and protein-level changes in enzymes regulating glucose metabolism, including key targets of HIF-1α, suggesting that the combination treatment not only disrupts metabolic flux but also alters upstream hypoxia-driven gene expression programs (Fig. 3F and G). To determine whether these enzyme-level changes translated into measurable alterations in metabolic output, we performed targeted metabolomics using LC-MS/MS on HSJD-DIPG007 cells treated with the phenformin-DCA combination. After 24 h of combination treatment, significantly altered metabolite levels shown in the volcano plot (Fig. 3H) and heatmap (Fig. 3I) reveal broad metabolic reprogramming. Several of the upregulated metabolites had corresponding increases in genes involved in their biosynthesis, suggesting active metabolic reprogramming.

Transcriptomic changes explain some of the observed metabolite shifts. Repression of key glycolytic and TCA-cycle enzymes (HK2, ENO1, PGK1/2, PDHA1, DLAT, IDH3B, FH, SUCLG2) (Fig. 3F and G) is mirrored by depletion of their immediate products, including 3-phosphoglycerate, phosphoenol-pyruvate, citrate, succinate and succinyl-CoA (Fig. 3H and I), indicating a bottleneck in glucose-fueled anaplerosis. Similarly, transcriptional induction of PCK2 and G6PD coincides with accumulation of glucose-6-phosphate, erythrose-4-phosphate and NADPH, pointing to a shift of carbon into gluconeogenic/pentose-phosphate pathway (PPP) routes which may assist redox buffering. Down-regulation of GYS1, AGL and PHKG2 is consistent with decreased UDP-glucose, while reduced PDH-complex gene expression, combined with OXPHOS inhibition, explains the increase in pyruvate and lactate despite lower LDHA mRNA and protein. However, when we measured lactate at 48 h post-combination treatment in the same cell line (HSJD-DIPG007), lactate levels declined below control levels (Fig. 1N). Finally, the rise in oxidized cofactors (NAD, NADPH) coupled with a fall in NADH underscores a global shift toward a more oxidizing cytosolic environment, aligning with the significant increase of the oxidative-stress marker HMOX1 (Fig. 3G). Together, the metabolic gene arrays and metabolite data, combined with Extracellular Flux data from Fig. 1, highlight shut-down of canonical glycolysis/TCA flux with possible rerouting of carbon into PPP-driven redox-defense buffering when HSJD-DIPG007 cells are treated with phenformin and DCA.

Combination treatment triggers transcriptomic reprogramming involving DNA repair and cell stress

To elucidate more comprehensive transcriptional consequences of combined mitochondrial and glycolytic inhibition, we performed RNA-sequencing on HSJD-DIPG007 cells treated with phenformin and DCA for 24 h and compared gene expression to vehicle-treated controls to identify top differentially expressed genes beyond our mechanistically focused RNA arrays shown in Fig. 3. Multidimensional scaling (MDS) confirmed clear transcriptomic separation between groups, with the combination arm forming a distinct cluster (Fig. 4A). Consistent with this, the combination treatment exerted a markedly greater effect on the transcriptome than either drug alone. Applying a threshold of ≥ 2-fold change with a false discovery rate (FDR) ≤ 0.05, the combination treatment resulted in 1,578 upregulated and 2,047 downregulated genes. In comparison, DCA alone upregulated 1,053 and downregulated 347 genes, while phenformin upregulated 438 and downregulated 349 genes (Fig. 4B).

To refine mechanistic insight, we used a non-directed approach and pathway-level analysis (Reactome) to reveal enrichment of key biological pathways. Reactome analysis identified significant overrepresentation of pathways involved in DNA double-strand break repair via nonhomologous end joining (homology directed repair (HDR) through homologous recombination (HR) or single-strand annealing (SSA) and DNA double-strand break repair) (Fig. 4C). This enrichment implies that the phenformin and DCA combination causes sufficient metabolic and oxidative stress to potentially generate the presence of DNA double-strand lesions, prompting activation of DNA repair routes. This may create a vulnerability that could increase radiosensitization, separate from the effects of hypoxia reduction (Fig. 3A). Among the top differentially expressed genes (DEGs) in the combination treatment group, EREG, HEY1, P3H4, ABCD3, PHLDA1, IGF2BP3, FBXO32, PPP1R10, PGM2L1, SNAI1, ZNF146, and ETAA1 exhibited the greatest fold changes (Fig. 4D, Supplemental file 1).

To assess whether transcriptomic patterns were conserved across different DIPG cell line models, we performed RNA sequencing on two additional cell lines (SU-DIPG-VI and SU-DIPG17) treated with the phenformin-DCA combination and combined the data with HSJD-DIPG007 to find the top shared changes. Directed Reactome analysis of the pathways previously identified in HSJD-DIPG007 showed similar significant enrichment of pathways involved in DNA double-strand break repair via nonhomologous end joining (HDR through HR or SSA and DNA double-strand break repair) (Fig. 4E). To further validate that RNA level changes translated to the protein level, we selected three of the most significantly increased expressed genes across all 3 cell lines (TRIB3, SLC7A11, and ASNS indicated with arrows in Fig. 4F) and probed using immunoblotting. As shown in Fig. 4G, H and I, all 3 proteins increased following combination treatment, compared to control. We also noticed that many of the genes that increased across all 3 cell lines were seen in both the RT2 array (Fig. 3F and G) and single cell line RNASeq (Fig. 4D, Supplemental File 1) (for example HMOX1, SERPINE1 and NDRG1). Several of the top identified DEGs in HSJD-DIPG007 were also identified across the 3 cell lines, including FBXO32, PPP1R10 and PGM2L1, while the remainder were unique to the cell line highlighting inherent differences between cell lines, yet conserved general patterns in DNA repair when treated with the combination.

Phenformin and DCA combination treatment induces oxidative stress, DNA damage, cell cycle arrest, and apoptosis in DIPG cells

Both hypoxia and mitochondrial inhibition are associated with elevated oxidative stress and the generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and we observed changes in these pathways in our transcriptomics results. Therefore, we measured whether the phenformin and DCA combination increases mitochondrial and cytosolic ROS levels in SU-DIPG17 cells following treatment. The most pronounced increase in ROS was observed in cells treated with the combination, as measured by MitoSOX Red and DHE staining (Fig. 5A and B). These findings align with transcriptomic upregulation of HMOX1 (Figs. 3G and 4H), a stress-responsive gene activated in response to redox imbalance, suggesting that the phenformin-DCA combination causes oxidative stress.

Enrichment analysis from transcriptomics indicated changes in the DNA damage and repair responses, so we next examined whether the combination treatment induces DNA damage. Western blotting across the three cell lines SU-DIPG17, HSJD-DIPG007 and SU-DIPG-VI revealed an increase in γ-H2AX, a marker of DNA double-strand breaks, along with increased phosphorylation of CHK2, a critical checkpoint kinase activated in response to DNA damage, and reduced phosphorylation of BRCA1, a key component of HR repair, in SU-DIPG17 and HSJD-DIPG007 cells (Fig. 5C and D). This, combined with the transcriptional array data (Fig. 3F) and RNASeq data (Fig. 4), suggests the combination not only induces genotoxic stress but may also impair HR repair at both the transcriptomic and protein levels.

We next asked whether this damage leads to functional consequences in terms of cell cycle progression and cell survival. Flow cytometry analysis revealed an increased proportion of SU-DIPG17 and HSJD-DIPG007 cells in the sub-G1 phase (Fig. 5E) following combination treatment, consistent with DNA fragmentation and cell cycle arrest. Western blotting showed increased levels of cleaved PARP and cleaved caspase-3 in all three cell lines (Fig. 5F), supporting induction of apoptosis. Furthermore, we also observed total PARP1 protein decreased (top band) in all three cell lines and this is consistent with the transcriptomic data. Apoptosis was corroborated by Annexin V staining and flow cytometry, which confirmed an increase in apoptotic cell death in the combination group relative to single-agent treatments in both SU-DIPG17 and HSJD-DIPG007 (Fig. 5G). Additionally, we observed reduced expression of MCL-1, a key anti-apoptotic factor known to support glioma cell survival, in all three cell lines treated with the DCA and phenformin combination (Fig. 5F), which may contribute to apoptotic susceptibility in metabolically stressed cells. These results demonstrate that the phenformin-DCA combination drives oxidative stress and genotoxic injury, and impairs DNA repair capacity, resulting in apoptotic cell death.

The combination of phenformin and DCA improved the radiosensitivity of DIPG cells in vitro

Phenformin alone enhances the effects of radiation in DIPG cells in vitro and in vivo [5], and the phenformin and DCA combination induces redox stress and signs of DNA fragmentation while altering DNA repair in vitro (Figs. 3G, 4 and 5), so we explored whether combining phenformin with DCA would also improve the radiosensitivity of DIPG cells using a triple combination strategy. Using the gold standard clonogenic assays, both SU-DIPG17 and HSJD-DIPG007 cell lines were treated with vehicle, phenformin, DCA or combination treatment for 24 h, with or without irradiation. Colonies were allowed to form for several weeks before assessing colony survival. When compared with phenformin as a single radiosensitizing drug, the combination of phenformin and DCA further enhanced the radiosensitivity of both DIPG cell lines. The lowest number of colonies were in the triple combination groups (phenformin-DCA-radiation) for both neurosphere model HSJD-DIPG007 and monolayer model SU-DIPG17 (Fig. 6A). This finding was supported by data showing the highest levels of DNA damage (measured by γ-H2AX, Fig. 6B, C) and apoptosis (assessed via cleaved caspase 3, Fig. 6D) induced by the triple combination of phenformin, DCA and radiotherapy when compared to any other double combination or single treatment.

In vivo evaluation of DCA, phenformin, and radiotherapy combination treatment

Our in vitro findings demonstrated the radiosensitizing effect of DCA and phenformin combined, so we proceeded to evaluate the efficacy of combining DCA, phenformin, and radiotherapy in vivo. To determine if the triple combination of phenformin, DCA and radiotherapy reduces tumor burden and improves overall survival in a mouse model, we conducted a small pilot study using the HSJD-DIPG007 orthotopic model. Upon treatment initiation, we observed significant weight loss in the drug-treated groups, indicating potential toxicity (Fig. 6E). To mitigate this, we limited the treatment duration: radiotherapy was delivered within two weeks, and drug treatment (DCA and phenformin) was administered over a four-week period. This shortened schedule was designed to reduce treatment-related toxicity while still capturing the potential interaction between drug treatment and radiation. Following the completion of four-week treatment, one mouse per treatment group was culled for histological analysis (Fig. 6F). Post-treatment immunohistochemistry (IHC) revealed a marked reduction in tumor burden (H3K27M staining) and tumor cell proliferation index (Ki-67) across all treatment groups—including radiotherapy alone, dual-drugs (DCA + phenformin), and triple combination groups (radiotherapy + DCA + phenformin)—when compared to the vehicle-treated control (Fig. 6F). Despite some suggestions of tumor burden reduction immediately following the treatment course, no survival benefit was observed in any treatment group at the endpoint (Fig. 6G). This was also confirmed by IHC staining with H3K27M at endpoint showing similar tumor burdens across all groups (Fig. 6H). While the median survival for the control group was 74 days, the radiation alone group had a median survival of 94.5 days. However, this result was not statistically significant, possibly due to small group size and large variation in survival. Our current study used 20 Gy focal brainstem irradiation using SARRP, while our previous study used 10 Gy whole-brain irradiation using XRAD-320 [5]. Despite these differences in radiation modality, dose, and platform, the effect of radiation is overall consistent with minor to no improvement in overall survival at these doses.

Discussion

DIPG remains a universally fatal pediatric brain tumor, with radiotherapy only providing transient benefit. Growing evidence, including our own, suggests that DIPG cells exhibit unique mitochondrial metabolic dependencies, making OXPHOS a potential therapeutic target [5]. Potent complex I inhibitors such as phenformin have shown radiosensitizing activity in vitro and in vivo [5, 15] but their clinical translation has been limited by dose-limiting toxicities, including lactic acidosis. In this study, we tested a dual-metabolic strategy, combining phenformin with the PDK inhibitor DCA, to suppress compensatory glycolysis, mitigate phenformin-induced lactate accumulation, and enhance anti-tumor efficacy. Both our group and others have reported that single agents or double combinations of DCA, radiation, and phenformin (or the related biguanide metformin) can prolong survival in animal models of DIPG, glioma, and other cancers [5, 6, 15, 35–38]. However, no prior studies have examined the triple combination of radiotherapy, DCA, and phenformin in DIPG. The durability of benefit, the potential for additive toxicity, and the underlying metabolic mechanisms for this approach remain undefined, prompting us to evaluate the phenformin–DCA combination, alone and with radiotherapy, in both mechanistic in vitro assays and a small pilot orthotopic in vivo study. In vitro, DCA shifted DIPG metabolism from glycolysis toward OXPHOS, reducing lactate production and extracellular acidification, and normalizing phenformin-induced lactate accumulation. The combination produced greater ATP depletion, AMPK activation, oxidative stress, and apoptosis than either agent alone, while down-regulating hypoxia signaling, glycolytic and TCA-cycle enzymes, and impairing DNA damage repair. These metabolic and transcriptional effects were consistent across multiple DIPG cell lines. The combination also reduced hypoxia in neurospheres and increased radiosensitivity in vitro, likely through combined effects on oxygenation, oxidative stress, and DNA repair capacity. This finding aligns with prior reports in other pre-clinical cancer models, like melanoma [39] or gliomas [36], where DCA and other glycolysis inhibitors have been shown to alleviate metabolic acidosis while enhancing the efficacy of biguanides like metformin and phenformin [40].

Despite these promising in vitro results, our pilot orthotopic study showed that the triple regimen (radiotherapy + phenformin + DCA) failed to prolong median survival relative to radiotherapy alone. A short-term reduction in proliferation markers (Ki-67) and tumor burden (H3K27M) was observed in one mouse per group immediately after treatment, but these effects were not sustained. Mice receiving drug-containing regimens experienced substantial weight loss, suggesting systemic toxicity. Given the small cohort size, a treatment effect cannot be excluded, but the lack of survival benefit and observed toxicity suggest the dosing schedule and intensity may have been poorly tolerated.

Our findings emphasize that the therapeutic window for simultaneous complex I and PDK inhibition in vivo may be narrow, especially with radiotherapy, and likely schedule- and dose-dependent. Clinical experience with other potent OXPHOS inhibitors underscores this risk: IACS-010759 was discontinued owing to dose-limiting toxicities, including neurotoxicity and hyperlactatemia [22]. Adding glycolytic inhibitors may alleviate lactate induced by OXPHOS inhibitors, but it may also further compound side effects. In our study, the shortened treatment course was chosen to minimize toxicity but may also have limited efficacy. Weight loss could reflect systemic metabolic stress rather than accelerated tumor progression. Optimizing dose, schedule, and sequence, or substituting more tolerable agents, will be essential. Prior work shows metformin + DCA + radiation was tolerated in mice with a survival benefit [15], supporting the strategy if drug choice and delivery are optimized. Metformin is generally well tolerated but has not shown consistent benefit in solid tumors [41]. Phenformin, by contrast, delivers stronger complex I inhibition but carries an unfavorable safety profile, with lactic acidosis limiting clinical utility. Next steps should prioritize agents with an intermediate profile, greater mitochondrial potency than metformin but a wider therapeutic index than phenformin, and with schedules that minimize systemic metabolic stress. In parallel, rational combinations may widen the therapeutic window. Preclinical data indicate that complex I inhibition can enhance T-cell surveillance and responsiveness to immune checkpoint blockade [42, 43], meaning that perhaps lower, better-tolerated doses of OXPHOS-targeting drugs could be paired with immunotherapy. Together, these avenues warrant systematic evaluation in DIPG models with careful attention to dose and sequence with radiotherapy, before any claim of clinical benefit can be made.

In summary, we provide proof-of-concept that rational co-inhibition of OXPHOS and glycolysis can normalize phenformin-induced lactate accumulation, reduce hypoxia, and enhance radiosensitivity in DIPG in vitro models. However, translating these effects into a survival benefit will require strategies that minimize toxicity, potentially through alternative agents, biomarker-guided dosing, or intermittent schedules. Further work is warranted to define the optimal therapeutic window and to establish whether this approach can be safely integrated with radiotherapy, immunotherapy or other drugs.
