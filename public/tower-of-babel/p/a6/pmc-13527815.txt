Self-Medication Practices in Nigeria: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis
Authors: Chibuzor G. Muoka, Nnedimma V. Akparanta, Favour A. Taribo, Chiemela O. Onubogu, David A. Asiegbu, Chidubem J. Azubike, Iheomachi O. Nwokoro, Chukwuebuka E. Athan, Ugwunna Ede, Emmanuel I. Uwaechina
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Self-medication is a common health-seeking practice in Nigeria and is associated with inappropriate medicine use, adverse drug reactions, and antimicrobial resistance. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence of self-medication in Nigeria and synthesize evidence on its patterns, determinants, and consequences.Methodology:A systematic search of PubMed, Google Scholar, and African Journals Online (AJOL) was conducted for studies published between January 2000 and June 2025. Eligible studies reported self-medication practices among Nigerian populations. Study selection followed PRISMA guidelines, and methodological quality was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool. Random-effects meta-analysis using a generalized linear mixed model was performed to estimate pooled prevalence with 95% confidence intervals (CI). A prediction interval was computed to estimate the expected range of true prevalence in future similar studies. The review protocol was registered in PROSPERO (CRD420251130710).Results:Ninety-one studies involving 37,359 participants were included, with 86 contributing to the meta-analysis. The pooled prevalence of self-medication was 67.8% (95% CI: 63.0%–71.0%), with substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 98.4%). Prevalence was highest among occupational groups (78.1%) and in workplace settings (81.3%). Antibiotics were the most commonly self-medicated drugs, followed by analgesics and antimalarials. Major drivers included perceived minor illness, previous treatment experience, time constraints, financial barriers, and easy access to medicines. One qualitative study was narratively synthesised but excluded from the quantitative meta-analysis.Conclusion:Self-medication is highly prevalent in Nigeria, with widespread non-prescription antibiotic use contributing to antimicrobial resistance. Strengthening medicine regulation, improving healthcare access, expanding health insurance coverage, enhancing public education, and implementing antimicrobial stewardship programmes are essential to curb inappropriate self-medication and its consequences.

Introduction

William Osler observed that the tendency to take medicines is a unique trait distinguishing humans from animals [1], highlighting the practice of self-medication (SM), which is defined as the use of medicines by individuals to treat self-recognized symptoms or illnesses, as well as the continued or intermittent use of previously prescribed drugs for chronic or recurrent conditions without professional supervision [2]. Although often perceived as convenient, time-saving, and cost-effective, SM carries significant risks, including inappropriate drug use, adverse drug reactions, harmful drug interactions, masking of serious diseases, and the development of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [3,4].

Globally, self-medication is a common health-seeking behaviour and is frequently regarded as a means of reducing pressure on healthcare systems, particularly in low- and middle-income countries where access to formal healthcare may be limited [5]. In Nigeria, studies have consistently documented high levels of self-medication among diverse population groups, including students, healthcare workers, pregnant women, rural residents, and the general public [3,6-8]. The widespread availability of over-the-counter medicines, weak enforcement of prescription regulations, high healthcare costs, poverty, and cultural attitudes favouring self-care have all contributed to the persistence of this practice [9-11,12]. Other important drivers include previous positive experiences with medications, recommendations from family and friends, and exposure to pharmaceutical advertisements [10,12]. The COVID-19 pandemic further amplified self-medication behaviours as fear of infection, movement restrictions, and disruptions to healthcare services encouraged many individuals to self-treat or use medicines for preventive purposes [13,14].

One of the most important consequences of widespread self-medication is its contribution to AMR, a major global public health threat. The non-prescription use of antibiotics, inappropriate dosing, and incomplete treatment courses are commonly reported practices in Nigeria [3,4]. These behaviours compromise treatment effectiveness and contribute to increased morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. Additionally, the concurrent use of herbal remedies and conventional medicines without adequate knowledge of potential interactions poses further health risks [10,13]. Although self-medication may be appropriate for the management of minor ailments when supported by adequate knowledge and the responsible use of approved over-the-counter medicines, irrational self-medication remains a significant public health concern.

This systematic review and meta-analysis estimated the pooled prevalence of self-medication in Nigeria and explored sources of heterogeneity. The findings provide evidence to inform policy, strengthen medicine regulation, and guide interventions promoting rational medicine use while reducing the public health impact of self-medication.

Methods

Data Sources and Search Strategy

Electronic database searches were conducted using a predefined search strategy. The databases searched were PubMed, Google Scholar, and African Journals Online (AJOL). The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to self-medication and Nigeria using Boolean operators ("AND" and "OR"). Search terms included "self-medication," "over-the-counter drugs," "drug misuse," "non-prescription drug use," "antibiotic misuse," "Nigeria," and "prevalence of self-medication." An example of the PubMed search strategy was: ("Self Medication"[Mesh] OR "self-medication" OR "self medication" OR "over-the-counter drugs" OR "non-prescription drug use" OR "drug misuse" OR "antibiotic misuse") AND ("Nigeria"[Mesh] OR Nigeria). Searches were limited to studies published in English between January 2000 and June 2025. Due to the large number of records retrieved from Google Scholar and AJOL, relevance ranking (as determined by each database's default algorithm) was applied, and the first 200 most relevant articles from Google Scholar and the first 300 most relevant articles from AJOL were screened for eligibility. The final search was conducted on 28 June 2025. The Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were followed to ensure a transparent selection process. The review study protocol (CRD420251130710) was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) [15].

Eligibility Criteria and Study Selection

Studies conducted in Nigeria that investigated self-medication among the general population or specific groups were eligible. Reviews, editorials, case reports, conference abstracts, non-English publications, studies lacking full text, unclear methodology, or primary data were excluded. Quantitative and qualitative studies published in English between January 2000 and June 2025 were included.

Two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts after removing clearly irrelevant records. Potentially eligible studies underwent full-text review, with disagreements resolved through discussion with additional reviewers. Three reviewers independently verified reasons for full-text exclusions. The selection process followed predefined eligibility criteria, ensuring methodological rigour and transparency, while the list of excluded studies was provided in the supplementary material.

Quality Assessment and Data Extraction

The methodological quality of the included studies was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) for quantitative and qualitative studies [16]. The tool evaluates the quality of primary studies using seven criteria, with responses recorded as “yes,” “no,” or “can’t tell.” Studies scoring 6–7 “yes” responses were classified as high quality, those with 4–5 as moderate quality, and those with 1–3 as low quality. Three reviewers independently appraised all studies, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion with the remaining reviewers. Further details of the risk of bias assessment are provided in the supplementary article (Table S3a&b). Data were extracted using a standardized Excel spreadsheet. The following information was collected from each study: author(s), year of publication, study location, study population, sample size, study design, prevalence of self-medication, commonly used medications, reasons for self-medication, and reported outcomes, including side effects. Data extraction was performed independently by three authors and subsequently verified by the remaining authors to ensure completeness and accuracy.

Statistical Analysis

Meta-analytical techniques were employed to estimate the pooled prevalence of self-medication in Nigeria using R statistical software (version 4.5.2) with the meta, metafor, and tidyverse packages [17,18]. Prevalence estimates were pooled using a random-effects generalized linear mixed model (GLMM) with logit transformation (PLOGIT) and Clopper–Pearson confidence intervals [19,20]. The random-effects model was selected a priori because substantial methodological and clinical heterogeneity across studies was anticipated [19]. Results were presented using forest plots [17]. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q test and the I2 statistic, which quantifies the proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity [21]. I2 values of <50%, 50–70%, and >70% were interpreted as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively [21]. Prediction intervals were computed to estimate the expected range of true prevalence in future similar studies [22]. Sensitivity analysis was conducted using a leave-one-out approach to assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimate [23]. Publication bias was evaluated through visual inspection of funnel plots and formally tested using Egger's regression test and Begg's test for funnel plot asymmetry [24]. Funnel plot interpretation considers the relationship between study effect estimates and their precision. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity based on population group, study setting, and geographic region [25]. Where publication bias was suspected, a trim-and-fill analysis was performed to estimate the potential impact of missing studies and adjust the pooled prevalence accordingly.

Results

Search Results

A total of 642 records were identified from PubMed (n = 142), Google Scholar (n = 200), and African Journals Online (AJOL) (n = 300). After the removal of 170 duplicate records, 472 records remained. A further 364 records were excluded as clearly irrelevant based on title screening, leaving 108 records for title and abstract screening. At this stage, 17 records were excluded for not meeting eligibility criteria (including one editorial, four review articles, and twelve non-Nigerian studies). The remaining 91 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and all met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final review. The study selection process is presented in Figure 1.

PRISMA flow chart showing the study selection process and results.

Characteristics of Included Studies

Ninety-one Nigerian studies involving 37,359 participants were included. Most (90/91) employed cross-sectional designs, while one qualitative study contributed only to the narrative synthesis. Probability sampling was used in 52% of studies, 43% used non-probability methods, and 5% did not report sampling techniques. Quality assessment rated 8.8% of studies as high quality, 87.9% as moderate, and 3.3% as low quality; none were excluded. Most studies had unclear representativeness and non-response bias, limiting generalisability. Geographically, studies were concentrated in Southern Nigeria, particularly the South-West, with relatively few from Northern regions. Study populations included university students, community residents, clinic attendees, pregnant women, healthcare workers, and occupational groups. Overall, the included studies provided broad but geographically uneven evidence on self-medication practices in Nigeria.

Prevalence of Self-Medication

Eighty-six studies involving 35,655 participants reported quantitative prevalence estimates, ranging from 15.9% to 100%. The pooled prevalence of self-medication in Nigeria was 67.8% (95% CI: 63%–71%) using a random-effects meta-analysis. Considerable heterogeneity was observed (I2 = 98.4%; prediction interval:

26%–92%), reflecting variations in study populations, settings, geographical locations, sampling methods, recall periods, and definitions of self-medication. Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis demonstrated a stable pooled estimate (66.7%–68.0%), indicating that no individual study substantially influenced the findings. Funnel plot asymmetry, together with significant Egger's (p = 0.0052) and Begg's (p = 0.0020) tests, suggested possible publication bias, although this may reflect clinical heterogeneity. Trim-and-fill analysis estimated an adjusted prevalence of 58% (95% CI: 53%–62%), but this exploratory estimate should not replace the primary pooled prevalence because of the substantial heterogeneity across studies.

Subgroup analysis

The prevalence of self-medication by the categorical moderators (study population, study setting, and geopolitical zones) is presented in Table 2. By study population, the prevalence estimates were 78.1% (95% CI: 58.7%–89.9%; I2 = 97.8%) among occupational groups, 74.6% (95% CI: 60.0%–85.3%; I2 = 97.3%) among healthcare-related students, 73.4% (95% CI: 60.8%–83.1%; I2 = 98.8%) among non-healthcare students, 69.0% (95% CI: 54.2%–80.8%; I2 = 98.9%) among general community populations, and 48.6% (95% CI: 33.6%–63.8%; I2 = 98.5%) among pregnant/postpartum women. In terms of study setting, the highest prevalence of self-medication was estimated in workplace settings (81.3%, 95% CI: 62.3%–92.0%; I2 = 96.9%; p < 0.0001), followed by academic institutions (73.9%, 95% CI: 64.8%–81.3%; I2 = 98.4%; p < 0.0001), community settings (69.2%, 95% CI: 58.2%–78.4%; I2 = 98.6%; p < 0.0001), and health facility settings (57.3%, 95% CI: 47.4%–66.7%; I2 = 98.3%; p < 0.0001). For geopolitical zones (figure 4), the prevalence estimate was higher in the North-Central zone (78.1%, 95% CI: 62.8%–88.3%; I2 = 97.8%; p < 0.0001) compared with the South-West zone (63.4%, 95% CI: 54.2%–71.7%; I2 = 98.8%; p < 0.0001) and North-East zone (58.1%, 95% CI: 26.5%–84.1%; I2 = 98.0%; p < 0.0001).

Common Illnesses Leading to Self-Medication

A total of 44 studies reported illnesses for which self-medication was practiced. Cough was the most frequently reported indication (56.8% of studies), followed by fever (54.5%). Headache and body pain were each reported in 50.0% of studies. Diarrhea was reported in 43.2% of studies, while sore throat was reported in 36.4%. Common cold (34.1%), malaria (27.3%), and catarrh (22.7%) were also documented. Toothache was among the least frequently reported conditions (18.2%). Other reported conditions included COVID-19, sickle cell disease, infantile colic, and various dental, ear, eye, and menstrual conditions. Detailed data are provided in the supplementary article – Table S6.

Reasons for Practicing Self-Medication

A total of 72 studies reported quantified reasons for self-medication in Nigeria (Table 3). Perceived minor illness was the most commonly reported reason, identified in 47.2% of studies, followed by previous experience/knowledge (40.3%), time-related factors (37.5%), financial barriers (36.1%), and access to drugs (34.7%). Geographic access barriers (22.2%), health system failures (20.8%), and social influence (19.4%) were also reported, while fear related to COVID-19 was least frequently reported (6.9%). Drugs Used in Self-Medication

A total of 73 studies reported drugs used in self-medication. Antibiotics were the most frequently reported drug class (85.0% of studies), with amoxicillin and ampicillin/cloxacillin commonly used. Antipyretic analgesics were reported in 68.5% of studies, while opioid analgesics and steroids were reported in 5.5% and 2.7%, respectively. Paracetamol was the most commonly used antipyretic analgesic, followed by NSAIDs such as ibuprofen, diclofenac, aspirin, and piroxicam. Antimalarials were reported in 52.0% of studies, mainly artemisinin-based combination therapies and chloroquine. Vitamins and supplements, particularly vitamin C, were reported in 41.1% of studies. Respiratory drugs and herbal remedies were each reported in 27.4% of studies. Other drug classes included sedatives/psychotropics (19.2%), antihistamines (13.7%), antidiarrheals (9.6%), and anthelmintics (5.5%). Detailed data are provided in the supplementary article – Table S7.

Sources of Drugs Purchase for Self-Medication

The source of drug purchase was reported in 55 studies. Patent medicine stores were most common (81.8%), followed by pharmacies (72.7%), often overlapping with patent medicine stores. Other sources included family and friends (43.6%), home stocks (30.9%), hospital pharmacies (27.3%), and drug hawkers (25.5%). Less frequent sources were herbalists/faith-based outlets (9.0%), markets (7.3%), and online platforms (5.5%). Detailed data are provided in the supplementary article – Table S7.

Sources of Information for Drug Use

The source of information or who suggested the drug use was reported in 35 studies. Healthcare professionals were most frequently cited, with pharmacists (37.1%) and doctors (28.5%) as the leading contributors. Other sources included prior personal experience or self-initiative (60%), informal contacts such as family and friends (85.7%), and patent medicine vendors (42.8%). Medicine-related sources, such as drug leaflets and textbooks (20.0%), media and advertisements (54.2%), and other sources such as

classrooms, alternative medicine, and traditional healers (25.7%). Detailed data are provided in the supplementary article – Table S7.

Side Effects Reported due to Self-Medication

A total of 28 studies reported side effects associated with self-medication practices. Gastrointestinal effects were the most frequently reported (50.0%) and included diarrhea, nausea/vomiting, abdominal pain, cramps, ulcers, and heartburn. Dermatological effects (35.7%) included rashes, itching, and skin irritation. Neurological effects (28.6%) included headache, dizziness, visual disturbances, and mood-related symptoms. Systemic or general side effects (35.7%) included weakness, allergies, and adverse drug reactions. Reproductive and other serious outcomes (7.1%) included miscarriage, birth defects, teeth discoloration, and injection abscesses. Metabolic and cardiovascular effects (10.7%) included increased appetite, frequent urination, weight gain, hypoglycemia, and heart problems. Other reported effects (17.9%) included sleep disturbances, drowsiness, deafness, drug interactions, and disabilities. Detailed data are provided in the supplementary article – Table S7.

Factors Associated with the Practice of Self-Medication

A total of 55 studies reported significant socio-demographic factors associated with self-medication practice. Several socio-demographic factors were associated with self-medication, including education (34.5%), gender (23.6%), age (21.8%), occupation/employment (16.4%), marital status (10.9%), income (10.9%), year of study/department/faculty (10.9%), and residence/location (9.0%). Overall, lower education level, lower income, younger age, and certain occupational groups (students, unemployed individuals, and housewives) were more frequently associated with higher prevalence of self-medication (p < 0.05 in most studies). Gender associations varied across studies, with some reporting higher prevalence among males and others among females. Detailed data are provided in the supplementary article – Table S6.

Discussion

This review provides the largest synthesis of self-medication practices in Nigeria to date, drawing on 91 studies involving 37,359 participants. Most studies were of moderate quality (87.9%), while 8.8% were high quality and only 3.3% were low quality, supporting the reliability of the available evidence for policy development. A notable finding was the uneven geographical distribution of studies. Excluding multizonal studies (7.6%), most originated from Southern Nigeria (71.5%), whereas all Northern geopolitical zones combined contributed only 20.9%. This suggests that self-medication practices in Northern Nigeria, particularly rural communities, remain under-investigated. Sociocultural and economic barriers, coupled with security challenges such as the Boko Haram insurgency in North-East Nigeria, where only three studies were identified, may partly explain this disparity [103]. The lack of representative data from Northern Nigeria may limit the development of targeted interventions that address regional differences in healthcare access, health literacy, and attitudes toward conventional medicine.

The pooled prevalence of self-medication in Nigeria was approximately 68%, with a prediction interval of 26%–92%. This estimate exceeds those reported in several other low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), including Ethiopia (44.0%), India (53.6%), Ghana (54.0%), and Pakistan (64.0%) [104-107]. The high prevalence in Nigeria likely reflects limited healthcare access, high out-of-pocket expenditure, weak regulation of medicine distribution, and the prominent role of informal healthcare providers [108]. Notably, Nigeria's prevalence is substantially higher than Ghana's despite similarities in epidemiological and socioeconomic contexts. This difference may be partly explained by Nigeria's extensive medicine distribution network, which includes approximately 200,000 Patent and Proprietary Medicine Vendors (PPMVs) operating under a tiered accreditation system [109]. In contrast, Ghana's Licensed Chemical Seller system is generally more standardized and subject to stronger regulatory oversight [110]. While Nigeria's PPMV network improves medicine availability, it also increases access to prescription medicines without adequate control, thereby promoting self-medication.

The wide prediction interval observed in this review indicates substantial between-study heterogeneity and suggests that self-medication varies considerably across populations and settings. Occupational groups, including artisans, market women, and bank employees, had the highest prevalence (78.1%). This may reflect attempts to avoid healthcare costs, loss of income associated with clinic visits, and inadequate health insurance coverage. Similar findings have been reported in China, where uninsured individuals frequently preferred self-medication as the first response to illness [111,112].

Students also exhibited high rates of self-medication, with prevalence estimates of 74.6% among healthcare-related students and 73.4% among non-healthcare students, both exceeding the 44.1% reported among tertiary students in Ghana [106]. The slightly higher prevalence among healthcare students suggests that pharmacological knowledge alone does not guarantee rational medicine use and may instead foster overconfidence in self-treatment. This finding is particularly concerning because these students represent future prescribers and dispensers of medicines. Healthcare workers similarly demonstrated a high prevalence of self-medication (66.5%), indicating that professional training does not necessarily translate into appropriate medicine use. Comparable findings have been reported among healthcare workers in Malaysia (77.6%) and Spain (59.4%), with physicians often being the most affected group [113,114]. Among patients receiving care in health facilities, the prevalence of self-medication was 59.2%, consistent with reports from sub-Saharan Africa showing prevalence estimates ranging from 45% to 89% [115]. Similarly, 69.0% of individuals in community-based populations practiced self-medication, closely aligning with the overall pooled estimate and previous reports from sub-Saharan Africa [115]. These findings suggest that self-medication is deeply embedded in Nigerian society and may contribute to adverse outcomes such as drug toxicity, allergic reactions, dependence, and reduced effectiveness of prescribed therapies. For example, a study from Ekiti State reported that approximately 10% of participants who self-medicated had difficulty discontinuing medicine use [33].

With regard to women's health, nearly half (49%) of pregnant and postpartum women practiced self-medication. This prevalence exceeds the global estimate of 32% and rates reported in Iran (38.5%), Mexico (21.9%), and Ethiopia (26.6%), although it remains lower than the 66% reported in Ghana [106,116–119]. Self-medication during pregnancy is particularly concerning because of its reported association with adverse maternal and fetal outcomes, including preterm birth, low birth weight, congenital malformations, and maternal morbidity [119]. Although increased antenatal care attendance and awareness of medication risks may contribute to the lower prevalence compared with Ghana, the finding that nearly one in two women self-medicates during pregnancy highlights persistent gaps in medicine safety education. Geopolitical zone analysis showed the highest prevalence in North-Central Nigeria (78.1%), followed by North-West (73.3%), South-East (71.4%), South-South (71.2%), South-West (63.4%), and North-East (58.1%). Despite the limited number of studies from Northern Nigeria, prevalence estimates remained high across the region. However, urban sampling bias cannot be excluded, emphasizing the need for studies in rural northern communities where healthcare access is more restricted.

The most frequently reported drivers of self-medication were perceived minor illness (47.2%), previous experience or knowledge (40.3%), time-related factors such as long waiting times (37.5%), financial barriers (36.1%), and easy access to medicines (34.7%). Social influence (19.4%) and health system failures (20.8%) were also commonly reported. Unlike findings from Ghana, where long waiting times were the leading reason for self-medication, perceived minor illness was the predominant driver in Nigeria [106]. Collectively, these factors reflect broader health system challenges, including high out-of-pocket healthcare costs, limited insurance coverage, inadequate staffing, and weak enforcement of prescription regulations. The National Drug Policy (NDP) set a target of increasing awareness of the risks of self-medication among 40% of the population by 31st December 2024 [128]; however, the persistently high prevalence observed in this review suggests that this objective was not achieved. Addressing self-medication therefore requires a multifaceted approach that combines regulatory enforcement, public education, and health system strengthening.

Antibiotics were the most frequently self-medicated drug class, being reported in 85.0% of studies, followed by antipyretic analgesics (68.5%), antimalarials (52.0%), and herbal remedies (27.4%). This pattern is consistent with reports from Syria, Pakistan, and Uganda, where antibiotics and analgesics were among the most commonly used medicines for self-medication [120-122]. However, the prominence of antimalarial use in our study reflects the epidemiological realities of malaria-endemic regions such as Nigeria and Uganda. The widespread use of antibiotics without prescription is particularly concerning because it directly contributes to antimicrobial resistance (AMR) [123]. According to the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation, approximately 263,400 deaths in Nigeria were associated with AMR in 2019, representing one of the highest AMR-related mortality burdens globally and exceeding deaths from malaria, HIV/AIDS, and enteric infections combined [124,125]. From an economic perspective, AMR is also projected to impose substantial economic costs, potentially reducing Nigeria's GDP by 5–7% by 2050 [125,126]. Although the National Action Plans (NAP) on AMR identified weak antimicrobial stewardship as a major challenge [125,127], the high frequency of antibiotic self-medication observed in this review suggests limited progress in addressing this problem. Unlike analgesics or antimalarials, the inappropriate use of antibiotics has important population-level consequences because it exerts selective pressure on bacterial populations, thereby promoting the emergence and spread of resistant organisms [129].

Drug sources also warrant attention. Patent medicine stores (PPMVs) were the most frequently reported source of medicines (81.8% of studies), followed by pharmacies (72.7%). Although Nigerian regulations permit PPMVs to sell only approved over-the-counter medicines in underserved areas [109], our findings suggest that prescription-only medicines, including antibiotics, are commonly obtained through these outlets. This observation aligns with concerns highlighted in the National Action Plans on AMR regarding weak regulatory enforcement and the uncontrolled distribution of medicines [127]. Other important sources included family and friends (43.6%), home medicine stocks (30.9%), and drug hawkers (25.5%). The frequent use of social networks as sources of medicines underscores the need for community-level interventions that address medicine sharing and informal treatment advice.

Adverse effects associated with self-medication were commonly reported. Gastrointestinal symptoms, including nausea, diarrhoea, and abdominal pain, were the most frequent adverse events (50.0% of studies), followed by dermatological (35.7%) and neurological effects (28.6%). Serious outcomes, including miscarriage and congenital abnormalities, were reported in 7.1% of studies. These findings highlight the often-overlooked harms associated with unsupervised medicine use. Given the low rates of adverse drug reaction reporting in Nigeria, the true burden is likely substantially underestimated despite ongoing efforts to strengthen pharmacovigilance [125,127]. Educational level was the socio-demographic factor most frequently associated with self-medication, appearing in 34.5% of studies. Lower educational attainment was generally associated with higher self-medication rates, consistent with findings from other LMICs [123]. However, the high prevalence observed among healthcare students suggests that knowledge alone may be insufficient to promote rational medicine use. Other factors commonly associated with self-medication included age, gender, occupation, income, and place of residence.

Limitations and Strengths

This review was limited by reliance on predominantly cross-sectional, self-reported studies, substantial heterogeneity, variable definitions of self-medication, publication bias, moderate study quality, and underrepresentation of Northern Nigeria. Most studies also did not distinguish appropriate from inappropriate self-medication or assess its clinical appropriateness. Despite these limitations, it is the first comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis of self-medication in Nigeria, synthesising data from 91 studies involving 37,359 participants. Adherence to PRISMA guidelines, robust random-effects meta-analysis, and subgroup analyses by population, setting, and region strengthen the validity and policy relevance of the findings.

Implications for Practice, Policy and Future Research

Nigeria has developed several policy frameworks to address self-medication and antimicrobial resistance, including the National Drug Policy (2021), the National Action Plan 1.0 on AMR (2017–2023), and the current National Action Plan 2.0 (2024–2028) [125,127,128]. Despite these initiatives, implementation remains suboptimal. NAP 2.0 acknowledges uneven progress, sectoral disparities, and inadequate funding for key AMR activities. The plan allocates approximately USD 77.6 million over five years, with a substantial proportion dedicated to improving access to quality antimicrobials and promoting their rational use [125]. The findings of this review provide evidence to guide prioritization of these investments. Regulatory efforts should focus on strengthening enforcement of prescription-only medicine laws, particularly among Patent and Proprietary Medicine Vendors (PPMVs). Regular inspections, stricter sanctions for non-compliance, and improved monitoring of medicine distribution channels are essential. Expanding health insurance coverage may also reduce financial barriers that drive self-medication and decrease reliance on informal healthcare providers. Public health interventions should address the most commonly reported drivers of self-medication, including perceived minor illness, reliance on previous experience, long waiting times, and medicine accessibility. Awareness campaigns funded under NAP 2.0 should therefore target these specific behavioural and health-system determinants rather than relying solely on general educational messages [125].

Healthcare facilities should implement measures to reduce waiting times and improve access to care through enhanced triage systems, appointment scheduling, and service delivery models. In addition, Antimicrobial Stewardship (AMS) programmes, Drug and Therapeutics Committees, and Infection Prevention and Control Committees should be strengthened to promote rational medicine use and improve prescribing practices. These recommendations are consistent with NAP 2.0, which advocates for the establishment of AMS committees across secondary and tertiary healthcare facilities [125]. The high prevalence of self-medication among healthcare students highlights the need to strengthen undergraduate training in rational medicine use and antimicrobial stewardship. Professional regulatory bodies should also promote continuing professional development programmes focused on antimicrobial stewardship and responsible prescribing practices. NAP 2.0 already includes provisions for the development and accreditation of AMR-related training programmes that could support these objectives [125]. Future research should prioritise Northern Nigeria, particularly the North-East, and rural communities where evidence remains limited. Qualitative studies are needed to provide deeper insight into the behavioural, cultural, and health-system factors driving self-medication. Future primary studies should also distinguish between responsible and inappropriate self-medication practices to enable more targeted policy responses. The research funding allocated under Strategic Objective 6 of NAP 2.0 provides an opportunity to address these important evidence gaps [125].

Conclusion

Self-medication affects about two-thirds of Nigerians, with antibiotics most frequently misused, increasing antimicrobial resistance. Stronger regulation of medicine sales, improved healthcare access, expanded health insurance, public education, antimicrobial stewardship, and greater research investment are needed. Effective implementation of the National Action Plan on AMR (2024–2028) is essential to reduce inappropriate self-medication and its consequences.
