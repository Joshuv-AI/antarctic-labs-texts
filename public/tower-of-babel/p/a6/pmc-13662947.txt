Identification of Heterogenous Treatment Response to Corticosteroids Among Patients With Ventilator-Dependent Respiratory Failure
Authors: Charles R Terry, Kit N Simpson, Andrew J Goodwin, Dee W Ford
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBACKGROUND:Corticosteroids increasingly are recommended as adjunctive treatments in critical illness syndromes; however, the existing literature supporting their benefit remains uncertain. Ventilator-dependent respiratory failure (VDRF) is a critical illness syndrome with low-acuity and high-acuity subphenotypes identifiable with electronic health record (EHR) data.RESEARCH QUESTION:Do conditional average treatment effects (CATEs) from corticosteroids with respect to hospital mortality differ by clinical subphenotypes in VDRF?STUDY DESIGN AND METHODS:In this multicenter, retrospective observational cohort, we measured CATE by clinical subphenotype among patients > 18 years of age receiving > 48 hours of mechanical ventilation with adjusted doubly robust estimator models using EHR data from the first 24 hours after initiation of mechanical ventilation.RESULTS:Low-acuity and high-acuity subphenotypes from the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC) and Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III) cohorts showed heterogeneous CATEs on mortality resulting from corticosteroid treatments (low-acuity MUSC, −4.1%; low-acuity MIMIC-III, −5.2%; high-acuity MUSC, 9.3%; and high-acuity MIMIC-III, 4.5%) with mean differences of 13.4% (95% CI, 11.8%-14.9%) and 9.7% (95% CI, 9.3%-10.1%), respectively. Protective effects in the low-acuity subphenotype were attenuated among patients with ARDS, pneumonia, or sepsis identified by administrative diagnosis codes; however, the risk of harm was consistent for the high-acuity subphenotype.INTERPRETATION:Our results show that clinical subphenotypes in VDRF can identify heterogeneous treatment effects from corticosteroids. These findings support the usefulness of clinically derived subphenotypes and may inform enrichment strategies for future prospective clinical trials.Keywords: acute respiratory failure, corticosteroids, doubly robust estimation

Corticosteroids remain an increasingly accepted but still controversial treatment for improving outcomes and organ dysfunction in ARDS, pneumonia, and septic shock. Recent trials in community-acquired pneumonia (CAP)1 and ARDS2,3 showing mortality benefit have brought renewed interest in their use and compelled the American Thoracic Society, European Society for Intensive Care Medicine, and the Society for Critical Care Medicine to update their guidelines to recommend conditionally using corticosteroids in ARDS and severe CAP.4,5 Nevertheless, these results have not been confirmed or reproduced widely, raising uncertainty with respect to the dose, timing, duration, specific drug, and populations that benefit from their use.

Subphenotypes comprising ARDS,6 pneumonia,7 and sepsis8 (APS) increasingly are recognized as important drivers of heterogeneity in critical illness syndromes that affect both prognosis and treatment. Large retrospective cohorts or secondary data analysis of randomized controlled clinical trials with extensive data and biomarker testing have identified subphenotypes with differential responses to therapies or treatments that previously were considered equivocal or not useful.8,9 An observational analysis of corticosteroids in a small 2-center observational cohort study of patients with ARDS secondary to COVID-19 identified a differential survival benefit with corticosteroid use for hyperinflammatory vs hypoinflammatory ARDS phenotypes.10

Although comprehensive subphenotyping models are important and validated biological constructs for identifying latent heterogeneity, they require specialized biomarkers and measurements that are not yet readily available and may be limited by logistical and financial barriers. Classifier models using readily available clinical data have identified hyperinflammatory and hypoinflammatory ARDS phenotypes successfully in retrospective research cohorts.11 Similarly, our group trained and validated unsupervised clustering models in patients with ventilator-dependent respiratory failure (VDRF) using clinical data from the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC) health system and the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III) database.12,13 Although these models were able to identify prognostically significant subphenotypes among patients with acute VDRF and to demonstrate the feasibility of secondary analysis with clinical data from diverse care settings, 12 their ability to identify subphenotypes with heterogeneity of treatment effect is unclear. Therefore, we sought to examine if treatment responses to corticosteroids differed by VDRF cluster.

Study Design and Methods

Study Design and Setting

In this retrospective, observational cohort study we compared the clinical outcomes of patients in both VDRF clusters who received corticosteroids with those who did not using inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) and multivariable regression analysis. Patient encounters were included from the MUSC VDRF and the MIMIC-III cohorts derived from electronic health record data collected for routine clinical care. The MUSC cohort comprises patient encounters from a multicenter health system of diverse academic and community hospitals admitted from 2016 through 2023, whereas the MIMIC-III cohort consists of patient encounters from a single-center academic hospital admitted from 2001 through 2012.12,13

Patient Population and Clustering Phenotypes

Patients included were 18 years of age or older who received at least 2 days of mechanical ventilation in an ICU setting, as identified by International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision, procedure codes for invasive mechanical ventilation cross-referenced with ventilator-specific electronic health record data. 12,14 Details of the VDRF computational phenotype are summarized in e-Table 1 and were reported previously.12 In brief, patients older than 18 years receiving care in an ICU location with mechanical ventilation charges and ventilator-specific data for 48 to 720 hours met the criteria for VDRF. Consistent with our previous approach, we used the same cluster assignments from the existing 0-to-24 hours k-means clustering with multiply imputed cluster analysis model to recapitulate our previously observed high-acuity and low-acuity clusters.12 Patients in the high-acuity cluster are typified by lower compliance, worse hypoxemia, and more shock or organ dysfunction. Patients in the low-acuity cluster are typified by higher albumin concentration, lower minute ventilation, and less organ dysfunction (e-Fig 1). Clustering predictor variables did not include clinical outcomes or treatment with corticosteroids. Study size was determined by number of patient encounters who met the VDRF definition in both cohorts and received steroids after mechanical ventilation.

Baseline Variables

All baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory variables were obtained from electronic health record data for both cohorts. Time-weighted averages of clinical and laboratory data from the first 24 hours after initiation of mechanical ventilation were used in the analysis. Elixhauser comorbidity indices were calculated using all available International Classification of Diseases, 9th and 10th Revision, billing diagnosis codes from the encounters.15 ARDS, pneumonia, and sepsis labels were determined from billing diagnosis codes according to composite coding definitions (e-Table 2) for MUSC and MIMIC-III cohorts, respectively.

Steroid Treatment

Steroid treatment was defined in the MUSC cohort as any encounter where the patient received an antiinflammatory corticosteroid dose of ≥ 150 mg of hydrocortisone/d for at least 3 consecutive days after initiation of mechanical ventilation. Patients who received corticosteroids but did not meet treatment criteria were excluded from the primary analysis, but were included as a sensitivity analysis (e-Tables 3–6, e-Fig 2) of our results. Patients who did not receive corticosteroids during the encounter constituted the control group. The medication tables in the MIMIC-III cohort were not detailed enough to record daily dosing schedules, and therefore we were unable to calculate a total daily dose. For the MIMIC-III cohort, encounters were classified as receiving corticosteroid treatment if a patient received any corticosteroids on the same day or any time after initiation of mechanical ventilation.

Multiple Imputation and Inverse Probability Weighting

Covariate balance between groups was achieved using IPTW for each cluster using time-weighted averages of data collected from the first 24 hours after intubation. Propensity scores were stratified by cluster to account for hypothesized interaction with corticosteroid treatment. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression by pooling model estimates from each imputed data set according to Rubin’s rules.16 Because no predicted nonlinear relationships between predictor variables and corticosteroid exposure or hypothesized interactions among covariables were present, these were not included in any of the propensity models (e-Tables 7, 8). Propensity scores represent the conditional probability of receiving treatment dose steroids for the MUSC cohort or receiving any steroids for the MIMIC-III cohort based on the first 24 hours of data after initiation of mechanical ventilation. Given the rates of missingness in the clinical data, propensity scores for IPTW were calculated using multiple imputation with 20 imputed data sets to address data missingness.17 Overlap of propensity score distributions, deviance for all propensity score models, mean and range of IPTW weights, and effective sample sizes are reported in e-Figures 3–10 and e-Tables 9–12. Complete case analysis using IPTW with single imputation using median values for covariables was included for sensitivity analysis (e-Tables 13–19).

Covariate balance was assessed using standardized mean differences for each continuous covariable and raw mean differences for categorical variables before and after IPTW. A standardized mean difference and raw mean difference threshold of < 0.1 for each baseline variable was indicative of balanced groups and are reported in e-Figures 11–18).

Time-Dependent Covariate Survival Analysis

Cox hazard models including corticosteroid treatment as a time-dependent covariable were included for both MUSC and MIMIC-III cohorts to account for immortal time bias. Cox hazard models included doubly robust models, IPTW models, covariate-adjusted models, and unadjusted models for both cohorts. Non-doubly robust Cox hazard model results are reported as sensitivity analyses in e-Tables 13, 15, 17, 19. Statistical interaction between corticosteroid treatment and cluster assignment was tested by including an interaction term in the model.

Doubly Robust Estimation of Causal Effects

To estimate the predicted hospital mortality with and without corticosteroid treatment, we used the IPTW and relevant covariates to create doubly robust estimators (DREs) for each cluster.18 Treated and untreated DRE models were trained using actual treatments from all available encounters in each cohort and cluster. Each model was used to estimate the factual and counterfactual probability of hospital mortality for each encounter, whether the patient received or did not receive corticosteroids, using logistic regression. The marginal predicted mortality difference was calculated for every encounter and was compared between both clusters in the MUSC and MIMIC-III cohorts, respectively. Weighted models were pooled with robust SE estimates that accounted for the lack of independence among imputed data sets according to Rubin’s rules.

Doubly robust estimating models were derived using propensity scores calculated from each cluster to account for interaction between clusters and corticosteroid treatment. Models included biologically and clinically significant covariates such as age, biological sex, acuity of illness, comorbidity burden, ARDS, sepsis, and pneumonia. Covariates were chosen based on biological significance and prior literature.15,19–22 A directed acyclic graph of model covariates is included in e-Figure 19. Parsimonious models identified using backward selection from all available covariables are reported in e-Tables 20–28 and e-Figure 20.

The primary outcome was the conditional average treatment effect of corticosteroid treatment on hospital mortality within each cluster of patients with VDRF. Mean differences in predicted mortality were compared between each cluster using Welch’s 2-sample t test. The proportion of encounters within each cluster experiencing benefit or harm was calculated by estimating the cumulative density function for each cluster and cohort and integrating the area under the curve that is greater or less than a predicted mortality difference of 0 (e-Appendix 1).

All descriptive statistics for baseline variables were reported as medians and interquartile ranges (IQRs) except where otherwise noted. Two-sided P values of < .05 were considered statistically significant for all analyses. Statistical analysis was conducted in R version 4.4.0 software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). The survival, survey, Weightit, MatchThem, gtsummary, and tidyverse packages in R were used to complete this analysis.

Patient encounters included from the MUSC and MIMIC-III cohorts numbered 2,721 and 7,550, respectively (Figs 1, 2). Treatment dose corticosteroids were administered in 873 patient encounters (32.1%) in the MUSC cohort and any corticosteroids were administered in 2,169 patient encounters (28.7%) in the MIMIC-III cohort. Covariate Balance After Propensity Score Adjustment

After propensity score adjustment, both clusters in the MUSC and MIMIC-III cohort were well balanced with pooled standard mean differences and raw differences of < 0.1 for all covariables (e-Figs 11–18).

Doubly Robust Time-Dependent Cox Hazard Models

DRE Cox models using corticosteroid treatment as a time-dependent covariate identified significant interaction between cluster assignment and corticosteroid use in the MUSC and MIMIC-III cohorts (Table 3). Primary hazard ratios for corticosteroid treatment and cluster assignment were not statistically different in the overall cohort, although point estimates supported a consistent directionality toward benefit from corticosteroids treatment in the MUSC and MIMIC-III cohorts and harm from high-acuity cluster status with respect to hospital mortality (Table 3).

Doubly Robust Estimating Models and Average Treatment Effects of Corticosteroids

DRE models for the low-acuity cluster supported an overall protective average treatment effect for corticosteroids in both cohorts. Among all VDRF encounters from the MUSC cohort in the low-acuity cluster, receiving treatment-dose corticosteroids showed an estimated average absolute risk reduction of 4.1%. Sixty-eight percent in the low-acuity cluster were predicted to have lower mortality resulting from corticosteroids. Likewise, the low-acuity cluster from the MIMIC-III cohort that received any corticosteroids showed an estimated average absolute risk reduction of 5.2%, and 76% of the cluster were predicted to have lower mortality as a result of corticosteroids. High-acuity clusters were consistently predicted to be harmed by receiving treatment dose or any steroids in both cohorts (Table 4). The improved survival in the low-acuity cluster was attenuated when subsetting cohorts for encounters when a billing diagnosis for ARDS, pneumonia, sepsis, or septic shock was present; however, the increased mortality risk in the high-acuity cluster remained consistently elevated in both cohorts (Fig 3).

Discussion

Our findings support a heterogeneity of treatment effect with corticosteroids with respect to hospital mortality by VDRF cluster assignment within 24 hours of intubation. High-acuity cluster assignment consistently predicted increased hospital mortality with harm predicted for 66% and 76% of the MUSC and MIMIC-III high-acuity clusters, respectively. The low-acuity clusters were predicted to have a protective effect from corticosteroid treatment in the MUSC and MIMIC-III cohorts; however, the mortality benefit was attenuated in both cohorts when only encounters with billing diagnoses for ARDS, pneumonia, sepsis, or septic shock were included.

Recently updated guidelines from the American Thoracic Society and the Society of Critical Care Medicine now conditionally recommend administering corticosteroids for patients with ARDS in addition to other accepted indications in severe CAP, septic shock, and obstructive lung diseases.4,23 These guidelines noted limitations inherent in the heterogeneous subgroups of ARDS and acknowledge that patients with different causes or phenotypes may respond differently. Heterogeneity of treatment effect within ARDS is complicated all the more by diagnostic uncertainty in clinical practice.19,24 Among studies noting beneficial effects for corticosteroids, patients were more likely to benefit from corticosteroid treatment in the setting of significant hypoxemia (PaO2 to FIO2 ratio < 200),2 early administration, septic shock,25 or CAP or COVID-19 pneumonia.1,3

Among the 2 VDRF clusters, the high-acuity cluster consistently was predicted to be harmed as a result of corticosteroid use despite a higher severity of illness,12 a finding that is in contrast to prior studies that have found an association between improved outcomes and corticosteroid use in more severely ill populations.1,25 Steroid type, dose, duration of exposure, timing of steroid administration in the course of critical illness, and extrapulmonary organ dysfunction may influence these disparate findings; however, the inability to account for subphenotypes also may help to explain these patterns.

Interestingly, predicted mortality risk reduction in the low-acuity cluster for both groups was attenuated when modelling only patient encounters with billing diagnoses for ARDS, pneumonia, and sepsis, whereas increased mortality risk in the high-acuity cluster remained constant. Given the imprecision inherent in billing diagnosis codes, the difference in treatment effects for the low-acuity cluster between the full cohorts and the APS subset may represent a form of selection bias, in which patients with more diagnostic uncertainty were more likely to respond to corticosteroids. Alternatively, if the treatment effect truly represents benefit for patients with low-acuity cluster VDRF without APS syndromes, this would suggest a novel benefit for corticosteroids among a subgroup of patients with VDRF that is more generalizable than solely within the context of APS syndromes, as currently understood.26–28

Mechanistic evidence suggests that ventilator-induced lung injury is mediated at least in part by the inflammatory response to mechanical strain of positive pressure ventilation and can be attenuated with low-tidal volume ventilation.29,30 As more recent research has elucidated further the role of diaphragmatic injury resulting from mechanical ventilation,31,32 the mediation effect of respiratory system elastance,33 and the generalizability of inflammatory phenotypes to patients with ARDS and non-ARDS causes of acute VDRF,34 the physiologic characteristics of invasive mechanical ventilation itself and patient-ventilator dyssynchrony contribute to the dysregulated inflammatory host responses that perpetuate organ dysfunction, which in turn are affected by underlying biological phenotypes. In addition to lung protective ventilation, animal models support the role of high-dose corticosteroids attenuating diaphragmatic dysfunction, suggesting a putative mechanism whereby early administration may improve clinical outcomes in mechanically ventilated patients without APS syndromes.

Our findings are hypothesis generating given the nonrandomized nature of this cohort, risk of confounding, and the limitations related to missingness and imprecision of clinical data. Our approach using multiply imputed, doubly robust estimating models aims to ensure appropriate covariate balance and adjustment.

Interpretation

Clinically derived subphenotypes of VDRF can classify patients into groups with heterogeneity of treatment effect resulting from corticosteroids. The high-acuity clusters from both MUSC and MIMIC-III cohorts consistently predicted harm resulting from corticosteroid treatment, whereas corticosteroids were protective or neutral for low-acuity clusters. These findings suggest that clinically derived VDRF phenotypes can enrich for steroid-responsive patients. Randomized and mechanism-focused studies are needed to identify whether predicted treatment effects are evident among patients with VDRF.

Take-Home Points

Study Question:

Can clinical subphenotypes in ventilator-dependent respiratory failure identify patients who are responsive to corticosteroids?

Results:

Clinical subphenotypes can identify patients who may be harmed by corticosteroids and may select for those who will respond to corticosteroids, as well.

Interpretation:

Clinical phenotyping with electronic health record data can identify heterogeneous treatment responses to corticosteroids among patients with ventilator-dependent respiratory failure.
