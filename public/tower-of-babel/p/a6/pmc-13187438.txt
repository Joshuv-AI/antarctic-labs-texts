The role of expandable graphite particle size and microstructure on the dielectric and thermal properties of polyethylene-based composites
Authors: A Łapińska, A J Panas, N Grochowska, J Kotkowski, R Przesmycki, M Liszewska, Ł Osuchowski, Z Mierczyk, I Wyrębska, S Kowalczyk
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis work demonstrates the decisive role of expandable graphite (EG) particle size and processing route in tailoring the microstructure and multifunctional performance of linear low density polyethylene (LLDPE)-based composites. In the studies, the EG of low: 90 ml/g (EG096), medium: 350 ml/g (EG350), and high: 500 ml/g (EG500) expansion rates were chosen, which are related to the shortest, up to ~ 200 μm; medium, up to ~ 1 mm; and the longest vermicular structures, up to over 1 mm, after expansion. The main finding is that the EG096, with the shortest vermicular structure, produced the most homogeneous morphology, characterised by minimal voids and agglomeration. The highest dielectric performance was achieved by the sample containing 15 wt% of EG096, the most microporous system with the largest pore volume, combining an enhanced dielectric constant (ε′ = 13 at 3.6 GHz) and dielectric loss. We propose that the microporosity of the EG enhances electromagnetic wave dissipation via multiple reflections and local dielectric losses, with short vermicular EG structures supporting a connected network that boosts microwave-frequency dielectric performance. Processing route strongly influenced dielectric behaviour, with hot pressing shown to preserve the vermicular structure better than extrusion. While the effect on thermal diffusivity and conductivity was less pronounced, the homogeneous microstructure remained attractive for thermal management, with EG096_20 extruded samples reaching λ = 0.86 W m−1 K−1. Unlike previous studies, this work systematically correlates EG expansion rate, vermicular morphology, and microporosity with both dielectric and thermal properties, establishing key design parameters for next-generation dielectric and thermal management materials.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-45520-2.Subject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Carbon structures remain an attractive solution in many newly defined domains of applications despite being well-known materials for years. The renaissance in the exploration and investigation of carbon structures was undoubtedly related to the isolation of graphene in 2004, followed by the discovery of carbon nanotubes1,2. Despite more than two decades of intensive research, graphene as a commercial product remains immature, particularly in the form of nanoplatelets, which fall short of the strict definition of “graphene”3. Moreover, the high cost of commercially available graphene nanoplatelets significantly limits their widespread application. This has intensified the search for economically viable materials capable of reproducing the key functionalities of graphene. A straightforward alternative lies in modified forms of graphite, which can deliver comparable properties at a fraction of the cost. In this context, the present work explores the potential of expandable graphite as a practical graphene substitute. Before pursuing advanced functionalization strategies, it is essential to identify the most promising type of expandable graphite for further modification and for integration into polymer composites, and this is one of the aims of this work.

Expandable graphite (EG), may be characterised by different expansion rates, which relate to its grain size and the after-expansion vermicular structure length4. EG is a particularly popular material used as an additive for flame-retardant purposes5,6. For example, Lorenzetti, A., et al. investigated EG expansion rate and intercalant influence on thermal stability and flammability of polyurethane-based composites7. The main observation made was that the expansion rate does not significantly influence the thermal stability or flammability of polyurethane (PU)-based foams. However, the type of intercalant matters, sulfur provided better flame retardancy than phosphorus. Thus, being inherently flame-retardant, EG is an attractive choice in functional composites manufacturing. Therefore, within this work, we pose a question about the influence of EG expansion rate on the dielectric and thermal properties of polymer composites. To our best knowledge, up to now, very little attention has been given to this topic, despite the frequent investigation of those properties due to the potential for further application of such materials, e.g. in consumer electronics for heat dissipation purposes or for electromagnetic shielding, camouflage, energy storage, etc.8–10.

One of the commonly used polymer matrices for composites is polyethylene (PE) due to its low cost, versatility, low water absorption, chemical inertness, and others11–13. EG as a filler in low-density polyethylene (LDPE)-based composites was reported by Wei et al.14, who investigated their thermal conductivity. It was significant that the EG/LDPE composite was prepared using a multi-step process to obtain a 3D network, which indeed led to a remarkable thermal conductivity of κ ~ 20 W/(m K). Sadly, the authors did not take into consideration the expansion rate of EG or the microstructure influence on said properties, which are extremely sensitive to any fluctuations. Additionally, the prominent EM shielding performance was observed. However, dielectric properties, which are an inherent feature of the former and also may be sensitive to sample processing techniques, were not determined.

Setting aside the issue of the filler and PE type used, there are many examples of PE-based composites with carbon nanofillers with exceptional properties. For instance, the orientation of ultra-high molecular weight PE (UHMWPE) macromolecules filled with nanodiamond resulted in an absolutely remarkable thermal conductivity of κ ~ 60 W/(m K) and low dielectric losses10. High-density polyethylene (HDPE) filled with expandable graphite and colloidal graphite, reported by Ch.-M. Ye et al. exhibited thermal conductivity κ ~ 1.6 W/(m K) and κ ~ 0.8 W/(m K), respectively15. The authors claimed that none of the applied theoretical models (e.g. Agari, Maxwell-Eucken, or Cheng and Vochan), besides Zieblend (but still not perfectly), agreed with the results shown. Unfortunately, no deep microstructure analysis was shown, which may be one of the potential reasons for discrepancies between experimental and theoretical models. Additionally, I. Krupa et al. found the differences between HDPE and low-density polyethene (LDPE) filled with graphite in terms of their thermal conductivities16. The LDPE-based composites revealed lower thermal conductivity than the HDPE-based composites due to the lower degree of crystallinity of LDPE.

Thermal properties combined with dielectric ones may indicate the qualification of a polymer composite for application in electronic packaging, such as a dielectric substrate17. An ideal dielectric substrate should be highly thermally conductive, with a high dielectric constant and low dielectric losses18. On the other hand, polymer composites of high dielectric constant and low loss tangent may be attractive for high-speed electronics19. Considering PE-based composites, as previously mentioned, PE (LDPE and HDPE) is seen as an excellent material in electric engineering due to its insulating behaviour, low dielectric constant, etc.8. Panwar V. et al. measured HDPE and graphite nanocomposites both in low and radio frequency and claimed that the materials follow the power law of percolation theory, with the threshold estimated to be ~ 7 wt% of graphite loading, and exhibit a relatively high dielectric constant (ε′ = 3.12 ∙106 at 20 Hz and ε′ = 1.54 ∙103 at 1 MHz)20. On the other hand, Shi M. et al. investigated the influence of graphite particle size and lamination layer number on the dielectric properties of linear LDPE (LLDPE)-based nanocomposites. They found that the dielectric constant strongly relies on layer thickness, while the dielectric loss is insensitive to this factor21. The best performance was found in said work for 36 layers and for a graphite particle size of 15 μm (ε′ ~ 3, ε′′ ~ 0 in 0.001–1 MHz).

In the light of previous findings, this work investigates the influence of expandable graphite (EG) with varying expansion rates on the dielectric and thermal properties of polyethylene (PE)-based composites. Particular attention is given to the microstructure, providing mechanistic insight into how processing routes can be leveraged to tailor material performance. Processing techniques were selected from widely accessible industrial methods, and the best-performing conditions are highlighted. By employing low-cost, commercially available feedstocks, this study demonstrates viable pathways for tuning functional properties in PE-based composites. Moreover, it establishes a foundation for transforming a well-known carbon material into advanced solutions for next-generation consumer electronics.

Results and discussion

Structural analysis

Expandable graphite structural analysis

SEM

Figure 1 presents a SEM photograph of the graphite used in the studies. Both before and after expansion, observation was made to ensure the quality of the delivered material. The EG096 flakes (Fig. 1a) are distinguishable from the EG350 and EG500 with their significantly lower lateral size and no, at first glance, layered structure. The measured lateral size varied, and the flakes of lateral size above 200 μm may be found. The EG350 and EG500 revealed the flakes of lateral size above 350 and 500 μm, which is consistent with the technical specifications. The vermicular structure is visible in all of the used graphite; however, the EG096 presents significantly shorter chains of lengths 100 to slightly above 200 μm, in contrast to EG350 and EG500. The expanded EG350 exhibits an intermediate length of the chains up to even 1 mm, but a short one, such as 200 μm, could also be found. The most extended chains after expansion are presented by the EG500, which exceed 1 mm in length and are clearly visible to the naked eye. The measured examples of individual flakes were presented in Fig. S2. The expanded graphite is typical of those shown previously in the literature4.

The adsorption isotherms measured for all studied graphite samples are classified as type V according to IUPAC, indicating the presence of mesopores and very limited nanoporosity. The facile and rapid expansion of graphite led to the formation of separated graphite sheets, materials exhibiting significantly enhanced porosity compared to commercially available samples, three of which have an SBET below 0.5 2/g. After 3 h of thermal treatment at 250 °C, the SBET of graphite powder increased to 1.6 2/g. Depending on the graphite source, the obtained carbon nanostructures exhibited varying SBET values, with the highest value of 1.68 m2/g observed for the EG096 sample. This may be attributed to the higher residue content of sulfuric acid or other intercalants used for expandable graphite preparation, which likely promotes temperature-driven exfoliation of graphite layers. Despite these differences, all obtained samples exhibited a micro–mesoporous structure with a relatively high proportion of wide mesopores (> ~ 20 nm). However, their total pore volume (~ 0.04 m3/g) remained low and was predominantly composed of void spaces formed between small, agglomerated sheets and/or interconnected frameworks derived from deformed graphitic layers (Table 1).

PE/EG composites structural analysis

Microsection analysis

Concerning the LLDPE-based composites, the microsection analysis was performed first and is shown in Fig. 4, presenting a front view and Fig. S3 of the edges for both types of manufactured samples. The best EG dispersion is clearly for the EG096_20 samples; however, the hot pressing (Fig. 4a) introduced potential, but not significant agglomeration, which is almost not observed for extruded samples. The higher expansion rate and the longest vermicular structures after expansion result in more significant potential agglomeration. Due to the relatively long structures, the agglomeration may not be obvious. But dense, large areas of EG may be considered as potential agglomerates, while long and thin areas may be a separate structure. Additionally, despite careful manufacturing procedure, some of the samples possessed air voids and such voids are observed in Fig. S3 for both types of samples. However, the lowest amount of air voids is observed for the EG096_20 sample made directly from hot pressing. Then, the longer the vermicular structure, the more gaps are visible. Thus, one may roughly conclude that the longer the vermicular structure, the more severe the amount of empty gaps during the processing, regardless of the process type.

Functional properties analysis

Dielectric properties

Dielectric constant

Figure 8 presents the dielectric constant of PE-based composites (∅ = 60 mm) in the microwave range. The highest dielectric constant results among the analysed sample sets were noted: for the EG096_15 sample of 15wt% EG loading, ε’ = 13 at 3.6 GHz; for the EG350_15 sample of 15 wt% EG loading, ε’ = 8.2 at 3.6 GHz; and for the EG500_20 sample of 20 wt% EG loading, ε’ = 5.4 at 3.6 GHz.

Dielectric constant of PE–based samples filled with (a) EG096, (b) EG350, and (c) EG500 of different concentrations.

The overall highest dielectric constant is observed for EG with the lowest expansion rate, EG096 (Fig. 8a). Increasing the EG content in the PE matrix results in a higher dielectric constant; however, saturation is observed above 15 wt% EG096 loading. At higher loadings, with probable percolation pathways created, a distinct and characteristic band emerges at the lowest frequencies, which can be attributed to Maxwell-Wagner-Sillars (MWS) polarisation. Such findings have been previously reported for graphene-polymer composites24.

As the expansion rate increases, corresponding to larger particle sizes, a pronounced decrease in the dielectric constant is observed. For EG350 (Fig. 8b), a similar saturation effect occurs, though less strongly, and weak indications of MWS polarisation remain at the lowest frequencies. In contrast, for the highest expansion rate, and thus the largest particle size (Fig. 8c), the dielectric constant decreases further. In this case, no saturation effect or MWS polarisation is detected. The lower dielectric response is most likely associated with the reduced number of polymer-filler interfaces and insufficient formation of micro-capacitor networks25.

These observations may be explained by the fact that smaller expansion rates, corresponding to smaller particle sizes, result in a greater number of interfacial boundaries, thereby enhancing MWS polarisation. Nevertheless, contrary findings have been reported in the literature, e.g., by Kranauskaite et al. and Rubice et al.26,27, who observe an increasing dielectric constant with increasing particle size. In those studies, no MWS polarisation effects were detected, which is likely the key factor underlying the observed discrepancies.

Additionally, above the lowest frequency in the investigated range, the dielectric constant becomes relatively stable and only weakly frequency-dependent. It is a result of the gradual suppression of interfacial charge-accumulation processes as frequency increases. The increasing frequency means the charge carriers at the interface are no longer able to follow the alternating electric field, and thus fast polarisation mechanisms, such as electronic and atomic polarisation, govern the dielectric response at higher frequencies28.

Dielectric loss

A similar behaviour is observed in Fig. 9 of dielectric losses. The EG096 samples, particularly at higher EG concentrations, exhibit relatively high dielectric losses, with clear indications of MWS polarisation and interfacial conductivity at lower frequencies29. Next, the increasing expansion rate results in decreasing dielectric losses, similar to the observations made based on Fig. 8. The dielectric losses of EG350 are roughly over two times lower than for EG096 and dielectric losses of EG500 are approximately ten times lower than for EG096, which is an even higher difference than observed in the relative decrease of dielectric constant between these sets of samples. Additionally, saturation effects were observed at the highest EG concentration for EG096 and EG350, but they were not strongly pronounced in the dielectric losses. Saturation is not observed in the EG500 samples.

Dielectric loss of PE–based samples filled with (a) EG096, (b) EG350, and (c) EG500 of different concentrations.

The most pronounced dielectric losses results among the analysed sample sets were noted for the EG096_20 sample of 20wt% EG loading, ε” = 1.76 at 3.6 GHz; moderate for the EG350_20 sample of 20 wt% EG loading, ε’ = 0.52 at 3.6 GHz; and low for the EG500_10 sample of 10wt% EG loading, ε’ = 0.02 at 3.6 GHz.

Tangent loss

The tangent loss (Fig. 10) continues naturally the trend observed in the dielectric constant and losses analysis. The tan δ of the EG096 samples (Fig. 10a) increases with the increased EG concentration up to ~ 0.17 at 2 GHz for 20 wt%. Then, above 5-6 GHz, it decreases, but for the highest EG loading (15-20 wt%), tan δ stays relatively high. Next, the moderate values are observed for the EG350 samples. The highest tan δ is observed for EG350_20, but tan δ of EG350_15 remains relatively high, also above 6 GHz, while for the rest of the EG350 samples it goes to zero. The lowest tan δ is again observed for EG500 samples, which exhibit low values even for the highest concentrations (EG500_20, tan δ < 0.05). The highest values of tan δ occur for the lowest frequencies (< 2 GHz), and then rapidly fall near 0 for f > 5 GHz.

Based on the above analysis, the following conclusions can be drawn. Firstly, despite the use of expandable graphite (EG) with varying expansion rates, the filler’s morphology remains crucial. The most pronounced dielectric properties, both dielectric constant and dielectric losses, were observed for the sample with the lowest expansion rate and, consequently, the smallest vermicular structures (EG096). This effect may be attributed to the larger number of interfacial “contact” points and the formation of more “microcapacitors”30. But it may also arise from a more compact structure. In general, the dielectric properties of carbon-filled polymer composites result from conductivity mismatches between the components, leading to Maxwell-Wagner-Sillars (MWS) polarisation. The number of microcapacitors in the composite is strongly dependent on the distribution of fillers within the polymer matrix31. Larger particle sizes and vermicular chains promote the formation of agglomerates, and an increased number of air gaps are trapped during the technological process, as confirmed by microsection images (Fig. 4). This is the reason for the less pronounced dielectric performance in the EG500 system. Additionally, the larger particle size results in a lower number of effective polymer-filler and reduced interfacial area available for charge accumulation24.

Furthermore, Fig. S6 compares the dielectric constant and losses of two types of samples: discs of = 60 mm and a thickness of 2 mm (hot-pressed), and discs of = 15 mm and a thickness of 5 mm (extruded and subsequently hot-pressed). Interestingly, the extruded samples exhibited lower dielectric properties than the hot-pressed ones, regardless of the EG type. This behaviour is most likely due to the fact that extrusion improves filler dispersion in polyethylene (PE), but simultaneously destroys the vermicular graphite structures, thereby disturbing the formation of effective percolation paths. In contrast, hot pressing strongly orients filler particles, forcing them to align perpendicularly to the pressing axis, which may be favourable, especially in thin samples33.

Additionally, EG500 exhibits the lowest specific surface area (SBET) and mesoporous structure, which may indicate a reduced number of EG-polymer interfacial boundaries and, consequently, weaker dielectric properties. Santos R. M. et al. demonstrated that larger graphite particle sizes exhibit stronger agglomeration, which significantly influences the AC conductivity of graphite nanoplatelet/polypropylene composites32. Similarly, Sever K. et al. reported that larger expanded graphite particles result in a faster percolation threshold and higher AC conductivity, which may contribute to reduced dielectric performance33.

Another possible factor behind the significantly decreased dielectric properties of EG500 is the higher amount of air trapped during processing, as well as its lower compatibility with the LLDPE matrix. Supporting this, Hamidinejad M. et al. showed that microcellular HDPE/graphene composites can exhibit extremely low dielectric losses34.

Specific heat capacity

Thermal properties, including those described in this paragraph and in further studies, were measured only for the injection-moulded composites, as these samples were considered the most representative for evaluating the influence of the processing route on heat transport in the final materials. Figure 11a presents the experimental results of specific heat capacity obtained from all additives of manufactured samples, i.e. polymer resin: LLDPE (denoted as PE in the figure), and all graphite types: EG096, EG350 and EG500. The melting temperature determined in this work was 118 °C, marginally lower than the value specified in the manufacturer’s technical data sheet (TDS). The obtained results are typical for these types of materials, and some differences, especially in graphite’s specific heat capacity, may exist. This is primarily due to the difference in the declared purity of the material, expressed in pure carbon content. The declared carbon content of the EG 096 material is 96% by weight (Table 4). The specific heat values cp for this material are higher. They exceed the specific heat values cp of the EG350 and EG500 structures by approximately 20% relative value. The differences in the specific heat values cp of the EG350 and EG500 structures are about 4% and may be due to minor differences in carbon content, but are generally within the limits of measurement inaccuracy. During the study of expanded graphite structures, significant volatile component release was observed. This is likely due to moisture release. The data presented here were derived from repeated heating-cooling cycles corrected for mass change. In the case of a property such as specific heat, in the case under consideration, the resultant value of specific heat capacity, effects such as intrinsic graphite structure are reflected in, among others, pore size, pore volume or potential air adsorption35,36, should play a less important role, although they determine the conditions for possible adsorption, especially of moisture. Thermal properties

The relationship between the measured effective thermal diffusivity of the composites and temperature is shown in Fig. 12. The effective thermal diffusivity for all composites exhibits a linear, decreasing dependence on higher temperatures. The highest values of effective thermal diffusivity are obtained for the highest EG concentration, regardless of the EG type, which is a direct result of the mixture rule37. As mentioned, a slight decrease in thermal diffusivity with the temperature rise can be due to changes in the properties of both EG38 and the polymer, which is in a rubbery state39,40. It may also have originated in more intense phonon scattering, as was stated in Lazarenko A. et al.41. However, the mentioned phonon scattering needs to be treated here as an apparent phenomenon due to the nature of the investigated materials, as was already explained elsewhere42. Anytime within the following description, the phonon picture will be mentioned; it needs to be considered as apparent and not related to the phonon in a strict physical sense.

Effective thermal diffusivity of: (a) EG096, (b) EG350, (c) EG500 samples in temperature function obtained via modified Angstrom method. The figure shows raw experimental data without removed data points corresponding to irregular heating regime parts of the recorded signals.

The effective thermal diffusivity decrease contributes to a similar dependence of effective thermal conductivity measured by the modified Angstrom method (Fig. 13). Since the matrix is semicrystalline and there is no data on polymer orientation, the description of the phenomena behind the changes in polymer properties is complex. Bai et al.43 briefly describe the drop in thermal conductivity of a polymer with rising temperature (above glass transition temperature) as a result of a decrease in the phonon mean free path. Xu et al.44 link it with intrinsic phonon scattering within crystallites, becoming dominant over increasing phonon population. According to Choy et al.45, perfect PE crystals exhibit T-1 dependence, suggesting three-phonon Umklapp scattering processes. Weaker dependence, as in our case, could indicate that the main factor limiting the heat transfer may be phonon scattering by defects. When it comes to amorphous polymers, on the other hand, Agarwal et al.46 propose that the thermal conductivity decrease could result from the increasing phonon scattering of vacant sites and microvoids. When describing semicrystalline materials, all of the above factors, as well as phenomena at the phase boundary, may play a role. Moreover, the above-mentioned analysis relates to well-defined polymers, while within this work, the additional additive is present, as well as the microscopic phenomena.

Across the literature, it may be noticed that carbon-based polymer composites aimed at thermal management are a popular topic. The authors use different types of carbon to enhance thermal conductivity. Focusing only on graphite and expandable graphite, Lebedev S. M. and Gefle O. S. investigated natural graphite in the LLDPE resin manufactured by injection moulding47. They reported κ = 6.5 W/(m K) for 30 vol% graphite loading. Sadly, the authors do not take into consideration the microstructure or the particle size of the filler, which is particularly important in the thermal properties analysis. The particle size influence on the thermal properties was investigated in Debelak B. and Lafdi K., who reported the lack of impact of exfoliated graphite particle size in the epoxy resin on the thermal conductivity for the highest graphite concentration48. The three investigated graphite samples with small, medium and large particle sizes achieved a similar κ = 4.3 W/(m K) for 20 wt% of graphite loading. But for the lower graphite concentration, the significant differentiation of thermal conductivity was observed. The outstanding thermal conductivity was reported by Wu et al., who achieved κ = 11.28 W/(m K), but for drastically high EG concentration (60 wt%) for composites based on LLDPE manufactured by melt blending49.

However, little attention is paid to the thermal properties measurement techniques in the literature. Therefore, Fig. 14b compares the results with the values obtained using the Hot Disk and the modified Angstrom method obtained for the room temperature. The latter are consistently lower than the ones obtained using the TPS method; however, the filler concentration dependency exhibits a similar trend, and the results are consistent within the uncertainty range. This observation supports the thesis of D’Oliveira et al.50 that the discrepancies between the obtained values can result not only from the type of graphite particles and their dispersion (as in this case, the samples were the same) but also from using different measurement methods. In our case, there is no basis to blame the precision and accuracy of the used instruments, as suggested by the mentioned authors, since both apparatuses have been carefully calibrated. Both methods have strong theoretical foundations and, as mentioned, gave consistent values in the given uncertainty range51,52,62.

Based on the summary presented in Table 3, the following conclusion regarding the novelty of this work can be drawn. Recent studies on graphite-based polymer composites have mainly focused on improving electrical, dielectric, and/or thermal performance through selected processing strategies, such as solution casting or melt processing, as exemplified by Tarannum F. et al.’s work, who focused on EG/PEI composites manufactured by a solution mixing technique and provided electrical and thermal conductivity properties53. Another example is Ryse et al., who reported both dielectric and thermal conductivity studies of PA11 and copolymer composites filled with EG. However, their studies did not address, in a comparative manner, how two distinct processing routes affect dielectric performance within a single material system, nor were they designed to relate particle size and expansion-derived microstructure directly to both dielectric response and detailed thermal transport analysis54. Longo et al. examined the effect of different mixing methods on EG/epoxy nanocomposites and analysed structural, thermal, electrical and water-transport-related properties. Nevertheless, that work was focused on thermosetting epoxy systems and on the influence of mixing methodology, rather than on a comparative evaluation of expandable graphite particle size, expansion characteristics and processing route in relation to dielectric behaviour and effective thermal transport in thermoplastic LLDPE-based composites55. In contrast, the present study provides a comparative analysis of expandable graphite with different particle sizes and expansion characteristics, processed via two different routes, and examines their influence on dielectric properties. Subsequently, the representative processing route was selected for a deeper thermal analysis. To the best of our knowledge, such a side-by-side analysis combining graphite particle size, expansion-related microstructure, and processing route within a single LLDPE-based composite system has not been reported in this form.

Conclusions

This study elucidates the critical role of expandable graphite (EG) particle size and intrinsic microstructure in governing composite microstructure and, consequently, thermal and dielectric performance.

The results demonstrate that the shortest vermicular structure (EG096) yielded the most homogeneous microstructure, characterised by minimal voids and suppressed agglomeration. This morphology proved decisive for property optimisation. The most microporous system, EG096_20, with the highest total pore volume, delivered the best dielectric performance, combining a high dielectric constant and low dielectric loss. This is a result of a microporous network facilitating electromagnetic wave dissipation through multiple reflections at the material interfaces, supported by local interfacial polarisation.

The increasing particle size of EG results in a gradual deterioration of dielectric performance, mainly due to increased microstructural defects and a reduced number of polymer-filler interfaces, which limit local interfacial polarisation.

The processing route was identified as a second decisive factor in optimising dielectric performance. Hot pressing preserved the vermicular structure more effectively than extrusion, leading to superior dielectric performance. Importantly, controlled adjustment of the vermicular structure enables deliberate tuning of dielectric behaviour.

The representative samples were chosen to deepen the analysis of effective thermal conductivity. While dielectric performance is significantly influenced by EG particle size, this strong dependence is not evident for effective thermal diffusivity and conductivity. However, a smaller particle size results in fewer microstructural flaws, such as trapped air gaps or agglomeration. As microstructural imperfections significantly disturb the effective thermal conductivity in polymeric composites, it may be concluded that the EG096 system provided the most homogeneous structure, resulting in the most stable effective thermal conductivity and yielding κ = 0.86 W m-1 K-1 for EG096_20, the best-performing sample.

Overall, these results establish particle size, the intrinsic EG microstructure, and the processing route as powerful levers for tailoring multifunctional performance in EG-based composites, offering clear design principles for next-generation dielectric and thermal management materials.

Methods

Materials

The polyethylene (PE) resin of trade name Dowlex 2629.10UE in the form of a powder (as for rotational moulding) was purchased from Resinex (Poland). The PE used is characterised by a density of ρ = 0.935 g/cm3 and a melting temperature of 124 °C, as given in the Technical Data Sheet (TDS) delivered by the producer. The producer specifies this type of resin as enhanced LLDPE.

Expandable graphite (EG) was purchased from ProGraphite Shop (Germany) of trade names Bläh-Graphit 95 50 350 and Bläh-Graphit 99 32 500, and from Sinograf, named as EG096 (Poland). The details concerning the most relevant features of the used graphite are shown in Table 4.

The graphite was expanded in a nitrogen atmosphere at 250 °C for 3 h in a Nabertherm furnace to obtain powder, which was further used in the PE-based composites.

Sample preparation

Before the next processing steps, the LLDPE powder and expanded EG were mixed at the assumed proportions for 1 h using a shaker mixer.

Samples of = 15 mm, and t = 5 mm

To prepare the thick samples ( = 15 mm, t = 5 mm) for thermal conductivity and dielectric measurements, mixtures of PE and EG powders were extruded using a Haake Minilab II extruder in a co-rotating twin-screw configuration. The resulting filament was chopped into granules, which were then placed into a steel mould positioned on the plate of a hot press preheated to 145 °C. Once the polymer melted, the granules were manually pressed with a spatula to minimise the amount of trapped air. Subsequently, 3 - 4 additional portions of granules were added, melted, and kneaded until the mould was filled. Finally, the material was hot-pressed to form smooth, compact cylinders. Prior to the measurements, one surface of the samples was further lathed and sanded to ensure good contact with the Hot Disk and modified Angstrom method equipment sensors.

Samples of = 60 mm, and t = 2 mm

The samples were prepared by hot pressing. A total of 11–12 g of the PE/EG powder mixture was loaded into a pre-prepared steel mould ( = 60 mm, t = 2 mm), which was positioned between Teflon sheets. The heating plates were set to 145 °C. The pressing cycle was as follows: after the powder was transferred into the mould, an initial pressing was performed for 5 s at a force of 10 kN, after which the plates were lifted to allow degassing of the sample. Subsequently, the sample was pressed again for 20 s under a pressure of 25 kN. The mould containing the sample was then removed from the heating plates and allowed to cool, after which the sample was taken out of the mould. Prior to the measurements, the surfaces of the samples were further sanded to ensure good contact with DAKTL-2.

The samples were prepared in varying EG concentrations in a PE matrix. Three sets of samples were prepared, different from each other in the EG type. The summary of the prepared sets of samples is presented in Table 5; the photography of representative samples is presented in Fig. S1.

SEM

A Quanta 3D FEG microscope was utilised for SEM analysis. Some of the SEM imaging was conducted in low vacuum mode with an accelerating voltage set between 10 and 20 kV. The SEM images presented in the manuscript were captured using the secondary electron (SE) detector.

XRD

The X-ray diffraction profile for the sample was recorded using a Rigaku Ultima IV diffractometer with Co Ka radiation (λ = 1.78897 Å) and operating parameters of 40 mA and 40 kV, with a scanning speed of 1.0°/min.

Density

The buoyancy method of density measurement was exploited using the Radwag PD 210.X2 balance with the density kit. The reference liquid used during measurements was ethanol. The measurements were performed at room temperature, repeating each weighing 5 times and averaging.

Raman spectroscopy

The Raman measurements were performed using an in Via Reflex Raman microscope (Renishaw) equipped with an EMCCD detector (Andor Technology Ltd., Oxford Instruments, Belfast, UK). The Raman signal was acquired using a CW laser with a wavelength of 532 nm. The laser power on the sample was ca. 12 mW. A 50 × (N.A. = 0.75, Leica) magnification lens was used for the measurements. For each sample, three maps of 31 × 21 points were acquired with a step of 1 μm and an exposure time of 1 s, with one accumulation per point. The instrument was calibrated using an internal silicon wafer, and the spectrum was centred at 520.5 cm–1.

The Raman spectra were processed in WiRE 5.5 software. The following operations were performed on the spectra: removal of cosmic rays, smoothing using the Savitzky-Golay function, and background subtraction. The samples were analysed by comparing the intensity ratio of the graphite 2D peak (2717 cm−1) to the intensity of the polymer peak (1129 cm−1).

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC)

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) was carried out using a Perkin-Elmer Pyris 1 power compensation calorimeter equipped with an Intercooler 1 system, within the temperature range of − 20 °C to 130 °C. Each measurement included at least two heating and cooling cycles. The experiments were performed under a nitrogen atmosphere with a flow rate of 20 ml/min. To minimise curve distortion during the investigation of cp(T) and to ensure reliable data collection in both heating and cooling runs, the measurement was conducted in segments: linear temperature ramps were interspersed with isothermal intervals, allowing the sample temperature to stabilise. This procedure enabled high measurement accuracy, within a few percent, for both heating and cooling processes59. The raw measurement data were processed according to the three-curve method to obtain specific heat values60. A 57.45 mg sapphire specimen was applied as the reference. Representative specific heat characteristics were determined by approximating the heating and cooling data of the second measurement cycle. A cubic polynomial was used for approximation.

Thermal properties

Hot Disk

The thermal conductivity of the composites and the neat polymer at room temperature was measured using the transient plane source method. Due to its transient nature, the method used is an indirect method of measuring thermal conductivity. Therefore, the measurement described in the standard essentially provides values for thermal diffusivity and volumetric heat capacity61. The tests were performed using a Hot Disk TPS 2500 S instrument with a Kapton-isolated sensor with a radius of 2 mm. The input power (14–28 mW) and the time of measurement (4–10 s) were adjusted to the dimensions and properties of the samples so that the probing depth and the characteristic time were within the acceptable range (specified by the ISO 22007-2:2022 standard). For each material, at least four measurements at an interval of 40 min were performed.

Thermal diffusivity

The thermal diffusivity process was investigated using the transient method with periodic temperature changes forcing, employing a modified Angstrom method. This approach enables using amplitude attenuation and phase shift data, obtaining two independent values ​​of the tested parameter with high temperature resolution. The linearly variable oscillation base allows for scanning mode testing, which allows for the determination and accurate reproduction of the temperature dependence of thermal diffusivity. Nevertheless, given the non-uniform composition of the samples analysed, the findings should be interpreted as effective or even apparent thermal diffusivity, especially when accounting for pore convection and radiation effects. The experiments were conducted on a custom-designed experimental stand, which was described more precisely62.

The thermal diffusivity measurements within this work were performed within a common temperature range from 10 to 80 °C. In some cases, the range was extended to include low temperatures, from ‒15 °C or higher, up to about 110 °C. Data from these studies justified the possibility of extrapolating the results of other measurements.

The calculation of the effective thermal conductivity in a temperature function was expanded to the range of ‒20 to 80 °C to reveal broader temperature characteristics. The measurement points out of the experimental range were calculated using previously experimentally determined specific heat of each used graphite and LLDPE, experimentally measured density and theoretically extrapolated thermal diffusivity out of the experimental range. The theoretical dependence of effective thermal diffusivity, including extrapolation into the range from ‒20 °C, was calculated based on a linear fitting of the thermal diffusivity data and a 3rd degree polynomial fitting of the DSC data.

Dielectric properties

To assess the dielectric properties of manufactured PE/EG samples, the DAK-TL-2 measurement system from SPEAG, in conjunction with the ZVA67 vector analyser by Rohde & Schwarz, was utilised. Measurement probes are constructed from sections of 50-Ω coaxial transmission cable, with the dielectric material contacted at one severed end. These probes are linked to the VNA to determine the complex reflectance (S11). Derived from the measured reflectance, the dielectric parameters are calculated using the probe’s measuring range, dimensions, and sample thickness. The algorithms are based on the equations developed by Baker-Jarvis. The DAK-TL-P approach is recognised as both the inaugural precise and rapid numerical method for addressing issues about improper integrals, complex functions, and equation inversions. This system synchronises with S-parameter data collection, allowing dielectric values to be computed and visualised in real-time as the system operates. The reflectance data is translated into the complex permittivity of the test material. The DAK software relies on a method introduced by Ellison and Moreau.

All the measurements were performed at room temperature.
