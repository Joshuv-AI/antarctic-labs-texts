Harmonizing Cardiovascular Health Data from National Surveys: Challenges and Opportunities
Authors: Bridget Vermeulen, Lisa J. Ware, Gastón Perman, Innocent Maposa, Darwin R. Labarthe
Published: 2024

To the Editor:

In our recent article,1 we used the American Heart Association’s guidelines2,3 to harmonize cardiovascular health (CVH) data across 5 countries from various geographic world regions and World Bank income levels. We showed that a universal decline occurs in ideal CVH prevalence as people’s age increases, with country-specific factors driving declining health.1 In this letter, we highlight the challenges in harmonizing CVH data from national surveys, and we propose recommendations to optimize CVH data collection going forward.

The American Heart Association developed guidelines to score and track CVH using a combination of metrics (relating to diet, sleep, physical activity [PA], cigarette smoking status, body mass index, blood pressure, glucose, and total cholesterol).2,3 By utilizing a decision matrix and combining scoring criteria, 6 of the 8 CVH metrics (PA level, cigarette smoking status, body mass index, blood pressure, blood glucose level, and total cholesterol levels) were successfully harmonized, and a comprehensive CVH score was created.1 Harmonizing population survey data from countries that have differing income levels is challenging. Data for CVH metrics are not collected routinely across populations, and differences in data-collection methods (for example, the Global PA Questionnaire vs. the PA Vital Sign) may prevent data harmonization.1 The lack of data available for CVH metrics in population-level surveys creates a significant limitation for comparison of the rates and drivers of declining CVH across countries and regions.

The increasing burden of noncommunicable disease motivates the need for primordial prevention strategies.2 Comprehensive and standardized CVH surveys are needed, with clear, harmonized methods to register data and name variables in datasets. To avoid, for example, missing data, appropriate quality-assurance techniques are needed. Many data sources are now widely available, such as the World Health Organization STEPwise approach to surveillance (WHO STEPS), the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES; from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention), the Health Survey for England, and the Brazilian National Health Survey). However, little effort has been made to date to standardize CVH data collection across different surveys. The Global Health and Population Project on Access to Care for Cardiometabolic Diseases (HPACC) is a novel data resource that fills this gap,4 but additional efforts are necessary to encourage broader consideration of this approach in the design of new surveys.

Use of standardized protocols would facilitate research collaboration and enable researchers to compare data without excessive harmonization efforts. An online repository of standardized CVH protocols for population surveys would promote such use.
