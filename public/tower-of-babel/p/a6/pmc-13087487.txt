Mechanochemical Synthesis of Lead‐Free Halide Layered Double Perovskites: A Green and Scalable Approach
Authors: Teresa Ixchel Escobedo‐Perez, Rafael Osorio‐Sotelo, Diego Solis‐Ibarra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe search for nontoxic and stable alternatives to halide perovskites has driven significant research into lead‐free double perovskite compositions. Among these, layered variants offer unique properties, but their synthesis often relies on solvent or heat‐intensive methods. Herein, we demonstrate a scalable and solvent‐free mechanochemical approach for the synthesis of ⟨111⟩‐oriented layered double perovskites (LDPs). Three distinct solid solutions, Cs4Cu1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12, Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12 and Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Bi2Cl12 (x = 0–1), were successfully obtained in batch sizes of up to 5.0 g. All compounds can be obtained in less than 30 min and retain their expected optical properties, highlighting mechanochemistry as a green and scalable route to lead‐free layered perovskites.Keywords: green chemistry, halides, layered double perovskites, mechanochemistry, scalability

Introduction

In recent years, halide perovskites with formula AMX3, where A is a monovalent cation (e.g., Cs+, CH3NH3 +, CH(NH2)2 +, C(NH2)3 +), M is a divalent cation (e.g., Pb2+ or Sn2+), and X is a halide (Cl−, Br−, I−), have garnered a fair amount of attention due to their outstanding optoelectronic properties such as tunable bandgaps, high absorption coefficients, strong defect tolerance, low trap density, long carrier lifetimes, and large charge‐carrier mobilities [1, 2]. Consequently, perovskite‐based optoelectronic devices have shown a remarkable performance, including solar cells, photodetectors, light‐emitting diodes (LEDs), transistors and lasers [1, 3]. Notably, halide perovskite solar cells have reached power conversion efficiencies (PCE) larger than 26%, which is comparable to commercial crystalline Si cells [4, 5]. However, if perovskite technologies are to be commercialized, efforts must be directed towards improving their long‐term stability under heat, humidity, and light exposure [3, 6]. Likewise, it is crucial to replace water‐soluble toxic lead as the de facto M‐site cation as well as finding a more sustainable and scalable synthetic method for these materials, at least in comparison to the conventional solution‐based methods [1, 2, 3, 7].

The main strategies to improve the stability and reduce the toxicity of lead halide perovskites (APbX3) have been their dimensional reduction to make them more stable to heat and humidity [3, 6], and the substitution of lead with more benign metal and semimetal cations [3, 8]. These efforts have led to the discovery of multiple families of lead‐free perovskite‐inspired materials among which we can find double perovskites [1, 9] and layered double perovskites (LDPs) [8, 10, 11, 12, 13]. Double perovskites with general formula A2M(I)M(III)X6 incorporate heterovalent cations: M(I) (e.g., Ag+) and M(III) (e.g., Sb3+, Bi3+) as M‐site cations, considerably expanding the compositional landscape of lead‐free perovskites (Figure 1A) [3, 14, 15]. Continuous exploration of heterovalent substitution and dimensional reduction of double perovskites led to the recent discovery of a new family of lead‐free perovskite‐inspired materials: LDPs [3, 14]. These materials can be understood as the 2D version of double perovskites and are classified as ⟨100⟩, ⟨110⟩, or ⟨111⟩ depending on the direction on which the perovskite‐type inorganic framework is “cut” to produce the dimensional reduction [3].

Schematics of lead‐free halide perovskites with different crystal structures. (A) A2M(I)M(III)X6 3D cubic double perovskite and (B) A4M(II)M(III)2X12 layered double perovskite (LDP). Generated using CrystalMaker [16].

LDPs with formula A4M(II)M(III)2X12 incorporate a divalent transition metal M(II) (e.g., Cu2+, Mn2+, Cd2+) and a trivalent cation M(III) (e.g., Sb3+, Bi3+) as M‐site cations which produces vacancies and therefore a 2D structure (Figure 1B) [3, 14, 15]. Following their discovery in 2017 by Vargas et al., who reported the synthesis and characterization of Cs4CuSb2Cl12, a semiconductor with a direct bandgap of 1.0 eV [10], the number of studies dedicated to ⟨111⟩‐oriented LDPs have significantly increased. This growing interest is largely due to the promising optical, electronic, and magnetic properties exhibited by these materials, coupled with their outstanding stability under humidity, thermal stress, and light exposure [3, 8, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 17, 18, 19]. Remarkably, Cai et al. incorporated Cs4CuSb2Cl12 nanocrystals into a device that functions as a high‐speed photodetector [14], while Yukta et al. identified Cs4CoBi2Cl12 nanocrystals as promising candidates for photoelectrochemical devices [20].

Currently, most halide perovskites (with or without lead) are synthesized by solution processing. Commonly, the halide precursors are dissolved in organic solvents, such as N, N‐dimethylformamide (DMF), γ‐butyrolactone (GBL), and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), to produce thin films, powders, or crystals. As for LDPs, they are commonly synthesized by precipitation in concentrated acidic solutions, solvothermal synthesis or by high temperature solid‐state synthesis. While these synthetic methods have produced excellent results in a relatively simple manner, they have some disadvantages [21, 22, 23]: The limited variety of precursor‐friendly solvents, the use of large volumes of polluting, toxic, corrosive and/or expensive solvents [24, 25], their energy intensive nature, difficult scale‐up, and compositional and stoichiometric restrictions due to the need for a common solvent to all the precursor salts [21, 22, 23]. Thus finding alternative synthetic methods that can overcome all or at least most of these limitations is of great interest.

A promising alternative is mechanochemistry, a synthetic methodology mediated by the direct absorption of mechanical energy to carry out chemical reactions. Its main advantage is that it does not require solvents [2, 7, 21, 22, 23, 26]. At laboratory scale, the most common mechanochemical approach is batch processing by hand grinding (i.e., mortar and pestle), or ball milling in shaker or planetary mills using vessels and grinding balls made of hard materials (e.g., zirconia, agate or stainless steel) [2, 27]. Mechanochemistry is a sustainable method mainly because it mitigates the use of solvents which prevents waste, and significantly reduces the risks and energy consumption associated to the synthesis as well as to the solvent handling, removal and proper disposal [27, 28, 29, 30]. Other advantages of mechanochemical synthesis, in comparison to solution‐based methods, are shorter synthesis times, increased stoichiometric control, phase‐pure products, high yields, reproducibility and easier scale‐up. Also, by avoiding the solubility restrictions of some of the halide perovskite precursors, mechanochemistry paves the way for obtaining novel materials inaccessible by other synthetic methods [2, 7, 27, 28]. Consequently, in 2019 the International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry (IUPAC) identified mechanochemistry as one of the ten technologies that will change the world because it is an eco‐friendlier approach to chemical transformations [31].

Mechanochemical synthesis of halide perovskites has been steadily increasing over the last decade following the study of Prochowicz et al. in which they reported the first mechanochemical synthesis of CH3NH3PbI3 by ball milling [32]. Currently, numerous studies demonstrate that mechanochemistry is capable of producing phase‐pure APbX3 perovskites while maintaining a high degree of stoichiometric control [33, 34, 35, 36]. This becomes evident in studies on halide perovskites with mixed A‐site cations and/or anions (X), e.g., Cs0.05(CH(NH2)2)0.95PbI3 and (CH3NH3)PbCl1.5Br1.5 [34, 35, 36]. It is worth mentioning that the halide perovskites synthesized by mechanochemical protocols preserve their outstanding optoelectronic properties compared to their counterparts synthesized by solution‐based methods [7, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36]. Alongside these efforts, some mechanochemical syntheses of lead‐free halide perovskites have been reported, including 2D layered perovskites: Cs3Sb2Br9 and Cs3Bi2Br9 [37], double perovskites: Cs2NaBi1‐x Fe x Cl6 [1], Cs2AgInBr6 [38], Cs2AgBiBr6 [39, 40, 41], (CH3NH3)2TlBiBr6, and Cs2AgSbBr6 [40], and the ⟨111⟩‐oriented LDP: Cs4CuSb2Cl12 [42]. Beyond demonstrating synthetic scope, mechanochemistry is also widely regarded as an easily scalable method. A notable example is the kilogram‐scale mechanochemical synthesis of CsSnBr1.5Cl1.5 reported by Hong et al., which highlights the scalability of mechanochemical protocols for producing lead‐free halide perovskites for optoelectronic applications [47].

While thin films are most compatible with optoelectronic device fabrication, powders are generally more accessible. Accordingly, there has been growing interest in powder‐based applications and in processing routes that start from powders to produce halide perovskite thin films [35, 39, 43, 44, 45, 46]. For instance, Hong et al. [47] used mechanochemically synthesized Cs2SnBr1.5Cl1.5 powders directly to fabricate a photodetector, whereas Rodkey et al. reported the formation of high‐quality Cs2AgBiCl6 thin films via an all‐dry pulsed laser deposition process using mechanochemically synthesized double perovskites as precursors [39].

The LDP compositions studied here were selected based on their structural robustness, optoelectronic relevance, and suitability for solvent‐free mechanosynthesis. In particular, materials with formula Cs4M(II)M(III)2Cl12 exhibit exceptional stability and compositional flexibility, allowing systematic substitution at the M(II) site while preserving the 2D perovskite structure [3, 8, 10, 13, 14, 15]. The inclusion of Mn2+ introduces optically active centers for probing host‐to‐dopant energy transfer, while Sb3+ and Bi3+ analogs broaden the compositional space accessible by mechanochemistry [3, 11, 12, 17, 18, 19]. Herein, we report the mechanochemical synthesis of ⟨111⟩‐oriented LDPs: Cs4Cu1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12, Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12 and Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Bi2Cl12 (x = 0–1). Moreover, we show that the materials obtained by this green methodology retain their expected optical properties and can be obtained in unprecedented quantities.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization

We synthesized microcrystalline powders of the following families of solid solutions: Cs4Cu1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12, Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12 and Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Bi2Cl12 (x = 0–1) by weighing stoichiometric amounts of the relevant chloride precursors and grinding them either manually or in a planetary mill. First, we implemented a modified protocol based on Singhal et al. mechanochemical synthesis of Cs4CuSb2Cl12 [42] to reproduce the experiment and assess if this method could be applied to other ⟨111⟩‐oriented LDP families. The manual grinding method consisted of weighing stoichiometric amounts of the chloride reagents, manually grinding the precursors in an agate mortar, sintering the resulting powder, repeating, and recovering the product for further characterization. We added sintering steps to eliminate the moisture acquired by the powder during grinding under ambient conditions. Alternatively, the planetary milling method consisted of weighing stoichiometric amounts of the relevant chloride reagents, transferring them to the milling vessel, and milling them automatically to obtain the desired microcrystalline powder. We used agate balls and milling vessels instead of stainless steel to avoid possible contamination from metallic ions.

As a first approach, we synthesized 1.0 g of Cs4CuSb2Cl12 (Table S1) and 0.6 g of each member of the Cs4Cd1− x Mn x Sb2Cl12 and Cs4Cd1− x Mn x Bi2Cl12 (x = 0–1) families (Tables S2 and S3), by the manual grinding method previously described. We used powder X‐ray diffraction (PXRD) to characterize the obtained solids. The corresponding patterns are shown in Figure S1. The experimental diffractograms agree with the simulated patterns indicating that the desired products were obtained with high crystallinity and purity through manual mechanochemical synthesis [10, 11, 12].

Having successfully shown that mechanochemical synthesis is a viable method to obtain LDPs, and since manual grinding is a labor‐intensive method with limited scalability, we implemented planetary milling as to ensure a more uniform, controlled, and efficient milling method to obtain phase‐pure products. At first, we synthesized 5.0 g of each member of the Cs4Cu1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12 (x = 0–1) family (Table S4) and 2.0 g of each member of the Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12 and Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Bi2Cl12 (x = 0–1) families (Tables S5 and S6), at high milling speed: 750 rpm (speed limit for agate vessel and balls) and 60 min of milling time. We used PXRD to characterize the obtained solids, the corresponding patterns are shown in Figure 2. The diffraction patterns of Cs4Cu1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12 (Figure 2A), Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12 (Figure 2B), and Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Bi2Cl12 (Figure 2C) (x = 0–1), showed that the obtained products are highly crystalline and that there are no observable secondary phases for any of these materials [10, 11, 12, 13], thus showing that mechanochemical synthesis via planetary milling can produce ⟨111⟩‐oriented LDPs with high purity. After structural analysis, the morphology of Cs4CuSb2Cl12 (Figure S2), Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12 (x = 0–0.20) (Figure S3) and Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Bi2Cl12 (x = 0–1) (Figure S4) was investigated via scanning electron microscopy (SEM) image analysis. The SEM images demonstrated that their average particle sizes are ∼2 μm with exception of Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Bi2Cl12 x = 0.20 and 1.0, with average particle sizes >4 μm.

PXRD diffractograms of (A) Cs4Cu1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12, (B) Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12, and (C) Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Bi2Cl12 (x = 0–1) families of ⟨111⟩‐oriented layered perovskites with increasing Mn content (x) synthesized by planetary milling.

Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12 and Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Bi2Cl12 (x = 0–1) family members were also studied by UV−vis absorption and photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy. From the corresponding spectra (Figure 3) we concluded that these materials maintain their optical properties (absorption and emission) in comparison to their counterparts synthesized by solution‐based methods [11, 12]. In the Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12 family, the absorption spectra exhibit a single broad band centered at approximately 310–320 nm, which remains nearly unchanged throughout the entire composition range (Figure 3A). This band is assigned to electronic transitions within the [SbCl6]3− octahedra, specifically of the 5s2 → 5s15p1 type. Its diffuse shape indicates that these transitions are not strongly influenced by spin–orbit coupling (SOC) or significant dynamic distortions. The band's width and the absence of doublet features suggest a relatively symmetric local environment and a less structured electronic configuration.

Absorbance (Abs.), excitation (Ex.), and emission (Em.) spectra for (A) Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12, and (B) Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Bi2Cl12 for different x values obtained via planetary milling.

Conversely, the Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Bi2Cl12 family displays a broad absorption band in the UV region as well; however, unlike previous reports—which describe a doublet structure arising from SOC and dynamic Jahn–Teller effects—no distinct splitting is observed here (Figure 3B). This discrepancy may stem from limitations in instrumental resolution, overlap of closely spaced transitions, or local structural perturbations induced by Mn2+ incorporation. Nevertheless, the position and overall shape of the band remain consistent with characteristic Bi3+ absorption. The excitation spectra (PLE) of the Sb‐based compounds show a single, well‐defined peak centered at 320 nm, associated with excitation of the [SbCl6]3− units and indicative of a single dominant energy transfer pathway (Figure 3A). The spectral profile remains virtually unchanged with increasing Mn2+ content, suggesting that the excitation mechanism is largely unaffected by dopant concentration. No additional peaks or significant changes are detected in the visible region, indicating negligible direct absorption by Mn2+ ions. In contrast, the Bi‐based compounds exhibit more complex and structured excitation spectra, with distinct peaks spanning 295–355 nm (Figure 3B). These features coincide with the absorption bands of Bi3+, confirming that the energy absorbed by the host lattice is efficiently transferred to the Mn2+ centers. Moreover, slight variations in the spectral shape with increasing Mn2+ concentration may reflect enhanced Mn–Mn interactions and a more dynamically perturbed electronic environment. As previously reported, the Mn2+ d–d transitions are both symmetry and parity‐forbidden and, thus, intrinsically weak; therefore, they are not shown in Figure 3. For a more detailed discussion of the PL mechanism in these materials, the reader is referred to the reports by Vargas et al. and Holzapfel et al. [11, 12, 48].

PL in both series is governed by the 4T1(G) → 6A1(S) transition of Mn2+ ions in octahedral coordination. Although this transition is formally spin‐ and parity‐forbidden, it becomes partially allowed through magnetic exchange coupling and/or local structural distortions. The partial mismatch between the absorbance and excitation profiles for these materials arises from the selective nature of Mn2+ energy transfer process, as previously reported for Mn2+‐doped LDPs, where only a subset of host‐related electronic transitions effectively sensitizes the 4T1(G) → 6A1(S) transition of Mn2+ centers [11, 12, 48]. For the Sb‐based family (Figure 3A), the emission occurs around 620 nm and reaches maximum intensity at x ≈ 0.20 in accordance to previous reports, indicating optimal energy transfer from the host lattice to Mn2+ at this concentration [11]. Beyond this level, the luminescence intensity decreases due to concentration quenching, most likely caused by Mn2+–Mn2+ interactions that favor nonradiative relaxation pathways. In the Bi‐based family (Figure 3B), the maximum emission appears at 602 nm, corresponding to a slightly shorter wavelength. This shift suggests a less compressed octahedral environment surrounding Mn2+, consistent with the larger volume reported for the MnCl6 octahedron in the Bi‐containing lattice. The highest emission intensity is achieved at a lower dopant concentration (x ≈ 0.10), and the overall emission efficiency is markedly superior to that of the Sb‐based analogs [12].

Optimization of Milling Parameters

With aims of optimizing the mechanochemical synthesis of ⟨111⟩‐oriented LDPs via planetary milling we carried out a series of tests on a 5.0 g‐scale (Tables S7 and S8). On our first test we followed the reaction mixture for the synthesis of Cs4CuSb2Cl12 at a reduced milling speed: 375 rpm, to get a better insight on the phase transformations taking place throughout the milling. We selected Cs4CuSb2Cl12 due to its strongly contrasting optical properties, which allow a rapid visual confirmation of phase formation. In Figure 4A we can appreciate the color evolution of the reaction mixture at different milling times ending with the desired black microcrystalline powder. PXRD analysis confirmed the obtention of the desired phase, the final experimental pattern (t = 100 min) showed an excellent match with the expected diffraction pattern [10], and no evidence of secondary crystalline phases (Figure 4B). We then selected a member of each of the ⟨111⟩‐oriented LDP families. For Cs4Cu1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12 we selected Cs4CuSb2Cl12 (x = 0.0), once again, due to its optical properties, as previously mentioned. For Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12 and Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Bi2Cl12 we selected the members with the highest emission intensity: Cs4Cd0.80Mn0.20Sb2Cl12 (x = 0.20) and Cs4Cd0.90Mn0.10Bi2Cl12 (x = 0.10), since we assumed it would be easier to identify the desired phase formation using UV light (λ = 365 nm). We conducted the syntheses at a high milling speed: 750 rpm and determined that it is possible to obtain Cs4CuSb2Cl12 (Figure 5A), Cs4Cd0.80Mn0.20Sb2Cl12 (Figure 5B) and Cs4Cd0.90Mn0.10Bi2Cl12 (Figure 5C) in short milling times (t ≤ 30 min). The high crystallinity and purity of the products is confirmed by the diffraction patterns obtained through PXRD (Figure 5). Besides eliminating the use of solvents, toxic or hazardous byproducts, or the need for high temperatures, our synthetic method, at 20–30 min, is substantially shorter than any previously reported method. Moreover, even at 5.0 g‐scale, this approach represents, to the best of our knowledge, the largest‐scale synthesis of these materials reported to date.

(A) Color evolution of the reaction mixture processed in the planetary mill at low speed (375 rpm) during the synthesis of Cs4CuSb2Cl12, characterized by its dark color. (B) PXRD diffractograms of Cs4CuSb2Cl12 reaction mixture with increasing milling time.

Color evolution of the reaction mixture processed in the planetary mill during the synthesis of (A) Cs4CuSb2Cl12, (B) Cs4Cd0.80Mn0.20Sb2Cl12, and (C) Cs4Cd0.90Mn0.10Bi2Cl12 at high milling speed (750 rpm), the last two reactions were followed using a UV light lamp (λ = 365 nm). The PXRD diffractograms of the final products are shown on the right.

Conclusions

We demonstrated that it is possible to mechanochemically synthesize ⟨111⟩‐oriented LDPs, such as Cs4Cu1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12, Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Sb2Cl12, and Cs4Cd1−x Mn x Bi2Cl12 (x = 0–1) families, through manual grinding or planetary milling. Both methods effectively produced highly crystalline and phase‐pure materials without the use of solvents, toxic byproducts or high temperatures. Moreover, this method offers short reaction times, as short as 20 min, and large scale, up to at least 5.0 g, although larger amounts should be feasible. Notably, the resulting materials maintained their optical properties in comparison to their solvent‐synthesized counterparts [11, 12]. The ability to synthesize LDPs on a multigram‐scale and in an environmentally friendly way demonstrate their viability as sustainable materials for optoelectronic and energy‐related applications.
