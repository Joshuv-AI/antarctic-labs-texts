Isolated neurosarcoidosis with a primary lesion in the cauda equina
Authors: Yusuke Yoshimura, Junko Kanda-Kikuchi, Takayuki Hara, Izumi Sugimoto
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
A man in his late 50s without notable medical background was admitted with subacute onset of bilateral lower extremity weakness. Blood and physiological examinations revealed no significant abnormalities. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) examination revealed elevated cell count and protein levels and an immunoglobulin G index of 2.01. T1-weighted MRI showed swelling and enhancement of the cauda equina. After admission, the patient developed bowel and bladder incontinence, deteriorated to manual muscle test 0 and developed right trochlear, trigeminal and facial nerve palsy. He underwent a cauda equina biopsy and was diagnosed with neurosarcoidosis. After methylprednisolone pulse therapy and corticosteroid treatment, cauda equina syndrome including lower extremity weakness and cerebral nerve palsy improved. The patient’s daily activities improved to the baseline level over 2 months after discharge. Serum and CSF soluble interleukin-2 receptor levels were within the reference range and decreased with the improvement of neurological and imaging findings.

Background

Sarcoidosis is an idiopathic systemic disease histologically characterised by non-caseating granulomas.1 Neurosarcoidosis is the neuromuscular manifestation of sarcoidosis, and its incidence is symptomatic in about 5% of patients with sarcoidosis2 and about 25% of autopsied patients with sarcoidosis.3 The spinal cord involvement is infrequent, affecting about 6% of neurosarcoidosis.2 Fifty-three cases of spinal neurosarcoidosis were reported including only seven cases with cauda equina involvement.

We here present a case of isolated neurosarcoidosis with a primary lesion in the cauda equina in a Japanese man in his late 50s.

Case presentation

A man in his late 50s was admitted to our hospital to assess the subacute onset of bilateral lower extremity weakness. One month before admission, he noticed difficulty in rising from decubitus and developed weakness in his left lower limb. The weakness worsened and extended to the right side within a month. He became unable to walk 3 days before admission. He had no notable medical, social or family history and no history of prior infection. Orthopaedic specialists ruled out musculoskeletal disorders.

Results of culture growth and viral DNAs in serum and CSF were negative, excluding infectious diseases. The oligoclonal band of serum and CSF, albuminocytological dissociation in CSF and serum anti-GM1 and anti-GQ1b IgG antibody results were all negative. These findings made the possibilities of multiple sclerosis, neuromyelitis optica and chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyradiculoneuropathy, respectively, less likely. Flow cytometry of blood and CSF and bone marrow examination showed no indication of malignant lymphoma. A nerve conduction study showed no abnormalities in amplitude or speed, despite lower extremity weakness, suggesting a potential proximal conduction block. Contrast-enhanced and gadolinium-enhanced MRI scans on day 3 of hospitalisation showed swelling of the cauda equina (figure 2A,B).

Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted spinal MRI on day 3. (A) Sagittal view. (B) Transverse view at L3/4 shows swollen cords of the cauda equina.

The lack of disease-specific positive findings proved challenging to establish a diagnosis. Despite the lack of previous infection and protein cell dissociation, when considering the clinical findings of muscle weakness and reduced tendon reflex of the lower extremity, we speculated that the primary pathogenesis of the disease was similar to that of Guillain–Barré syndrome (GBS). The speculation was further supported by the fact that the proximal conduction block could not be ruled out and the MRI scans showed swelling of the cauda equina. Therefore, we attempted intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg) therapy on days 4–7. However, the IVIg therapy did not halt the progression of neurological symptoms. The patient’s bilateral lower extremity weakness further worsened, reaching iliopsoas, 1+/1+; GM, 0/0; quadriceps, 1/1; hamstrings, 1-/1-; TA, 0/0; and Gc, 0/0 on day 14.

Furthermore, he developed trochlear palsy (diplopia in downgaze) on day 21. The MRI on day 22 showed enhanced bilateral oculomotor nerve (figure 3). On day 30, he developed trigeminal palsy (masseter muscle weakness) and left facial palsy (left mouth dropping). The lack of efficacy of IVIg therapy reduced the likelihood of GBS.

Contrast-enhanced T1-weighted brain MRI on days 22 and 167. (A) Day 22. Bilateral oculomotor nerves are enhanced (arrow). (B) Day 167 (after the introduction of corticosteroid therapy). No enhancement of bilateral oculomotor nerve.

To investigate the cauda equina lesion, suspecting malignant lymphoma, we performed a cauda equina biopsy on day 34. The bone from the inferior margin of L3 to the superior margin of L5 was removed to provide a good field of view and expose the dura mater widely enough. The dura was incised in the midline, and the cauda equina was dissected, revealing that some cauda equina in ventral location were slightly greyish-white and enlarged and seemed to be affected nerves in microscopic view. Figure 4A shows a cord of the cauda equina, the surface of which looks normal, whereas figure 4B shows an enlarged one with swelling, redness and frizzy capillary blood vessels. Both somatosensory and motor evoked potential monitoring showed limited response to the electrical stimulation and dissected about 1 cm length of the cords shown in figure 4A,B. Histological examination of the cord in figure 4A showed no abnormalities. In contrast, the one in figure 4B revealed CD68-positive epithelioid cell granuloma without caseation and sparse and focal small lymphocytic infiltration, compatible with sarcoidosis (figure 4C,D).

Outcome and follow-up

Despite the absence of a final histological diagnosis, due to severe neurological symptoms, we introduced methylprednisolone (mPSL) 1000 mg/day on days 51–53, 57–59 and 64–66, suspecting encephalomyelitis. Whole-body CT scan conducted as infectious disease screening before mPSL pulse therapy revealed the mass lesions in the left subscapularis muscle. Diplopia disappeared on day 54. The patient was able to slightly move his toes on day 59, and bilateral lower extremity weakness gradually improved: iliopsoas, 4-/3-; quadriceps, 4/4+; TA, 4-/4; and Gc, 3+/3+ on day 71. Stable urination and defecation patterns were observed from the same period. Gadolinium enhancement of the cauda equina and bilateral oculomotor nerve in MRI gradually disappeared. We then introduced oral prednisolone 70 mg/day (1 mg/kg/day) on day 76, which was tapered weekly. After introducing corticosteroid therapy, serum sIL-2R levels decreased, whereas ACE and lysozyme levels remained the same. Brain MRI on day 167 showed no enhancement of the bilateral oculomotor nerve.

After the pathological diagnosis of neurosarcoidosis was confirmed based on marked non-caseating granulomas, a systemic examination was performed to confirm no extraneuromuscular lesions. The patient was discharged on day 195 and continued prednisolone treatment at 6 mg/day. At the time of discharge, the MMT results had improved: iliopsoas, 5/5; GM, 5/5; quadriceps, 5/5; hamstrings, 5-/5; TA, 5/4; Gc, 5/5; EHL, 4/5; FHL, 5-/5; EDL, 4-/5-; and FDL, 4/5. Physical therapy was provided throughout the period of hospitalisation, and ADL improved to cane walking at discharge.

The patient’s daily activities improved to the baseline level over a period of 2 months after discharge. He regained the ability to walk without a cane and resumed commuting by train. The patient attended monthly follow-up visits at our outpatient clinic for approximately 2 years.

Discussion

To our knowledge, only seven cases of neurosarcoidosis with cauda equina involvement have been reported (table 2). Including the patient in the current case, three other patients lacked extraneuromuscular involvement. Corticosteroid therapy was applied in all cases, but the outcome varied depending on the case, with some patients improving and others developing vision loss or relapsing.

The patient in the current case was characterised by cauda equina syndrome and trochlear, trigeminal and facial nerve palsy that occurred during the disease. Despite presenting radiological findings at some sites on the neuraxis, these were not symptomatic. Specifically, MRI revealed bilateral enhancement of the oculomotor nerves, and a CT scan detected a mass in the subscapularis muscle.

The absence of extraneural involvements and of disease-specific findings made the early diagnosis difficult. Cauda equina biopsy was invasive but necessary to diagnose neurosarcoidosis and determine the treatment plan.

No specific sarcoidosis serum or CSF biomarkers have been identified yet. ACE, sIL-2R and lysozyme are known to be useful markers but have insufficient sensitivity and specificity to diagnose sarcoidosis.4 This case indicates that the ACE, sIL-2R or lysozyme levels in the reference range do not exclude the possibility of sarcoidosis. In contrast, serum sIL-2R level correlates with the activity of sarcoidosis and can predict progression and relapse after discontinuation of therapy.5 In the present case, serum and CSF levels of sIL-2R declined after initiation of corticosteroid therapy, accompanied by the receding of neurological abnormalities and improved MRI findings.

In conclusion, we present the case of isolated neurosarcoidosis with a primary lesion in the cauda equina in a man in his late 50s. This was a complicated case in which examination results lacked disease-specific findings and cauda equina biopsy played a decisive role in diagnosing neurosarcoidosis. In the case of a patient presenting with clinical cauda equina disease evidenced by MRI enhancement, but lacking definitive diagnosis via CSF or other biomarkers, and exhibiting symptom worsening on empirical anti-inflammatory therapy, clinicians should consider the possibility of sarcoidosis and contemplate performing a cauda equina biopsy.
