Systematic evaluation and meta-analysis of the diagnostic accuracy of ultrasound contrast in predicting the efficacy of systemic therapy for advanced hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Mingsheng Zhu, Hang Li, Xiaolu Liang, Zuobiao Li, Gaohui Chen, Zhijie Zhuang, Haili Huang, Wei Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundContrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) is a dynamic, noninvasive imaging modality capable of real-time visualization of tumor perfusion characteristics. This systematic review and meta-analysis quantitatively evaluated the diagnostic efficacy of CEUS in predicting the response to systemic therapy in advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), aiming to validate its potential clinical value as an imaging biomarker.MethodsDatabases including PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane Library were searched up to May 31, 2025. Diagnostic studies using modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) or Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1 as reference standards were included to evaluate CEUS prediction of systemic treatment response. Two researchers independently screened literature and extracted data, assessing methodological quality using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool. Sensitivity and specificity were pooled using a bivariate random-effects model, and area under the curve (AUC) was calculated via summary receiver operating characteristic (SROC) curve plotting.ResultsFive studies involving 114 HCC patients were included. The pooled sensitivity of CEUS for predicting treatment response was 0.88 [95% confidence interval (CI): 0.66–0.96], with a specificity of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.59–0.96). The SROC curve showed that CEUS demonstrated good discriminatory ability with an AUC of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.90–0.94). Spearman correlation analysis revealed no threshold effect (P≥0.05). Sensitivity analysis results were stable, with no single study significantly influencing the overall outcome.ConclusionsCEUS demonstrates promising potential in predicting the efficacy of systemic therapy for advanced HCC and serves as an effective adjunct to computed tomography (CT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) in evaluating treatment response. However, due to the limited number of included studies and sample sizes, further validation of its diagnostic performance and clinical utility through large-scale, multicenter, prospective, high-quality research is still required.

Key findings

• This meta-analysis confirms that contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) demonstrates promising potential for predicting treatment response to systemic therapy in advanced hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), with a pooled sensitivity of 0.88, specificity of 0.86, and an area under the curve of 0.92.

What is known and what is new?

• Treatment response evaluation in advanced HCC relies heavily on computed tomography/magnetic resonance imaging (CT/MRI) using modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors or Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors 1.1. While CEUS can visualize tumor perfusion dynamically, its diagnostic accuracy for predicting systemic therapy efficacy lacked comprehensive validation.

• This study provides the first quantitative evidence supporting CEUS as an accurate tool for prediction of systemic therapy response, validating its role as a practical adjunct to CT/MRI.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• CEUS can enable identification of treatment responders and non-responders, facilitating timely therapy adjustments and personalized management.

• CEUS demonstrates promising potential in predicting the efficacy of systemic therapy for advanced HCC. Further validation of its diagnostic performance and clinical utility requires large-scale, multicenter, prospective, high-quality studies.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) ranks as the sixth most common malignancy worldwide (865,269 new cases in 2022, or 4.3% of all cancers) and the third leading cause of cancer-related death (757,948 deaths in 2022, or 7.8% of all cancer-related deaths) (1-3). Significant advances have been made in the field of systemic therapy for HCC in recent years. The application of molecular targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has markedly improved overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS) in patients with advanced disease. Systemic therapy has now become the first-line treatment option for advanced HCC (4,5). However, with the expansion of systemic treatment regimens, the spectrum of adverse reactions has undergone significant changes (6), encompassing the digestive, circulatory, hematologic, and endocrine systems. Severe cases may interfere with patients’ daily activities and even trigger critical events such as upper gastrointestinal bleeding, liver failure, or death (7).

Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1 and modified Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (mRECIST) criteria remain the most commonly used standards for evaluating tumor response, both of which rely on contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) or magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) as the primary imaging modalities (8). However, the widespread application of computed tomography (CT) and MRI is constrained by factors such as limited equipment availability, procedural complexity, high cost, risk of allergic reactions (9), renal impairment (10), radiation exposure (11,12), and contraindications related to metallic implants (13). Consequently, there is an urgent need for imaging techniques capable of dynamically reflecting post-treatment alterations in tumor blood flow perfusion.

Contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) has emerged as a research focus for predicting systemic treatment efficacy in HCC due to its ability to display tumor perfusion in real time, ease of operation, lack of radiation exposure, and high reproducibility. Studies indicate that CEUS can predict long-term outcomes by reflecting early changes in tumor perfusion, thereby enabling efficacy assessment and treatment optimization (14). CEUS possesses unique capabilities for quantifying dynamic perfusion parameters such as peak intensity (PI), time to peak (TTP), perfusion slope, and area under the curve (AUC). These parameters reflect tumor angiogenesis and hemodynamic changes, often preceding morphological alterations (15). Despite the immense potential of CEUS, existing research has primarily focused on its diagnostic value, assessment of response to local therapy, and postoperative monitoring of recurrence. Limited exploration has been conducted regarding its ability to predict the efficacy of systemic treatment for advanced HCC (16-19). This highlights a major gap in the literature, underscoring the urgent need for a more comprehensive assessment of CEUS’s diagnostic performance in predicting systemic treatment outcomes. To address this gap, this study quantitatively evaluates the diagnostic performance of CEUS in predicting systemic treatment efficacy for advanced HCC through a systematic review and meta-analysis. This study followed the Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Diagnostic Test Accuracy (Version 2023) guidelines (20) and is presented in accordance with the PRISMA-DTA reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-aw-2395/rc).

Methods

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and the Cochrane Library databases for relevant studies published from the inception of each database through May 31, 2025. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH), free-text terms, and their synonyms using Boolean logic. Key search terms and synonyms included “contrast-enhanced ultrasound”, “hepatocellular carcinoma”, “systemic therapy” (e.g., “sorafenib”, “lenvatinib”, “regorafenib”, “immunotherapy”), and related keywords.

Study selection criteria

Two researchers (M.Z. and H.L.) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts, resolving discrepancies through discussion.

Data extraction

Two researchers (M.Z. and Z.L.) independently extracted data including: first author, publication year, patient characteristics,study design, sample size, treatment regimen type, contrast agent type, follow-up duration, and diagnostic performance metrics (TP, FP, FN, TN). Imaging assessment of tumor treatment response follows RECIST 1.1 or mRECIST criteria: complete response (CR): complete disappearance of contrast enhancement in the target lesion during the arterial phase; partial response (PR): at least 30% reduction in the diameter of the target lesion with contrast enhancement during the arterial phase; stable disease (SD): neither sufficient shrinkage to qualify for PR nor sufficient increase to qualify for progressive disease (PD); PD: target lesions showing at least a 20% increase in contrast-enhanced diameter during the arterial phase. Based on the above radiological efficacy assessment, patients post-treatment were categorized into the response group (CR + PR + SD) and the non-response group (PD).

Quality assessment

Two researchers independently assessed study quality using the Quality Assessment of Diagnostic Accuracy Studies-2 (QUADAS-2) tool, resolving discrepancies through discussion. Assessment covered four bias risk domains (patient selection, indicator testing, reference standard, process and timing) and three applicability domains (patient selection, indicator testing, reference standard). Each domain was categorized as “low risk”, “high risk”, or “uncertain”.

Statistical analysis

Data was analyzed using the Stata 15.0 software (Midas commands) (StataCorp LP, College Centre) and Review Manager (RevMan) 5.4 (The Nordic Cochrane Centre, The CochraneCollaboration). A 2×2 contingency table was constructed for each study, extracting TP, FP, FN, and TN data. A bivariate random-effects model was used to report pooled point estimates of sensitivity and specificity with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Stratified receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted to calculate the AUC. To investigate potential threshold effects, Spearman’s correlation analysis was performed (P<0.05 indicating a threshold effect). Heterogeneity was quantitatively assessed using the I2 index, with I2>50% indicating substantial heterogeneity. Given the small number of included studies (n=5) and the inability of the bivariate random-effects model to reliably estimate covariate effects in such small sample sizes, no subgroup analysis or meta-regression analysis was performed. To validate the robustness of the results, sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding individual studies to assess the stability of the pooled results. Publication bias was evaluated using a Deeks funnel plot with regression line (P<0.10 indicating possible asymmetry).

Results

Literature selection process

The initial search yielded 252 publications. After independent assessment by two researchers, 60 duplicate publications were removed. Based on titles and abstracts, 112 studies were excluded because they were irrelevant to the research topic; based on publication type, 55 were excluded (including 11 animal studies, 36 conference abstracts or reviews, and 8 case reports). Additionally, 2 non-English publications were excluded. Ultimately, 23 full-text articles underwent evaluation, of which 18 were excluded due to incomplete data or inability to extract valid indicators. Five studies involving 114 HCC patients were finally included. The study selection process is illustrated in Figure 1.

Characteristics of the included studies

Table 1 summarizes the descriptive characteristics of the 5 included studies (21-25). These studies were published between 2021 and 2024. Three studies prospectively recruited patients, while two studies retrospectively enrolled participants. The included studies employed various systemic therapies, including tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) combined with anti-programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) antibodies, atezolizumab combined with bevacizumab (T+A), and lenvatinib. Contrast agents also varied across studies, with 4 using Sonazoid and 1 using SonoVue. In 5 studies, CEUS timing after systemic therapy ranged from 1 to 8 weeks, with most studies conducting follow-up 2 to 4 weeks post-treatment. Reference standards differed by study, including CT, MRI, and CECT, with most studies using a 2- to 12-week post-treatment window as the reference standard. Among the patients enrolled, Takada et al.’s study initially included 35 HCC patients receiving atezolizumab and bevacizumab therapy, but ultimately only 28 patients were able to complete both pre- and post-treatment CEUS examinations (22).

Pooled estimates of sensitivity and specificity

Spearman correlation analysis revealed no significant negative correlation between sensitivity and specificity (P=0.67), suggesting no threshold effect. Based on a bivariate random-effects model, the pooled sensitivity of CEUS for predicting systemic therapy efficacy was 0.88 (95% CI: 0.66–0.96), with a specificity of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.59–0.96) (Figure 3). The AUC was 0.92 (95% CI: 0.90–0.94), with the curve shape approaching the upper left corner, indicating excellent overall diagnostic performance (Figure 4, Table 2). Heterogeneity was high for sensitivity (I2=76.77%) across all studies, while it was moderate for specificity (I2=46.62%).

Heterogeneity analysis

In this study, the I2 statistic was used to assess heterogeneity among studies. Overall, heterogeneity was high for sensitivity (I2=76.77%), while moderate heterogeneity was observed for specificity (I2=46.62%). These results indicate that although the overall diagnostic performance was relatively consistent, substantial variation existed in sensitivity across individual studies. Among individual studies, Uchikawa et al. (23) reported the lowest sensitivity (0.55, 95% CI: 0.32–0.77), while Zhao et al. demonstrated the greatest variability in specificity (0.50, 95% CI: 0.01–0.99) (21).

Sensitivity analysis

Given the limited number of included studies (n=5), conducting subgroup analyses or meta-regression was not feasible due to the difficulty in obtaining stable and reliable subgroup effects using a bivariate random-effects model. To validate robustness, sensitivity analysis was performed. By sequentially excluding individual studies, we observed minimal shifts in the 95% CI ranges for pooled sensitivity, specificity, and AUC, indicating overall model robustness (Table 3).

Discussion

Systemic therapy has been demonstrated to significantly improve local and systemic control of HCC (26). sorafenib became the first targeted agent proven to enhance survival in advanced HCC patients, laying the foundation for further advancement in systemic treatment for HCC (27). Following the success of the IMbrave150 trial, the combination of atezolizumab and bevacizumab has become a first-line treatment for advanced HCC, significantly extending OS (19.2 vs. 13.4 months) and improving objective response rates (28). Subsequently, dual-immunotherapy regimens (e.g., CheckMate 9DW) further expanded the treatment landscape for advanced HCC, achieving a 3-year survival rate exceeding 35% (29). However, the relatively low objective response rate (ORR) and disease control rate (DCR) prevent some patients from benefiting from survival improvements (30). These patients face the challenge of drug switching, and without timely and effective predictive tools, treatment delays may occur.

Currently, HCC treatment response evaluation primarily relies on mRECIST and RECIST 1.1 criteria. Key updates in RECIST 1.1 include a reduced number of target lesions and a more stringent definition of disease progression (31), based on changes in the sum of maximum diameters. In contrast, mRECIST evaluates only the viable tumor tissue—specifically, the contrast-enhanced portion of the lesion visible during the hepatic arterial phase—rather than the entire lesion (32,33). Although these techniques provide information on lesion morphology and enhancement patterns, they are limited in capturing the complex biological changes that occur following targeted therapies and immunotherapies. Recent studies have also revealed that myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) may participate in immune escape mechanisms, further complicating the assessment of treatment response in HCC (34). Therefore, identifying an imaging modality capable of accurately predicting systemic treatment response at an early stage holds considerable clinical value.

According to current clinical guidelines (5,35), CEUS is widely applied in the diagnosis, treatment, and follow-up of advanced HCC. A meta-analysis by Wang et al. has demonstrated that CEUS achieves diagnostic accuracy comparable to CT/MRI (36), while offering advantages such as non-invasiveness, radiation-free imaging, repeatability, absence of nephrotoxicity, and low cost (37,38), positioning it as a potential tool for predicting treatment efficacy (39). However, current research remains relatively limited in the field of predicting systemic treatment efficacy. There is a particular lack of large-scale, multicenter prospective clinical studies to validate these applications. Against this backdrop, this meta-analysis represents the first systematic exploration of the role of CEUS in predicting the efficacy of systemic therapy for advanced HCC, providing a critical reference for subsequent research in this field. Our meta-analysis results of this study indicate that the pooled sensitivity of CEUS for predicting systemic therapy efficacy is 0.88 (95% CI: 0.66–0.96), with a specificity of 0.86 (95% CI: 0.59–0.96). with an AUC of 0.92 (95% CI: 0.90–0.94), reflecting potential clinical value. Our findings are further corroborated by another study conducted by Huang et al., which demonstrated that hyperenhancement margins present in CEUS-derived Kupffer phase images serve as a promising imaging biomarker for predicting response to anti-PD-1/programmed cell death ligand 1 (PD-L1) therapy in HCC patients (40). Additionally, a study investigating CEUS-based prediction of combined ICI and anti-angiogenic therapy for unresectable HCC. This study also demonstrated that CEUS quantitative parameters may predict the efficacy of systemic therapy for HCC. Significant differences were observed between responders and non-responders in the rise time (RT) ratio, time-to-peak ratio, decay time ratio, peak enhancement ratio, wash-in rate ratio, perfusion index ratio, and washout rate ratio. Additionally, survival analysis revealed that patients with lower RT ratios exhibited better PFS (41). Compared to traditional imaging methods such as CT and MRI, CEUS provides critical information on tumor hemodynamics, aiding in optimized treatment monitoring and offering supplementary evidence for systematic treatment efficacy assessment.

In patients undergoing systemic therapy for liver cancer, changes in tumor vascular structures and hemodynamics can lead to disruption or even necrosis of tumor-supplying vessels. Reduced blood perfusion slows intravascular flow velocity, increasing the difference between diastolic and systolic velocities (42). These hemodynamic alterations induce corresponding changes in the signal frequency received by the ultrasound probe. Thus, compared to static structural alterations on CT/MRI, CEUS can capture quantitative metrics such as PI, TP, wash-in slope, and AUC in real time, thereby predicting treatment efficacy at an early stage (43). Liang et al. developed a comprehensive nomogram by integrating quantitative parameters derived from early CEUS with baseline clinical and pathological characteristics, enabling accurate prediction of treatment outcomes in advanced HCC patients receiving combined anti-PD-1 and anti-VEGF therapy (44).

The time gap between early CEUS assessment and later reference imaging standards may lead to reclassification of patient treatment response status. Early perfusion improvements may progress before subsequent evaluations, while delayed efficacy may be misinterpreted as early failure. This phenomenon precisely highlights the core value and clinical positioning of CEUS studies: their objective is not to completely replace traditional final efficacy assessments, but to identify potential non-responders earlier, enabling timely treatment adjustments to avoid unnecessary treatment toxicity and financial burden for patients. The variation in assessment timepoints across included studies reflects ongoing efforts in clinical practice to identify the optimal predictive window. Thus, despite temporal misalignment between early CEUS evaluations and subsequent reference imaging, this does not diminish CEUS’s unique potential as an early monitoring tool.

Despite CEUS’s promising potential in predicting systemic treatment response, study results exhibit significant heterogeneity, particularly regarding sensitivity (I2=76.77%). This heterogeneity likely stems from multiple factors. Currently, systemic therapy has become the first-line treatment for advanced HCC. However, first-line regimens are costly, and advanced HCC patients often present with significant tumor burden, varying degrees of hepatic impairment, portal hypertension, and gastroesophageal varices, leading to differences in clinical treatment strategies. Despite this, CEUS demonstrates significant clinical value in monitoring treatment response due to its ability to provide real-time, dynamic assessment of tumor perfusion. Shen et al. confirmed that multiple ICI-based combination regimens effectively prolong median PFS and median OS in advanced HCC patients through different regulatory pathways (45). Secondly, there are also differences among centers in the contrast agents used, operational techniques, and experience in CEUS examinations. The clinically commonly used contrast agents for ultrasound imaging primarily include Sonazoid and SonoVue. Although differences exist in their pharmacokinetic properties and CEUS timing, the hemodynamic information reflected by both agents demonstrates good consistency in evaluating treatment response, providing reliable basis for efficacy assessment (46). A meta-analysis also indicates that different contrast agents exhibit variations in reflecting key enhancement features of HCC (47). Another prospective study based on the Liver Imaging Reporting and Data System (LI-RADS) [2017] guidelines compared the diagnostic performance of the two contrast agents for HCC and also confirmed the differences between them (48). The primary tumor response assessment criteria currently used in clinical practice include mRECIST and RECIST 1.1. Although these two standards differ in their underlying principles and methodologies, they demonstrate good consistency in their overall determination of treatment response (49,50).

Specifically, the extremely high sensitivity heterogeneity implies that the answer to the question “How many treatment responders can ultrasound contrast imaging identify” varies significantly across different studies. For instance, Uchikawa et al. reported a sensitivity as low as 0.55 (23), while Eso et al. reported a sensitivity of 0.90 (25). Therefore, we cannot regard the pooled sensitivity (0.88) as a stable, universal performance indicator. It more likely represents a broad spectrum of performance across different treatment regimens, operational techniques, and patient populations. Clinicians should be aware that when using ultrasound contrast for early prediction at their center, the success rate in identifying effective treatment may carry significant uncertainty. In contrast, heterogeneity in specificity was only 46.62%, with a higher pooled value (0.86). This indicates that despite the wide specificity confidence interval (0.01–0.99) observed in Zhao et al.’s study (21), ultrasound contrast demonstrates greater overall consistency and reliability in the task of “excluding ineffective treatment” (i.e., identifying non-responders). This finding holds significant clinical value, potentially aiding clinicians in early identification of patients unlikely to benefit from treatment. This enables timely adjustment of therapeutic regimens, thereby avoiding unnecessary treatment-related side effects and economic burdens.

In summary, while heterogeneity does not negate the overall value of ultrasound contrast, it clearly delineates its optimal application scenarios: the evidence is more robust when this technology is used as an early screening tool to identify potentially ineffective treatments. However, when employed as a confirmatory tool to predict all effective treatments, greater caution is warranted, with full consideration given to local technical capabilities and patient characteristics.

To our knowledge, this study represents the first systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the predictive performance of CEUS for the therapeutic efficacy of systemic treatments in advanced HCC. There are certain limitations in this study. First, although CEUS has been incorporated into relevant guidelines as an emerging imaging technique, its application in clinical practice for evaluating systemic therapy efficacy remains limited. High-quality evidence is still scarce, and widespread adoption requires time. However, existing studies have demonstrated its promising predictive potential. Second, all included studies were based on Asian populations, and some employed retrospective designs. Given regional variations in the epidemiology and tumor biology of HCC (51,52), the applicability of these findings to Western populations requires further validation. Finally, CEUS results are significantly influenced by operator experience, equipment performance, with the scanning and imaging process being particularly susceptible to operator expertise; simultaneously, patient factors such as obesity may reduce image quality of deep lesions due to acoustic attenuation.

Future research should prioritize the following directions: establishing a standardized parameter system for CEUS-based efficacy evaluation; conducting multicenter, large-sample prospective studies to validate the applicability of CEUS across different treatment regimens; and investigating the combined use of CEUS with other imaging modalities such as CT and MRI to achieve a multimodal, precision-based assessment of systemic treatment response.

Conclusions

CEUS demonstrates potential clinical value in predicting the efficacy of systemic therapy in advanced HCC and shows potential as a complementary tool to CT or MR imaging in assessing treatment response. By providing real-time visualization of tumor perfusion changes, CEUS facilitates early identification of treatment response, thereby offering a basis for optimizing therapeutic strategies and developing personalized treatment plans. However, due to the limited number of included studies and sample sizes, further validation of its diagnostic performance and clinical utility through large-scale, multicenter, prospective, high-quality research is still required.
