Experiences with the use of asfotase alfa in three patients with childhood-onset hypophosphatasia
Authors: Francesca Pigliaru, Davide Noto, Maria Luisa Brandi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryHypophosphatasia (HPP) is a rare inherited metabolic disorder characterized by deficient activity of serum alkaline phosphatase due to loss-of-function mutations in the ALPL gene. Diagnosis confirmation, management, and follow-up of HPP are challenging, and clinical guidance is scant due to the difficulty in gathering high-level evidence. Asfotase alfa is the first long-term enzyme replacement therapy indicated in patients with pediatric-onset HPP to treat the bone manifestations of the disease. In the absence of large clinical studies, sharing clinical observations is an important source of information and support for clinicians. This article presents three case studies. A woman affected by HPP with manifestations of the disease at pediatric age began asfotase alfa therapy at the age of 43 years, resulting in improvements in bone density and epilepsy management. A man diagnosed with HPP in childhood had a severely compromised quality of life. He experienced improvements in bone fragility, respiratory function, sleep quality, and physical function with asfotase alfa therapy. Finally, a woman with infantile-onset HPP, presenting with severe bone deformity, musculoskeletal pain, pseudofractures, and cardiovascular and neurological involvement, showed radiological and clinical improvements in bone health, reduced pain, and increased walking autonomy during asfotase alfa treatment.Learning pointsDelayed diagnosis of HPP is common and can cause morbidity.Asfotase alfa significantly improves clinical outcomes in childhood-onset HPP, even when initiated in adulthood.Asfotase alfa therapy provides benefits beyond bone health.Monitoring treatment response requires a comprehensive clinical evaluation beyond DXA measurements.Real-world clinical experience is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies for HPP.

Background

Hypophosphatasia (HPP) is a rare inherited metabolic disorder caused by loss-of-function mutations in the ALPL gene, resulting in a systemic deficiency of tissue-nonspecific alkaline phosphatase (TNSALP) function (1). Since TNSALP dephosphorylates pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP) (2), elevated circulating PLP levels are a well-established biochemical feature of HPP. The accumulation of PLP, along with inorganic pyrophosphate, a potent inhibitor of mineralization, contributes to defective skeletogenesis and potential hypercalcemia. The renal impairment that can be observed in patients is usually secondary to chronic hypercalcemia (3).

In addition, severe TNSALP deficiency, as could be seen in perinatal and infantile HPP, reduces pyridoxal availability, causing cerebral vitamin B6 deficiency (3) and resulting in seizures (4). HPP severity varies based on residual enzyme activity and age of onset. Early-onset forms are often fatal, with infantile HPP characterized by seizures, respiratory impairments, and high mortality. Late-onset patients present with nonspecific complications, such as recurrent fractures. Limited clinical guidance leads to delayed diagnosis, misdiagnosis, and inappropriate therapy, which can cause progressive organ damage (1). Asfotase alfa is an enzyme replacement therapy approved multinationally for pediatric-onset HPP (5). While clinical trials have clearly demonstrated their efficacy in improving skeletal mineralization, muscle strength, and mobility (6), real-world data, although less controlled, offer valuable complementary evidence on treatment effectiveness and safety in routine clinical practice.

This article presents three asfotase alfa-treated pediatric-onset HPP cases, discussing treatment choices and patient outcomes.

Methods

Asfotase alfa was administered according to the Summary of Product Characteristics (https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/product-information/strensiq-epar-product-information_en.pdf). All procedures were performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients provided written informed consent for treatment and the publication of anonymized clinical data.

Case 1

Case presentation

A girl, born in the late 1970s, exhibited very low levels of serum ALP at the age of 2 months. During childhood, she developed pelvic structural deformity, a limping gait, and severe scoliosis. Rotoscoliosis and musculoskeletal hip pain persisted into adulthood. Periodontitis and recurrent spontaneous tooth loss began when the patient was 22–23 years old, leading to the need for several dental implants. In the same period, the patient experienced two seizures and started treatment with topiramate, which led to seizure control.

In subsequent years, she sustained a fracture of the metatarsus and a spontaneous fracture of the left femur of the pertrochanteric subtype two years before starting the treatment. After one month, bone densitometry performed using a Hologic Horizon A DXA scanner (Hologic Inc, USA) showed osteopenia of the lumbar and femoral sites (lumbar area, T score: −1.4 and Z score: −1.1; L4, T score: −2.0 and Z score: −1.7; femoral neck, T score: −2.1 and Z score: −1.8; and total femur, T score: −2.1 and Z score: −1.8). After 8 months, she experienced a spontaneous fracture of the pelvis while sleeping. Subsequently, a spontaneous fracture of the external femoral condyle occurred due to rotation of the left knee and required surgical intervention with a plate implant.

Investigation

The patient was referred to our specialized center one year prior to treatment initiation (F.I.R.M.O., Florence). HPP was suspected based on her medical history and symptoms; biochemical tests showed low levels of bone ALP (<1.5 μg/L, normal range: 4.9–26.6 μg/L) and low vitamin D levels (15.9 ng/mL, sufficiency range: 30–100 ng/mL). A supplementation with 10,000 I.U. of vitamin D weekly was initiated. No hypercalcemia or renal impairment was detected.

Genetic testing for ALPL gene mutations revealed sequence variants c.262 G>A [p.(Glu88Lys)], associated with childhood HPP, and c.620 A>C [p.(Gln207Pro)], possibly contributing to the autosomal recessive transmission of childhood HPP. Those two identified variants are consistent with a compound heterozygous state, based on genetic interpretation and the observed clinical phenotype, but, unfortunately, parental segregation analysis was not available to confirm this. The diagnosis of pediatric-onset HPP was confirmed based on these results when the patient was 43 years old.

The total ALP level was 16 U/L (normal range: 35–104 U/L), and the bone ALP level was 1.52 μg/L (normal range: 4.9–26.6 μg/L), while the vitamin B6 level was 22.0 ng/mL (normal range: 4.10–43.7 μg/L), with no need for supplementation. An anabolic drug, teriparatide, was prescribed off-label at 20 μg/day subcutaneously, based on published evidence suggesting potential benefits in selected adults with HPP. Three months later, the bone ALP level remained low (3.90 μg/L, normal range: 4.9–26.6 μg/L), and C-terminal telopeptide, a marker of type 1 collagen breakdown, was elevated (1.530 ng/mL, normal range: 0.11–0.74 ng/mL).

The patient was admitted to the intensive care unit several months before starting therapy due to loss of consciousness and recurrent seizures and was then transferred to the neurology unit. A brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scan showed altered vein structures in the left corona radiata, with a hyperintense area in the periventricular white matter on long repetition time sequences. After contrast medium administration, fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) imaging revealed areas of abnormality, specifically an enhancement in the frontoparietal subarachnoid area. A skeletal X-ray showed a comminuted fracture of the tibia, severe rotoscoliosis, deformity of the rib cage, left hip arthroplasty, a non-consolidated fracture of the proximal epiphysis of the left femur, and healed fractures of the ischium and pubic rami. A follow-up brain MRI confirmed previous findings and suggested a diagnosis of status epilepticus associated with HPP. A third brain MRI 5 months before treatment initiation showed improvements. The anti-epileptic therapy was modified, resulting in improved neurologic status.

Four months before treatment initiation, the patient underwent surgical treatment for a left tibia fracture. Despite postoperative rehabilitation, internal rotation of the left leg persisted, and the patient required a four-wheel walker.

Treatment, outcome, and follow-up

At the start of the therapy, a treatment plan for asfotase alfa was approved at a dose of 1 mg/kg/day for 60 kg, administered subcutaneously 6 days per week. Four months after starting treatment, administration was reduced to 3 days per week due to the unavailability of the drug and lack of reimbursement. The prescribed dose has been used since November 2023, and the patient has maintained the clinical improvements achieved without any signs of deterioration. Bone densitometry eight months after starting therapy showed improved scores in vertebrae L1, L2, L3, and L4; the T score increased from −1.4 at baseline to 0. Following initiation of asfotase alfa, no further seizures occurred; the patient returned to work and regained many functional abilities, despite the presence of healed fractures prior to therapy, which still necessitate the use of a wheelchair for long distances. Although neuropsychological testing is currently pending, mood and cognitive functions have improved.

Case 2

Case presentation

A 63-year-old male presented with disease onset in childhood, marked by early tooth loss (before age 4 years) with roots and spontaneous fractures (right wrist at ages 7 and 8 years; tibia at age 22 years; and ribs at ages 43, 49, 50, and 59 years).

In recent years, in adulthood, he reported frequent musculoskeletal pain that was resistant to nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs). Serum ALP levels were low (19–21 U/L, normal range: 46–116 U/L). He experienced coxalgia with limited function of the right hip and NSAID-resistant muscle pain in his limbs and spine. Reduced bone mass was first documented 14 years prior to therapy and was treated with alendronate 70 mg per week orally 10 years prior to therapy. He also had a history of recurrent renal calculi, chalazion, paresthesia, hand weakness, and nasal polyps. The patient was diagnosed with fibromyalgia. He left his job because of limb stiffness and pain and began receiving an invalidity pension. He required cars with automatic transmissions, was severely impaired in daily activities and speech, used auxiliary devices to stand and walk, and was unable to complete the 6-min walk test. He was treated with duloxetine for major depressive disorder, which further impaired his working ability (estimated to be reduced between 74 and 99%). He also experienced tendinopathy consistent with calcific periarthritis and limited joint mobility.

Investigation

He was referred to a specialized center five years before starting enzyme replacement therapy, and genetic analysis demonstrated a heterozygous mutation of c.327C>A (Asp109Glu) in exon 5 of the ALPL gene. No information was available on the genetics or biochemical data of his parents, as they were deceased; the children had normal levels of ALP and vitamin B6. The patient’s adult daughter presented with premature loss of deciduous teeth; genetic testing was negative.

Treatment, outcome, and follow-up

The patient has received asfotase alfa 100 mg/mL, 60 mg 6 days per week, 1 mg/kg/day for 60 kg subcutaneously, since the beginning of 2023, upon approval of the Pharmacy of the University Hospital of Cagliari, Italy. Since the initiation of this therapy, bone fragility has improved, with no further fractures reported. Clinical benefits were also noted in respiratory function, sleep quality, and physical performance, as the patient no longer required a wheelchair and used only a walking stick. Although unable to complete a spirometry test, he showed increased exercise tolerance, even during cardiologic assessments. No adverse events were reported except for short diarrhea episodes.

Case 3

Case presentation

A woman, born in the late 1980s, had no family history of pathological fractures or dental disease. Her father had type 2 diabetes, and her mother had autoimmune thyroiditis. At the age of 1 year, she was diagnosed with neonatal-onset HPP. Symptoms included feeding difficulty, growth retardation (body weight was 4.77 kg at 2 months of age, Z-score: −3.58 (curves WHO 2007)), macrocephaly, diastasis of the cranial sutures, double malleolus, flared thorax with Harrison sulcus, blue sclerae, and generalized hypotonia. The hypothesis of ‘benign prenatal HPP’ is interesting, but her capacity for social interaction was age-appropriate. The patient’s serum ALP level was 48 U/L at approximately 5 months of age (range not available for that laboratory). Her mother’s level was 11 U/L, and her father’s level was 75 U/L (normal range: 46–116 U/L). Thoracic X-ray images suggested the presence of rickets. The mother’s medical history does not reveal any relevant clinical data that could be correlated with reduced ALP levels (no fractures, no musculoskeletal pain, and no dental pathology).

At that time, no specific therapy for HPP was available, and vitamin D supplementation was not routinely recommended, as not always indicated in patients with HPP, including the present case. The medical team informed the family that a fatal outcome was possible within a short time, but if the child survived the first year, the prognosis could become more favorable. The clinical evolution was not odonto-HPP in the postnatal years but a moderately severe infantile form.

The parents were informed that there was a 25% risk of a second affected child, as the transmission was autosomal recessive. A prenatal diagnosis could be achieved by assessing ALP in amniocytes or chorionic villi; however, this assessment has been shown to be unreliable. However, it would be difficult to distinguish homozygous from heterozygous fetuses, given the mother’s low enzyme levels.

Investigation

Four years prior to starting asfotase alfa, genetic testing confirmed the diagnosis, showing a homozygous mutation in haplotype c.787T>C (p.Phe263Leu), c793-31C, c862+20T, c862+51A, c863-12G, c863-7C, and c876G, which is considered benign/likely benign but may contribute to a biochemical phenotype when combined with other variants. Genetic analysis revealed the presence of the c.327C>A (p.Asp109Glu) variant in exon 5 of the ALPL gene, which introduces glutamate at position 109 instead of aspartate. This specific variant has been documented in existing literature and supported by in silico predictive modeling as being associated with hypophosphatasia (7). While these factors point toward potential pathogenicity, the lack of family segregation studies and formal functional assays means its classification cannot be definitively confirmed at this time. Therefore, we interpreted the clinical significance of this mutation by correlating it directly with the patient’s clear biochemical markers and observed physical symptoms. This interpretation, alongside persistently low ALP levels and the clinical phenotype, supported the definitive diagnosis of HPP. This haplotype could be in linkage disequilibrium with a second, unidentified mutation in the coding regions of the gene, as it was identified in patients with only one mutation in the ALP gene. The mother carried these same mutations in heterozygous form.

Treatment, outcome, and follow-up

Therapy with asfotase alfa was initiated when the patient was 27 years old, when the drug became available through special funding from the Italian Agency for Drugs (AIFA). Asfotase alfa, at 80 mg, was administered subcutaneously at the dose of 2 mg/kg, 0.70 mg every 2 days; several doses were delayed due to administrative reimbursement issues.

Six months after the initiation of the enzyme replacement therapy, the patient regained full weight-bearing capacity in the right leg, where a tibial fracture had remained unhealed for 1 year and had required surgical treatment. The orthopedic and pediatric teams observed improvement based on clinical and radiological signs. Unfortunately, we do not have sequential X-rays of the tibia from the years when the patient had difficulty healing her fractures; fortunately, since she began treatment with asfotase alfa, she has not suffered any further fractures.

During periods of asfotase alfa discontinuation, occurring 1 and 2 years after treatment initiation, vitamin B6 levels were 223 μg/L and 1,204 μg/L, respectively (normal range: 3.6–18 μg/L). Vitamin B6 was analyzed in a private laboratory in Cagliari (Sardinia). The therapy has continued uninterrupted since the second year of treatment, and the vitamin B6 level has decreased to 6.7 μg/L. Muscle strength has increased, no new caries or fractures have occurred, and walking autonomy has improved. The patient no longer reports muscle or bone pain, and the frequency of headaches has been reduced. In 2022, during therapy, the patient showed an excellent response by completing the 6-min walk test without support devices, walking 300 meters in 5 min. Arterial hypertension, which affected the patient from the age of 9 years and for which she was treated with calcium antagonist therapy, is now controlled. The health assessment questionnaire (HAQ) showed improvements after therapy initiation, particularly in the areas of performing physically demanding activities (such as climbing stairs, walking >1 km, and carrying loads). The therapy was well tolerated, with no treatment-related adverse events.

As of this report, the patient is no longer using NSAIDs, is employed in a library, and has an improved quality of life. She reports no difficulties in social relationships.

Discussion

These case reports present examples of adult patients with pediatric-onset HPP treated with asfotase alfa to support decision-making and optimize care for this rare condition.

Small clinical studies have demonstrated the safety of asfotase alfa in HPP children and its efficacy in improving respiratory function and bone mineralization (8, 9). Even when initiated later, this therapy significantly improved symptoms, and symptom worsening was observed during therapy discontinuation (9).

In our work, only short periods of therapy discontinuation characterized the clinical history of patient 3, associated with increased serum vitamin B levels but not with symptom recurrence.

In patient 1, no effects were observed after reducing the dosage to 3 days per week for 1 month. Patient 1 began therapy at 45 years old and patient 2 at 63 years old; both experienced a significant improvement in their condition and quality of life, as did patient 3, who started therapy at 27 years old. No fracture was observed during enzyme replacement therapy, although the three patients had had several fractures before.

Dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) may be unreliable for assessing bone mineral density in HPP patients, not only due to disease complexity but also because of the evolving nature of bone remodeling during treatment (10). Therefore, complementary imaging and clinical assessments are recommended to more accurately evaluate treatment efficacy.

While objective measures such as the 6MWT and HAQ were utilized in some cases, the retrospective and real-world nature of this series meant that standardized tools, such as the SF-36 or BOT-2, were not systematically collected. Furthermore, the applicability of performance-based tests such as the BOT-2 or 6MWT may be limited in patients with significant baseline motor impairments or those relying on assistive devices. In such instances, potential therapeutic benefits might be masked by chronic functional deficits, reducing the sensitivity of these tests to detect meaningful clinical improvement.

This case series underscores the severe and cumulative impact of pediatric-onset hypophosphatasia when left undiagnosed into adulthood, as well as the critical importance of early recognition and intervention to improve long-term outcomes.

Our experience suggests that asfotase alfa is well tolerated and effective in reducing disease burden, even when initiated in adulthood.
