Concomitant Pulse Field Ablation Based LAA Isolation Plus LAAC
Authors: Christian-Hendrik Heeger, Martin W. Bergmann
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Pulmonary vein isolation (PVI) remains the cornerstone of atrial fibrillation treatment by catheter ablation. However, several studies identified the left atrial appendage (LAA) as a potential target for catheter ablation via LAA isolation, especially in patients not responding to PVI.1 Because of a high risk for thrombus formation and stroke after LAA isolation, LAA occlusion via interventional LAA closure (LAAC) is crucial for these patients.1, 2, 3 Although radiofrequency-based LAA isolation showed relatively good results, cryoballoon-based LAA isolation was shown to be faster and safer.4,5 Recently, pulsed field ablation (PFA) has been introduced as a novel energy source for cardiac tissue ablation primarily for the treatment of atrial fibrillation.6,7 Its primary benefit is improved safety through myocardial tissue–specific irreversible electroporation.8 However, recent findings on long-term durability after PFA-based LAA isolation showed very poor results in comparison to radiofrequency- and cryoballoon-based techniques.9 The recent case shows that PFA is able to treat LAA-originated atrial tachycardias (AT) and durably isolate the LAA.10

The case report by Komatsu et al10 showed an interesting case of LAA-derived AT in a patient with durable PVI successfully treated by PFA-based LAA isolation. Because of the known high risk for thrombus formation and stroke after LAA isolation, the authors performed concomitant LAAC after successful LAA isolation. The LAAC procedure was planned via computed tomography, and a 40-mm WATCHMAN FLX Pro device (Boston Scientific) was chosen. Because of an observed circular edema around the LAA orifice, downsizing to 35 mm was discussed but finally the initially chosen 40-mm device with a high compression of 29% was successfully implanted. After 1 month, the edema had disappeared and a final compression of 15% was measured via computed tomography. Furthermore, no recurrence of AT occurred within 6 months of follow-up, suggesting the chosen strategy to be successful. From the authors’ perspectives, this case report shows that concomitant PFA-based isolation and LAAC were feasible, although a circular edema was observed. Preprocedural imaging and sizing were useful key factors for success, and the decision for accepting a higher compression was correct. This observation may help in planning further concomitant PVI/LAA isolation and LAAC procedures in the future. Recently, PVI and LAAC for stroke prevention are increasingly performed as concomitant procedures. There are several studies that investigated a combined concomitant approach of performing a thermal-based PVI and LAAC in a single procedure, and safety and efficacy have been shown.11, 12, 13, 14 The recently published OPTION trial showed that PVI and LAAC (either sequential or concomitant) were noninferior to oral anticoagulation with respect to a composite of death from any cause, stroke, or systemic embolism at 36 months.15 However, only thermal-based PVI has been used and only 40.8% of patients have been treated concomitantly. The novel ablation modality of PFA has significantly reduced procedure duration and potentially increased safety because of specific ablation of cardiac tissue with sparing smooth muscle cells and nerval cells.16 Only a few case reports and registries are available for concomitant PFA-based PVI plus LAAC.17,18 The multicenter COCONUT study will present more data on this important topic from a large-scale patient population (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier NCT06861673). The findings of this case should be taken into consideration when planning concomitant PVI plus LAAC or LAA isolation plus LAAC procedures.
