Strategic analysis of the health information system for communicable disease control in Afghanistan
Authors: Enayatullah Hayat, Narges Neyazi, Hossein Dargahi, Ebrahim Jaafaripooyan, Ali Mohammad Mosadeghrad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA well-functioning Health Information System (HIS) is a foundational component for controlling communicable diseases (CDs), especially in fragile and conflict-affected settings like Afghanistan. However, Afghanistan’s HIS suffers from structural, technical, and political challenges that compromise its ability to deliver real-time, accurate, and integrated health data. This study aims to conduct a strategic SWOT analysis of Afghanistan’s HIS in managing CDs and to propose targeted solutions to improve its performance and responsiveness.MethodsThis qualitative study used the Framework Method to explore perceptions of the Afghan health information system’s HIS performance in managing CDs, guided by the World Health Organization HIS framework and SWOT analysis. Forty-nine semi-structured interviews with diverse health stakeholders were conducted, analyzed inductively and deductively to identify key themes. Rigor was ensured through triangulation, independent coding, and reflexive analysis.ResultsThe study identified 11 strengths, 20 weaknesses, 9 opportunities, 12 threats, and 22 solutions. Key strengths included the availability of digital tools (e.g. DHIS2) and well-established surveillance programs. However, systemic weaknesses such as lack of real-time reporting, underreporting from the private sector, limited research on CDs, technical staff shortages, and low community awareness persist. Opportunities include donor support and digital innovations, while threats like political interference, funding cuts, and brain drain severely hinder HIS functionality.ConclusionAfghanistan’s HIS has a solid foundation but faces operational challenges. To enhance its effectiveness in CDs control, national efforts should prioritize infrastructure upgrades, workforce capacity-building, digital integration, and localized innovations. Implementing these targeted solutions can help Afghanistan transition toward a more resilient, reliable and real-time health information system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14372-x.

Introduction

A Health Information System (HIS) encompasses an organized framework designed to systematically gather, manage, process, share, and apply health data in ways that inform decisions across all tiers of the health sector. As a foundational component of an effective healthcare system, it plays a critical role in supporting planning, management, and policy formulation [1]. In fragile contexts like Afghanistan—where CDs remain highly prevalent [2]—a resilient and integrated HIS is essential for outbreak detection, resource allocation, and coordinated health responses.

Afghanistan continues to experience a high burden of CDs, with tuberculosis affecting approximately 75,000 people annually and causing nearly 10,000 deaths [3]. In 2024 alone, the country reported over 81,000 malaria cases, 59,000 measles cases, 175,000 acute watery diarrhea (AWD) cases, and thousands of cases of COVID-19, dengue, CCHF, and acute respiratory infections (ARI) [4]. These diseases contribute significantly to morbidity and mortality, especially among children and vulnerable populations.

Afghanistan’s geography, with its mountainous terrain and predominantly rural population, poses logistical challenges for health service delivery and real-time data collection [5, 6]. While electricity reaches over 85% of the population [7], it is often unstable and largely reliant on solar energy, particularly in off-grid regions [8]. Similarly, telecommunication coverage exceeds 90% [9], but internet connectivity remains unreliable in remote areas [10]. These limitations directly impact the functionality of HIS, though solar power and mobile technologies offer promising opportunities for decentralized digital health solutions.

The country’s health system is shaped by political instability, donor dependency, and economic fragility, reflected in its low Human Development Index (0.462) [11] and GDP of approximately US$14 billion [12]. External funding, which sustains key programs, has declined since 2021 due to sanctions and shifting donor priorities [13–15].

Despite these constraints, the Ministry of Public Health (MoPH), supported by international partners, has introduced significant reforms. Key initiatives include the Basic Package of Health Services (BPHS) and the Essential Package of Hospital Services (EPHS), which are delivered through a tiered network that ranges hierarchically from community-level health posts to regional hospitals [16]. Digital and surveillance systems such as the Health Management Information System (HMIS), District Health Information Software 2 (DHIS2), Integrated Disease Surveillance and Response (IDSR), and Health Resources and Services Availability Monitoring System (HeRAMS) have been introduced to enhance data-driven planning and coordination [17–19].

HMIS supports data standardization, reporting, and service monitoring, while DHIS2 aims to unify fragmented digital systems and improve access to real-time health data [15, 18]. The National Disease Surveillance and Response (NDSR) system monitors 17 notifiable diseases through indicator-based surveillance, though event-based components remain underdeveloped [21]. Persistent challenges—including limited flexibility, poor data quality, delayed reporting, and insufficient private sector engagement—undermine the system’s performance [22, 23].

In this context, a well-functioning HIS is crucial for Afghanistan’s ability to detect outbreaks early, coordinate responses, and meet global health security commitments. A unified system not only enhances coordination and accountability in a heavily NGO-dependent health sector but also supports continuous disease surveillance and resource optimization.

Given this background, the study examines the structure, performance, and contextual barriers of Afghanistan’s HIS for CDs management. Using a qualitative approach grounded in stakeholder perspectives and guided by the WHO HIS building block, it identifies key strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats, and strategic solutions to support evidence-based policy and international assistance in fragile settings.

Methods

Study design

This qualitative study employed the Framework Method [24] to explore how stakeholders within the Afghan health system perceive and experience the performance of the HIS in managing CDs through semi-structured interviews. The Framework Method is appropriate for applied health research involving multiple participant groups and enables a structured approach to thematic analysis. The HIS building block of the WHO’s health system framework was adopted to guide the analysis, focusing on domains such as system development, data collection, analysis, utilization and dissemination, quality assurance, and information security [25].

The interview guide was developed using the SWOT framework and HIS subcomponents to ensure systematic exploration of strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats related to HIS performance.

Participant recruitment and sampling

Participants were purposively selected from across the Afghan health sector to ensure a wide range of perspectives. This included policymakers, NGO staff, health facility managers, and frontline service providers with direct or indirect involvement in HIS and CD-related activities. Snowball sampling was additionally employed to identify further informants. Inclusion criteria required a minimum of three years of professional experience in the health system, as well as current or recent engagement with HIS operations or policy.

Data collection

Data were collected through 49 semi-structured interviews conducted between February and September 2024. Interviews were conducted in Dari or Pashto and subsequently translated into English for analysis. A pre-tested interview guide was used to explore participants’ experiences and perceptions of HIS performance, along with elements related to the SWOT domains. Interviews lasted between 15 and 44 min (mean duration approximately 30 min) and were audio-recorded following verbal informed consent. All interviews were transcribed verbatim, translated into English, and anonymized prior to analysis. Identifying information, including demographic characteristics that could compromise confidentiality, was removed from the transcripts.

An anonymized version of the interview text is provided in Supplementary File 1 (CD Management interview text (1)) to enhance transparency and support the credibility of the analysis.

Interviews were continued until thematic saturation was reached at the level of core categories relevant to the study objectives, defined as the point at which no new major themes related to HIS performance and system-level challenges emerged. While saturation was achieved for identifying key weaknesses and strategic priorities, the study did not aim to elicit detailed, implementation-level mechanisms for proposed solutions. As such, the depth of the data reflects participants’ strategic and policy-oriented perspectives rather than operational guidance.

Data analysis

Verbatim transcripts were analyzed using the Framework Method. The analytic process included transcription, data familiarization, open coding, development of a coding framework, matrix charting, and interpretive synthesis [24]. An inductive approach allowed themes to emerge organically from participants’ narratives, ensuring context-specific insights. These themes were then structured deductively using the HIS subcomponents and SWOT framework to align findings with policy-relevant domains and support strategic decision-making.

Coding followed a systematic procedure. Initially, transcripts were read repeatedly for familiarization, and open coding was applied to identify meaningful units of text. A preliminary coding framework was developed inductively and iteratively refined through team discussions. Two researchers independently coded a subset of transcripts, compared coding decisions, and resolved discrepancies through consensus to enhance analytic rigor. The finalized coding framework was then applied to all transcripts and charted into an analytic matrix.

In line with the Framework Method described by Gale et al. (2013) [24], data were organized into a matrix format to support systematic comparison across cases and themes. Rows represented individual participants, while columns represented codes and higher-order analytic categories derived from the thematic framework. This matrix was used iteratively to chart summarized data, compare patterns across participants, and support interpretive synthesis across HIS and SWOT domains. Due to confidentiality considerations and space limitations, the full matrix is not presented; however, its use informed all stages of the analysis and interpretation.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval for this study was obtained from the Tehran University of Medical Sciences Ethics Committee (IR.TUMS.SPH.REC.1400.262). All participants gave verbal informed consent after being briefed on the study’s aims, the voluntary nature of participation, confidentiality measures, and their right to withdraw at any time. All transcripts were anonymized, and data were securely stored.

To ensure the trustworthiness and credibility of our findings, several strategies were employed throughout the study. Data triangulation was achieved by including participants from multiple institutional levels and professional roles, providing a comprehensive view of HIS performance in managing communicable diseases. Informal member checking was conducted during interviews to confirm that participants’ intended meanings were accurately captured. To enhance analytic reliability, two researchers independently coded the transcripts, and discrepancies were resolved through discussion. An audit trail was maintained to document decisions and support transparency in the analytic process.

Results

The study involved 49 participants: 96% male, majority with MD or master’s degrees, aged primarily between 40 and 60 years, and predominantly having 10 to 20 years of experience. Participants included policymakers, service providers, NGO workers, managers (of public health facilities, including district and provincial health centers, hospitals, and other health service delivery units), and medical university lecturers. These participants were selected because they oversee HIS operations at the facility level, are responsible for data reporting, quality assurance, and coordination of communicable disease (CD) management activities, and thus provide critical insight into both operational and systemic aspects of the HIS. In addition to managers, our study included policymakers, NGO staff, service providers, medical university lecturers, and a parliamentarian to ensure diverse perspectives across different levels of the health system. Participants were purposively selected to capture variations in role, experience (minimum three years in the health system), and engagement with HIS-related activities, enabling a comprehensive understanding of HIS performance and challenges in managing CDs. HIS development

According to a participant answers, the strengths of the HIS are the platforms such as DHIS2, HMIS, and NDSR, form the backbone of national health data management, enabling collection, storage, and analysis across all levels of care, including community/village health posts, basic and comprehensive health centers, district hospitals, and provincial and national referral hospitals (p.11). In the domain of disease surveillance, systems such as DEWS, IDSRS, syndromic surveillance for Acute Flaccid Paralysis (AFP), and NTP focus on outbreak detection and specific disease monitoring. Supporting these systems are population and facility-based health surveys like AHS, DHS, MIS, and SPA, which gather periodic, representative data on health outcomes, service quality, and access. Based on participants’ responses, these systems are generally effective but face operational limitations at local levels.

Conversely, this system faces multiple weaknesses, including limited technical expertise, lack of real-time reporting, outdated infrastructure, and partial coverage of platforms like DHIS2. Several participants highlighted that the absence of female educators and a donor focus on a narrow set of CDs further limits system inclusivity and effectiveness. Threats such as brain drain, political interference in hiring process of managers, funding cuts, public unawareness, and the government-imposed ban on female education also undermine sustainability. However, opportunities exist in donor interest in CDs and the potential use of social media and television for awareness campaigns.

Over the past two decades, Afghanistan has made notable progress in establishing a foundational digital health infrastructure. One Public Health Expert stated, “The best achievement of the last two decades in Afghanistan’s health sector is the development of a strong surveillance system for diseases like TB, polio, and malaria” (p.3). A WHO Officer noted, “DHIS2 works well for certain programs, but it still doesn’t include Afghan National Program to Combat AIDS, HIV and Hepatitis (ANPASH) data. This is a major gap” (p.8).

According to participants’ responses, several priorities were identified to address existing challenges in the health information system. These included improving data-related training and IT infrastructure, recruiting qualified HIS managers and securing incentives through stakeholder negotiation, enhancing the timeliness of communicable disease surveillance through internet-based tools such as WhatsApp, and regularly updating digital systems.

Based on the results, participants also emphasized the importance of promoting awareness in local languages, leveraging trusted community figures—particularly female educators—mandating free media messaging, advocating for allowances to support female education, and aligning donor efforts with national health priorities.

Information collection

According to participants’ responses, information collection within Afghanistan’s HIS benefits from established mechanisms such as surveillance conducted through the BPHS and the EPHS, along with the use of DHIS2 as a nationwide data collection platform. Additionally, institutional structures like GD ME&HIS contribute to data management at the national level. Despite these systems, challenges remain—particularly the underreporting of CDs by the private sector and reliance on self-reporting systems, which limit data completeness and reliability. Several participants reported that the lack of an accurate population estimate, making it difficult to calculate disease burden and coverage rates. Social factors such as health-related stigma also discourage individuals from reporting illnesses or seeking care. However, opportunities exist to improve data collection through mHealth innovations and real-time digital platforms.

Data collection is still hampered by weak cooperation and systemic overload. As a Public Health Expert observed, “Many private clinics do not cooperate with the MoPH for CDs reporting — they fear penalties or taxes” (p.37). Additionally, the system’s capacity is overstretched. One participant described the strain on reporting mechanisms: “Because clinics are overloaded, it’s impossible to report all cases. Each doctor visits up to 100 patients daily” (p.5). Infrastructure poses another barrier, as a MoPH Staff Member remarked, “Our system is still mostly paper-based, and many roads are impassable for report collection” (p.18), underscoring logistical challenges in remote regions.

Solutions may include the use of population-based statistics, awareness campaigns to reduce stigma, and incentives that encourage private sector participation in disease reporting (strengthening public–private partnerships).

Information analysis

Information analysis in Afghanistan’s HIS benefits from the presence of trained analysts and the availability of national and international studies based on the country’s CDs data. However, the process faces several challenges. Many health centers lack advanced digital tools and data analytics software, which limits the depth and speed of analysis. Many participants noted that data is often reviewed only on a quarterly basis, reducing its usefulness for timely decision-making. In addition, poor data quality further complicates analysis, weakening the foundation for evidence-based interventions.

Despite these challenges, there are promising opportunities. Emerging technologies such as AI, IoT, and advanced data platforms offer significant potential to transform analysis. Yet, there are some systemic barriers that cause frequent system outages due to unreliable electricity and internet access in many areas. These barriers also negatively affect data quality assurance and security.

Analytical capacity is hindered by delays and technological constraints. A Policy Maker noted, “We receive data quarterly, not daily. By the time it’s analyzed, the situation may already have changed” (p.40), emphasizing the need for more real-time analysis. While there is hope for future innovations. A Hospital Manager pointed out, “IoT and smart tools would be ideal, but without internet and electricity in rural areas, it’s not realistic yet” (p.22), highlighting the disparity between potential and practicality in low-resource settings.

Based on participants’ answers, practical steps include installing solar panels at health centers to ensure stable electricity, amplifier antennas to enhance internet connectivity in underserved areas, investing in digital technology, and promoting routine use of data in decision-making.

Information use and distribution

According to participants’ responses, information use and distribution within Afghanistan’s HIS shows some strengths, such as data-driven planning by the MoPH, which reflects a growing institutional commitment to evidence-based decision-making. However, limitations remain. Academic research is still sparse, and health data is underutilized in program evaluation processes. These challenges are further compounded by weak interdepartmental coordination, which hinders data sharing and collaborative planning.

Efforts are made to utilize existing data for planning. As one MoPH Director stated, “Although flawed, the data is still used in national planning. We make the best of what’s available” (p.42).

Despite these constraints, there are valuable opportunities to strengthen the use of health data. Regional partnerships can support knowledge exchange and capacity-building, while advanced analytics offer the potential for trend-based predictions that could enhance public health responses.

However, a lack of institutionalized research practice affects data use at a broader level. According to an Academic, “University professors aren’t required to conduct research, which weakens data use for policymaking” (p.1), stressing the need for structured academic engagement.

To address these challenges, stronger research mandates are needed—particularly policies that encourage academic research in universities and hospitals, including integrating research activity into staff promotion criteria.

Information quality assurance

Based on participants’ responses, Afghanistan’s HIS benefits from the built-in data validity features of DHIS2, which support more reliable and standardized reporting. This strength helps ensure that the data collected, particularly on infectious diseases, maintains a level of internal consistency and verification.

Digital platforms like DHIS2 provide reliable data frameworks, yet infrastructural issues affect data quality and timeliness. A Provincial Health Officer explained, “DHIS2 has good validity, but the local clinics often lack electricity or internet to send timely data” (p.29), revealing the disconnect between system capacity and local realities.

Despite this, the system faces several weaknesses. Many indicators within the HMIS are poorly defined or outdated, limiting their usefulness for monitoring and evaluation. Additionally, much of the data is still collected and reported manually, increasing the likelihood of errors and delays. Compounding these issues is the lack of trained staff and appropriate tools, particularly at the district level, where frontline data collection is most critical.

There are, however, meaningful opportunities to strengthen quality assurance. Engaging communities in the process of data verification can improve both accuracy and trust in reported figures, especially for sensitive disease categories.

The system also contends with significant threats, including frequent staff turnover, which disrupts continuity in data handling, and abrupt system outages due to electricity and internet disruption that can result in data loss or reporting delays.

According to participants’ answers, potential solutions include building local capacity through targeted training and resource allocation, as well as enhancing HMIS indicators to ensure they are better aligned with disease surveillance needs and international standards. Information security

Based on participants’ responses, Afghanistan’s HIS has taken important steps toward improving information security, including the implementation of role-based access control, which ensures that sensitive data is only accessible to authorized personnel. This is a key strength in maintaining confidentiality and data integrity within the system. However, significant weaknesses remain. Frequent device failures and a shortage of IT maintenance staff undermine the stability and reliability of the system, especially in rural or resource-limited areas.

Despite these gaps, there is clear potential for secure digital transformation. With the right investments and planning, the system could evolve to provide safer, more efficient digital health data management.

Still, several threats pose ongoing risks. Roadblocks, natural disasters, and other disruptions can lead to data loss or hinder secure data transmission, especially when digital backups are not in place. - Data security remains fragile due to reliance on manual processes. A District Health Officer shared, “Most data are sent manually. If there’s a flood or roadblock, it’s often lost forever” (p.16), emphasizing the urgent need for digital backups and resilient infrastructure to ensure data protection in emergencies.

According to participants’ answers, key solutions include hiring dedicated technical staff and eliminating data redundancy while enhancing security protocols through platforms like DHIS2. These efforts would help ensure that health data, particularly related to infectious diseases, is both protected and readily accessible when needed.

Discussion

This study explored the strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats of the HIS in Afghanistan for CDs management. Drawing on perspectives of stakeholders from government, NGOs, academia, and service providers, the findings present a nuanced picture of a system with great potential but also significant gaps. In this discussion, we contextualize these findings using global evidence from similar low- and middle-income and post-conflict countries.

HIS development and system integration

Afghanistan’s integration of DHIS2, HMIS, and national surveys (AHS, DHS) stands out as a key strength. As one public health expert noted, “The best achievement of the last two decades is the strong surveillance system for diseases like TB, polio, and malaria” (p.3). However, gaps remain in coverage — for example, the Afghanistan National Program to Combat AIDS and Hepatitis (ANPASH) is still not included in DHIS2. This mirrors issues found in countries like South Sudan, where fragmented donor-supported vertical programs have delayed full national HIS integration. Therefore, it is essential to increase domestic budget support for health security and system strengthening [25].

Data collection barriers

Overburdened health workers, underreporting from the private sector, and reliance on paper-based systems undermine data collection. As one ex-chancellor stated, “Clinics are overloaded… a single doctor sees up to 100 patients daily” (p.5). Similar issues have been observed in China, where more than 25% of tuberculosis cases documented in hospital records were never reported to the national TB surveillance system, resulting in an underestimation of the country’s true TB burden [26]. In Ethiopia, use of mHealth tools by health extension workers improved data timeliness and completeness even in remote areas [27]. Afghanistan could adopt a similar model, beginning with digitally-enabled basic reporting tools and community health worker support.

Information analysis and use

Although specialists exist, the study revealed that data is often under-analyzed and decisions are not informed by real-time insights. A policy maker remarked, “We receive data quarterly, not daily. By the time it’s analyzed, the situation may already have changed” (p.40). This issue is consistent with findings in Uganda, where slow data turnaround hindered outbreak response [28]. Meanwhile, the use of AI and machine learning for disease detection and surveillance has greatly enhanced public health outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa [29]. In Afghanistan, investment in training and simple analytical tools could be a foundational step toward smarter, data-driven decisions.

Quality assurance and infrastructure

Stakeholders acknowledged the value of DHIS2 but cited unreliable electricity, lack of internet, and weak validation systems in peripheral clinics. As a provincial officer mentioned, “DHIS2 has good validity, but local clinics often lack electricity or internet to send timely data” (p.29). A document review found that implementing solar energy in healthcare system proved effective in addressing energy shortages in India, sub-Saharan Africa, and comparable sittings [30]. Afghanistan may benefit from piloting such hybrid systems in rural provinces.

Data security and system resilience

Manual data transport and lack of IT repair technicians lead to frequent losses and delays. “Most data are sent manually… if there’s a flood or roadblock, it’s often lost forever,” shared a district health officer (p.16). In response to similar vulnerabilities. In Tanzania, investing in HMIS has resulted in improvement in tool utilization and data accessibility in recent years [31]. In Afghanistan, leveraging accessible tools—such as social media platforms like WhatsApp, which is widely available across the country—for transmitting document images could significantly enhance the resilience and security of the health information system.

Cultural relevance and community engagement

Cultural sensitivity emerged as a critical facilitator of information accuracy. People listen more to religious leaders and female health workers that mentioned by some participants. In Pakistan, the successful polio campaign was heavily dependent on mobilizing local religious figures and community health educators [32]. Afghanistan can replicate these strategies, especially in regions with strong traditional leadership.

It is important to note that participants primarily articulated solutions at a conceptual level, emphasizing desired outcomes rather than procedural steps, reflecting their institutional and policy-oriented positions.

Study limitations

This study has several limitations. First, its context-specific focus limits the transferability of findings beyond Afghanistan, as participant perspectives were shaped by the country’s unique socio-political and economic conditions and an evolving political landscape. Second, while thematic saturation was achieved for identifying major system weaknesses and strategic priorities, the exploratory design did not consistently capture detailed, implementation-level mechanisms for proposed solutions. Participants largely articulated policy-level priorities rather than operational pathways; therefore, the suggested solutions should be interpreted as strategic directions rather than actionable interventions. Future research using more focused or participatory qualitative approaches is needed to examine implementation strategies in greater depth and to enhance applicability across comparable settings.

Policy Implications

To enhance the performance and resilience of Afghanistan’s HIS, national and international stakeholders should prioritize the following:

Develop a unified national HIS roadmap that integrates DHIS2, HMIS, and surveillance systems into a single, interoperable platform covering both public and private sectors, with standardized indicators and reporting requirements.Institutionalize digital health capacity-building by training healthcare workers in data use, analysis, and real-time reporting—particularly at district and community levels—and by strengthening skills related to confidential data handling to improve reporting of stigmatized conditions.Strengthen infrastructure through investments in solar-powered electricity, offline-compatible reporting tools, and decentralized IT support to address persistent connectivity and power constraints.Promote inclusive and community-engaged approaches by involving academic institutions, NGOs, and trusted local actors (e.g., female health workers, community elders, and religious leaders) in health communication and outreach activities. Such engagement can help reduce stigma associated with communicable diseases, improve service uptake, and enhance the completeness and accuracy of reported data.Improve population denominators and data validity by integrating routine HIS data with population-based surveys, community mapping, and periodic micro-census approaches, particularly in displacement-affected and underserved areas.Align donor investments with national HIS priorities to ensure sustainability, reduce duplication, and support underreported or stigmatized conditions that may fall outside high-profile donor agendas.

Conclusion

This study assessed the current state of Afghanistan’s HIS in the context of CDs control. Findings indicate that while platforms such as DHIS2 and HMIS are in use, their effectiveness is hindered by inconsistent data reporting, limited infrastructure, insufficient technical personnel, and a lack of research on CDs. Persistent challenges—including unreliable internet access, frequent electricity outages, and inadequate real-time data flow—continue to obstruct timely and accurate disease surveillance.

Nevertheless, participants highlighted several opportunities for system enhancement, including the integration of mobile technologies, increased community engagement, and localized innovations such as signal-amplifying antennas and solar-powered infrastructure. Advancing HIS performance will require a gradual, context-sensitive approach that builds on existing capabilities while addressing systemic gaps.

The implementation of these targeted interventions could enable Afghanistan to develop a more resilient, reliable, and real-time HIS capable of supporting timely public health decision-making and more effective disease control strategies.
