Syncope and 2:1 Atrioventricular Block in a Middle-Aged Man
Authors: Arihant Jain, Nathan Balamurugan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Case SummaryA man in his early 50s with no cardiovascular risk factors presented after a brief syncopal episode and was found to have bradycardia with 2:1 atrioventricular block on electrocardiogram. Careful inspection of the inferior leads showed hyperacute T waves with subtle ST-segment elevation and reciprocal ST-segment depression, suggesting evolving inferior occlusion myocardial infarction. He underwent urgent coronary angiography, which revealed proximal right coronary artery occlusion, followed by successful percutaneous revascularization. This case highlights that syncope with new atrioventricular block may reflect acute coronary occlusion requiring early reperfusion therapy.Take-Home MessagesSyncope with newly developed bradyarrhythmia or atrioventricular block warrants meticulous assessment of the inferior leads for subtle ischemic changes, even when chest pain is absent. Dynamic hyperacute T waves with reciprocal inferior ST-segment changes in high-grade atrioventricular block should prompt suspicion of early inferior occlusion myocardial infarction; reperfusion is the key treatment for this symptomatic bradycardia.

Clinical Vignette

A man in his early 50s with no known cardiovascular risk factors presented to the emergency department 45 minutes after a brief, witnessed syncopal episode lasting approximately 15 seconds, from which he recovered fully without residual symptoms. On arrival, he was alert, hemodynamically stable, and bradycardic with heart rate 42 beats/min, with an otherwise unremarkable physical examination. Answer and Discussion

The ECG demonstrates 2:1 atrioventricular block, with alternating P waves failing to conduct to the ventricles and resulting in a ventricular rate in the low 40s. In this setting, high-grade atrioventricular block should raise concern for ischemia involving the atrioventricular node, particularly in the context of inferior wall myocardial infarction due to right coronary artery occlusion.1 Close inspection of the inferior leads reveals hyperacute T waves with subtle ST-segment elevation and reciprocal ST-segment depression in leads I and aVL (Figure 2), a pattern highly suggestive of an evolving inferior wall occlusion myocardial infarction, most often due to acute right coronary artery occlusion.1,2 Hyperacute T-wave morphology and subtle ST-segment changes have been recognized as early markers of acute coronary occlusion, even before fully developed ST-segment elevation becomes apparent.3 In a patient presenting with syncope, new-onset bradycardia, and high-grade atrioventricular block, these findings strongly favor ischemia-mediated conduction disturbance rather than primary degenerative conduction system disease or a purely vagal mechanism.1,2Figure 2Initial 12-Lead Electrocardiogram Explained With Annotations

The patient underwent urgent activation in the cardiac catheterization laboratory and immediate coronary angiography, which confirmed a proximal right coronary artery occlusion and successful percutaneous revascularization. A repeat ECG obtained just before revascularization showed fully evolved inferior ST-segment elevation (Supplemental Figure 1) with reciprocal changes, corroborating dynamic progression from an early, subtle occlusion myocardial infarction pattern to a diagnostic inferior ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction. Management strategies limited to observation with atropine (option A), elective permanent pacing for presumed intrinsic conduction disease (option B), or outpatient ambulatory monitoring (option D) all would fail to address the culprit coronary occlusion and risk progression to extensive infarction, complete heart block, and hemodynamic compromise.
