Neuroprotective strategies for atrial myxoma resection surgery in a child with cerebral infarction from embolic stroke
Authors: Sue-Anne Han Jia Chyn, Joyce Darshinee Sirisani, Kok Wai Soo, Khairul Anuar Abdul Aziz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Cardiac myxoma is a rare benign primary cardiac tumor in children with a high risk of cerebral embolization due to its friable and mobile nature. We present a child who developed an acute left middle cerebral artery infarct from embolization from a left atrial myxoma. He underwent decompressive craniectomy followed by myxoma resection 5 days later. Perioperative management focused on neuroprotection, including mild hypothermia, cerebral oximetry monitoring, and optimized hemodynamic and metabolic parameters. No hemorrhagic complications occurred, and the patient had a favorable neurological recovery without tumor recurrence. In myxoma-related embolic stroke, surgical resection remains the definitive treatment. Although the timing of surgery remains debated, delaying surgery even with antithrombotic bridging still poses a risk of recurrent embolism. With multidisciplinary coordination to balance the risks of hemorrhagic transformation and further embolization, early surgery can be both safe and effective.

INTRODUCTION

Primary cardiac tumors in children are rare and mostly benign, with a prevalence of 0.017%–0.28% in autopsy series.[12] Unlike in adults, where myxomas are common,[3] pediatric cases more often involve rhabdomyomas, fibromas, or teratomas.[1] Myxomas may be sporadic or familial[4] and can present incidentally or with obstruction, arrhythmia, constitutional symptoms, or embolization.[1234] Embolization occurs in 30%–50% of myxoma cases,[56] with neurological complications seen in 20%–45% of pediatric cases.[24] We report a previously healthy child who developed an acute ischemic stroke from a left atrial myxoma, highlighting the risks of embolic stroke and the challenges in perioperative management.

CASE REPORT

A previously healthy 2-year and 5-month-old boy presented with a 3-day history of fever, reduced oral intake, and cough, followed by a left focal seizure. Blood investigations revealed elevated C-reactive protein (35.9 mg/dL), along with a normal white blood cell count. A computed tomography (CT) brain revealed an acute infarct in the left middle cerebral artery (MCA) territory, with cerebral edema, midline shift, and early hydrocephalus [Figure 1]. He underwent emergency fronto-parietal-temporal decompressive craniectomy and 48 h of cerebral protection [Figure 2].

Echocardiography showed a large, irregular mass (25 mm × 28 mm) in the left atrium, with mild mitral regurgitation and preserved left ventricular function [Figures 3 and 4]. Magnetic resonance imaging confirmed CT brain and echocardiogram findings. After completing cerebral protection, he was extubated and noted to have right-sided hypertonia, weakness, and an upgoing plantar reflex.

Preoperative echocardiography showing the left atrial myxoma with a broad-based stalk attached to the posterior wall of the left atrium

Preoperative echocardiography showing severe mitral inflow obstruction

He was transferred to a cardiac center 4 days postcraniectomy for tumor resection. Surgery was performed the next day via median sternotomy with aortic and bicaval cannulation. He was cooled to 35°C, and cerebral oximetry remained stable. A transseptal approach was used, and a friable, broad-based left atrial mass was completely excised with its stalk. Cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB) and aortic cross-clamp times were 55 and 35 min, respectively.

In the intensive care unit, postoperative care focused on neuroprotection and recovery. Physiological targets included normoxia, normocapnia, normoglycemia, normothermia, and age-appropriate mean arterial pressure and central venous pressure. Head elevation at 30°–45° was used to maintain cerebral perfusion, as no intracranial pressure monitoring was available. Neurological responses, pupillary reflexes, and a 5-lead electroencephalogram were used to monitor for seizures. He was extubated the following day, and echocardiography showed good cardiac function and no residual mass. Histopathology confirmed cardiac myxoma with stellate lepidic cells in a myxoid stroma. A follow-up CT scan showed no new infarct or hemorrhage.

On postoperative day 5, he was transferred back to the referring hospital. Two months later, he had a brief, self-resolving focal seizure requiring a second anticonvulsant. Follow-up assessments showed no neurological deficits or tumor recurrence. Cranioplasty is scheduled for the coming month.

CASE DISCUSSION

Cardiac myoma is a rare benign primary cardiac tumor in children.[1] Our patient presented with constitutional symptoms and ischemic stroke due to cerebral embolization.

While arteriopathies are the leading cause of pediatric arterial ischemic stroke (AIS).[7] Tona et al.[7] found that most pediatric strokes from cardiac myomas were ischemic, with a slight male predominance despite the tumor being more common in females, with most patients presenting with hemiparesis or seizure.[4] Mortality from AIS caused by cardiac myoma can reach 20%, while permanent neurological morbidity may affect 50%–80% of patients.[4]

AIS from cardiac myoma may result from embolized tumor fragments, thrombus formation on the tumor, or both.[6] These emboli often cause multiple ischemic lesions, especially in the anterior cerebral circulation, predominantly the MCA.[57] The MCA supplies the frontal, parietal, and temporal lobes-areas associated with hemiparesis, seizures, and altered consciousness, as seen in our patient.

Managing cardiac myoma complicated by AIS presents unique challenges. Thrombolysis is generally ineffective against tumor embolism.[5] While surgical resection is the definitive treatment,[24] its timing must balance the risk of recurrent embolism against the danger of hemorrhagic transformation during CPB, which requires systemic anticoagulation. This raises concerns in patients with recent infarcts or those who have undergone neurosurgical intervention.

In a series by Rao et al., 67 cases of AIS due to cardiac myoma were studied. Among 11 patients who underwent thrombectomy, eight later had tumor resection 17–62 days poststroke; five developed hemorrhagic transformation, and two required decompressive craniectomy.[5] In contrast, Wu et al. reported a case with thrombectomy and resection within 7 days of stroke onset, with no hemorrhagic complications.[8] Stefanou et al. similarly reported no hemorrhagic transformation in 12 patients who underwent surgery 2–26 days after AIS.[6]

Although there were no significant mortality differences between early and delayed surgical resection,[5] delays were associated with recurrent embolic events despite bridging antithrombotic therapy.[56] Stefanou et al.[6] reported a higher embolic stroke recurrence risk with delayed surgery. Thus, surgical timing must be individualized based on the patient’s clinical status and the evolution of the stroke.

In our case, the risk of hemorrhage transformation during CPB was a major concern post-craniectomy. Hence, anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents were withheld, and the patient was transferred to our center early. Intraoperative neuroprotective strategies[139] included mild hypothermia to reduce cerebral metabolism, maintaining adequate arterial pressure for cerebral perfusion, a transseptal approach postcross-clamp to limit manipulation and embolization risk, complete tumor resection to prevent recurrence, and minimizing CPB time, in addition to a lower activated clotting time target to reduce heparin exposure.

Postoperatively, strict neuroprotective measures were maintained as invasive intracranial pressure monitoring was not available. Optimal blood pressure, oxygenation, glucose control, normothermia, and head elevation were employed to maintain cerebral perfusion and minimize cerebral metabolic demand.[10] A continuous electroencephalogram was essential to detect subclinical seizures that could increase cerebral metabolism.[10]

This case highlights the importance of considering cardiac causes in pediatric AIS and the role of echocardiography in early detection. It also emphasizes the need for coordinated multidisciplinary care in managing cardiac and neurological emergencies. While the timing of tumor resection remains debated, our experience suggests that, with meticulous planning and neuroprotective strategies, early surgery can be safe and effective.
