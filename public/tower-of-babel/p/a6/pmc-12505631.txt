Can climate-resilient city construction promote healthier middle-aged and older adults? Evidences from China
Authors: Chao Zhou, Jiyue Zhang, Shenwei Wan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTo proactively mitigate climate risks, address the growing challenge of population aging, and promote sustainable well-being among older adults, the Chinese government launched a pilot program for climate-resilient cities (CRC). However, empirical evidence on the health impacts of CRC initiatives for middle-aged and older populations remains limited.MethodsThis study integrates data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS, 2011–2020) with the China City Statistical Yearbook. The difference-in-difference (DID) estimation strategy was applied to assess the causal effects of CRC implementation on health outcomes among middle-aged and older adults.ResultsThe construction of climate-resilient cities significantly improved self-rated health. The health benefits of CRC development were mediated through reduced exposure to extreme weather events, improved air quality, enhanced energy efficiency, and better outcomes in cognitive function, social participation, and thermal adaptability. Subgroup analyses revealed stronger positive effects among individuals with chronic diseases, as well as those living in urban areas and western regions of China.ConclusionClimate-resilient city initiatives substantially enhance the health and well-being of middle-aged and older adults, particularly in vulnerable populations and regions. These findings highlight the importance of incorporating climate resilience into urban planning and public health strategies to address both environmental challenges and population aging.Keywords: Health, Climate-resilient city, Middle-aged and older adults, DID, Effectiveness evaluation

Introduction

With the deepening of economic and social transformation, China has entered the stage of "deep aging" and the degree of aging belongs to the upper middle level in the world [1]. According to statistics, it is expected that by 2050, the proportion of older adults population in the total population will exceed 10%, entering the stage of severe aging [2]. In the next 30 years, China is still in the rapid deepening period of aging. The most significant feature of China’s demographic transition is the life expectancy and disease risk due to population aging. China's population aging presents the characteristics of urban and rural differences. The number of older adults in urban areas is more than that in rural areas, but the degree of aging in rural areas is higher than that in urban areas. With the deepening of population aging, it has brought challenges to the supply of public services and the sustainable development of the social security system. The first World Assembly on Ageing first proposed the concept of "healthy aging" which includes health, mental health and active social participation [3]. Among them, health decline is the most important factor affecting the quality of life of individuals in their later years [4]. To a certain extent, the health problems faced by middle-aged and older adults are closely related to climate change [5, 6]. Extreme weather caused by climate change, such as high temperature, rainstorm and typhoon, poses a direct threat to the health of middle-aged and older adults. Uncertainty and disaster risks brought about by climate change can lead to anxiety and depression [7], especially in the case of insufficient social support systems. Globally, countries such as Germany, Japan and the United States have established a comprehensive medical support system to cope with the health challenges brought by population aging [8]. In the face of the growing long-term health needs of middle-aged and older adults, some forward-looking countries in the world have successfully adopted innovative methods to build climate-resilient urban infrastructure to cope with the challenges brought by climate risks and provide institutional support for ensuring the health and quality of life of older adults [9].

China is also actively carrying out the construction of climate resilient cities (CRC). Effective climate adaptation actions can reduce the climate risks faced by countries and regions, and are conducive to ensuring economic and social development and ecological environment security. CRC construction has become an important way for China to improve the ability of cities to cope with climate risks [10].The city is the main gathering place of human production and life, which concentrates all kinds of factor resources and economic and social activities. CRC construction can strengthen disaster prevention and mitigation measures, improve air quality and health services, and effectively alleviate the impact of health problems caused by climate change on middle-aged and older adults. CRC construction emphasizes the emergency management and prevention of meteorological disasters to ensure the safety and health of older adults. In addition, CRC construction can improve air quality, increase green space and improve the resilience of infrastructure, improve the quality of life of middle-aged and older adults, provide a healthier living environment for middle-aged and older adults, and more importantly, provide long-term protection for the health of middle-aged and older adults. Therefore, it is very important to evaluate the impact of CRC pilot on the health of middle-aged and older adults. The current lack of empirical research on climate resilient city construction and individual health highlights the need for a comprehensive framework to better understand the CRC pilot situation, especially its impact on the health of middle-aged and older adults. Based on CHARLS data from 2011 to 2020, this paper examines the impact of CRC construction on the health of middle-aged and older adults. The experimental groups in this study included the first and second CRC pilots, and the control group included non-CRC pilots (see Fig. 1).

The first and second batch of pilot projects for climate-resilient cities

The possible marginal contributions of this study are mainly in three aspects: First, China's CRC construction is still in the pilot stage. Different from the existing literature that evaluates the impact of CRC policies on urban resilience and human settlements, this paper explores the impact of CRC construction on the health of middle-aged and older adults. Second, the existing research usually uses cross-sectional data or basic regression methods, which does not completely solve the endogenous problem. This study overcomes these limitations, and uses the quasi-experimental nature of the CRC pilot program and the DID method to evaluate the policy effect, which can more accurately test the causal relationship between CRC construction and the health of middle-aged and older adults. Thirdly, this study explores the mechanism of CRC construction affecting the health of middle-aged and older adults from multiple perspectives. Based on the "dual system" model of CRC construction, this study constructs a mechanism framework of CRC construction to improve the health of middle-aged and older adults by alleviating extreme weather, optimizing air quality, improving energy conversion rate, increasing cognitive ability, improving social ability and enhancing temperature adaptability.

Theoretical framework and research hypotheses

Direct impacts of climate-resilient city construction on the health of middle-aged and older people

Climate change is one of the most pressing global challenges in the 21 st century. Due to the influence of biological and social factors, middle-aged and older adults are vulnerable to climate change. Climate change is making heat waves more frequent, intense and persistent, severely affecting the body of middle-aged and older adults [11]. Li et al. emphasize the importance of resilient urban planning to reduce climate risks [12]. Effective urban planning such as urban green infrastructure can mitigate surface floods, alleviate the urban heat island effect, and promote the sustainable development of cities [13]. The experience of sponge cities and low-carbon pilot cities shows that the relationship between urban climate resilience and health needs to consider many factors, and it is not enough to focus on a single climate factor. Therefore, it is of great significance to explore the multiple paths of the impact of climate adaptive city construction on the health of middle-aged and older adults. CRC builds a new paradigm of urban resilience construction and management to comprehensively and multi-dimensionally improve the city's ability to cope with extreme weather and long-term climate change risks, so as to ensure the well-being of urban residents, especially the health of middle-aged and older adults in urban and rural areas. Based on this, H1 is proposed:

H1: The construction of CRC has a positive impact on improving the health level of middle-aged and older adults.

H1: The construction of CRC has a positive impact on improving the health level of middle-aged and older adults.

Indirect effects of climate-resilient city construction on the health of middle-aged and older people

CRC construction aims to cope with extreme climate events. According to the natural physical and socio-cultural attributes of climate risks, this paper constructs a "dual system" theoretical framework based on governance system and governance capacity [14]. Based on the "dual system" theoretical framework of climate resilient city construction, this study proposes six mechanism paths for CRC construction to improve the health level of middle-aged and older adults from both macro and micro perspectives.

Middle-aged and older adults are at high risk of heat-related deaths. Extreme weather events caused by climate change can increase the psychological stress and anxiety of middle-aged and older adults [15]. In particular, older female are more vulnerable to extreme high temperatures than male [16]. The theory of resilient cities points out that measures such as strengthening infrastructure and optimizing spatial layout can improve the ability of middle-aged and older adults to cope with natural disasters and climate change [17, 18]. In order to cope with climate change, cities need to strengthen their resilience to short-term extreme events and long-term climate risks through long-term planning. CRC construction can regulate urban microclimate, reduce heat island effect and reduce rainstorm waterlogging by increasing natural infrastructure such as green space, parks and water bodies. Through the construction of summer centers and shelter facilities, the accessibility of public facilities layout has been improved, which provides a safe place for middle-aged and older adults to cope with extreme weather and reduces their exposure to high temperature or cold environment. The construction of CRC alleviates the damage of extreme weather to the environment and provides a more livable living environment for middle-aged and older adults. Therefore, this study proposes H2.

H2: CRC construction improves the health of middle-aged and older adults by alleviating extreme weather.

H2: CRC construction improves the health of middle-aged and older adults by alleviating extreme weather.

Rising temperatures will aggravate air pollution, leading to an increased risk of respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, while middle-aged and older adults are more sensitive to air pollution due to the aging of their physical functions, and are "vulnerable groups" affected by climate change and environmental pollution. The theory of environmental equity points out that there is often an uneven distribution of environmental resources in urban planning, and air pollution is more likely to be concentrated in low-income communities or areas with a higher degree of aging [19]. By increasing vegetation coverage, expanding ecological infrastructure such as urban green space and wetlands, reducing air pollutant concentrations and improving air quality, CRC construction provides the cleaning living environment for middle-aged and older adults and reduces the unfair impact of air pollution on older adults. Therefore, this study proposes H3.

H3: CRC construction improves the health of middle-aged and older adults by optimizing air quality.

H3: CRC construction improves the health of middle-aged and older adults by optimizing air quality.

Improving energy efficiency is an important means to address climate change and the energy crisis [20]. Cities are regarded as a complex ecosystem whose operation depends on the input and output of energy, resources and environment [21]. CRC construction emphasizes improving living conditions by reducing greenhouse gas emissions, optimizing energy structure, and reducing energy use costs, especially in terms of basic living needs such as heating and cooling, to provide more stable and cheaper energy supply for middle-aged and older adults. This adaptation reduces air pollution from fossil fuel combustion, reduces health risks associated with respiratory and cardiovascular diseases, and improves climate resilience. Therefore, this study proposes H4.

H4: CRC construction improves the health of middle-aged and older adults by increasing energy conversion rate.

H4: CRC construction improves the health of middle-aged and older adults by increasing energy conversion rate.

Environmental psychology points out that good urban environments such as green spaces and parks can relieve psychological stress and enhance brain function through cognitive stimulation [22]. For middle-aged and older adults, improving cognitive ability can reduce the risk of cognitive decline and dementia. Through the construction of livable communities, CRC construction optimizes urban spatial layout and infrastructure construction, alleviates urban heat island effect, improves urban environmental quality, and reduces the health risks of environmental burden to middle-aged and older adults, thereby reducing medical costs. Indirect release more resources for the cognitive development of middle-aged and older adults, such as participating in social activities, receiving education, using cognitive training tools, etc., to enhance the social participation of middle-aged and older adults, delay the decline of their working ability, and further enhance the economic value and health status of middle-aged and older adults in society. Therefore, this study proposes H5.

H5: CRC construction improves the health of middle-aged and older adults by improving cognitive ability.

H5: CRC construction improves the health of middle-aged and older adults by improving cognitive ability.

City is the core place of social interaction, and its spatial structure, environmental design and functional layout directly affect the social interaction of residents [23]. In the face of lifestyle changes caused by climate change, older people are more likely to feel helpless and experience symptoms of depression and anxiety, while enhanced family and community support can alleviate loneliness [24]. Enssle and Kabisch found that the increase in green space significantly increased the frequency of social interaction and interaction within the community, helping middle-aged and older adults to broaden their social networks [25]. Through the construction of pleasant public spaces, such as parks, pedestrian streets, community squares, etc., CRC provides more social opportunities for middle-aged and older adults. At the same time, by encouraging walking and low-carbon transportation, improving community connectivity, shortening social distance, promoting social interaction among community residents, and helping middle-aged and older adults to establish and maintain a strong social network. Therefore, this study proposes H6.

H6: CRC construction improves the health of middle-aged and older adults by improving their social ability.

The design of urban environment directly affects the ability of residents to adapt to temperature changes [26]. Middle-aged and older adults are more susceptible to high temperature weather due to poor thermoregulation. For middle-aged and older adults, the optimization of the urban environment can significantly reduce the threat of temperature changes to their health. CRC construction strengthens the temperature regulation function of the ecosystem by increasing green infrastructure such as urban forests, wetlands, and parks, and provides a more livable climate environment for middle-aged and older adults. At the same time, CRC creates a more comfortable urban environment for middle-aged and older adults by building shading cooling infrastructure and aging-friendly buildings to help them adapt to temperature changes. It is worth noting that CRC construction reduces the cost of middle-aged and older adults to adapt to temperature changes by improving building energy conservation, optimizing energy structure and community design, thus freeing up more resources for health management. Therefore, this study proposes H7.

H7: CRC construction improves the health of middle-aged and older adults by enhancing temperature adaptability.

Study design and methods

Data source

The data used in this study are from China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and China City Statistical Yearbook. The five-round survey of CHARLS covered the implementation period of CRC, and the data included the health information of residents aged 45 and above in various provinces and cities, which better met the research needs of this study. We acknowledge that panel attrition, including mortality and loss to follow-up, may introduce sample selection bias in CHARLS data. This study uses CHARLS 2011–2020 five-phase data, and the final total sample size included in the analysis was 16,880 people.

Empirical model construction and variables selection

The implementation of CRC in China provides a quasi-natural experimental background for this study. Due to the different policy pilots and implementation time in each city, this study used the difference-difference model (DID) to evaluate the impact of CRC construction on the health of middle-aged and older adults. The explained variable is self-rated health (SRH). The "self-rated health status" in CHARLS was used as the evaluation standard of self-rated health status. CHARLS provides respondents with two negative options ('very unhealthy' and 'somewhat unhealthy'), two positive options ('very healthy' and 'relatively healthy') and a neutral option ('average') to measure self-rated health status. The total score of self-rated health ranged from 1 to 5 points, and the higher the value, the better the self-rated health.

The core explanatory variables is CRC construction. Using the regional distribution of CHARLS data, we generate policy dummy variables based on the implementation time of CRC in different cities to explore the impact of CRC implementation on the health of middle-aged and older adults during the sample period. Since CHARLS data are collected only from national surveys in 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020, the focus of this study is to examine the policy effectiveness of CRC construction in this specific period of time.

First, the study mitigates extreme weather (extreme). Extreme weather events, including high temperatures (HTD), low temperatures (LTD), and heavy precipitation (IPD), serve as key indicators of climate variability [27]. The mechanism is quantified by aggregating the total days of extreme heat, cold, and rainfall. This approach provides a robust measure of reduced exposure to adverse weather conditions.

Second, the study optimizes air quality (air). Air quality is evaluated using the Air Quality Index (AQI), a standardized scale ranging from 0 (optimal air quality) to 500 (severe pollution). The AQI quantifies pollutant concentrations and communicates associated health risks [28]. The study employs Air Pollution Index (API) data for 2011 and 2013 and transitions to AQI data for 2015, 2018, and 2020. This ensures a consistent assessment of air quality improvements.

Third, the study enhances energy conversion efficiency (energy). Energy consumption intensity, defined as energy use per unit of gross domestic product (GDP), measures regional energy efficiency. The metric is calculated as the ratio of total energy consumption to gross regional product, reflecting the energy required per billion yuan of GDP. Lower intensity indicates higher energy efficiency. This supports green, low-carbon economic development and contributes to climate change mitigation.

Fourth, the study improves cognitive function(cognition). Cognitive function is assessed using the CHARLS Harmonized Cognitive Assessment Protocol (CHARLS-HCAP), which evaluates episodic memory and mental status [29]. Episodic memory (0–20 points) is measured through immediate (0–10) and delayed (0–10) word recall tasks, while mental status (0–11 points) includes time orientation (0–5), serial subtraction (0–5), and figure drawing (0–1). Total cognitive scores (0–31 points) are derived by summing these components. Higher scores indicate better cognitive performance.

Fifth, the study enhances social engagement (social). Social engagement is quantified by the frequency of participation in activities such as visiting friends, playing community games (e.g., mahjong, chess, or cards), providing unpaid assistance, engaging in physical activities (e.g., dancing, qigong), and participating in community, volunteer, or educational programs [30]. The average frequency across eight activities characterizes social engagement. Ancillary activities, such as stock investment and internet use, are excluded from the primary analysis.

Sixth, the study enhances temperature adaptation (warm). Temperature adaptation is assessed through perceptions of indoor temperature maladaptation, as indoor conditions are more closely linked to heat-related health issues in older adults than outdoor temperatures [31]. The mechanism is measured on a scale where 0 indicates suitable conditions, 1 denotes relatively hot or cold, 2 indicates very hot or cold, and 3 indicates extremely hot or cold. This scale captures the degree of thermal comfort.

Model validity testing

Parallel trends test

When using DID to evaluate the policy effect, it is necessary to meet the parallel trend hypothesis, that is, before the implementation of CRC, the trend of self-rated health status of the treatment group and the control group should be consistent. In order to test this hypothesis, this study uses the event study method to estimate the dynamic effect and test the parallel trend. The regression model combines the interaction between policy implementation and year dummy variables, with 2015 (Pre-2) as the reference group. The results of Fig. 3 show that there was no significant difference in self-rated health status between policy cities and non-policy cities before 2015. However, after the implementation of CRC policy, the self-rated health status of middle-aged and older adults living in policy cities has improved significantly. These findings provide strong evidence that the DID model used in this study satisfies the parallel trend hypothesis, which proves the robustness of the benchmark regression results shown in Table 3.

Parallel trends test for self-rated health

Placebo test

In order to ensure that the estimation results are not affected by accidental factors, this paper draws on the practice of Wang and Chen [9], and conducts a placebo test by randomly assigning cities in the treatment group. In this paper, 1658 samples are randomly selected from all 17327 samples as the "pseudo" control group, and then the "pseudo" policy variables are constructed for re-estimation. In this paper, Monte Carlo simulation is used to repeat the above random experiments 500 times, and 500 "pseudo" regression coefficients and corresponding P values are obtained accordingly. The specific distribution is shown in Fig. 4. The results show that most of the "pseudo" regression coefficients are not significant, which is far from the true estimates in the baseline regression. This indicates that the coefficient estimates in the benchmark regression are small probability events in the placebo test and are unlikely to be obtained by chance. This result excludes the possibility that the estimation results in the baseline regression are disturbed by unobservable factors.

Placebo test for self-rated health

Endogenous treatment

In order to solve the endogenous problems caused by missing variables and reverse causality, this paper uses 2SLS for endogenous test. The average slope of the prefecture-level city where the sample is located is selected as the instrumental variable [35], which is independent of human activities, but is related to the difficulty of CRC construction and has nothing to do with the health status of middle-aged and older adults. Therefore, the instrumental variables meet the requirements of correlation and exogeneity. The estimation results of 2SLS are shown in Table 4. Column (13) is the estimation results of the first stage of instrumental variable regression. The results show that the instrumental variable (elevation) is highly correlated with the policy variable (CRC). At the same time, the Kleibergen-Paap LM test and Kleibergen-Paap Wald F test also prove that the instrumental variables selected in this paper do not have the problems of insufficient recognition and weak recognition. The test results show that the instrumental variables are effective. Column (14) shows the results of the second stage of instrumental variable regression. The estimated coefficient of CRC for SRH is 15.9236, which is significant at the level of 1%. The estimation results show that CRC construction has a positive impact on improving the health of middle-aged and older adults. The estimation results of the above instrumental variables show that the conclusions obtained by the benchmark regression in this paper are still valid after overcoming the potential endogenous problems, which ensures the robustness of the research conclusions.

Exclusion of other policy interferences

During the implementation of the Carbon Reduction Cities (CRC) pilot program, several other closely related policies, such as the Low-Carbon City Pilot [37] and the New Energy Demonstration City Pilot [38], were simultaneously advancing. These policies often share overlapping infrastructure, resources, and strategic objectives, which may introduce spatial and temporal correlations affecting the estimation of CRC's impact. To account for this, our study includes dummy variables representing the presence of Low-Carbon City and New Energy Demonstration City pilot policies as interference controls in the regression model (Eq. 1).

The regression results after controlling for these interference policies are presented in Table 5. Models (17), (18), and (19) show that the CRC coefficient remains positive and statistically significant, consistent with baseline estimates. This indicates that even after accounting for related policy shocks, CRC construction positively impacts the health of middle-aged and older adults, supporting the robustness and reliability of our findings.

Replacement of Estimation method

In order to avoid the bias of the function form, this paper introduces the double machine learning model (DDML), and uses the random forest algorithm to predict and solve the benchmark regression again [39]. The regression results are shown in Table 5. Model (20) controls the fixed effect of districts and counties, the fixed effect of time and the linear term of control variables in the full sample interval. Model (21) further controls the quadratic term of the control variable, and the regression coefficient of CRC is still significant and the value does not change much. It can be found that the regression coefficient of CRC construction to SRH is significant at the level of 5%, so it can be inferred that the core conclusion of this paper is relatively robust.

The causal effect of CRC construction on SRH is re-estimated. The estimation results are shown in Table 6, in which the CRC-psm coefficients are significant, which is consistent with the coefficient symbol and significance level of the baseline estimation results, which confirms the reliability of the conclusions of this paper.

Heterogeneity analysis

Urban-rural heterogeneity

There are differences in infrastructure, resource allocation and environmental conditions between urban and rural areas, which will lead to differences in the policy effects of CRC. This study defines a regional dummy variable, which encodes rural areas as 1 and urban areas as 0. The results of Table 8 models (31), (32) show that in urban areas, the coefficient of CRC is 0.0790, and the significance level is 10% (p < 0.1). In urban areas, there is a significant positive impact, but in rural areas, the coefficient of CRC is 0.0245, not significant. This shows that the policy effect of CRC construction is not significant in rural areas, and the effect of policy implementation is weak. It may be that the high density of urban population and the perfect infrastructure of the city make the policy effect of CRC large, wide and more obvious. The rural areas are sparsely populated, and the project benefits and impacts are scattered, requiring additional support and resource investment. These conclusions reflect the different effects of CRC construction in different regions and emphasize the importance of local adaptation strategies and policy implementation.

Chronic diseases heterogeneity

People with chronic diseases tend to be more sensitive to environmental changes, so taking climate-adaptive measures may have a more direct impact on their health and quality of life. In this study, according to whether the middle-aged and older adults have chronic diseases, the samples were divided into chronic disease group and non-chronic disease group. The results of Table 8 model (33), (34) showed that the coefficient of CRC in chronic group was 0.0584, and the significance was 5%. In the non-chronic group, the CRC coefficient was 0.0678, which was not significant. It shows that CRC construction has a significant positive impact on people with chronic diseases. Although it has a positive impact on people without chronic diseases, it is not as significant as the impact on people with chronic diseases. It may be because people with chronic diseases may be more sensitive to environmental changes and more dependent on medical facilities and health support. Therefore, the improvement of air quality and temperature regulation brought about by CRC construction have a greater impact on their quality of life.

From a clinical perspective, individuals with chronic respiratory, cardiovascular, or metabolic conditions are particularly vulnerable to environmental factors such as air pollution and temperature extremes. The CRC initiative, by improving air quality and enhancing urban temperature regulation, likely reduces exposure to harmful pollutants and thermal stress, which are well-documented triggers and exacerbators of chronic conditions [41]. For example, improved air quality can decrease the incidence and severity of respiratory diseases such as asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), while better temperature adaptability can reduce cardiovascular strain [42]. These mechanisms help explain the stronger health benefits observed among those with chronic diseases, as their baseline vulnerability makes them more responsive to environmental improvements.

City heterogeneity

Central cities tend to have better infrastructure and resources, while non-central cities may face more constraints. This study divides the sample into central city group and non-central city group according to whether the middle-aged and older adults live in central cities. The results of model (35), (36) in Table 9 showed that the CRC coefficient of the Centre city group was 0.1110, and the significance was 10%. The CRC coefficient of the non-centre city group was 0.0124, but it did not reach statistical significance. It shows that CRC construction has a more positive impact on the residential population in central cities. The possible reason is that central cities usually have better infrastructure, more concentrated resources and funds, and more extensive support for adaptation measures, which is conducive to the implementation of CRC construction. Non-central cities may have weak infrastructure and limited resource allocation, which limits the effectiveness of CRC construction.

Regional heterogeneity

In order to reveal the specific impact of CRC construction on middle-aged and older adults in different regions, we analyzed the eastern, central and western regions respectively. According to the living area of middle-aged and older adults, this study divides the samples into three groups: eastern, central and western. The results of models (37), (38) and (39) in Table 9 show that in the western region, the coefficient of CRC is 0.2015, and the significance level is 1%, but the promotion effect on the central and eastern regions is not significant. It may be because the infrastructure in the western region is relatively weak, and the improvement brought by CRC construction is more obvious.

Research conclusions and perspectives

Based on the panel data of CHARLS from 2011 to 2020, this study uses a multi-period DID model to analyze the impact of CRC construction on the health of middle-aged and older adults. The study found that: (1) CRC construction increased the self-rated health score of middle-aged and older adults by 0.0463. After a series of robustness tests, the research conclusions are still true and reliable. (2) CRC construction improves the health of middle-aged and older adults by alleviating extreme weather, optimizing air quality, improving energy conversion rate, increasing cognitive ability, improving social ability and enhancing temperature adaptability. (3) Heterogeneity analysis showed that CRC construction had a more significant positive impact on the health of middle-aged and older adults with chronic diseases and living in urban, western and central areas.

Based on this, this study proposes four policy recommendations to enhance the health and well-being of middle-aged and older adults through strategic urban and environmental interventions, with relevance to global sustainable development goals. First, the development of CRC should be prioritized as a core component of national and regional urban planning strategies. Policy frameworks and financial mechanisms should incentivize widespread adoption of CRC initiatives, optimizing urban planning and environmental design to meet the health needs of middle-aged and older adults, thereby creating safer, more accessible, and healthier living environments. Second, robust response strategies for extreme weather events should be established, including advanced weather warning systems and enhanced urban infrastructure for flood control and drainage. Rigorous implementation of environmental regulations, coupled with the promotion of renewable energy and reduction of industrial and transportation-related emissions, will improve air quality and protect respiratory health. Third, community-based programs should address the psychological, social, and adaptive needs of middle-aged and older adults. Educational hubs offering climate adaptation training and diverse social engagement activities, alongside accessible public facilities with temperature-regulated environments, will support resilience to climate change. Fourth, targeted investments in CRC initiatives should focus on populations with chronic conditions, particularly in urban and underserved regions. Strengthened healthcare systems, regular health monitoring, specialized medical support, and community infrastructure tailored to chronic disease management will significantly enhance health outcomes, contributing to equitable and sustainable urban development.

This study has several important limitations that warrant careful consideration. First, the primary data source, CHARLS, relies predominantly on subjective self-report measures, such as self-rated health (SRH), which may introduce reporting bias and limit the inclusion of objective health indicators. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted with caution, acknowledging the inherent constraints of subjective assessments. Additionally, CHARLS data are subject to sample selection bias due to panel attrition, including mortality and loss to follow-up, which may affect the representativeness of the sample. Although efforts were made to control for potential confounders, the lack of application of methods such as inverse probability weighting to adjust for attrition may limit the robustness of the results. Second, the focus on CRC implementation in selected pilot cities restricts the generalizability of the results. These pilot cities are not fully representative of the broader Chinese urban population, and the multifaceted nature of CRC as a policy package means that its implementation and effectiveness can vary substantially across different local contexts. Moreover, the observed effects depend heavily on baseline conditions, available supplementary resources, temporal and spatial factors, and local administrative capacity. It is also critical to recognize that short-term outcomes observed in middle-aged and older adults may not translate into long-term sustainable impacts or be applicable to other demographic groups. Therefore, extrapolation of these findings to non-pilot cities, future periods, or different regions should be approached with caution. Future research should aim to incorporate objective health measures, address attrition bias more comprehensively, and explore broader, more diverse settings to strengthen the robustness and applicability of the conclusions.
