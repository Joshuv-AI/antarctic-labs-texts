Combined biosynthesis and site-specific incorporation of phenylalanine derivatives from aryl aldehydes or carboxylic acids
Authors: Shelby R Anderson, Abigail P Spangler, Amanda M Forti, Michaela A Jones, Roman M Dickey, Aditya M Kunjapur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractApplications of genetic code expansion (GCE) in live cells are widespread and continually emerging, yet they have been limited by their reliance on the supplementation of non-standard amino acids (nsAAs) to cell culturing media. Here, we report the design of a single engineered bacterial host that performs the steps of phenylalanine derivative semi-synthesis and site-specific incorporation within target proteins. Our platform pathway exhibits broad substrate specificity towards commercially ubiquitous, achiral building blocks of aryl aldehydes or carboxylic acids, producing the family of nsAAs that are most frequently used for GCE. We demonstrate biosynthesis of 12 distinct nsAAs, and we observe combined biosynthesis and incorporation for these chemistries using common orthogonal translation systems (OTSs) based on a fluorescent reporter assay. We additionally show that the combination of nsAA biosynthesis and GCE steps can extend the chemical reach of the intrinsic biological containment strategy of synthetic auxotrophy from reliance on nsAAs to instead reliance on low-cost and achiral building blocks. Our platform should aid industrial-scale manufacturing of proteins that contain nsAAs and democratize access to expensive or commercially unavailable chemistries for labs that lack separations or traditional synthesis expertise.Subject terms: Synthetic biology, Metabolic engineering, Biosynthesis

Introduction

Genetic code expansion (GCE) enables chemical diversification of proteins in living systems for a growing number of applications1, including the manufacture of conjugation-ready therapeutic proteins2 and intrinsic biological containment of engineered microbes3. Many of these applications are enabled by site-specific ribosomal incorporation of phenylalanine derivatives, which are the most widely used class of non-standard amino acids (nsAAs), also known as non-canonical amino acids (ncAAs) or unnatural amino acids (UAAs). Currently, most nsAAs are produced by chemical synthesis4, though several promising biocatalytic or photobiocatalytic synthesis platforms are under development by ourselves and others5–10. However, whether nsAA synthesis occurs chemically or biologically, the current synthesis paradigms for GCE applications in live cells necessitate a downstream step of nsAA isolation followed by supplementation to cell culture media. Besides the added costs and labor of separate nsAA synthesis and isolation steps, the requirement of nsAA supplementation also imposes constraints that limit the performance or scalability of GCE in diverse application contexts11–14. For example, in bioreactor settings that are relevant for on-site manufacturing of therapeutics, these constraints can include the distribution, toxicity, and cellular uptake rate of the nsAA. For emerging contexts in which engineered microbes are designed as synthetic auxotrophs for biological containment, a current constraint is that an nsAA must be supplied to a target environment to support microbial growth.

Combined nsAA biosynthesis and GCE based on site-specific incorporation (SSI) within proteins using a single engineered organism could address such limitations and create opportunities to harness principles from synthetic biology, such as on-demand nsAA synthesis, nsAA sensing, and intercellular communication15. For these and other reasons, pioneering efforts during the last two decades have begun to demonstrate the coupling of nsAA biosynthesis or semi-synthesis and SSI for certain nsAAs12,16–27. However, past efforts that reported de novo biosynthetic pathways for nsAAs have exhibited highly restricted substrate scope, usually by relying on distinct tailoring enzymes from secondary metabolism to convert a native metabolite to one target nsAA, such that each desired nsAA requires a different pathway. In contrast, biocatalysis-oriented efforts have leveraged single polyspecific enzymes as platforms for the synthesis of diverse nsAAs in cell-free contexts. However, these biocatalytic strategies have not been demonstrated to function in the context of growing cells that are most actively translating new proteins, which is necessary when combining biosynthesis and incorporation. Between these categories is the concept of a semi-synthesis pathway that functions in live cells, which could leverage enzyme polyspecificity while interfacing with native metabolism for cofactors, amine donors, or carbon skeletons that could be added onto a supplemented precursor. Previously, such a concept has been shown for combined biosynthesis and SSI of phenylcysteine derivatives from supplemented aryl thiols28. However, limitations of that approach are that phenylcysteine derivatives can be degraded by cysteine S-conjugate β-lyases found in many organisms29, and their thioether bonds can be prone to oxidation by reactive oxygen species, which could affect downstream applications and protein stability30.

Given the extensive application of > 80 phenylalanine derivatives1 in GCE technologies and the lack of a promiscuous biosynthetic pathway to access these encodable and relatively stable nsAAs, we aimed to design a platform to produce these chemistries from inexpensive and readily accessible precursors. We imposed four requirements on our envisioned biosynthetic pathway design: (1) Accept inexpensive and commercially available substrates; (2) Employ promiscuous enzymes to access diverse nsAA targets from diverse substrates; (3) Produce the desired nsAA in fermentative contexts; and (4) Function with high-fidelity when co-expressed with an orthogonal translation system (OTS) for combined nsAA biosynthesis and SSI. In relation to the first two requirements, we31 and others32 recently demonstrated the design of efficient and polyspecific three-enzyme cell-free biocatalytic cascades for the synthesis of phenylalanine derivatives from inexpensive aryl aldehyde precursors. Our cascade features an L-Threonine Transaldolase (L-TTA or TTA) from Pseudomonas fluorescens (ObiH)33–36, a phenylserine dehydratase from Ralstonia pickettii (RpPSDH)37, and an aromatic aminotransferase from E. coli (TyrB). The cascade proceeds through two intermediates - a β-hydroxylated α-amino acid and a keto acid - each of which can be a high-value product on its own38.

Inspired by the success of this cascade in dilute aqueous settings, here we investigate what it would take to perform similar reactions in live cells and with coupling to various OTSs (Fig. 1). The performance of a semi-synthesis pathway in a live cell environment can be met with numerous additional challenges relative to a cell-free environment due to differences in relative enzyme abundance, availability of co-factors and co-substrates, transport and toxicity of pathway metabolites, and competing reactions. Given also that our prior cell-free enzyme cascade did not include the step of GCE, the system that we sought to create in this manuscript is rather distinct. Using strains of E. coli that are engineered to stabilize aryl aldehydes39–41, we show successful biosynthesis and incorporation of 12 relevant nsAAs that vary in size and polarity from either aryl aldehyde or carboxylic acid precursors in metabolically active cells that are cultured in a simple 96-well plate format. We illustrate how this platform empowers synthetic biology applications, such as extending the permissive chemical range of the biological containment technique of synthetic auxotrophy to synthetic aryl aldehydes.

A single strain is designed to express two to four enzymes for the biosynthesis of phenylalanine derivates from supplemented aryl carboxylic acid or aryl aldehyde precursors, as depicted in the top row of the cell. Results

Biosynthesis of model beta-hydroxylated nsAAs in live cells

To prototype our biosynthetic pathway in live cells, we selected four model substrates that we envisioned could correspond to widely used nsAAs for applications such as bio-orthogonal conjugation, photo-crosslinking, and biological containment: 4-azido-benzaldehyde (1b) for biosynthesis of 4-azido-L-phenylalanine (1e); 4-acetyl-benzaldehyde (2b) for biosynthesis of 4-acetyl-L-phenylalanine (2e), 4-cyano-benzaldehyde (3b) for biosynthesis of 4-cyano-L-phenylalanine (3e), and 4,4-biphenylaldehyde (4b) for biosynthesis of 4,4-L-biphenylalanine (4e) (Fig. 2a, b). Besides their applied utility, these structurally and electronically diverse L-phenylalanine derivatives could shed initial light on substrate tolerance in live-cell contexts. In our recent design of a one-pot biocatalytic cascade, we determined that a SUMO-tagged variant of the TTA (s-TTA) from Pseudomonas fluorescens, specifically s-ObiH, can accept these four model aldehyde substrates31 (Supplementary Fig. 1a).

We examined the rate of formation of β-hydroxylated nsAAs upon supplementing these aldehyde substrates at 1 mM concentrations to 96-well plate cultures of engineered E. coli cells that were transformed for heterologous expression of s-ObiH (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Our host strain, E. coli RARE.Δ1641, contains 6 gene knockouts from its progenitor E. coli RARE strain39 and 10 additional gene inactivations that were shown to enable the stabilization of the di-aldehyde terephthalaldehyde, for a total of 16 aldo-keto reductase (AKR) and alcohol dehydrogenase (ADH) gene knockouts. We observed the desired β-hydroxylated nsAA products in the supernatant by HPLC at an endpoint of 20 h (Supplementary Fig. 1c). Next, we screened s-ObiH and five SUMO-tagged homologs of ObiH with the model aldehydes as before to ascertain the role of L-TTA homolog selection on β-hydroxylated nsAA titer. All variants were active in live cell contexts under the conditions tested, with the optimal L-TTA being chemistry-specific (Supplementary Fig. 1d). For the biosynthesis of 2c and 3c, s-PbTTA was the best performer, though peak areas were comparable between it, s-BuTTA, and s-ObiH. In contrast, for the biosynthesis of 4c, s-ObiH generated over two-fold the peak area of any other L-TTAs tested, with two replicates resulting in over four-fold compared to the next best strain.

Establishing a full biosynthetic pathway from aldehydes to nsAAs in live cells

We briefly evaluated the potential for site-specific incorporation of the β-hydroxylated nsAA to determine if sidechain chemistries of interest could be introduced within proteins without the need for removal of the β-hydroxy group. However, screening via a routine nsAA incorporation assay with a commercially available β-hydroxylated nsAA (β-OH O-methyl-L-tyrosine) indicated that it was not a substrate for a large panel of published MjTyrRS/tRNACUA pairs (Supplementary Fig. 2). Accordingly, we gained additional rationale for β-hydroxy group removal and sought to accomplish this by extension of the pathway to include a phenylserine dehydratase from Ralstonia pickettii (RpPSDH) and the native aromatic aminotransferase in E. coli (TyrB). Given the improvement of protein yield we observed from the addition of a SUMO-tag to diverse TTAs, we inquired about its effect on RpPSDH expression level. We transformed E. coli BL21 for heterologous expression of either RpPSDH or a SUMO-tagged variant (s-RpPSDH), with the goal of supplementing β-hydroxylated nsAA and forming non-hydroxylated nsAAs through the action of native aminotransferases. We supplied 5 mM L-threo-phenylserine and monitored the formation of phenylalanine. We observed nearly two-fold higher levels of phenylalanine formed when using the cells that express s-RpPSDH, likely due to its higher expression level (Supplementary Fig. 3).

We next sought to evaluate whether we could convert supplemented aryl aldehydes to non-hydroxylated nsAAs in cell cultures grown in a simple 96-well plate format, which is a convenient high-throughput screening format. We coupled one of the two highest performing SUMO-tagged L-TTAs from our previous screen (either s-ObiH or s-PbTTA) with s-RpPSDH on a single pCOLA vector for heterologous expression. We then monitored the conversion of 1 mM aldehydes supplemented to live cells at mid-exponential growth phase (OD600 = 0.4–0.6) to the anticipated phenylalanine derivatives (Fig. 2c). We were excited to see desired nsAA products 1e-3e in the hundred micromolar range when supplying 1b-3b and that we still produced detectable 4e at low micromolar levels when supplying 4b despite the poor activity of all our tested TTAs on 4b (Fig. 2d). To determine if native TyrB levels could be limiting reaction yields, we investigated TyrB overexpression as well as supplementation of excess amine donor (L-Glu). We co-transformed the pCOLA vector containing s-TTA and s-RpPSDH with a pZE vector containing TyrB in the RARE.∆16 strain and measured nsAA titers in the presence and absence of excess L-Glu 20 h after 1 mM aldehyde addition. However, we did not observe an increase in titer from TyrB overexpression or L-Glu supplementation under the conditions tested (Supplementary Fig. 4).

To understand the influence of the aldehyde-stabilizing host strains on biosynthesized nsAA titer, we transformed different E. coli host strains with our pathway plasmid containing only s-TTA and s-RpPSDH. We chose the wild-type E. coli MG1655 strain, the originally published RARE strain that contains 6 deletions of aldehyde reductases (RARE.∆6), the RARE.∆16 strain mentioned earlier, and the ROAR strain, which contains 6 deletions of aldehyde dehydrogenases (ALDHs) in addition to the deletions in RARE40. We first performed a standard aldehyde stability assay, observing greater retention of supplemented aldehydes 1b-4b after four and 20 h in the engineered strains relative to the wild-type (Supplementary Figs. 5–8). We next compared production of nsAAs 1e-4e 20 h after IPTG induction and supplementation with the corresponding aldehyde precursor. Notably, we observed minimal titers of each of these four model products when using the MG1655 strain. Comparatively, we observed >five-fold titers of 1e in RARE, >30-fold titers of 2e in RARE and RARE.∆16, >30-fold titers of 3e in RARE.∆16 and ROAR, and >nine-fold titers of 4e in RARE (Fig. 2e). These results highlighted the value of harnessing an aldehyde-stabilizing chassis to allow the implementation of this designed biochemical pathway in live cells.

To gain insight into what step might be limiting nsAA titers, we analyzed expected intermediates and potential byproducts. Although our lack of standards for the β-OH nsAA and keto acid intermediates limited precise quantification of their concentration, we observed that the remaining peak area of the β-OH nsAA intermediate upon expression of the entire biosynthetic pathway was less than 5% that seen upon expressing only a TTA (Supplementary Fig. 9). Additionally, based on comparable UV-absorbance of a few available β-OH nsAAs and their corresponding L-Phe derivatives, we estimate that for each of the four chemistries less than 100 µM β-OH nsAA accumulated as detected in the culture media (Supplementary Table 1). Collectively, these results strongly suggest that the PSDH is not the limiting step. To better understand whether the limiting step was upstream or downstream of this step, we next quantified the remaining aldehyde for each of the four chemistries, as well as the alcohol and carboxylic acid byproducts due to oxidation or reduction in the cellular environment (Supplementary Figs. 10–13). The sum of the remaining reactant aldehyde and its associated redox byproducts in the RARE.∆16 host amounts to 29.6%, 30.3%, 55.6%, and 11.7% of the supplemented 1 mM 1b, 2b, 3b, and 4b, respectively. For chemistries 1–3, this indicates that the TTA-catalyzed reaction is the limiting step when the full biosynthetic pathway is expressed in live cells. For chemistry 4, due to an incomplete mass balance and high hydrophobicity of pathway compounds, it is less clear if any individual enzymatic reaction, low solubility, or membrane association could be limiting.

Demonstration of the extended biosynthetic pathway starting from carboxylic acids

We next investigated whether we could extend our pathway to demonstrate biosynthesis of our model nsAAs 1e-4e from their associated carboxylic acids 1a-4a. Anticipating that activity and soluble protein expression of carboxylic acid reductase (CAR) homologs could vary, we tested a panel of six from our previously published collection42. These were individually cloned onto a pZE vector to evaluate activity in live cell contexts in the aldehyde-stabilizing RARE.Δ16 strain. The CARs were co-expressed with Sfp from Bacillus subtilis (BsSfp), a phosphopantetheinyl transferase required for CAR activation43. We supplied 2 mM of the carboxylic acids 1a-3a or 1 mM of 4a (due to lower solubility) to RARE.Δ16 cells induced to express six different CARs during mid-exponential phase growth (Supplementary Fig. 14). We monitored reactions for 2 h, given an interest in identifying fast-acting CAR variants. We detected aldehyde products in every case and observed that aldehyde product titers were comparable in many instances. Many CARs demonstrated high activity on acids 1a-3a, while all showed moderately low activity on 4a. We proceeded with the MavCAR construct for subsequent experiments, as we had known the protein to express well under various conditions.

To determine whether co-expression of MavCAR could allow a single strain to carry out the extended biosynthetic pathway from supplemented carboxylic acids, we transformed E. coli strains for heterologous expression of MavCAR, BsSfp, either s-PbTTA (for 1–3) or s-ObiH (for 4), and s-RpPSDH. Initially, we utilized a two-plasmid system, both containing a medium-high (15–40) copy number origin of replication (ori) for both the MavCAR with BsSfp (pZE vector, ColE1 ori) and the s-TTA with s-RpPSDH (pCOLA vector, ColA ori) constructs. However, this initial design resulted in impacts on cell growth and poor titers (Supplementary Fig. 15). Consequently, we moved the MavCAR construct to a lower copy number plasmid (pACYC, p15A ori). With the second design, consisting of the original pCOLA vector containing the s-TTA and s-RpPSDH co-transformed with the pACYC vector containing MavCAR and BsSfp, we observed more robust growth (Supplementary Fig. 16). We then sought to understand the influence of exogenously supplied substrate concentration on desired nsAA product titers by supplying 1 mM, 2 mM, or 4 mM 1a-4a. Endpoint HPLC measurements of the culture media indicated successful production of the desired nsAAs, with 4e detectable in all replicates only when 4 mM 4a was supplied, even though the addition of 4 mM 4a results in insolubility at the start of the reaction (Fig. 2f, g). These results indicate that the influence of substrate concentration on product titers is chemistry-dependent.

Identification of relevant systems for SSI of targeted nsAAs

To achieve SSI of the nsAAs biosynthesized by this pathway, we next needed to identify a suitable genetically encoded OTS that would be capable of catalyzing aminoacylation of an orthogonal tRNA with the nsAA at biosynthetically attainable titers. To measure this, we also needed to co-express a reporter protein that contains a codon targeted for suppression by the OTS. We began by performing a well-established fluorescence assay with commercially sourced nsAAs 1e and 4e (0.5 mM) or no nsAA supplemented to engineered strains of E. coli. These E. coli strains were transformed to express one of five previously reported OTSs derived from the Methanocaldococcus jannaschii tyrosyl-tRNA synthetase (MjTyrRS)/tRNA pair and a green fluorescent protein (GFP) reporter containing an in-frame UAG codon targeted for suppression (Fig. 3a). We performed an initial screen to identify the OTSs that maximized the fold-change increase in fluorescence normalized to optical density (FL/OD) between the presence and absence of each nsAA (Supplementary Fig. 17).

To examine the dose-response of nsAA incorporation rates at concentration ranges more relevant to our biosynthetic titers, we selected the better-performing OTSs for each chemistry from our initial screen. Fortunately, several OTSs for 1e (Fig. 3b) and the BipARS for 4e (Fig. 3c) exhibited ≥ 3.5-fold change in FL/OD at concentrations as low as 100 µM 1e or 5 µM 4e, which correspond to nsAA titers attainable using our biosynthetic pathway. The high affinity of the BipARS for 4e is a fortunate coincidence for our pathway, given our earlier observed low micromolar yield.

High-fidelity site-specific incorporation of biosynthesized nsAAs using aldehydes or carboxylic acids

With suitable OTSs identified, we prepared genetic constructs that enabled co-expression of the OTS, the reporter protein that contains the in-frame UAG codon, and the biosynthetic pathway, all in a single strain. We began with the core biosynthetic pathway starting from aldehydes with the objective of incorporating biosynthesized 4e from supplemented 2 mM 4b in LB media (Fig. 3d). We performed the biosynthesis and incorporation experiment in the E. coli RARE.∆16 strain co-transformed with one vector (pCOLA) containing the biosynthetic pathway enzymes (s-ObiH and s-RpPSDH) along with a second vector (pRepSynth, p15A ori) containing a reporter protein (GFP_TAG) and OTS (BipARS/tRNACUA pair). After purification, trypsin digestion, and LC/MS/MS analysis of our reporter protein (Supplementary Fig. 18), we observed high-fidelity incorporation of biosynthesized 4e (98%) when conducting a search that considered masses that could correspond to common misincorporation events from OTS-catalyzed misacylation of standard aromatic amino acids. This result illustrates that our combined system for biosynthesis and incorporation of phenylalanine derivatives can function robustly even for the model chemistry that was least tolerated by our biosynthetic pathway enzymes.

To determine whether we could convert carboxylic acids to incorporated nsAAs, we selected 1e as a commercially relevant target, given the significantly lower cost of 1a compared to 1e. We transformed cells of the ROAR strain40 for heterologous expression of the biosynthetic pathway, the OTS, and the reporter and supplied 2 mM of 1a precursor (Fig. 3e) to live cells in LB media. We utilized a three-plasmid system consisting of 1) a pZE vector harboring MavCAR and BsSfp, 2) a pCOLA vector harboring the biosynthetic pathway enzymes (s-ObiH and s-RpPSDH) and 3) a pRepSynth vector harboring a reporter protein (GFP_TAG) and OTS (NapARS/tRNACUA pair). After purification, trypsin digestion, and LC/MS/MS analysis of our reporter protein (Supplementary Fig. 19), we observed high-fidelity incorporation of biosynthesized 1e (>99%), with either possible azide reduction to 4-amino-phenylalanine (pAF)44 or misincorporation of tyrosine observed at minor levels (0.3%; pAF and Tyr differ by only 1 amu). This approach could offer substantial cost savings for commercial-scale manufacturing of therapeutic proteins that contain azide functionality.

Optimization of genetic constructs for improved reporter protein yield

Having seen that our system could achieve high-fidelity, we next sought to improve reporter protein yields by increasing the rate of nsAA synthesis, ideally with sufficient nsAA available by mid-exponential phase for maximal reporter protein translation. We hypothesized that constitutive expression of biosynthetic genes may improve performance and chose the strong constitutive promoter from the Anderson promoter library (BBa_J23100), which performed well compared to the inducible system in terms of final nsAA titers (Supplementary Fig. 20). Furthermore, based on a lowering of cell fitness and biosynthetic titers that we had observed when using the high-copy CloDF origin of replication (Supplementary Fig. 20), we also hypothesized that changes in the copy number of plasmids might lower burden. We instead used combinations of medium-to-high copy ColE1 and ColA, together with the low-copy p15A origin. Another change we made to our genetic constructs to facilitate measurement of reporter protein yield was to remove the His-tags that had been included for each of the biosynthetic enzymes, leaving a His-tag only on the reporter. This plasmid design was transformed into the aldehyde-stabilizing strain RARE.Δ16, along with a pRepSynth plasmid carrying ATC-inducible aaRS and GFP, and constitutively expressed MjtRNATyrCUA (Fig. 3f).

We reasoned that an interesting comparison to make would be the level of reporter protein produced by supplementation of either an aldehyde precursor or the corresponding nsAA to cells that express our combined system, and that the performance in both cases would depend on the concentration of the exogenously supplied molecule. As such, we supplied varying concentrations of aldehyde or nsAA for the 4-acetyl (2b or 2e) and 4-cyano (3b or 3e) chemistries, first on a 96-well plate scale. We measured the concentration of supplemented aldehyde at which the reporter protein signal saturated. For 2b/2e, cells containing the biosynthetic pathway (s-PbTTA, s-RpPSDH) and incorporation machinery (TetRS-C11/tRNACUA) reached maximum FL/OD at concentrations as low as 1.5 mM 4-acetylbenzaldehyde (2b), achieving 74% of the signal obtained with supplemented 1.5 mM 4-acetylphenylalanine (2e) (Fig. 3g). Excitingly, for the 4-cyano system, cells harboring the pCNFRS MjTyrRS variant and supplemented with just 0.75 mM 4-cyanobenzaldehyde (3b) achieved equal FL/OD output to cells supplemented directly with 0.75 mM of the corresponding nsAA (Fig. 3i). Next, we scaled up the system to 50 mL culture volumes in baffled shake flasks and subsequently purified the Ub-GFP reporter (Supplementary Fig. 21). We achieved yields of 22 mg/L reporter protein containing biosynthesized 2e (Fig. 3h) and 33 mg/L protein containing biosynthesized 3e, with confirmation by intact protein MS (Fig. 3j). These protein yields are promising considering the number of genes under heterologous expression, and they encouraged us to next investigate additional substrates.

Investigating the substrate scope of this system more extensively

Having now validated and optimized the combined system for semi-synthesis and incorporation of four model phenylalanine derivatives, we next investigated the ability to utilize additional aldehyde and carboxylic acid precursors. We examined eight chemistries that we had previously validated in vitro (5–12), most of which correspond to nsAAs previously used in GCE (except for 8e). These nsAAs feature valuable functionalities, including bio-orthogonal conjugation handles (8e), halogen substituents (6e, 11e), motifs for designer enzyme construction (9e, 10e), and groups documented to influence hydrophobic packing interactions (5e).

Due to the optimization of our genetic constructs and the simplicity of working with our system, we screened nsAA biosynthesis and incorporation within a GFP reporter protein in a two-day 96-well plate culturing experiment. Given that the investigation of substrate scope for the combined system would also require screening of the optimal OTS, we investigated four polyspecific MjTyrRS variants in combination with the broadly specific enzymes s-ObiH, s-RpPSDH (Fig. 4a), and optionally MavCAR (Fig. 4b). With the implementation of the improved expression systems for the biosynthetic pathway, consisting of constitutively induced expression of the TTA and PSDH on a pZE vector (KanR), of the IPTG inducible MavCAR and BsSfp on a pACYC vector (CmR), we designed a new and compatible third plasmid containing the OTS and reporter protein. For the subsequent experiments, we utilized a plasmid backbone containing a ColA origin of replication, streptomycin resistance, and ATC inducible expression of the aaRS and GFP reporter containing an in-frame TAG codon.

In addition to measuring GFP fluorescence, we confirmed nsAA biosynthesis by mass spectrometry for each of the 8 nsAAs shown in Fig. 4c (Supplementary Figs. 22–36) and quantified nsAA production (Supplementary Table 2). We observed higher titers from the aldehyde precursor compared to from the carboxylic acid precursor, as expected due to the higher cell burden associated with CAR expression and additional plasmid maintenance. When supplementing aldehydes associated with these chemistries, titers ranged from 10 to 272 µM. The highest titers were observed for the production of 4-bromo-phenylalanine, 6e, with 272 µM from the aldehyde precursor 6b and 170 µM from the carboxylic acid precursor 6a. Additionally, supplementation of 1 mM 5b, 11b, and 12b resulted in >100 µM titers of 2-naphthylalanine (5e), 4-fluoro-phenylalanine (11a) and 3-trifluoromethyl-phenylalanine (12e). Of the chemistries that we explored, less well tolerated were para-borono and benzoyl moieties present in 9 and 10, respectively.

Encouragingly, we also observed increases in GFP fluorescence (normalized to OD₆₀₀) upon supplementation with either aldehyde or carboxylic acid precursors relative to the no-precursor control, with our initial panel of polyspecific aaRSs for incorporation of a target nsAA. We observed a > 1.5-fold increase in the fold change in FL/OD600 for all 8 target nsAAs initializing from an aldehyde precursor (Fig. 4d), and for all 7 target nsAAs initializing from a carboxylic acid precursor (Fig. 4e). While the non-bulky chemistries containing ring substituents at the para position appear to be the most well-tolerated by the biosynthesis pathway and the incorporation machinery, based on our past characterization of substrate tolerance in vitro31 we would also expect meta-substituted phenylalanine derivatives to be promising biosynthetic targets and good candidates for the combined system if compatible OTSs were used.

As this platform could have widespread benefits for industrial biomanufacturing, we sought to evaluate the relative burden of each of the genetic constructs on the metabolically active cells. We therefore monitored the growth of 7 strains, both with and without induction of the genetic constructs at mid-exponential growth phase (Supplementary Fig. 37). Compared to the RARE.Δ16 strain transformed with a plasmid containing the OTS and reporter, we see a minor burden in a two-plasmid system for the additional plasmid containing the TTA and PSDH genes. This results in a 14% decrease in the biomass after 17 h for a system with inducible biosynthetic pathway gene expression and a 19% decrease for that with constitutive expression. As expected, the burden is further increased with the addition of a third plasmid for expression of the CAR and Sfp, with 39% relative decrease in biomass compared to the one plasmid system. However, ultimately each of the strains was able to grow and accumulate biomass over the 17 h during which optical density was monitored at 200 µL/well scale in a plate reader. Minimizing the number of plasmids by genome integration of one or more of the pathway genes may help to reduce the additional burden associated with plasmid maintenance.

Control of bacterial survival by provision of biphenylaldehyde

The ability to couple biosynthesis and incorporation of nsAAs through semi-synthesis strategies that harness low-cost synthetic precursors creates new opportunities in synthetic biology and biosensing, where cellular function could be governed by the concentration of certain aryl aldehydes or carboxylic acids in a transcription factor-independent manner. An exemplary cellular function is survival, where previous work has demonstrated the ability of nsAAs to control survival by mediating the full-length translation and proper folding of essential proteins. Pioneering efforts in this direction reported synthetic auxotrophs, which are intrinsically biologically contained due to their reliance on a chemical not found in nature45,46. E. coli strains that were designed to rely on biphenylalanine, or BipA, (4e) included some that exhibit no observable escape under various conditions, including after 100 days of continuous culturing, one of which is the strain denoted DEP.e547. Importantly for the present effort, continuous culturing resulted in adaptation to the applied pressure of growing on low micromolar concentrations of supplemented 4e. As chiral molecules, 4e and other nsAAs may be cost-prohibitive to supply at scales required for environmental applications of biocontained strains.

To create a synthetic auxotroph responsive to biphenylaldehyde (4b), we transformed an evolved biphenylalanine-dependent strain, DEP.e5, for heterologous expression of our core biosynthetic pathway, consisting of s-ObiH and s-RpPSDH (Fig. 5a, b). We then performed a growth-based assay that measures the ability of 4b to support the growth of DEP.e5 through production of 4e within the cell. We were interested in measuring growth in the medium intended to be permissive as well as potential escape from biocontainment in the medium lacking a synthetic molecule. While the DEP.e5 strain has not been observed to escape, it is possible that its transformation with such a promiscuous pathway could enable it to biosynthesize unintended nsAAs from culture media components or native metabolites. We included a no-substrate case as a negative control, a case of supplying 10 µM 4e as a positive control, and our experimental case of supplying 500 µM 4b. In qualitative initial experiments, we observed that cells of the DEP.e5 strain transformed with our pathway were only able to grow when 4e or 4b were provided in liquid media. Again in a qualitatitive assay format, we subsequently confirmed growth was due to reliance on the supplemented synthetic molecules by plating on permissive (containing 4e) or non-permissive (without 4e) solid media conditions and observed growth only under permissive conditions with cells previously grown in the presence of 4e or 4b (Fig. 5c). To more rigorously quantify the frequency of escape from biocontainment, we repeated the assay with growth of the transformed DEP.e5 strain in liquid media that was supplied with 500 µM 4b for 3 days, a longer culture time under permissive conditions than standard escape frequency assays owing to the slower growth of this strain. After the 3-day incubation period, the culture was washed and plated on permissive or non-permissive plates. On the permissive plates, we observed an average of 5.33 × 107 c.f.u.s/mL (Supplementary Fig. 38). We observed no c.f.u.s throughout 6 days of monitoring on the non-permissive plates at an assay detection limit of 3.99 × 10−8 escapees per c.f.u. (Fig. 5d) calculated on the basis of the number of cells plated (Supplementary Fig. 39).

To gain insight into whether the transformation of the DEP.e5 strain with a polyspecific biosynthetic pathway could influence escape rates at higher cell densities (and thus lower escape assay detection limits), we simply provided 4e rather than 4b to either the untransformed DEP.e5 strain or to the transformed DEP.e5 strain. We further investigated two different versions of transformed DEP.e5 strains, one transformed with the original plasmid that features inducible expression of the pathway genes (denoted pSA-2) or another that features constitutive expression (denoted pSA-4), which we found further improves the system in terms of growth on biphenylaldehyde. At an assay detection limit of 6.58 × 10−10 escapees per c.f.u. for DEP.e5, 1.79 × 10−9 escapees per c.f.u. for DEP.e5 transformed with pSA-2, and 1.45 × 10−9 escapees per c.f.u. for DEP.e5 transformed with pSA-4 (Fig. 5e), we observed no c.f.u.s on non-permissive plates throughout 14 days of monitoring (Supplementary Figs. 40, 41). Collectively, these results indicate the ability to use our pathway to create synthetic auxotrophs that can grow in the presence of user-defined synthetic aldehydes while maintaining their biocontainment, albeit there is a need to improve strain fitness when the aldehyde is provided.

Discussion

The current paradigm of nsAA synthesis, isolation, and supplementation to live cells cultures for SSI within proteins is a major constraint on the application of GCE at industrially relevant scales and for emerging environmental uses. Here, we demonstrate the design of a synthetic biochemical pathway that functions in live bacterial cells and its coupling to orthogonal translation systems for combined nsAA biosynthesis and SSI within a single strain. This approach reduces the problem of sourcing a phenylalanine derivative to a problem of sourcing an aryl aldehyde or aryl carboxylic acid, which are simple achiral building blocks. These synthetic compounds are widely available, and because carboxylic acids are also ubiquitous in metabolism, this creates opportunities to biosynthesize the precursors towards total biosynthesis from renewable feedstocks. This coupling of metabolic engineering to GCE could address other bottlenecks in GCE, such as transport, nsAA toxicity (by maintaining a lower steady state in some cases), or nsAA concentration (by increasing intracellular concentration in other cases).

The coupling of nsAA biosynthesis and SSI was accomplished using a pathway consisting of up to five highly polyspecific enzymes that are capable of functioning together in a single cell. Given the promiscuity of the biosynthetic pathway observed in vitro31, we expect that the coupled system in live cells will accept additional aryl aldehyde and carboxylic acid substrates. Our system demonstrated that even at low biosynthetic yields for certain chemistries, coupling with a high-affinity OTS can still achieve protein incorporation with high fidelity. Further optimization of expression levels, enzyme activity, and substrate tolerance, particularly for bulky substrates, and even protein engineering of our naturally occurring pathway enzymes, may enhance the biosynthetic platform. Alternatively, the engineering of OTSs to exhibit higher affinity towards nsAAs has been relatively underexplored, though there have been recent successful evolution campaigns that characterize the lower limit of detection48. Engineering higher-affinity OTSs should serve as a less burdensome means of improving target protein yield when coupling nsAA biosynthesis and incorporation. By concluding this study with the creation of a strain whose survival depends on the biosynthesis and incorporation of biphenylalanine from biphenylaldehyde, we now have a unique selection platform for directed evolution or adaptive laboratory evolution of any of the steps in this process.

Improved system for the biosynthesis and incorporation of nsAAs

For chemistry 2, E. coli RARE.∆16 was co-transformed with 1) a single reporter/AARS/tRNA plasmid denoted pRepTetRS-C11 encoding expression of an ubiquitin-fused GFP reporter containing an amber suppression codon and a C-terminal hexahistidine tag and TetRS-C11/tRNACUA and 2) a pZE vector encoding strong constitutive expression of both s-PbTTA and s-RpPSDH (pSA-6*). For chemistry 3, similarly, E. coli RARE.∆16 was co-transformed with 1) a single reporter/AARS/tRNA plasmid denoted pReppCNFRS encoding expression of an ubiquitin-fused GFP reporter containing an amber suppression codon and a C-terminal hexahistidine tag and pCNFRS/tRNACUA and 2) pSA-6*.

For a fluorescence-based incorporation assay, overnight cultures of the strains described above were used to inoculate 3 mL LBL containing 15 µg/mL Kan and 17 µg/mL Cm. Overnight cultures were used to inoculate 300 µL volumes of MOPS EZ Rich media lacking the aromatic amino acids (Y, W, F) with 2% glucose and 100 mM L-Thr at a 1:100 inoculation ratio in a 96-deep-well plate with appropriate antibiotic. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a shaking incubator at 1000 RPM. At mid-exponential phase (OD600 ~ 0.5), cultures were induced with 0.2 µM aTc and 0–2 mM of aldehyde (2b or 3b) or nsAA (2e or 3e) was added, and subsequently incubated at 30 °C, 1000 RPM. A no-substrate (negative control) was included to account for background fluorescence. After 18 h, the culture was diluted 4x with PBS, and 200 µL was transferred to a black, clear-bottom 96-well plate for OD600 and FL (485 525 excitation and emission) measurement on a Spectramax i3x plate reader.

For further confirmation of incorporation of the biosynthesized nsAA in the target protein, the strains described above were cultured at 37 °C in 75 mL of MOPS EZ Rich media lacking the aromatic amino acids (Y, W, F) with 2% glucose and 100 mM L-Thr in a 250 mL baffled shake flask in duplicate. At an OD600 ~ 0.5, the culture was induced with 0.2 µM aTc and 1 mM 2b or 1 mM 3b precursor was added (to the strains containing TetRS-C11 or pCNFRS, respectively). Cultures were then grown at 30 °C for an additional 18 h. The cells were harvested by centrifugation (7000 × g at 4 °C for 6 min), supernatant was removed, and the pellet was stored at −80 °C prior to purification. Purification of the reporter proteins containing biosynthesized 2e or 3e was performed as described above. Purified fractions were pooled, concentrated, and dialyzed against dialysis buffer (25 mM HEPES, pH 7) with a 10 kDa molecular weight cutoff centrifugal filter (Amicon Ultra, Millipore). The sample was then run on an SDS-PAGE gel with a Thermo ScientificTM SpectraTM Multicolor Broad Range Protein ladder to confirm reporter protein production and purification by size. The gel was then stained with Bio-Safe Coomassie stain (Bio-Rad) and destained with water. The protein concentration was measured by a Bradford assay using bovine serum albumin as a reference. Intact protein MS was performed in ESI positive mode using a Waters Acquity UPLC protein BEH C4 columns 1.7 um 2.1 m X 50 mm operated using 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B). Gradient conditions were 5% B to 95% B in 5 min.

Biosynthesis and incorporation of an expanded substrate panel

For initialization from aldehyde precursors, E. coli RARE.∆16 was co-transformed with a pZE vector encoding strong constitutive expression of both s-ObiH and s-RpPSDH (pSA-4*) and one of four OTS/reporter constructs (pRepTetRS-C11*, pRepNapARS*, pRepBipARS*, pReppCNFRS*). For initialization from carboxylic acid precursors, RARE.∆16 was transformed with pSA-4*, pACYC-MavCAR-sfp, and one of four OTS/reporter constructs (pRepTetRS-C11*, pRepNapARS*, pRepBipARS*, pReppCNFRS*).

Screening for incorporation of biosynthesized 5e – 12e was performed similarly to the fluorescence-based incorporation screen for 2b and 3b. Overnight cultures of the strains described above were used to inoculate 3 mL LBL containing appropriate antibiotics (15 µg/mL Kan and 25 µg/mL Carb for the 2-plasmid system; 10 µg/mL Kan, 11.3 µg/mL Cm, and 16.7 µg/mL Carb for the 3-plasmid system). Overnight cultures were used to inoculate 300 µL volumes of MOPS EZ Rich media lacking the aromatic amino acids (Y, W, F) with 2% glucose and 100 mM L-Thr at a 1:100 inoculation ratio in a 96-deep-well plate with appropriate antibiotic. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C in a shaking incubator at 1000 RPM. At mid-exponential phase (OD600 ~ 0.5), cultures were induced with 0.2 µM aTc and 1 mM of aldehyde or 2 mM of carboxylic acid precursor was added per condition, and subsequently incubated at 30 °C, 1000 RPM. A no-substrate (negative control) was included as well to account for background fluorescence. After 18 h, the culture was diluted 4x with PBS, and 200 µL was transferred to a black, clear-bottom 96-well plate for OD600 and FL (485 525 excitation and emission) measurement on a Spectramax i3x plate reader.

Synthetic auxotroph growth on aldehyde precursor

E. coli DEP.e5 was transformed with a pZE vector encoding aTc-inducible expression of both s-ObiH and s-RpPSDH (pSA-2). Cultures were inoculated from a frozen stock and grown overnight in 3 mL LBL media with 12.75 µg/mL Cm, 15 µg/mL Kan, 10 μM biphenylalanine (4e), 100 µM biphenylaldehyde (4b), 0.2% (wt/vol) L-arabinose, 0.2 µM aTc, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 0.005% SDS, and 100 mM L-Thr at 34 °C. Cells were washed 4x in LB media with 12.75 µg/mL Cm, 25 µg/mL Kan, 0.2% (wt/vol) L-arabinose, 0.2 µM aTc, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 0.005% SDS, and 100 mM L-Thr to remove the essential nutrient of biphenylalanine. Overnight cultures were then used to inoculate experimental cultures in 200 μL volumes in a Greiner clear bottom 96 well plate (Greiner 655090) at 100x dilution in LB media with 12.75 µg/mL Cm, 15 µg/mL Kan, 0.2% (wt/vol) L-arabinose, 0.2 µM aTc, 20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 0.005% SDS, and 100 mM L-Thr. Substrate was added at inoculation under the following experimental conditions performed in triplicate: no substrate, 10 µM 4e, or 500 µM aldehyde precursor 4b. Cultures were grown for 50 h in a Spectramax i3x plate reader with medium plate shaking at 30 °C and with absorbance readings at 600 nm.

After 50 h growth, 5 µL of each experimental triplicate was plated on both non-permissive and permissive LB-agar to the confirm growth observed in liquid culture was due to the presence of supplemented or biosynthesized essential nutrients, rather than the escape of the synthetic auxotroph. Non-permissive agar contained 12.75 µg/mL Cm, 15 µg/mL Kan, 0.2% (wt/vol) L-arabinose, 20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 0.005% SDS. Permissive agar included the essential nutrient biphenylalanine (4e) at 10 µM. Plates were incubated at 30 °C for 18 h.

Quantifying growth of the engineered synthetic auxotroph

Cultures of DEP.e5 pSA-2 were inoculated from a frozen stock and grown overnight in 3 mL LBL media with 12.75 µg/mL Cm, 15 µg/mL Kan, 10 μM biphenylalanine (4e), 0.2% (wt/vol) L-arabinose, 0.2 µM aTc, 0.005% SDS, and 100 mM L-Thr at 34 °C. Then, the cells were washed 4x in LB media with 100 mM L-Thr to remove the essential nutrient of biphenylalanine, and subsequently used to inoculate at a 100x dilution the 3 mL culture volumes containing 100x LB media with 12.75 µg/mL Cm, 15 µg/mL Kan, 0.2% (wt/vol) L-arabinose, 0.2 µM aTc, 0.005% SDS, 100 mM L-Thr, and 500 µL 4b. Cultures were grown for 3 days in a 34 °C incubator with shaking at 250 rpm. Next, the cells were washed 4x in LB media and resuspended in fresh LB media following washes. Three biological replicates were plated on permissive media (12.75 µg/mL Cm, 30 µg/mL Kan, 0.2% (wt/vol) L-arabinose, 0.2 µM aTc, 0.005% SDS, 10 µM 4e) following 10-fold serial dilutions in LB to determine the number of viable c.f.u.s plated, and then monitored for at least 6 days at 34 °C. To conduct serial dilutions, cells were pipetted up and down 10 × in each new well, new pipette tips were used for each round of serial dilutions, and multiple serial dilutions for each condition were plated to ensure cells were appropriately diluted 10-fold. The cell counts from permissive plates were also used to calculate the assay limit as 1/(average total viable c.f.u.s). Three biological replicates were also plated on non-permissive solid media (12.75 µg/mL Cm, 30 µg/mL Kan, 0.2% (wt/vol) L-arabinose, 0.2 µM aTc, 0.005% SDS) without previous serial dilutions and monitored for up to 6 days at 34 °C. Colonies were counted after the specified number of days, and escape frequencies were calculated as colonies observed on non-permissive media lacking the synthetic auxotroph’s nsAA per average viable c.f.u.s plated for each strain.

Testing for escape of the synthetic auxotroph

To further ensure that the transformation of the biosynthetic pathway would not lead the microbe to escape from its biocontainment, we performed a standard solid media escape assay15. For this assay, cultures of DEP.e5, DEP.e5 pSA-2, and DEP.e5 pSA-4 were inoculated from a frozen stock and grown overnight in 3 mL LBL media with 12.75 µg/mL Cm, 15 µg/mL Kan, 10 μM biphenylalanine (4e), 0.2% (wt/vol) L-arabinose, 0.2 µM aTc, 0.005% SDS, and 100 mM L-Thr at 34 °C in biological triplicate. Then, the cells were washed 4x in LB media with 100 mM L-Thr to remove the essential nutrient of biphenylalanine, and resuspended in fresh LB with 100 mM L-Thr following the washes. As described previously, three biological replicates were plated on permissive media (12.75 µg/mL Cm, 30 µg/mL Kan, 0.2% (wt/vol) L-arabinose, 0.2 µM aTc, 0.005% SDS, 100 mM L-Thr, 10 µM 4e) following 10-fold serial dilutions in LB with 100 mM L-Thr. The entirety of the remaining culture was then plated on non-permissive media (12.75 µg/mL Cm, 30 µg/mL Kan, 0.2% (wt/vol) L-arabinose, 0.2 µM aTc, 0.005% SDS, 100 mM L-Thr) in large petri dishes (145 × 20 mm) without serial dilutions and monitored for up to 14 days at 34 °C.

HPLC analysis

Metabolites and synthetic biochemicals of interest were quantified via high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using an Agilent 1100 Infinity model equipped with a Zorbax Eclipse Plus-C18 column (part number: 959701-902, 5 µm, 95 Å, 2.1 × 150 mm). To quantify compounds of interest, an initial mobile phase of solvent A/B = 95/5 was used (solvent A, water, 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid; solvent B, acetonitrile, 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid) and maintained for 5 min. A gradient elution was performed (A/B) with: gradient from 95/5 to 50/50 for 5–12 min, gradient from 50/50 to 0/100 for 12–13 min, gradient from 0/100 to 95/5 for 13–14, and equilibration at 95/5 for 14–15 min. A flow rate of 1 mL min−1 was maintained, and absorption was monitored at 210, 250, 270, 280 and 300 nm.

UPLC-MS analysis

Confirmation of nsAA production was performed using one of two instruments and methodologies. The first, a Waters AQUITY Arc UPLC H-Class with a diode array coupled to a Waters AQUITY QDa Mass Detector. Product was analyzed using a Waters Cortecs UPLC C18 column with an initial mobile phase of solvent A/B = 95/5 (solvent A, water, 0.1% formic acid; solvent B, acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) and maintained for 5 min. A gradient elution was performed (A/B) with: gradient from 95/5 to 10/90 for 5–7 min, an isocratic flow at 10/90 for 7–10 min, then gradient from 10/90 to 95/5 for 10–10.5 min and a final isocratic step at 95/5 for 10–12 min. A flow rate of 1 mL min-1 was maintained. For the second methodology, UC-MS analysis was run on an Agilent 1290 UHPLC-MS equipped with a 1260 DAD and 6125 SQD. UV-Vis data was collected at 210 and 254 nm, and MS data was collected from 100–1250 m/z in both positive and negative modes. Separations were conducted using an Agilent EclipsePlusC18 RRHD column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.8 µm), which was operated at 40 °C with a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min using 0.1% formic acid in water (solvent A) and 0.1% formic acid in acetonitrile (solvent B). Gradient conditions were 0 min, 5% B; 0.5 min, 5% B; 2.5 min, 95% B; 3.0 min, 95% B; 3.1 min, 5% B, for a total run time of 3.5 min.

Statistics & reproducibility

We performed all experiments in triplicate except where otherwise noted. No statistical method was used to predetermine sample size, and no data were excluded from the analyses. The experiments were not randomized, and the investigators were not blinded to allocation during experiments and outcome assessment.
