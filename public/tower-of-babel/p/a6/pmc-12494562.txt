Hidden Plasmodium diversity revealed in southeastern Asian passerines using next-generation amplicon sequencing
Authors: Dragomir Damnjanović, Masoud Nazarizadeh, Milena Nováková, Nguyễn Văn Linh, Oleg Tolstenkov, Anvar Kerimov, Andrey Bushuev, Jan Štefka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractMonitoring haemosporidian (Genus Plasmodium) infections in passerine birds is essential for understanding the intricate dynamics of avian malaria and its implications for ecology and evolution of avian populations. In this study, we investigated the prevalence and diversity of malaria infections caused by Plasmodium species in three passerine species in Cat-Tien National Park, Vietnam. Using next-generation amplicon sequencing (NGS) of haemosporidian cytochrome b gene, we identified two known and ten novel Plasmodium lineages. Our genetic analysis revealed a high rate of Plasmodium infections in the little spiderhunter (Arachnothera longirostra; Nectariniidae), the white-rumped shama (Copsychus malabaricus; Muscicapidae) and the blue-winged pitta (Pitta moluccensis; Pittidae). Species delimitation methods identified five distinct operational taxonomic units (OTUs), consistently across the majority of the used methods. Each of the passerine species was infected with a specific subset of the total Plasmodium diversity. Phylogenetic analysis showed that sympatric Plasmodium OTUs are not closely related and possess overlapping host preferences. Our findings may reflect differences in habitat use, such as the vertical strata occupied by different bird species, which contribute to varying exposure levels to suitable vectors, thereby influencing infection rates and parasite diversity. Our findings corroborate the view that avian malaria parasites are not uniformly opportunistic; rather, their distribution is filtered by host identity and ecology. Understanding these dynamics is crucial for avian conservation and broader ecological studies, as avian malaria serves as a model for studying parasite-vector-host co-evolution and the impact of environmental changes on disease dynamics.Keywords: Malaria, Species delimitation, Plasmodium, Alpha and beta diversity, Passerines

Introduction

Haemosporidian parasites (Phylum Apicomplexa), including Plasmodium spp., Haemoproteus spp., and Leucocytozoon spp., are widely prevalent among songbirds (Order Passeriformes), and their study provides crucial insights into avian disease dynamics and ecology (de Souza Penha et al., 2023; Isra’a, 2025). While many passerine hosts have coevolved with these parasites leading to mostly subclinical or mild infections, chronic low-level parasitemia can still impose subtle fitness costs – such as reduced reproductive success, impaired mobility or migration, and increased risk of anemia – that cumulatively impact host population dynamics (Schoenle et al., 2017; Gangoso et al., 2024). Moreover, avian haemosporidians form an integral part of ecological communities, where they mediate species interactions (e.g. via influencing predation or competition) and they can regulate host population sizes, thereby affecting overall ecosystem functioning (van Hoesel et al., 2020). Over evolutionary timescales, the persistent host-parasite co-evolution has shaped the trajectories of both groups, with parasite pressure driving genetic diversity in bird populations (for instance, maintaining diverse immune gene variants) and fostering resilience against emerging diseases (Rivero de Aguilar et al., 2024). Consequently, monitoring haemosporidian parasitemia in songbirds yields valuable ecological and evolutionary context – it serves as a hallmark of ecosystem health and helps inform conservation and wildlife management strategies by detecting changes in parasite prevalence or the emergence of novel strains early on (Galvin et al., 2021). Ultimately, avian populations are prone to haemosporidian infection (species of the genera Haemoproteus, Plasmodium and Leucocytozoon) by the continuous distribution from tropical to boreal regions (Valkiunas, 2005; Ishtiaq et al., 2007; Schultz et al., 2011; Svoboda et al., 2015; Ricklefs et al., 2017; Ciloglu et al., 2020).

Avian Plasmodium species exhibit considerable variation in host specificity, ranging from lineages with broad host ranges across diverse avian taxa to lineages predominantly specialized on species of particular bird families or genera (Fallon et al., 2005; Hellgren et al., 2009, 2015, 2021; Ellis et al., 2020; Strehmann et al., 2023). In Haemoproteus and Leucocytozoon, recent phylogenetic studies revealed a tendency to infect closely related host species, suggesting that they possess narrow host specificity (Ejiri et al., 2011; Ellis et al., 2020). In contrast, Plasmodium lineages are also found in more distantly related avian host species (Kazlauskiene et al., 2013; Ellis et al., 2014, 2018; Platonova et al., 2021) as the co-speciation patterns with their avian host are not rigidly refined to a specific avian genus (Carreno et al., 1997). The relationship between avian hosts and haemosporidian parasites is influenced by various ecological factors such as exposure to vectors, vertical habitat niche preferences and avian migratory lifestyle (Ellis et al., 2014; Ricklefs et al., 2017; Galen et al., 2018a). Insight into phylogenetic relationships among haemosporidian parasites helps identify proportions of similarities in host preferences across available avian fauna and trace the co-evolutionary history between parasites and their avian hosts (Hellgren et al., 2007; Rutledge et al., 2017; Córdoba et al., 2021). This inference can determine whether closely related parasites commonly infect similar arrays of bird species and thus estimate the width of host specificity in Plasmodium lineages (De la Torre et al., 2022).

Haemosporidian cytochrome b (cytb) gene is a reliable and commonly used genetic marker for identification of haemosporidian lineages, delimitation of novel species and for reconstruction of phylogenetic relationships (Ishtiaq et al., 2007; Galen et al., 2018a; Noni and Tan, 2023). It is commonly used for investigating the prevalence and diversity of the haemosporidian parasites across a wide range of avian taxa (Ishtiaq et al., 2006; Ricklefs et al., 2017). Moreover, mitochondrial cytb allows detection of species boundaries between Plasmodium, Haemoproteus and Leucocytozoon lineage with the use of the genetic distance-based and tree-based species delimitation methods (Galen et al., 2018a; Oliveira et al., 2020). Species delimitation by genetic distance and tree-based methods allow detection of species boundaries between lineages and identification of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) without the use of morphological data. Species delimitation methods have already provided inference of species borderline between lineages in various parasite taxa (Galen et al., 2018a; Madrid et al., 2020; Nazarizadeh et al., 2023). The continuously growing number of haemosporidian lineages stored on the MalAvi database represents a relevant domain where additional inference of species boundaries by genetic and morphological means is required (Bensch et al., 2009a). The majority of haemosporidian cytb sequences have only assigned lineage names, whereas a significantly smaller subset contains a taxonomic species status. As the official consensus for delimitation of new lineages does not exist yet, a difference in a single nucleotide polymorphic site proved sufficient to denote novel lineages apart, and in certain case studies, a novel species (Galen et al., 2018a; Noni and Tan, 2023). Prevalence and diversity of haemosporidian parasites have been monitored extensively within avian species throughout European temperate habitats (Hellgren, 2005; Svoboda et al., 2015; Von Rönn et al., 2015; Dimitrov et al., 2018), whereas infrequent studies across the Indomalaya realm disclose rich haemosporidian fauna by numerous novel lineages (Ishtiaq et al., 2007; Muriel et al., 2021; Noni and Tan, 2023). Despite the growing body of research focused on monitoring haemosporidian parasites in songbirds, a limited number of studies have applied next-generation sequencing (NGS) to compare the diversity of haemosporidian parasites across different songbird species (Pacheco et al., 2018; Šujanová et al., 2021). This advanced sequencing technology offers a more comprehensive and precise examination of parasite diversity and genetic variation, enabling researchers to uncover cryptic and hidden species and better understand the complexities of host-parasite interactions (Noni and Tan, 2023). The application of NGS can facilitate the identification of low-frequency parasite lineages and provides an accurate inference on prevalence and diversity that ultimately contributes to more effective conservation strategies and disease management practices. Modern molecular tools, including genomic sequence-capture approaches, now enable the recovery of hidden haemosporidian diversity directly from infected birds, offering unprecedented resolution for exploring parasite population structure, evolutionary history, and host-parasite co-evolution beyond standard Sanger sequencing. These advances highlight the value of integrating phylogenetic frameworks with NGS to better characterize parasite diversity and host associations at regional scales (Ellis et al., 2014; Ricklefs et al., 2017; Galen et al., 2018a).

Considering that the Indomalaya biogeographical realm harbors high avian diversity (Spaans et al., 2018), the occasional avian studies revealed abundant novel diversity of haemosporidian parasites (Ishtiaq et al., 2007; Ivanova et al., 2015; Pattaradilokrat et al., 2015; Muriel et al., 2021). Therefore, further investigations of avian haemosporidians in this region will discover novel lineages and record the new localities of known Plasmodium lineages. Specifically, for the first time in Vietnam area, in this study we: (i) Describe the prevalence and diversity of haemosporidian parasites within three passerine model species commonly breeding in tropical forests of southeast Asia; (ii) Reconstruct the phylogenetic relationships between newly discovered and already known species of haemosporidian parasites; (iii) Estimate the number of haemosporidian operational taxonomic units (OTU) using four species delimitation methods; (iv) Calculate alpha and beta diversity of haemosporidian fauna within and across passerine host species.

Materials and methods

Study species, sampling, and PCR amplification

We selected three regularly breeding passerine species due to their relatively frequent occurrence in the forests and wetlands of National Park Cát Tiên Lam Dong (107.40°E, 11.41°N), Vietnam. Three focal species include Arachnothera longirostra, Copsychus malabaricus, and Pitta moluccensis. The little spiderhunter (A. longirostra) is a sedentary small-sized passerine from the sunbird family Nectariniidae. Its curved bill shape enables preying on spiders and other arthropods, although it regularly feeds on flower nectar within forest edges, mangrove and secondary forests. Species of the genus Arachnothera are widespread from the Indian subcontinent, across Southeast Asia, the Malayan islands, up to the Philippines (Dickinson, 2003; Lohman et al., 2010; Rahman et al., 2010; Nor Hashim and Ramli, 2013; Constantinescu et al., 2014). The white-rumped shama (C. malabaricus or Kittacincla malabarica) is a non-migratory insectivorous flycatcher (Muscicapidae) that commonly breeds in mixed Dipterocarpus (Dipterocarpaceae) and bamboo forests, forest clearings and secondary regrown forests across the Indomalaya realm (Fan et al., 2009; Ng et al., 2017). The blue-winged pitta (P. moluccensis) is a migratory ground-foraging suboscine passerine (Pittidae) commonly found in overgrown understories of primary and secondary forests of Southeast Asia and the Malayan region (Ericson et al., 2019; Erritzoe, 2020).

Birds were attracted using vocal recordings of a respective species and trapped with mist nets. Birds were captured in morning hours from 6:30 to 10:30 h local time (GMT+7) within forest edges of Cát Tiên National Park, Lam Dong, Vietnam. Blood samples (c.10–60 μl) were obtained from the bird’s ulnar vein by venipuncture and collected by non-heparinized capillary tubes (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Drawn blood was stored in 70% ethanol at 4 °C. Samples were collected during the breeding season (February-May) in three consecutive years (2018–2020).

DNA extraction was performed using the Qiagen DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit following the manufacturer’s instructions. Double-stranded DNA concentration was measured with a Qubit Flex Fluorometer (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). To distinguish infected from non-infected samples, amplification of the Plasmodium cytochrome b (cytb) gene was performed using two nested PCR reactions following Hellgren et al. (2004). In the first PCR run, the primer pair HaemNFI (5′-CAT ATA TTA AGA GAA ITA TGG AG-3′) and HaemNR3 (5′-ATA GAA AGA TAA GAA ATA CCA TTC-3′) were used. In the second PCR run, Plasmodium/Haemoproteus-specific primers HaemF (5′-ATG GTG CTT TCG ATA TAT GCA TG-3′) and HaemR2 (5′-GCA TTA TCT GGA TGT GAT AAT GGT-3′), producing an expected fragment of approximately 478 bp (Hellgren et al., 2004), were used. Each PCR run included positive controls (DNA from previously confirmed Plasmodium-infected samples) and negative controls (nuclease-free water) to validate results and detect contamination. PCR products were visualized on 1.5% agarose gels stained with ethidium bromide. Samples showing the expected 478 bp band were scored as positive, while samples that showed no amplification in the first run were re-amplified with fresh reagents. Only samples that remained negative after two independent PCR runs were classified as negative. PCR products were purified using AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter, Brea, California, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Amplicon library preparation, sequencing, and data processing

Only PCR-positive samples were selected for amplicon library preparation and bioinformatic processing to obtain high-quality Plasmodium cytb sequences from Illumina data for accurate lineage identification and downstream diversity analyses. Libraries were prepared following the Illumina 16S Metagenomic Sequencing Library Preparation protocol. Samples were indexed with the Illumina Nextera XT kit (Illumina, San Diego, USA), normalized, and pooled into a single library with a final concentration of 25 nM. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina MiSeq platform (250 bp paired-end reads) by Novogene (Cambridge, UK). Raw reads were demultiplexed, and adapter sequences were removed by the sequencing service provider. All raw data were deposited in the GenBank database under the accession numbers listed in Supplementary file S1. Read quality was assessed with FastQC v0.12.0 to confirm Phred scores, read length, and total read count. Primer sequences (HaemF-HaemR2) and Nextera index tags were removed using Trimmomatic v0.39 (Bolger et al., 2014). Reads were processed in R using the DADA2 pipeline (Callahan et al., 2016), including quality filtering, dereplication, chimera removal, and merging. Forward and reverse reads were trimmed to 207 bp and 218 bp, respectively, using the following settings in the filterAndTrim function: maxN = 0; maxEE = c(2,2); truncQ = 5; rm.phix = TRUE; matchIDs = TRUE; multithread = TRUE. Paired reads were merged using the mergePairs function with the option “justConcatenate = TRUE”, allowing concatenation without overlap. Additionally, placeholder ‘N’ bases were added to preserve the reading frame.

Individual birds were considered to carry multiple infections when sequencing results revealed the presence of two or more distinct Plasmodium lineages (OTUs) in the same sample. To reduce the risk of including sequencing artefacts or low-level contaminants, only lineages that (i) appeared in at least two independent PCR reactions or samples, (ii) represented ≥ 5% of the total read count within that sample, and (iii) consisted of at least 100 reads per sub-library were retained. This approach allowed reliable detection of double, triple, or quadruple infections per individual, as also reported in previous studies (Drews and Westerdahl, 2019; Roved et al., 2022). After filtering, sequence length, quality, and translation frame were verified in Geneious Prime® v2020.1.2 (https://www.geneious.com). Plasmodium lineages were identified using BLAST searches against the MalAvi database (Bensch et al., 2009a).

Mitochondrial analysis

Plasmodium cytb sequences were trimmed and aligned using the ClustalW algorithm implemented in Geneious Prime® v2020.1.2 (Biomatters Ltd., Auckland, New Zealand). Mitochondrial DNA polymorphism, as well as nucleotide and haplotype diversity statistics for each Plasmodium lineage, grouped independently by host species, were calculated using DnaSP v6.12.1 (Rozas et al., 2017). Haplotype relationships were inferred using TCS (Templeton, Crandall, and Sing) networks generated in PopArt v1.7 (Leigh and Bryant, 2015). The Plasmodium mitochondrial dataset from all three avian host species was combined to visualize the number and prevalence of shared and host-specific lineages (Leigh and Bryant, 2015). The novel cytb sequences were deposited in the NCBI (GenBank accession numbers: PQ817137-PQ817146) and the MalAvi database (https://130.235.244.92/Malavi/about.html; Bensch et al., 2009a), and are listed in Supplementary file S1.

Species delimitation computation analysis

Given the limitations of current databases (MalAvi and NCBI), particularly the low number of haemosporidian lineages with formal taxonomic descriptions, we conducted a series of single-locus species delimitation analyses to explore potential species boundaries between evolutionary lineages within our dataset. Relying on widely used single-locus markers, these analyses offer a standardized means of inferring genetic clusters (Galen et al., 2018a; Oliveira et al., 2020; Nazarizadeh et al., 2023). We define each distinct cluster as an Operational Taxonomic Unit (OTU): a unique sequence group that approximates species-level boundaries under a single-locus framework (Galen et al., 2018a; Oliveira et al., 2020; Nazarizadeh et al., 2023). Although OTUs replace formal species designations in the absence of morphological or multilocus data, they provide a practical scaffold for recognizing putative evolutionary lineages within the dataset.

Detection of lineages in the Plasmodium species complex was carried out using two genetic distance-based and two tree-based species delimitation model tools available in open source “ITaxoTools” repository (Vences et al., 2021). Genetic distance-based species delimitation included: Assemble Species by Automatic Partitioning (ASAP), which identifies species boundaries by detecting significant gaps in genetic distances among sequences, requiring no predefined thresholds (Puillandre et al., 2021); and Automatic Barcode Gap Discovery (ABGD), which uses the Jukes-Cantor (JC69) nucleotide substitution model to statistically identify barcode gaps separating species groups (Puillandre et al., 2012). Tree-based delimitation methods comprised: Bayesian Poisson Tree Processes (bPTP), utilizing Bayesian inference to detect species boundaries based on branching patterns indicative of speciation events within phylogenetic trees (Zhang et al., 2013), run for 500,000 MCMC generations with a thinning interval of 500; and Generalized Mixed Yule Coalescent (GMYC), which statistically identifies thresholds between species-level diversification and population-level coalescence from branching patterns within ultrametric phylogenetic trees (Pons et al., 2006). Genetic distance-based analyses (ASAP and ABGD) utilized a dataset of 152 Plasmodium cytb sequences from three avian host species. For tree-based analyses (bPTP and GMYC), this dataset was expanded by including sequences for three outgroup species, Leucocytozoon_neotropicalis (L_PIRIE01), Leucocytozoon majoris (L_CB1), and Leucocytozoon fringillinarum (L_TFUS04), downloaded from the MalAvi database.

We employed these four complementary singlelocus delimitation methods (ASAP, ABGD, bPTP, GMYC) to mitigate methodspecific biases and increase confidence in lineage boundaries inferred from cytb. We adopted an a priori consensus criterion: clusters congruently inferred by at least three of the four delimitation methods were recognized as OTUs, whereas discordant cases were conservatively lumped to avoid oversplitting. This cross-validation across distance and tree-based frameworks accords with current recommendations for avian haemosporidians, where formal taxonomy is incomplete and single-marker datasets benefit from methodological triangulation (Bensch et al., 2009a; Outlaw and Ricklefs, 2014; Galen et al., 2018a).

Plasmodium phylogenetic analysis and lineage identification

Taxonomy of the filtered cytb lineages were analyzed to the level of genus with the use of Basic Local Alignment Search Tool software (BLAST) (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi) and MalAvi databases (Bensch et al., 2009a). Novel Plasmodium lineage names were abbreviated from avian host species Latin name (see Supplementary file S1). Substitution models set up for construction of a phylogenetic relationships between Plasmodium variants were reconstructed with use of Bayesian inference (BI) in BEAST v.1.8 and Mrbayes v.3.2.6 with GTR+I+X and GTR+I+G substitutional models, respectively (Ronquist and Huelsenbeck, 2003). The models were selected using the greedy algorithm in PartitionFinder v.2.1.1. (Lanfear et al., 2017). Coalescent tree in BEAST software was calculated with the use of an exponential relaxed clock and prior of 50 million generations with 10 thousand sample rates. Verification of sufficient prior and posterior ESS values (above 200) was carried out in Tracer v.1.7.2 (Rambaut et al., 2018). TreeAnotator was used to generate a tree with a 25% “burn in” (Drummond et al., 2012). MrBayes analysis was carried out with 10 million MCMC and with sample frequency of every 100 trees. The final phylogeny of Plasmodium lineages was visualized with FigTree v1.4.4. (https://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/). Tree Leucocytozoon species (L_TFUS04 Leucocytozoon fringillinarum, L_CB1 Leucocytozoon majoris, and L_PIRIE01 Leucocytozoon neotropicalis) were used as outgroup for inferring phylogenetic relation between retrieved Plasmodium lineages. Similarly, for reconstruction of phylogenetic relationship of novel lineages from this study and previously described species, we included three Theileria parva (GenBank: Z23263.1), Theileria luwenshuni (GenBank: ON684326.1), and Theileria orientalis (CP102585.1) sequences from a sister taxa as suitable outgroup of collective dataset composed of species of Plasmodium, Haemoproteus and Leucocytozoon (Kumar et al., 2022).

Alpha and beta diversity

Prevalence and abundance of Plasmodium lineages and OTUs per avian host species were calculated using the Microbiota Amplicon Characterization Implement (MOCHI) tools (Zheng et al., 2022). We quantified within-host species diversity (alpha diversity) and between-host species community differences (beta diversity) to test whether parasite assemblages differ among the three bird hosts, e.g. due to species-specific habitat use and potential vector exposure. Our objectives were to characterize (i) how many Plasmodium lineages occur within each host species and how evenly they are distributed, and (ii) whether the overall Plasmodium community composition clusters by host species. For alpha diversity, we used Shannon’s index (sensitive to both richness and evenness) and Simpson’s index (less influenced by rare lineages, emphasizing dominance) to capture complementary aspects of diversity. Employing both indices reduces the risk of conclusions being driven by a single metric: higher Shannon values indicate more lineages with balanced abundances, whereas lower Simpson values indicate dominance by one or a few lineages. Evenness was assessed using Shannon- and Simpson-based measures, which indicate how uniformly lineages are distributed (values closer to 1 represent greater evenness). For beta diversity, we used weighted UniFrac, which integrates phylogenetic relatedness of lineages with their relative abundances, providing a phylogenetically informed measure of community dissimilarity well-suited to mitochondrial lineage datasets.

Statistical comparisons of alpha diversity metrics across different avian host species were conducted using the Kruskal-Wallis test. To further explore significant differences among groups, post-hoc pairwise comparisons were carried out using Dunn’s test. The resulting distance matrices were visualized using Principal Coordinates Analysis (PCoA), which enabled the identification of clustering patterns in Plasmodium communities across host species. To statistically validate the observed differences in community composition, a series of non-parametric multivariate analyses were performed, including Permutational Multivariate Analysis of Variance (PERMANOVA), Analysis of Similarities (ANOSIM), and Multi-Response Permutation Procedures (MRPP) (Zheng et al., 2022). These analyses were all executed within the MOCHI environment. PERMANOVA was used to quantify the proportion of variance in community composition explained by host species, while ANOSIM and MRPP provided complementary tests to further confirm the robustness of the observed differences among avian host-associated Plasmodium assemblages.

Results

Genetic diversity and prevalence of Plasmodium infections

Out of the 138 passerine specimens sampled, 93 individuals (69%) carried a Plasmodium infection. All the haemosporidian infections observed in individuals of the three passerine species were caused exclusively by lineages from the genus Plasmodium. None of the detected lineages were associated with the genera Haemoproteus and Leucocytozoon. Multiple infections, defined as the presence of two or more distinct high-confidence Plasmodium OTUs in a single individual (see Section 2), were identified in 26.1% (36/138) of all sampled birds (Supplementary file S2). Specifically, double infections were recorded in 15.9% (22/138) of birds, while triple and quadruple infections were found in 8.7% (12/138) and 1.4% (2/138) of birds, respectively. The lowest prevalence of infection was detected in A. longirostra with 44.6% of infected birds (individuals carrying single or multiple infections). High prevalence of Plasmodium infection was evident in C. malabaricus and P. moluccensis, with 83.3% and 82.1% of birds found positive, respectively. Double infections were relatively equally present in A. longirostra and C. malabaricus, with prevalences of 10.7% and 13.0%, respectively, whilst the highest double infection rate was observed in P. moluccensis (32.1%).

Collectively, 12 different Plasmodium lineages were detected in the three avian host species. A detailed insight into single and multiple infections is provided in Table 1 and Supplementary file S2. No significant difference in the prevalence of Plasmodium infection among species was observed (Kruskal-Wallis test, χ2 = 2, df = 2, P = 0.37).

Species delimitation analysis of Plasmodium lineages

Three of the four species delimitation methods (ASAP, ABGD and bPTP) provided identical results in inferring species boundaries among the 12 Plasmodium lineages, identifying five possible operational taxonomic units (OTUs, Fig. 1). Specifically, lineages P_ARALON1, P_ARALON2, P_ARALON3, P_ARALON4, P_ARALON5 and P_ARALON6 were recognized as a singular operational taxonomic unit OTU1 (green in Fig. 1). Lineage P_COPMAL3 and lineage P_COPMAL4 were delimited as independent OTU2 (blue in Fig. 1) and OTU3 (orange in Fig. 1) units, respectively. Lastly, lineages (P_COPMAL1, P_COPMAL2) and (P_PITMOL1, P_PITMOL2) were delimited as the OTU4 (purple in Fig. 1) and OTU5 (red in Fig. 1) Plasmodium species (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). OTU1 was found to be the most abundant and most prevalent subset of the Plasmodium lineages, infecting all three passerine species. OTU2 has been detected in a few individuals of A. longirostra and C. malabaricus. The least prevalent OTU3 and OTU4 were detected only in a few individuals of C. malabaricus. Lastly, OTU5 was found to infect only individuals of P. moluccensis. In contrast, the Bayesian GMYC method overestimated the number of delimited species (Fig. 1), which is manifested in delimiting multiple OTU clusters out of a single Plasmodium cytb lineage. This resulted in generating more OTUs by GMYC than the number of cytb lineages available in the dataset.

The TCS haplotype network depicted a clear separation of the Plasmodium OTU units by a high number of single-nucleotide polymorphisms (Fig. 2). Each of the OTU units was separated by a few dozen single-nucleotide polymorphisms, while the distance between lineages within a single OTU ranged from one to three mutational steps.

Phylogenetic relationships of Plasmodium lineages

The phylogenetic dataset was composed of haemosporidian cytb sequences obtained from the MalAvi database. Sequences that contained a complete taxonomic species status were used. Therefore, each species was represented by a single cytb sequence (Fig. 3). OTU1, which comprises five Plasmodium lineages (P_ARALON1, P_ARALON2, P_ARALON3, P_ARALON4, P_ARALON5, and P_ARALON6) formed a single branch that was closely related to Plasmodium delichoni, Plasmodium ashfordi, Plasmodium nucleophilum, Plasmodium collidatum, and Plasmodium paranucleophilum. Collectively, these species formed a monophyletic subset with 0.94 posterior probability (pp). OTU2 (P_COPMAL3) showed the closest genetic relationship with Plasmodium matutinum, Plasmodium lutzi, and Plasmodium homocircumflexum (pp = 0.55). Furthermore, OTU3 (P_COPMAL4) was positioned as a single branch with Plasmodium elongatum with maximum posterior probability (pp = 1). Alpha and beta diversity of Plasmodium fauna across avian host species

We found statistically significant differences of Plasmodium lineages among the three avian host species using the Kruskal-Wallis statistical method for Simpson (P = 0.001) and Shannon alpha diversity indices (P = 0.001). Similarly to the alpha diversity indices, the Simpson evenness index of alpha diversity showed statistically significant differences of Plasmodium lineages among the avian species studied (P < 0.001), whereas the Shannon index estimated a non-significant evenness of Plasmodium (P = 0.679).

The post-hoc pairwise statistical analysis of the alpha diversity estimated by the Simpson and Shannon index verified significant differences in Plasmodium lineages between A. longirostra and P. moluccensis (P = 0.002), and between C. malabaricus and P. moluccensis (P = 0.001) (Table 2). Moreover, post-hoc pairwise statistical analysis of evenness alpha diversity indices showed a significant difference in Plasmodium lineages between A. longirostra and P. moluccensis (P = 0.005), and between P. moluccensis and C. malabaricus (P = 0.001) for the Simpson’s statistics, but not for the Shannon index. None of the diversity tests were significant between A. longirostra and C. malabaricus, suggesting a lack of Plasmodium differentiation (Table 2).

Discussion

Overview of genetic diversity

The Indomalaya realm is well known for its high avian biodiversity (Robson and Allen, 2005). Despite the fact that only a handful of studies on avian haemosporidian in India, Myanmar, Malaysia and South Korea are available, each of them reported rich diversity of Plasmodium spp. and Haemoproteus spp., showcasing an affluent occurrence of novel lineages (Ishtiaq et al., 2007; Ivanova et al., 2015; Muriel et al., 2021). To fill the knowledge gap, our study spanning three consecutive breeding seasons (2018–2020) in Cát Tiên National Park, Vietnam, provided comprehensive insights into the prevalence and diversity of Plasmodium lineages in three avian hosts commonly found in the tropical forests and wetlands of the Indomalaya realm (A. longirostra, C. malabaricus, and P. moluccensis) (Bushuev et al., 2021). Currently, the number of haemosporidian lineages found to infect A. longirostra is scant, with only two Haemoproteus lineages identified (Ishtiaq et al., 2007). Previous literature reported that in C. malabaricus three Haemoproteus and two Plasmodium lineages were identified in total. Lastly, there were no records on haemosporidian infection in P. moluccensis on the MalAvi database (Bensch et al., 2009b; Ivanova et al., 2015; Muriel et al., 2021).

The result of the present study, using amplicon-based NGS sequencing, revealed a high level of hidden Plasmodium diversity among the three passerine bird species, identifying 12 genetic lineages. The high-resolution data obtained using amplicon sequencing revealed intricate host-parasite associations and provided new insights into the prevalence of avian malaria in Southeast Asia. The amplification of cytb genes of Plasmodium proved to be equally successful in all three passerine host species. The Plasmodium lineages found in single and multiple infections were accurately inferred by the DADA2 toolkit (Callahan et al., 2016). Our findings are consistent with recent studies employing high-throughput sequencing, which repeatedly demonstrate that NGS significantly enhances the resolution of parasite diversity screening compared to traditional Sanger sequencing methods. For instance, previous studies on avian haemosporidians using Sanger sequencing often reported fewer parasite lineages and consistently missed lower-abundance or mixed infections due to reliance on dominant consensus sequences, leading to underestimates of true diversity (Egizi et al., 2013; Ogbuigwe et al., 2025). In contrast, recent NGS-based approaches revealed extensive intra-host genetic diversity, unmasking co-infections that were hidden or unresolved by standard techniques (Bailly et al., 2022; Ogbuigwe et al., 2025). Similarly, our study revealed a relatively high incidence of multiple Plasmodium infections, with more than one-quarter of the sampled birds harboring two or more parasite lineages. Comparable investigations in Southeast Asian avifauna using NGS also reported substantially higher co-infection rates and uncovered secluded parasite lineages not detectable by conventional sequencing (Ogbuigwe et al., 2025). Therefore, our results further confirm the superior sensitivity and discriminatory power of NGS methodologies in revealing the complex ecological dynamics and hidden genetic diversity of avian malaria parasites.

Species delimitation analysis finds five putative species lineages

We discovered two known and ten novel Plasmodium lineages, whereas lineages of other haemosporidian genera were absent. This likely reflects the fact that our sampling took place at forest edges and wetlands in the lowland of Cát Tiên during the breeding season, a setting that favors mosquito (e.g. Culex spp.) abundance and the transmission of Plasmodium spp. In contrast, vectors of Haemoproteus spp. (Culicoides biting midges and some hippoboscids) and Leucocytozoon (blackflies, Simuliidae) are often patchy, microhabitat-restricted, and strongly seasonal in tropical forests (Valkiunas, 2005; Medeiros et al., 2015; Yanmanee et al., 2023). The three focal bird species also exploit distinct strata and resources: nectar-seeking A. longirostra at edges and mid-story flowers, understory- and clearing-foraging C. malabaricus, and ground-dwelling P. moluccensis. These ecological differences can differentially expose them to mosquito guilds while limiting encounters with blackflies (typically associated with well-oxygenated, flowing streams) or with Culicoides spp. concentrated in specific moist substrates and openings (Dickinson, 2003; Nor Hashim and Ramli, 2013; Ng et al., 2017). Such host-vector contact heterogeneity can yield parasite assemblages dominated by Plasmodium spp. even where other genera occur regionally (Ishtiaq et al., 2007; Ivanova et al., 2015; Bushuev et al., 2021; Muriel et al., 2021). Therefore, the absence of Haemoproteus spp. and Leucocytozoon spp. in our dataset likely reflects the intersection of local vector communities, seasonal dynamics, and host behavior/stratum use rather than true absence from the broader avifauna.

Of the 12 Plasmodium lineages recovered, three delimitation methods (ASAP, ABGD, and bPTP) converged on five well-differentiated OTUs, whereas GMYC inferred many more OTUs, often splitting single lineages into multiple units (Fig. 1). This mismatch aligns with the documented tendency of GMYC to overestimate species numbers under certain conditions (Talavera et al., 2013; Kamali et al., 2024) and with other findings from avian malaria, where GMYC erroneously divided single Plasmodium lineages (Galen et al., 2018b). Similarly, in our dataset, the likely causes of GMYC’s overestimation include shallow within-lineage sampling (many lineages represented by few samples), uneven coalescent depths across the dataset, and uncertainty in ultrametric-tree inference. GMYC is particularly sensitive to such branching-rate heterogeneity (e.g. deep coalescent events and variable diversification rates) (Guo and Kong, 2022), which can spuriously split a single evolutionary lineage into multiple OTUs. We therefore prioritize the congruent ASAP, ABGD, and bPTP results: five robust OTUs, each separated by clear inter-lineage polymorphisms and supported by numerous polymorphic sites that minimize ambiguity in species boundaries (Fig. 2). These five genetic clusters likely represent species-level lineages, yet formal taxonomic validation, especially morphological and life-history evidence, will be required prior to formal description (Garnham, 1966; Valkiunas, 2005; Valkiunas and Iezhova, 2018).

Phylogenetic relationships of Plasmodium OTUs show no strict specificity towards the three hosts

The phylogenetic clustering of novel OTU units with previously described Plasmodium species revealed that sympatric OTU units are not phylogenetically closely related (Fig. 3), a finding which is in agreement with previous studies (Ishtiaq et al., 2007; Medeiros et al., 2015). This phylogenetic relationship provides a fractional insight into the host-parasite assemblage and host preferences. Moreover, Ellis and Bensch (2018) uncovered that phylogenetically closely related species of Plasmodium do not necessarily exhibit similarity in host preferences and do not possess a defined range of avian species they infect. In fact, multiple species of Plasmodium demonstrate the ability to infect a broad range of avian species across various families of songbirds (Valkiunas, 2005; Valkiūnas et al., 2006). This might imply that the specificity of vector-parasite assemblages is associated with higher taxonomic levels of vector fauna, such as family or genus. Specifically, the transmission of Plasmodium species infecting similar sets of avian species is facilitated by availability of sympatric dipteran vectors targeting a similar range of vertebrate host species (Gager et al., 2008; Medeiros et al., 2015). For example, the presence of Culex mosquito species significantly increases the likelihood of avian hosts becoming infected with multiple Plasmodium species (Medeiros et al., 2015). Due to the generalist lifestyle of Plasmodium spp., multiple studies have found no direct relationship between Plasmodium spp. prevalence and avian host specificity (Ricklefs et al., 2002; Durrant et al., 2006; Medeiros et al., 2015; Ellis et al., 2020). Instead, the adaptability of Plasmodium spp. enables exploitation of various avian hosts across diverse habitats, suggesting potential co-evolutionary dynamics where hosts develop resistance mechanisms and parasites counter with new strategies to evade the host’s immune defenses. Moreover, avian population abundance and body mass have a minor effect on the prevalence and diversity of haemosporidian species parasitizing within a single host species (Ellis et al., 2020). This interaction between host characteristics and parasite adaptability shapes the distribution, prevalence, and impact of avian haemosporidians in different bird populations, emphasizing the significance of vectors in avian-haemosporidian system studies (Medeiros et al., 2015; Yanmanee et al., 2023).

Alpha and beta diversity of Plasmodium across host species

Alpha diversity, which includes both the number of species (species richness) and their relative abundances (species evenness) within a local assemblage (Gotelli and Ellison, 2006), was thoroughly assessed in the present study. Our analysis revealed significant variation in Plasmodium cytb lineage diversity among the three avian host species. Specifically, the Shannon and Simpson indices indicated that the parasite assemblage in P. moluccensis was significantly less diverse and less evenly distributed than those in A. longirostra and C. malabaricus. This result was corroborated by significant z-values and P-values for both diversity indices. P. moluccensis exhibited markedly lower parasite diversity and evenness, characterized by dominance of only one or a few Plasmodium lineages. In contrast, multiple lineages were more evenly distributed in A. longirostra and C. malabaricus hosts. Although the three avian species studied occur in sympatry, they exploit distinct resource niches and forest vegetation strata in the tropical forests of Southeast Asia (Dickinson, 2003; Nor Hashim and Ramli, 2013; Ng et al., 2017), likely influencing their exposure and susceptibility to different parasite assemblages. Such differences suggest a degree of host specialization or limited parasite exchange, aligning with the idea that certain hosts can act as ecological filters, maintaining only a subset of the broader parasite diversity present in their environment (Fecchio et al., 2019a).

In addition to the alpha diversity patterns, the beta diversity analyses (PERMANOVA, ANOSIM, and MRPP) highlighted pronounced differences in Plasmodium assemblage composition among the avian hosts. All three indices consistently pointed to clear host-specific patterns in parasite assemblages, indicating that each bird species harbors a distinct subset of Plasmodium lineages. These results imply that parasite assemblages were more similar among individuals of the same host species and markedly different between individuals of different host species, a finding consistent with previous studies showing that host identity strongly structures haemosporidian assemblages (Scordato et al., 2014; Fecchio et al., 2019a; Schares et al., 2020). Moreover, our results revealed that the strongest separation of Plasmodium assemblages is observed between C. malabaricus and P. moluccensis, indicated by all three beta diversity metrics. Thus, despite the lack of strict host specificity of the OTUs recorded in the phylogenetic analysis, both the alpha and beta diversity indices of Plasmodium lineages in the three avian host species indicate species-specific exposure to a fraction of the total Plasmodium fauna (Table 2, Table 3, Fig. 2). This pattern likely reflects ecological and behavioral differences: C. malabaricus occupy dense understory thickets and forage close to the ground (Wells, 2010; Davison, 2022), A. longirostra specialize in nectar feeding in the lower to mid-story vegetation (del Hoyo, 2007; Round, 2008), and P. moluccensis are ground-dwelling insectivores that probe leaf litter in moist undergrowth (Wells, 2010; Davison, 2022). While A. longirostra and P. moluccensis differ in foraging mode, they often overlap in secondary forests and edge habitats, which may facilitate greater sharing of parasite fauna, whereas the more specialized microhabitat use of C. malabaricus may expose them to a distinct set of mosquito vectors and Plasmodium lineages. Similar host-driven structuring of haemosporidian communities has been reported in other systems. In Amazonian bird communities, sites with similar host composition tended to harbor similar parasite assemblages (Fecchio et al., 2018a, 2018b; Garcia-Longoria et al., 2022), while in European forests, variation in forest structure affected parasite prevalence in a species-specific manner, implying that hosts with overlapping niches often support more similar parasite communities (van Hoesel et al., 2020).

Our findings of significant host-related differences in Plasmodium diversity and composition dovetail with some patterns reported in the literature, while contrasting with others, reflecting the complexity of host specificity in avian malaria. On one hand, numerous studies have demonstrated that avian malaria parasites often exhibit host specificity at some taxonomic or ecological level (Bensch et al., 2000; Križanauskiene et al., 2006; Huang et al., 2015). For example, broad-scale analyses show that avian Plasmodium parasites tend to infect phylogenetically related groups of hosts – in effect behaving as phylogenetic host specialists that exploit clustered subsets of the available host community (Fecchio et al., 2019b; Ellis et al., 2020). This tendency could explain why P. moluccensis hosts a different parasite assemblage than A. longirostra and C. malabaricus in this study: parasites may specialize on certain bird lineages, and P. moluccensis-specific parasites might not readily infect birds of other families. Indeed, co-evolutionary theory predicts that over time, host-parasite associations can become tightly adapted. The co-evolution of avian malaria parasites with their hosts is known to shape host specificity and parasite distribution (Lauron et al., 2015; Ellis et al., 2020). Host-specialist parasite lineages can arise via long-term co-evolution with a particular host clade, leading to unique lineage-host pairings as observed in P. moluccensis. Such co-evolved specialists would manifest as lower diversity (if only a few specialized lineages infect that host) and distinct phylogenetic clustering of parasite lineages per host, exactly the pattern we report.

On the other hand, avian Plasmodium are also renowned for their ability to switch hosts and infect multiple species, which can blur strict host specificity (Ricklefs et al., 2002; Fecchio et al., 2018a). Some community surveys found that Plasmodium lineages are often shared among different host species, especially those living in the same area (Dimitrov et al., 2018; Fecchio et al., 2018b). For instance, comparative studies in Melanesia found that Plasmodium genetic variation was structured mainly by geography (islands) rather than by host species, implying that many Plasmodium lineages there were generalists infecting diverse birds (Olsson-Pons et al., 2015). In contrast, Haemoproteus parasites in that system showed strong host species associations (Olsson-Pons et al., 2015; Ellis et al., 2020). Our results suggest that, at least in the area of study, certain Plasmodium lineages display a degree of host preference more akin to a Haemoproteus-like pattern. The pronounced difference between P. moluccensis and the other hosts hints that some Plasmodium lineages may be effectively host-specific (infecting only P. moluccensis, for example) or that P. moluccensis has traits limiting it to a subset of parasites. This host specificity is further supported by the low evenness in P. moluccensis infections, i.e. one parasite genotype dominates, which is expected if only a specialized parasite can establish well in that host. Such a scenario resonates with the concept of host-parasite co-evolution driving specialization: over evolutionary time, P. moluccensis and its dominant Plasmodium strain might have developed a relatively exclusive relationship. Overall, our findings reinforce that avian Plasmodium host specificity is context dependent. In biodiverse tropical systems, even typically generalist parasites like Plasmodium can exhibit host-associated structure, especially when host species differ greatly in ecology or phylogeny (Fecchio et al., 2018b).

Conclusions

We found significant differences in Plasmodium variant diversity and evenness among A. longirostra, C. malabaricus and P. moluccensis, which highlight the role of host species in structuring parasite communities (Križanauskiene et al., 2006; Huang et al., 2015). These results support the view that host-parasite associations in wild bird communities are driven by a combination of co-evolutionary history and ecological fitting (Fecchio et al., 2018b; Bushuev et al., 2021). Host-specific parasite assemblages, as seen here, likely result from long-term co-evolution and selective pressures that favor certain parasite lineages in certain hosts, reinforced by ecological isolation (niche partitioning) among hosts. Our findings contribute to a growing recognition that avian malaria parasites are not uniformly opportunistic; rather, their distribution is filtered by host identity and ecology in important ways (Carreno et al., 1997; Fecchio et al., 2018a). Understanding these host-partitioned patterns is crucial for unravelling the evolutionary ecology of disease in biodiversity-rich systems. It also has practical implications for conservation and wildlife health, as it suggests that the vulnerability of bird species to malaria parasites can depend on unique host-parasite combinations and the integrity of ecological interactions that have evolved over time. Continued comparative research on host specificity in avian Plasmodium – integrating phylogenetic, ecological, and biogeographic perspectives – will further illuminate how host-parasite co-evolution and community assembly processes generate the diversity of malaria parasites observed in nature (Carreno et al., 1997; Galen et al., 2018a; Ellis et al., 2020). Such insights are key to predicting how these systems might respond to environmental changes, host species declines, or introductions, ultimately informing strategies for managing avian diseases in changing ecosystems.
