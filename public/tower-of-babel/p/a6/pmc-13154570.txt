A prognostic model utilizing disulfidptosis-related long noncoding RNAs to differentiate pathological grades in hepatocellular carcinoma: functional analysis of AL031985.3
Authors: Ling Yang, Fei Li, Hailin Yu, Xuefeng Gu, Wei You
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Disulfidptosis, a recently identified form of cell death, plays a crucial role in cancer progression, specifically by altering intracellular disulfide bonds within the actin cytoskeleton. Our research centres on developing a prognostic model based on disulfidptosis-related long noncoding RNAs (DRLRs) to differentiate high-grade (G3-G4) from low-grade (G1-G2) Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC). Utilizing transcriptomic data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA), we employed univariate Cox regression analysis, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator(LASSO) regression, and multivariate Cox regression to ultimately identify nine key drlncRNAs (AL442125.2, AC018529.2, AL031985.3, AC119150.1, LINC02256, AC026979.4, POLH-AS1, MED8-AS1, and AC026356.1). These were subsequently used to construct a prognostic risk signature model. This 9-DRLR model demonstrated robust discriminative capacity, achieving Area Under the Curve(AUCs) of 0.845, 0.841, and 0.885 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival prediction in G3-G4 HCC patients, respectively. The model provides strong prognostic stratification of HCC patients by grade and correlates with biological processes linked to tumour aggressiveness and immune evasion, as shown by Gene Ontology (GO) and gene set enrichment analyses (GSEA). High-risk groups exhibited markers of immune dysfunction, elevated tumour mutation burden (TMB), and increased sensitivity to targeted therapies including 5-Fluorouracil and Dasatinib. AL031985.3 emerged as a key lncRNA within this model, with its significant overexpression in HCC tissues versus adjacent controls (p < 0.001) validated through quantitative Real-Time PCR (qRT-PCR). Functional analyses revealed AL031985.3 as a critical oncogenic driver, where its knockdown significantly suppressed colony formation (p < 0.01) and reduced invasive capacity (p < 0.01) in vitro. These findings were corroborated in vivo, with orthotopic implantation and experimental pulmonary metastasis models demonstrating attenuated tumour growth and modulated epithelial-mesenchymal transition markers following AL031985.3 silencing. Our findings underscore the prognostic value of the DRLR signature and establish AL031985.3 as a potential therapeutic target for advanced HCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-026-04311-9.

Introduction

HCC constitutes the third most common cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [1]. Due to the vast heterogeneity in patient etiologies and clinical presentations, establishing a universally applicable staging system for HCC remains a formidable challenge. Unlike other solid tumours, the prognosis of liver cancer is influenced by two conditions: the underlying liver disease and the carcinoma itself [2]. The pathological grading of HCC is widely acknowledged as a pivotal determinant of post-treatment intrahepatic recurrence. Accurate staging is imperative for selecting appropriate therapeutic strategies and assessing patient prognosis [3, 4].

The Edmondson-Steiner (E-S) grading system, also known as the World Health Organization (WHO) grading system, serves as a pathological indicator of differentiation in HCC [5, 6]. The E-S grading system is histologically based on the shape and size of HCC cells. Within the TCGA database, the pathological grades for Liver Hepatocellular Carcinoma (TCGA-LIHC) are primarily determined by the degree of cellular differentiation using the E-S grading criteria. Grade 1 (well-differentiated) indicates that the liver cancer cells closely resemble normal hepatocytes, forming trabecular patterns with minimal nuclear atypia and rare mitotic figures. Grade 2 (moderately differentiated) signifies slight cellular atypia, with thickened trabecular arrangements and occasional mitotic activity. Grade 3 (poorly differentiated) denotes marked cellular atypia with increased mitotic figures and disordered or solid trabecular structures. Grade 4 (undifferentiated) reflects highly atypical liver cancer cells with loss of hepatic characteristics, diffuse growth, and active mitosis. Other clinical staging systems, such as the China Liver Cancer (CNLC) staging system based on the Child-Pugh score, have demonstrated significant utility in treatment planning and prognostic assessments. Several non-invasive liver function scoring models are employed in clinical practice, including Child-Pugh, albumin-bilirubin (ALBI), and albumin-indocyanine green (ALICE) scores [7].

Among the myriad staging classifications for liver cancer, the E-S grading system holds indispensable value; a higher degree of differentiation (Grades 1–2) generally portends a better prognosis, whereas poorer differentiation (Grades 3–4) correlates with worse outcomes, with E-S 3–4 grades being particularly associated with poor prognosis in HCC patients. Kim et al. have shown a significant association between E-S 3–4 grading and early HCC recurrence (< 1 year) [8]. Zhou et al. developed a novel nomogram model that successfully predicts E-S 3–4 grade HCC by integrating various clinicopathological factors [9]. Microvascular invasion (MVI) also constitutes a crucial predictive indicator for post-operative recurrence in HCC, with patients bearing E-S 3–4 grades having a 2.97-fold increased risk of MVI compared to well-differentiated counterparts [10]. Even in the absence of MVI, E-S grading can impact postoperative recurrence in HCC patients [11]. Xia et al. developed a new scoring system, termed microvascular invasion-satellite foci-Edmondson Steiner(MSE), based on three pathological factors: microvascular invasion, satellite foci, and differentiation grade. This system has proven effective in predicting both overall survival (OS) and recurrence-free survival (RFS) in resected HCC cases [12]. These findings underline the significance of the E-S grading system as a prognostic factor for HCC. Furthermore, with advancements in radiomics and artificial intelligence technologies, researchers are exploring the potential of non-invasive methods (e.g., Computed Tomography (CT), Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI), and contrast-enhanced ultrasound) for preoperative prediction of E-S grading, offering new tools for clinical decision-making. A cohort of 494 HCC liver resection cases has been utilized to train machine learning classifiers on CT radiomic features for the determination of MVI and E-S grading, establishing relevant histologic scores [13]. Mao et al. have developed a preoperative model based on gadolinium-ethoxybenzyl diethylenetriamine pentaacetic acid (Gd-EOB-DTPA)-enhanced MRI radiomics, capable of distinguishing high-grade HCC from low-grade HCC [14]. Subsequently, Wang et al. have utilized contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) through ensemble learning to improve the accuracy of E-S grading in HCC patients [15]. Emerging technologies, such as machine learning and CEUS, are increasingly being applied in E-S grading predictions; however, the consistency and reliability of predictive models and biomarkers across different studies still require further validation, and the relationship between E-S grading and the molecular mechanisms of HCC remains to be elucidated.

In summary, the E-S grading contributes to the formulation of individualized treatment plans, with well-differentiated tumours potentially more amenable to local therapies and poorly differentiated ones necessitating systemic treatment. Thus, refining the accuracy of pathological grading, exploring novel biomarkers, and developing non-invasive diagnostic technologies hold substantial significance for therapeutic decision-making and prognostic evaluation in HCC. In the context of E-S staging, G1-G2 is regarded as low-grade pathological grading, whereas G3-G4 is considered high-grade. Identifying novel specific markers distinguishing high-grade from low-grade pathological HCC forms the primary objective of our current research.

Dysregulation of regulated cell death (RCD), including apoptosis, necroptosis, and ferroptosis, plays a pivotal role in tumour progression [16]. Disulfide-dependent cell death is a newly identified type of RCD regulated by the cystine/glutamate antiporter SLC7A11 under glucose deprivation conditions [17]. Glucose deprivation disrupts the NADPH supply, while SLC7A11-mediated cystine uptake continuously depletes NADPH, leading to the accumulation of intracellular disulfide bonds on cytoskeletal proteins, ultimately inducing rapid cell death. Wang et al. found that combined treatment with endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress inhibitors and glucose transporter inhibitors (LUTi) significantly suppresses tumour growth both in vitro and in vivo by inducing disulfide stress and subsequent disulfide-dependent cell death [18]. This finding reveals the potential of disulfide-dependent apoptosis as a therapeutic intervention target in oncological settings.

Long-chain non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), transcripts exceeding 200 nucleotides in length, are incapable of translation into proteins [19]. Through the regulation of RNA, DNA, and proteins, they impact the differentiation, proliferation, and migration of cancer cells. Numerous studies have substantiated the potential of lncRNAs as biomarkers or therapeutic targets in HCC, with aberrant expression intimately linked to malignant progression. For instance, in HBV-associated liver cancer, HDAC2-AS2 promotes cancer progression by inhibiting the cytotoxicity of intratumoural CD8 + T cells [20]. High expression of LINC01532 results in lenvatinib resistance among liver cancer patients, leading to poor prognosis [21]. LncRNA FTX acts as an oncogene, enhancing the malignant characteristics of HCC via the RBMX/lnc-FTX interaction and the lncRNA FTX/miR-374a-3p/HMGB1 signalling pathway [22]. Collectively, these studies highlight the potential clinical applicability of lncRNAs in the diagnosis, treatment, prognosis, and recurrence of HCC.

Recent evidence has increasingly highlighted the pivotal role of non-coding RNA regulatory networks in modulating programmed cell death pathways and driving HCC heterogeneity. For instance, specific microRNAs have been shown to orchestrate ferroptosis by targeting key metabolic genes like FDX1, thereby influencing tumour progression [23]. Similarly, complex circular RNA-mediated competitive endogenous RNA (ceRNA) axes, such as the miR-873/SOX4 pathway, significantly contribute to the invasive and metastatic potential of HCC [24]. These mechanistic insights underscore the potential of non-coding transcripts, particularly long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), to serve as precision regulators of novel cell death modes like disulfidptosis.

The systematic identification of hub genes and candidate therapeutic targets via integrated bioinformatic frameworks has become a robust paradigm for dissecting the molecular landscape of liver cancer [25]. Despite ongoing efforts to explore novel intervention strategies, including those derived from natural products [26], there remains a pressing clinical need for reliable biomarkers that can stratify patients based on pathological grade and predict prognosis. Building upon these conceptual foundations, the present study aims to construct a prognostic model based on disulfidptosis-related lncRNAs (DRLRs) and investigate the functional significance of the representative transcript AL031985.3 in high-grade HCC.

The clinical significance of pathological grading in HCC has been increasingly recognised, with recent clinicopathological analyses demonstrating that Vav1 protein expression correlates significantly with tumour differentiation, Tumor, Node, Metastasis (TNM) stage, and patient prognosis [27]. This finding underscores the critical role of histological grade as a prognostic determinant and provides a logical foundation for stratifying patients based on differentiation status. Concurrently, the power of integrative bioinformatics in systematically identifying molecular targets in HCC has been robustly demonstrated, with studies successfully leveraging computational approaches to uncover hub genes and candidate therapeutic targets [25]. Such methodological frameworks offer confidence for our multi‑step screening strategy to identify disulfidptosis‑related long non‑coding RNAs (DRLRs) using TCGA data.

Beyond conventional transcriptomic analyses, recent proteogenomic investigations have linked metabolic reprogramming to novel prognostic markers. For instance, KIF5B has been identified as a key driver of HCC malignancy through disruption of metabolic homeostasis, with downstream signalling pathways beginning to be elucidated [28]. This provides a compelling framework for our hypothesis that DRLRs may promote high‑grade HCC progression by influencing disulfidptosis—a process intimately connected with cysteine metabolism and redox balance. The mechanistic parallels between metabolic dysregulation and disulfidptosis suggest that lncRNAs involved in these pathways may represent critical nodes in HCC pathogenesis.

Despite the burgeoning interest in long non‑coding RNAs (lncRNAs) and disulfidptosis in HCC research, several critical gaps remain unaddressed in the existing literature. First, while numerous lncRNA‑based prognostic signatures have been developed for HCC, the majority treat HCC as a homogeneous entity, overlooking the profound prognostic implications of pathological grading. The Edmondson‑Steiner grading system has been robustly associated with early recurrence, microvascular invasion, and overall survival, underscoring the necessity for grade‑specific prognostic models. Second, although disulfidptosis was only recently identified as a distinct form of regulated cell death, preliminary studies have begun exploring disulfidptosis‑related lncRNAs (DRLRs) in cancer. However, none of these studies have incorporated pathological grading as a core stratifying variable, nor have they systematically validated the biological functions of key DRLRs within their models. Third, the majority of published prognostic signatures remain purely computational, lacking experimental validation that bridges bioinformatic predictions with functional evidence—a critical step for establishing biological plausibility and clinical translatability.

To address these gaps, the present study introduces several novel elements. We present, to the best of our knowledge, the first DRLR‑based prognostic model specifically tailored for patients with poorly differentiated (G3‑G4) HCC, thereby acknowledging pathological grade as a pivotal determinant of clinical outcomes. Beyond model construction, we performed comprehensive experimental validation of the most clinically significant DRLR—AL031985.3—through in vitro functional assays, orthotopic tumour models, and experimental pulmonary metastasis studies, demonstrating its oncogenic role and its regulatory relationship with epithelial‑mesenchymal transition markers. This integrated approach, progressing from computational modelling to molecular characterisation and in vivo validation, distinguishes our work from purely in silico prognostic signatures. Furthermore, we systematically characterised the association between the DRLR signature and key features of the tumour immune microenvironment, tumour mutational burden, and drug sensitivity, uncovering potential mechanisms linking DRLRs to immune evasion and metabolic reprogramming in high‑grade HCC. Collectively, these findings establish the 9‑DRLR model not only as a robust prognostic tool but also as a biologically anchored signature with translational relevance for personalised therapy.

Furthermore, preclinical models have validated the synergistic anti‑tumour effects of combination targeted therapies, demonstrating that molecular subtype‑guided therapeutic strategies may enhance treatment efficacy in HCC [29]. These findings offer experimental pharmacological support for computational predictions of drug sensitivity in high‑risk patient subgroups and highlight the translational potential of biomarker‑driven therapeutic decision‑making. Collectively, these studies establish a robust contextual foundation that accentuates the novelty and clinical relevance of our 9‑DRLR prognostic model, positioning it within a rapidly evolving landscape of HCC research that integrates molecular profiling, metabolic characterization, and personalised therapeutic approaches.

Materials and methods

Data sources

This study utilized comprehensive RNA sequencing datasets from the Cancer Genome Atlas TCGA-LIHC project, obtained through the Genomic Data Commons (GDC) portal (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). Supplementary clinical data were collated via the cBioPortal platform (http://www.cbioportal.org/). Inclusion criteria mandated the following: (1) a definitive pathological grading of HCC specimens and (2) availability of complete RNA-Seq data for both lncRNAs and messenger RNAs (mRNAs). Patients lacking recorded or complete survival data were systematically excluded from this analysis. Ultimately, the study encompassed a cohort of 362 HCC patients, specifically 230 patients with grades G1-G2 and 130 patients with grades G3-G4 according to the pathological classification. A focal point of this research was to investigate the interplay between lncRNAs and genes related to the biochemical process known as disulfidptosis. The foundational framework for identifying disulfidptosis-related genes in this study was derived from the seminal work of Liu et al., whose proteomic and functional characterisations established a core cohort of ten key regulators (SLC7A11, SLC3A2, NUBPL, NDUFA11, LRPPRC, OXSM, NDUFS1, GYS1, RPN1, and NCKAP1) that orchestrate disulfide stress-induced actin cytoskeleton collapse [17]. This specific gene set has been extensively validated across diverse oncological contexts as the biological ‘gold standard’ for disulfidptosis, ensuring that our downstream analyses of lncRNA-mediated regulation were anchored in a robust and internationally recognised transcriptomic baseline. To ascertain the correlation between the expression levels of lncRNAs and these selected genes, we employed the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, leveraging the ‘wilcox.test()’ function within the R statistical software environment. The identification of DRLRs was driven by two criteria: a correlation coefficient greater than 0.4, coupled with a p-value lower than 0.001. Due to the origination of patient data from the TCGA database, stringent compliance with TCGA’s publication policies and guidelines was observed, thereby obviating the necessity for further ethical review or approval processes.

Formulation and validation of a prognostic risk evaluation model

The initial phase of model formulation involved the application of a univariate Cox regression analysis. This statistical method was utilized to identify differentially regulated DRLRs that demonstrated a statistically significant association with OS in the patient cohort, delineated by a p-value threshold of less than 0.05. To address potential overfitting, a common pitfall in predictive modeling, a LASSO regression analysis was subsequently conducted.

Statistical modelling and parameter configuration

The construction of the prognostic risk signature followed a rigorous, two-stage statistical framework implemented within the R environment. Initially, LASSO regression analysis was performed using the glmnet package (version 4.1-6) to reduce dimensionality and alleviate multicollinearity among the disulfidptosis-related lncRNAs. To ensure model stability and convergence, the iteration maximum was specified as maxit = 1000 with the family parameter set to cox. The optimal penalty parameter lambda was determined through a 10-fold cross-validation procedure; specifically, the value of lambda that minimised the partial likelihood deviance (lambda.min) was selected as the threshold to retain candidates with robust predictive potential.Following the preliminary screening of 18 candidate lncRNAs, a multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was executed to formulate a parsimonious prognostic model. A stepwise selection process—integrating both forward and backward directions—was employed to optimise the model based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC). The final 9-DRLR signature was established by identifying the variable combination that yielded the minimum AIC value, thereby ensuring the model attained an optimal balance between predictive accuracy and statistical economy. The risk score for each patient was calculated as a linear combination of the expression levels of these nine lncRNAs weighted by their respective multivariate Cox regression coefficients, according to the formula: Risk score = Σ (expression level of lncRNAi × coefficienti). Following this, a multivariate Cox regression analysis was performed to construct the optimal prognostic risk model, selecting DRLRs that produced the lowest Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) value. The individualized risk score for each patient within the G3-G4 HCC category was calculated using a linear combination of the expression levels and corresponding coefficients of selected DRLRs, adhering to the equation: Risk score = (Expression level of lncRNA A * Coefficient of lncRNA A) + (Expression level of lncRNA B * Coefficient of lncRNA B) + ….

The participants from the training cohort were then bifurcated into low-risk and high-risk groups based on their median risk scores. To evaluate the model’s predictive capability, Kaplan-Meier survival curves were generated using the ‘Survival’ and ‘Survminer’ R packages. Additionally, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were plotted with the ‘timeROC’ package to assess the temporal accuracy of the risk predictions. Comparative ROC analyses were also undertaken to authenticate the prognostic power of the risk model against individual clinical variables. In terms of predictive accuracy, the model’s performance was further substantiated through C-index analysis facilitated by R packages such as ‘dplyr’, ‘rms’, and ‘pec’. The visualization of risk score distribution, patient survival status, and the DRLR expression risk heatmap was accomplished using the ‘pheatmap’ package. Furthermore, to illustrate the discriminative potential of the model, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) plots were generated using the ‘limma’ and ‘scatterplot3d’ R packages. These three-dimensional visualizations effectively differentiate between low-risk and high-risk categories in G3-G4 HCC patients. The prognostic value of the model in the context of clinical characteristics was assessed via both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, establishing its independent prognostic significance. A nomogram was constructed for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year OS for patients, using the ‘rms’ package in R. Calibration curves are utilized to evaluate the predictive accuracy of nomograms.

For the purpose of validation, we employed bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations) to evaluate the internal stability of the risk model within the original G3‑G4 HCC cohort. An additional bootstrap‑derived validation set was generated, and model performance was quantified through time‑dependent ROC curves, calibration plots, and decision curve analysis. This method is extensively endorsed for internal validation processes in the context of prognostic model development [30–33].

To evaluate the performance of the 9-DRLR model across different HCC aetiologies, we performed subgroup analysis within the G3-G4 HCC cohort from TCGA-LIHC. Patients were stratified into two major aetiological subgroups: virus-related HCC (including HBV and HCV infection, n = 69) and non-virus-related HCC (including alcohol-related, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, and other causes, n = 61). Due to the absence of patients with survival beyond 5 years in the non-virus-related subgroup, time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were generated at 1, 2, and 3 years for this subgroup, whereas 1-, 3-, and 5-year ROC curves were analysed for the virus-related subgroup. Calibration curves and decision curve analysis (DCA) were also constructed for each subgroup to assess predictive accuracy and clinical utility. All analyses were performed using the ‘timeROC’, ‘rms’, and ‘rmda’ packages in R.

Evaluation of pathway enrichment and investigation of gene set enrichment

In our study aimed at discerning differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between low-risk and high-risk groups, we employed a comprehensive differential expression analysis. For the differential expression analysis, we utilized the ‘limma’ package embedded in the R programming environment. Genes were filtered for significant differential expression based on a stringent threshold: a log2 fold change exceeding 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) maintained below 0.05. Subsequent to the identification, the DEGs were systematically subjected to functional enrichment analysis using the GO database. The GO database serves as an essential resource, categorically organizing genes into three pivotal ontological domains: biological processes (BP), molecular functions (MF), and cellular components (CC). The aim of this analysis was to unravel the specific functional roles that these DEGs play within the biological context. Furthering our exploration, Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) was conducted to identify critical biological functions and pathways that correlate with advanced histological grades (G3-G4) of HCC. To achieve comprehensive insights, we utilized three extensive gene set collections: c5.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols. gmt, c2.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols. gmt, and h.all.v2022.1.Hs.symbols.gmt. The criteria for significance in enrichment were set with both the P value and FDR required to be below the 0.05 threshold. This stringent level of significance was crucial to ascertain the pathways that potentially differentiate the progression between the two risk groups. The entire enrichment analysis process was facilitated by R packages including ‘org.Hs.eg.db’, ‘clusterProfiler’, and ‘enrichplot’, which were instrumental in executing and visualizing the functional enrichment and GSEA results.

Assessment of the immunological landscape

To navigate the complex immunological landscape within pathological grade G3-G4 HCC specimens, we adopted the CIBERSORT computational algorithm. This method was instrumental in quantifying the relative proportions of a diverse array of immune cell types present within each tumor sample. To validate our results, we ensured statistical significance was achieved post-analysis by applying a stringent threshold of P < 0.05, ensuring the robustness of our results. Additionally, to conduct an exhaustive analysis of immune cell abundance and functional disparities among samples, we employed the single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) methodology. The execution of this analysis was carried out within the R statistical software ecosystem, wherein advanced packages were incorporated. These included ‘limma’ for potent differential expression analysis, ‘reshape2’ for sophisticated data restructuring, ‘ggpubr’ for generating publication-quality graphical visualizations, ‘GSVA’ for profound gene set variation analysis, and ‘GSEABase’ for effective manipulation of gene set collections. The results of our analyses were visually encapsulated through box plots that adeptly highlighted the variability in immune cell proportions across G3-G4 HCC samples.

Drug selection for pathological grades G3-G4 HCC patients using OncoPredict

We utilized the R package OncoPredict, developed by Maeser et al. to predict drug responses in patients with HCC, specifically targeting those with pathological grades G3-G4. The package OncoPredict was first applied to calculate drug sensitivity scores for each patient sample, enabling a personalized assessment of potential therapeutic options. To analyze variations in drug responsiveness between different patient risk groups, the ggpubr and limma R packages were subsequently used. These tools facilitated the comparison of drug sensitivity scores between low-risk and high-risk cohorts, employing a statistical significance threshold set at P < 0.05 to ensure robustness of the results. An essential metric in evaluating drug sensitivity was the half maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50). A lower IC50 value indicates heightened sensitivity to the drug, suggesting its potential efficacy in clinical application for the respective patient group. This information is pivotal in guiding the clinical decision-making process for selecting the most appropriate pharmacological interventions for patients with advanced-grade HCC. To dissect the pharmacological implications of the signature’s core component, a focused single-gene drug sensitivity analysis was conducted for AL031985.3. Utilising the OncoPredict R package and the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) v2 training set, we estimated the half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) for a comprehensive panel of compounds. This approach facilitated the identification of therapeutic agents exhibiting differential efficacy between high- and low-AL031985.3 expression phenotypes within the high-grade HCC cohort.

Cell culture and transfection

HCC cell lines, namely HCC-LM3, Hep3B2.1-7, and HepG2, in addition to the human hepatic cell line L02, were utilised. These cell lines were sourced from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Medical Sciences situated in Shanghai, China. The cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM), which was enriched with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) obtained from Gibco. Furthermore, cultures were supplemented with antibiotics, specifically 100 units per millilitre of penicillin and 100 micrograms per millilitre of streptomycin, also from Gibco. The culturing conditions were rigorously controlled at a temperature of 37 degrees Celsius with a humidified atmosphere containing 5% carbon dioxide. In order to investigate gene silencing, small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting AL031985.3 (si-AL031985.3), as well as a non-targeting control siRNA (si-NC), were synthesized by GenePharma. The transfection of HCC cells was performed using Lipofectamine 3000, following the detailed protocol provided by the manufacturer, Invitrogen.

Quantitative real-time PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

A total of 32 paired fresh HCC and adjacent non‑tumour tissue samples were collected from patients who underwent curative liver resection at Jiangsu Cancer Hospital between January 2021 and November 2025. All experimental protocols were executed in strict adherence to the ethical tenets stipulated in the Declaration of Helsinki. Prior to specimen procurement, comprehensive written informed consent was meticulously obtained from each participant following a detailed explanation of the study objectives and data usage. Following surgical excision, all tissue specimens were immediately cryopreserved in liquid nitrogen and subsequently transferred to a -80 °C ultra-low temperature freezer to preserve molecular integrity for downstream analysis. Tissue samples were anonymised immediately upon collection to ensure patient confidentiality, and all procedures involving sample handling, storage, and analysis were performed in strict compliance with institutional ethical guidelines. This study was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and received approval from the Ethics Committee of Jurong Hospital Affiliated to Jiangsu University (Approval No. JRSRMYY-2023-042).

To investigate the expression levels of the prognostic hub DRLRs in various HCC cell lines, we performed qRT-PCR analysis. The study encompassed the utilization of three distinct HCC cell lines, namely Hep3B2.1-7, HepG2, and HCC-LM3, whilst employing the L02 cell line as the normal hepatocyte control. These cell lines were procured from the Cell Bank of the Chinese Academy of Sciences in Shanghai. Initially, total RNA was isolated from each cell line employing the TRIzol reagent, adhering stringently to the manufacturer’s specified protocol to ensure sample integrity and purity. Subsequently, the synthesis of complementary DNA (cDNA) was performed to serve as a template for the qRT-PCR process. This molecular analysis employed the ubiquitously expressed housekeeping gene Glyceraldehyde-3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase (GAPDH) as an internal control to facilitate data normalization, utilizing the 2 − ΔΔCt method to quantify gene expression changes accurately. The primer sequences are as follows: Forward primer for AL031985.3: CCTGGGATGTTTAGGGGAAT; Reverse primer: TCATCAGTGGGTCTCAACCA; Forward primer for GAPDH: GCACCGTCAAGGCTGAGAAC; and Reverse primer: TGGTGAAGACGCCAGTGGA.

Bioinformatic analyses of AL031985.3 and the eight DRLRs

To elucidate the potential involvement of AL031985.3 in disulfidptosis and immune processes, we performed comprehensive in silico analyses integrating multiple bioinformatic resources. Co‑expression analysis was conducted using Spearman correlation to identify genes associated with AL031985.3 in the G3-G4 TCGA‑LIHC cohort, with a focus on previously reported disulfidptosis‑related genes and immune‑related genes. We performed GO enrichment analysis and GSEA on AL031985.3 to explore the GO biological pathways involving its co-expressed genes and the GSEA biological pathways enriched in groups with high versus low AL031985.3 expression. The upstream transcriptional regulatory network of AL031985.3 was interrogated using AnnoLnc2, which provides transcription factor binding predictions. Experimentally validated transcription factor binding sites were retrieved from ENCODE ChIP‑seq clusters via the UCSC Genome Browser (hg38, chr1:40464319–40466767). High‑confidence motif scanning was performed using the JASPAR CORE 2024 database. Additionally, protein interaction partners of AL031985.3 were predicted using the catRAPID algorithm (RNAct database), with a particular focus on both canonical RNA‑binding proteins (RBPs) and non‑canonical RBPs implicated in cytoskeletal dynamics and epigenetic regulation. These multi‑layered analyses aimed to construct a putative “transcription factor–lncRNA–RBP” regulatory axis linking AL031985.3 to disulfidptosis and immune function.

Similarly, to explore the potential mechanistic involvement of the remaining eight DRLRs in disulfidptosis, we performed comprehensive correlation analyses between each DRLR and a curated set of well-characterized disulfidptosis-related genes. Gene selection was based on an extensive literature review encompassing key regulators of actin cytoskeleton dynamics, glucose metabolism, NADPH homeostasis, and the WAVE regulatory complex. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated using the ‘cor.test’ function in R, with a stringent threshold of |r| > 0.4 and P < 0.001 considered statistically significant. Co‑expression networks were visualized using the ‘pheatmap’ and ‘ggplot2’ packages. Additionally, we conducted Kaplan–Meier survival analysis for each individual DRLR in the G3‑G4 HCC cohort using the ‘survival’ and ‘survminer’ R packages to assess their independent prognostic value. Differential expression between tumour and adjacent non‑tumour tissues was evaluated using the Wilcoxon rank‑sum test.

Assessment of cell proliferation

We assessed the proliferation of HCC cells utilizing the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8, Beyotime, China). This assay was performed in strict accordance with the detailed protocol provided by the manufacturer. Initially, HCC cells were seeded at a density of 2 × 10^3 cells per well into a sterile 96-well culture plate. These cells were then incubated under optimal growth conditions overnight to ensure complete adherence and viability. Following this incubation period, the CCK-8 reagent, was added to each well precisely following the manufacturer’s instructions. Optical density measurements were subsequently obtained to quantify cell proliferation.

Colony formation assay

To conduct the colony formation assay, a defined number of 500 cells per well were seeded into 6-well culture plates. This setup was maintained under optimal culture conditions to promote cell growth until distinct, visible colonies had developed. Upon reaching this stage, the cellular colonies were subjected to fixation with 1 ml of 4% paraformaldehyde added to each well. The fixation process was allowed to proceed for a period of 20 min, a step for maintaining the integrity of cellular morphology during subsequent analysis. Following fixation, the paraformaldehyde solution was aspirated from the wells, and a thorough rinse with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was performed to effectively remove all residual fixative. Subsequent to washing, 1 ml of crystal violet staining solution was introduced into each well, where it was incubated for another 20 min to ensure optimal staining of the colonies. Any excess dye was removed by washing with distilled water. Finally, the colonies that formed were documented photographically.

Wound healing assay

To evaluate cell migration capabilities, a wound healing assay was performed. HCC cells were cultured in six-well plates under optimal conditions until they reached a confluence exceeding 90%. At this stage, a uniform scratch was created across the center of each well using a sterile pipette tip, simulating an artificial wound. To ensure the integrity of the assay and eliminate any cellular debris, the wells were thoroughly rinsed twice with PBS, following which a serum-free medium was introduced to create a controlled environment devoid of growth factors. Initial imaging was undertaken using an inverted microscope to document the baseline wound area, and the average gap width was precisely measured using a calibrated caliper. Subsequent imaging was performed 48 h post-scratch to assess the degree of wound closure, thereby providing insights into the migratory dynamics of the HCC cells.

Assays for evaluating cell migration and invasion

The evaluation of cell migration and invasion was conducted using Transwell chambers. For the migration assays, a suspension comprising 4 × 10⁴ HCC cells in 200 µL of serum-free medium was introduced into the uncoated upper chambers of the Transwell system. In contrast, the invasion assays necessitated additional preparation steps, wherein the upper chambers were pre-treated with a layer of Matrigel Basement Membrane (BD Biosciences), consisting of 50 µL, to simulate the extracellular matrix environment. Both assay types were consistently maintained at physiological conditions of 37 °C with 5% CO2 for a 24-hour incubation period to allow for adequate cellular migration or invasion through the membrane. At the conclusion of the incubation period, cells that had successfully traversed the membrane were subjected to fixation using 4% paraformaldehyde. This was followed by a staining procedure employing 0.1% crystal violet to facilitate visualisation. The stained cells were subsequently imaged and quantitatively analysed using an inverted microscope. Five random fields per chamber were selected to ensure representative and unbiased quantification of cell migration and invasion.

Orthotopic HCC model

An orthotopic HCC model was established in specific pathogen-free (SPF)-grade BALB/c nude mice (Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd.). Using computer-generated randomisation, five male mice per cohort were assigned to control or experimental groups. Direct hepatic inoculation beneath the liver lobe capsule was performed with 1 × 10⁶ transfected HCC-LM3 cells (sh-AL031985.3 or short-hairpin non-targeting control(sh-NC)). At the 8-week endpoint, mice were deeply anaesthetised via intraperitoneal sodium pentobarbital (50 mg/kg) and euthanised by cervical dislocation. Hepatic tissues were harvested for cryogenic storage. Anaesthesia protocols and predefined humane endpoints minimised distress, with all procedures conducted under the aforementioned ethical approval.

Experimental pulmonary metastasis model

To assess metastatic potential in vivo, an experimental lung metastasis model was established. Six-week-old male athymic nude mice (Shanghai SLAC Laboratory Animal Co. Ltd., China) were housed under SPF conditions. HCCLM3 cells, stably transfected with either short hairpin RNA targeting AL031985.3 (sh-AL031985.3) or non-targeting control (sh-NC), were harvested. Knockdown efficiency was confirmed by qRT-PCR prior to injection. Cells were resuspended in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at a density of 2.5 × 10^7 cells/mL. Each mouse (n = 5 per group) received a 200 µL suspension (5 × 10^6 cells) via tail vein injection. After eight weeks, mice were humanely euthanised. Lungs were excised, and macroscopic surface metastases were photographed.Tissues were then fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 48 h for subsequent histological analysis. Haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on paraffin-embedded sections to visualise and quantify metastatic nodules. All procedures received prior approval from the Medical Ethics Committee of Jurong Hospital Affiliated to Jiangsu University and complied with national and institutional guidelines for animal welfare.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

Murine tissues underwent 48-hour fixation in 4% paraformaldehyde followed by paraffin embedding. Section (5 μm) were blocked with 5% goat serum to mitigate non-specific binding. Primary antibody incubations employed: anti-E-cadherin (1:500; Abcam ab76055), anti-N-cadherin (1:500; Abcam ab76011), anti-Vimentin (1:250; Abcam ab8978), and anti-Slug (1:100; Cell Signaling 9585). Sections were subsequently treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500; Santa Cruz sc-2004) for 2 h at 37 °C. IHC scoring utilised a semi-quantitative system: staining intensity (0 = negative; 1 = weak; 2 = moderate; 3 = strong) and percentage positivity (1 = 0–10%; 2 = 11–50%; 3 = 51–80%; 4 = 81–100%). The immunohistochemical score (IHS) represented the product of intensity and percentage scores. Two consultant histopathologists, blinded to sample allocation, independently performed all evaluations.

H&E staining

Tumour specimens were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (24 h), dehydrated through graded alcohols, and embedded in paraffin. Section (4 μm) were stained using a commercial H&E kit (Beyotime, Shanghai; C0105S) per manufacturer’s instructions. Morphological assessment utilised an OLYMPUS BX53 microscope (Tokyo, Japan).

Statistical analysis

Comprehensive data processing and visualization tasks were conducted using R software version 4.1.3, which is publicly accessible at http://www.R-project. Subsequently, to ensure robust and precise statistical analysis, SPSS statistical software version 19.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA) was employed. For evaluating the results and determining statistical significance, a p-value of less than 0.05 was used as the criterion for significance.

Identification of key DRLRs and development of the DRLR-based model

Initially, a comprehensive screening process led to the identification of 598 Long Noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) coexpressed with disulfidptosis-related genes, as depicted in Fig. 1A. This extensive initial list was subsequently refined through a preliminary univariate Cox regression analysis, which identified 60 DRLRs with potential significance. To further distill the most pertinent DRLRs, we employed the LASSO regression, which effectively streamlined the selection down to 18 DRLRs. As illustrated in Fig. 1B and C, this method enabled the reduction of dimensionality without compromising the integrity of the dataset. Subsequent multivariate Cox regression was conducted to construct a robust risk model, ultimately isolating nine significant DRLRs: AL442125.2, AC018529.2, AL031985.3, AC119150.1, LINC02256, AC026979.4, POLH-AS1, MED8-AS1, and AC026356.1, as shown in Fig. 1D. A novel risk score formula was developed using the coefficients derived from these lncRNAs, formulated as follows: Risk Score = (0.43649 × AL442125.2 expression) + (1.24735 × AC018529.2 expression) + (0.20803 × AL031985.3 expression) + (0.39729 × AC119150.1 expression) + (-2.37435 × LINC02256 expression) + (-4.72082 × AC026979.4 expression) + (0.29695 × POLH-AS1 expression) + (0.33376 × MED8-AS1 expression) + (0.30313 × AC026356.1 expression). Figure 1E depicts a correlation heatmap that elucidates the connections between the selected key DRLRs and disulfidptosis-associated genes, underscoring their relevance.

Prognostic robustness across aetiological subgroups

The 9-DRLR signature maintained robust discriminative capacity independent of HCC aetiology, as illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 3. In the viral-associated subgroup, the model achieved high predictive accuracy, with AUCs ranging from 0.816 to 0.885 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year overall survival (Supplementary Fig. 3B). Remarkably, in the non-viral subgroup, the signature demonstrated even superior performance, yielding AUCs between 0.850 and 0.897 for 1-, 2-, and 3-year survival predictions (Supplementary Fig. 3F). Calibration curves for both cohorts indicated excellent concordance between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes (Supplementary Fig. 3C, 3G). Furthermore, DCA confirmed that the 9-DRLR nomogram offered consistent net clinical benefit across both viral and non-viral populations, outperforming the treat-all or treat-none strategies (Supplementary Fig. 3D, 3 H).

Upon resampling with replacement, the clinical data from the internal validation and training cohorts demonstrated similar clinical characteristics, with no significant differences observed (P > 0.05, Supplementary Table 1). In the training dataset, patients were categorised into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median risk score. OS analysis of the DRLRs risk model in G3-G4 HCC patients stratified by clinical characteristics

To assess the prognostic significance of the DRLRs risk score concerning OS in patients diagnosed with grade G3-G4 HCC, we conducted a comprehensive stratification based on various clinical and pathological attributes. The stratification encompassed a range of demographic and pathological factors, such as age, sex, TNM stage, and the presence or absence of tumour vascular invasion. Through analysis, depicted comprehensively in Fig. 3A-I, it was evident that patients categorized under the high-risk grade G3-G4 cohort consistently manifested significantly diminished OS outcomes when juxtaposed with their low-risk counterparts. This consistent trend was apparent across several pertinent parameters: patients aged ≥ 60 years (P = 0.002, Fig. 3A) and < 60 years (P < 0.001, Fig. 3B), females (P = 0.001, Fig. 3C), males (P < 0.001, Fig. 3D), those with microvascular invasion (P < 0.001, Fig. 3F), without vascular invasion (P < 0.001, Fig. 3G), as well as those in stage I-II (P < 0.001, Fig. 3H) and stage III-IV (P = 0.045, Fig. 3I). These cumulative observations affirm the robust prognostic capacity of our disulfidptosis risk score in forecasting OS for G3-G4 HCC patients across a broad spectrum of clinical and pathological subgroupings, thereby demonstrating the model’s broad applicability. However, it is critical to acknowledge that the prognostic utility of the model was not substantiated in patients identified within the macrovascular invasion subgroup (P = 0.136, Fig. 3E). These findings underscore the applicability of our 9-DRLR prognostic model for prognostication in G3-G4 HCC patients with differentiated clinical and pathological characteristics.

The prognostic relevance of the risk score was assessed as an independent variable through comprehensive univariate and multivariate analyses. Furthermore, these results suggest that the 9-DRLR model offers a more nuanced and clinically pertinent methodology for risk stratification in advanced cases of HCC. Consequently, the 9-DRLR model could serve as a valuable tool in clinical settings, aiding in the development of tailored therapeutic strategies for patients classified at different risk levels.

The analysis demonstrated that 5-Fluorouracil, BPD-00008900, Dasatinib, Fulvestrant, and Pictilisib emerged as the top five agents eliciting the most profound sensitivity within this patient group, as illustrated in Fig. 7A-E. Comprehensive bioinformatic analysis of AL031985.3 and the eight DRLRs

We systematically interrogated the potential roles of the other eight DRLRs through integrated bioinformatic analyses. Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that among the nine DRLRs, only AL031985.3 and AL442125.2 were significantly associated with overall survival in G3‑G4 HCC patients (Fig. 8A; Supplementary Fig. 4, P < 0.05), whereas the remaining seven DRLRs did not exhibit independent prognostic significance individually (P > 0.05), underscoring the necessity of a multi‑gene signature to capture synergistic prognostic signals. Differential expression analysis showed that eight of the nine DRLRs (all except LINC02256) were significantly upregulated in HCC tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues (Supplementary Fig. 5, P < 0.05), with AL031985.3 displaying the most pronounced fold change (P = 2.22e‑16). Importantly, correlation analysis revealed that each of the eight non‑validated DRLRs was significantly co‑expressed with multiple genes central to disulfidptosis execution (Supplementary Fig. 6–14). Specifically, AL442125.2 correlated strongly with cytoskeletal cross‑linkers such as TLN1, IQGAP1, and FLNB (|r| > 0.5, P < 0.001); AC018529.2 was linked to glucose transporter SLC2A1 and the actin‑binding protein CAP1; AC026356.1 exhibited extensive correlations with genes spanning glucose uptake (SLC2A1), glycogen metabolism (GYS1), redox balance (G6PD, ME1), and actin cytoskeleton (ACTB, CAP1); AC026979.4 co‑expressed with the mechanosensitive proteins FLNA and MYH9; AC119150.1 associated with glutamate transporter SLC1A2 and glycogen phosphorylase kinase PHKB; LINC02256 and MED8‑AS1 both displayed robust correlations with WAVE complex components (NCKAP1, WASF2, ABI2) and Arp2/3 subunits (ACTR2, ACTR3); and POLH‑AS1 was significantly correlated with RPN1, ACTN4, and GYS1. These findings collectively suggest that the eight DRLRs are intricately linked to distinct facets of the disulfidptosis machinery, forming a coordinated regulatory network from metabolic defence to cytoskeletal execution.

The expanded pharmacogenomic profiling (Supplementary Fig. 15) revealed a distinct therapeutic landscape associated with AL031985.3 upregulation. Specifically, the high-expression group manifested significantly lower estimated IC50 values—indicative of enhanced sensitivity—to several potent antineoplastic agents. The top five identified candidates included GDC0810 (a selective oestrogen receptor degrader), Ipatasertib (an Protein kinase B(AKT) inhibitor), Paclitaxel (a microtubule stabiliser), MIRA-1 (a p53 reactivator), and PD173074 (an Fibroblast Growth Factor Receptor(FGFR) inhibitor). In contrast, high levels of AL031985.3 were correlated with predicted resistance to agents such as PF-4,708,671, JQ1, SB505124, RO-3306, and JAK1_8709. These findings suggest that AL031985.3 status may serve as a biomarker for stratifying response to cytoskeletal-targeting and kinase-inhibitory regimens.

Integrative bioinformatic analysis reveals a multidimensional regulatory axis linking AL031985.3 to disulfidptosis

AL031985.3 was functionally linked to disulfidptosis execution through cytoskeletal remodelling. GO enrichment analysis revealed significant association with cytoskeletal organisation, including microtubule and cortical actin dynamics (Supplementary Fig. 16; Supplementary Table 2). GSEA further demonstrated marked enrichment of actin-related pathways, such as regulation of the actin cytoskeleton and actin-mediated cell contraction (FDR < 0.01; Supplementary Table 3), alongside downregulation of fatty acid and amino acid metabolism. Co-expression analysis in high-grade HCC identified strong correlations with ten disulfidptosis-associated genes (|r|>0.4, P < 0.001; Supplementary Fig. 6), including key regulators of cytoskeletal dynamics (e.g., CYFIP1, CAPZA1, NCKAP1) and metabolic adaptation (e.g., GYS1, LRPPRC). These findings suggest AL031985.3 may modulate disulfidptosis sensitivity via cytoskeletal integrity and metabolic reprogramming.

To investigate the upstream regulatory landscape of AL031985.3, we first interrogated the AnnoLnc2 database. As shown in Supplementary Table 4, multiple transcription factors intimately linked to metabolic stress and disulfidptosis were predicted to bind the AL031985.3 promoter region, including HIF1A (a master regulator of glucose starvation response), TP53 (a negative regulator of SLC7A11), ATF2 (an oxidative stress‑responsive factor), JUN/FOS (AP‑1 complex, responsive to disulfide stress), STAT3 (a metabolic and immune regulator), and MYC (a driver of metabolic reprogramming). To further corroborate these predictions, we examined ENCODE ChIP‑seq data via the UCSC Genome Browser. Supplementary Table 5 reveal significant binding peaks for stress‑ and redox‑associated transcription factors, including ATF2 and MITF, within the regulatory region of AL031985.3. High‑confidence motif scanning using the JASPAR CORE 2024 database (Supplementary Table 6) further identified highly enriched binding sites for core stress‑responsive factors such as ATF4, STAT3, JUN, and HIF1A. These findings collectively suggest that AL031985.3 is subject to direct transcriptional control by a network of disulfidptosis‑related transcription factors, positioning it as a downstream integrator of diverse metabolic stress signals. Beyond transcriptional regulation, we explored the post‑transcriptional interactome of AL031985.3 using the catRAPID algorithm. Remarkably, AL031985.3 exhibited high‑confidence predicted interactions with a panel of core actin cytoskeleton proteins that lack canonical RNA‑binding domains (non‑canonical RBPs; Supplementary Table 7), including MYH9, FLNA, ACTN4, and TLN1. Despite the absence of known RNA‑binding motifs (non-canonical RNA-binding motifs(nRBDI) = 0), these proteins are direct effectors of the actin cytoskeleton collapse that characterises disulfidptosis. This suggests that AL031985.3 may serve as a physical scaffold, directly engaging with the cytoskeletal machinery to modulate its vulnerability to disulfide stress. Concurrently, AL031985.3 was predicted to interact with several canonical RBPs central to epigenetic and post‑transcriptional regulation (Supplementary Table 8), notably components of the N6-methyladenosine(m6A) methylation machinery (RBM15, RBM15B, YTHDC1, YTHDC2) and RNA editing enzymes (ADAR, ADARB1). These interactions imply that AL031985.3 might act as an epigenetic guide, recruiting m6A writers or editors to specific transcripts of disulfidptosis core genes (e.g., SLC7A11), thereby fine‑tuning their expression at the post‑transcriptional level. Collectively, these multi‑dimensional bioinformatic analyses support a “transcription factor (HIF1A/ATF4/TP53) – lncRNA (AL031985.3) – RBP (cytoskeletal/m6A machinery)” regulatory axis, positioning AL031985.3 as a multifunctional hub integrating upstream stress signals with downstream cytoskeletal and epigenetic effectors in the context of disulfidptosis.

Multi-dimensional bioinformatic analysis uncovers an immune regulatory network centred on AL031985.3

To elucidate the functional role of AL031985.3 in tumour immunity, we performed Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis and Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on its co‑expressed genes and high‑ versus low‑expression groups. GO analysis revealed significant enrichment of immune‑related terms, including ‘positive regulation of lymphocyte activation’ and ‘immune response‑regulating cell surface receptor signalling pathway’, implicating AL031985.3 in remodelling the tumour immune microenvironment (Supplementary Fig. 17, Supplementary Table 9). Consistently, GSEA demonstrated marked enrichment of immune‑related gene sets, such as ‘KEGG_CYTOKINE_CYTOKINE_RECEPTOR_INTERACTION’, ‘GOBP_LEUKOCYTE_MIGRATION’ and ‘HALLMARK_INTERFERON_GAMMA_RESPONSE’ (Supplementary Table 10). Notably, AL031985.3 concurrently regulated immune response and ‘cytoskeleton‑dependent cytokinesis’, suggesting a potential role in disrupting immunological synapse formation through actin cytoskeleton remodelling. Furthermore, Spearman correlation analysis showed that AL031985.3 expression was significantly negatively correlated with plasma cell infiltration abundance (P = 0.020, Supplementary Fig. 18). As key effectors of humoral immunity, reduced plasma cell infiltration may impair anti‑tumour responses, providing cytological evidence for AL031985.3‑mediated immunosuppression. Collectively, these findings indicate that AL031985.3 contributes to immune evasion by modulating immune‑related pathways and specifically inhibiting plasma cell infiltration.

As shown in Supplementary Table 11, the AnnoLnc2 database predicted that multiple transcription factors intimately linked to immune regulation were predicted to bind the AL031985.3 promoter region, including IRF1 (a master regulator of interferon signalling), STAT3/STAT4/STAT5B (core components of the JAK‑STAT pathway), NFKB1/RELA/RELB (NF‑κB subunits), FOXP3 (the lineage‑defining transcription factor of regulatory T cells), GATA3 (a key regulator of Th2 differentiation), RUNX1 (critical for haematopoiesis and immune cell development), SPI1 (PU.1) (a master regulator of myeloid cell differentiation), CEBPB (involved in inflammatory responses), and SMAD3 (downstream of TGF‑β signalling). To further corroborate these predictions, we examined ENCODE ChIP‑seq data via the UCSC Genome Browser. Supplementary Table 5 reveal significant binding peaks for immune‑related transcription factors, including ATF2 (involved in both oxidative stress and immune modulation), MITF (linked to redox signalling and tumour‑infiltrating lymphocytes), and the epigenetic regulator CBX3, within the regulatory region of AL031985.3. High‑confidence motif scanning using the JASPAR CORE 2024 database (Supplementary Table 12) further identified highly enriched binding sites for core immune‑related transcription factors, including Stat4, Runx1, Spi1, TBX21, Stat5b, RELB, FOXP3, NFKB1, Irf1, RELA, and CEBPB. These findings collectively suggest that AL031985.3 is subject to direct transcriptional control by a network of immune‑related transcription factors, positioning it as a downstream integrator of diverse immunological signals.

Beyond transcriptional regulation, we explored the post‑transcriptional interactome of AL031985.3 using the catRAPID algorithm. Remarkably, AL031985.3 exhibited high‑confidence predicted interactions with a panel of canonical RNA‑binding proteins central to epigenetic and post‑transcriptional regulation (Supplementary Table 8), notably components of the m6A methylation machinery (RBM15, RBM15B, YTHDC1, YTHDC2), RNA editing enzymes (ADAR, ADARB1), RNA‑induced silencing complex components (AGO1), and poly(A)‑binding proteins (PABPC1). These proteins are characterised by well‑defined RNA‑binding domains (nRBDI > 0) and are known to govern the fate of mRNAs encoding immune checkpoint molecules and cytokines.

Concurrently, AL031985.3 was predicted to interact with several non‑canonical RBPs that lack classical RNA‑binding domains but are pivotal for innate immune sensing (Supplementary Table 13). These include cytosolic RNA sensors such as IFIH1 (MDA5) and DDX58 (RIG‑I), endosomal Toll‑like receptors (TLR3, TLR7, TLR8), oligoadenylate synthases (OAS2, OAS3), DNA repair enzymes with immune functions (PARP1, PARP12), and the nuclear DNA sensor IFI16. Despite the absence of canonical RNA‑binding motifs (nRBDI = 0), these proteins recognise nucleic acids as pathogen‑associated molecular patterns and initiate antiviral immune responses. The predicted binding of AL031985.3 to these innate immune sensors raises the intriguing possibility that this lncRNA may act as a decoy or scaffold, sequestering these sensors or modulating their activation to prevent aberrant immune recognition and suppress interferon signalling. Furthermore, the inclusion of ILF3, a double‑stranded RNA‑binding protein involved in post‑transcriptional regulation of immune genes, adds another layer of complexity to this interactome. Strikingly, AL031985.3 also exhibited high‑confidence predicted interactions with core actin cytoskeleton proteins (Supplementary Table 7), including MYH9, FLNA, and ACTN4, which are direct effectors of the cytoskeletal collapse that characterises disulfidptosis. This observation hints at a potential physical link between immune regulation and cytoskeletal remodelling, suggesting that AL031985.3 might integrate signals from both pathways through its dual interactions with immune sensors and cytoskeletal effectors. Collectively, these multi‑dimensional bioinformatic analyses support a “transcription factor (IRF1/STAT3/NFKB1)–lncRNA (AL031985.3)–RBP (canonical/non‑canonical)” regulatory axis, positioning AL031985.3 as a multifunctional hub integrating upstream immune signals with downstream epigenetic, innate immune, and cytoskeletal effectors in the context of immune evasion and disulfidptosis.

The attenuation of AL031985.3 expression impedes proliferation, invasion, and migration of HCC cells

In patients with grade 3–4 HCC within the TCGA database, those exhibiting high expression of AL031985.3 have a reduced OS compared to those with low expression (Fig. 8A). QRT-PCR analysis of 32 clinical samples revealed a pronounced overexpression of AL031985.3 in HCC tissues compared to corresponding adjacent non-cancerous counterparts, as delineated in Fig. 8B.

This dysregulation was further corroborated through assessments in various cell lines, wherein AL031985.3 was significantly upregulated in HCC cell lines compared to the normal hepatic cell line, LO2, as shown in Fig. 8C. These findings strongly suggest its overexpression in malignant contexts.

To explore the functional significance of this aberrant expression, specific siRNAs were employed to achieve targeted silencing of AL031985.3. The effective knockdown was confirmed by qRT-PCR analysis (Fig. 8D). Subsequent assays revealed that AL031985.3 attenuation significantly hampered HCC cell proliferation and survival. The CCK-8 assays demonstrated a notable decrease in cell proliferation following AL031985.3 depletion (Figs. 8E). Complementing these results, clonogenic assays indicated a substantial decline in colony formation ability post-knockdown (Fig. 8F).

Moreover, the role of AL031985.3 in cell motility was assessed using wound healing and Transwell assays. The wound healing assays depicted a significant reduction in cellular migration subsequent to AL031985.3 downregulation (Fig. 8G). Similarly, Transwell assays revealed a marked impairment in both invasive and migratory capacities upon silencing of AL031985.3 expression (Fig. 8H). Collectively, these data elucidate that AL031985.3 is pivotal for the proliferation, invasion, and migration of HCC cells, thereby highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target in HCC management.

AL031985.3 depletion suppresses tumour growth and metastasis in vivo

The functional role of AL031985.3 was further investigated in vivo using orthotopic and experimental metastasis models. In the orthopic model, histopathological examination via H&E staining (Fig. 9A) demonstrated significantly larger tumour foci in sh-NC controls at 40× magnification. High-power examination (400×) revealed sh-NC tumour cells exhibiting spindle-shaped morphology, nuclear pleomorphism, and stromal invasion. In contrast, sh-AL031985.3 tumours displayed more organised architecture with reduced cytological atypia. IHC of orthotopic lesions (Fig. 9B, C) confirmed the pronounced upregulation of the epithelial marker E-cadherin and concomitant downregulation of mesenchymal markers (N-cadherin, Vimentin, Slug) in the sh-AL031985.3 group versus controls (Fig. 9B, C), indicating inhibition of EMT.

Discussion

HCC is characterised by a high mortality and aggressiveness worldwide, making the understanding of its pathogenesis and the exploration of methods to differentiate various pathological HCC grades critical for prognosis. Disulfidptosis is a newly identified form of regulated cell death triggered by glucose starvation, while lncRNAs can modulate cancer cell behaviour, including metabolism. Numerous studies indicate that DRLRs are vital for cancer prognosis. For instance, research by Yao et al. demonstrated that lncRNAs AC013652.1 and MCM3AP-AS1 can serve as potential therapeutic targets in colorectal adenocarcinoma by inhibiting disulfidptosis. Zhang et al. identified a DRLRs model that can predict the prognosis, TMB, immune cell infiltration, and response to immunotherapy and targeted therapy in lung adenocarcinoma patients [34–36] In HCC-related studies, several DRLRs have been identified as capable of evaluating the immune microenvironment and drug sensitivity of HCC [37, 38]. Xu et al. successfully constructed a prognostic risk model for HCC based on the characteristics of DRLRs, with elevated AC026412.3 expression shown to promote HCC cell proliferation and induce gefitinib resistance.

Utilising the TCGA transcriptome dataset, we identified DRLRs associated with disulfidptosis in HCC and constructed a prognostic model comprised of AC018529.2, AC026356.1, AC026979.4, AC119150.1, AL031985.3, AL442125.2, LINC02256, MED8-AS1, and POLH-AS1. Through Cox regression analysis, we validated its independent prognostic value, with Kaplan-Meier survival analysis indicating a significantly reduced OS in the high-risk group. Our model effectively stratified HCC patients according to tumour pathological grading, demonstrating robust predictive capability. Moreover, GO and GSEA revealed the model’s association with key biological processes in tumour aggressiveness and immune evasion. High-risk patients, particularly those with grade III-IV, exhibited increased markers of immune dysfunction, higher tumour mutation burden, and enhanced sensitivity to specific therapeutic agents, suggesting potential pathways for targeted intervention.

HCC is characterised by significant heterogeneity driven by diverse causative agents, ranging from chronic viral hepatitis to metabolic dysfunction. The inclusion of aetiological subgroup analysis represents a critical enhancement to our study, addressing the inherent heterogeneity of HCC. The consistent predictive performance of the 9‑DRLR model across both virus‑related and non‑virus‑related G3‑G4 HCC patients underscores its broad applicability and robustness. Notably, the model maintained high AUC values in the non‑virus‑related subgroup despite the shorter follow‑up period, suggesting its potential utility in diverse clinical contexts. While the sample size of each subgroup was relatively modest due to the limited number of G3‑G4 HCC cases in TCGA, the observed consistency in model performance provides preliminary evidence for its aetiology‑independent prognostic value. Future studies with larger, multi‑centre cohorts are warranted to further validate these findings and to explore potential aetiology‑specific biological mechanisms underlying the DRLR signature.

Our research confirmed the involvement of various lncRNAs in cancer development and progression, with AL031985.3 identified as a key component of this model. AL031985.3, a frequently highlighted lncRNA in numerous studies, has gained considerable attention for its role in HCC. Several studies have utilised AL031985.3 as one of the key biomarkers in constructing prognostic models for HCC patients. These models predict patients’ OS by assessing AL031985.3’s expression levels in conjunction with other lncRNAs or genes [39, 40]. The expression of AL031985.3 is closely related to the tumour microenvironment (TME) of HCC, particularly in terms of immune cell infiltration and immune therapy response. Studies have shown significant associations between the expression levels of AL031985.3, immune checkpoint gene expression, and immune subtypes [41, 42]. A compelling observation from our drug sensitivity analysis is the predicted susceptibility of AL031985.3-high tumours to Paclitaxel. Given that disulfidptosis is mechanistically driven by the catastrophic collapse of the actin cytoskeleton due to disulfide stress, the enhanced sensitivity to Paclitaxel—a chemotherapeutic agent that stabilises microtubules and disrupts cytoskeletal dynamics—lends biological plausibility to our findings. It is conceivable that AL031985.3 creates a state of ‘cytoskeletal fragility’, rendering cancer cells hyper-responsive to agents that further perturb structural integrity. Additionally, the sensitivity to Ipatasertib implicates the PI3K/AKT survival pathway as a potential co-target in this subgroup. We unequivocally acknowledge, however, that these pharmacological associations are derived from algorithmic inference using the OncoPredict framework. The absence of corroborative in vitro cell viability assays remains a limitation of the current study. Furthermore, several lncRNAs, including AL031985.3, have been found to predict the prognosis of HCC patients and offer new insights into immuno-targeted therapy. Some research has explored the correlation between AL031985.3 and chemotherapy sensitivity in HCC patients, finding that its expression may influence responses to specific chemotherapeutic agents. Although AL031985.3 is associated with HCC prognosis, immune microenvironment, and chemotherapy response, its precise molecular mechanisms remain incompletely understood, requiring further experimental investigation to elucidate.

The integration of multi‑dimensional bioinformatic analyses has enabled us to construct a coherent, albeit preliminary, framework linking AL031985.3 to disulfidptosis. The convergence of upstream transcription factor predictions, ENCODE ChIP‑seq validation, high‑confidence motif scanning, and protein interactome predictions strongly suggests that AL031985.3 is not merely a passive bystander but an active participant in the molecular circuitry governing disulfidptosis. Specifically, the identification of HIF1A, ATF4, TP53, and AP‑1 components as putative upstream regulators positions AL031985.3 at the crossroads of glucose starvation, oxidative stress, and metabolic reprogramming. Downstream, the predicted direct interactions with core actin cytoskeletal proteins (MYH9, FLNA, ACTN4, TLN1) provide a plausible physical mechanism by which AL031985.3 might influence the actin cytoskeleton—the ultimate executioner of disulfidptosis. Moreover, its association with m6A methylation machinery (RBM15, YTHDC1) and RNA editing enzymes (ADAR) unveils an additional layer of potential epigenetic regulation, whereby AL031985.3 could modulate the stability or editing of mRNAs encoding disulfidptosis regulators. The “transcription factor–lncRNA–RBP” regulatory axis we propose encapsulates these observations and provides a testable hypothesis for future experimental validation. We fully acknowledge that these in silico findings are correlative and require direct functional confirmation. Nonetheless, they offer a rational roadmap for subsequent investigations, which will include loss‑ and gain‑of‑function studies to assess the impact of AL031985.3 on SLC7A11 expression, NADPH homeostasis, and actin cytoskeleton integrity under glucose‑starved conditions, as well as RIP‑qPCR or CLIP‑seq to validate its interactions with key RBPs. Should these predictions be validated experimentally, they would establish AL031985.3 as a critical node in the disulfidptosis network and a potential therapeutic target in high‑grade hepatocellular carcinoma.

Similarly, a series of bioinformatic analyses linked AL031985.3 to immune regulation. Specifically, the identification of IRF1, STAT3, NFKB1, and FOXP3 as putative upstream regulators positions AL031985.3 at the crossroads of interferon signalling, inflammation, and regulatory T‑cell function. Downstream, the predicted direct interactions with canonical RBPs (RBM15, YTHDC1, ADAR) provide a plausible epigenetic mechanism by which AL031985.3 might modulate the stability, editing, or translation of mRNAs encoding immune checkpoint molecules and cytokines. Moreover, its association with non‑canonical RBPs, particularly innate immune sensors (TLRs, RIG‑I, MDA5), unveils an additional layer of potential immune modulation, whereby AL031985.3 could directly influence the sensing of nucleic acid danger signals and the subsequent activation of antiviral immunity. The concurrent interaction with cytoskeletal proteins (MYH9, FLNA) further suggests that AL031985.3 may physically couple immune signalling with the cytoskeletal remodelling that underpins disulfidptosis, offering a potential mechanistic link between these two critical processes in high‑grade hepatocellular carcinoma. The “transcription factor–lncRNA–RBP” regulatory axis we propose encapsulates these observations and provides a testable hypothesis for future experimental validation. We fully acknowledge that these in silico findings are correlative and require direct functional confirmation. Nonetheless, they offer a rational roadmap for subsequent investigations, which will include loss‑ and gain‑of‑function studies to assess the impact of AL031985.3 on immune checkpoint expression, T‑cell activation, and innate immune sensing, as well as RIP‑qPCR or CLIP‑seq to validate its interactions with key RBPs. Should these predictions be validated experimentally, they would establish AL031985.3 as a critical node in the immune regulatory network of high‑grade hepatocellular carcinoma and a potential therapeutic target for immunotherapy.

Our study suggests that functional cell experiments confirmed AL031985.3’s capacity to enhance HCC cell proliferation, invasion, and migration abilities. Critically, the oncogenic function of AL031985.3 was substantiated in vivo through dual murine models. Orthotopic implantation models assays revealed thatcoherent modulation of EMT markers: E-cadherin expression was elevated in AL031985.3-silenced tumours, whereas mesenchymal indicators (N-cadherin, Vimentin, Slug) were markedly suppressed. Its critical involvement in metastasis was robustly demonstrated using an experimental lung colonisation assay. The significant reduction in pulmonary metastatic burden following AL031985.3 knockdown underscores its function in promoting disseminated disease, a hallmark of advanced HCC.These collective findings mechanistically align AL031985.3 with HCC metastatic progression and provide in vivo validation of its therapeutic tractability.

Although our functional validation focused on AL031985.3 as the most clinically and biologically prominent member of the 9‑DRLR signature, we acknowledge that comprehensive experimental interrogation of all model components would further strengthen its biological plausibility. The decision to prioritise AL031985.3 was guided by its dual clinical significance: Survival analyses revealed that, amongst the nine signature components, only AL031985.3 and AL442125.2 possessed independent prognostic value for overall survival in high-grade (G3–G4) HCC (Log-rank P < 0.05). The remaining seven lncRNAs, while essential for the aggregate risk score, did not exhibit statistical significance individually. This observation underscores the imperative of the 9-DRLR combinatorial signature, suggesting that these transcripts likely function through synergistic networks rather than as solitary drivers. Furthermore, AL031985.3 displayed the most robust differential expression profile between tumour and adjacent non-tumour tissues, consolidating its status as the primary candidate for elucidating the disulfidptosis-related oncogenic phenotype.

Nevertheless, we recognise that the remaining eight DRLRs remain experimentally unexplored. To address this gap, we performed extensive in silico analyses that revealed strong and mechanistically coherent correlations between each DRLR and genes central to disulfidptosis, covering actin cytoskeleton organisation, glucose metabolism, redox homeostasis, and actin nucleation pathways. For instance, AL442125.2’s association with TLN1 and IQGAP1 positions it as a potential modulator of disulfide‑induced cytoskeletal collapse, while AC026356.1’s broad correlation network suggests it may act as a master regulator integrating metabolic and structural signals. These bioinformatic observations are further corroborated by emerging literature: Regarding AC026356.1, studies have confirmed its high expression in HCC tissues and its association with poor patient prognosis [43]. Functional experiments demonstrated that m6A-modified AC026356.1 stabilises IL11 mRNA by binding IGF2BP1, activating the IL11/STAT3 signalling pathway and promoting HCC cell proliferation, migration, and liver metastasis [43]. Another study constructed a ceRNA network, revealing that AC026356.1 participates in tumour progression by regulating the miR-30d-5p/FAP axis. In lung cancer, silencing AC026356.1 inhibited the proliferation and migration of cisplatin-resistant cells and acted as a downstream target of METTL14/IGF2BP2 to regulate stemness [44]. These studies provide direct evidence for the oncogenic role of AC026356.1 in HCC. Concerning POLH-AS1: Recent research indicates that POLH-AS1 is associated with herpes simplex virus infection, neuroactive receptor interaction, and the cAMP signalling pathway; its silencing inhibited the proliferation, invasion, and migration of Hep3B and HepG2 cells [45], suggesting a potential pro-tumorigenic role in HCC.

Despite enhancing our understanding of AL031985.3’s role in HCC and highlighting its potential as a prognostic biomarker and therapeutic target, thereby offering new directions for personalised treatment strategies in HCC, our study still has certain limitations. First, we acknowledge the absence of validation in an independent external multi‑centre cohort as a principal limitation of this study. Future validation of the 9‑DRLR model using independent multi‑centre external data will be essential to confirm its generalisability across diverse populations. Secondly, the exploration of AL031985.3’s role in HCC progression is still in its nascent stages, necessitating further clinical investigations, especially prospective studies, to validate AL031985.3’s accuracy and reliability as a prognostic and therapeutic response predictive marker. Third, further molecular experimental validation is required to substantiate our findings and elucidate the mechanism by which DRLRs AL031985.3 enhances HCC invasion and metastasis. Fourth, we acknowledge that as the molecular landscape of disulfidptosis continues to expand, additional genes with potential regulatory roles have been identified subsequent to the initiation of our research. Whilst our 9-DRLR signature demonstrated high predictive fidelity based on the foundational 10-gene repertoire, the exclusion of recently emerging candidates constitutes a temporal limitation of our prognostic model. Future iterations incorporating the most up‑to‑date disulfidptosis‑related gene sets will be necessary to further refine the prognostic model and enhance its predictive accuracy for high‑grade HCC. Finally, we acknowledge that the lack of comprehensive in vitro and in vivo validation for all nine DRLRs remains a limitation of the current study. Future investigations in our laboratory will focus on dissecting the individual and synergistic contributions of these lncRNAs using RNA interference and multiplexed functional assays to fully resolve their roles in disulfidptosis-mediated tumour progression.

Our study is the first to reveal the differential expression characteristics of DRLRs across different pathological grades of HCC. By constructing and validating this newly developed risk prediction model, we successfully achieved precise evaluations of high histological grade (G3-G4) in HCC patients. Further research discovered that downregulation of AL031985.3 significantly inhibited HCC cell migration and invasion capabilities. Based on these findings, we propose new strategies for implementing individualised immunotherapies for HCC patients, whilst confirming the potential value of AL031985.3 as a therapeutic target for high-grade pathological (G3-G4) HCC, providing new insights for clinical diagnosis and treatment.
