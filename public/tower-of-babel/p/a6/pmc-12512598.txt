AI-driven abstract generating: evaluating LLMs with a tailored prompt under the PRISMA-A framework
Authors: Gizem Boztaş Demi̇r, Şule Gökmen, Yağızalp Süküt, Kübra Gülnur Topsakal, Serkan Görgülü
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to assess and compare ChatGPT-4o and Gemini Pro’s ability to generate structured abstracts from full-text systematic reviews and meta-analyses in orthodontics, based on adherence to the PRISMA Abstract (PRISMA-A) Checklist, using a customised prompt developed for this purpose.Materials and methodsA total of 162 full-text systematic reviews and meta-analyses published in Q1-ranked orthodontic journals since January 2019 were included. Each full-text article was processed by ChatGPT-4o and Gemini Pro, using a PRISMA-A Checklist-aligned structured prompt. Outputs were scored using a tailored Overall quality Score OQS derived from 11 PRISMA-A checklist. Inter-rater and time-dependent reliability were assessed with Intraclass Correlation Coefficients (ICCs), and model outputs were compared using Mann–Whitney U tests.ResultsBoth models yielded satisfactory OQS in generating PRISMA-A checklist compliant abstracts; however, ChatGPT-4o consistently achieved higher scores than Gemini Pro. The most notable differences were observed in the “Included Studies” and “Synthesis of Results” sections, where ChatGPT-4o produced more complete and structurally coherent outputs. ChatGPT-4o achieved a mean OQS of 21.67 (SD 0.58) versus 21.00 (SD 0.71) for Gemini Pro, a difference that was highly significant (p < 0.001).ConclusionsBoth LLMs demonstrated the ability to generate PRISMA-A-compliant abstracts from systematic reviews, with ChatGPT-4o consistently achieving higher quality scores than Gemini Pro. While tested in orthodontics, the approach holds potential for broader applications across evidence-based dental and medical research. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses are essential to evidence-based dentistry but can be challenging and time-consuming to report in accordance with established standards. The structured prompt developed in this study may assist researchers in generating PRISMA-A-compliant outputs more efficiently, helping to accelerate the completion and standardisation of high-level clinical evidence reporting.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-06982-4.

Introduction

Large language models (LLMs) represent a significant advancement in natural language processing (NLP), a branch of artificial intelligence (AI) focused on enabling computers to process and generate human language. Initially designed for basic text completion and classification tasks, LLMs have evolved with the integration of transformer architectures, allowing them to interpret context, generate coherent responses, and adapt to complex linguistic structures. Recent developments have expanded their capabilities beyond simple language tasks [1]. Advancements in deep learning, along with techniques such as prompt engineering and manual model fine-tuning, have contributed to improving LLM capabilities, allowing them to be tailored for specific domains [2, 3]. This adaptability has facilitated the development of specialised models tailored to particular tasks and applications. LLMs are well-suited for text summarisation due to their ability to process and understand linguistic structures beyond simple word matching. By leveraging transformer architectures, these models assign weighted importance to different parts of a text, allowing them to prioritise key information while maintaining coherence and logical flow [4, 5].

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses are among the most extensive forms of scientific research, integrating large volumes of evidence to answer specific clinical or scientific questions. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses involve multiple critical steps, all requiring a high level of methodological rigor [6, 7]. To ensure consistency and transparency in reporting, the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines were introduced. Many high-quality journals now require adherence to PRISMA guidelines, including the use of structured checklists to enhance reporting standards [6–8]. To further standardise abstract writing, the PRISMA Abstract (PRISMA-A) Checklist was developed. Given the structured nature of systematic reviews and meta-analyses, summarising key findings while adhering to these standards is both challenging and time-consuming [7, 9]. However, understanding the working mechanism of LLMs and guiding the models through structured prompts can assist researchers in this process. A recent study demonstrated that prompt engineering enhances LLMs’ ability to perform complex research tasks in orthodontics [3]. Similarly, Dagdelen et al. [10] found that fine-tuning and structured prompting improved model performance in scientific text processing, outperforming traditional methods in accuracy and information extraction.

Previous studies have evaluated the ability of LLMs to generate scientific abstracts without structured guidance [11, 12]. Notably, these studies did not employ prompt engineering or fine-tuning techniques to improve output quality and adherence to reporting standards. To enhance structural consistency, models can be guided through prompt engineering or fine-tuned on structured datasets, allowing for greater control over output quality and adherence to specific formatting requirements. This is especially important in fields where summarisation requires not only linguistic accuracy but also the extraction of key findings from comprehensive datasets [2, 3, 10].

This study aims to evaluate the ability of Gemini Pro and ChatGPT-4o to generate systematic review and meta-analysis abstracts in orthodontics in accordance with the PRISMA-A Checklist. A structured prompt, specifically designed to align with PRISMA guidelines, was developed and implemented to guide the models in abstract generation. The null hypothesis is that ChatGPT‑4o and Gemini Pro will yield equivalent Overall Quality Scores (OQS) when generating PRISMA‑A‑compliant structured abstracts from full‑text systematic reviews and meta‑analyses in orthodontics.

Sample size determination and post-hoc power analysis

A systematic search identified 162 eligible full-text articles; all eligible records were included and processed by both models (n = 162 abstracts per model). We included the entire eligible sample rather than excluding records to meet a pre-specified sample size [12, 13]. A post-hoc power analysis was performed using G*Power software (Franz Faul, Universität Kiel, Kiel, Germany) because the full set of eligible records was used (n = 162 abstracts per model). Based on the observed group means and pooled standard deviation, Cohen’s d was 0.36 (pooled) [14]. With α = 0.05, the observed post-hoc power for an independent two-sample comparison is ≈ 0.91. For guidance, an effect of this size would require ~ 119 subjects/group for 80% power, ~ 159/group for 90% power and ~ 196/group for 95% power.

Prompt engineering

A structured prompt-engineering approach was adopted to generate abstracts conforming to the PRISMA-A Checklist [3]. Recognising the complexity of guiding a large language model in a specialised domain, we employed an iterative chain-of-thought prompting technique. Successive pilot tests were conducted, each using 10 randomly selected systematic-review abstracts adhering to PRISMA-A. After each round, we quantified checklist-item omission rates and formatting errors, and two senior orthodontic researchers independently reviewed the outputs to confirm completeness and accuracy. Incorporating their feedback, we refined the prompt to guide the model through sequential subtasks thereby leveraging the model’s attention mechanisms to minimise omissions and ensure full PRISMA-A compliance. In the final iteration, both models produced abstracts without any omissions, and both experts confirmed that the outputs consistently satisfied all PRISMA-A criteria. Abstract generation process

The full-text articles were carefully processed before being input into the models to prevent any influence from pre-existing abstracts or identifiable metadata. Each systematic review and meta-analysis was extracted and saved as a Word document, with all metadata—including the original abstract, journal details, and any additional article identifiers—removed. LLMs rely on pattern recognition and probabilistic text generation. If an identifiable abstract were present, the models might prioritise that section rather than synthesising information from the complete article. During preparation of Word documents for LLM input, all tables—which often contain critical quantitative details such as participant counts, effect estimates, and subgroup analyses—were preserved as editable objects rather than flattened or manually converted to text. Likewise, figures (including flow diagrams and risk-of-bias graphics) were retained in their original form to ensure that visually encoded information remained available. This dual strategy avoids data loss that can occur with plain-text extraction. While including tables and figures may introduce some formatting “noise,” we judged this preferable to risking inadvertent truncation or bias; by leveraging the multimodal parsing capabilities of ChatGPT-4o and Gemini Pro, we ensured comprehensive input for abstract generation. To standardise input conditions, each Word file was uploaded into the model alongside the structured prompt. A new chat session was initiated for each document to eliminate any potential influence from previous interactions. Initial abstract generation was performed on ChatGPT-4o (version 4.0) and Gemini Pro (version 2.0) between 10 and 11 February 2025. A one-month stability check was conducted on 10–11 March 2025 using the same model versions. No provider-issued updates or fine-tuning occurred during this interval. Evaluation process

To assess the quality of the generated abstracts, an Overall Quality Score (OQS) was developed based on the PRISMA-A Checklist. 11 checklist items were used for scoring (Table 2) [9, 12, 13]. The ‘Title’ item was excluded from our OQS because it pertains to article metadata rather than the abstract body itself, and titles are generally dictated by journal style rather than model output. Moreover, title accuracy falls outside the scope of abstract content evaluation, as it cannot be assessed for completeness or consistency in the same way as the other PRISMA-A items. Therefore, we limited the OQS to the 11 PRISMA-A checklist items that evaluate abstract content and structure.” Each PRISMA-A Checklist item was evaluated using a three-point scale:

Overall quality score (OQS) framework based on 11 PRISMA items

The total OQS thus ranges from 0 to 22, with higher scores indicating better structural adherence and factual accuracy. This composite score is a pragmatic and widely accepted measure of abstract quality in evidence-synthesis research [9, 12, 13].

Two independent researchers conducted the scoring. In cases of uncertainty, a third senior researcher was consulted for adjudication. Before the main evaluation, a training phase was conducted using 10 sample abstracts, where both researchers reviewed abstracts together to establish a consistent scoring approach [13]. Following this, each abstract was independently scored based on a full-text review. To ensure a detailed assessment, each checklist item was evaluated individually, and scores for specific abstract sections, such as the methods section, were analysed separately. Additionally, a total score was calculated for each abstract, with a maximum possible score of 22 if all 11 criteria were fully met. The final OQS score for each abstract was determined by averaging the scores assigned by both researchers. We evaluated each generated abstract against the PRISMA-A checklist by requiring both structural inclusion and factual accuracy of each reporting item. Specifically, a checklist item was scored as present only if it appeared in the abstract and its reported value matched the source full text; items that were mentioned but contained incorrect or incomplete data were scored as absent. We did not compare generated abstracts to the original published abstracts—since their PRISMA-A adherence varied widely—focusing instead on fidelity to the source documents. Other dimensions such as readability, linguistic fluency, and academic writing style were beyond the scope of this analysis. To assess the temporal stability of model‑generated abstracts, we repeated the PRISMA‑A scoring of each abstract one month after the initial evaluation, under identical conditions and without any model updates. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) were calculated for each checklist item based on the mean scores across the two time points. ICC values ranged from 0.92 to 1.00, indicating excellent test–retest reliability for both ChatGPT‑4o and Gemini Pro. We also evaluated the consistency of scoring between two independent researchers on the initial assessment. ICCs for inter‑rater agreement on each checklist item ranged from 0.93 to 1.00, demonstrating a high level of concordance between evaluators.

Statistical analysis

Statistical methods were employed to assess differences between groups. The Shapiro-Wilk Kolmogorov–Smirnov tests were used to evaluate whether the data followed a normal distribution. Both tests led to the same conclusion regarding distributional assumptions. As the results indicated a deviation from normality, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied to compare differences between groups. Inter-rater reliability was assessed using the Intraclass Correlation Coefficient (ICC) to evaluate the consistency of measurements between researchers. Additionally, time-dependent ICC analysis was conducted to examine the reproducibility of abstract generation over different time points, accounting for potential variability due to continuous model updates. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 21), with a significance level set at p < 0.05. Data visualisation and graphical representations were conducted using R software (Version 4.2.2).

Statistically significant differences were observed between ChatGPT-4o and Gemini Pro in several PRISMA checklist items. Risk of Bias (ChatGPT-4o: 1.98 ± 0.11, Gemini Pro: 1.93 ± 0.27, p = 0.019), Included Studies (ChatGPT-4o: 1.96 ± 0.17, Gemini Pro: 1.87 ± 0.32, p = 0.002), Synthesis of Results (ChatGPT-4o: 1.96 ± 0.18, Gemini Pro: 1.82 ± 0.37, p < 0.001), and Funding (ChatGPT-4o: 2 ± 0, Gemini Pro: 1.79 ± 0.6, p < 0.001) all showed significantly higher scores for ChatGPT-4o compared to Gemini Pro.

When comparing the structural components of the abstracts, significant differences were found in the Methods (ChatGPT-4o: 7.77 ± 0.41, Gemini Pro: 7.62 ± 0.53, p = 0.007), Results (ChatGPT-4o: 3.93 ± 0.25, Gemini Pro: 3.7 ± 0.47, p < 0.001), and Other (ChatGPT-4o: 4 ± 0, Gemini Pro: 3.75 ± 0.68, p < 0.001) sections, with ChatGPT-4o consistently outperforming Gemini Pro.

Regarding overall performance, a statistically significant difference was observed between the two models (ChatGPT-4o: 21.67 ± 0.53, Gemini Pro: 21 ± 1, p < 0.001). Both models performed well in generating PRISMA-A Checklist-compliant abstracts, but ChatGPT-4o demonstrated a higher overall adherence to reporting standards, particularly in key methodological and results-oriented sections.

Discussion

In this study, a structured prompt was specifically developed based on the PRISMA-A Checklist to guide two Pro large language models—ChatGPT-4o and Gemini Pro—in generating structured abstracts for systematic reviews and meta-analyses in orthodontics. This tailored prompt ensured that the models were evaluated under standardised conditions, focusing on their ability to address each required checklist item. According to our knowledge, this is the first study in dentistry and orthodontics to systematically evaluate abstract generation ability of LLMs using a widely accepted reporting framework such as PRISMA-A. Overall, both models demonstrated satisfactory performance, highlighting their potential as supportive tools for academic writing.

In the present study, a tailored prompt was developed through prompt engineering to guide large language models in generating abstracts that aligned with the PRISMA-A Checklist. This structured approach aimed to enhance model performance by clearly directing the output towards established reporting criteria. Prior studies have highlighted the utility of prompt engineering in enabling LLMs to perform complex academic tasks [3, 10]. By contrast a previous investigation which evaluated LLM-generated abstracts for RCTs based on the CONSORT-A checklist, relied on more generic prompts and reported lower quality scores in comparison to original abstracts [12]. This comparison suggests that the level of prompt specificity and structural alignment may substantially influence the quality and completeness of generated abstracts.

Both large language models achieved high OQSs in generating PRISMA-compliant abstracts in the field of orthodontics. However, ChatGPT-4o consistently outperformed Gemini across multiple PRISMA-A Checklist items, suggesting a more refined ability to interpret and synthesise complex scientific content. This may be attributed to ChatGPT-4o’s broader training dataset and reinforcement learning strategies, which likely enhance its sensitivity to the nuances of scientific language and reporting. While earlier studies found Gemini to perform more favourably in general academic support tasks (e.g., literature navigation, reference formatting), these involved less structured tasks and did not rely on checklist-based assessments [15, 16]. A recent comparative study in the context of cybersecurity education similarly found no statistically significant performance difference between ChatGPT and Gemini Pro, yet observed that ChatGPT benefitted more from prompt engineering by generating more detailed and coherent responses to open-ended questions [17]. These insights align with our findings, where the structured and checklist-oriented nature of the PRISMA Abstract task may have conferred an advantage to ChatGPT-4o.

Although the overall OQS difference of 0.67 points (~ 3%) may seem small, it reflects substantial gains in the Methods and Results domains—areas with the greatest manual editing burden. GPT 4o’s superior performance on complex items like “Synthesis of Results” and “Risk of Bias Assessment” suggests it can significantly reduce revision time, offering practical efficiency benefits for researchers. While Gemini Pro remains a capable performer overall, GPT-4o’s superior performance on essential reporting items suggests it may more efficiently streamline abstract preparation.

Among all PRISMA-A checklist items, “Specify the methods used to present and synthesise results” received the lowest scores from both GPT-4o and Gemini Pro. This item demands that abstracts explicitly state whether results were summarised narratively or via vote counting, which meta-analytic model was applied, and which heterogeneity or sensitivity analyses were conducted. Such procedural details are typically dispersed across the Methods and Results sections and articulated in non-standardised, technical language. Accurately abstracting them requires advanced cross-textual retrieval of the original methodological descriptions, precise semantic interpretation of statistical terminology, and generation of concise, standardised phrasing within the constraints of an abstract. While both models handle concrete numerical extraction competently, they struggle with this level of methodological reasoning and abstraction, resulting in the lowest performance on this critical synthesis criterion.

In the RESULTS section—including both the “Included Studies” and “Synthesis of Results” criteria—both GPT-4o and Gemini Pro performed well, but GPT-4o’s advantage was more pronounced. GPT-4o accurately reported the total number of included studies and participants and summarised relevant study characteristics (e.g., study design, interventions, follow-up durations). It also reported, for each outcome, the number of contributing studies and participants, the meta-analytic summary, and the direction of effect. Gemini Pro similarly retrieved and structured most in-text information; however, when critical quantitative details appeared only in tables, GPT-4o—thanks to our preservation of tables as editable objects in the Word inputs—could directly extract and integrate these values, whereas Gemini Pro frequently could not. It is possible that, with fully narrative, text-only inputs, these differences might be less pronounced. These findings likely reflect GPT-4o’s enhanced table-based reasoning capabilities, as evidenced by its superior performance on benchmarks such as TabFact and WikiTableQuestions [18]. Notably, when critical quantitative details were presented exclusively in tables—such as participant counts, subgroup analyses, or meta‑analytic effect sizes—GPT‑4o demonstrated superior performance due to our preservation of tables as editable objects in the Word inputs. GPT‑4o reliably extracted and integrated these values into its summaries, whereas Gemini Pro frequently omitted or misreported them. Given that systematic reviews and meta‑analyses inherently rely on tabular data, users planning to submit table‑inclusive abstracts may find GPT‑4o the preferable choice for ensuring comprehensive and accurate table‑driven summaries.

In contrast, clearly defined and explicitly reported elements such as “Objectives,” “Funding,” and “Registration” allowed both models to achieve near-perfect OQSs. These items are often presented in uniform and easily identifiable formats within full texts, which aligns well with how LLMs process structured and contextually predictable data. Consequently, these categories benefited from greater accuracy, reflecting the models’ ability to detect and reproduce information when it appears in standardised forms.

While both models achieved satisfactory OQSs in generating structured abstracts aligned with PRISMA-A standards, it is important to interpret these results within the context of their intended use. Systematic reviews and meta-analyses represent the highest tier of scientific evidence, requiring careful methodology, rigorous analysis, and transparent reporting [6, 7]. Although large language models like ChatGPT-4o and Gemini Pro show promise as supportive tools in this process—particularly for structuring and drafting abstracts—their outputs must not be accepted uncritically. Researchers must thoroughly review and verify all generated content to ensure accuracy, scientific validity, and contextual appropriateness. LLMs can serve as effective assistants, but they cannot replace the depth of domain expertise, critical judgement, and ethical responsibility that underpin high-quality scientific reporting [3, 19, 20].

Our study has several limitations. We preserved all tables as editable objects and retained figures to safeguard quantitative and visual data, but model performance on purely narrative, text-only sources may differ. Our evaluation measured both structural adherence to the PRISMA-A checklist—ensuring each item’s presence and correct placement—and the accuracy of extracted data elements; however, we did not assess readability, linguistic fluency, or the clinical validity of model outputs against original abstracts or patient-level outcomes. The rapid evolution of LLMs, with ongoing updates and retraining, may affect reproducibility over time, and despite high inter-rater reliability, some interpretative variability in scoring remains possible. Focusing solely on orthodontic systematic reviews limits generalisability to other dental specialties or broader medical fields, and our specific prompt engineering workflow represents just one of many viable strategies—alternative designs or fine-tuning approaches could yield different results.

Conclusion

Both ChatGPT‑4o and Gemini Pro, guided by our tailored prompt, can efficiently generate PRISMA‑A–compliant abstracts from systematic reviews and meta‑analyses—providing a practical, reproducible tool to streamline abstract writing and enhance the dissemination of high‑level clinical evidence. Although tested in orthodontics, this structured prompt-engineering approach requires further validation to determine its applicability to other areas of dentistry and broader medical fields. With leading journals increasingly requiring compliance with reporting frameworks such as PRISMA and CONSORT, our approach may serve as a foundation for AI-assisted tools that support researchers in generating structured, standard-compliant scientific outputs. Crucially, these models are designed to augment—not replace—expert judgment: all AI-generated content must undergo critical human review to verify scientific accuracy, contextual relevance, and integrity, particularly when summarising complex systematic reviews and meta-analyses.
