ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundThe triple burden of malnutrition (TBM)— the coexistence of undernutrition, overweight, and micronutrient deficiencies such as anaemia—is a growing public health challenge in low- and middle-income countries. Amid Ecuador’s rapid nutrition transition, we examined the prevalence of TBM and its components among preschool children and assessed inequalities by sociodemographic characteristics and poverty.MethodsWe analysed nationally representative data from the 2022–23 National Survey on Child Undernutrition among children aged 0–59 months. Nutritional outcomes were defined using WHO standards. Weighted bi-probit, logistic and ordinal logistic regression models were used to examine associations between malnutrition and child, maternal, household characteristics and poverty indicators, adjusting for demographic factors.FindingsStunting or wasting affected 17.1% (95% CI: 17.2–19.1) of children under 5 (n = 1,293,325), initially higher in boys, then shifting to girls by age three, with the highest prevalence among Indigenous children. Overweight prevalence was 4.9% (95% CI: 4.5–5.4), higher in boys, and highest among White children aged 18–53 months. Coexistence of undernutrition and overweight affected 0.6% (95% CI 0.5–0.7) or ∼7760 children, primarily boys under three. Among children aged 6–59 months (n = 1,172,450), severe anaemia affected 14.8% (95% CI 13.9–15.7) and 0.33% (95% CI 0.24–0.40; n = 3869) experienced TBM, disproportionately among socially disadvantaged Indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorian boys under three.InterpretationChildhood malnutrition in Ecuador exhibits overlapping, socially patterned, and spatially clustered burdens, concentrated in early childhood. Addressing TBM requires integrated, early, equity-focused “triple-duty” targeted strategies that concurrently tackle undernutrition, overweight, and anaemia as interconnected manifestations of structural inequities.FundingThis research was funded by the 10.13039/501100000272NIHR (grant NIHR134801) using UK international development funding from the UK Government to support global health research.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed, Scopus, Embase, Web of Science, and regional databases (LILACS, SciELO) for studies published between January 2010 and 2025 using the terms “double burden of malnutri∗”, “stunt∗”, “wast∗”, “overweight∗”, “child∗”, “anaem∗”, “haemogl∗”, “body mass index”, “growth pattern∗”, “Ecuador”, “South America”, and “Latin America”, without language restrictions.

The double burden of malnutrition (DBM)—the coexistence of undernutrition and overweight within individuals, households, or populations—has become a hallmark of the global nutrition transition, particularly in low- and middle-income countries undergoing rapid urbanisation and dietary shifts. In 2024, global estimates indicated that 23.2% of children under five were stunted, 6.6% wasted, 5.5% overweight, and 40% anaemic—the latest WHO anaemia figures date from 2019. In Latin America, stunting (∼10%) and wasting (∼1.3%) have remained relatively stable since 2012, while overweight rose modestly (by ∼2%), and anaemia declined from 22% to 19% in 2019.

Ecuador diverges from these trends. As of 2024, stunting prevalence among children under five was 17.7%, nearly double the regional average. Wasting (∼0.9%) remained slightly lower than the Latin American mean, while overweight declined from 6.9% in 2012 to 4.7%—half the regional average (9.9%) and below the global estimate. Anaemia prevalence was 23.5%, above the regional average (19.0%), though recent data suggest figures exceeding 40%, placing Ecuador among the countries with the highest global risk. Prior national and subnational studies documented elevated stunting and anaemia among Indigenous children and rising overweight in wealthier groups. However, no recent research has jointly examined the double and triple burden–the coexistence of undernutrition, overweight, and anaemia–using up-to-date nationally representative data, nor explored their social, ethnic, and poverty gradients.

Added value of this study

Using data from the 2022–23 National Survey on Child Undernutrition (ENDI), this is the first nationally representative study to assess the concurrent prevalence of undernutrition (stunting or wasting), overweight, and anaemia among Ecuadorian children aged below 5 years. We mapped their prevalence and concurrence by age, sex, ethnicity, and poverty. Malnutrition affected nearly one in four children. Undernutrition was more prevalent among boys and Indigenous children; overweight was more common among White and wealthier urban children. Nearly half of undernourished children were also anaemic, and one in five were severely anaemic. These findings provide the first national-level evidence that Ecuador’s nutrition transition is producing divergent nutritional risks across social groups, with the double and triple burden now extending beyond urban and affluent settings to include marginalised and Indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorian populations.

Implications of all the available evidence

Ecuador faces a persistent and inequitable triple burden of malnutrition in early childhood. Estimates from specialised sources are often misleading, and our data now provide more accurate updated corrections. Our findings highlight the need to move beyond “double-duty” actions toward integrated “triple-duty” strategies that address undernutrition, overweight, and anaemia simultaneously. Policy responses must focus on early-life nutrition, maternal health, and food-system reform while embedding ethnicity, geography, and poverty indicators into monitoring frameworks. Tackling structural inequalities through equity-based, intersectoral approaches will be essential to breaking the intergenerational cycle of malnutrition.

Introduction

Monitoring early childhood growth—a key marker of lifelong cognitive and physical development—is a critical societal responsibility. Robust evidence links inadequate nutrition and care during pregnancy, infancy, and early childhood to long-term adverse health outcomes including impaired physical growth, cognitive development, immune function, educational attainment, increasing the risk of chronic diseases, reduced productivity, and intergenerational disadvantage in adulthood.1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7 Understanding early growth patterns and the aetiology of their disparities is essential to designing timely, targeted interventions that prevent malnutrition—stunting, wasting, overweight, and anaemia—often driven by nutritional deficiencies among vulnerable populations living in poverty.

The different forms of malnutrition are interconnected,8 and their coexistence within individuals, households, or populations9 gave rise to the concept of the double burden of malnutrition (DBM), now recognised as a major public health challenge globally.10 Evidence suggests that “double-duty” interventions addressing both undernutrition and overweight simultaneously may be more efficient and cost-effective than single-focus approaches.11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17

As of 2024, an estimated 150.2 million children under five globally (prevalence 23.2%, 95% CI 22.6–23.8) were living with stunting, 42.8 million (6.6%, 95% CI 5.3–8.1) were affected by wasting, and 35.5 million (5.5%, 95% CI 4.9–6.1) were children with overweight or obesity.18,19 While prevalence of children exhibiting stunting has declined by a third since 2000, reductions among those affected by wasting have been modest (8.7%), and the prevalence of overweight or obesity in this population continues to rise, with regional variations.

Ecuador, an ethnically diverse Latin American country, comprises four natural regions—the Coast, Highlands, Amazon, and Insular (Galápagos)— with distinct geographic and socioeconomic profiles that shape livelihoods, service access, and poverty. Indigenous peoples are descendants of pre-colonial populations who retain elements of their social, cultural, economic, and political institutions. Afro-Ecuadorians trace their heritage to Africans who survived the transatlantic slave trade. Montubios are rural coastal communities with distinct agricultural traditions and cultural practices. Mestizos—individuals of mixed Indigenous and European (or other) ancestry—represent most of the population and embody hybrid cultural traditions. Whites are socially and culturally identified with European ancestry.20

In 2022, Ecuador reported one of the highest stunting rates among preschool children in Latin America (17.7%), nearly twice the regional average (9.9%), though down from 24.3% in 2012.21 Wasting ranged from 2.5–5%, above the South American average of 1.3%, while overweight affected 4.7% of children—half the regional average (9.9%).18,19 Anaemia prevalence among children aged 6–59 months was 23.5% in 2022, slightly down from 25.7% in 2012, mirroring trends in neighbouring countries with milder disease now being more common.22 Supplementary Table S1 provides national, regional, and global estimates from the past decade for temporal comparisons.

Low- and upper-middle-income countries are home to 72% of the global population of children under five and account for 70% of those living with stunting, 81% of those affected by wasting, and 73% of those with overweight or obesity.19 Ecuador has 1.29 million children under five (representing 7.6% of the population, 2022 Census20) and is undergoing rapid nutritional and demographic transitions. Internal rural-urban migration, increased sedentarism and consumption of ultra-processed foods, and altered gut microbiota in young children23 may elevate future cardiometabolic disease risk.24 In 2024, 25% of Ecuadorians lived below the national poverty line (∼US$6.85/day), with rural poverty nearly twice as high and extreme poverty affecting nearly 10%.25

In this context of profound social and economic change, we used data from the National Survey on Child Undernutrition (Encuesta Nacional de Desnutrición Infantil (ENDI 2022–2023))15 to examine the coexistence of children living with stunting and those affected by wasting (combined due to the low prevalence of wasting), children with overweight or obesity (also combined due to the low prevalence of obesity), and anaemia among children under five. Analyses were stratified by age, sex, and ethnicity, and we assessed the associations of maternal, household, and health system determinants with child growth during the first five years of life.

Methods

Study design and data sources

The ENDI 2022–2023 data was a representative national household survey of health, nutrition, and related socioeconomic indicators conducted by the National Institute of Statistics and Censuses (INEC). Included in the survey is the collection of data on nutritional status of children aged under 5 years in each household surveyed. Data collected between 12th July 2022 and 4th July 2023, were used. The survey used a stratified, two-stage probabilistic design15,16 with 147 strata across continental Ecuador (Supplementary Methods). A stratum corresponded to a combination of province and urban vs. rural areas. Within each stratum, Primary Sampling Units (PSUs) were selected with a probability reflecting the number of private dwellings from the 2010 Census although with the population represented by the 2022 Census.15,16,26 The median (Q1-Q3) number of PSUs was 18 (11–25) and ranged 3–85 per stratum. The median (Q1-Q3) number of households with children under 5 years, randomly selected from the Census list of eligible households within a PSU was 7 (6–8). An expansion factor associated with a PSU was the inverse probability of its selection and indicated how many PSUs within that stratum it represented, and adjusted for nonresponse and calibration.15,16

Individual-level variables

Child sex was recorded as male or female and referred to as boys and girls. Standardised anthropometric growth indicators were available—height-for-age z scores (HAZ), weight-for-age (WAZ), body-mass-index-for-age (BMIz), and weight-for-length/height (WHZ), estimated using the WHO reference population.27,28 The prevalence of children living with stunting (HAZ < −2 SD), affected by wasting (WHZ < −2 SD), and with overweight (WAZ > +2 SD) was estimated. Haemoglobin (Hb) concentrations were available for children aged 6–59 months (not applicable in children<6 months) with anaemia classified as Hb < 10.5 g/dL (mild,≥7.0 to <10.5 g/dL to; severe, <7.0 g/dL15,16) and adjusted for altitude. All measurements used standardized and validates survey instruments.16 Individual-level variables included demographic characteristics (sex, age, and ethnicity), recent illness and health interventions including antiparasitic treatment and receipt of nutritional supplements (micronutrients, iron, and vitamin A).

Maternal variables included demographics, education (primary or less vs. any secondary or higher), employment and income, prenatal care, mode of delivery, gestational age, and breastfeeding.

Household-level variables

Household-level variables included monthly income, crowding, geographic location (by geoclimatic regions representing Coast, Sierra, or Amazon), residence type (urban or rural), waste management, water quality (evidence of contamination with E. coli and chlorine treatment), internet access, housing tenure, and parental cohabitation. Household poverty indicators included income poverty, unmet basic needs, and participation in the ‘Bono de 1000 días’ cash-transfer programme (BONO) which started in 2022.29

Statistical analyses

Three binary outcomes were analysed: children living with stunting or wasting, affected by overweight or obesity, and by anaemia. Two ordinal outcome reflected anaemia severity (none, mild and severe) and malnutrition severity (no condition, one condition only, any two and all three [TBM]). All analyses accounted for the complex survey design, incorporating weights for sampling, stratification, clustering, and non-response.15,16 INEC design details are in Supplementary Methods. Estimates are nationally representative and allow derivation of expected population counts based on the 2022 Census. A survey-weighted bivariate probit model jointly estimated the probabilities of children experiencing undernutrition and those with overweight or obesity (defined as the double burden of malnutrition [DBM]) among children aged 0–59 months, accounting for their correlation through latent shared determinants. Latent variables were assumed jointly normal, and a statistically significant correlation coefficient supported simultaneous estimation. Multivariable models (bi-probit, logistic, and ordinal logistic) included age, sex, ethnicity, and their interaction (retained if p < 0.05). All remaining coefficients represent associations between household, maternal, service access, and child health characteristics and the outcome, adjusted for age, sex, and ethnicity. Interactions between these covariates and demographic factors were tested and are presented if statistically significant. Coefficient signs indicate the direction of association. Analyses were exploratory and not intended to support causal inference. Model fitted age-specific prevalence patterns were plotted, stratified by sex and ethnicity.

Anaemia, its severity, and TBM analyses were restricted to children aged 6–59 months and used survey weighted logistic and ordinal logistic models. Patterns in age-specific prevalence, stratified by sex and ethnicity were derived and plotted and adjusted analyses were conducted as for the bi-probit model. Breastfeeding survey analyses among children under three used similar reasoning with specific weights (Supplementary Methods). Tertile classification was applied to province-level spatial estimates, and prevalence rates were mapped to visualize the dual dynamics between DBM, TBM, and their individual components on one hand, and income poverty and receipt of BONO on the other, as well as the relationship between income poverty and BONO receipt. The aim was to detect inconsistencies at provincial level between BONO coverage and those mostly affected by individual malnutrition conditions as well as DBM and TMB. Detailed spatial analyses were limited by lack of household coordinates. Joint and conditional probabilities were also combined with Census data to estimate population counts. Statistical models are detailed in Supplementary Methods.

An additional analysis was done in children under 3 years–using different survey weights exclusively designed to represent this population to assess associations of breastfeeding practices with early growth.

Analyses were implemented in STATA 19.5 (SVY procedures, StataCorp. 2025. Stata Statistical Software: Release 19. College Station, TX: StataCorp LLC). The maps were plotted using R (A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). The funders had no role in the study design; data collection, analysis, or interpretation; writing of the report; or the decision to submit the manuscript for publication.

Ethical considerations

The data used in this study are publicly available and do not contain any personally identifiable information. This research is based exclusively on secondary data from the National Survey on Chronic Child Malnutrition (ENDI), collected by the National Institute of Statistics and Census of Ecuador (INEC) under the framework of Executive Decree No. 1211 and the National Statistical Programme 2021–2025. Data collection and management were conducted in accordance with national regulations and standardised protocols, ensuring confidentiality, data protection, and compliance with established ethical and legal standards.

Results

Population demographics–survey summary

The survey aggregated estimates are shown in Table 1 (selected) and Supplementary Table S2. The analysis sample consisted of 21,531 children from 18,645 households (85.7% had one child sampled, 14.3% had two or more). Survey summary data revealed that nearly one in four (23.0% [95% CI 22.1–24.0]) Ecuadorian children were either living with stunting or wasting (18.1% [95% CI 17.2–19.1]) or with overweight or obesity (5.5% [95% CI 5.1–6.0])—these figures may include both conditions. About 38.6% [95% CI 37.2–40.0] were anaemic, including 14.8% [95% CI 13.9–15.7] severely anaemic. The breastfeeding survey in children below 3 years showed similar patterns with 25.5% [95% CI 24.1–26.9] malnourished, 20.5% [95% CI 19.2–21.9] living with stunting or affected bywasting, 5.9% [95% CI 5.2–6.5] deemed overweight/obese, and 43.0% [95% CI 41.2–44.8] anaemic. Survey-weighted conditional analyses in 6–59 months children (with denominators defined as the total number of children within each nutritional status subgroup) showed marked clustering of anaemia. Among children with stunting or wasting, 45.9% (95% CI 43.4–48.5) were anaemic, and 20.3% [95% CI 18.3–22.4] were severely anaemic. Among children affected by overweight, 31.9% [95% CI 27.1–37.1] were anaemic and 13.6% [95% CI 10.8–17.0] severely anaemic. The highest anaemia burden was observed among children with concurrent stunting or wasting and overweight or obesity: 62.5% [95% CI 51.2–72.5] were anaemic and 25.5% [95% CI 17.3–35.8] were severely anaemic.

Patterns by age sex and ethnicity

Stunting/wasting increased with age, peaking earlier in boys (24.7% at 18 months) than girls (19.6% at 25 months) then declining. Nevertheless, after age 3 years, living with stunting or wasting was more likely in girls than in boys (Fig. 1a). Indigenous children had the highest stunting prevalence reaching 46.9% at 30 months in boys and 44% at 34 months in girls. White and Mestizo children followed national patterns, with prevalence declining faster after 2 years of age. Stunting/wasting prevalence was higher in rural (p = 0.0003) and Andean areas and increased with altitude (p < 0.0001). Increased risk included markers of household poverty-income poverty, unmet basic needs, overcrowding-food insecurity, lower maternal education, vaginal delivery, and prematurity (Supplementary Table S4).

Children of Afro-Ecuadorian mothers were least likely to be affected by overweight.

Anaemia prevalence was higher among children living in the Andean region, at high altitude, and in households with greater poverty and food insecurity. Additional risk factors included lower maternal age and education, vaginal delivery, recent antiparasitic treatment, and recent diarrhoea. Patterns were similar for severe anaemia, which increased by 10.6% [95% CI 8.2–13.0] for every 500 m rise in altitude. After adjustment for child demographics, children of Indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorian mothers were 58% more likely to have severe anaemia than those of Mestizo mothers.30

Health care and health care interventions associated with malnutrition indicators

More prenatal check-ups, private healthcare use, caesarean delivery, and longer gestation were associated with lower risk of stunting or wasting but higher risk of overweight (Table 3, Supplementary Table S4). Greater prenatal care, private healthcare, and caesarean delivery were linked to lower anaemia risk. Receipt of BONO, supplements, vitamin A and iron prescription were also positively associated with the stunting or wasting—reflecting targeted interventions. Partial or total iron intake were protective (p ≤ 0.0001).

Antiparasitic treatment (p = 0.020), recent diarrhoea (p = 0.024), and its prolonged duration increased anaemia risk (p = 0.0011). Children in households receiving BONO were more likely to be livingwith stunting/wasting (p < 0.0001), with associations holding after adjustment for income poverty, overcrowding, and unmet basic needs.

Breastfeeding survey in under 3 years of age

Breastfeeding patterns and their adjusted associations with malnutrition are summarised in Tables 1 and 3. The analysis is based on a sample of 11,513 children under the age of 3. Breastfeeding at birth was associated with lower risk stunting or wasting (p = 0.0043). Longer exclusive breastfeeding or never having been exclusively breastfed increased the likelihood of stunting or wasting (p = 0.0029 and p = 0.0022), and the latter also increased overweight risk (p = 0.021). Anaemia was more common in children breastfed at the time of interview and was more than twice more likely among Montubio children breastfed at birth (p = 0.021). In minimally adjusted multivariable model (adjusted for age, sex, and ethnicity), anaemia was less likely among children breastfed within 24 h of birth. In addition, among Mestizo (p = 0.0038) and Montubio (p = 0.029) children, those who had never been exclusively breastfed had lower odds of anaemia compared with those who had been exclusively breastfed.

Regional distribution of malnutrition and poverty indicators

The Amazon region showed the highest poverty across all indicators (Table 1): 52.0% [95% CI 49.0–54.0] of households had income poverty, 59.0% [95% CI 56.0–62.0] had unmet basic needs, and 46.0% [95% CI 43.0–48.0] received cash transfers. Corresponding figures were lower in the Coast (39.0%, 49.0%, and 41.0%) and lowest in the Andes (26.0%, 24.0%, and 29.0%).

Overweight and obesity prevalence was highest on the Coast (5.4%), followed by the Andes (4.5%) and Amazon (3.8%). Stunting/wasting was similar in the Amazon and Andes (∼21%) but lower on the Coast (14.3%). Concurrent stunting or wasting and overweight or obesity was most common in the Andes (∼0.7%), then Amazon (0.6%) and Coast (0.5%), with no notable variation by demographics.

Anaemia prevalence was highest in the Andes (44.0% [95% CI 42.0–46.0]), followed by Amazon (40.0% [95% CI 38.0–43.0]) and Coast (34.0% [95% CI 32.0–37.0]). Severe anaemia prevalence mirrored this pattern: 19.0% [95% CI 18.0–20.0], 15.0% [95% CI 13.0–17.0], and 12.0% [95% CI 11.0–13.0], respectively. Discussion

This study presents the first nationally representative analysis of the double burden of malnutrition (DBM) and anaemia—together referred to as the triple burden Fig. 2 of malnutrition (TBM)—among children under five years in Ecuador, using Fig. 3 data from the 2022–2023 National Survey on Child Undernutrition. We examined the coexistence of stunting, wasting, overweight, and anaemia, and their patterns by age, sex, ethnicity, socioeconomic status, and geographic location.

Nearly one in four children were living with either stunting or wasting or were affected by overweight or obesity or both, one in seven had severe anaemia, and a critical, spatially clustered subgroup experienced multiple forms of malnutrition, excessively affecting children by their demographics and household poverty. Severe anaemia was present in one fifth of children with stunting or wasting, in more than one in eight children with overweight or obesity, and in one quarter of children with both conditions. A substantial number of children (3,869) experienced the triple burden of malnutrition, particularly affecting Indigenous and Afro-Ecuadorian populations, with consistently higher prevalence among boys than girls across ethnic groups.

The disproportionately high prevalence of anaemia—including severe forms—observed among Afro-Ecuadorian children may be partly attributable to haemoglobinopathies, which affect an estimated one in five individuals in this population.30 These inherited disorders can contribute to persistently low or borderline haemoglobin concentrations or to severe anaemia. TBM was also higher than average among Afro-Ecuadorian boys and girls, underscoring the layered biological and structural vulnerabilities affecting this group.

Our findings reflect Ecuador’s complex and multidimensional nutritional landscape shaped by geographic diversity and distinct local cultures, as well as by persistent socioeconomic deprivation and entrenched structural inequities–particularly those affecting ethnic minority populations. This landscape has been further transformed by urbanisation and accelerated dietary transitions, characterised by a shift from whole and minimally refined foods toward highly processed, energy-dense products. These patterns likely reflect cumulative life-course exposures, whereby nutritional disadvantage, poverty, and social marginalization experienced in early life, increasing vulnerability to both undernutrition and overweight. While broadly aligned with official national statistics, our disaggregated analysis highlights the presence of overlapping conditions—particularly the coexistence of undernutrition, overweight and anaemia in the same children and not accounted for in standard reporting. As a result, official estimates likely overstate the individual prevalence of each condition26 by not distinguishing between children experiencing multiple forms of malnutrition simultaneously.

The nutritional profile of Ecuador’s children reflects the shifting nutritional habits of countries un dergoing the middle-income transition. Advanced analyses revealed that nearly one in four children under 5 years was malnourished: 17.5% were living with stunting or wasting, 4.9% with overweight/obesity, and 0.6% were simultaneously affected by both conditions. Anaemia affected 38.6% of children aged 6–59 months, with 14.8% being severely anaemic, thus placing Ecuador among the countries with the highest prevalence in the region.22,31,32 Moreover, nearly half of 6–59 months children living with stunting or wasting were anaemic (46.4%), and one-fifth severely anaemic, reflecting the biological and social interdependence of growth faltering and micronutrient deficiency and the figures are more concerning in children under 3 years of age—3390 children having all three conditions with 40% of whom combined with severe anaemia.

Sex and ethnic disparities

Sex-based and ethnic inequalities were pronounced. Boys exhibited higher prevalence across all forms of malnutrition—stunting, wasting, overweight, and anaemia—than girls, particularly before 3 years of age, consistent with evidence that male infants are biologically more vulnerable to growth restriction and infection under conditions of deprivation.33,34 Indigenous children experienced the highest burden of stunting (37%) and anaemia, while White and Mestizo children had the greatest prevalence of overweight (5.9%). These findings echo long-standing structural inequities in access to healthcare, education, and nutritious foods. In the Sierra and Amazon Indigenous communities, low crop diversity, cash-oriented farming, and harsh environmental conditions deepen poverty and isolation, perpetuating malnutrition across generations and potentially limiting the effectiveness of intercultural health policies and regulations.35, 36, 37 Conversely, the higher overweight burden among affluent populations suggests that nutritional transition is diverging along social lines, reflecting persistent inequality in health outcomes.

These results confirm that malnutrition and its inequalities in Ecuador are not trending towards diminishing fast enough as hoped despite concerted government efforts including cash transfers, nutrient supplementation, and early maternal nutrition multiple programmes (Bono de Desarrollo Humano since 2003, Bono Joaquin Gallegos Lara since 2010 and Bono de 1000 días since 2022 or BONO).

Undernutrition persists in Indigenous, rural, poor and/or marginalized settings in the Sierra, while overweight is greater in White and wealthier households. The co-occurrence of anaemia, stunting and overweight illustrates the emerging “triple burden”—energy excess coexisting with micronutrient deficiency within the same population, on top of DBM.13,17 Such patterns reflect shifts in food systems, driven by processed food consumption, urbanisation, and socioeconomic stratification across Latin America.11, 12, 13, 14,38

Regional contrasts and environmental determinants

Marked regional heterogeneity was evident. Stunting and wasting was greatest in Andean and Amazon regions (∼22%), while overweight was highest on the Coast (6%). Anaemia and severe anaemia prevalence increased with altitude across all ages and were greatest in the Andes (44%).

Markers of poverty showed that 35% of children lived in income-poor households, 40.3% in households with unmet basic needs, and 24.3% in overcrowded conditions. Although 36.5% of children received the BONO cash transfer—and children experiencing income poverty, unmet needs, or overcrowding were more than three times as likely to receive it—national coverage did not consistently align with areas of greatest need. The positive adjusted associations estimated between the outcomes and poverty-related markers should not be interpreted as adverse effects or unintended programme impacts. They reflect the intentional targeting of government policies toward socioeconomically disadvantaged populations—considering their implementation at the same time as the data collection. Nevertheless, as shown in Fig. 5, several Andean provinces exhibited high levels of the double and triple burden of malnutrition but low BONO coverage. Additional mismatches between nutritional outcomes, income poverty, and BONO distribution are shown in Fig. 5 and Supplementary Fig. S2. These spatial contrasts mirror disparities in poverty, sanitation, and access to health services, highlighting the role of geography and infrastructure in shaping child health. The Amazon region, which has the highest levels of poverty and unmet basic needs, also had the highest BONO coverage—twice that of the Andean region and 22% higher than the Coastal region—yet high stunting persisted, indicating that cash transfers alone were insufficient to offset nutritional deficits. Without parallel investments in food quality, water safety, and healthcare, income support alone is unlikely to break the cycle of deprivation. In contrast, the Coastal region reflects a more advanced nutrition transition, with urbanisation and dietary change contributing to rising obesity and reduced consumption of traditional foods,24 patterns also observed elsewhere in Latin America.39

Maternal, household, and social determinants

Maternal education, income, and social protection emerged as central determinants of child nutrition. Stunting and anaemia (including severe) were significantly more common in children from households facing income poverty, overcrowding, or poor sanitation, and in those whose mothers were younger or less educated. Conversely, higher maternal age, education, and social security affiliation were protective, echoing findings from other studies.35,36,39 Frequent prenatal visits, longer gestation, and delivery in private facilities were associated with lower stunting and anaemia risk, indicating the nutritional benefits of better access to or quality of maternal healthcare. However, overweight risk rose with socioeconomic status, reflecting the nutrition transition’s shift from scarcity to excess among the more advantaged. This contrast mirrors regional patterns in which malnutrition becomes a “disease of inequality at both ends”.13,38

Only 21% of the children under 5 years received iron/multivitamin/minerals for anaemia prevention despite the prevalence of anaemia being excessively high in non-recipients (37.1% 95%CI 35.5–38.7).

Breastfeeding and early feeding practices

The analysis among children under 3 years revealed complex associations between breastfeeding and nutritional outcomes. While early initiation and non-exclusive breastfeeding were associated with lower adjusted odds of stunting and anaemia, excessively prolonged breastfeeding without adequate complementary feeding increased the risk of both outcomes. In Ecuador, only half of newborns were breastfed within 1 h of birth and 55.6% within 48 h, falling short of global targets.40 Exclusive breastfeeding rates have improved over time but remain below optimal levels; complementary feeding practices are often delayed or lacking diversity.41 Breastfeeding protects against infection and anaemia, but after six months, appropriate complementary feeding becomes critical. Culturally tailored support and better data on the timing, quality, diversity, and adequacy of complementary foods are needed.

Micronutrient interventions and persistent anaemia

Ecuador’s National Food and Nutrition Program (2000) demonstrated that distribution of a micronutrient-fortified complementary food through routine health services substantially improved dietary intake, reduced the odds of anaemia by 58%, and produced meaningful gains in weight, highlighting the potential impact of targeted preventive strategies in vulnerable populations.42 Despite national programmes providing iron, multivitamin, and antiparasitic treatments, anaemia prevalence remains high. Iron uptake, even partially consumed, was protective against stunting, and its effect was even stronger with greater adherence. Children who consumed supplements had higher stunting risk, but full adherence seemed to have had some (non-significant) protective effect compared to partial. This may reflect underlying morbidity or caregiving constraints, rather than intervention inefficacy.

Supplements intake may be subjected to challenges related to access, acceptability, and adherence, particularly in rural and Indigenous areas.43 Poor water quality, with E. coli contamination, further compounded anaemia risk. The coexistence of anaemia and overweight observed in approximately 22,000 children reflects poor dietary quality despite adequate energy intake—a pattern increasingly common in urbanised settings—although the most recent available evidence is from 2012.35 Behavioural interventions to promote the use of locally available, nutrient-dense foods—such as eggs and iron-rich staples—remain underutilised due to conflicting health advice and cultural perceptions on their use.43 Addressing anaemia in Ecuador thus demands context-specific, food-based approaches alongside supplementation.

Public health and policy implications

Our findings highlight the importance of considering a shift from the DBM model and its associated double-duty interventions toward a more comprehensive approach that addresses the TBM. Coordinated “triple-duty” actions addressing stunting, micronutrient deficiencies, and overweight should be considered in light of regional experience with integrated programming.11 In Peru, substantial reductions in childhood stunting were achieved through a first-1000-days package linking maternal care, child growth monitoring, micronutrient supplementation, and conditional cash transfers.44 Although anaemia reduction was also prioritised, progress was slower, underscoring the greater complexity of addressing micronutrient deficiencies.45 The Peruvian model, primarily targeting chronic undernutrition, was implemented alongside national targets and routine monitoring systems intended to strengthen cross-sector coordination. In Ecuador, where persistent stunting and anaemia coexists with rising overweight, integrated strategies will need to strengthen diet quality, food system regulation, and safeguards against policy trade-offs to address the triple burden. Tackling these intertwined challenges requires coordinated, equity-focused “triple-duty” actions that address all forms of malnutrition simultaneously.11,17 Policy priorities include: (1) strengthening evidence based information into early-life interventions—maternal nutrition, prenatal care, exclusive breastfeeding, and complementary feeding; (2) improving food systems and regulating marketing of ultra-processed foods; (3) expanding water, sanitation, and healthcare access in rural areas and Indigenous communities recognizing barriers such as racism and prejudice; (4) embedding ethnicity and poverty indicators into national monitoring frameworks.

Integration across sectors—health, education, agriculture, and social protection—would be critical to disrupt intergenerational cycles of malnutrition. Targeted nutrition education linked to cash transfer schemes could enhance their effectiveness. Finally, community-based, culturally grounded approaches are essential to ensure that interventions reach those most affected.

Limitations and future directions

The cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and the development of reliable multivariable diagnostic models. Wasting and stunting may be transient or acute and thus underreported. Anaemia assessment relied solely on haemoglobin measurement, limiting identification of underlying micronutrient deficiencies or considering targeted haemoglobinopathies’ testing. Our dataset did not include information on vaccination status or other infection-prevention measures, which may influence child growth, particularly in the context of the re-emergence of infectious diseases.

The counterintuitive positive association between BONO receipt, and other governmental help and adverse outcomes—including malnutrition and anaemia—likely reflects the programme’s targeting of the most socioeconomically deprived households and the concomitant timing of implementation and data collection, such that there was probably insufficient time for beneficial effects to become apparent. Nevertheless, maps revealed mismatches between BONO coverage and high prevalence of malnutrition.

Cash-transfer exposure is dynamic and may vary with household economic conditions. Nevertheless, the ENDI survey’s scale, design, and disaggregated socioeconomic data strengthen representativeness and offer timely unbiased evidence on Ecuador’s child nutrition status. Future research should employ longitudinal and mixed-methods designs to explore causal mechanisms linking poverty, diet, and biological outcomes, and to evaluate the effectiveness of integrated “triple-duty” policies.

Conclusion

Ecuador faces a structurally rooted nutrition emergency affecting early childhood. Stunting and anaemia persist alongside rising overweight, reflecting intertwined biological and social vulnerabilities and appear to be locally clustered. Addressing this crisis requires urgent, integrated, and equity-oriented strategies that act early in life and target the elements that sustain malnutrition. Transitioning from fragmented interventions to informed, coordinated triple-duty policies will be essential to achieve equitable child growth and health in Ecuador.
