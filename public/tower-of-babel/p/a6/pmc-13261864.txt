Background

The first transcatheter edge-to-edge (TEER) procedure using the TriClip system (Abbott) was performed in 2017 as part of an early research study at the Minneapolis Heart Institute Foundation.1 In April 2020, the TriClip received CE mark approval in the European Union, followed by U.S. Food & Drug Administration approval in April 2024, after which it began to be adopted into clinical practice in the U.S. Two-year follow-up data from the TRILUMINATE trial in 2024 confirmed sustained reduction of tricuspid regurgitation (TR) and a significant reduction in heart failure hospitalizations with the TriClip.2

The safety procedure framework for TEER encompasses several key domains, beginning with patient selection through multidisciplinary heart team discussion and including assessment of frailty, anticoagulation management, and anatomical suitability. Optimization of guideline-directed medical therapy before intervention is essential. Comprehensive preprocedural imaging with both transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography (TEE) is required to confirm anatomic eligibility and guide procedural planning. Intraprocedural safety is supported by general anesthesia, ultrasound-guided femoral vein access, and multiplane TEE with 3-dimensional and fluoroscopy coguidance. Real-time TEE assessment is critical during clip deployment to confirm adequate leaflet insertion depth, assess grasp quality, and evaluate residual regurgitation before device deployment. Continuous hemodynamic monitoring is maintained throughout the procedure. The most commonly reported complications include single-leaflet device attachment, clip migration, and leaflet perforation. Here, we report a case of leaflet perforation as a complication of tricuspid TEER.

Case Presentation

The patient was a 68-year-old man with known coronary artery disease and a history of anterior wall myocardial infarction with subsequent primary percutaneous coronary intervention to the left anterior descending artery and failed percutaneous coronary intervention for chronic total occlusion of the right coronary artery. He had ischemic cardiomyopathy with left ventricular systolic function of 15%, persistent atrial fibrillation, and severe symptomatic functional TR (Figure 1) with moderate right ventricular dilation.

He presented to the emergency room with right-sided heart failure symptoms including abdominal distension and lower limb swelling for 3 months, along with shortness of breath (NYHA functional class III). On examination, he was alert and oriented to time, place, and person. His blood pressure was 106/66 mm Hg, with a heart rate of 80 beats/min and oxygen saturation of 94% on room air. He had elevated jugular venous pressure, abdominal distension, and +2 lower limb edema. During admission, he was started on intravenous diuretics and required ascitic drainage. He drained 9.5 L over 48 hours, with a calculated serum-ascites albumin gradient of 1.6, consistent with a transudative cause owing to chronic heart failure. He underwent right and left heart catheterization. Coronary angiography demonstrated a patent left anterior descending artery stent and chronic total occlusion of the right coronary artery, with a normal left circumflex artery.

The patient was discharged on guideline-directed medical therapy and oral furosemide 80 mg twice daily. Because of prohibitive surgical risk (TRI-SCORE: 14%, GLIDE score: 1), he was subsequently admitted for elective TEER after structural heart team discussion.

Procedure Course

The procedure for TEER of the tricuspid valve was performed under general anesthesia using the TriClip system. The first TriClip XTW device was successfully deployed between the septal and anterior leaflets, resulting in a significant reduction in TR (Figure 2). A second TriClip XTW device was then advanced to a position between the septal and posterior leaflets to achieve further reduction. Before deployment of this second clip, a sudden worsening of the TR jet was noted, originating from the posterior leaflet (Figure 3). Comprehensive TEE assessment revealed an iatrogenic perforation of the posterior tricuspid leaflet. The mechanism of perforation was most likely multifactorial, resulting from a combination of leaflet tissue fragility related to chronic remodeling and mechanical stress from the clip arms during grasping. The second clip was withdrawn, as it was implicated in the leaflet injury and was exacerbating the regurgitation.

A revised strategy was implemented. The team decided to replace and deploy the second clip at a more central location on the septal-anterior coaptation line, adjacent to the first clip, to reduce annular size. The team decision was to attempt to close the perforation of the posterior leaflet by plug. The defect measured approximately 0.5 cm (Figure 4). A 6-mm vascular plug device (the largest size available) was deployed; however, it failed to seal the perforation or reduce the TR, and it was therefore retrieved (Figure 5). The procedure concluded with a residual severe eccentric TR (Figure 6, Video 1), with a mean gradient of 3 mm Hg. The patient was extubated in the catheterization laboratory and was transferred to the coronary care unit in stable condition, clinically euvolemic.

Follow-up echocardiography showed severe TR. The patient was discharged on guideline-directed medical therapy. He was seen and evaluated in the outpatient setting after 1 month and was found to have mild lower limb edema but no ascites, and he was maintaining his activities of daily living. There have been no further admissions for right-sided heart failure for the past 6 months. The plan is to continue medical therapy, and if this fails, to consider the TricValve system (Products & Features) or surgical intervention.

Discussion

This case represents a rare but serious complication iatrogenic perforation of the posterior tricuspid leaflet. Rahhab et al3 reported a case of posterior leaflet perforation during MitraClip implantation requiring surgical bailout. Similarly, Sanchis et al4 described delayed anterior leaflet perforation in a tethered valve 1 month after clip implantation, attributing the injury to excess leaflet tension. In our case, the perforation occurred acutely during device manipulation in the setting of long-standing functional TR with significant leaflet tethering and annular dilation. These anatomical factors increase the technical complexity of the procedure and underscore the importance of real-time multiplane TEE for early detection of complications and improved intraprocedural guidance. Additionally, continuous heart team discussion during the procedure enhances the likelihood of success and helps prevent further complications.

The precise mechanism of the leaflet perforation cannot be determined with certainty. However, we believe the perforation was most likely multifactorial. Specifically, it may have resulted from a combination of leaflet tissue fragility related to chronic remodeling, together with mechanical stress exerted by the clip arms of the TriClip XTW during grasping. Excessive tension on a remodeled and possibly friable leaflet could have contributed to the development of the perforation.

Percutaneous closure of iatrogenic leaflet perforation using vascular plugs has been described in limited case reports. Kłapyta et al5 reported bailout use of an Amplatzer Septal Occluder for anterior leaflet perforation during MitraClip implantation, achieving reduction of acute mitral regurgitation but complicated by severe hemolysis requiring surgery. More recently, successful closure of anterior mitral leaflet perforation using an Amplatzer Vascular Plug 4 was described, with excellent short-term outcomes.6 In our case, a 6-mm vascular plug was attempted but failed to seal the posterior tricuspid leaflet perforation, likely given a combination of factors: lack of sufficient surrounding tissue for anchoring, leaflet friability, and an increase in perforation size during device manipulation, which ultimately exceeded the size of the available plug. This experience highlights the technical challenges of plug closure in the tricuspid position, where leaflet fragility and complex geometry may limit device success. Moreover, delaying plug deployment to allow for tissue healing and fibrosis around the perforation edges may theoretically improve the chances of successful closure, although this strategy requires further investigation.

Saxon et al7 recently reported on 16 patients who underwent transcatheter tricuspid valve replacement (TTVR) with the Evoque valve after prior tricuspid TEER. Procedural technical success was achieved in 93.7% of cases, with significant improvements in TR severity and NYHA functional class at 30-day follow-up.7 Importantly, in cases where existing clips may interfere with valve expansion or leaflet mobility, electrosurgical leaflet modification using the CLEFT (clip liberation to facilitate TTVR) technique has been successfully employed to optimize leaflet mobility prior to valve replacement.7 In our patient, while the vascular plug bailout was unsuccessful, TTVR represents a potential future option should he develop refractory heart failure symptoms despite optimal medical therapy.

Conclusions

This case emphasizes the critical role of high-quality intraprocedural imaging and ongoing multidisciplinary heart team evaluation in recognizing complications early, guiding bailout strategies, and optimizing outcomes. Further data are needed to define the role, timing, and technical approach of percutaneous closure strategies for leaflet perforation in the tricuspid position.
