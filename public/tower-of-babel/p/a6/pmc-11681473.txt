Circulating Plasma Proteins in Aortic Stenosis: Associations With Severity, Myocardial Response, and Clinical Outcomes

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEchocardiographic indexes of aortic stenosis may not comprehensively reflect disease morbidity. Plasma proteomic profiling may add prognostic value in these patients.Methods and ResultsProximity extension assays (Olink) of 183 circulating cardiovascular and inflammatory proteins were performed in a prospective follow‐up study of 122 asymptomatic/minimally symptomatic patients (mean±SD age, 69.1±10.9 years; 61% men) with moderate to severe aortic stenosis and preserved left ventricular ejection fraction. Protein signatures of higher‐risk echocardiographic subgroups were determined. Associations of proteins with the primary composite outcome (heart failure hospitalization, progression to New York Heart Association class III‐IV, or all‐cause mortality) were evaluated using competing risk analyses, with aortic valve replacement being the competing risk. Network analysis unveiled mutually exclusive communities of proteins and echocardiographic parameters, connected only through NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide). Members of the tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily (TNFRSF1A, TNFRSF1B, and TNFRSF14), and trefoil factor‐3 were major hub proteins among the circulating biomarkers. Left ventricular global longitudinal strain >−15% was associated with higher levels of proteins, primarily of inflammation and immune regulation, whereas aortic valve area <1 cm2, E/e’ >15, and left atrial reservoir strain <20% were associated with higher levels of NT‐proBNP. Of 14 proteins associated with the primary end point, phospholipase‐C, C‐X‐C motif chemokine‐9, and interleukin‐10 receptor subunit β demonstrated the highest hazard ratios after adjusting for clinical factors (q<0.05).ConclusionsPlasma proteins involved in inflammation and immune regulation were differentially expressed in patients with aortic stenosis with reduced left ventricular global longitudinal strain, and associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Their incorporation into aortic stenosis risk stratification warrants further assessment.

Aortic stenosis (AS), the most prevalent valvular heart disease, is associated with increased morbidity and mortality. 1 Aortic valve replacement (AVR) is indicated for symptomatic AS, but symptom ascertainment in older subjects with multiple comorbidities is often challenging. Furthermore, standard echocardiographic indexes of AS severity may not closely reflect the degree of myocardial insult. 2 Circulating biomarkers reflecting underlying pathophysiological processes may provide additive risk stratification to currently applied indicators. 3 To date, B‐type natriuretic peptide remains the only biomarker endorsed by current guidelines to guide AVR in asymptomatic patients with severe AS. 4

Aside from clinical‐grade biomarkers, systems biology approaches have been applied in AS to unravel broader biomarker associations and discover future therapeutic targets. Proteomics, 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 metabolomics, 11 and transcriptomics 12 have yielded insights into AS pathophysiology, including lipid infiltration, oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, pathologic leaflet mineralization, and fibrocalcification. 1 Characterization of the protein signature in AS has been performed in both plasma 5 , 6 , 7 and aortic valve tissue following AVR, 8 , 9 , 10 but tissue sampling may be limited by variable AVR uptake 3 or compromised by preexisting severe valve calcification. By contrast, readily accessible plasma allows for more inclusive sampling and study of biological processes during earlier stages of AS. However, plasma proteomic studies specifically focusing on prognosis in AS are lacking in the literature. Earlier studies examined biobanked samples of patients with widely variable degrees of AS, or community‐based subjects who were later screened for incident AVR. 5 , 6 Furthermore, interactions between pathologic processes underpinning circulating biomarkers and echocardiographic indexes remain poorly understood.

Apart from aortic valve area (AVA), markers of myocardial decompensation, including left atrial (LA) and left ventricular (LV) strain, and LV diastolic function, have been associated with worse outcomes in AS. 13 , 14 , 15 It is now recognized that even patients with moderate AS are at increased risk of mortality. Measurement of the extent of cardiac damage in response to valvular dysfunction has been proposed to identify those at higher risk. 16 Because the optimal timing of AVR in asymptomatic patients with moderate or severe AS with preserved LV ejection fraction (LVEF) is unresolved, biomarkers predictive of adverse outcomes could have an important clinical role.

To this end, we aimed to identify plasma protein signatures as indexes of disease severity in AS, and determine their prognostic associations in a cohort of asymptomatic/minimally symptomatic patients with moderate to severe AS and preserved LVEF.

Methods

Study Cohort

Participants were from the prospective, multicenter, SAVIOUR (Singapore Assessment of Valvular Insufficiency and Obstruction Using Biomarkers) study. The inclusion and exclusion criteria have previously been described. 3 Briefly, patients from 5 major hospitals in Singapore between 2011 and 2018 who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were recruited: peak transaortic velocity (V max) ≥3.0 m/s or AVA by continuity equation ≤1.2 cm2, New York Heart Association (NYHA) class I/II, and LVEF ≥50% (Figure 1). A modified Charlson Comorbidity Index was determined as previously described. 3 All participants were at least 21 years of age and provided informed consent. Ethical approval was obtained from the local institutional review boards, and the study complied with the Declaration of Helsinki. The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author on reasonable request.

Study design.

Study design of 122 participants with available proteomic evaluation. AVA indicates aortic valve area; LVEF, left ventricular ejection fraction; NYHA, New York Heart Association; TTE, transthoracic echocardiography; and Vmax, peak transaortic velocity.

Transthoracic Echocardiography

Comprehensive transthoracic echocardiography was performed at recruitment and interpreted in accordance with American Society of Echocardiography guidelines. Measurements of LA reservoir strain (LASr) and LV global longitudinal strain (LV‐GLS) were as previously described. 13 Indexes of AS included V max, mean transvalvar pressure gradient, dimensionless index, and AVA by continuity equation (and indexed for body surface area). Severe AS was defined per clinical guidelines as V max ≥4 m/s or mean transvalvar pressure gradient ≥40 mm Hg. 4

Proteomic Evaluations

Plasma samples were collected at the time of recruitment and stored in EDTA tubes at −80°C. Using Olink proximity extension assay technology (Olink Proteomics, Uppsala, Sweden), 183 circulating proteins (Data S1. List of circulating biomarkers from Olink proteomic analyses) from 2 commercially available panels were quantified. Validation data and assay information of each panel are available at https://olink.com/products‐services/target/cardiometabolic‐panel/ (Olink Target 96 Cardiovascular III) and https://olink.com/products‐services/target/inflammation/ (Olink Target 96 Inflammation panel). Four subjects had quality control warnings in the latter panel, but these subjects were retained after determining that the overall proteome profile showed no sign of being outliers at the sample level based on principal component analysis. Projection diagrams for the first 4 principal components showed that those samples on the dominant principal components were positioned in the middle of the sample distributions.

Clinical Outcomes

The primary outcome was the composite of heart failure (HF) hospitalization, progression to NYHA class III to IV symptoms, or all‐cause mortality. Clinical events were reliably ascertained through research evaluations and electronic health records of public health care institutions. Follow‐up was censored at the date of first clinical event that constituted the primary outcome, date of surgery if AVR was performed, or date of last follow‐up for subjects without a clinical event or AVR.

Statistical Analysis

Clinical characteristics in the severe and moderate AS groups were reported as mean±SD, median (interquartile range), or percentages, and compared by the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Baseline characteristics of the identified sample (n=122) were compared with the original cohort (n=173) by the binomial test that accounts for the nature of data to ensure that the identified sample was reflective of the original cohort, and not subject to selection bias. Missing data were excluded from analyses. Pairwise correlations between circulating biomarkers and echocardiographic parameters were evaluated. Enrichment pathway analysis was performed to identify network clusters and protein connectivity. Protein signatures of “higher‐risk” echocardiographic subgroups, defined by previously reported prognostic cutoffs (AVA <1 cm2, LV‐GLS >–15%, 15 LASr <20%, 13 and E/e’ >15 14 ) were evaluated in linear regression analyses with these echocardiographic subgroups as independent variables to determine if significant differences in protein abundance were present, after adjusting for clinical confounders. Protein profiles were likewise compared across stages of AS based on the extent of cardiac damage, using a modification of the classification proposed by Vollema et al (stage 0–2 indicating no, LV or mitral valve/LA damage vs stage 3–4 indicating tricuspid valve/pulmonary vasculature or right ventricular damage). 16 Competing risks analyses of the associations of individual proteins and echocardiographic variables with the primary outcome, with AVR as the competing risk, were performed using cmprsk R package, both in univariable and multivariable models adjusting for Charlson Comorbidity Index, NYHA class (II versus I), and serum creatinine. P values were adjusted for multiple testing using q value, with variables showing a false discovery rate <0.05 considered statistically significant in both univariable and multivariable analyses. All 122 patients with proteomic sampling had complete follow‐up data. Statistical analyses were performed with R, 4.3.1. (Vienna, Austria).

Results

Among 173 patients enrolled in SAVIOUR, Olink (Cardiovascular III, Inflammation) assays were performed in a convenience sample of 122 patients (mean±SD age, 69.1±10.9 years; 61% men; 94% NYHA class I) (Figure 1). There were no differences in baseline characteristics between the sampled (n=122) and original cohort (n=173) (Table S1). Of the 122 patients with proteomic sampling, severe AS was present in 59 (48%) patients. There were no differences in clinical characteristics between patients with moderate versus severe AS. Those with severe AS had higher body surface area–indexed LA volume and LV mass, pulmonary arterial systolic pressure, and lower LASr (all P<0.05; Table 1). Higher‐risk subgroups AVA <1 cm2 consisted of 82 (67%) patients, LASr <20% consisted of 17 (14%) patients, LV‐GLS >–15% consisted of 28 (23%) patients, E/e’ >15 consisted of 49 (40%) patients, and 27 (22%) had stage 3 to 4 AS‐related cardiac damage. 16 The primary outcome occurred in 40 (33%) patients over a mean follow‐up of 1012 (interquartile range, 611–1717) days. AVR was performed in 39 patients (32%), for exertional symptoms in 22 cases, progressive AS in 10, syncope in 2, physician preference in 4, and for an unknown indication in 1 patient.

Network Analysis of Plasma Proteins and Cardiac Imaging Variables

Figure 2 shows the undirected graph representing the conditional dependence structure between circulating biomarkers and echocardiographic parameters, estimated by the graphical lasso estimator implemented in the huge R package. Network cluster analysis unveiled 2 independent clusters, representing mutually exclusive communities of circulating proteins and echocardiographic parameters, in which NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) was the only plasma biomarker connected to echocardiographic measures. All proteins assayed were densely connected to one another in an exclusive cluster. In this network, tumor necrosis factor receptor superfamily members TNFRSF1A, TNFRSF1B, and TNFRSF14, and trefoil factor 3 (TFF3) mediated the high connectivity within circulating biomarkers, displaying the highest node degree distributions, neighborhood connectivity, and stress centrality. Echocardiographic indexes of cardiac structure and function, and AS severity, were highly connected in a separate cluster but were unconnected to circulating proteins.

Protein Signature by Myocardial Response and AS Severity

Plasma proteins differentially abundant by higher‐risk AS subgroups at their designated echocardiographic cutoffs are shown in Table S2 (univariable analyses). To account for potential confounding by clinical factors on protein abundance, the analysis was adjusted for age, sex, body mass index, hypertension, diabetes, and serum creatinine. The proteins with significant intergroup differences in relative abundance (q<0.05) are shown in Table 2, and corresponding volcano plots and heat map of selected protein and echocardiographic indexes in subgroup comparisons are shown in Figure 3. LV‐GLS >–15% displayed associations with the highest number of differentially abundant biomarkers, predominantly involved in inflammation and immune regulation. Of these 10 differentially abundant proteins, MCP3 (monocyte chemotactic protein‐3), involved in inflammation, immune regulation, angiogenesis, and reparative effects, was the most upregulated (q<0.001). The protein signatures of LASr <20%, E/e’>15, and AVA<1 cm2 were similar, showing common upregulation of NT‐proBNP, a myocardial stress biomarker. The inflammatory marker interleukin‐5 was upregulated with AVA<1 cm2 and interleukin‐2 (downregulated) with LASr <20% (q<0.05). Categorization by extent of cardiac damage (stage 0–2 versus 3–4) 16 yielded no differentially expressed proteins (q>0.05).

Prognostic Performance for Clinical Outcomes

Univariable and multivariable associations of circulating protein biomarkers with the primary outcome following competing risk analyses are shown in Table S3. The volcano plot of biomarkers significantly associated with the primary outcome are provided in Figure 4. In multivariable models adjusting for comorbidity index, serum creatinine, and NYHA class (II versus I), 14 proteins were significantly associated with increased risk of the primary outcome (PLC [phospholipase C], IL10RB [IL10‐receptor subunit β], CXCL9 [C‐X‐C motif chemokine‐9], CD40 [cluster of differentiation‐40], FABP4 [fatty acid–binding protein‐4], CHI3L1 [chitinase‐3‐like protein‐1], CDCP1 [CUB domain‐containing protein‐1], HGF [hepatocyte growth factor], TNFRSF9, CXCL10, FAS [TNF receptor superfamily member‐6], β‐NGF [β‐nerve growth factor], CPA1 [carboxypeptidase A1], and MCP2; all q<0.05; Table 3). Of the prognostically significant biomarkers, only TNFRSF9, HGF, and CHI3L1 displayed differential abundance by myocardial response, all being specific for LV‐GLS>–15% (Table 2).

Discussion

Among asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic patients with moderate to severe AS and preserved LVEF, proteomic evaluation identified (1) independent clustering of circulating protein biomarkers and cardiac structural/functional parameters, (2) a central role of immune modulation and inflammation in mediating the connectivity of circulating biomarkers in AS, (3) distinct protein signatures for echocardiographic indexes of higher‐risk AS subgroups, and (4) novel prognostic circulating biomarkers that were not differentially abundant by echocardiographically defined AS severity (Figure 5). These findings provide insights into underlying biological pathways mediating adverse clinical outcomes in AS, which may be driven more by maladaptive myocardial responses rather than the severity of valvular obstruction. 2 , 3

Proteomic Evaluation in Risk Stratification of AS

Proteomics can screen large numbers of proteins simultaneously to identify novel candidate biomarkers, but its use in predicting clinical outcomes in AS has been limited. Ljungberg et al identified 5 unique proteins that were associated with eventual AVR in AS, 6 whereas Vidula et al identified interleukin‐6 and fibroblast growth factor‐23 as the most important markers of clinical outcomes across a wide spectrum of AS severity in patients identified with native calcific AS enrolled in a large biobank. 5 Notwithstanding these prior reports, the current study is unique in several respects. It is the first to examine the proteome in patients with established significant AS followed up prospectively, lending clinical relevance to the findings. Uniquely, competing risk analyses were used in this work to obviate confounding by AVR, which may be performed for nondefinitive indications, such as equivocal symptoms or physician preference, or conversely, not performed because of comorbidity burden or patient refusal despite clinical indications. 3 , 13 Additionally, comprehensive echocardiographic profiling permitted analyses of cardiac structural and functional relationships to circulating proteins, and the identification of protein signatures of “high‐risk” echocardiographic subgroups, not reported in other studies. 5 , 6

Major Hub Proteins in the Partial Correlation Network Observed in Patients With AS

Partial correlation analysis demonstrated absence of direct correlations between echocardiographic variables and circulating biomarkers, except NT‐proBNP. This remarkable observation of orthogonality suggests that biological processes in AS, including lipid infiltration, mineralization, and fibrocalcification of valve leaflets, oxidative stress, and chronic inflammation, 1 occur largely independently of measures of cardiac structure and function used to guide clinical management. TNFRSF1A, TNFRSF1B, TNFRSF14, and TFF3 were identified in the network analysis as protein hubs, indicating that plasma proteomic differences largely reflect variation in immune regulation and inflammation in these patients with AS. The 3 TNFRSF members modulate immune responses and are involved in a complex network of costimulatory and coinhibitory actions. 17 Interactions between immune cells, valvular endothelial cells, and interstitial cells lead to inflammation, lipid uptake, angiogenesis, fibrosis, and calcification. TFF3, involved in host defense and mucosal repair, is upregulated in inflammatory diseases and cancers and was associated with incident kidney disease and myocardial ischemia, reflecting a restitutive and protective mechanism. 18 TFF3 was similarly found to be the hub in biomarker network analysis in chronic HF, and predicted clinical outcomes in HF. 19 Although the exact molecular mechanisms underlying their role in AS remain uncertain, the identification of TNFRSF and TFF3 suggests systems‐wide immune regulation and inflammation represent the major biological processes modulating the risk in this condition.

Differential Abundance of Biomarkers in “High‐Risk” Subgroups

Despite the overall lack of connectivity between protein levels and echocardiographic variables, we observed disparity in biomarker profiles by indexes of myocardial response and AS severity. Significantly, biomarkers differentially abundant for LV‐GLS, a sensitive marker of LV systolic dysfunction, were largely related to inflammation and immune regulatory processes. In contrast, differentially expressed proteins were sparse in higher‐risk LASr, E/e’, and AVA subgroups, which all shared NT‐proBNP, involved in response to hemodynamic load, as the common upregulated protein. This suggests that valvular obstruction, LV diastolic dysfunction, and passive LA filling in AS are more tightly related to hemodynamic stress. Protein biomarkers associated with more impaired LV‐GLS reflected a greater diversity of biological processes, including atherosclerosis and angiogenesis. Notably, the chemokine MCP3, involved in monocyte homeostasis and mobilization to sites of inflammation, 20 was most differentially abundant with LV‐GLS>–15%. Its protective role in cardiac remodeling through mesenchymal stem cell recruitment and migration of circulating angiogenic cells 21 may suggest concurrent inflammatory and reparative mechanisms during myocardial decompensation, manifesting as LV strain reduction.

Association of Biomarkers With Clinical Outcomes

In competing risk analyses, 14 prognostically significant circulating biomarkers were identified. Notably, NT‐proBNP was not among them, losing its statistical significance following adjustment for multiple testing. Three proteins, PLC, CXCL9, and IL‐10RB, stood out as markers with the highest risk prediction of both designated clinical outcomes. PLC most strongly predicted clinical outcomes in AS, with a subdistribution hazard ratio of >2. PLC is a multifunctional enzyme involved in signal transduction and cardiac hypertrophy through various signaling pathways. 22 Although its role in AS has not been defined, PLC is upregulated after myocardial infarction in scar and viable myocardium in animal models, suggesting a role in scar remodeling and HF, 23 and is also involved in fibroblast growth factor‐23 signaling of cardiac fibroblast proliferation and migration. 24 Interestingly, Vidula et al reported fibroblast growth factor‐23 to be of 1 of the most important predictive biomarkers in their study, lending credence to the prognostic role of the PLC/fibroblast growth factor‐23 pathway in AS. 5 CXCL9 is involved in the chemotaxis of T cells to sites of inflammation, with elevated levels reported in hypertension, post–myocardial infarction cardiac fibrosis, and HF. 25 , 26 The prognostic role of CXCL9 in AS has not been studied, but its abundance in calcified regions of human aortic valves suggests an immunomodulatory role in activating proliferation and migration of cardiac fibroblasts, leading to cardiac fibrosis and T‐cell–mediated LV remodeling in response to pressure overload through the CXCR3‐LFA1‐ICAM1 axis. 25 , 26 , 27 IL‐10RB binds a number of the interleukin‐10 cytokine family, including interleukin‐10, interleukin‐22, and interleukin‐26, and is a subunit of the interferon‐λ receptor, which binds interleukin‐28 and interleukin‐29. 28 Interleukin‐10 has an anti‐inflammatory role and suppressive effect on adaptative and innate immunity, 28 reducing hypertrophy and fibrosis, reversing cardiac remodeling, and preventing the transition to HF in transverse aortic constriction‐induced pressure overload in animal models, 29 whereas interleukin‐22 prevented cardiomyocyte apoptosis and inhibited myocardial fibrosis. 30 The roles of interleukin‐26, interleukin‐28, and interleukin‐29 in cardiac fibrosis remain unknown, but the cardioprotective nature of interleukin‐10 and interleukin‐22 suggests that the increased risk associated with IL‐10RB may reflect compensatory upregulation in response to myocardial maladaptation to pressure overload.

Among the prognostically significant proteins, TNFRSF9, CHI3L1, and HGF were uniquely the only biomarkers predictive of the primary outcome as well as being differentially abundant by LV‐GLS >–15%. TNFRSF9 activates T lymphocytes in cellular immune responses and is involved in inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, atherosclerosis, and vasculitis. 31 It is upregulated in experimental myocardial infarction and associated with increased risk of major adverse cardiovascular events in patients with acute coronary syndrome. 32 CHI3L1, involved in inflammation, fibrosis, and extracellular remodeling, is upregulated in calcific AS and associated with increased mortality. 33 Although not associated with AS severity, myocardial expression of CHI3L1 was 6 times higher in experimental pressure overload in mice, consistent with its higher relative abundance with worse LV‐GLS in our study. 33 HGF, a cytokine expressed in cardiac smooth muscle cells and fibroblasts, promotes cell survival via antioxidant, antifibrotic, and angiogenic activity, 34 , 35 and higher levels have been associated with increased mortality in patients with acute HF, regardless of LVEF, and independently of NT‐proBNP. 36 In the setting of AS, HGF upregulation with worse LV‐GLS has also been reported in patients undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement, 37 likely reflecting the extent of cardiac injury and fibrosis. Higher HGF also predicted failure of reverse remodeling following transcatheter aortic valve replacement, 37 further underlining its prognostic potential.

Clinical Implications

Surgical timing in the often older, less mobile subject with AS is clinically challenging because of difficult symptom ascertainment. For patients deemed asymptomatic, current guidelines recommend AVR for LVEF <50%, V max ≥5 m/s, or serum B‐type natriuretic peptide >3 times normal. 4 Our study highlights the many other plasma proteins associated with key biological processes exhibited poor correlations with echocardiographic parameters, and they may represent a largely untapped biomarker resource for risk stratification in AS. Among these, 14 proteins independently predicted a higher risk of adverse outcomes, but not NT‐proBNP, even though the latter was associated with echocardiographically determined AS severity. 13 Our observations suggest that a valve‐ or even imaging‐centric approach to staging AS intervention may not be adequate. Further study to evaluate a combination of conventional imaging‐based indexes and identified candidate proteins in risk stratification of patients with AS is warranted.

Limitations

Biomarker findings from this study were from a single cohort with a limited sample size, and should be tested in larger independent cohorts to ascertain their reproducibility. As our patients were a selected subgroup with no or minimal symptoms and preserved LVEF, findings should not be generalized to patients with AS who are more symptomatic or have reduced LVEF. However, identification of additional prognostic biomarkers in these latter patients is moot as AVR would already be indicated. Inclusion of patients with mild AS may provide better proteomic discrimination of clinical outcomes, but this subgroup was by design, not recruited. Biomarkers studied were from preselected, commercially available panels, and findings may differ from other bioanalytical platforms with broader protein coverage. Proteomic analyses may be influenced by other systemic/inflammatory diseases, but the prevalence of previous cancer, connective tissue disease, and liver disease was low in this cohort and unlikely to influence the results significantly.

Conclusions

In patients with significant, albeit asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic, AS and preserved LVEF, plasma proteome profiles were related to key clinical outcomes. The plasma proteins assayed in this work were largely independent of cardiac structure and function and echocardiographic estimates of AS severity. Protein biomarkers involved in inflammation and immune regulation were differentially abundant in patients with AS with reduced LV‐GLS and associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Our findings suggest that current approaches to management of AS could benefit through incorporation of selected plasma proteins into the risk stratification matrix.
