Accelerating the Structure Exploration of Diverse Bi–Pt Nanoclusters via Physics‐Informed Machine Learning Potential and Particle Swarm Optimization
Authors: Raphaël Vangheluwe, Carine Clavaguéra, Minh‐Tue Truong, Dominik Domin, Huy Cong Pham, Mihai‐Cosmin Marinica, Nguyen‐Thi Van‐Oanh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Bimetallic Bi–Pt nanoclusters exhibit diverse structural motifs, including core‐shell, Janus, and mixed alloy configurations, due to the unique bonding characteristics between Bi and Pt atoms. Using density functional theory refinements from ChIMES physically machine‐learned potential and CALYPSO particle swarm optimization global searches, 34 Bi20‐Pt20 nanoclusters are systematically classified. The results reveal that Bi atoms predominantly occupy surface sites, driven by charge transfer effects. Cohesive energy trends alone prove insufficient for structure differentiation, necessitating a data‐driven approach employing principal component analysis and K‐means clustering. Furthermore, vibrational, electronic, and infrared spectral analyses provide additional insights into structure‐property relationships. The findings offer an original framework for the automated classification and analysis of bimetallic nanoclusters, enhancing the understanding of their stability and functional properties.

Introduction

Bimetallic nanoparticles (NPs), characterized by synergistic interactions between constituent metals, have emerged as critical materials in catalysis, energy systems, and biomedical applications[ 1 ] While traditional bimetallic systems often combine metals with identical or compatible crystal structures to enhance atomic compatibility and stability, a novel class of bimetallic NPs—combining bismuth (Bi, rhombohedral) with face‐centered cubic (fcc) noble metals such as gold (Au), platinum (Pt), and palladium (Pd)—has attracted considerable attention in catalysis[ 2 , 3 , 4 ] and biomedical[ 5 , 6 , 7 ] applications. Due to the inherent immiscibility of Bi with fcc metals, these systems frequently exhibit phase segregation or metastable alloy formations, challenging conventional synthesis paradigms while unlocking unprecedented catalytic and electronic properties. The structural diversity of Bi‐noble metal NPs, ranging from core‐shell and Janus configurations to surface alloys, directly influences their reactivity and stability, making their controlled synthesis a critical challenge with significant implications for application performance.[ 8 , 9 ]

One of the defining characteristics of Bi‐noble metal systems is their ability to mitigate performance degradation under reaction conditions. For example, Pt–Bi NPs synthesized via wet impregnation on activated carbon (AC)[ 10 ] under reducing conditions form a homogeneous mixed alloy. However, under oxidizing conditions, Bi oxidizes to Bi3+ and segregates to the surface, forming a protective core‐shell structure where a metallic Pt‐rich core is shielded by a Bi‐enriched oxide shell. This dynamic structural adaptability—transitioning between mixed alloy and core‐shell configurations—explains the enhanced stability of Pt–Bi/AC compared to monometallic Pt/AC, as Bi sacrificially prevents Pt oxidation and mitigates coke formation. This mechanism significantly enhances catalyst durability in methane‐mediated guaiacol deoxygenation, a process critical for biofuel upgrading. Similarly, PtBi/C catalysts synthesized using a water‐in‐oil microemulsion method[ 11 ] exhibit crystalline Pt cores decorated with surface Bi structures, as confirmed by molecular dynamics (MD) and chemical physical analysis. This configuration not only prevents Pt degradation but also optimizes glycerol electrooxidation, a reaction vital for fuel cell applications. Recent advances have focused on shape‐controlled architectures[ 12 ] enabled by colloidal synthesis. Trace Bi (1–13% surface coverage) stabilizes concave Au and Pd NPs, which adopt morphologies such as tetrahedra and stella octangula. These high‐index facets and strained surfaces significantly enhance alcohol oxidation activity. The synthesis of Pt–Bi and Pd–Bi bimetallic NPs through solvothermal and hydrothermal approaches[ 13 ] offers promising routes for developing high‐performance electrocatalysts. Pt‐rich Pt–Bi structures with irregularly shaped thin morphologies are more suitable for the hydrogen evolution reaction, whereas cubic‐like Pt–Bi NPs prove to be better catalysts for the oxygen reduction reaction.

Beyond structural stabilization, Bi introduces electronic effects by lowering Pt's d‐band center, boosting catalytic efficiency in formic acid oxidation compared to monometallic Pt NPs.,[ 14 ] and enhancing resistance to CO poisoning through preferential Bi surface segregation—an effect arising from both geometric blocking of adsorption sites and electronic modification of Pt surface atoms.[ 14 , 15 ] Additionally, Bi's low cost relative to noble metals enables cost‐effective designs, reducing Pt/Pd usage without compromising activity.[ 16 ]

Despite these promising applications, the structural and electronic origins of their enhanced performance remain poorly understood. Traditional computational methods face significant challenges: semi‐empirical approaches like GFN2‐xTB yield nonphysical interatomic distances due to inadequate parametrization for heavy metallic elements,[ 17 ] while ab initio and density functional theory (DFT) calculations become prohibitively expensive for nanoscale systems with complex electronic interactions. This gap hinders detailed exploration of structural motifs (core‐shell, Janus, random alloys) and their dynamic evolution under operational conditions, limiting rational design of next‐generation catalysts.

To address these limitations, this work employs machine‐learned interatomic potentials (ML‐IAPs) as a scalable alternative to DFT, offering near‐DFT accuracy at reduced computational cost. Specifically, the Chebyshev Interaction Model for Efficient Simulation (ChIMES)[ 18 , 19 ] a physically informed ML potential combining multibody cluster expansion with Chebyshev polynomials—is trained via an active learning framework. This approach iteratively refines training data by sampling configurations from MD simulations and structural optimizations, ensuring robust coverage of configurational space, including metastable states and transition pathways. Integrated with the CALYPSO global optimization algorithm,[ 20 ] which employs particle swarm optimization (PSO) to efficiently navigate high‐dimensional energy landscapes, the ChIMES‐CALYPSO workflow enables systematic exploration of low‐energy Bi–Pt NP configurations. Subsequent DFT refinements validate these structures, providing a reliable foundation for catalytic studies, ligand‐NP interaction analyses for advanced radiotherapy applicaitions,[ 21 ] and assessments of NP thermal stability (e.g., melting points for reactor melt wire applications[ 22 ]). The characterization through coordinated structural, electronic, and vibrational descriptors reveals how core‐shell, Janus, and mixed alloy morphologies balance thermodynamic stability with interfacial charge transfer.

Methodology

ChIMES Physically Informed Machine Learning Potential

The ChIMES method is a physics‐informed ML‐IAP designed to simulate covalent bonding, reactive chemistry, and phase transformations in materials under extreme conditions. Unlike conventional neural network ML‐IAPs such as Behler–Parrinello Neural Network,[ 23 , 24 ] several modern ML‐IAPs rely on explicit n‐body formulations, including permutationally invariant polynomial[ 25 ] (PiP) models, fast learning of atomistic rare events,[ 26 ] and tabulated Gaussian Approximation Potentials.[ 27 ] These approaches, like ChIMES, decompose the total energy into n‐body contributions—typically 2‐body, 3‐body, or 4‐body terms—and use a specific functional form to represent each interaction (e.g., polynomials, kernels, or tabulated functions). What distinguishes ChIMES is its use of Chebyshev polynomials applied to transformed interatomic distances within fully connected atomic clusters, enabling fine control over short‐ and medium‐range interactions and ensuring a physically interpretable model. By contrast, the atomic cluster expansion[ 28 , 29 ] (ACE) follows a systematic basis‐set expansion approach. While ACE can be used to represent arbitrary body‐order interactions in a formally complete way, its formulation differs significantly from the cluster‐centric, physically motivated energy decomposition employed in ChIMES. Overall, ChIMES combines the interpretability and transferability of physically informed models with the flexibility of machine learning, offering robust performance even outside the training data domain.

The ChIMES total energy can be written as (1) EChIMES=∑iNEi+∑j>iNEij+∑k>j>iNEijk+∑l>k>j>iNEijkl

For example, the two‐body energy term is expressed as (2) Eij=fp(rij)+fcut(rij)∑α=1Θ2BcαeiejTα(sijeiej) where T α denotes Chebyshev polynomials of the first kind (defined on [−1,1]), modulated by permutationally invariant coefficients cαeiej for element pairs e i e j . The transformed distance S ij maps r ij to the [−1,1] interval via a Morse‐like function, prioritizing short‐range interactions for accuracy. A Tersoff‐style smooth cutoff f cut(r ij) stabilizes long‐range interactions, while a penalty function f p(r ij) —activated below a pair‐specific inner cutoff —prevents unphysical close contacts. In this study, the ChIMES cutoff distances are 8/5/4 Å for 2B/3B/4B interactions, respectively. The model employs polynomial orders of 12/7/3 for 2B/3B/4B interactions, balancing computational efficiency and fidelity.

Originally developed for carbon systems under extreme conditions, ChIMES accurately predicted phase transitions such as shock‐driven graphite‐to‐diamond conversion via hexagonal intermediates and melting points, aligning with experiments. It models nonequilibrium processes like nanodiamond‐to‐nanoonion transformations, critical for detonation synthesis studies. Beyond carbon, ChIMES was extended to molecular systems (e.g., liquid water, capturing hydrogen‐bond dynamics).[ 30 ] Notably, this work marked the first application of ChIMES to bimetallic NPs (e.g., Pt–Bi), demonstrating its adaptability to metallic bonding and alloyed systems. This expansion underscored ChIMES’ versatility in addressing diverse challenges while retaining its core strengths: physics‐driven design, explicit many‐body interactions, and transferability across phases and chemistries.

Training Set Generation and Active Learning

The training dataset was generated using DFT calculations performed with the ORCA 6 package,[ 31 ] employing the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof functional with D3BJ dispersion correction and the LANL2DZ basis‐set paired with the HayWadt effective core potential to account for relativistic effects and to balance accuracy and computational cost. The resolution‐of‐identity approximation for both Coulomb integrals and Hartree–Fock Exchange integrals was employed for computational efficiency. Due to DFT's steep scaling, NP models were restricted to diameters below 1 nm, yielding structures with 43–59 atoms. Initial configurations included three morphologies: core‐shell NPs with Bi cores and Pt shells were constructed from the mono‐element rhombohedral and fcc crystal structures and a mixed NiAs‐type crystal due to the isoelectronic with the Bi–Pt system.[ 32 ] Spherical NPs were constructed using the NaNoCrystal interface.[ 33 ] These initial structures were optimized using DFT, and 25 configurations were included in the training set. 62 perturbed conformations (atomic displacements of 0.2–0.6 Å) were further added to enhance sampling.

Active learning was employed to iteratively refine the ChIMES model. MD simulations were conducted in large‐scale atomic/molecular massively parallel simulator[ 34 ] using a 0.1 fs timestep and the Nosé–Hoover thermostat at temperatures spanning 150–1000 K. Trajectories were extended from 100 to 500 ps as the model improved. Configurations unseen in the training data (yet identified as acceptable while under force thresholds) were labeled with DFT and incorporated into the dataset, expanding it to 125 configurations.

PSO

The trained and fitted model was leveraged for its fast optimization time to sample large amounts of the Bi:Pt NPs configurations. To do so, the ML‐IAP was paired with a global optimization algorithm, namely particle swarm optimization[ 35 ] thanks to the Crystal structure AnaLYsis by PSO (CALYPSO) program.[ 20 ]

The particle swarm optimization method naturally led to our goal of exploring the configurational space of the system, while removing the biases linked to the choice of an initial structure. CALYPSO generates random geometries, although with symmetry constraints, for a given chemical composition. Each local geometry was evaluated, optimized, and iteratively improved over the PSO generations. In our case, the local structural optimization was handled via the ChiMES potential and allowed us to drastically reduce the computational cost over a traditional DFT method. In our case, we applied the global optimization algorithm to the configurational space of 40‐atom NPs, Bi20Pt20, leading us to a total of 34 optimized structures. These structures were obtained over 20 generations, for an overall evaluation of 700 new structures.

Computational Details

As for the training set, DFT calculations were performed to optimize geometries and analyze electronic properties of 40‐atom Bi20Pt20 nanoclusters. Geometry optimizations utilized the KDIIS algorithm with tight convergence criteria (energy: 1 × 10−6 Ha, gradient: 3 × 10−4 Ha Å−1) and a Grid4 integration grid. Vibrational frequencies were computed via the NumForce module to confirm local minima. IR spectra were generated from dipole derivatives, while an atomic dipole moment corrected Hirshfeld charge analysis, selected based on its validated performance in our prior studies of gold nanoclusters,[ 36 ] was conducted using Multiwfn 3.8 version.[ 37 ] This method provided robust charge partitioning in metallic systems due to its basis‐set insensitivity and alignment with experimental charge transfer trends.[ 38 ] Electronic DOS calculations were also performed with the Multiwfn code.

Multivariate Classification Methodology and Datasets

In the literature, multivariate analysis techniques, specifically PCA and linear discriminant analysis, were recently been used to analyze the morphologies of bimetallic NPs.[ 39 ] PCA and K‐means clustering[ 40 ] were implemented here to classify the structural motifs of 34 optimized Bi20Pt20 nanoclusters (40 atoms, 1:1 ratio). IR spectra were preprocessed using Modified Akima spline interpolation (SciPy version 1.15.2) to standardize frequency ranges (19.76–189.32 cm−1), avoiding artifacts from cubic spline oscillations. For electronic DOS and VDOS spectra, kernel density estimation with Gaussian kernels (bandwidth = 0.2) generated smooth profiles. PCA was applied independently to IR, DOS, and VDOS datasets using scikit‐learn (version 1.6.1), retaining the first two PCs for each spectral type to capture dominant variations while reducing dimensionality. These spectral PCs were then combined with structural and electronic descriptors to construct a unified dataset for systematic analysis.

The final dataset comprises 26 standardized features spanning three key domains to decode structure‐property relationships in sub‐nm Bi–Pt nanoclusters: 1) Spectroscopic Features: First two PCs of IR spectra (IR_PC1, IR_PC2), VDOS (VDOS_PC1, VDOS_PC2), and DOS (DOS_PC1, DOS_PC2), capturing 67.35%, 48.64%, and 46.03% cumulative variance, respectively; 2) Structural Coordination and Distances: Bi–Bi, Pt–Pt, and Bi–Pt coordination numbers (CN) (BiBi_coord, PtPt_coord, BiPt_coord) and radial distribution‐derived first‐peak distance extrema (BiBi_rmin/rmax, PtPt_rmin/rmax, BiPt_rmin/rmax), used as boundaries to integrate and find each CN; 3) Geometric Symmetry and Shape Descriptors: Total CN, radius of gyration (Rgyr), asphericity, acylindricity, anisotropy (all three calculated from the principal moments of the gyration tensor[ 41 , 42 ]), and principal moments of the inertia tensor (Ix, Iy, Iz); 4) Electronic Properties: Cohesion energy (CohE, i.e., the stabilization brought by the structure), dipole moment (Dip), and frontier orbital metrics including highest occupied molecular orbital (HOMO), lowest unoccupied molecular orbital (LUMO) energies, and band gap (BG).

K‐means clustering (scikit‐learn, Lloyd's algorithm[ 43 ]) was performed on standardized PCA scores, with the optimal cluster count determined via the elbow method (Euclidean inertia). Hierarchical clustering (Wasserstein distance, average linkage) was tested but discarded due to poor interpretability. Data preprocessing and model parameters are detailed in the SI.

Results and Discussion

ChIMES Model Training and Validation

Initial ChIMES models relied solely on force matching, but incorporating energy terms during training, which significantly enhanced predictive accuracy, reducing the mean absolute error by 50% and the root‐mean‐square error (RMSE) by 66%. The final model, trained on 125 configurations, achieved stable MD simulations spanning 1 ns and captured a wider force range (Figure S1, Supporting Information), reflecting improved sampling of the potential energy surface. When trained jointly on forces and energies, the model achieved RMSE values of 1.99 eV Å−1 (forces) and 0.059 eV/atom (energies), as shown in Figure S1, Supporting Information. Posttraining, ChIMES‐enabled geometry optimizations were performed to validate against DFT references. From the radial distribution function (RDF) analysis, the Bi–Pt nearest‐neighbor distance described by ChIMES is 2.59 ± 0.07 Å, which is consistent, albeit a small underestimation, with regard to the DFT predictions (2.8 Å). This fidelity enables reliable exploration of bimetallic NP structures, even for configurations absent from the training data.

Structure Labeling of Bi–Pt Nanoclusters

Bimetallic nanoclusters often exhibit disordered and flexible geometric structures due to insufficient metal–metal bonding.[ 44 ] However, we observe specific structural motifs, such as core‐shell and Janus, with the most dominant being mixed alloy structures (see Figure 2 ). The structural motifs of the 34 optimized Bi–Pt nanoclusters, refined via DFT from ChIMES‐CALYPSO global searches, were first automatically classified using CN and RDF) analysis. For each configuration, the average CNs for Bi–Bi, Pt–Pt, and Bi–Pt pairs were normalized to the total coordination. The Core‐shell structures are defined by dominant Bi–Pt coordination (>60% of total CN) and a sharp Bi–Pt RDF peak at 2.8 Å, with minimal Bi–Bi bonding (CN < 2). Janus configurations are highlighted by segregated bonding environments with high Bi–Bi CN, RDF peak at 3.27 Å and/or high Pt–Pt CN, RDF peak at 2.85 Å, alongside limited Bi–Pt coordination (CN < 3). Finally, mixed alloys present moderate Bi–Pt CN (30–50%) and broadened RDF peaks, reflecting disordered arrangements.

40‐atom DFT optimized CALYPSO structures, relative energy in eV/atom, and manual classification of the morphologies. Bi atoms in violet, Pt atoms in gray.

Due to the small size of the nanoclusters, these CN/RDF classifications achieved only 87% agreement with manual labels, which further resolved subclasses (mixed‐Janus, Janus‐mixed, mixed‐core‐shell), highlighting subtle atomic segregation trends. These manually categorized structures, sorted by cohesive energy in descending order, are displayed in Figure 2. Structural analysis reveals that Bi atoms are predominantly located on the surface of the nanoclusters. This surface segregation of Bi can be attributed to charge transfer from Bi (+) to Pt (‐) (0.148 ± 0.016 a.u), which stabilizes Bi at the outermost sites. Furthermore, cohesive energy alone proved insufficient for resolving structural diversity, as overlapping energy ranges spanned multiple classes. To decode these relationships, we extracted 26 variables (CNs, radial distribution extrema, shape descriptors, electronic and vibrational properties) and employed PCA and K‐means clustering to reduce dimensionality and identify latent structural families from different datasets.

DOS, IR, and VDOS Spectral Datasets

The application of PCA and K‐means clustering to IR, DOS, and VDOS spectral datasets yielded distinct structural classifications for the 34 Bi20Pt20 nanoclusters. PCA reduced the dimensionality of the IR spectra (19.76–189.32 cm−1 frequency range) to three (PCs, capturing 77% of the cumulative variance, while DOS and VDOS datasets also required three PCs each to explain only 60% of their variance. The first two PCs of each spectral type dominated morphological discrimination, with IR‐PC1 (58% variance) correlating strongly with Bi–Pt vibrational modes at 114–170 cm−1. We also discovered that the outlier structure (Cluster 3, ID 1, singleton) displays anomalous IR activity across the entire frequency range (see Figure 3 ). This structure is the fourth most unstable and exhibits a Janus morphology. It has the largest dipole moment (7.38 D) compared to the other structures, likely due to interfacial charge transfer inherent to the Janus configuration.

IR intensity of K‐means clusters with vertical markings of local peak.

This large dipole moment is representative of the Janus structures, which present as a group a higher average than the other classifications (4.56 ± 2.64 D versus 2.45 ± 1.14 D). On the other hand, core‐shell and mixed configurations are not easily distinguished apart using the dipole moment, with very similar values for each subgroup, 2.17 ± 1.28 and 1.92 ± 0.73 D, respectively.

The DOS‐PC1 contains only 29.79% variance, reflecting electronic delocalization near the Fermi level, especially in the region near the LUMO energies. K‐means clustering of the DOS‐PCA transformed data over the first three PCs revealed four distinct groups (see Figure 4 ). Among these, cluster 1 (containing six structures, see Figure 2) stands out from the other groups. However, using only DOS‐PC1 data, the structures can be separated into two groups. The energy range (−21.77–5.44 eV) remains the same for all structures. Analysis of the HOMO‐LUMO gap (also referred to as the BG) further distinguishes two groups with significantly different BG values, with the following median and median absolute deviation (0.23 ± 0.05 eV) and (2.92 ± 0.04 eV). The HOMO energies are quite similar across all clusters, while differences primarily arise from variations in LUMO energy values. Unfortunately, this split in the LUMO values does not correspond to differences in the morphologies. More detailed PCA and K‐means analysis, along with an alternative hierarchical clustering, for IR, VDOS, and DOS can be found in the SI.

Although IR, VDOS, and electronic DOS spectra offer valuable insights into the vibrational and electronic properties of nanoclusters, these spectral signatures alone are not sufficient to reliably distinguish between different structural motifs such as core‐shell, Janus, and mixed configurations. As demonstrated in our analysis, overlapping features and spectral similarities—particularly in disordered structures—limit the discriminatory power of IR, VDOS, and DOS. For instance, similar low‐frequency modes or delocalized electronic states can be found across morphologies, leading to ambiguity. Consequently, a multidimensional approach combining spectral data with structural, geometric, and electronic descriptors is essential to accurately classify and understand the subtle atomic arrangements characteristic of each motif (Figure 5 ).

26‐Variable Dataset

The 26‐variable dataset, integrating spectral (IR, DOS, VDOS PCs), structural (CNs, radial distribution extrema), geometric (radius of gyration, asphericity), and electronic (cohesive energy, dipole moment) descriptors, revealed significant correlations among variables. Correlation analysis (see Figure S5, Supporting Information) identified redundancy in features with coefficients >0.7, notably between Bi–Bi CN (BiBi_coord) and Bi–Bi radial distribution minima (BiBi_rmin), as well as between Pt–Pt coordination (PtPt_coord) and Pt–Pt radial extrema (PtPt_rmin). Geometric descriptors such as radius of gyration (Rgyr) exhibited moderate correlations with asphericity and acylindricity, reflecting shared shape‐dependent information. PCs of IR (IR_PC1, IR_PC2) showed strong intraspectral correlations due to overlapping vibrational modes, while DOS and VDOS PCs exhibited weaker intercorrelations. PCA reduced dimensionality while preserving variance, with the first few PCs capturing the majority of variance (e.g., 67.35% for IR, 48.64% for DOS, 58.58 % for VDOS). The correlation circle (see SI Figure S6, Supporting Information) confirmed strong variable interdependencies, highlighting redundancy. Further study of structural outliers for the 26 variables is available in the Figure S6 and S7, Supporting Information.

K‐means clustering on the PCA‐reduced 26‐variable dataset, using the elbow method to select four clusters, achieved 52.94% overall agreement with manual labels across three main structural classes: core‐shell (66.67% accuracy), Janus (100%), and mixed alloys. Mixed configurations exhibited lower accuracy (44.44%), attributed to overlapping electronic and vibrational signatures in disordered arrangements.

The confusion matrix (see Figure S9, Supporting Information) highlighted challenges in distinguishing mixed subclasses due to subtle atomic segregation patterns, underscoring the necessity of multivariate descriptors for resolving structural heterogeneity. While clustering on PCA‐reduced dimensions showed meaningful grouping, feature reduction (e.g., to 13 variables) further improved clustering quality by eliminating redundancies, though removing strongly correlated variables alone had minimal impact.

Reduced 13‐Variable Dataset

To refine the 26‐variables dataset, a systematic feature selection process was applied. The selection method consisted of the following steps: 1) Correlation Analysis: A correlogram was computed to examine pairwise correlations between variables. Features with strong linear dependencies (correlation coefficient >0.7) were considered redundant; 2) PC Contributions: The PCA correlation circle was used to identify variables contributing similarly to the PCs. Features positioned close to each other in the loading plot were likely redundant; 3) Variance Thresholding: Features with very low variance across all samples were removed, as they provided minimal discriminatory power; 4) Recursive Feature Elimination: A backward elimination approach was applied, where one variable was removed at a time, and the clustering performance (measured by confusion matrix) was evaluated after each step. The process continued until the optimal balance between dimensionality and clustering accuracy was achieved; 5) Domain Knowledge Consideration: Finally, features known to be physically relevant for the classification of materials properties were prioritized over purely statistically significant ones.

Through this iterative approach, the final subset of 13 features was selected, ensuring that essential information was retained while improving clustering performance. The final selected features were:

Spectral signatures: PCs of IR, DOS, and VDOS spectra (IR_PC1, IR_PC2, DOS_PC1, DOS_PC2, VDOS_PC1, VDOS_PC2), preserving dominant variations in vibrational and electronic profiles.

Atomic coordination: CN (BiBi_coord, PtPt_coord) and nearest‐neighbor distances (BiPt_rmin, BiBi_rmin, PtPt_rmin), derived from RDFs, to quantify atomic segregation patterns.

Shape and symmetry: The radius of gyration (Rgyr) provides additional geometric insight into nanocluster compactness and spatial distribution, complementing coordination‐based descriptors.

K‐means clustering on the combined PCA‐transformed features segregated the nanoclusters into distinct groups (Figure S10, Supporting Information), exhibiting strong alignment with manual structural labels.

Cluster 1 (3 structures) consists exclusively of core‐shell configurations, characterized by cohesive electronic profiles (narrow DOS peaks) and symmetric Pt–Pt coordination (RDF first‐peak distance: 2.75 Å). Cluster 2 (4 structures) comprises the Janus and Janus‐mixed structures, distinguished by interfacial charge transfer (dipole moment >5 D). Cluster 0 and Cluster 4 (24 structures) are broadly composed of the mixed alloys, with Cluster 0 containing the sole mixed‐Janus configuration (broad VDOS peaks <100 cm−1, Bi–Pt CN: 7.5) and Cluster 4 the lone mixed‐core‐shell variant. Clusters 3 and 5 (3 structures) contain the leftovers of disordered mixed alloys, highlighting residual atomic heterogeneity. Conclusions and Perspectives

In this study, we established a robust computational framework combining the ChIMES machine‐learned potential with CALYPSO global optimization and multivariate analysis to systematically explore and classify structural motifs in 34 Bi20Pt20 nanoclusters. Key findings reveal that Bi atoms preferentially occupy surface sites due to charge transfer from Bi to Pt, while cohesive energy alone proved insufficient for structural discrimination, necessitating a multidimensional approach. A comprehensive 26‐variable dataset was collected, spanning spectral, structural, geometric, and electronic descriptors—including CN, radial distribution extrema, and the first two PCs of IR, DOS, and VDOS spectra. This dataset achieved 52.94% agreement between automated clustering and manual classification, resolving core–shell, Janus, mixed‐alloy, mixed‐Janus, and mixed‐core‐shell configurations. PCA of vibrational (IR, VDOS) and electronic (DOS) spectra identified critical features, such as Bi–Pt vibrational modes (114–170 cm−1), low‐frequency modes (<100 cm−1), and electronic delocalization near the Fermi level, which distinguish structural families. Through systematic feature selection (correlation analysis, variance thresholding, and domain knowledge), the 26‐variable dataset was refined to 13 key descriptors, retaining essential geometric, spectral, and electronic signatures while enhancing clustering interpretability. This reduced dataset validated the synergy between data‐driven methods and domain knowledge, resolving subtle atomic segregation trends. Notably, the framework uncovered metastable interfacial configurations, exemplified by outlier structures with anomalous IR spectral signatures. This methodology not only advances the understanding of atomic‐scale segregation in bimetallic systems but also provides a scalable tool for high‐throughput screening of alloy catalysts. Future work will extend this approach to larger NPs and diverse bi‐ and trimetallic compositions, enhancing predictive design of cost‐effective, high‐performance materials for sustainable energy applications.
