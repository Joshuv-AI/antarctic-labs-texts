Mode of action guided metagenomic natural product discovery reveals convergent evolution of a ClpP-targeting motif
Authors: Jingbo Kan, Kaylyn Spotton, Adrian Morales-Amador, Yozen Hernandez, Ján Burian, Cecilia Panfil, Melinda A. Ternei, Robert E. Boer, Abir Bhattacharjee, Sean F. Brady
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The discovery of natural products with specific modes of action from metagenomes remains challenging. Here, we present resistance-CONKAT-seq, a pipeline that links biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) to self-resistance genes, enabling identification of metabolites with desired molecular targets. Using clpP-directed resistance-CONKAT-seq, we identify the calprotamides, which activate native ClpP and enhance its activity. Cryo-EM and bioinformatic analyses reveal that the calprotamides’ medium-chain N-acylphenylalanine substructure is a convergently evolved ClpP-targeting motif and identify additional BGCs predicted to encode this moiety, including some with co-localized clp genes. The synthesis of structures bioinformatically inspired by two such clp-linked BGCs, desmethyl jomthonic acid C and tuscamide, reveals that both enhance ClpP activity. Extending our bioinformatically guided synthesis study to additional BGCs lacking nearby clp genes shows that ClpP activity enhancement correlated with antibacterial activity, with the strongest enhancers exhibiting narrow-spectrum antibiotic activity. These findings establish N-acylphenylalanine as a previously unrecognized but common natural motif for targeting ClpP, which should help guide the discovery of both natural and synthetic ClpP modulators for antibiotic and anticancer development. Resistance-CONKAT-seq offers a scalable method for exploring biosynthetic dark matter for metabolites with desired modes of action.

Introduction

The majority of bacteria present in the global microbiome are uncultured and remain an untapped reservoir of bioactive small molecules1,2. Heterologous expression of biosynthetic gene clusters (BGCs) cloned using DNA extracted directly from environmental samples (metagenomic DNA) provides a means to access this chemical diversity. Although traditional culture-based natural product discovery programs have heavily relied on random screening to identify bioactive small molecules with clinically promising modes of action (MOAs), the large-scale heterologous expression and random screening of metagenomic BGCs are labor intensive. A targeted search for natural products with specific, high-value MOAs is much more appealing for metagenomic discovery3–6. It is now well established that, in some instances, a natural product BGC encodes a mutated copy of its molecular target that evades antibiotic binding7–9. This resistance mechanism is known as self-resistance. As a result, focusing discovery efforts on BGCs that co-localize with genes that encode molecular targets of interest presents a strategy for prioritizing clusters that produce natural products with a desired MOA10–12. This approach has been used successfully with cultured isolates due to the ease with which individual bacteria genomes can be sequenced, assembled and bioinformatically interrogated13–15. Unfortunately, all three of these steps remain a challenge with metagenomes, preventing a similar target-directed search for natural products produced by the uncultured bacterial majority. Even when using recently developed long-read sequencing methods that can generate fully assembled, large contiguous metagenomic sequences16, the depth of sequencing is insufficient to capture the full diversity of soil metagenomes, making direct identification of rare BGCs difficult.

To overcome this limitation, we previously developed a targeted sequencing approach called CONKAT-seq (co-occurrence network analysis of targeted sequences). This workflow enables the identification of rare physically clustered biosynthetic genes (i.e. BGCs) from complex (meta)genomic samples17,18. CONKAT-seq leverages PCR amplification of conserved biosynthetic domains to detect co-occurrence patterns among genes across arrayed (meta)genomic libraries. The ability to sequence amplicons at high depth makes this method both powerful and scalable, allowing the discovery of BGCs from low-abundance microbial populations that constitute the majority of soil biodiversity. This approach has proven effective for systematically uncovering natural product BGCs, both from environmental metagenomes and libraries of cultured bacterial genomes. The PCR amplicon sequencing data used by CONKAT-seq offers an unmatched scale of metagenomic exploration, which we hypothesized would allow for the identification of rare resistance gene-associated BGCs. Here, we report the development of resistance-CONKAT-seq (Fig. 1a), which links potential self-resistance genes encoding resistant variants of molecular targets with biosynthetic gene amplicon networks. By tracking their co-localization, this approach enables the identification of metagenomic BGCs that may encode natural products with specific MOAs. This method can be applied to any conserved essential gene of interest, but as a test case we searched for clpP-associated BGCs in soil metagenomic libraries. ClpP is interesting because it is a validated yet underexplored antibiotic target, and although a growing number of ClpP-active molecules have been identified in recent years, no ClpP-targeting drug has reached clinical use. ClpP is a highly conserved serine protease that degrades damaged proteins and is important for maintaining a healthy cell, making it a promising target for both antibacterial and cancer therapies19–21. The ClpP protease complex comprises two heptameric rings that stack to form a functional tetradecameric proteolytic core, which associates with a hexameric ATP-dependent unfoldase (e.g. ClpX, ClpC). Unfoldases recognize, unfold, and translocate protein substrates into the ClpP catalytic chamber for degradation. In this work, we use ClpP-directed resistance-CONKAT-seq and identify the calprotamide antibiotics that both activate native Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) ClpP and enhance its activity once activated. We further show that the medium-chain (C6-C8) N-acylphenylalanine substructure found in the calprotamides is a common, yet overlooked, natural motif for targeting ClpP. This N-acylphenylalanine substructure represents a case of convergent evolution, in which a chemical motif has independently evolved across distinct biosynthetic pathways to target ClpP. Our identification of this evolutionary convergence establishes a molecular handle for discovering ClpP-targeting compounds. Guided by this insight, we synthesize additional ClpP-active molecules, bioinformatically predicted from uncharacterized BGCs, some of which exhibit antibiotic activity. While here we target ClpP, by changing resistance gene-specific degenerate primers, resistance-CONKAT-seq can be tuned to any potential molecular target thereby providing a generalized approach for the discovery of natural products with desired MOAs from complex metagenomes.

Results

Screen for clpP genes in arrayed metagenomic libraries

For this study, we used metagenomic DNA to develop a pair of degenerate primers that amplified diverse clpP genes (Supplementary discussion, Supplementary Fig. 1, Supplementary Table 1). Using this primer set, we screened an archived soil metagenomic DNA cosmid library containing >2 ×107 clones for clpP co-localized BGCs. This library was pre-arrayed in 384-well plates with ~25,000 clones per well (Supplementary Table 2)25. We used well-specific barcoded clpP degenerate primers to amplify clpP genes from each library well. To facilitate the identification of nonribosomal peptide synthetase (NRPS) and polyketide synthase (PKS) BGCs, adenylation (A-) and ketosynthase (KS-) domain sequences were previously amplified from each library sub pool using domain-specific degenerate primers17. For resistance-CONKAT-seq, we integrated this data into the CONKAT-seq algorithm to track co-localization of clpP genes with A- and KS- domain networks (Fig. 1a). Predicted resistance-CONKAT-seq networks were verified by PCR using primers targeting the clpP node and one biosynthetic node, confirming that the co-occurrence of clpP and biosynthetic gene amplicons matched the expected well patterns predicted by resistance-CONKAT-seq. This led to our identification of a verified network containing a clpP node and two KS-domain nodes (Fig. 1b). Cosmids associated with this network were recovered from the archived library, sequenced and annotated. The BGC, which we have called the CPA BGC (Supplementary Fig. 2), was recovered on two overlapping cosmids: AZ52-w347 and AZ52-w337 (Fig. 1c). It did not closely resemble a BGC associated with a known natural product or any uncharacterized BGCs found in publicly available databases.

To show the generality of resistance-CONKAT-seq, we expanded the screening by including two additional self-resistance genes, DNA gyrase subunit B (gyrB) and serine-tRNA ligase (serS), and applied the approach to two additional metagenomic soil libraries. This expanded screening identified four additional validated networks, corresponding to one gyrB-associated NRPS BGC, two serS-associated NRPS BGCs, and one serS-associated PKS BGC (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Expression and characterization of calprotamide A–C from the CPA BGC

Using transformation-associated recombination in yeast we re-assembled the CPA BGC into a single continuous fragment of DNA carried on an E. coli-Streptomyces-yeast bacterial shuttle vector (Fig. 1c, Supplementary Table 3). This construct, pTAR-CPA, was conjugated into Streptomyces albus J1074 for heterologous expression studies. Unfortunately, no clone-specific metabolites were detected in cultures of this strain. In an effort to induce production, we used miCASTAR26 to introduce constitutive Streptomyces promoters into the CPA BGC. In one construct we inserted a promoter in front of cpaH, the first gene in the large NPRS/PKS operon (pTAR-CPA-v1), and in a second construct we inserted a bidirectional constitutive promoter cassette between a transcription factor (cpaG) and the clpP gene (cpaF) (pTAR-CPA-v2) (Fig. 1d). In cultures of S. albus transformed with these constructs, we detected a similar set of clone-specific metabolites (Fig. 1e). The most intense signals corresponded to features with m/z values of 530, 655 and 673. The highest signal intensities were observed in cultures containing pTAR-CPA-v1 and therefore this construct was grown in large-scale for metabolite isolation studies.

Each clone-specific mass observed in the crude extract appeared as two distinct HPLC peaks, which were independently purified (Fig. 1e). Once purified, each pair of peaks interconverted overtime (Supplementary Fig. 4). The structure of one isomer of each pair was elucidated by HRMS and NMR (Fig. 1f, Supplementary Figs. 5–39, Supplementary Table 4). The 530 feature was determined to be an N-acylated β-methylphenylalanine-leucine-alanine tripeptide with a butyraldehyde moiety between its leucine and alanine residues. In the case of the 655 feature the C-terminal carboxylic acid of the hybrid peptide-polyketide structure was replaced with a bis-γ-lactam bicyclic moiety, and in the case of the 673 feature one γ-lactam was hydrolyzed (Supplementary Fig. 40). We have given these natural products the trivial names calprotamide A (1), B (2) and C (3), respectively. To the best of our knowledge the bis-γ-lactam bicyclic ring in calprotamide B has not been seen previously in any natural product.

To study the interconversion of isomers seen in the calprotamides, we incubated one isomer of calprotamide B in a mixture of D2O and DMSO (1:1) and then analyzed the sample by LCMS/MS. After 24 h, the sample had converted to a mixture of two isomers with the new isomer gaining one mass unit (Supplementary Fig. 41a). A detailed MS/MS analysis of the new heavy isomer revealed that the deuterium was incorporated in the fragment containing the C21 methine (Supplementary Fig. 41b, c). The proton on this methine is surrounded by two carbonyls and therefore predicted to be acidic, likely leading to facile racemization of this center in all three calprotamides.

The biosynthesis of the calprotamides could be rationalized based on a detailed analysis of the CPA BGC (Fig. 2). The predicted biosynthetic scheme begins with an initiation module and a PKS elongation module giving rise to the monounsaturated lipid seen in all three structures. This is then extended with three NRPS modules and one PKS module to produce a tripeptide containing an intervening butyraldehyde moiety. Release of this intermediate would give calprotamide A. Further extension of one malonyl CoA and a serine would produce the precursor to calprotamide B. The CPA BGC does not encode a type I thioesterase, instead CpaM contains a C-terminal condensation (C) domain. We predict this C domain produces the bis-γ-lactam ring system from the precursor shown on CpaM. In this two-step cyclization scheme the C domain would first carryout a condensation between the serine alpha carbon and the alanine carbonyl to give one γ-lactam and then the second γ-lactam would form through condensation of the alanine amide and the terminal thioester. A similar terminal C-domain catalyzed process is proposed to yield the β-lactone/γ-lactam bicyclic ring system found in oxazolomycin27. For calprotamide B, we propose that the amine from the alanine is used to close the second ring instead of the hydroxyl as seen in oxazolomycin biosynthesis. Antibiotic activity

We tested the calprotamides for activity against a panel of common bacterial pathogens and eukaryotic cells. No growth inhibitory activity was observed for any of these organisms (Supplementary Table 5). Given that genes within the assembled DNA fragment containing the CPA BGC show highest identity to genes found in diverse Actinomycetes species, and the presence of the potential clpP self-resistance gene in the BGC, we hypothesized that the calprotamides might specifically inhibit the growth of Actinomycetes. We therefore expanded our test panel to include 10 diverse Streptomyces strains. Calprotamide A was inactive against this panel of bacteria; however, calprotamide B inhibited the growth of 4 Streptomyces strains (Table 1). ClpP activity

We initially tested the calprotamides for their effect on Mycobacterium tuberculosis (Mtb) Clp protease activity as this is most extensively studied ClpP system from Actinomycetes due to its appeal as an antibiotic target. The Mtb ClpP1/P2 complex can digest small peptides by itself (peptidase activity), and in the presence of an ATP-dependent unfoldase (ClpC1), it can degrade larger protein substrates (protease activity). Interestingly, when the Mtb ClpP protease was first studied in vitro it was inactive28. Activity was only reconstituted with the serendipitous discovery of synthetic N-blocked dipeptides that activate the native protease29,30. The most potent of these synthetic activators is benzoyl-leucine-leucine (Bz-LL)31. Although proposed to exist, no natural metabolites with Bz-LL-like activity had been identified30,32. We therefore tested the calprotamides for their effect on both peptidase and protease activity, as well as their ability to activate native ClpP. As they have distinct binding sites and effects on Clp activity, the natural products metamarin33, which binds ClpC1 and restricts its dynamics34, and ADEP135, which binds ClpP2 and interfere with ClpC1 binding36, were used as controls.

Calprotamides A and B, like metamarin, enhanced the protease activity of ClpP1/P2:C1, whereas ADEP1 inhibited this activity (Fig. 3a). Both calprotamides also caused an ADEP-like increase in the peptidase activity of ClpP1/P2 alone (Fig. 3b). Metamarin had no effect on this activity, as it binds ClpC1, which is not present in the peptidase assay (Fig. 3b). Without Bz-LL, neither ADEP1 nor metamarin stimulated Mtb ClpP1/P2 activity at the highest concentration we tested (0.5 mM). However, both calprotamide A and B activated native ClpP. In fact, they were both more potent native ClpP activators than Bz-LL (Fig. 3c), indicating that the calprotamides represent a previously elusive example of a natural compound with the ability to activate native ClpP. Interestingly, both calprotamide A and B exhibited similar activity in all three Mtb Clp enzyme assays; however, only calprotamide B demonstrated measurable antibiotic activity. We hypothesized that this discrepancy might be due to reduced cellular accumulation of the negatively charged calprotamide A. To investigate this, we used S. aureus as a model system, as neither compound affected it’s ClpP activity or inhibited its growth. In this context, we found that calprotamide B accumulated at approximately three times the level of calprotamide A (Supplementary Fig. 42), potentially explaining the observed difference in antibacterial activity.

Cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM) structure

To explore how the calprotamides affect ClpP, we determined the cryo-EM structure of calprotamide A bound to Mtb ClpP1/P2. Given the comparable activity of calprotamide A and B in functional assays, we focused our analysis on calprotamide A, as calprotamide B is less stable and exhibited low solubility in protein buffer, making it a poor candidate for preparing high-quality grids. Based on our cryo-EM data, the full tetradecameric complex could not be resolved under C1 symmetry due to poorly defined density at the ligand binding sites. We therefore focused on the two-protomer complex and performed local refinement and intensive 3D classification, which yielded a class of two-protomer units with improved density at the binding site. All structural analyses reported here are based on the two-protomer complex.

In our structure, calprotamide A binds the apical surface of ClpP2 at the same general binding site as ADEP (Fig. 4a, b). The N-acylphenylalanine moiety, shared by both calprotamide A and ADEP, occupies the ClpP2 H pocket in each structure, and residues forming the H pocket show no major differences in their interactions with this fragment (Supplementary Fig. 44a). Beyond this common pharmacophore, the structural features of these two natural products largely extend in opposite directions (Fig. 4c). The electron density for the C-terminal portion of calprotamide A is less well-defined than that observed for the N-acylphenylalanine motif that binds the H pocket, suggesting that it is more conformationally flexible. Residues predicted to interact with the non-N-acylphenylalanine portions of each ligand show only minor side-chain rearrangements between the two structures (Supplementary Fig. 44b). The largest apparent difference occurs at R207; however, the electron density for this side chain in the ADEP-bound structure (PDB 6vgn) is insufficient to confirm a definitive structural change. To further compare the difference in binding between calprotamide A and ADEP, we performed alanine mutagenesis of two key residues in Mtb ClpP2, R207 and Q101. R207 forms a salt bridge with calprotamide A but not with ADEP, whereas Q101 forms a hydrogen bond with ADEP but not with calprotamide A (Supplementary Fig. 45a). As predicted by our model, the R207A mutation more strongly impaired calprotamide A-mediated enhancement of Mtb ClpP1P2 peptidase activity than it did ADEP-mediated enhancement. Conversely, the Q101A mutation had a greater effect on ADEP-mediated enhancement than on calprotamide A-mediated enhancement (Supplementary Fig. 45b). Whether the unusual bis-γ-lactam bicyclic moiety in calprotamide B forms additional interactions with Mtb ClpP2 or the ClpP1/P2:C1 complex remains to be determined. It is possible that the bis-γ-lactam serves primarily to mask the negative charge that would normally be generated upon release by the thioesterase, thereby improving bioavailability. Although no major structural rearrangements were observed at the ligand binding site, calprotamide A binding induced two more distal conformational changes. Specifically, it promoted ordering of loops in both the ClpP2 N-terminal region and the ClpP2 handle region near the catalytic sites (Fig. 4d). Notably, changes in these regions have been independently observed in the ADEP- and Bz-LL-bound structures, respectively. However, while calprotamide A binding produced electron density consistent with only partial ordering, the corresponding regions in the ADEP and Bz-LL complexes adopt fully formed secondary structures (a β-hairpin and β-sheet, respectively). In both the ADEP and Bz-LL structures, all ligand binding sites are fully occupied, whereas only partial ligand occupancy was observed in our calprotamide A complex. Thus, the limited conformational changes observed in our structure may reflect an intermediate state resulting from incomplete ligand occupancy.

Calprotamide A and ADEP both enhance Mtb ClpP1/P2 peptidase activity. As calprotamide A and ADEP bind to the same region of ClpP2, this activity enhancement likely occurs through a shared mechanism. However, due to the limited conformational changes observed in Mtb ClpP upon binding either ligand, the underlying mechanism of activation remains unresolved. Interestingly, calprotamide A enhances ClpC1-dependent protease activity, whereas ADEP inhibits this activity by displacing ClpC1 from the ClpP1/P2 complex. Although both ligands bind the ClpP2 H pocket, they create distinct surfaces at the apical region of ClpP2, where ClpC1 interacts. Based on the structure of ClpP1/P2 alone, it remains unclear how the surface formed by the ClpP2-calprotamide A complex promotes enhanced ClpC1-dependent proteolysis.

Convergent evolution of a medium-chain N-acylphenylalanine motif for ClpP engagement

The closely related medium-chain (C6-C8) N-acylphenylalanine substructures found in calprotamide and ADEP antibiotics bind ClpP2 in the same orientation mimicking the IGF loop that endogenous unfoldases (e.g., ClpPC1 and ClpX) use to engage ClpP2. The phenylalanine residue fits into a conserved cleft typically occupied by the unfoldase’s IGF phenylalanine, and the acyl group mimics the hydrogen bonding pattern of the isoleucine-glycine backbone of the tripeptide anchoring these natural products to the protease. While structurally related, these two N-acylphenylalanine moieties are encoded by evolutionary distinct biosynthetic pathways (Fig. 5a, left). In the case of the calprotamides the lipid moiety arises from a type I PKS system, while the ADEP triene is produced by a type II PKS system. The methylphenylalanine in the calprotamides and phenylalanine in the ADEPs are then both added to their respective lipids by an NRPS module. The emergence of the medium-chain N-acylphenylalanine motif in biosynthetically unrelated natural products is a clear example convergent evolution for ClpP engagement. Inspired by our discovery of the convergently evolved N-acylphenylalanine moiety for engaging ClpP, we conducted a bioinformatic search for other BGCs that might encode Clp active molecules with N-acylphenylalanine substructures. To do this, we scanned sequenced NRPS BGCs for the presence of a phenylalanine-specific A-domain and a co-localized clp gene (e.g., clpP, clpA/C or clpX). This subset of BGCs was then manually searched for phenylalanine-selective A-domains, either downstream of PKS modules or following a lipid-incorporating initiation moiety (i.e., a coenzyme A ligase (CAL) containing module or a condensation starter (Cs) domain). This identified eight predicted N-acylphenylalanine encoding BGCs co-localized with a clp gene that could serve as a potential self-resistance gene (Fig. 5b, Supplementary Table 7). These BGCs were found in diverse Gram -positive and -negative bacteria, including members of the Actinomycetia, Betaproteobacteria, Clostridia, and Sphingobacteriia classes. Among the BGCs with co-localized clp genes, six appear to encode previously uncharacterized natural products. A BGC from Streptomyces hawaiiensis encodes the ClpP-targeting antibiotic ADEP, and a BGC from Streptomyces caniferus encodes the jomthonic acids37,38, a known natural product family that had not been previously reported to have ClpP activity.

To explore the possibility that jomthonic acid might possess overlooked ClpP activity, we synthesized desmethyl jomthonic acid C (4, Supplementary Figs. 46–49) and tested it for its effect on Mtb ClpP. Although less potent than the calprotamides, it enhanced Mtb ClpP1/P2 peptidase activity (Fig. 5c). To investigate whether other BGCs we identified encode uncharacterized natural products with ClpP activity, we bioinformatically predicted the product of the hybrid PKS-NRPS BGC from Mycobacterium tusciae JS617 (Supplementary Figs. 50–53). We then synthesized this predicted structure, which we named tuscamide (5), and assayed it for ClpP activity. Like desmethyl jomthonic acid C, tuscamide also enhanced Mtb ClpP1/P2 peptidase activity. In fact, it had comparable activity to both calprotamide A and B (Fig. 5d).

The identification of four structurally distinct ClpP active natural products, linked only by their medium-chain N-acylphenylalanine substructures, suggests that uncharacterized BGCs encoding this moiety are likely to represent an untapped reservoir of structurally diverse ClpP-targeting compounds. Notably, this shared substructure that engages the H pocket corresponds to the smallest ADEP fragment reported to retain antibacterial activity39. Together, our bioinformatic and activity analyses suggested that the N-acylphenylalanine moiety was likely a previously unappreciated, yet potentially common, ClpP-targeting motif that has convergently evolved in diverse bacteria. As not all BGCs will contain a self-resistance gene, we carried out a broader search of sequenced BGCs for those that encode N-acylphenylalanine moieties. This search identified more than 100 previously uncharacterized BGCs containing an NRPS initiation module consisting of a phenylalanine-selective A-domain and Cs (condensation starter) domain, which initiates lipopeptide biosynthesis by appending a fatty acyl chain onto the first amino acid. Interestingly, a number of the BGCs we identified in both our original clp gene-driven bioinformatic analysis and this broader search are hybrid NRPS-PKS systems indicating that they could encode both the lipid and the phenylalanine seen in a medium-chain (C6-C8) N-acylphenylalanine motif. In purely NRPS BGCs, the CAL or Cs initiation domain could instead introduce a primary metabolism-derived medium-chain fatty acid and therefore no specific fatty acid biosynthesis genes would be required.

While not all these BGCs are expected to encode a ClpP-targeting molecule, this collection is potentially enriched for ClpP activity. We bioinformatically predicted the encoded peptide sequences and randomly selected ten uncharacterized BGCs whose products could be predicted from existing A-domain specificity data, were synthetically accessible, and contained a possible cyclization site (e.g., serine or threonine) (Supplementary Tables 8, 9). Most of these BGCs originated from Actinomycetes species, including four from Mycobacterium, two from Streptomyces, one from Microbispora, and one from Rhodococcus. The remaining two BGCs were derived from Paenibacillus and Mucilaginibacter. Each predicted lipopeptide was synthesized using Fmoc-based solid-phase peptide synthesis (SPPS) and with the 2-hexenoic acid lipid moiety from the calprotamides (Supplementary Table 10, Supplementary Fig. 54).

The resulting molecules ranged from five to ten amino acids in length, with ring sizes varying from four to eight residues. All compounds were evaluated for their ability to modulate Mtb ClpP peptidase activity and for antibiotic activity against a panel of Streptomyces and Mycobacterium strains (Fig. 6a). We used an expanded panel of Actinomycetes, as we had previously observed narrow-spectrum Actinomycete activity for N-acylphenylalanine containing structures, and most BGCs that inspired our synthetic efforts originated from this genus. Several molecules enhanced Mtb ClpP1/P2 peptidase activity, with 6 (myscamide, Fig. 6b, Supplementary Figs. 55, 56) and 13 (paenilamide, Fig. 6c, Supplementary Figs. 57, 58) producing the strongest enhancement at levels comparable to calprotamide B. Enhancement of ClpP1/P2 activity correlated with antibacterial activity, with both of these compounds exhibiting antibiotic activity against a narrow spectrum of Actinomycete species. Discussion

Our discovery of the calprotamides validates the power of resistance-CONKAT-seq to target the discovery of natural products with specific molecular targets from metagenomes. It also provides additional compelling evidence that the soil metagenome encodes unexplored biosynthetic diversity. The calprotamides represent a previously unreported natural product family with an unprecedented bis-γ-lactam bicyclic ring system. They also exhibit ClpP activity as they enhance both Mtb ClpP protease and peptidase activities. In native cells expressing the self-resistant ClpP protein, the ClpP tetradecamer may form a heteromeric complex that is resistant to activation by the compound it produces. While ClpP activators like Bz-LL have been postulated to exist in nature30,32, there were no characterized examples before our discovery of the calprotamides. This type of ClpP protease activator is likely produced by other bacteria29; however, they may have been overlooked in previous studies if they exhibit a narrow spectrum of activity, similar to the calprotamides. It will be interesting to further explore the activity spectrum of the calprotamides to identify what factors contribute to their narrow range of activity.

The independent convergent evolution of medium-chain (C6-C8) N-acylphenylalanine moieties in multiple ClpP-active natural products as well as BGCs co-localized with clp genes suggests that it is a privileged natural motif for modulating ClpP activity. Notably, this structural motif has now been linked to ClpP activation, enhancement, and inhibition by natural products. While Fig. 5b focuses on the eight BGCs that are co-localized with a putative clp self-resistance gene, we believe this underestimates the potential uncharacterized reservoir of BGCs that encode ClpP-active natural products through an N-acylphenylalanine motif. Target-based self-resistance genes are not found in all BGCs, and therefore uncharacterized BGCs encoding N-acylphenylalanine, despite lacking a nearby clp gene, also have the potential to be enriched for the ability to encode ClpP-modulating compounds. Even when only looking for BGCs that use Cs domains to introduce an acyl substituent we identified more than 100 uncharacterized BGCs that are predicted to encode N-acylphenylalanine substructures. This number would increase when including CAL domain and hybrid PKS-NRPS BGCs that are predicted to encode N-acylphenylalanine substructures. While these BGCs will not all produce ClpP-active metabolites, we predict the collection of BGCs is likely to be enriched for ClpP activity.

The N-acylphenylalanine motif should be broadly useful for identifying ClpP-targeting compounds in both synthetic chemistry campaigns and natural product discovery efforts. The strategy we used here to identify tuscamide, myscamide, and paenilamide likely represents a hybrid of these two approaches. While it is often possible to predict the peptide product of an NRPS BGC with reasonable confidence, it remains challenging to predict the specific lipid incorporated by a Cs domain. We used the 2-hexenoic acid lipid moiety from the calprotamides in our syntheses. Although these exact structures may not all occur in nature, we predict that a subset of these BGCs will naturally incorporate similar medium-chain lipids for targeting ClpP. In other cases, appending the ClpP-targeting motif to a naturally encoded NRPS scaffold may increase the likelihood of identifying ClpP-active antibiotics, as the NRPS-derived core is likely to have evolved for bioactivity and bioavailability.

It is possible that natural products containing a medium-chain (C6-C8) N-acylphenylalanine motif have been overlooked in previous discovery studies both because most BGCs remain silent in the laboratory and because ClpP-active natural products, at least in some instances, appear to have narrow spectra of activity. For example, calprotamide B was only active against a subset of the Actinomycetes we tested. Although broad spectrum antibiotics have historically been prioritized, in recent antibiotic development efforts there has been a growing interest in narrow spectrum compounds40–44. Overlooked Clp-active natural products may be particularly well suited to this task. Further, it is possible that not all ClpP-active molecules are produced for antibacterial activity. ClpP effects diverse processes including antibiotic resistance, stress response, virulence, and biofilm formation45–47. Therefore, some ClpP-active molecules may function as context-dependent modulators that confer survival advantages under specific conditions.

Natural product discovery programs have commonly relied on random phenotypic screening of cultured bacteria to identify bioactive small molecules with clinically promising MOAs. The targeted discovery of natural products with desired MOAs from not only cultured bacteria but also underexplored metagenomes is an appealing alternative to this traditional approach. Resistance-CONKAT-seq provides a general strategy for addressing the problem of target-directed discovery of natural products from metagenomes. In this study, we directed resistance-CONKAT-seq towards metagenomic BGCs associated with clpP genes; however, this approach can be easily expanded to identify BGCs with other molecular targets by using primers targeting any potential self-resistance genes.

Methods

Amplification of clpP genes and targeted sequencing

A soil metagenomic cosmid library was previously constructed and archived as glycerol stocks. The library was arrayed across two 384-well plates with each well containing ~25,000 cosmid clones. Degenerate clpP primers barcoded to individual wells were used to generate clpP amplicons across the entire library using 10 μL PCR reactions. For detailed methods on degenerate primer design, see the Supplementary Information. The reaction mix included 5 μL of 2× GoTaq® Master Mix (Promega), 0.5 μL of barcoded primers (100 μM), 1 μL of template DNA (20 ng/μL), and 3 μL of H2O. PCR was performed using the following conditions: 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 35 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 30 s with a final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplicons across a plate were pooled and gel-purified according to the expected amplicon size. The purified amplicons were used to seed a second-round PCR to add individual plate barcodes as well as Illumina adaptors. The PCR reactions were 10 μL of 2× FailSafe™ PCR PreMix G (Biosearch Technologies), 0.5 μL of Illumina universal forward primer (100 μM), 0.5 μL of Illumina indexed reverse primer (100 μM), 5 μL of purified DNA (100 ng), 0.4 μL of rTaq polymerase (Bulldog Bio), and 3.6 μL of H2O. The reaction conditions for the second-round PCR were an initial denaturing step of 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 6 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 70 °C for 30 s, and 72 °C for 45 s, with a final extension step at 72 °C for 5 min. The PCR products were purified using Agencourt AMPure XP magnetic beads (Beckman Coulter) and quantified using a 2200 TapeStation (Agilent Technologies). Amplicons were pooled equimolarly and sequenced at a final concentration of 10 nM on an Illumina NextSeq platform.

Resistance-CONKAT-seq analysis and selection criteria for clpP: AD/KS networks

To identify clpP genes that were associated with AD/KS network clusters, the clpP OTUs were analyzed using CONKAT-seq17,18 together with OTUs for adenylation (AD) and ketosynthase (KS) domains. OTUs were required to share at least 3 common wells to be considered part of the same gene network. Generated networks were visualized using Cytoscape48 and candidates were selected based on containing at least one clpP OTU and at least one AD or KS domain. The following criteria were used to remove false positive networks: 1. to prevent highly prevalent amplicons across the whole library, networks with normal distribution of amplicons (less than 50% wells) were kept, 2. to prevent cross-contaminations, networks with normal distribution (not too close to each other) of clpP-AD/KS co-occurrences were kept, and 3. to prioritize the networks with high likelihood of real connections, networks with high (≥50%) co-occurrence rates were kept. Remaining networks were validated by PCR. For detailed methods about the recovery and assembly of the CPA BGC, see the Supplementary Information.

Heterologous expression

The TAR-assembled and a control pTAR-lys vector were individually transformed into E. coli S17 by heat shock and integrated into the genome of Streptomyces albus J1074. Validated conjugants were used to generate a spore stock which was then used to seed 5 mL of trypticase soy broth (Oxoid) followed by incubation at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 48 h to promote robust mycelial growth. After the initial incubation, 0.05 mL of starter culture was used to inoculate 50 mL of R5a medium (100 g/L of sucrose, 5 g/L of yeast extract, 10.12 g/L of MgCl2·6H2O, 0.25 of g/L K2SO4, 0.1 g/L of casamino acids, 21 g/L of MOPS, 10 g/L of D-glucose, 2 g/L of NaOH, 5.88 mg/L of CaCl2, 80 μg/L of ZnCl2, 400 μg/L of FeCl3·6H2O, 20 μg/L of MnCl2, 20 μg/L of CuCl2, 20 μg/L of Na2B4O7·10H2O, 20 μg/L of (NH4)6Mo7O24·4H2O, pH 6.8) in 125 mL baffled flasks for incubation at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 10 days.

Crude extract LC-MS analysis

Fermentation cultures (500 μL) from the heterologous expression culture were extracted with ethyl acetate (1:1 v/v). The extract then dried under reduced pressure using a SpeedVac™ Vacuum Concentrator (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and dissolved in 100 μL of HPLC-grade methanol. Insoluble particulates were removed by centrifugation at 21,300 × g for 2 min and filtering using 0.22 μm membrane filter (Millipore). The material was analyzed by high-resolution mass spectrometry.

Biosynthetic gene cluster refactoring

The CPA BGC was refactored using miCASTAR as previously described26. Briefly, sgRNA primers targeting sequence regions for promoter replacement were designed and synthesized (IDT). The EnGen sgRNA Synthesis Kit (NEB) was then used to generate sgRNAs, which were purified with an RNA Clean & Concentrator Kit (Zymo Research). The TAR-assembled CPA BGC (pTAR-lys vector) was digested with Cas9 Nuclease (NEB) and sgRNAs at 37 °C overnight. Linearized DNA was purified using the ZR Research BAC DNA Miniprep Kit (Zymo Research). A donor DNA fragment containing prototrophic markers, a strong Streptomyces promoter, and homology arms flanking the insertion site was amplified by PCR. For homologous recombination, the donor DNA and Cas9-digested CPA BGC were co-transformed into Saccharomyces cerevisiae BY4727 via the standard spheroplast method. Yeast colonies harboring correctly integrated constructs were selected on synthetic complete (SC) medium lacking specific amino acids. Positive clones were screened by PCR for precise insertion, followed by plasmid extraction. Validated constructs were electroporated into E. coli S17 cells and subsequently conjugated into Streptomyces albus J1074 for heterologous expression.

Scaled production and isolation of calprotamides

Spore of Streptomyces albus J1074 conjugated with the refactored CPA BGC v1 was inoculated into 5 mL of trypticase soy broth (Oxoid) and incubated at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 48 h. Then, 100 μL of the starter culture was added to five 2.8 L baffled flasks containing 1 L of R5a medium, followed by incubating at 30 °C with shaking at 200 rpm for 6 days. The 5 L fermentation culture was extracted by two rounds of 1:1 v/v ethyl acetate, and the crude extract was concentrated using a rotary evaporator. The concentrated sample was then passed through a C18 column (50 g, RediSep Rf) and separated using a reverse-phase linear gradient method (5-100% H₂O/acetonitrile, 0.1% formic acid) over the course of 30 min. Fractions containing the desired compounds were purified by semipreparative high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) using an XSelect CSH Phenyl-Hexyl 130 Å column (150 mm × 10 mm, 5 µm, Waters) under the following conditions: 5% B for 0.1 min, 5–40% B from 0.1 to 5 min, 40–60% B from 5 to 40 min, 60-100% B from 40 to 45 min, 100% B from 45 to 50 min, 100–5% B from 50 to 55 min, and 5% B from 55 to 60 min. Fractions containing calprotamide A–C were purified using an XBridge semipreparative C18 column (150 mm × 10 mm, 5 µm, Waters) under different conditions. For calprotamide A: 5% B for 0.1 min, 5–40% B from 0.1 to 5 min, 40–55% B from 5 to 40 min, 55–100% B from 40 to 45 min, 100% B from 45 to 50 min, 100–5% B from 50 to 55 min, and 5% B from 55 to 60 min. For calprotamide B: 5% B for 0.1 min, 5–40% B from 0.1 to 5 min, 40–50% B from 5 to 40 min, 50–100% B from 40 to 45 min, 100% B from 45 to 50 min, 100–5% B from 50 to 55 min, and 5% B from 55 to 60 min. For Calprotamide C: 5% B for 0.1 min, 5–20% B from 0.1 to 5 min, 20–40% B from 5 to 40 min, 40-100% B from 40 to 45 min, 100% B from 45 to 50 min, 100–5% B from 50 to 55 min, and 5% B from 55 to 60 min. All HPLC purification steps were with an injection volume of 50 μL, water as solvent A and acetonitrile as solvent B in the presence of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid. The flow rate was 2.5 mL/min.

Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assays

To obtain the saturated cultures, Streptomyces spores were seeded in 5 mL of trypticase soy broth (Oxoid) followed by incubation at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 48 h. M. smegmatis mc2 155, M. avium subsp. paratuberculosis ATCC 43544, M. gallinarum ATCC 19710, and M. seoulense DSM 44998 glycerol stocks were seeded in 5 mL of 7H9 liquid medium (0.94 g/200 mL of 7H9 powder, 50% glycerol, and 40% glucose, pH = 7) followed by incubation at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 48 h. M. tuberculosis mc2 6206 glycerol stock was seeded in 10 mL of defined 7H9 medium (0.94 g/200 mL of 7H9 powder, 50% glycerol, 20 mL/200 mL of ADC, 10 mg/mL of leucine, and 5.76 mg/mL of calcium pantothenate, pH = 7) followed by incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 14 days. Candida albicans glycerol stock was seeded in 5 mL of YPD medium followed by incubation at 30 °C and 200 rpm for 16 h. Other tested bacterial glycerol stocks were seeded in 5 mL of LB medium followed by incubation at 37 °C and 200 rpm for 16 h. The saturated cultures were then diluted by 1:10,000 folds in 10 mL of Production Medium (20 mL/L of glycerol, 10 g/L of peptone, 5 g/L of Meat extract, pH 7) for Streptomyces, 7H9 medium for M. smegmatis, M. avium, M. gallinarum, and M. seoulense, defined 7H9 medium for M. tuberculosis, YPD medium for Candida albicans, and LB medium for other tested bacteria. The stock solution of compounds was serial diluted 2-fold in a 96-well plate (ThermoFisher Scientific) containing corresponding culture media, then an equal amount of diluted culture was added to each well and mixed by pipetting. The plates containing Streptomyces were incubated at 30 °C for 48 h. The plates containing M. smegmatis were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h. The plates containing M. tuberculosis, M. avium, M. gallinarum, and M. seoulense were incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 7 days. The plates containing Candida albicans were incubated at 30 °C for 16 h. The plates containing other bacteria were incubated at 37 °C for 16 h. The top and bottom rows of plates contained an equal volume of media alone to prevent edge effect. The last column did not contain antibiotic to serve as a control for cell viability. The MIC of antibiotics was determined by visual inspection as the concentration in the well that prevents bacterial growth compared to control wells. The MIC assays were performed in at least duplicate.

Human cell cytotoxicity assay

The cytotoxicity of calprotamides was performed using MTT (3-(4,5-Dimethyl-2-thiazolyl)-2,5-diphenyl-2H-tetrazolium bromide) assay. Briefly, HEK293 cells (ATCC CRL-1573) were diluted with DMEM medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% bovine serum and 1% glutamate at the density of 5000 cells/well in a 96-well plate and incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 24 h. Compounds were serially diluted in DMEM medium and added to incubated cells and the mixture plate was incubated for 48 h. The supernatant in each well was aspirated and 100 μL of 0.5 mg/mL MTT solution in DPBS buffer (Gibco) was added with gentle mixing. After 4 h incubation at RT, 100 μl of solubilization solution (40% DMF (v/v), 15% SDS (w/v), 2% acetic acid (v/v)) was added to cells and mixed using a mini shaker. After a final incubation at RT for 1 h, the plate was transferred to an Infinite M NANO+ (TECAN) and the absorbance of wells were measured at 570 nm. Paclitaxel and 0.2% DMSO were used as positive and negative controls, respectively.

Overexpression and purification of Mtb ClpP1P2, ClpC1 and S. aureus ClpP

Escherichia coli BL21 (DE3) harboring plasmids encoding 14×HisTag-bdSUMO-ClpP1, 14×HisTag-bdSUMO-ClpP2, 14×HisTag-ClpC1 (Mtb), or 14×HisTag-ClpP (S. aureus) were cultivated in 1 L of LB medium at 37 °C and 200 rpm until an optical density at 600 nm (OD600) of 0.8. The cultures were then cooled to 4 °C for 20 min and induced with 1 mM of isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) and grown at 16 °C and 200 rpm overnight. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 8000 × g for 20 min and resuspended in 100 mL of lysis buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 250 mM NaCl, 1 mM β-mercaptoethanol (BME), 1% Triton X-100, 0.1 mg/mL DNase I, 0.1 mg/mL lysozyme, 1 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride (PMSF), 0.1 mg/mL trypsin inhibitor, 1 µg/mL pepstatin, 1 µg/mL leupeptin, and 1 mM benzamidine). The suspension was incubated on ice for 15 min and the cells were then lysed via sonication. Cell lysates were centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C to remove cell debris. The supernatant was then applied to a 5 mL HiTrap chelating Ni²⁺ affinity column (GE Healthcare) pre-equilibrated with wash buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8.0, 250 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole, 1 mM BME). Column was subsequently washed with ~50 column-volume (CV) of Ni-wash buffer (20 mM Tris pH = 8.0, 250 mM NaCl, 30 mM imidazole). Bound proteins were eluted using 50 mL elution buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8.0, 250 mM NaCl, 300 mM imidazole, 1 mM BME). For ClpP1 and ClpP2, the bdSUMO tag was removed by incubation with 1 µg/mL bdSENP1 protease49. Eluted proteins were concentrated to 5 mL using an Amicon Ultra centrifugal filter unit (100 kDa cutoff, Millipore) and Mtb ClpP1 and ClpP2 were further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) on a Superdex 200 10/300 GL column (GE Healthcare) equilibrated in 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.3), 150 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl₂, and 1 mM dithiothreitol (DTT). Fractions containing the purified proteins were pooled and concentrated using Amicon Ultra filters (100 kDa cutoff). Protein concentrations were determined using the Quick Start Bradford Protein Assay Kit (Bio-Rad). Purified proteins were aliquoted and stored at −80 until further use.

In vitro ClpP protease assays

Mtb ClpP protease activity was assessed using the Mtb ClpP Protease Assay Kit (ProFoldin) according to manufacturer instructions. For ClpP1P2C1 assays, 1 μM ClpP1P2 (monomer) and 1 μM ClpC1 (monomer) were combined in Assay Buffer supplemented with 8 mM MgCl₂ and 2 mM ATP. Test compounds were pre-incubated with the ClpP1P2 or ClpP1P2C1 complexes for 5 min before the addition of 0.3 μL 100× ZVKM-AMC (for ClpP1P2 assay) or 200 μM FITC-casein (AAT Bioquest, for ClpP1P2C1 assay) as fluorogenic substrates. For the S. aureus ClpP assay, 1 μM S. aureus ClpP (monomer) was incubated in a 30 μL reaction mixture containing 100 mM HEPES (pH 7.0), 100 mM NaCl, and 200 μM Suc-LY-AMC (AAT Bioquest). Fluorescence signals were recorded using an Infinite M200 Pro plate reader (Tecan) at 37 °C with excitation/emission wavelengths of 380/460 nm (AMC) or 480/520 nm (FITC). All assays were performed in duplicate, and the data were plotted using GraphPad Prism 10.

Cryo-EM sample preparation and data acquisition

Cryo-EM grids (Quantifoil R 1.2/1.3 400 Mesh, Au) were prepared on a Vitrobot IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific) set to 100% humidity at 22 °C. To maintain humidity, two hydrated Kimwipes were affixed to the chamber walls. Prior to freezing the mixture was supplemented with 0.05% Digitonin and then incubated for 1 min on grids blotted at force 1 for 6–7 s before plunging into liquid ethane. Cryo-EM grids were first screened on a 200-kV Talos Arctica transmission electron microscope. Data were collected by SerialEM50 on a 300-kV Titan Krios transmission electron microscope operated at a nominal magnification of ×105,000, corresponding to a calibrated pixel size of 0.663 Å (image dimensions of 5760 ×4092 px) on the specimen level. Images were collected using a slit width of 20 eV on the GIF Quantum energy filter (Gatan) and a defocus range from −1.0 to −2.0 μm with a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan) in super-resolution counting mode. Three movie stacks were recorded per hole in a 3×3 matrix of holes acquired using beam-image shift with a maximum shift of 3.5 μm. Exposures of 1.2 s were dose-fractionated into 40 frames (30 ms per frame) with an exposure rate of ~1.313 electrons per Å2 per frame, resulting in a total electron exposure of 52.52 electrons per Å2. The data collection parameters are summarized in Supplementary Table 6.

Cryo-EM image processing

Data were processed in CryoSPARC v4.6.2 (Structura Biotechnology Inc.)51,52 with standardized preprocessing. Motion correction and contrast transfer function (CTF) estimation were conducted using CryoSPARC’s patch-based algorithms53. Particles were selected using CryoSPARC’s template picker (particle diameter: 180 Å; angular sampling: 2 degrees). Following extraction into ~250 Å boxes with 2× binning, particles were cleaned through four rounds of 2D classification to exclude extraneous particles resulting in 1,627,466 particles. These were subject to non-uniform refinement in CryoSPARC (C1 symmetry) with reference model (EMD-21196 REF) filtered to 40 Å. This yielded a 2.8 Å reconstruction, which was further improved to 2.6 Å by CTF-refinement54 and reference-based motion correction (Bayesian polishing55) in CryoSPARC. The resulting map showed poorly resolved density for calprotamide A, likely reflecting partial ligand occupancy. To better resolve the ligand binding region, we performed C7 symmetry expansion (11,388,629 particles) followed by local refinement using a mask encompassing a single stacked two-protomer ClpP1/P2 unit. One round of 3D-classification without image alignment was performed using this two-protomer focused mask (4 classes). This yielded two key classes, one class (13%) shows improved density of calprotamide A (bound), while the other (20%) shows no occupancy (unbound). These two were subjected to the final round of local refinement, which yielded the final reconstruction of calprotamide A bound complex (1,464,563 particles at 2.77 Å, Supplementary Fig. 43c) and calprotamide A-unbound complex (2,252,037 particles at 2.80 Å, Supplementary Fig. 43c), respectively. Mask-corrected FSC curves were calculated in CryoSPARC based on the 0.143 criterion (Supplementary Fig. 43d, e). Local resolution estimation was performed in CryoSPARC (Supplementary Fig. 43f) and representative densities of different regions were shown (Supplementary Fig. 43g).

Model building and refinement

For the calprotamide A-bound complex, the structure of apo-Mtb ClpP1P2 complex (PDB: 6VGK) was fit into our cryo-EM density and a model of our NMR-determined calprotamide A was docked into the corresponding density in Chimera56. This was subsequently refined iteratively using ISOLDE57, Coot58 and Phenix59. For the calprotamide A-unbound complex, calprotamide A-bound complex was docked into the corresponding cryo-EM density in Chimera and subsequently refined iteratively using ISOLDE, Coot and Phenix. The reported resolution for models was calculated in Phenix based on the 0.5 criterion. Structural model refinements and validations were summarized in Supplementary Table 6 and figures were prepared in ChimeraX60.

Compound accumulation assay

S. aureus cells were cultured overnight in LB at 37 °C and 200 rpm and normalized to ~10⁹ CFU/mL in fresh LB. Bacterial suspensions were incubated with 4 μg/mL of test compounds in 14 mL round-bottom tubes at 37 °C with agitation (200 rpm) for 1 h. Following incubation, cells were harvested by centrifugation at room temperature (10,000 × g, 1 min), washed three times with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and resuspended in LB to match the initial supernatant volume. The cell suspension and supernatant were extracted separately with 1:1 v/v ethyl acetate. The organic phase was evaporated to dryness under reduced pressure, resuspended in 100 μL of methanol and centrifuged (12,000 × g, 2 min) to remove particles. Compounds were quantified via LC-HRMS using 5 μL injection volume, with concentrations calculated by integrating peak areas. Experiments were performed in duplicate and the data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 10.

N-acyl phenylalanine search

Genomes downloaded from the NCBI assembly and the JGI GOLD databases were scanned using antiSMASH v6.1.0 to detect NRPS BGCs. Using the antiSMASH output, a simple text search of candidate BGC region GenBank files was used to filter only regions containing at least one predicted Phe module using the pattern ‘specificity.+phe.*’ and C-starter domain using the pattern ‘aSDomain.+Condensation_Starter’. BGC regions containing Clp genes were identified in one of two ways. For genomes that were fully annotated before the antiSMASH analysis, a text search was performed on the BGC region GenBank files using the pattern ‘gene = ”clp’. For unannotated (or sparsely annotated) genomes, the open reading frames of the genomes were extracted and were analyzed using mmseqs v14.7e28461 and a profile alignment built from the seed alignment for ClpP in PFAM62 (PF00574), ClpX and ClpA/C in NCBIFAM63 (TIGR00382). These BGCs were then manually examined for likely N-acetylated Phe modules, characterized by phenylalanine-selective A-domains either downstream of PKS modules or following lipid incorporating initiation domains such as coenzyme A ligase (CAL) containing module or a condensation starter (Cs) module.

Prediction of tuscamide

The structure prediction of tuscamide was generated based on an analysis of PKS domains, A-domain specificities, and NRPS modification domains (epimerization and N-methylation domains) present in its BGC (Supplementary Fig. 50). A-domain substrate specificity codes were compared to both the antiSMASH output and an in-house specificity code table collated from previously characterized natural products. For A-domains with more than two mismatches (<80% identity) to reference Stachelhaus codes, predictions were made using the antiSMASH ‘NRPSpredictor2 small clusters prediction’ tool. The initial fatty acid substrate was predicted based on a conserved glycine residue (Supplementary Fig. 50c), which is associated with a preference for medium-chain fatty acids64.

Prediction of additional library compounds

Structural predictions of additional N-acylphenylalanine compounds were generated based on A-domain substrate specificity and the presence of additional modification domains (i.e. epimerization and N-methylation domains) present in each BGC. A-domain substrate specificity codes were compared to both the antiSMASH output and an in-house specificity code table collated from previously characterized natural products. Amino acid identity predictions were determined by the A-domain specificity code with the closest match. In cases where multiple amino acids shared the same code or there was more than one code with the same match percentage, one was selected from the consensus of predictions generated by the antiSMASH NRPSPredictor2 tool.

N-acylphenylalanine compound synthesis

Standard Fmoc-based solid-phase peptide synthesis was used to produce all synthetic compounds, for more detailed methods see the Supplementary Information.
