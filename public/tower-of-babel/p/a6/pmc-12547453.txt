Prospective evaluation of mechanomyography versus triggered electromyography for intraoperative assessment of cortical breaches during instrumented lumbar surgery
Authors: Harshit Arora, Hassan Darabi, Francis Farhadi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionPedicle screws are essential in spinal arthrodesis surgeries but pose potential risks due to their proximity to neural and vascular structures. Traditionally, tEMG has been used to assess screw placement, though its invasiveness, low specificity, and cost limit its applicability. MMG may offer a promising alternative for routine clinical use. Our study compares the relative accuracy of triggered electromyography (tEMG) versus mechanomyography (MMG) in detecting intraoperative cortical bony breaches.MethodsUsing an equivalence trial design, consecutive patients undergoing posterior lumbosacral spinal arthrodesis were prospectively enrolled at a single institution. Pedicle screw trajectories were queried in real-time using combined tEMG and MMG-based evaluations at pretap, tap, post-tap and postscrew placement stages. Intraoperative computed tomography (CT) scans were performed to evaluate pedicle screw trajectories according to the Gertzbein-Robbins (GR) classification. Receiver operating characteristic curve analysis were performed to evaluate the relative accuracy of tEMG and MMG threshold potentials to detect cortical breaches. Pain and quality-of-life outcomes were evaluated up to 3 months postoperatively.ResultsA total of 303 consecutive lumbar pedicle screw trajectories were included (61 participants; mean age: 61.3 ± 9.7 years; male-to-female ratio: 32:29). 7 grade C-E GR cortical breaches were identified in a total of 5 subjects, with 5 (71.4%) classified as Grade C and 2 (28.6%) as Grade E. Baseline demographics were comparable between the breach and nonbreach groups. The tEMG and MMG AUC values determining predictive ability for breach detection were comparable for the pretap (AUC 0.82 vs. 0.80, p = .442) and post-tap stages (AUC 0.71 vs. 0.79, p = .380). Follow-up pain and functional assessments revealed significant improvements at last follow-up.ConclusiontEMG and MMG demonstrate high and equivalent accuracy to detect cortical breaches intraoperatively. Adequate utilization of either technique may enhance pedicle screw placement accuracy, reducing intraoperative complications and improving surgical outcomes.Keywords: Equivalence trial, Gertzbein-Robbins classification, MMG, Pedicle screw, tEMG

Introduction

Pedicle screw insertion is integral to spinal instrumentation and is widely employed for various spinal pathologies, including degenerative spinal conditions, spinal deformities, tumors, and trauma [1,2]. Despite various advantages, proximity to critical anatomic structures potentially increases the risk of complications namely nerve root and spinal cord injury, vascular injury, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak [3]. Previous studies have reported cortical screw breach rates in the thoracolumbar spine ranging between 1.7 % and 20%, highlighting the need for accurate techniques to mitigate these risks [4,5].

Triggered electromyography (tEMG) has been traditionally used to detect cortical breaches during pedicle screw placement [6]. However, its limited specificity, higher costs, reliance on operator expertise, and invasive methodology have partially limited its widespread clinical adoption [5,7]. Mechanomyography (MMG), a relatively newer approach, offers a promising alternative by measuring mechanical oscillations generated during triggered muscle contractions [8]. Compared to tEMG, MMG provides potential advantages, including its comparatively noninvasive nature, reduced susceptibility to interference, and surgeon-driven device management make it a potentially more practical tool for intraoperative use [7].

While previous work has highlighted the potential of MMG in detecting pedicle screw breaches, the present study for the first time prospectively assesses the relative accuracy of tEMG and MMG in detecting cortical breaches in the same patient cohort [7].

Materials and methods

A single-center equivalence trial was conducted at the University of Kentucky (IRB 76122). Consecutive adult patients undergoing posterior lumbosacral spinal arthrodesis were included in the study. Informed consent for trial participation of each participant was obtained by trained study personnel prior to enrollment. Decision-making regarding surgery was based on usual standard-of-care guidelines including failed nonoperative management for at least 3 months. Exclusion criteria included the presence of local wound issues, osteoporosis, active infection, foreign body sensitivity, implanted pacemakers, morbid obesity with body mass index (BMI) > 45 kg/m2, immunocompromised states, or use of medications potentially impairing wound healing.

Following enrollment, baseline demographic data, including age at surgery, sex, BMI, comorbidities, and prior surgical history, were collected for all included participants. Additionally, intraoperative findings, such as the number of levels involved, total trajectories, duration of surgery, and postoperative adverse events, were systematically recorded in prospective fashion.

In this equivalence trial, the null hypothesis states that tEMG and MMG differ in their ability to predict cortical pedicle breaches, meaning the 2 modalities are not equivalent in predictive accuracy. Our primary objective was therefore to reject this null hypothesis by demonstrating that tEMG and MMG have comparable predictive abilities, as measured by sensitivity, specificity, and area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUROC).

A power analysis determining the required sample size was conducted prior to the start of the trial to determine the required sample size. An alpha threshold level of 0.05 and power of 80% were used, with an assumed difference of 15% in the predictive ability of MMG versus tEMG in detecting cortical screw breaches, based on pilot data from 18 pedicle screws (data not shown). Based on these parameters, a total sample of 300 pedicle screws was estimated to be sufficient, and this target was met in the final analysis.

All participants included in the study underwent posterolateral fusion, during which a single spine surgeon exposed the facet joints and created bilateral pedicle screw trajectories at the targeted surgical levels, using either fluoroscopic (GE OEC Elite, Chicago, IL, USA) or navigation guidance (BrainLab, Munich, Germany). All pedicle screw trajectories were assessed intraoperatively using a standard tEMG device followed by the SentioTM MMG device (Depuy Synthes, Raynham, MA, USA). Measurements were recorded for the pretap and post-tap stages using ball tip probes. In cases involving thin-wall breaches or where surgical judgment deemed it necessary, the surgeon created new trajectories, and subsequent measurements were recorded for the revised trajectories.

Intraoperative computed tomography (CT) scans (Airo, Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) were performed to evaluate the pedicle trajectories in all cases. Breaches were classified using the Gertzbein-Robbins (GR) classification system as follows: Grade A, indicating a fully intra-pedicular screw with no breach; Grade B, screw exceeding the pedicle cortex by less than 2 mm; Grade C, exceeding the pedicle cortex by 2–4 mm; Grade D, exceeding the pedicle cortex by 4–6 mm; and Grade E, screw exceeding the pedicle cortex by more than 6 mm [9]. CT scans were independently evaluated by 2 reviewers (HA and HD) to identify medial or inferior cortical breaches at the index surgical levels. Both reviewers were blinded to the tEMG and MMG threshold potential results during their imaging review. Any discrepancies in the classification were resolved through consensus with the senior author (also blinded to the threshold potential results).

Functional assessments for all study participants were recorded at baseline (preoperatively) and at 6-weeks and 3-months follow-up. Pain levels in the back and leg were evaluated using the Numeric Rating Scale (NRS) [10], while disability was assessed using the Oswestry Disability Index (ODI) [11]. Additionally, the Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System - 10 (PROMIS - 10) Global was utilized to quantitatively record the overall physical and mental health of participants [11].

Descriptive statistics were implemented for the statistical analyses, with measures of central tendency, including mean and median, reported for continuous variables, and frequencies and percentages reported for categorical variables. Paired and unpaired t-tests were conducted to compare the mean values of continuous variables, while chi-square tests were used to assess statistical differences between categorical variables. The MMG device recorded values from 1-20 mA, with any readings exceeding 20 mA recorded as >20 mA. To facilitate statistical analysis, these unspecified values were assigned readings of 21 mA. The sensitivities and specificities of both tEMG and MMG for breach detection were assessed at the pretap and post-tap stages using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves. The accuracy of each modality was quantified as the AUROC curve and optimal detection thresholds were estimated from the ROC analysis. The DeLong test was used to assess differences in AUROC curves along the 2 trajectory stages [12]. A p-value <.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2 [13].

Results

A total of 303 pedicle trajectories were evaluated across 61 participants, with the number of trajectories for each participant ranging between 4 and 8 (1 to 3 segmental level surgeries). A total of 65 participants were initially enrolled in the study from a cohort of 92 patients screened for eligibility. 27 participants were excluded as per the eligibility criteria initially, 3 were withdrawn due to failure to perform combined intraoperative tEMG and MMG recordings, and 1 subject did not undergo an intraoperative CT (see Supplementary Fig. 1). The cohort had a male-to-female ratio of 32:29, with a mean age of 61.3 ± 9.7 years and an average BMI of 31.6 ± 6.9 kg/m². Among the included participants, 11 (18%) had diabetes mellitus type 2, 20 (32.8%) had hypertension, and 6 (9.8%) had chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) (Table 1).

Most participants underwent arthrodesis at 1 level (n = 39, 63.9%), followed by 2 levels (n = 19, 31.1%) and 3 levels (n = 3, 4.9%). The mean duration of surgery was 275.3 ± 77.4 minutes, and the average hospital stay was 4.0 ± 2.5 days. Additionally, 14 participants (23%) had a history of prior lumbar decompression surgery. Among the 303 pedicle screw trajectories, 2 blinded reviewers graded the screw trajectories using the GR classification, resulting in a total of 26 GR grade-based discrepancies (8.6%), but only one of which (0.3%) involved a discrepancy between a low (GR grade B) vs high grade (GR grade C-E) breach, which was subsequently resolved by the senior reviewer.

A total of 7 high-grade cortical breaches (GR grade C-E) were identified in 5 participants (8.2%), while 56 participants (91.8%) had no breaches. Amongst the cortical breaches, 5 were classified as Grade C (71.4%) and 2 as Grade E (28.6%). Additionally, 12 screw trajectories were classified as low GR grade B breaches, which were not included in the subsequent statistical analysis. Baseline demographics did not differ significantly between high-grade versus nonbreach/low grade breach groups (Table 1).

Functional outcomes were assessed at baseline, 6 weeks, and 3 months postoperatively. NRS back and leg scores were significantly higher at baseline and decreased at follow-up (p < .001). A similar improvement was observed in the ODIv2.1a scores (p < .001). Moreover, PROMIS-10 physical and mental subscores revealed significant improvements at follow-up timepoints (p < .001) (Table 2).

The relative tEMG and MMG sensitivities, specificities, and AUROC values (pretap 0.82 vs. 0.80, p = .442, and post-tap 0.71 vs. 0.79, p = .380) for breach detection were comparable (Table 3; also see Fig. 1, Fig. 2). No adverse events related to the research procedure were noted during the follow-up period. Further, no neurological or vascular adverse events were noted during the follow-up period in any of the participants with a high-grade cortical breach.

Discussion

For the first time, this study directly compares the relative accuracy of tEMG and MMG in detecting GR grades C-E cortical breaches during instrumented posterior lumbar arthrodesis procedures. A small proportion of trajectories demonstrated GR grades C-E breaches with no distinguishing baseline characteristics between participants with or without such intraoperative CT findings. Overall, significant improvements in functional outcomes were observed for all study participants. AUROC values for the pretap and post-tap trajectory stages showed comparable accuracy in detecting cortical breaches. These findings support the equivalence of these modalities in their overall diagnostic accuracy, indicating that both tEMG and MMG may be used interchangeably for breach detection during lumbar surgeries.

Intraoperative neurophysiological monitoring (IONM) has been widely implemented to reduce complications and reoperation rates associated with spinal surgeries. Techniques such as somatosensory evoked potentials (SSEP), motor evoked potentials (MEPs), and EMGs are commonly used to monitor patients intraoperatively.[14] While these techniques are commonly employed, their effectiveness can vary depending on clinical and intraoperative factors [15,16].

While the use of tEMG for pedicle screw placement and breach detection has been extensively studied, research on the accuracy of MMG for breach detection remains limited [[17], [18], [19]]. A systematic review by Mikula and colleagues assessed pedicle screw tEMG stimulations, reporting an overall sensitivity of 0.86, a specificity of 0.96, and an AUROC of 0.98 for detection of cortical breaches in the lumbar region. While our study demonstrated similar specificities to those reported in the review, the observed variability in the sensitivity and AUROC values could be attributed to several technical factors, including implementation of heterogenous methodologies and devices for current stimulation and data collection, differences in cortical breach assessment criteria, variations in pulse duration, and differences in employed tEMG conductors across studies. Despite this variability, the reproducible high specificity of tEMG reinforces its role in minimizing false positive cortical breach detection and thereby in improving intraoperative decision-making to potentially reduce the overall incidence of surgical complications [5]. tEMG electrodes detect signals above a baseline noise level but are susceptible to electrical interference in the surrounding environment, resulting in a potentially low signal-to-noise ratio during intraoperative use [20]. In contrast, MMG captures mechanical signals from muscle contractions following nerve stimulation, potentially yielding a comparatively higher signal-to-noise ratio. This advantage may thus allow MMG to achieve higher sensitivity at low signal thresholds [21]. However, in our study, we noted equivalent sensitivities for cortical breach detection by tEMG and MMG during both the pretap and post-tap trajectory stages, thus highlighting their comparable effectiveness and underscoring the intraoperative utility of both these modalities in identifying cortical breaches [22]. Despite equivalent predictive abilities, tEMG remains more widely used in current clinical practice as compared to MMG, likely owing to the lack of available standardized MMG devices and relative unfamiliarity with its intraoperative applications [7].

In a prior study, Zakaria and colleagues underscored the utility of MMG probes in identifying medial and inferior breaches, reporting combined sensitivities and specificities of 0.804 and 0.806, respectively. Their findings highlight the potential role of MMG in similarly detecting breaches prior to final screw insertion, thereby reducing the overall risk of nerve injury [7]. While our study used the same commercially available MMG device, the study methodologies differ in key aspects. Zakaria and colleagues reported the predictive ability of MMG during the pilot hole and screw stages, whereas our study evaluated MMG at the pre- and post-tap stages. Additionally, their methodology involved initiating stimulation at 15 mA and gradually lowering the current until the signal disappeared; in contrast, our study systematically assessed MMG current thresholds across a range starting from 0 and extending to >20 mA. Finally, screw breaches were only classified as a binary outcome with directional breach categorization but without any specification of the degree of breeching. We employed a graded classification system in our study, allowing for a more detailed assessment of breach severity [7,9].

Most studies evaluating the utility of tEMG in detecting cortical screw breaches have employed CT imaging as the preferred assessment methodology due to its superior sensitivity in identifying breaches in comparison to conventional radiographic techniques [5,23]. Further, our study stratified breaches into no/low-grade breaches (GR grade A-B) versus high grade breaches (GR grade C-E), to facilitate more precise estimation of threshold values and predictive abilities for detecting significant breaches, which could be associated with a higher incidence of associated neurological and vascular complications [24]. However, we did not observe any neurological or vascular complications during the follow-up period in our cohort, which could have potentially affected the threshold and the ROC analysis, owing to the relatively variable noise-to-signal ratio [5]. Future studies should test the validity of our findings by prospectively enrolling larger cohorts, potentially allowing for a more comprehensive comparison of the 2 modalities.

While tEMG has been widely used for pedicle screw breech assessments and for other intraoperative purposes, potential intraoperative roles for MMG are still under exploration. MMG offers several practical advantages over tEMG, including lower susceptibility to electrical interference from the surrounding environment, lower impedance variability, and enhanced user-friendliness [7,[25], [26], [27]]. These qualities may not only offer improved relative reliability of MMG, but they also mitigate the need for the presence intraoperatively of a neuromonitoring technologist, likely making MMG more cost-efficient and accessible to a wide variety of operating room settings. Recent studies have also pointed to potential roles for MMG in peripheral nerve decompression surgery, paraspinal muscle functional assessments, and minimally invasive approaches for nerve localization [[28], [29], [30]]. Beyond spinal surgery, other prospective diagnostic and therapeutic roles have been posited for MMG related to control of prosthetic limbs, assessment of respiratory muscle function, and in stratification of various neuromuscular disorders [[31], [32], [33]].

Our study harbors certain strengths including its prospective design, systematic assessment of intraoperative parameters and functional outcomes, and the use of a blinded review process for breach classification. However, the study is limited by its relatively small sample size, particularly in the breach group, which may affect the statistical power and generalizability of the findings. Additionally, the short follow-up period restricts the ability to assess for long-term outcomes and complications. Further, bone-specific factors such as bone mineral density can predispose to pedicle screw breaches, but these were not specifically assessed in the study. Future studies with extended follow-up periods may also be necessary to validate our findings and better gauge the clinical implications of cortical screw breaches.

Conclusion

Intraoperative tEMG and MMG demonstrate overlapping accuracy, specificity, and sensitivity in detecting cortical screw breaches, highlighting their equivalent diagnostic accuracy. These findings underscore the critical role of these intraoperative monitoring modalities in minimizing the risk of neural and vascular injuries and in potentially enhancing surgical safety and patient outcomes.
