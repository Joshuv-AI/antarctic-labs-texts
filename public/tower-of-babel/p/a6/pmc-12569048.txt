Experimental evaluation of alterations in crude oil properties induced by hydrochloric acid, reservoir rock powder, and surfactants
Authors: Mohsen Mohammadi Taghiabadi, Amir Hossein Saeedi Dehaghani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study investigates how hydrochloric acid (HCl) at different concentrations and reservoir rock powder (RRP), combined with two surfactants, cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB, cationic) and Tween 80 (nonionic), affect the physicochemical properties of crude oil. The parameters evaluated were interfacial tension (IFT), viscosity, emulsion droplet size, sludge formation, and chemical composition. Tests were conducted with HCl concentrations of 10%, 15%, 20%, and 28%, and with surfactants at the critical micelle concentration (CMC) and one-fifth of CMC. Higher acid concentrations promoted sludge, peaking at 28%. In acidic media, RRP induced asphaltene aggregation, which increased viscosity and IFT. FTIR confirmed chemical changes, showing growth of carbonyl bands linked to acid-oil reactions and polar asphaltene aggregation. These adverse effects were moderated by surfactants. CTAB at higher dosage (370 ppm) gave the greatest reductions in viscosity and IFT via strong electrostatic interactions, while Tween 80 achieved substantial reductions at lower dosage (100 ppm) through steric stabilization, with little added benefit at higher concentration. Emulsion stability was highest at 15–20% HCl, indicated by narrow, unimodal droplet size distributions with the lowest values. Between these, 15% HCl in seawater or diluted seawater offered the optimal balance by minimizing sludge, preserving interfacial properties, and enhancing emulsion stability. Overall, CTAB provided the strongest mitigation of RRP-induced effects under these conditions.Keywords: Hydrochloric acid, Reservoir rock powder, Surfactants, Physicochemical properties, Asphaltene sludge, EmulsionSubject terms: Chemistry, Engineering, Environmental sciences

Introduction

Crude oil recovery and processing efficiency depend strongly on physical properties such as interfacial tension (IFT), viscosity, emulsion stability, and asphaltene behavior1. Acidizing is a common well-stimulation method that increases reservoir permeability by dissolving mineral deposits and removing pore blockages, thereby enhancing hydrocarbon flow. While effective, acidizing can alter crude oil properties and promote asphaltene precipitation, making it essential to understand and manage these effects2–4.

The behavior of crude oil in response to environmental factors, such as exposure to acidic solutions, is critical for optimizing various operational processes in the petroleum industry. Acidic treatments with hydrochloric acid (HCl) have been widely employed in operations related to well stimulation, scale removal, and enhanced oil recovery (EOR). These treatments primarily aim to increase the permeability of reservoir rocks and, consequently, improve oil flow through porous media5,6. However, the use of acids at varying concentrations significantly alters the physical properties of crude oil. For instance, IFT and viscosity, which directly affect oil flow within a reservoir and emulsion stability, respectively, may be altered by acid-induced reactions7,8. One of the most pressing issues related to deposit formation in oil production is the precipitation of asphaltenes, which often causes severe blockages in flow lines and processing facilities. These high-molecular-weight components of crude oil tend to precipitate when destabilized, potentially due to changes in temperature, pressure, or chemical composition. Asphaltene precipitation can lead to several operational challenges, such as reduced production efficiency; therefore, effective management and mitigation strategies are essential9–11. Acidic conditions, particularly those involving high concentrations of HCl, tend to promote asphaltene deposition, thereby exacerbating operational difficulties. Such conditions destabilize asphaltenes and significantly impede the extraction and production of oil. Consequently, there is a strong need for effective mitigation strategies to reduce these adverse effects and ensure sustained productivity12–14.

Reservoir rock powder (RRP) is used in this study as a laboratory analogue for formation fines, small mineral particles released during acidizing or other stimulation treatments. These particles can migrate with the produced fluids, interact with crude oil components, and modify interfacial properties by adsorbing polar species such as asphaltenes and resins. Additionally, dissolution of reactive minerals in contact with acid can release multivalent ions (e.g., Ca2+, Mg2+), which promote asphaltene aggregation and emulsion destabilization. Investigating RRP-oil interactions under acidic conditions provides a more realistic simulation of field scenarios, where both chemical and particulate effects occur simultaneously.

Another critical issue, in addition to asphaltene deposition, is the formation and stability of emulsions during oil production. Surfactants, acid concentration, and environmental conditions are among the key factors influencing the stability of crude oil emulsions. The entrapment of oil droplets within aqueous phases results in emulsions that complicate processing and degrade oil quality15–17. Surfactants such as CTAB and Tween 80 effectively reduce IFT and alter surface wettability to make reservoir surfaces more water-wet; thus, they are widely applied in EOR. Other important factors affecting the stability and behavior of emulsions include pH and temperature, with high temperatures and acidic conditions having particularly notable effects. In general, synergistic effects from the combined use of surfactants with other agents enhance performance in reducing IFT and stabilizing emulsions. Various studies have demonstrated the essential role of surfactants in controlling the physicochemical properties of crude oil during acidizing treatments. These surfactants function by adsorbing at the oil-water interface, thereby reducing IFT and stabilizing emulsions18–22. In addition, surfactants prevent the accumulation of asphaltenes by adsorbing onto asphaltene surfaces, thereby reducing intermolecular interactions. Furthermore, they help retain asphaltenes in the oil phase by preventing sedimentation, thus improving fluid flow and significantly enhancing productivity in extraction operations6,23.

In 2023, Silin et al. investigated acid-in-oil emulsions and their physicochemical properties. According to their findings, HCl-based emulsions exhibit distinct characteristics compared to other types of emulsions. For example, they display high viscosity, enhanced stability, and small droplet sizes. In petroleum processing, such increases in viscosity and stability hinder the efficient separation of oil and water phases24. Abbasi et al.2022 examined the stability of acid emulsions during well stimulation with HCl and found that higher concentrations of HCl significantly enhance emulsion stability. They attributed this to stronger intermolecular interactions between the oil and acid phases, which make phase separation more difficult25. In 2005, Poteau et al. investigated the influence of pH on the stability and dynamic behavior of asphaltene aggregates at the oil-water interface. Their results demonstrated that pH variation enhances the surface activity of asphaltenes, thereby influencing emulsion stability26. Abdollahi et al.2014 studied the precipitation of acid-induced sludge in crude oil and found that increasing acid and iron ion concentrations lead to higher sludge formation. From a practical standpoint, they emphasized the need for careful control of these variables to avoid operational challenges during acid treatments27. Furthermore, Rane et al.2012 investigated the adsorption kinetics of asphaltenes at the oil-water interface. They showed that asphaltene concentration directly affects IFT and emulsion stability, ultimately impacting oil production rates28. Collectively, these works emphasize the importance of the chemistry of acid, surfactants, and asphaltenes in determining emulsion and interface behavior. However, most previous studies have focused either on molecular simulations, bulk precipitation tests, or simplified water-oil systems and have not considered the simultaneous role of acid, surfactants, and reservoir rock components in integrated conditions. This distinction identifies the gap that motivates the present investigation.

This study aims to investigate the physicochemical properties of crude oil, specifically IFT, viscosity, emulsion droplet size, and asphaltene sludge formation, under varying HCl concentrations (10%, 15%, 20%, and 28%). Additionally, the influence of reservoir rock powder (RRP), both in the presence and absence of surfactants (CTAB and Tween 80), will be examined. Unlike prior studies that have primarily focused on HCl-crude oil-surfactant systems without considering the mineralogical contributions of reservoir rock solids, this work introduces a novel coupling of RRP and surfactants under acidizing conditions. The dissolution of RRP minerals in acid releases reactive ions (e.g., Ca2+, Mg2+) and alters interfacial chemistry, potentially modifying asphaltene aggregation, emulsion stability, and sludge formation in ways not captured in conventional acidizing models. By systematically varying both salinity and surfactant type/concentration, this research identifies synergistic effects, a gap that has not been comprehensively addressed in the literature. Therefore, the primary objective is to evaluate the interactions among acid concentration, mineral dissolution, surfactants, and the physicochemical properties of crude oil to inform the development of more effective acidizing treatments in petroleum operations. The results are expected to offer practical guidance for improving oil mobility and reducing sludge formation, thereby increasing the efficiency of crude oil production.

Acid solution

Hydrochloric acid (HCl), a type of mineral acid, was used in this study as a stock solution of 30.2 wt% (weight%), with a density of 1.15 g/cm3. The pH of the stock solution was measured as 0.021 using a standard pH meter calibrated for pH 0–14. The stock solution was of analytical purity, and its density was measured using a pycnometer to confirm the concentration via standard density-concentration relationships. This solution was diluted to the target concentrations of 10, 15, 20, and 28 wt% using a weight-based mixing method with five types of base water: distilled water (DW), formation water (FW), seawater (SW), two-fold diluted seawater (SW2), and ten-fold diluted seawater (SW10). Dilutions were prepared by accurately weighing the HCl solution and base water using a precision balance (± 0.001 g) and mixing thoroughly to ensure homogeneity. For example, to prepare a 10 wt% HCl solution, 33.11 g of 30.2 wt% HCl stock was mixed with 66.89 g of base water to yield 100 g of solution.

This weight-based dilution approach ensures precise control of ionic composition and reproducibility of the diluted seawater samples.

Rock sample

The reservoir rock powder (RRP) used in this study was prepared from core samples obtained from an oil field in the Persian Gulf region, matching the source of the crude oil samples. The core samples were crushed and ground using a mill, then sieved to a particle size of < 75 μm to ensure uniformity and maximize surface area for acid-rock interactions. The RRP was characterized using X-ray fluorescence (XRF) analysis to determine its elemental composition, revealing a dominance of CaO (indicative of carbonate minerals) alongside minor amounts of SiO2, MgO, and other oxides, as shown in Table 3. The fine particle size and high carbonate content make the RRP representative of the reservoir’s reactive mineralogy during acidizing. For each experiment, 2 g of RRP was added to 23 cc of acid solution, which was then combined with 23 cc of crude oil, resulting in a total phase volume of 46 cc (1:1 oil-to-acid ratio). This RRP dosage was consistently applied across all experiments to ensure standardized acid-rock interactions under controlled laboratory conditions.

For each test, 23 cc of the acidic solution was added to a test tube, followed by the gradual addition of 23 cc of crude oil (1:1 volume ratio). This ratio was chosen based on both literature precedent and preliminary screening tests. Viscosity measurement

Viscosity measurements were performed using an Anton Paar rheometer, calibrated in accordance with ISO 17,025 standards for testing and calibration laboratories. The instrument is routinely maintained and calibrated by the official Anton Paar representative to ensure traceability and accuracy of results. All viscosity tests followed ASTM guidelines for kinematic viscosity determination of liquid petroleum products, adapted to the rotational rheometer configuration. Based on the nature and viscosity of the samples, the CC27 spindle was selected as the appropriate measuring tool. Measurements were conducted at 25 ± 0.1 °C with equilibration of samples for 15 min prior to testing. Viscosity was recorded over a shear rate range of 1–1000 s−1 using 300 linearly spaced data points, corresponding to an average increment of approximately 3.34 s−1 per point. This linear sweep provided uniform coverage across the shear rate range. For each run, raw data of viscosity, shear rate, and shear stress were collected and averaged over 300 points. Final values reported represent the mean of three replicate tests, with deviations within ± 4%.

IFT measurement

The drop shape analysis method, specifically the pendant drop technique, was employed in this study as a standard and reliable approach for measuring IFT. This method is particularly well-suited for evaluating changes in IFT of liquids exposed to different chemical environments, including acidic solutions. To accurately assess changes in IFT attributable to acid exposure, measurements were conducted on both untreated crude oil and acid-contacted oil samples in a distilled water environment. Distilled water was selected as the aqueous phase to provide a reproducible, ion-free baseline, ensuring that observed changes in IFT could be attributed solely to variations in HCl concentration, reservoir rock powder (RRP), and surfactant type, rather than variable ionic compositions of the brines (DW, FW, SW, SW2, SW10) used in acid preparation. This approach isolated the effects of acid treatment and RRP-surfactant interactions on the oil’s interfacial properties, as the brines indirectly influenced the oil during aging. The experimental setup consists of several key components, each contributing to the precision of the measurement process. One of the essential elements is the syringe injection pump, which allows for precise control of the droplet volume. A high-resolution CCD camera is integrated with the imaging system to capture and record the droplet profile accurately. The entire system is enclosed to maintain controlled test conditions and minimize external disturbances, thereby enhancing measurement accuracy. Image analysis was performed using specialized software that processes the captured droplet profiles and calculates IFT based on well-established computational algorithms. This integrated system enables high-precision and reproducible IFT measurements, which are critical for understanding the effects of acidification on crude oil properties. Measurements were conducted at 25 ± 0.1 °C. The instrument was calibrated against the known IFT of distilled water-air at 25 °C (72.0 ± 0.5mN/m) prior to each measurement session. Oil droplets were formed in a distilled water environment using a precision syringe pump. Crude oil droplets of approximately 5–7µL were formed at the tip of a syringe needle in the aqueous phase. Each droplet was equilibrated before imaging. Images were recorded every 5 s for a total of 60 s, and the equilibrium IFT values were reported. Every condition was tested in triplicate, and the uncertainty of IFT values was within ± 2mN/m (~ 4% of the measured value). High-resolution CCD images were analyzed using proprietary software to calculate IFT. A schematic of the setup is shown in Fig. 129–32.

Determination of emulsion droplet size

After aging, three samples of the interfacial emulsion were removed from each tube and placed on separate glass slides. Each section was covered with a standard slide and only slight pressure was applied to obtain a thin, uniform layer. Care was taken to avoid deformation and the sphericity of the droplets was confirmed by microscopic examination before imaging. The approach adopted ensured reproducibility and yielded consistent results across replicate experiments and multiple imaging fields. Microscopic images were taken at a fixed magnification to ensure clarity and clarity of the droplets. For scale calibration, a ruler image (scale 1 mm) was taken at the same magnification as the droplet samples. In ImageJ, this 1 mm length was converted to pixels and provided a calibration factor for all subsequent analyses. The droplet diameter was then measured in millimeters and converted to microns for reporting. To minimize errors due to scale variations, droplet boundaries were manually traced in ImageJ, rather than relying solely on automatic thresholding.

For each section, multiple fields of view were imaged, and more than 300 droplets per condition were measured in replicates. The results were exported to Excel, where normalized histograms of droplet size distribution were generated. The mean droplet diameter and droplet size distribution were used as the primary stability measures, as smaller mean droplet sizes are widely associated with higher emulsion stability. In addition, the width of distribution was reported as a complementary indicator of size uniformity. Emulsion droplet size measurements were performed in triplicate (n = 3) and are expressed as mean ± SD. The relative deviation was < 5%. The schematic of the visual study procedure is shown in Fig. 2.

The schematic of the visual study procedure for emulsion droplet size determination. Emulsion droplets from crude oil-acid mixtures were spread on a glass slide and imaged by optical microscopy. Droplet size distributions were obtained from at least 300 droplets using ImageJ.

Sludge measurement

After sampling the oil and interfacial layers, the remaining contents of the test tube were filtered through an acid-resistant 80-mesh sieve and left to drain for 25 min. The 25-minute settling period was selected based on preliminary sedimentation behavior, where shorter durations led to incomplete drainage of oil through the 80-mesh sieve. The collected residue was washed with n-heptane to remove residual crude oil, ensuring that the measured sludge primarily consists of insoluble asphaltene-based material, and to partially remove residual acid by displacing aqueous droplets. The retained sludge was dried at 70 ± 1 °C until constant weight (mass variation < 0.01 g between consecutive weighings). The dry sludge was weighed on an analytical balance (± 0.001 g). Sludge content measurements were performed in triplicate (n = 3) and reported as mean ± SD. The relative deviation was < 11%. An image of a sludge sample is presented in Fig. 3.

FTIR spectroscopy

Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) is a widely used technique for identifying molecular bonds and functional groups in organic and inorganic materials. It operates by measuring the absorption of infrared radiation by a sample, providing insights into its chemical structure. By analyzing the resulting infrared spectra, molecular interactions and functional group characteristics can be identified. In this study, FTIR was employed to investigate the chemical composition and functional group changes in crude oil samples treated with varying concentrations of HCl and selected additives. Spectra were recorded within the wavenumber range of 400–4000 cm⁻¹. The FTIR analysis focused on characteristic functional groups such as alkanes, aromatics, carbonyls, and hydroxyls, which offer insights into the chemical transformations occurring due to acid–additive interactions. The FTIR spectra were collected in transmission mode using KBr pellets with a PerkinElmer Frontier FTIR spectrometer (ISO-9001 certified, equipped with Automatic Performance Verification for routine self-checks). Peak areas were integrated within specific wavenumber ranges (Table 5) after baseline correction and normalization. Ten FTIR indices were calculated from the spectra to semi-quantitatively characterize structural features of crude oil and asphaltenes. These indices, expressed as peak area ratios of specific bands (Eqs. 1–10), include aromaticity, aliphaticity, and chain-length-related indices. Aromatic and aliphatic indices are widely used to differentiate hydrocarbon types, whereas aliphatic length indices reflect methylene to methyl ratios associated with branching and chain extension. These indices are semi-quantitative and dependent on peak assignment accuracy. therefore, they are better suited for comparative analysis across conditions rather than absolute structural quantification. Functional group variations were evaluated by comparing the intensities of peaks in the aliphatic and aromatic regions33–37.

Results and discussions

All experimental data presented represent the average of three independent replicates (n = 3). Error bars correspond to standard deviation (SD), which was consistently ~ 4% for viscosity and ~ 4% for IFT, and ~ 5% for droplet size, and ~ 11% for sludge measurements. These results confirm the high reproducibility and reliability of the reported data. During the experiments, parameters such as pressure, temperature, sampling, and methodology were held constant to ensure that observed changes were attributable solely to chemical alterations resulting from acid and additives exposure.

The experiments in this study were conducted under controlled laboratory conditions at ambient pressure and a constant temperature of 25 °C, except for the 24 h aging step performed at 70 °C to simulate reservoir thermal exposure. In actual field acidizing, pressures may exceed 20–40 MPa and temperatures can range from 60 °C to over 120 °C, depending on depth and reservoir type. Elevated temperatures accelerate acid-mineral reaction rates, modify the solubility of mineral dissolution products, and can alter asphaltene aggregation thresholds. Likewise, high pressures influence CO₂ solubility, interfacial tension, and emulsion stability, potentially shifting the balance between stabilization and coalescence mechanisms observed in this work. Surfactant adsorption and micellization are also temperature- and salinity-dependent, and their efficiency may vary under reservoir HPHT conditions.

Viscosity

Viscosity tests were conducted to evaluate changes in the chemical composition and physical properties of crude oil after aging in contact with various acidic solutions. During the experiments, parameters such as pressure, temperature, sampling, and methodology, which can significantly influence fluid viscosity, were held constant. This ensured that any observed changes in viscosity were attributable solely to chemical alterations resulting from acid exposure. This section discusses the viscosity of crude oil following contact with HCl at four different concentrations (10%, 15%, 20%, and 28%). Each concentration was tested in combination with five types of base water: DW, FW, SW, SW2, and SW10. The results are presented in Fig. 4.

Oil viscosity (mean ± SD, n = 3) after contact with HCl at different concentrations in five types of base water. Error bars represent the standard deviation of triplicate measurements (± 4%).

Increasing the concentration of HCl correlates with enhanced emulsion stability at the oil-water interface. These emulsions exhibit higher viscosity and greater resistance to flow due to the dispersion of fine oil droplets within the continuous aqueous phase. HCl modifies the interfacial properties between oil and water, promoting emulsion stabilization. Emulsions formed under such conditions typically possess elevated viscosity, particularly when the dispersed droplets are microscopically small. Acidic environments also influence molecular interactions among asphaltene molecules. At low pH, carboxyl and phenolic groups protonate, reducing the electrostatic repulsion that normally prevents aggregation. In addition, resin-asphaltene interactions, which contribute to steric stabilization, may be disrupted under highly acidic conditions, further promoting asphaltene precipitation. The type of base water used in acid formulation significantly affects crude oil behavior. DW, which contains few reactive ions, undergoes minimal interaction with HCl, resulting in limited changes in the crude oil’s chemical composition and viscosity. In contrast, FW, rich in ions and minerals, reacts with HCl to form salts and other compounds, substantially altering oil viscosity. Similarly, SW, which contains high chlorides, sulfates, sodium, and other ions, reacts with HCl to form complex ionic compounds. These reactions can destabilize emulsions and significantly influence viscosity41–43.

As shown in Fig. 4, oil viscosity increases with rising acid concentration. At 10% HCl, the viscosity ranges from approximately 850 to 1000 cP. When the acid concentration is increased to 15%, viscosity rises to approximately 1150–1450 cP. This upward trend continues at 20% and 28% concentrations, with some base water systems showing viscosities between 1400 and 1770 cP at the highest concentration. The relationship between viscosity and acid concentration is likely driven by physical aggregation of crude oil components, particularly asphaltenes, as supported by increased sludge formation observed in Sect. 3.3. Acid exposure, through protonation of polar functional groups (e.g., nitrogen and oxygen-containing groups in asphaltenes and resins) by HCl, destabilizes colloidal systems, promoting asphaltene aggregation and coagulation44. Mullins (2011) provides a broad review of asphaltene properties across various crude oils, without specific acidizing or salinity experiments, but the protonation-driven aggregation mechanisms described are transferable to our high-acid, low-ionic-strength conditions (e.g., SW10), where asphaltene content plays a dominant role similar to the general cases discussed. This is evidenced by the gravimetric sludge measurements (Sect. 3.3), which show a significant increase in sludge mass, consistent with viscosity increases, as evidenced by FTIR spectral changes (Sect. 3.5), leading to asphaltene aggregation that increases internal resistance to flow and, consequently, viscosity. Furthermore, acidic environments reduce asphaltene solubility by lowering pH and disrupting the balance between dispersion forces and van der Waals attractions, especially in asphaltene-rich regions where acid penetration is significant.

Apart from acid concentration, the type of base water also significantly affects oil viscosity. We observed that viscosity increases significantly in FW compared to DW, as shown in Fig. 3. FW, rich in divalent ions (Ca2+, Mg2+) as indicated by its composition (Table 2), likely promotes asphaltene flocculation through ionic bridging, as inferred from increased viscosity in ion-rich systems. These divalent cations may facilitate charge neutralization, enhancing aggregation and increasing viscosity. When combined with HCl, acidity further destabilizes the colloidal suspension and has a synergistic effect in increasing viscosity45. SW and its dilute forms contain varying amounts of ionic species such as Na+, Ca2+, Mg2+, and SO42−. SW and SW2 maintain ionic strength levels high enough to support aggregation through electrostatic screening and bridging of multivalent ions, especially at high acid concentrations. In SW10, a significant reduction in ionic content weakens these interactions. At low HCl concentrations, protonation alone cannot induce significant aggregation, resulting in low viscosity. However, at 28% HCl, we observed a sharp viscosity increase, which we hypothesize is due to protonation-driven asphaltene aggregation, though direct evidence via spectroscopic analysis is needed to confirm asphaltene structural changes. the protonation effect dominates, inducing strong asphaltene aggregation despite a low ion background46. DW does not contain any significant ions to support stabilization or destabilization of asphaltenes through electrostatic mechanisms. Therefore, the viscosity trend in DW reflects the net effect of HCl acidity. Viscosity increases steadily but moderately, with no evidence of the sharp spikes observed in ionic waters. This suggests that ion-mediated coagulation plays an important role in systems with FW and SW.

The data reveal a threshold acid concentration between 15% and 20% HCl, beyond which oil viscosity increases sharply, particularly in ion-rich environments. This threshold appears to correspond to a critical flocculation point, where asphaltene particles exceed their solubility limit or the stability afforded by electrostatic repulsion. In SW10, the delayed viscosity increase observed at 28% HCl confirms that while ionic species accelerate the onset of asphaltene aggregation, strong acidity alone is sufficient to induce comparable effects. The maximum viscosity (1774.933 cP) was recorded at 28% HCl in FW, more than double the viscosity measured at 10% HCl in SW10 (846.863 cP). This substantial increase underscores the synergistic interaction between acid concentration and base water ionic strength, with the highest viscosities occurring in high-salinity waters under highly acidic conditions. Overall, the results highlight the combined influence of two key parameters, acid concentration and base water composition, on crude oil viscosity. As acid concentration increases, heavier molecular structures are formed, contributing to viscosity growth. Additionally, base waters with higher salt content exacerbate this effect by facilitating asphaltene flocculation and aggregation. The error bars (± SD, n = 3) were consistently within 4%, corresponding to error ranges of 1710–1838 cP for FW, 1468–1578 cP for SW, and 1450–1540 cP for SW2 at 28% HCl. The viscosity differences between FW and SW (~ 237 cP) and between FW and SW2 (~ 261 cP) are significantly larger than the experimental error ranges (e.g., ± 64 cP for FW, ± 55 cP for SW, ± 45 cP for SW2), confirming that these differences are systematic and not due to experimental variability. The difference between SW and SW2 (~ 24 cP) is smaller and closer to the error ranges, indicating less distinction between these conditions.

Next, the effect of RRP and its controllers (CTAB and Tween 80) on viscosity is investigated. Two grams of RRP were added to an acidic solution containing 15% HCl and base water. Then, two surfactants, CTAB and Tween 80, were used in two concentrations (CMC concentration and one-fifth of the CMC concentration) to control the effect of the RRP. The results are shown in Fig. 5.

Oil viscosity (mean ± SD, n = 3) after contact with 15% HCl and 2 g of RRP, with and without surfactants at different concentrations in three types of base water. Error bars represent the standard deviation of triplicate measurements (± 4%).

Crude oil viscosity after acid exposure is strongly influenced by acid concentration, RRP presence, and surfactant concentration. CTAB, a cationic surfactant, binds to the RRP surface, preventing particle aggregation. It interacts with polar oil components, such as asphaltenes, modifying micellar structures. Tween 80, a nonionic surfactant, disperses particles and micelles, though its weaker interactions with RRP reduce its efficiency18.

As shown in Fig. 5, when only an acidic solution containing 15% HCl and 2 g of RRP was used, the viscosity values increased significantly. The viscosity of the samples in the presence of RRP reached approximately 1270 cP for DW, 1360 cP for SW, and 1290 cP for SW2. This increase resulted from strong interactions between RRP and crude oil components, forming cohesive aggregates that raised viscosity. XRF analysis of the RRP (Table 3) shows a dominant composition with CaO (55.124%), indicating a carbonate-rich material. After exposure to 15% HCl, the initial reaction involves the dissolution of CaCO3, the formation of CaCl2, and the release of CO2 gas. This process increases the ionic strength and releases calcium ions (Ca2+) that create negatively charged interfaces and enhance emulsions through flocculation, especially in high salinity environments such as SW. Minor components such as SiO2, MgO, and Fe2O3 may contribute to surface activity by providing weak surface charges or amphiphilic properties, although their effect is secondary. Cl2 and trace metals such as Sr and Zn also show ionic interaction potentials but play a lesser role. In surfactant-free systems, these factors generally explain the highest viscosity recorded.

According to Fig. 5, we observed the addition of CTAB to the acidic solution in the presence of RRP (2 g, 15% HCl) reduced viscosity significantly. At 74 ppm, CTAB lowered viscosity to 1054.207 cP (DW), 1208.582 cP (SW), and 1201.559 cP (SW2), corresponding to reductions of 16.9%, 11.1%, and 6.8% relative to the RRP-only baseline (1267.96 cP for DW, 1359.394 cP for SW, 1289.634 cP for SW2). At 370 ppm, CTAB further reduced viscosity to 946.331 cP (DW), 930.007 cP (SW), and 1007.652 cP (SW2), indicating superior performance at higher concentrations. This suggests that CTAB, as a cationic surfactant, effectively binds to negatively charged RRP particles and oil-water interfaces, preventing aggregation and disrupting emulsion formation through electrostatic repulsion, though this mechanism requires validation through surface charge measurements. The enhanced reduction at 370 ppm likely results from stronger surfactant layers that inhibit droplet bridging, with more uniform effects in DW and SW2, while SW’s divalent cations may enhance CTAB’s stability47,48. While Rosen and Kunjappu (2012) offer a general textbook overview of surfactant interfacial phenomena without specific crude oil or acidizing contexts, and Mulligan et al. (2001) review surfactant-enhanced soil remediation (focusing on contaminated soils rather than reservoir rocks or asphaltenic crudes), the electrostatic binding and stability mechanisms in saline environments are analogous to our system, where divalent cations (e.g., Ca2+ from RRP dissolution) and acidic pH enhance CTAB’s performance despite differences in matrix and chemistry.

Tween 80, tested at 100 ppm and 500 ppm, showed notable viscosity reduction at the lower concentration, reaching 1001.11 cP (DW), 1133.97 cP (SW), and 1014.338 cP (SW2), corresponding to reductions of 21.0%, 16.6%, and 21.3%, respectively. At 500 ppm, reductions were less pronounced (1060.857 cP for DW, 1020.577 cP for SW, 1020.378 cP for SW2), indicating diminished effectiveness at higher doses. Tween 80’s non-ionic nature likely limits its interaction with RRP particles at high concentrations, reducing its ability to prevent aggregation. At 100 ppm, Tween 80 outperforms CTAB (average reduction of 19.6% vs. 11.6%), with viscosity differences (e.g., ~ 53–275 cP) exceeding the experimental error ranges (e.g., DW: ±27.98 cP for RRP-only, ± 30 cP for CTAB 74 ppm, ± 41.5 cP for Tween 80 100 ppm; SW: ±54 cP, ± 32.5 cP, ± 52.5 cP; SW2: ±35 cP, ± 58 cP, ± 38.5 cP), confirming the robustness of Tween 80’s performance at low concentrations. Tween 80 stabilizes emulsions via steric hindrance, forming a hydrophilic layer around droplets. This mechanism is supported by Sect. 3.4 (Figs. 6 and 7), where Tween 80 at 100 ppm yields smaller droplet sizes (6.10 μm in DW, 6.55 μm in SW, 6.73 μm in SW2) and narrower distributions compared to CTAB at 74 ppm (10.15 μm in DW, 12.20 μm in SW, 13.72 μm in SW2), indicating enhanced resistance to coalescence. In acidic solutions with calcite-dominated RRP (55.12% CaO, XRF analysis), Tween 80 adsorbs to particle surfaces and oil-water interfaces, reducing IFT and possibly forming micelles that aid oil dissolution. Salinity effects, particularly in SW with Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺, may compress Tween 80’s hydrated layers, moderating its effectiveness, while DW and SW2 show less ionic interference47.

The findings confirm that both CTAB and Tween 80 mitigate viscosity increases caused by RRP. Tween 80 is more effective at low concentrations (100 ppm), achieving 4.1–14.5% greater reductions than CTAB (74 ppm), with differences well above the experimental variability (error ranges ~ ± 30–58 cP). IFT

This section investigates the IFT of crude oil after exposure to HCl at four different concentrations: 10%, 15%, 20%, and 28%. Each acid concentration was tested in combination with five types of base water: DW, FW, SW, SW2, and SW10. The initial IFT of the crude oil before acid contact was 35.87 mN/m. The results of the IFT measurements under varying conditions are presented in Fig. 8. At high acid concentrations, the intensity of chemical reactions increases significantly, leading to substantial alterations in crude oil composition. Under such conditions, polar compounds may undergo transformation or decomposition into less polar species, which exhibit a reduced affinity for the oil-water interface. As a result, IFT increases sharply. Moreover, elevated acid concentrations lower the pH, further contributing to structural and chemical changes in the oil. Highly acidic environments can also degrade or modify surfactant molecules, reducing their surface activity and thereby contributing to a marked rise in IFT49.

Interfacial tension (IFT, mean ± SD, n = 3) of oil after contact with HCl at different concentrations in five types of base water. Error bars represent the standard deviation of triplicate measurements (± 4%).

At 10% HCl (Fig. 8), IFT decreases significantly in ion-rich base waters (FW, SW, SW2, SW10) compared to untreated crude oil (35.87 mN/m), with values as low as 15.32 mN/m in FW. This reduction is attributed to the enhanced surface activity of naturally occurring amphiphilic compounds, such as asphaltenes and resins, which is supported by FTIR analysis (Sect. 3.5) showing increased carbonyl and polar group absorption at 10% HCl, indicative of polar species contributing to interfacial activity. Divalent cations (Ca2+, Mg2+) in ion-rich waters likely further lower IFT by stabilizing interactions between these compounds and the oil-water interface through ionic bridging. At 15% HCl, IFT increases in most systems (e.g., 43.36 mN/m in DW), suggesting that higher acidity reduces the effectiveness of these amphiphilic compounds, potentially by altering their charge or solubility50. In high-salinity waters (FW, SW, SW2), ionic strength may buffer pH changes, maintaining lower IFT compared to DW, which lacks stabilizing ions and shows a pronounced IFT increase, indicating a less favorable interfacial structure under higher acidity.

Above 15% HCl, IFT generally increases across most base waters, likely because high acidity reduces the effectiveness of naturally occurring amphiphilic compounds, such as asphaltenes and resins, possibly by altering their surface activity. At 20% HCl, this trend is evident, with DW reaching a high IFT of 56.94 mN/m due to the absence of stabilizing ions, which exacerbates the loss of interfacial activity. Notably, SW10 shows a slight IFT reduction at 20% HCl (34.79 mN/m) compared to 15% HCl (36.45 mN/m), likely due to its moderate ionic strength facilitating compression of the electrical double layer at the oil-water interface, as described by DLVO theory. This compression enhances the surface activity of amphiphilic compounds, such as asphaltenes and resins, by reducing electrostatic repulsion and stabilizing their adsorption at the interface, thereby lowering IFT. At 28% HCl, IFT peaks in DW (58.28 mN/m), indicating that highly acidic conditions, without stabilizing ions, significantly impair interfacial properties. In ion-rich waters (FW, SW, SW2), divalent cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺) likely mitigate this effect, maintaining relatively lower IFT values.

The IFT recovery observed in SW10 at 20% HCl can be explained through of DLVO theory, which accounts for the balance between van der Waals attraction and electrostatic repulsion at the oil-water interface. In SW10, the moderate concentration of ions, including Na+, Ca2+, and Mg2+ (Table 2), compresses the electrical double layer surrounding polar molecules like asphaltenes and resins. This compression reduces the repulsive forces between these molecules, allowing them to pack more efficiently at the interface, thus enhancing interfacial film stability and reducing IFT. In contrast, higher ionic strengths in FW and SW may overly compress the double layer, leading to reduced surface activity at higher acid concentrations. The moderate ionic strength of SW10 likely strikes a balance, optimizing the interfacial behavior at 20% HCl.

The results suggest that IFT is minimized within a 10–15% HCl concentration range in ion-rich base waters for the tested crude oil, likely due to enhanced activity of naturally occurring amphiphilic compounds, such as asphaltenes and resins, which reduce IFT under moderate acidification. Above this range, IFT generally increases, possibly because high acidity reduces the effectiveness of these compounds, particularly in DW, which shows a continuous IFT rise from 38.35 mN/m at 10% HCl to 58.28 mN/m at 28% HCl due to the absence of stabilizing ions. In contrast, FW exhibits the lowest IFT values (e.g., 15.32 mN/m at 10% HCl, 42.74 mN/m at 28% HCl), likely due to divalent cations (Ca2+, Mg2+) stabilizing interfacial interactions, possibly through ionic bridging or charge screening. SW and its dilutions (SW2, SW10) also show lower IFT than DW, with SW10 achieving a significant reduction at 10% HCl (21.45 mN/m) due to its moderate ionic strength. However, SW10’s IFT fluctuates at higher concentrations (e.g., 36.45 mN/m at 15% HCl, 34.79 mN/m at 20% HCl), likely reflecting its sensitivity to ionic strength variations that affect the activity of amphiphilic compounds. SW and SW2 maintain more stable IFT values (e.g., 30 mN/m and 20 mN/m at 10% HCl, rising to 48 mN/m and 55 mN/m at 28% HCl), likely due to their higher ionic buffering capacity. In ion-rich waters, divalent cations may support interfacial stability, while in DW, the lack of ions exacerbates IFT increases under high acidity.

Overall, IFT increases with rising acid concentration, with DW showing the highest values due to the absence of ions, while FW and SW systems benefit from divalent cations that enhance IFT reduction. The standard deviations (± 4%) were small relative to the observed shifts in IFT, indicating that the differences across HCl concentrations and water types are statistically robust and reproducible.

In this section, the effect of RRP and its controllers (CTAB and Tween 80) on the IFT of oil is investigated. The results are shown in Fig. 9. CTAB is a cationic surfactant with a positively charged hydrophilic head and a long hydrophobic tail. When added into the system, the cations in CTAB are adsorbed on the surface of RRP using electrostatic interactions between the head of CTAB and the negative or polar groups on the rock powder surface. Thereby, this interaction produces a hydrophobic coating on the surface of the rock particles. Once formed, this layer of CTAB impedes the adsorption of the polar compounds in the oil phase onto the particles to such an extent that they do not reach the rock powder surface; hence, IFT will not increase. On the other hand, Tween 80 forms stable emulsions between the oil and water phases. Polar compounds are trapped in these emulsions and cannot reach the interface. Therefore, the presence of polar compounds at the interface is reduced, and thus, an increase in IFT is prevented18.

Interfacial tension (IFT, mean ± SD, n = 3) of oil after contact with 15% HCl and 2 g of RRP, with and without surfactants at different concentrations in three types of base water. Error bars represent the standard deviation of triplicate measurements (± 4%).

According to the results presented in Fig. 9, we observed that In the absence of surfactants, the IFT values ​​increased significantly compared to the baseline, reaching 79.37 mN/m (DW), 73.98 mN/m (SW), and 75.71 mN/m (SW2). This indicates that contact with the acidic RRP slurry facilitates the transfer of polar components to the crude oil phase and increases its interfacial energy. We hypothesize that RRP alone is not sufficiently dispersed and lacks surface activity, resulting in minimal changes in interfacial properties, but this needs confirmation through dispersion analysis (e.g., particle size distribution measurements). This increase may be due to the repulsion or dissolution of mineral ions and acid-soluble components from the rock powder into the aqueous phase, followed by their partial migration into the crude oil during aging. An increase in the content of polar or surface-active compounds in the oil phase, including possibly dissolved acidic or metallic species, leads to instability at the interface51. Hirasaki et al. (2011) investigated surfactant-enhanced oil recovery in non-acidic systems, focusing on low-salinity brines (TDS ~ 5,000 ppm vs. our SW ~ 46,000 ppm) and lighter crudes (API ~ 35° vs. our ~ 19°). While their study did not involve RRP or acidizing, their discussion of polar compound migration to interfaces is relevant to our RRP-induced IFT increase, where carbonate dissolution (CaO 55%, Table 3) introduces additional ionic effects51.

XRF analysis of the rock sample revealed a dominant CaO content (55.124%), which indicates a high carbonate content. CaCO3 undergoes an acid-base reaction upon contact with HCl to form calcium chloride (CaCl2), carbon dioxide (CO2), and water. This reaction not only releases a gas that can excite the interface but also introduces Ca2+ ions that change the ionic strength and can migrate into the oil phase and potentially form complexes with organic acids or asphaltenes, thereby increasing the polarity and IFT of the oil. Significant CO₂ release may also cause transient interface instabilities. We infer that minor components such as MgO, SO3, Cl, Fe2O3, Zn, and Sr further impact interface behavior, with iron and zinc complexing with carboxylic groups to enhance interfacial polarity52. Buckley and Fan (2007) measured IFT in crude oil–brine systems without acidizing, using lighter crudes and lower-salinity brines. Their findings on cation-induced polarity increases are applicable to our RRP-dissolution effects, where Ca²⁺ from carbonate (Table 3) enhances interfacial instability under acidic conditions52.

Adding CTAB, a cationic surfactant, significantly reduced the IFT values. At a concentration of 74 ppm, the IFT decreased to 52.44 mN/m (DW), 57.42 mN/m (SW), and 66.20 mN/m (SW2). Increasing the CTAB concentration to 370 ppm further reduced the IFT to approximately 51.94 mN/m (DW), 51.66 mN/m (SW), and 50.82 mN/m (SW2). The reduction in IFT is attributed to the ability of CTAB to adsorb onto negatively charged RRP particles, increasing their dispersion and potentially modifying the crude oil-water interface by limiting the migration of acidic or inorganic components into the oil phase. At higher concentrations, CTAB may interact directly with acidic components and asphaltenes in the oil phase, forming complexes that stabilize the oil-water interface. Its quaternary ammonium group can interact with anionic species, such as inorganic anions, reducing their mobility in the phases. The performance of CTAB is relatively sensitive to salinity, and higher ionic strength (such as SW and SW2) affects micelle formation and adsorption properties47.

Tween 80, a nonionic surfactant, significantly reduced IFT, particularly at higher concentrations. At 100 ppm, IFT values decreased to 62.59 mN/m (DW), 53.47 mN/m (SW), and 51.78 mN/m (SW2). At 500 ppm, IFT further decreased to 47.81 mN/m (DW), 49.57 mN/m (SW), and 45.80 mN/m (SW2), the lowest values recorded in this study. This pronounced reduction, especially in SW2, is likely due to a combination of steric stabilization and micelle-induced solubilization. Tween 80’s polyoxyethylene chains form a protective barrier at the oil-water interface, preventing undesirable migration of surface-active species, while at 500 ppm, micelles likely solubilize hydrophobic oil components, such as asphaltenes and resins, reducing their interfacial accumulation and further lowering IFT. The nonionic nature of Tween 80 enhances its resilience in high-salinity environments, contributing to the lower IFTs observed in SW and SW2.

The IFT values ​​varied with the type of base water used. DW generally showed higher IFT values ​​at all surfactant conditions, likely due to its lack of ionic strength, which limits surfactant efficiency and RRP dispersion. In contrast, SW and SW2 enhanced surfactant performance, probably due to the effects of salt that enhance micelle formation and surface activity. The lowest IFT was observed in the SW2 system with 500 ppm Tween 80, indicating a synergistic effect between lower salinity and optimal surfactant concentration. Salinity plays a dual role: it affects the electrostatic interaction of surfactants with solid and liquid phases and modifies the solubility and aggregation behavior of surfactant molecules. SW2, having lower salinity than SW, may offer an optimal balance between ionic strength and surfactant mobility. This could facilitate better surfactant adsorption at interfaces and prevent premature aggregation in the bulk phase.

Generally, if used alone in the crude oil, HCl, and RRP system, the addition of CTAB and Tween 80 decreases the rise in IFT. These surfactants reduce IFT by coating the surface of rock powder particles, minimizing the adsorption of polar compounds, and forming stable emulsions. For the RRP-surfactant systems, error bars (± 4%) confirmed that the significant reduction in IFT caused by CTAB and Tween 80 is consistent across replicates and not attributable to measurement scatter.

Sludge

Sludge formation during acidizing operations in oil reservoirs presents operational challenges, including formation damage and reduced permeability. In this study, sludge quantification was performed gravimetrically after thorough washing with n-heptane, ensuring that the measured mass primarily consisted of insoluble asphaltene-rich material with possible inorganic residues. While detailed compositional or morphological analyses (e.g., XRD, SEM-EDS) were not conducted in this work, FTIR analysis of treated oil (Sect. 3.5) indicated increased polar functionalities, supporting the hypothesis that sludge primarily comprises aggregated asphaltenes. This study reviews sludge due to the interaction of crude oil and acidic solutions containing up to four concentrations of HCl. The results are shown in Fig. 10.

Sludge weight (mean ± SD, n = 3) resulting from contact of crude oil with HCl at different concentrations in five types of base water. Error bars represent the standard deviation of triplicate measurements (± 11%).

One of the complex reactions when crude oil and HCl are mixed results in sludge formation. Increasing the HCl concentration increases sludge formation due to the protonation of polar compounds in crude oil, such as asphaltenes, resins, and heteroatom species containing nitrogen, sulfur, and oxygen. This protonation reduces the solubility of these compounds in the oil phase, destabilizes colloidal structures, and causes precipitation. Some base water contains cations like calcium (Ca2+) and magnesium (Mg2+), which react with HCl and precipitate as insoluble compounds. These compounds serve as nucleation sites for heavier materials to aggregate onto, such as asphaltenes and resins present in crude oil. HCl also creates emulsions with crude oil and base water. These are the unstable emulsions that, over time, will eventually become destabilized into sludge with accumulated solids. HCl also reacts with organic chemical compositions contained in crude oil, specifically asphaltenes, and resins, leading to the creation of insoluble substances that will later start forming sludge. Asphaltenes are large, polar molecules that are normally stabilized by resins in crude oil. Moreover, by adding a strong acid to the solution, like HCl, the solution pH drops, thus destabilizing these asphaltenes11,44,53,54. Houchin et al. (1990) studied asphaltene precipitation in field contexts without HCl, using crudes with asphaltene contents. Their insights on asphaltene destabilization are relevant to our acidizing conditions, though our high-salinity FW/SW enhances cation-driven nucleation11. Mullins (2011) provides a general asphaltene behavior model, applicable to our protonation-driven sludge formation, despite lacking specific acidizing data44. Almubarak et al. (2021) investigated sludge in acidizing with oil-based muds, differing from our asphaltene-rich crude, but their findings on acid-induced precipitation align with our results53.

Increasing effects associated with increased concentration of HCl on the quantity of sludge produced are denoted. It reveals that as the acid concentration increased from 10% up to 28%, the sludge amount for most base waters-in particular FW and SW- rises to maximum values, as shown in Fig. 10. This feature indicates that concentrated acid solutions have a higher possibility of exerting the sludge-promoting effect of acid because they show strong reactions with various compounds contained in crude oils and base waters. The highest concentrations of acid and chemical contents of oil and water produce more sludge at concentrations of about 20–28% concentration.

The type of base water significantly influences the volume of sludge produced. Base waters with high salt content, such as SW and FW, result in greater sludge formation. In contrast, DW, which lacks dissolved ions, consistently produced the lowest amount of sludge under all test conditions (Fig. 10). According to the data, sludge formation in DW increased from 0.25 g at 10% HCl to 2.3 g at 28% HCl. This trend reflects a direct relationship between acid concentration and sludge formation in the absence of excess ions. In this case, sludge generation is attributed solely to the protonation of crude oil components by HCl. The lack of ionic species means that acid-induced precipitation is not enhanced by electrostatic interactions or cationic bridging. As a result, sludge mass remains relatively low even at high acid concentrations. These findings underscore the synergistic role of acid strength and ionic content in promoting severe sludge formation.

FW consistently produced the highest sludge mass across all acid concentrations compared to other base waters. This behavior is attributed to its high ionic strength (3.94 M, Table 2) and elevated concentrations of divalent cations such as Ca2+ (0.253 M) and Mg2+ (0.0323 M), which serve as bridging agents between negatively charged asphaltene sites, promoting aggregation55. SW also exhibited a relatively high rate of sludge formation due to its ionic content (ionic strength 0.80 M, Table 2), with sludge mass increasing progressively from approximately 0.6 g at 10% HCl to 1.4 g at 28% HCl. This gradual rise supports the role of dissolved ions, particularly Ca2+ (0.0147 M) and Mg2+ (0.0517 M), in enhancing sludge formation through electrostatic screening and ionic bridging. In contrast, SW2 (ionic strength 0.40 M) produced 0.75 g of sludge at 10% and 15% HCl, increasing slightly to about 0.85 g and 1.3 g at 20% and 28% HCl, respectively. This modest reduction in sludge compared to undiluted SW reflects the halved ionic strength and divalent cation concentrations (Ca2+: 0.00735 M, Mg2+: 0.02585 M), which limit aggregation. Similarly, SW10 (ionic strength 0.08 M) showed further reduced sludge formation due to its lower cation concentrations (Ca2+: 0.00147 M, Mg2+: 0.00517 M), underscoring the role of reduced ionic strength in mitigating asphaltene aggregation. DW, with negligible ionic strength (< 0.001 M), consistently produced the lowest sludge mass (0.25 g at 10% HCl to 2.3 g at 28% HCl), reflecting the absence of ionic contributions to aggregation. These findings align with low-salinity water effect studies, which demonstrate that lower ionic strength reduces asphaltene aggregation by minimizing ionic bridging and electrostatic screening5,46.

SW and its dilutions showed transitional behavior. At lower dilutions (SW), ionic strength is still significant and promotes ionic bridging similar to FW, albeit to a lesser extent due to the lower concentrations of divalent ions. SW2, with half the salinity of SW, shows reduced sludge at 15% HCl, indicating a threshold effect where ionic bridging is less effective at moderate salt levels. However, sludge formation increases with higher HCl concentrations, suggesting that acid strength begins to dominate at reduced ionic strengths. SW10, the most dilute SW, shows minimal sludge formation at low acid concentrations. The weak ionic environment provides minimal support for aggregation, and only when the acid concentration is sufficiently high (20% and above) does sludge formation increase significantly. This further supports the hypothesis that at lower ionic strength, acid-induced destabilization must be very strong to overcome the lack of an ionic bridge. SW10 behaves similarly to DW at low HCl concentrations but deviates upwards at 20% and 28% HCl. This may be due to the onset of asphaltene instability after reaching a critical protonation level, especially when the ionic strength, although low, is not zero. Therefore, a synergistic mechanism involving partial ion and protonation effects may explain this phenomenon.

According to Fig. 10, the highest weight of the sludge corresponds to 28% HCl combined with FW, while the lowest weight of the sludge has been captured with 10% HCl and DW. As a result, increasing the concentration of acid, especially in base waters with a higher aggregate of dissolved ions, favors the formation of sludge. The sludge was highest in FW and SW but decreased sharply in diluted SW. Despite the heterogeneous nature of sludge formation, the error margins (± 11%) remained sufficiently low to confirm that the increasing sludge trends with higher HCl concentrations are reliable.

In the continuation, the impact of RRP and its controllers, CTAB and Tween 80, on the sludge amount was evaluated. The results are shown in Fig. 11.

Sludge weight (mean ± SD, n = 3) resulting from contact with 15% HCl and 2 g of RRP, with and without surfactants at different concentrations in three types of base water. Error bars represent the standard deviation of triplicate measurements (± 11%).

In the absence of surfactants, introducing RRP into acidified crude oil systems significantly increases sludge formation, which can be directly attributed to the mineralogical properties of the rock powder. The high CaO content (55.124%) indicates a calcite-rich composition. Under acidic conditions (15% HCl), calcite readily reacts with HCl, releasing Ca2+ ions into solution, which are well known to cause bridge-like flocculation of crude oil components, especially asphaltenes and acid moieties, through electrostatic interactions46. This mechanism leads to sludge coagulation and sedimentation. Experimental results confirm this effect; the sludge in DW, SW, and SW2 were 1.01, 1.83, and 1.43 g, respectively. These numbers show that SW often results in more sludge because SW has more salt and ions. The powdery nature of RRP creates additional nucleation surfaces where the interaction between the acidified crude oil and the rock minerals increases the formation of interfacial films. These films can stabilize emulsions that resist coalescence and separation, further enhancing sludge deposition56. This explains why sludge accumulation is greatest when using RRP without surfactants. While the concentrations of other components such as MgO (0.356%), Cl− (0.166%), and SO3 (0.138%) are relatively low, they may still contribute to the overall ionic environment and affect emulsification and interfacial behavior. In particular, Mg2+ can similarly cause the aggregation of oil components, although to a lesser extent than Ca2+.

In conclusion, the surfactant-free case suggests that RRP enhances sludge formation through three main mechanisms: ionic bridging, emulsion stabilization, and interfacial film formation on reactive surfaces. These findings emphasize the necessity of adding surfactants (such as CTAB or Tween 80) to reduce these effects and prevent formation damage during acidizing operations.

The addition of 74 ppm of CTAB resulted in a substantial reduction in sludge formation. In DW, SW, and SW2, sludge mass was reduced to 0.72 g, 0.85 g, and 0.69 g, respectively, compared to baseline values without CTAB. As a cationic surfactant, CTAB likely adsorbs onto the surface of RRP particles, preventing their aggregation. At a higher concentration of 370 ppm, CTAB further suppressed sludge formation across all base water types, reducing sludge mass to approximately 0.3 g. This indicates that CTAB is significantly more effective at higher dosages. At this concentration, CTAB forms a coating on particle surfaces, neutralizes negative surface charges, and inhibits the electrostatic interactions that drive particle flocculation. Moreover, CTAB is known to strongly adsorb onto negatively charged colloidal surfaces, including those of RRP particles and acidic crude oil molecules, thereby suppressing electrostatic attraction and stabilizing dispersion. CTAB also likely disrupts the oil-water interface by forming a surfactant monolayer, altering IFT, and preventing the development of a rigid interfacial film, which is often a precursor to sludge formation47.

In contrast, Tween 80, a nonionic surfactant, acts through a different mechanism. It reduces surface tension through steric hindrance rather than electrostatic interactions. At a concentration of 100 ppm, Tween 80 showed limited effectiveness, particularly in saline conditions, which could be due to insufficient surface coverage or competitive adsorption with ions at the oil-water interface. The sludge values for DW, SW, and SW2 were determined to be 0.73, 1.22, and 1.03 g. Tween 80 is a non-ionic surfactant that reduces surface energy, preventing the clustering of particles and resulting in lower sludge formation. Increasing the concentration to 500 ppm improved sludge reduction, up to 0.51–0.52 g in SW and SW2, suggesting that higher concentrations are needed to achieve an effective interfacial barrier in high ionic strength environments. However, even at a concentration of 500 ppm, Tween 80 did not match the performance of 370 ppm CTAB, indicating the superior ability of cationic surfactants to disrupt charge-induced interactions essential for sludge formation in such systems.

The addition of surfactants CTAB and Tween 80 significantly mitigated sludge formation in the presence of 2 g reservoir rock powder (RRP) at 15% HCl across different base waters (DW, SW, SW2). Specifically, CTAB at 74 ppm reduced sludge mass by 28.7% (from 1.01 g to 0.72 g) in DW, 53.6% (from 1.83 g to 0.85 g) in SW, and 51.7% (from 1.43 g to 0.69 g) in SW2, demonstrating high efficiency at low concentrations. In contrast, Tween 80 at 500 ppm achieved reductions of 53.5% (from 1.01 g to 0.47 g) in DW, 71.6% (from 1.83 g to 0.52 g) in SW, and 64.3% (from 1.43 g to 0.51 g) in SW2, requiring significantly higher concentrations for comparable or slightly greater reductions. Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis (Sect. 3.5) confirms the adsorption of both surfactants on RRP, with CTAB-treated RRP exhibiting characteristic peaks for C-H stretching (approximately 2800–3000 cm⁻¹) and quaternary ammonium groups, indicative of electrostatic adsorption onto negatively charged RRP and asphaltene surfaces. Tween 80-treated RRP shows peaks for C = O (approximately 1700 cm⁻¹), supporting its steric stabilization mechanism. These results indicate that CTAB, as a cationic surfactant, is more effective at lower concentrations, facilitating coating and dispersion, while Tween 80, a non-ionic surfactant, requires higher concentrations for similar effects.

Another important factor influencing sludge formation is the type of base water used. In all cases, SW produced more sludge than DW and SW2, likely due to its higher ionic strength and salt content, which promote particle aggregation and sludge accumulation. In contrast, DW and SW2, with lower ion concentrations, exhibited reduced sludge formation potential. Notably, in samples treated with 370 ppm of CTAB, sludge formation remained relatively constant across all base waters (DW, SW, and SW2), suggesting that the influence of salinity diminishes when electrostatic interactions are effectively suppressed by the surfactant. In contrast, samples without surfactant and those treated with Tween 80 showed marked variation in sludge mass between DW and SW, highlighting the continued importance of electrostatic interactions in salt-rich environments when charge-neutralizing agents like CTAB are absent or less effective.

In RRP-containing systems, the error bars (± 11%) demonstrated that sludge reduction achieved by CTAB and Tween 80 is reproducible, verifying that these effects are robust and not experimental artifacts.

Emulsion droplet size

This section examines the particle size distribution of emulsions formed from the interaction between crude oil and HCl at four different concentrations (10%, 15%, 20%, and 28%). Each concentration was tested in five types of base water: DW, FW, SW, SW2, and SW10. A microscopic view of the emulsion samples is shown in Fig. 12. The mean droplet size of the emulsion and the droplet frequency distribution are shown in Figs. 13 and 14, respectively.

Microscopic views of emulsion samples, (a) HCl 10%, (b) HCl 15%, (c) HCl 20%, (d) HCl 28%.

Average emulsion droplet size (mean ± SD, n = 3) resulting from contact of crude oil with HCl at different concentrations in five types of base water. Error bars represent the standard deviation of triplicate measurements (± 5%).

Size distribution of emulsion droplets resulting from contact of crude oil with HCl at different concentrations.

Emulsion stability in this study is quantitatively assessed based on the mean droplet size and the width of the droplet size distribution, as determined by optical microscopy and ImageJ analysis. Smaller mean droplet sizes and narrower, unimodal size distributions are indicative of higher emulsion stability, as they reflect reduced droplet coalescence and enhanced interfacial film strength. These metrics were chosen due to their direct correlation with emulsion stability and the experimental feasibility within the scope of this study. While direct measurements such as creaming half-life or Turbiscan stability index were not performed due to equipment limitations, the droplet size distribution provides a reliable proxy for stability, as smaller, uniformly distributed droplets are less prone to coalescence and phase separation. Emulsion stability depends on the viscoelasticity of the interface, and asphaltene accumulation increases the viscoelasticity; hence, it enhances the stability of the emulsions by preventing droplet coalescence. HCl is a strong acid and, therefore, ionizes completely in water; this results in H + and Cl- ions. In salt- and ion-rich waters (i.e., SW), H + can react with certain cations to form quite a variety of salts, some of which may bear on either the structure of the emulsion or stability or both. The stabilization of emulsions in the system is a function of the change in surface tension, ionic interactions, and the strength of the surface layer. Moderate acidification increases the surface activity of natural surfactants present in crude oil, allowing them to form stable surface layers. In addition, the presence of electrolytes in crude oil reduces surface tension and enhances adsorption kinetics by eliminating electrostatic repulsive forces. At high acid concentrations, the interface may become oversaturated or chemically unstable due to strong protonation and possible chemical degradation of surface agents. The resulting weakening of the surface layer leads to coagulation and an increase in droplet size54,57–61.

According to Figs. 13 and 14, for all base waters, an initial decrease in droplet size occurs from 10% to 20% HCl, followed by a slight increase at 28% HCl. This indicates an optimal acidity range that enhances interfacial film formation and emulsion stability. Moderate acid concentrations contribute to the partial protonation of natural surfactant species, such as asphaltenes and resins, which enhances their amphiphilic behavior and facilitates their aggregation at the oil-water interface. These interfacial films reduce coagulation, producing smaller and more stable droplets. At higher HCl concentrations (28%), the interfacial behavior changes due to excessive protonation, leading to a decrease in the elasticity of the interfacial film and increased droplet coagulation. Excessive acidity has been shown to disrupt emulsion stability by removing or neutralizing interfacial agents. This emphasizes the importance of maintaining chemical equilibrium at the interface.

Analysis of Figs. 13 and 14 indicates that at a HCl concentration of 10%, the emulsion particle size is relatively large. The droplet diameter within this concentration ranges between 15 μm (FW) and 19 μm (DW). It has a mean droplet diameter of 16.86 μm, thereby demonstrating the limitations of this concentration in achieving a reduction in droplet size. This results from the reduced acid concentration, which leads to a weaker force between the aqueous and oil phases, thus resulting in lower stability. At a HCl concentration of 15%, the particle size is smaller than in the previous case because of the increased acid concentration. The average droplet size is 15.055 μm, which is considerably lower than that at a 10% concentration. At this specific concentration, the stability of the emulsion is improved since the interactions among the smaller particles become stronger, which hinders phase separation. The 20% HCl solution yielded the lowest average droplet size among all the concentrations studied and had droplet sizes ranging from 11 μm (SW) to 15.29 μm (DW). The average droplet size was 12.89 μm, the lowest value recorded. The ability to form smaller and more stable particulates is thus increased with elevated acid levels. In this case, the balance between the forces is maintained effectively. When the concentration of HCl reaches 28%, the noticeable increase in the concentration of acid can cause adverse effects like the coagulation of the particles, leading to larger particle formation and instability of the emulsion. In this case, the stability of the emulsion could be reduced compared to the case of 20% concentration. At the specified concentration, the droplet size ranges from 14.826 μm (SW2) to 17.114 μm (DW). The average droplet size was calculated to be 16.16 μm, which indicates a further increase in droplet size compared to the case of 20% concentration. Higher acid concentrations can disturb stable emulsions and make them unstable. Thus, a concentration of 15–20% HCl is considered the most optimum for obtaining stable emulsions with tiny particle sizes.

As shown in Fig. 13, the composition of the aqueous phase used for HCl dilution significantly affected the emulsification results. DW, free of ionic content, consistently produced the largest droplets at all acid concentrations, with a mean droplet size of 19 μm at 10% HCl. In contrast, FW and SW, rich in electrolytes, produced much smaller droplets, especially at 15% and 20% HCl, with mean droplet sizes as low as 11 μm in SW at 20% HCl. The ionic composition of SW, as detailed in Table 2, includes significant concentrations of divalent cations such as Ca2+ and Mg2+, which play a critical role in enhancing emulsion stability. These divalent cations compress the electrical double layer at the oil-water interface, reducing interfacial tension (IFT) and enabling more efficient packing of interfacial films52. Additionally, Ca2+ and Mg2+ form ionic bridges between negatively charged functional groups, such as carboxylates in asphaltenes and resins, strengthening the viscoelastic interfacial film and inhibiting droplet coalescence57. This mechanism explains the smaller droplet sizes and narrower distributions observed in SW and FW compared to DW. For instance, at 10% HCl, the droplet size in FW (15 μm) is significantly smaller than in DW (19 μm), reflecting the higher ionic strength and divalent cation content in FW. SW, with a balanced ionic profile, also promotes stable emulsions, though its slightly lower divalent cation content compared to FW results in marginally larger droplets. In diluted SW (SW2 and SW10), the reduced concentrations of Ca2+ and Mg2+ lead to a gradual increase in droplet size, as fewer ions are available to support ionic bridging and double-layer compression. This confirms that salinity, particularly the presence of divalent cations, is a pivotal factor in modulating emulsion stability by enhancing interfacial film strength and reducing droplet coalescence.

The droplet size frequency curves (Fig. 14) support the interpretation of the average droplet data. Narrow, unimodal distributions were observed for emulsions at 15% and 20% HCl, indicating improved emulsion stability and resistance to coagulation. In contrast, 10% and 28% HCl resulted in broader, multimodal distributions, indicating dispersion and instability due to incomplete coating or coagulation during storage. Stable emulsions are usually associated with small droplet sizes, narrow distributions, and interfacial layers that resist coagulation.

These layers resist coalescence, resulting in smaller, more uniform-sized droplets. The slight shift observed in 28% HCl, where the peak shifts to about 8 μm, suggests a destabilizing mechanism, possibly due to excessive protonation. In contrast, 20% HCl shows a nearly unimodal distribution with a narrow peak and minimal secondary modes. The width and shape of the droplet size distribution are crucial indicators of emulsion stability. Narrow, unimodal distributions, observed at 20% HCl, correspond to systems where strong interfacial layers effectively suppress droplet coagulation. In contrast, broad, multimodal distributions at 10% and 28% HCl suggest inadequate stabilization mechanisms, leading to higher droplet populations and decreased emulsion stability. The droplet size frequency data reveal a nonlinear, concentration-dependent relationship between acid strength and emulsion stability. At 10% HCl, insufficient activation of interfacial agents leads to moderate stabilization and visible coagulation. At 15–20% HCl, optimized interfacial layers through protonation and ionic interactions result in a high-frequency, small-sized droplet distribution with minimal coagulation. At 28% HCl, excessive acidity destabilizes the interface structure, increasing droplet size heterogeneity. These results confirm the existence of an optimal acid concentration range (15–20% HCl) for maximum emulsion stability.

The deviations (± 5%) were modest compared to the large changes in droplet size, confirming that the optimal stability observed at 15–20% HCl is a genuine trend rather than experimental noise.

This section investigates the effect of RRP and its controllers (CTAB and Tween 80) on emulsion particle size. A microscopic view of the emulsion samples is shown in Fig. 15. The graphs in Figs. 6 and 7 show the average emulsion droplet size and the abundance of emulsion droplet sizes in this section, respectively.

Microscopic views of emulsion samples, (a) HCl 15% + 2 gr RRP, (b) HCl 15% + 2 gr RRP + 74 ppm CTAB, (c) HCl 15% + 2gr RRP + 370 ppm CTAB, (d) HCl 15% + 2 gr RRP + 100 ppm Tween 80, (e) HCl 15% + 2gr RRP + 500 ppm Tween 80.

We observed that RRP increases emulsion droplet size (e.g., from 18.006 μm to 20.029 μm in DW with 2 g RRP, Fig. 6), indicating that RRP adsorbs surface-active compounds such as asphaltenes and resins, thereby reducing their concentration in the oil bulk. This depletion increases the IFT and destabilizes the emulsion, leading to droplet enlargement. RRP particles interact with emulsion droplet surfaces due to their surface properties, disrupting the balance between repulsive and attractive interfacial forces. This disturbance promotes emulsion instability and accelerates the coalescence of smaller droplets into larger ones. RRP can also act as a physical “bridge” between droplets, facilitating their aggregation and increasing average droplet size. The addition of cetyltrimethylammonium bromide (CTAB), a cationic surfactant composed of a positively charged hydrophilic head and a long hydrophobic tail, mitigates these effects. CTAB preferentially adsorbs onto negatively charged RRP surfaces or those containing oxygenated functional groups. This adsorption alters the surface charge of RRP particles, increasing electrostatic repulsion and preventing particle aggregation. CTAB forms a stable coating on the particle surface, thereby reducing their mechanical bridging effect and preserving emulsion stability. As a result, emulsion droplet size remains small, and coalescence is suppressed. Tween 80, a nonionic surfactant, acts directly at the oil-water interface by forming a protective film over the oil droplets. We hypothesize that this layer reduces IFT and counters RRP destabilization by lowering surface free energy, but interfacial rheology studies are needed to confirm film formation. By lowering surface free energy, Tween 80 prevents droplet coalescence and size increase. Additionally, by competing with RRP for adsorption at the droplet surface, Tween 80 preserves the presence of native surfactants (e.g., asphaltenes and resins), which further stabilizes the emulsion18,62. Kumar and Mandal (2016) studied surfactant effects in alkali/salt systems without RRP or HCl, using a lighter crude. Their findings on Tween 80 stabilization are relevant to our results, though our acidic and high-salinity conditions amplify asphaltene-driven destabilization18.

As shown in Figs. 6 and 7, the addition of RRP to acidic solutions significantly increased the average droplet size of the emulsion, indicating a destabilizing effect on the emulsion. When 2 g of RRP was added to 15% HCl solutions, the average droplet size increased from 18.006 μm to 20.029 μm in DW, from 14.613 μm to 18.154 μm in SW, and from 15.49 μm to 18.002 μm in SW2. This increase can be attributed to the coalescence of the emulsion droplets caused by the presence of fine rock particles. Solid particles without surfactants can act as emulsion destabilizers by increasing droplet coalescence through film drainage and rupture at the oil-water interface63. Furthermore, the size distribution curve (Fig. 7) shows a broader and more right-sloping peak, confirming the formation of larger emulsion droplets in the presence of RRP. Mechanistically, this effect is rooted in physical and chemical interactions between RRP and the emulsion system. XRF analysis (Table 3) indicates that the rock sample is primarily composed of calcium oxide (CaO: 55.124%), consistent with the presence of carbonate minerals, such as calcite. Upon reaction with HCl, these minerals release calcium ions, which both contribute to destabilization. Calcium ions (Ca2+) compress the electrical double layer around the droplets, reducing repulsive forces and promoting cohesion64. Israelachvili (2011) provides a general theoretical framework for interfacial forces, not specific to acidizing or our crude (~ 10% asphaltenes). However, the double-layer compression mechanism is directly applicable to our high-salinity, Ca²⁺-rich system post-RRP dissolution64. In general, in the absence of surfactants, RRP acts as a coagulation agent, reducing emulsion stability by screening surface charge and disrupting droplet integrity.

As shown in Figs. 6 and 7, CTAB, a cationic surfactant, stabilized emulsions and significantly reduced droplet size. At a concentration of 74 ppm CTAB, the average droplet size in DW was decreased to 10.15 μm, in SW to 12.20 μm, and in SW2 to 13.72 μm. These results indicate partial stabilization, but relatively large droplets indicate an unfavorable surfactant concentration or surface coverage. Increasing the concentration to 370 ppm significantly increased stabilization and produced some of the smallest droplets recorded, 4.15 μm in DW, 3.94 μm in SW, and 3.40 μm in SW2, indicating that a threshold concentration is necessary for effective surface adsorption and emulsion stabilization. Mechanistically, CTAB molecules are adsorbed at the oil-water interface, with their hydrophobic tails anchored in the oil phase and their positively charged head groups extending into the aqueous phase. This configuration results in strong electrostatic repulsion between adjacent droplets, which prevents coagulation. The effectiveness of CTAB was particularly prominent in low-salinity water (SW2), probably due to the reduction in ionic screening, which allows the electrostatic barrier to remain intact and maximizes the repulsion between droplets65,66. Rahevar et al. (2023) studied controlled salinity water flooding with lighter crudes and lower salinity, but their findings on electrostatic repulsion in surfactant systems are relevant to our CTAB stabilization, enhanced by Ca2+ from RRP dissolution65. The frequency distribution plot (Fig. 7) shows that 370 ppm CTAB produces a sharp peak, confirming the formation of a uniform and fine emulsion, indicating a stable emulsion system.

Tween 80, a nonionic surfactant, was also effective in reducing emulsion droplet size and exhibited a clear concentration-dependent effect. At 100 ppm, average droplet sizes were 6.10 μm in DW, 6.55 μm in SW, and 6.73 μm in SW2. Increasing the concentration to 500 ppm led to further reductions: 4.63 μm (DW), 5.00 μm (SW), and 5.22 μm (SW2), indicating improved emulsion stability at higher concentrations. Unlike CTAB, Tween 80 stabilizes emulsions via steric hindrance rather than electrostatic repulsion. Its polyoxyethylene chains form a physical barrier around oil droplets, preventing close approach and coalescence. This steric mechanism is inherently less sensitive to ionic strength, which explains the consistent performance of Tween 80 across DW, SW, and SW2. The droplet size distribution shown in Fig. 7 confirms this behavior. At 500 ppm, Tween 80, like CTAB at 370 ppm, produced a narrow size distribution, indicative of strong emulsifying action and stable droplet formation. Notably, the smallest droplet size with Tween 80 was observed in DW rather than SW2, in contrast to CTAB’s behavior. This result aligns with the nonionic nature of Tween 80, whose adsorption and emulsification performance is minimally affected by salinity.

When comparing the two surfactants, CTAB at a concentration of 370 ppm achieved the smallest average droplet size across all base waters, suggesting that under optimal conditions, electrostatic stabilization may provide better emulsion control. However, Tween 80 at a concentration of 500 ppm provided nearly equivalent results with greater uniformity across different salinities, making it more robust to field conditions where water composition may vary. Furthermore, CTAB performance was more concentration-dependent, as indicated by the significant difference between 74 ppm and 370 ppm, whereas Tween 80 showed a more gradual and predictable trend from 100 ppm to 500 ppm. This suggests that nonionic surfactants may be preferred for operations where precise surfactant concentration control and maintaining constant water chemistry are challenging. For emulsions with RRP and surfactants, the error bars (± 5%) indicate that the observed differences between CTAB and Tween 80 are consistently reproducible, reinforcing the mechanistic interpretation.

FTIR analysis provides good information about the chemical changes in crude oil before and after its contact with HCl at different concentrations and in the presence of additives. FTIR is an effective method for identifying functional groups by analyzing characteristic absorption peaks in the infrared spectrum. In this study, FTIR was used to investigate the structural and compositional changes in crude oil samples exposed to different concentrations of acid (10, 15, 20, and 28%) and additives including surfactants CTAB and Tween 80. Key functional groups such as alkanes, aromatics, carbonyls, and hydroxyls were investigated to understand how reactions with acid, surfactant interactions, and asphaltene behavior affect the physicochemical properties of the oil. This section discusses the observed spectral changes and highlights the role of acid strength and additives in changing the molecular structure and stability of crude oil (According to Table Five and Eqs. 1–10).

Moderate acid concentrations (e.g., 10%) appear to support the retention or formation of S = O groups, while strongly acidic conditions (≥ 20%) promote their chemical breakdown. This underscores the importance of acid concentration control in maintaining the functional group integrity of sulfur-rich crude oils.

As shown in Figs. 16 and 17b, the FTIR spectra of crude oil samples reveal significant changes in absorption intensity within the carbonyl region (1656–1816 cm−1). In untreated crude oil, minimal absorption was observed in this range, indicating a low initial concentration of carbonyl-containing compounds. This observation is consistent with the calculated indices: aliphatic index (C = O) of 0.0157, aromatic index of 0.2164, and total C = O index of 0.0033. Treatment with 10% HCl resulted in a marked increase in carbonyl absorption, suggesting substantial formation of carbonyl groups under mildly acidic conditions. The corresponding indices increased significantly: aliphatic index to 0.1507, aromatic index to 1.9687, and total C = O index to 0.0329, indicating enhanced chemical reactivity in this region. At 15% and 20% HCl, absorption in the carbonyl region declined, accompanied by reductions in all indices. The aliphatic indices dropped to 0.0378 and 0.0255, while aromatic indices decreased to 0.3664 and 0.4066, and total C = O indices to 0.0111 and 0.0060, respectively. This trend suggests that moderate acid concentrations suppress the formation or stability of carbonyl species, likely due to shifts in reaction pathways or degradation of intermediates. Interestingly, treatment with 28% HCl led to renewed carbonyl absorption, with corresponding increases in the indices: aliphatic (0.1143), aromatic (1.0486), and total (0.0200). This resurgence suggests that under strongly acidic conditions, alternative reaction mechanisms may be activated, promoting carbonyl formation through different oxidative or rearrangement pathways. Overall, these results indicate a nonlinear relationship between acid strength and carbonyl group formation in crude oil. Peak reactivity occurred at 10% HCl, decreased at intermediate concentrations (15% and 20%), and partially recovered at 28% HCl. This behavior underscores the importance of acid concentration optimization in applications where carbonyl formation may affect downstream processes such as emulsion stability, wettability, or sludge formation.

As shown in Figs. 16 and 17c, the polar aliphatic index increases significantly at 10% HCl (0.2426), indicating enrichment of aliphatic functional groups under mildly acidic conditions. In contrast, higher acid concentrations, particularly 15% HCl (0.0487) and 20% HCl (0.0400), result in a marked decrease in this index. This reduction suggests acid-induced fragmentation of aliphatic hydrocarbon chains. These observations are consistent with the trend in the aliphatic length index (Fig. 17d), which also decreases notably at higher acid concentrations, e.g., 0.4534 at 20% HCl, indicating a shift toward shorter-chain hydrocarbons. This supports the hypothesis that stronger acidic conditions promote the chemical breakdown of long-chain aliphatic structures into smaller molecular fragments. A similar trend is observed in the polar aromatic index (Fig. 17c), which is highest at 10% HCl (3.1694), suggesting that polar aromatic hydrocarbons are initially stabilized or enriched under mild acid treatment. However, this index drops sharply at 15% HCl (0.4716) and 20% HCl (0.7782), indicating degradation or solubilization of aromatic ring structures at higher acid concentrations. These findings imply that low acid concentrations help preserve aromatic functional groups, whereas higher acidity leads to their chemical disruption. Additionally, the overall polar index, likely associated with polar functional groups such as hydroxyls, decreases progressively with increasing acid concentration, from 0.0529 at 10% HCl to 0.0115 at 20% HCl. This trend suggests a depletion of polar groups due to chemical reactions or emulsification processes at the oil-water interface. The decline in the polar index is particularly relevant to interfacial properties, as it likely contributes to reduced emulsion stability by diminishing the concentration of surface-active polar compounds. Consequently, higher acid concentrations may weaken emulsifying behavior and lower IFT, thereby affecting oil recovery efficiency.

These results suggest that while both surfactants contribute to preserving aliphatic chain length, CTAB is more effective, particularly at optimal concentrations, likely due to its stronger interaction with hydrophobic components of the crude oil.

Overall, the relative FTIR spectra and associated numerical indices confirm the degradative influence of RRP on crude oil under acidic conditions. This degradation is evident in the loss of aliphatic and aromatic functional groups, particularly carbonyl- and hydroxyl-containing species. The introduction of surfactants, CTAB and Tween 80, effectively mitigates these effects and contributes to the preservation of chemical integrity. Specifically, CTAB at lower concentrations demonstrates greater efficacy in stabilizing aliphatic structures, as reflected by increases in aliphatic indices and length indices. In contrast, Tween 80 is more effective in maintaining or restoring aromatic carbonyl functionality and enhancing overall chemical equilibrium, particularly at higher concentrations. Qualitative interpretation of the FTIR spectra supports the quantitative index analysis. Notable variations in the broadband region at 3100–3450 cm−1, attributed to O-H and N-H stretching vibrations, reflect changes in polar functional group content, in line with shifts in polar aliphatic and polar aromatic indices. Similarly, trends in the C-H stretching region (2900–3000 cm−1) align with changes in the aliphatic length index, suggesting that surfactants contribute to the stabilization of aliphatic hydrocarbon chains. Additionally, a reduction in peak intensity in the aromatic C = C stretching region (1600–1700 cm−1) post-acid treatment corresponds with declines in the polar aromatic index, further confirming the susceptibility of aromatic systems to chemical degradation under strongly acidic conditions. These findings underscore the importance of surfactant selection and concentration in preserving the functional group composition of crude oil, particularly during acidizing treatments in reservoir environments.

The findings of this study underscore the critical role of acid treatment in altering the physicochemical properties of crude oil. The observed reduction in aromatic content and loss of polar functional groups confirm the crude oil’s susceptibility to acid-induced degradation, particularly under strong acidic conditions. However, the application of surfactants, especially Tween 80, presents a promising strategy to mitigate these adverse effects. At higher concentrations (500 ppm), Tween 80 effectively stabilized polar and aromatic functional groups, preserved overall polarity, and supported the retention of aliphatic chain length. These results suggest that surfactant-assisted acidizing treatments can improve chemical stability and potentially EOR outcomes in reservoir environments.

Conclusion

This study systematically investigated the influence of hydrochloric acid (HCl), reservoir rock powder (RRP), and two surfactants (CTAB and Tween 80) on the physicochemical properties of crude oil. The results confirmed that acid concentration, type of base water, and surfactant chemistry are critical in determining viscosity, interfacial tension (IFT), sludge generation, emulsion stability, and molecular-level transformations.

Increasing HCl concentration consistently intensified sludge formation and viscosity growth, particularly in ion-rich environments such as seawater and formation water. The presence of RRP amplified these effects due to mineral dissolution and ionic release, highlighting the importance of accounting for solid-fluid interactions in acidizing treatments. Surfactants modified these outcomes: CTAB, owing to its cationic nature, showed strong ability to suppress sludge and viscosity increases when used at higher concentrations (370 ppm), while Tween 80 proved more effective at lower concentrations (100 ppm), particularly in stabilizing aromatic functionalities and reducing IFT. These results demonstrate that CTAB is better suited for controlling particle-induced flocculation and viscosity growth, whereas Tween 80 primarily enhances interfacial stability through steric hindrance. The study also identified that CTAB reduced sludge formation by up to ~ 70% and lowered viscosity by ~ 30% under RRP-rich acidic conditions, while Tween 80 reduced IFT to as low as ~ 45 mN/m in seawater systems at 500 ppm.

Emulsion stability analysis indicated that 20% HCl produced the smallest average droplet size and narrowest distribution, signifying maximum emulsion stability. Nevertheless, this concentration also led to substantial sludge formation and viscosity increase. By contrast, 15% HCl, especially in seawater or its two-fold dilution, provided a more balanced outcome by minimizing sludge accumulation, moderating viscosity, and sustaining favorable IFT values. Accordingly, the 15–20% HCl window emerges as the most suitable operational range, with 20% favoring maximum stability and 15% offering the best trade-off between stability and adverse side effects.

FTIR spectroscopy confirmed that acid-oil-surfactant interactions produced measurable chemical transformations. Protonation under acidic conditions altered hydroxyl, carbonyl, and aromatic functional groups, while CTAB promoted better retention of aliphatic at higher concentrations. Tween 80, by contrast, enhanced stabilization of aromatic carbonyl features. These molecular-level insights complement the macroscopic observations and explain the differential effectiveness of the surfactants.

It is important to emphasize that the definition of “optimal” presented here reflects only laboratory-scale physicochemical performance. The practical translation of these conditions to field acidizing operations must consider additional parameters such as corrosion risks, chemical costs, operational safety, and environmental impacts. While 15% HCl in seawater or SW2 demonstrated the most promising laboratory outcomes, comprehensive field validation remains necessary.

Future work should therefore investigate a wider range of surfactant chemistries, including more environmentally benign and cost-effective alternatives, under dynamic flow conditions and extended timescales. Furthermore, the coupling of acid-rock interactions with reservoir heterogeneity, scaling, and corrosion management strategies should be addressed to ensure safe, efficient, and economically viable application of the proposed treatments in petroleum operations.
