Self-compassion and self-efficacy among parents of children with autism: the mediating role of trust and the moderating role of social support
Authors: Dongchen Xu, Jiejia Chen, Hanyu Chen, Chuanyu Wu, Zhiheng Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundParents of children with autism often face significant challenges in the parenting process. Self-compassion and self-efficacy serve as crucial psychological resources that contribute to their mental well-being. While existing research has focused on self-compassion and self-efficacy, the psychological mechanisms through which self-compassion influences self-efficacy in parents of children with autism remain to be further explored. This study aims to investigate the relationship between self-compassion and self-efficacy among parents of autistic children, examine the mediating role of trust in this relationship, and assess the moderating effect of social support on the pathway from self-compassion to trust.MethodsIn this study, 400 parents of children with ASD from several rehabilitation institutions for autistic children were recruited in Jiangsu and Zhejiang Provinces in mainland China. Data were collected using a questionnaire method that included the Self-Compassion Scale–Short Form, the Parental Self-Efficacy Scale, the General Trust Scale, and the Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support.Results(1) Self-compassion positively predicts self-efficacy in parents of children with ASD; (2) trust partially mediates the relationship between self-compassion and self-efficacy; and (3) social support moderates the relationship between self-compassion and trust.ConclusionThis study advances understanding of the psychological mechanism underlying parental self-efficacy among parents of children with ASD by integrating self-compassion, trust, and social support within a unified framework. The findings suggest that self-compassion functions as an internal psychological resource that facilitates parents’ sense of efficacy by fostering a more trusting orientation, which helps them cope with parenting challenges more adaptively. Moreover, perceived social support plays a crucial contextual role by shaping the conditions under which self-compassion translates into trust, indicating that external support systems strengthen the effectiveness of self-compassion in the early stage of this psychological process. Together, these results highlight the interactive contribution of internal and external resources in promoting parental self-efficacy and underscore the importance of considering both psychological and social factors when developing interventions for parents of children with ASD.

Introduction

Parents of children with autism commonly experience high levels of psychological stress during long-term caregiving [1, 2]. Existing studies indicate that self-efficacy, as a crucial psychological resource for parents to cope with parenting challenges, plays a significant role in their psychological adaptation and caregiving behaviors [3]. However, due to factors such as intensive, long-term caregiving demands and uncertainties regarding the child’s future development, the self-efficacy of these parents may face ongoing challenges and be at risk of declining. Therefore, the mechanisms underlying the maintenance and development of self-efficacy among parents of children with autism in the context of chronic stress warrant further investigation. In recent years, self-compassion has received increasing attention as an important intrinsic psychological resource [4, 5]. It emphasizes treating oneself with understanding, acceptance, and a non-judgmental attitude when facing difficulties or failures, and is considered beneficial for coping with stressful situations and promoting the development of positive psychological functioning. Existing research has also identified a positive correlation between self-compassion and self-efficacy [6]. However, current studies have largely focused on the direct association between these two variables, leaving the underlying mechanisms by which self-compassion influences self-efficacy among parents of children with autism within complex social contexts insufficiently explained.

Against this background, trust provides a crucial perspective for understanding the psychological mechanisms through which self-compassion influences self-efficacy. Trust reflects an individual’s belief in the goodwill, reliability, and predictability of others and the social environment, which helps reduce the sense of threat and enhance psychological security in uncertain situations [7, 8]. Therefore, this study introduces trust as a mediating variable between self-compassion and self-efficacy. The open, accepting, and non-defensive self-attitude embodied by self-compassion may make parents of children with autism more receptive to feedback and professional guidance from others, thereby enhancing their trust in others. This increased trust, in turn, is likely to strengthen their perception of the effectiveness of their own parenting behaviors, thereby promoting the development of self-efficacy. Furthermore, the formation of trust is not solely driven by individual internal factors but is also influenced by external social environments.As a significant external resource, social support, which can be provided through emotional, informational, and instrumental means, may shape individuals’ cognitive evaluations of others in social interactions. Consequently, this study further proposes that social support may moderate the relationship between self-compassion and trust. In parents with higher social support, self-compassion is more likely to translate into positive expectations of others and the environment. Conversely, in contexts where social support is insufficient, the facilitative effect of self-compassion on trust may be constrained.

Based on the above analysis, this study constructs a moderated mediation model to examine how self-compassion influences the self-efficacy of parents of children with autism through trust, and to test the moderating role of social support in the first half of this mediation pathway. Within the framework of Triadic Reciprocal Determinism (TRD) in Social Cognitive Theory (SCT) [9], the proposed model highlights the dynamic interactions among individuals’ internal psychological resources (e.g., self-compassion), interpersonal cognitive processes (e.g., trust), and external social environmental conditions (e.g., social support) in shaping self-efficacy. By adopting an integrated “internal psychological resources–interpersonal cognition–external environmental conditions” perspective, this study aims to elucidate the dynamic mechanism underlying the formation of self-efficacy among parents of children with autism. Importantly, this research situates the proposed theoretical model within the sociocultural context of Chinese families, which is characterized by a collectivistic cultural orientation, strong norms of family responsibility, and intensive caregiving expectations. Such a context provides a meaningful setting for examining how self-compassion and trust operate in conjunction with social support to shape self-efficacy. Examining these mechanisms in a collectivistic culture extends existing research that has predominantly focused on Western, individualistic contexts, thereby enriching the cross-cultural understanding of self-efficacy formation. Moreover, parents of children with autism experience sustained caregiving demands and heightened psychological challenges, making this group a theoretically informative population for investigating psychological adjustment and social relational processes. Given that self-compassion, trust, self-efficacy, and social support are core psychological constructs with well-established theoretical foundations across cultures, this study contributes empirical evidence from a non-Western context and offers valuable insights for cross-cultural and comparative research.

The relationship between self-compassion and self-efficacy

Self-compassion refers to an individual’s ability to treat himself/herself with tolerance, understanding, and caring in the face of adversity, frustration, or his/her own inadequacies, to accept his/her own imperfections, to be less self-judgmental, and to recognize his/her own experience as a part of the common human experience [10]. It is of great significance to parents of children with autism to improve self-compassion [11, 12].Self-compassion not only helps to improve parents’ own mental health and alleviate the negative emotions associated with caring for a child. More importantly, it may also indirectly affect the child’s recovery. When parents of children with autism exhibit elevated levels of self-compassion, they tend to be able to respond to the various problems of their children in the process of growing up with a gentler and more tolerant mindset, thus creating an inclusive family environment for their children, which plays an important role in improving the problematic behaviors of autistic children. In this process, parents’ perceptions of their own capabilities are particularly crucial. Self-efficacy refers to perceptions and beliefs about one’s ability to achieve behavioral goals [13], and is of great significance to parents of children with autism in the parenting process [14]. Higher self-efficacy enables parents to proactively cope with child-rearing challenges and enhance caregiving quality, thereby facilitating children’s growth and development.

While both self-compassion and self-efficacy hold significant importance for parents, the specific relationship between these two variables among parents of children with autism remains unclear. Investigating their association is crucial for understanding parental mental health and for further supporting child rehabilitation. Against this background, this study aims to explore the relationship between self-compassion and self-efficacy among parents of children with autism. Drawing on existing theories and research findings, it can be hypothesized that parents with higher levels of self-compassion are able to understand and accept themselves with a more positive attitude, thereby maintaining an optimistic mindset and enhancing their self-efficacy. Consequently, this study proposes Hypothesis 1: There is a positive correlation between self-compassion and self-efficacy among parents of children with autism.

The mediating role of trust

Several studies have suggested that trust may be a potential mediating mechanism between self-compassion and self-efficacy. On the one hand, there is a link between self-compassion and trust. It has been found that self-compassion in caring for children helps parents to be tolerant and understanding of their own situation, not to be overly critical of themselves, and to be caring and supportive [15, 16]. Such an attitude is partly a reflection of their tendency to trust themselves as well as other people [17, 18]. Parents with higher levels of self-compassion demonstrate an enhanced capacity to recognize their difficulties as part of the shared human experience rather than personal isolation. This cognitive reframing facilitates reduced psychological defensiveness, thereby promoting more proactive help-seeking behaviors. Such behavioral patterns increase social interactions and foster interpersonal relationship development [19]. Furthermore, the establishment and maintenance of these positive social connections subsequently enhance trust in others. On the other hand, trust, as an essential factor for social coexistence, cooperation, and maintaining positive relationships, can enhance self-efficacy [20]. According to social cognitive theory, psychological states constitute one source of self-efficacy [13, 21]. Individuals’ cognitive appraisal of their environment and others’ behaviors may influence the formation of such psychological states. Consequently, when parents of children with ASD maintain higher trust in others, they develop stronger beliefs in their capability to manage parenting challenges. This trust provides psychological resilience, enabling proactive exploration of parenting solutions and reinforcing parental confidence in meeting their children’s needs [22].

Integrating these perspectives, this study posits that the relationship between self-compassion and self-efficacy may be mediated by trust. Specifically, parents of children with ASD with higher self-compassion are likely to exhibit greater trust in themselves and others when facing caregiving challenges. This enhanced trust facilitates more confident management of parenting demands, thereby increasing self-efficacy. Consequently, this study proposes Hypothesis 2: Trust mediates the relationship between self-compassion and self-efficacy among parents of children with ASD.

The moderating role of social support

Social support is defined as the provision of physical, emotional, informational, and instrumental help that an individual receives from his or her social network [23]. It serves as a protective resource that buffers the negative impact of psychological stress on the parents of children with autism, and promotes increased levels of mental health [24, 25]. Previous studies have also found that social support has been associated with self-compassion and trust, respectively [26, 27]. For parents of children with ASD, social support may moderate the relationship between self-compassion and trust, indicating differential effects of self-compassion on trust across varying social support levels. Specifically, those with higher social support, such as parents who have access to consistent family assistance, professional counseling, or shared experiences with other parents of children with ASD, demonstrate greater understanding and acceptance of parenting difficulties, thereby facilitating trust development. Conversely, parents with lower social support, who lack family involvement, professional resources, or peer connections, experience heightened isolation when facing child-rearing challenges. Chronic parenting stress may engender negative interpersonal attitudes, impairing trust formation. Thus, this study proposes Hypothesis 3: Social support moderates the relationship between self-compassion and trust among parents of children with ASD.

The present study

This study examines the underlying mechanisms between self-compassion and self-efficacy in parents of children with ASD. We aim to analyze the intrinsic relationship by investigating trust as a mediator and social support as a moderator. Building on previous research, we propose the following hypotheses: (1) Self-compassion positively correlates with parental self-efficacy; (2) Trust mediates the self-compassion-self-efficacy relationship, where self-compassion may enhance self-efficacy through increased trust; (3) Social support moderates the relationship between self-compassion and trust. These findings may inform targeted interventions to improve parental self-efficacy and mental health. The conceptual model is presented in Fig. 1.

Materials and methods

Participants

This study was conducted from April to May 2025 among parents of children with ASD in Jiangsu and Zhejiang provinces, mainland China. All participating parents were primary caregivers of ASD children. After obtaining informed consent and explaining the research purpose, 490 questionnaires were distributed. After excluding invalid responses (e.g., missing data), 400 valid questionnaires were retained, yielding an 81.63% valid response rate. The sample comprised 101 fathers and 299 mothers (mean age = 35.95 years, SD = 5.36). All ASD children had clinical diagnoses, including 305 boys and 95 girls (mean age = 6.51 years, SD = 2.47). The sample size for this study was primarily determined based on the number of items in the measurement instruments and the empirical findings in similar previous research. Existing literature suggests that a participant-to-item ratio of 10:1 is generally considered adequate [28, 29]. Furthermore, given the practical challenges associated with participant recruitment in research on the mental health of Chinese parents of children with autism, sample sizes in recent comparable studies have predominantly ranged from 200 to 300 participants [30–32]. Consequently, the sample size in this study not only meets the recommended participant-to-item ratio but also aligns with the prevailing research context in this field.

Measures

Self-compassion

Self-compassion was assessed using the Self-Compassion Scale–Short Form [33]. This scale has been validated in Chinese samples [34].It consists of 12 items (e.g., “I try to see my failings as part of the human condition.“), rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (almost never) to 5 (almost always). Higher scores indicate a higher level of self-compassion. In the present study, the scale demonstrated an internal consistency coefficient of 0.79.

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy was measured using the Parental Self-Efficacy Scale. This scale was originally developed for Japanese parents of children with autism and has subsequently been validated in Western samples of parents of autistic children [35, 36]. Following Brislin’s translation-back-translation procedure, two bilingual experts completed the translation and back-translation process for this study [37]. Additionally, three experts in psychology and special education reviewed the content to ensure the items were appropriate for the Chinese context. The scale consists of 6 items (e.g., “I can ascertain what my child wants to do.“), rated on a 4-point Likert scale ranging from 0 (disagree) to 3 (agree). Higher scores indicate greater parental self-efficacy. In this study, the scale demonstrated a Cronbach’s alpha of 0.89.

Trust

Trust was assessed using the General Trust Scale, which has been validated in Chinese samples [38, 39]. It consists of 9 items (e.g., “Generally, I trust others.“), rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (oppose) to 5 (agree). Higher scores indicate a higher level of trust. In the present study, the Cronbach’s alpha of this scale was 0.87.

Social support

The Multidimensional Scale of Perceived Social Support was employed to measure participants’ social support, which has been validated in Chinese samples [40, 41]. It consists of 12 items (e.g., “My family really tries to help me.“), rated on a 7-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (very strongly disagree) to 7 (very strongly agree). Higher scores indicate a higher level of perceived social support. In this study, the scale demonstrated an internal consistency coefficient of 0.94.

Data analysis methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and Hayes’ PROCESS macro (version 3.3). To test the hypotheses, Model 4 in PROCESS was employed to examine the mediating effect of trust, while Model 7 was used to analyze the moderating effect of perceived social support on the first half of the mediation path. All variables except parental gender were standardized prior to analysis. All parameter estimates were derived using bias-corrected bootstrapping with 5000 resamples to obtain 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [42].

Results

Common method deviation test

All research data were obtained from the parents’ self-reports, thus common method bias was assessed using Harman’s single-factor test [43]. The analysis yielded eight factors with eigenvalues exceeding 1, with the first factor accounting for less than 40% of the total variance. These results indicate no serious common method deviation in the present study.

Descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analyses were conducted for self-compassion, self-efficacy, trust, and social support (Table 1). The results revealed significant positive intercorrelations among: (a) self-compassion with self-efficacy, trust, and social support; and (b) self-efficacy with both trust and social support.

Mediated effects test

To examine the mediating role of trust in the relationship between self-compassion and self-efficacy among parents of children with ASD, we conducted mediation analysis using Model 4 in Hayes’ PROCESS macro for SPSS [42]. With gender of parent as a covariate, as shown in Table 2, the results demonstrated: (1) self-compassion significantly predicted self-efficacy (β = 0.19, p < 0.001); (2) self-compassion significantly predicted trust (β = 0.28, p < 0.001); and (3) trust significantly predicted self-efficacy (β = 0.18, p < 0.001) while self-compassion remained a significant positive predictor (β = 0.15, p < 0.01). The results indicate that after including trust as a mediating variable, the direct effect of self-compassion on self-efficacy remained significant, but with a reduced effect size compared to the model without the mediator. This suggests that trust partially mediates the relationship between self-compassion and self-efficacy in parents of children with autism. In other words, self-compassion can influence self-efficacy not only directly but also indirectly by enhancing the level of trust.

Discussion

Guided by TRD from SCT, this study clarifies the overarching psychological mechanism underlying the formation of self-efficacy among parents of children with autism [9]. Within this framework, self-compassion functions as a foundational psychological resource that shapes parents’ cognitive orientations toward caregiving challenges, while trust operates as a proximal cognitive mechanism through which these orientations are translated into efficacy beliefs. Social support, in turn, serves as a contextual condition that regulates the strength of this translation process. Rather than acting independently, these elements jointly contribute to parents’ beliefs about their competence and control, providing an integrated explanatory framework for the observed relationships among self-compassion, trust, social support, and self-efficacy.

First, the results indicate a positive correlation between parents’ self-compassion and their self-efficacy, thereby supporting H1. This finding aligns with previous studies, underscoring the significant role of self-compassion in enhancing self-efficacy [44, 45]. Within the context of Chinese culture, parents are often entrusted with high levels of parental responsibility and societal expectations [46]. While this cultural characteristic may reinforce their sense of commitment to parenting, it can also potentially exacerbate psychological burdens when caregiving challenges arise. Consequently, when parents face parenting difficulties, they are prone to excessively attribute the problems to their own perceived lack of capability. Self-compassion, by fostering acceptance and a non-judgmental understanding of these difficulties, helps parents avoid fully attributing challenges to personal shortcomings, thereby allowing them to maintain positive self-evaluations even within a cultural context of high societal expectations. Grounded in this mindset, parents are more likely to believe they possess the capacity to address parenting challenges and to create a favorable growth environment for their children [47]. These positive coping behaviors not only contribute to the child’s development but also further reinforce parents’ perceptions of their own competence, ultimately promoting an enhancement of their self-efficacy.

Second, the results of this study indicate that trust plays a partial mediating role in the relationship between self-compassion and self-efficacy, thereby supporting H2. This suggests that the effect of self-compassion on self-efficacy is not realized solely through an internal, individual pathway but is also indirectly enhanced by influencing the degree of trust parents of children with autism place in others within social interactions. This finding can be interpreted through SCT, which posits that an individual’s cognitive appraisal of the social environment serves as an important source for the formation of self-efficacy [13, 21]. Furthermore, Chinese collectivist culture emphasizes interdependence between the individual and the group [48]. Within this cultural context, social norms that prioritize relational orientation and interpersonal harmony may lead an individual’s self-attitude to significantly influence their orientation towards social interactions. Therefore, parents with higher levels of self-compassion are better able to accept their own limitations, reducing the social avoidance triggered by shame and self-blame. Consequently, they are more willing to engage in social interactions and establish open, positive interpersonal relationships. Such relatively positive social interaction experiences facilitate the formation of trust. This trust, in turn, makes it easier for parents to perceive understanding and goodwill during interactions, and to internalize these positive social experiences as affirmations of their own coping abilities, thereby strengthening their self-efficacy in parenting situations.

Lastly, this study also found that social support plays a moderating role in the relationship between self-compassion and trust, thereby supporting H3. This result not only validates the significant role of environmental factors as emphasized in SCT but also finds further explanation within the Chinese cultural context. According to SCT, environmental factors can influence the process through which individuals’ internal psychological resources are translated into social cognitive outcomes [9]. Research has also indicated that social support serves as a crucial protective factor for the mental health of parents of children with autism [49]. Within the collectiveist cultural context of China, individuals’ psychological adaptation and social behaviors often rely heavily on social relational networks such as family and community [48]. Social support not only provides emotional care and practical assistance but also conveys messages of social acceptance and validation [50]. Therefore, when parents perceive a higher level of social support, they are more likely to translate self-acceptance and understanding into trust toward others. Conversely, in situations where social support is insufficient, even if parents possess a degree of self-compassion, their sensitivity to social evaluation may lead to reduced social interaction, thereby limiting the formation of trust relationships.

Overall, this study extends the application of Social Cognitive Theory by demonstrating that parental self-efficacy among parents of children with autism in the Chinese context is best understood as the product of an integrated psychological–relational mechanism rather than isolated individual or environmental factors. By jointly considering self-compassion, trust, and social support, the findings highlight how internal self-regulatory resources, relational cognitions, and contextual support systems converge to shape parents’ efficacy beliefs within a socio-cultural context characterized by strong responsibility orientation and relational embeddedness. At the theoretical level, this study provides a contextualized and integrative perspective on self-efficacy formation, offering a coherent framework for future research on parenting and adaptation in Chinese cultural settings.

Implications

This study elucidates the mechanism through which self-compassion influences self-efficacy via trust and further reveals the moderating role of social support in the relationship between self-compassion and trust. These findings provide targeted practical implications for psychological interventions aimed at parents of children with autism. First, self-compassion, as a critical intrinsic psychological resource, should serve as a foundational objective in such interventions. Specifically, tailored self-compassion and mindfulness programs can be designed for these parents, with a focus on helping them cultivate a more gentle and understanding attitude toward their own emotional responses and parenting challenges within contexts of chronic stress and repeated setbacks. Through contextualized exercises, such interventions can alleviate parental self-blame and defensive reactions, thereby establishing the psychological groundwork for subsequent trust development. Second, given that trust mediates the relationship between self-compassion and self-efficacy, fostering trust should be considered a key mechanistic target in the intervention process. In practice, this can be achieved through structured interpersonal interactions designed to enhance parents’ trust in others and support systems. For instance, within parent-led support groups, small-scale and stable groups can be formed. Facilitated by professionals, these groups can gradually engage in activities involving experience sharing and emotional expression, which allows parents to feel heard and understood, thereby cultivating trust. This, in turn, makes them more receptive to the support and suggestions of others, ultimately strengthening their belief in their own parenting capabilities. Third, this study found that social support moderates the relationship between self-compassion and trust. In contexts characterized by high levels of social support, parents with higher self-compassion are more likely to extend their inner kindness into trust towards others and their social environment. Conversely, when social support is insufficient, the positive impact of self-compassion on trust may be constrained. Therefore, in practice, efforts should be made to strengthen the conditions for social support, such as establishing accessible professional support channels, fostering a non-judgmental service environment, and facilitating the development of social support networks. These measures can provide parents with a stable and reliable supportive environment, thereby amplifying the facilitative role of self-compassion in building trust. In summary, psychological support interventions targeting parents of children with autism should not only focus on enhancing self-compassion as an internal resource but also give priority to trust as a key mediating mechanism. Furthermore, improving the social support environment is crucial for effectively translating self-compassion into increased trust, which ultimately serves to strengthen parents’ sense of self-efficacy.

Limitations

There are some limitations to this study. Firstly, as this study employed a cross-sectional questionnaire design, it cannot definitively establish causal relationships among the variables. While it primarily examined the predictive role of self-compassion on self-efficacy, it is also plausible that self-efficacy may serve as a predictor of self-compassion. Therefore, future research should incorporate longitudinal or experimental designs to more clearly elucidate the potential bidirectional relationship between self-compassion and self-efficacy. Second, the present study collected demographic information such as parental gender and age but did not include variables such as educational attainment and income level. Future studies could incorporate these factors to more comprehensively examine their potential influence on the findings. Third, the current sample was predominantly drawn from the eastern coastal region of mainland China, an area that holds certain advantages in terms of economic development and availability of special education resources. It is necessary for future research to expand the sampling scope to enhance the external validity of the findings.Finally, this study relied heavily on the self-report method of data collection, and participants may have provided less accurate responses due to subjective factors. Future studies should use multiple methods (e.g., interviews) to collect data.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that self-compassion significantly enhances self-efficacy among parents of children with ASD, with trust serving as a mediating variable. Cultivating self-compassion yields multiple beneficial outcomes for these parents, including strengthened interpersonal trust and subsequent improvement in caregiving self-efficacy. Furthermore, the research identifies social support as a contextual moderator that potentiates this relationship under specific conditions. These findings provide an empirical foundation for developing targeted interventions for parents of children with ASD.
