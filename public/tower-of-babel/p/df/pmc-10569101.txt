Nanoscale Prediction of the Thermal, Mechanical, and Transport Properties of Hydrated Clay on 106- and 1015-Fold Larger Length and Time Scales
Authors: Xiaojin Zheng, Ian C Bourg
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoupled thermal, hydraulic, mechanical, and chemical (THMC) processes, such as desiccation-driven cracking or chemically driven fluid flow, significantly impact the performance of composite materials formed by fluid-mediated nanoparticle assembly, including energy storage materials, ordinary Portland cement, bioinorganic nanocomposites, liquid crystals, and engineered clay barriers used in the isolation of hazardous wastes. These couplings are particularly important in the isolation of high-level radioactive waste (HLRW), where heat generated by radioactive decay can drive the temperature up to at least 373 K in the engineered barrier. Here, we use large-scale all-atom molecular dynamics simulations of hydrated smectite clay nanoparticle assemblages to predict the fundamental THMC properties of hydrated compacted clay over a wide range of temperatures (up to 373 K) and dry densities relevant to HLRW management. Equilibrium simulations of clay–water mixtures at different hydration levels are analyzed to quantify material properties, including thermal conductivity, heat capacity, thermal expansion, suction, water and ion self-diffusivity, and hydraulic conductivity. Predictions are validated against experimental results for the properties of compacted bentonite clay. Our results demonstrate the feasibility of using atomistic-level simulations of assemblages of clay nanoparticles on scales of tens of nanometers and nanoseconds to infer the properties of compacted bentonite on scales of centimeters and days, a direct upscaling over 6 orders of magnitude in space and 15 orders of magnitude in time.Keywords: molecular dynamics simulation, radioactive waste isolation, bentonite barrier, THMC properties, clay dehydration, nanopore water

Bentonite, a fine-grained natural material composed predominantly of smectite clay nanoparticles, has been extensively studied as a potential backfill medium in geologic repositories for high-level radioactive waste (HLRW).1−3 Clays are considered high-quality barrier materials because of their low permeability, water and solute retention capacity, and high swelling pressure.4,5 A complicating factor is that heat generation due to the decay of nuclear wastes can strongly raise the temperature of bentonite barriers within HLRW repositories.6,7 The temperature near the waste canisters is expected to reach 373 K during the first few decades after repository closure in most repository designs and may reach up to 473 K in certain designs.2,8,9 This high temperature has the potential to cause significant thermal expansion and even bentonite desaturation.10,11 Eventually, the bentonite buffer is expected to gradually re-saturate through uptake of groundwater from the surrounding rock at a rate controlled by the high suction of unsaturated clay and by the low permeability of bentonite and host rocks.2,7 This transient drying and re-saturation is expected to last at least tens of years after repository closure.4,12

The thermal, hydraulic, mechanical, and chemical (THMC) processes outlined above are strongly coupled and raise potential concerns about the sealing efficiency of bentonite buffers.1,13,14 For example, thermal, hydraulic, and density gradients can trigger heterogeneity in the distribution of water saturation across bentonite buffers.15−18 The resulting large capillary and disjoining stresses can affect the structure of the buffer material from the scale of clay aggregates to that of the entire barrier, with strong impacts on the movement of liquid water and vapor in the barrier.18−21 Accurate prediction of these effects requires knowledge of the fundamental THMC properties of bentonite, including couplings between these properties.22

While a substantial amount of research has been conducted on the determination of the THMC properties of bentonite, knowledge gaps remain with regard to the evolution of these properties during progressive dehydration.17,24−26 In particular, experimental characterization of the heat capacity, thermal conductivity, and thermal expansion coefficient of bentonite remains insufficient to fully resolve the dependence of these properties on clay type, dry density, and water content.17,27−29 The water retention ability of clay (i.e., suction) has a strong influence on the evolution of water content in unsaturated buffer materials when subjected to drying or hydration paths, but the relevant data in the literature are scarce.30,31 Finally, swelling pressure, hydraulic conductivity, and water and solute diffusion coefficients have been studied predominantly at ambient temperatures in studies that examined highly compacted bentonites or in a limited range of dry densities in studies that examined high temperatures.31−34 In short, significant knowledge gaps persist with regard to key material properties that determine the response of bentonite barriers to the thermal, hydraulic, and mechanical stresses expected in HLRW repositories.

Computational chemistry approaches can provide important insight into the fundamental properties of hydrated nanoporous materials.35−37 In particular, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have been successfully deployed to understand the physical origins of various clay properties including heat capacity,38,39 water and solute diffusivities,40−42 and swelling pressure.43−45 However, these simulations have mostly been based on simplified microstructural models containing infinite stacks of parallel clay particles and carried out at room temperature or at fixed dry density.

In this paper, we report all-atom MD simulations of a large-scale assemblage of Na-montmorillonite nanoparticles (27 clay particles in total, each 12 nm in diameter) designed to predict the THMC properties of hydrated smectite over a wide range of temperature and dry density conditions, from 298 to 373 K and from 781 to 1827 kg m–3. The range of dry densities examined here covers the full range of interest in HLRW storage (on the order of 1300 to 1700 kg·m–3).46 Our range of dry densities extends to lower values as the preparation of the initial simulated configuration (through sequential insertion of oriented, randomly placed smectite particles) achieved dry densities only up to 781 kg·m–3 as discussed in our previous paper.23 The range of temperatures examined here covers the range considered in most repository designs (298 to 373 K). Our range of simulated conditions overlaps with most of the experimental database on the properties of compacted bentonite clay for HLRW isolation. As noted above, recent years have seen the emergence of alternative repository designs whereby temperature within the clay barrier may reach up to 473 K.2,9 The properties of compacted smectite at these higher temperatures will be examined in a future study. Our simulations build on approaches tested and validated in our recent examination of Na-smectite at low dry density,23 but we now progressively dehydrate the system to high dry densities to simulate clay dehydration processes observed in bentonite buffers. Our data analyses quantify a series of material properties of hydrated Na-smectite including thermal properties (heat capacity, thermal conductivity, thermal expansion coefficient), chemomechanical properties (suction), and mass transport properties (hydraulic conductivity, water and Na self-diffusivity). Simulation predictions are validated against experimental properties of bentonite clay measured on scales of centimeters and days. Our results demonstrate the potential of MD simulations to help characterize key properties of bentonite used in large-scale THMC simulators and thus to increase confidence in predictions of bentonite performance in the isolation of HLRW.

Results and Discussion

Heat Capacity

Our MD simulation predictions at dry densities greater than about 1200 kg·m–3 (χw ≲ 0.3), as well as our results for pure water (dark blue square at χw = 1), are aligned with experimental results and are consistent with a representation of the heat capacity of bentonite as a weighted sum of the heat capacities of water and clay.53,55 Minor deviations from the linear trend are observed in our simulation predictions and in the experimental database at dry densities between ∼800 and 1200 kg·m–3. The origin of these apparent small deviations from the ideal mixing rule is unclear. The heat capacity of pure liquid water (4157 to 4183 J·kg–1K–1) is three to four times larger than that of dry Na-montmorillonite (851 to 889 J·kg–1K–1),7,50 resulting in the observed increase in the heat capacity of bentonite with χw. The temperature dependence of CP, while not presented in Figure 2, is not negligible: our previous simulations demonstrate that it increases by 9.7 ± 0.9 J·kg–1K–1 per K as temperature increases from 298 K to 373 K at ρd = 781 kg m–3.23 The temperature dependence of heat capacity as a function of dry density is subjected to limited variations as shown in Figure A3 in the Supporting Information.

Thermal Conductivity

Thermal conductivity (λ) quantifies the capacity of bentonite to conduct heat in the presence of a temperature gradient and controls the maximum allowable temperature that can be applied in bentonite buffers.56Figure 3 compares our MD simulation predictions with experimental observations as a function of air volume fraction (ϕa). The compiled data sets, collected using various types of bentonites over a wide range of dry densities and water contents, collapse and merge into one line, indicating that the thermal conductivity of bentonite is predominantly controlled by ϕa. Despite high statistical uncertainties, our predictions are in agreement with the experimental results. The ϕa dependency can be explained by the fact that the thermal conductivity of air is more than 1 order of magnitude smaller than the thermal conductivities of water and clay.57 We note that our simulated systems are fully water-saturated at dry densities up to about 1400 kg m–3. At higher dry densities, the progressive dehydration of our simulated systems is not fully accommodated by a decrease in porosity (likely because of stacking defects of our clay particles), and the system becomes unsaturated.

Thermal conductivity of smectite and bentonite as a function of air volume fraction at T ≈ 298 K, with color representing dry density ρd, including MD simulation predictions from this work (squares) and experimental data.15,22,54,56,58,59,63,64 Experiments were performed using Kyeongju bentonite,15 GMZ07 bentonite,54 Kunigel V1 bentonite,22 Gyeongju bentonite,58 and MX-80 bentonite.56,59,63,65

The heat transfer process in unsaturated bentonite is intricate, as it involves conduction through solid minerals and liquid water in pore space as well as the latent heat transfer of vapor.54 In previous studies, the thermal conductivity of bentonite was inferred to be primarily controlled either by dry density or by water content.15,58,59 Plots of the thermal conductivity of bentonite as a function of water mass fraction and dry density can be found in Figure A5 in the Supporting Information. A high dry density improves the contact between clay particles and reduces the proportion of lower-conductivity water versus higher conductivity clay, but, at the same time, decreases pore space available for the latent heat transfer of vapor.17,60,61 As a result, the thermal conductivity of bentonite exhibits a weak dry density dependence in Figure A5. However, the thermal conductivity of bentonite tends to increase with water mass fraction because water has a higher thermal conductivity than air (as noted above) and, also, because high water contents may enhance the thermal contacts between solid particles or inhibit the formation of small cracks associated with capillary and disjoining stresses.15,59,60,62

Thermal Expansion Coefficient

Bentonite must maintain its ability to support the thermal expansion triggered by heated canisters without physically failing during dehydration.31 Our MD simulation predictions reveal that the thermal expansion coefficient (αP) of water–smectite mixtures decreases with increasing dry density and increases with the temperature (Figure 4). Our results are broadly consistent with experimental data on the αP values of bentonite and fill a data gap at low dry densities. The density effect may be explained by the fact that the expansion coefficient of water is almost one order of magnitude higher than that of clay minerals.66 During continued temperature elevation, the significant difference between the thermal expansion coefficients of water and clay contributes to the thermal pressurization of pore water in low-porosity bentonite because of differential expansion.67 The temperature dependence of αP is associated with the reduction of the number of hydrogen bonds in water with increasing temperature, which favors water expansion.68

Thermal expansion coefficient of smectite and bentonite as a function of dry density, with color representing temperature, including MD simulation predictions from this work (squares) and experimental results from the literature.13,14,72 Experiments were performed using MX-80 bentonite13,72 and Boom clay.14 For consistency, the compiled experimental αP values and MD simulation predictions in this figure are volumetric thermal expansion coefficients.

Whereas our predicted αP values at dry densities below 1600 kg m–3 clearly increase with temperature, predicted αP values at higher dry densities do not exhibit a clear temperature dependence. One possible origin of this disorder is the limited space available for particle rearrangements at high dry densities, which may cause thermal expansion to vary nonlinearly with temperature.6 Another possible origin is the partially saturated state of our simulated systems at high dry densities. The low αP values (less than 10–4) predicted at high dry densities (larger than 1750 kg m–3) indicate that the displacements and strains in bentonite induced by thermal expansion are very small, which explains the high scatter of reported experimental values. Additionally, the discrepancy in experimental observations may depend on the direction in which the measurements were performed (e.g., either normal or parallel to the clay particles), how compaction was applied (e.g., either uniaxial or isotropic compression), and local material inhomogeneities.12,66,69 We also note that thermal strain is effective-stress dependent, and in particular, the thermal volumetric strain under high confining stress may occur as contractive rather than expansive because of the mechanical weakening of contacts.13 Finally, we note that our simulations evaluate the thermal strain in undrained conditions and that additional processes related to osmotic and hydration effects are expected to impact the thermal strain of hydrated clay in drained conditions.70 At even higher temperatures, irreversible plastic thermal strain can be observed.71

Suction

Suction (Psc), which characterizes the driving force for water uptake in unsaturated bentonite, is an essential parameter in determining water transfer in bentonite and predicting the time needed for full saturation after emplacement of the engineered barrier.26,34 Because of their very high computational cost, Psc calculations were carried out only as a function of dry density at T = 298 K and as a function of temperature at ρd = 781 kg m–3. Our MD simulation predictions and experimental results compiled in Figure 5 show that bentonite suction increases significantly with dry density.17,30,73,74 The temperature dependence of Psc is weakly positive, as reported in Figure A6 in the Supporting Information. As shown in Figure 5, suction is very sensitive to small density changes at high dry densities, where water uptake occurs predominantly in narrow interlayer nanopores.30 At low dry density conditions with high water contents, capillarity takes control, which depends on the shape and arrangement of the clay particles, particularly in mesopores and macropores.75 The difference between experimental observations is attributed to the variability of natural bentonite and the chosen laboratory method. For example, suction is slightly higher for unconfined samples that can swell freely than for those kept at a constant volume,76 and bentonite with a large fraction of Ca exchangeable cations generally has a smaller suction than bentonite with predominantly Na ions.58,77

Results from Underwood and Bourg42 were based on MD simulations of randomly ordered smectite particles at room temperature. Experiments were performed using FEBEX bentonite74,78 and MX-80 bentonite.73

Results obtained in this study show that suction can be slightly negative at low dry densities (less than 1000 kg m–3), in agreement with the known cohesive nature of clayey media79 and with previous MD simulation results.42,80,81 Possible origins of these negative suctions include configurational entropy effects and specific coulomb interactions in the compressed electrical double layer (EDL) between charged clay surfaces.80,82−84 The strong increase in suction with high dry densities is associated with a reduction in clay basal spacing (also known as d-spacing).85 It is thought to result predominantly from two mechanisms: osmotic pressure associated with overlapping EDLs at basal spacings greater than about 1 nm (i.e., ρd ≤ 1400 kg m–3) and hydration repulsion associated with the hydration energy of exchangeable cations at smaller basal spacings, i.e., larger dry densities.37,81,86−88 A first-order prediction of the osmotic pressure of pure Na-smectite, estimated using Van’t Hoff’s law based on the simplistic approximation that exchangeable cations are uniformly distributed in the entire pore space, is represented by the blue dashed line in Figure 6. The agreement with measured suction values (within the scatter of the experimental results) indicates that the osmotic contribution dominates Psc at low dry densities, while the deviation at high dry densities is likely associated with the hydration repulsion phenomenon. The significant scatter of our predicted Psc values is consistent with previous applications of the Widom TPI technique in computing excess chemical potentials.42

In addition to suction, the swelling pressure (Psw) of bentonite also has been relatively extensively reported in the literature.58,89−93 To a first approximation, in individual water-saturated pores or in ordered clay aggregates, we expect Psw = Psc.94 For macroscopic scale bentonite samples, however, experimental results indicate that swelling pressure is consistently lower than suction.95−97Figure 6 compiles reported Psw and Psc values and shows that this difference is systematically on the order of 1.5 logarithmic units at a given dry density. The difference between Psc and Psw values observed in centimeter-scale experiments is likely attributable to structural heterogeneity on length scales larger than those examined in our simulations, such as the existence of clay aggregates and voids associated with overconsolidation and swelling hysteresis in clayey media.94,97 Our attempts to relate suction to swelling pressure using a regression relation based on all of the reported values in Figure 6 yielded log(Psw) = 0.94 log(Psc) – 1.55. The fitted swelling pressure results are broadly aligned with a variety of experimental Psw results for bentonite, as demonstrated in Figure A7 in the Supporting Information.

Molecular Diffusion Coefficients

Diffusion is an important transport mechanism in bentonite under natural hydraulic gradients. Figure 7 shows the measured and predicted self-diffusivities of water and Na ions, normalized by their counterparts in bulk liquid water. Experimental results follow a decreasing trend as dry density increases and self-diffusivities are up to one order of magnitude smaller in the direction normal (D⊥) versus parallel (D∥) to the preferred orientation of clay particles associated with the system anisotropy that is expected to develop because of uniaxial compaction of experimental samples.101,102 Simulation predictions show a much higher difference between diffusivities normal (rhombi) and parallel to the clay particles (squares), as expected because of the high degree of particle orientation in our simulated systems. The geometric mean of self-diffusivities predicted in the x, y, and z directions (Daverage = D∥2/3D⊥1/3, upward facing triangles) is in agreement with experimental results at dry densities up to about 1600 kg·m–3 despite the million-fold difference between the length scales of simulations (10–8 m) and experiments (10–2 m).

Simulation predictions are reported in directions parallel or normal to the preferred orientation of the clay particles (squares and rhombi) and as a geometric average of diffusion in all directions (upward facing triangles). Literature data were based on through-diffusion experiments using Kunigel-V1 and Kunipia-F bentonite,101,102 in-diffusion experiments using Kunipia-F bentonite,104 and radioactive tracer tests using Kunipia-F bentonite.85 In the experimental results of Sato and Suzuki (2003)101 and Suzuki et al. (2004),102 “parallel” and “normal” refer to directions relative to the partial orientation of clay particles induced by uniaxial compaction of the dry bentonite powder.

The temperature dependence of results shown in Figure 7 is much smaller than in the non-normalized data presented in Figure A9 in the Supporting Information, indicating that the activation energy of diffusion in compacted clay is close to that in bulk liquid water. The steeper decrease in our simulations versus experiments at ρd ≥ 1600 kg·m–3 is likely due to the presence of unsaturated conditions in our simulations versus fully saturated conditions in the experiments. We note that unsaturated conditions are expected in HLRW repositories in certain scenarios, but they are challenging to apply in diffusion experiments. The few experimental results obtained in these conditions are consistent with a sharp decrease in diffusivity associated with the reduced connectivity of the residual water phase.106,107

A significant difficulty in comparing simulations and experiments in Figure 7 is that simulation predictions of D⊥ are sensitive to the tortuosity of the pore network in the direction normal to the clay particles, which depends on the aspect ratio of these particles. The self-diffusivities D⊥ presented in Figure 7 were corrected by a factor of ∼5 to account for the smaller aspect ratio of our simulated clay particles (diameter 12 nm) versus real smectite particles (diameter ∼100 nm) as described in Figure A8 in the Supporting Information. Conversely, the self-diffusivities D∥ presented in Figure 7 are thought to be minimally impacted by tortuosity and predominantly reflect the reduced mobility of water and Na ions confined in nanopores where water and solute dynamics are strongly impacted by the charged clay surfaces.36,108 This effect increases continuously with dry density as water and Na ions become increasing tightly confined in clay interlayer nanopores, in agreement with experimental observations.77,85,109 Overall, the very low diffusivities observed here at high dry densities confirm that highly compacted bentonite strongly inhibits the diffusion of radionuclides and other contaminants, particularly under unsaturated conditions.

Hydraulic Conductivity

Hydraulic conductivity (kc) controls mass transport in bentonite and is one of the most critical inputs in large-scale simulators for the project of nuclear waste isolation. Experimental studies on the hydraulic conductivity of bentonite have been extensively performed in the literature, but predominantly at high dry densities (ρd > 1300 kg·m–3).58,74,78,100,110Figure 8 reports our MD simulation predictions for the hydraulic conductivity of water–smectite mixtures over a wide range of dry densities. Hydraulic conductivities normal and parallel to the clay particles are reported in Figure A11 in the Supporting Information. As noted in the previous section, the small size of clay particles in our simulations versus natural bentonite is expected to impact tortuosity in the direction normal to the clay particles by a factor of ∼5 and is accounted for, as in Figure 7, as described in eq A13 in the Supporting Information. Predicted hydraulic conductivities are 2 orders of magnitude larger in directions parallel versus normal to the clay particles as a result of the anisotropy of our model system. Similar anisotropy in hydraulic conductivity is observed in natural bentonite samples with a well-oriented clay fabric.111,112

Experiments were performed using Milos bentonite from Greece, Kutch bentonite from India, and Wyoming bentonite.33 Simulation predictions, represented by square symbols, are based on the harmonic mean of hydraulic conductivity measured in directions normal to and parallel to the clay particles.

Figure 8 shows that the logarithm of kc decreases linearly with increasing dry density, as expected due to the reduction of pore size in compacted bentonite.113−115 Additionally, the hydraulic conductivity of bentonite increases with temperature, as expected based on the temperature dependence of water viscosity.110,116 Our simulation predictions at ambient temperature are consistent with experimental results obtained for Na-bentonite on length scales of centimeters, compiled in Figure 8. Experimental results obtained for bentonites that contain significant levels of divalent exchangeable cations (e.g., Ca2+ and Mg2+) are not presented in Figure 8, as these ions inhibit clay swelling and hence promote the persistence of clay aggregates and larger pores.58,77 Depending on divalent cation abundance, hydraulic conductivities measured for bentonite with predominantly divalent exchangeable cations are 0.5 to 2 orders of magnitude larger than for Na-bentonite, as demonstrated in Figure A12 in the Supporting Information.

Differences between simulation predictions and experiments are expected because effects that take place over length scales larger than 20 nm, which cannot be predicted by our simulations, can strongly impact kc. These effects include possible structural heterogeneities on length scales of hundreds of nanometers (i.e., aggregates and voids initially present in dry bentonite powder may not entirely disappear upon rehydration)101 and also on length scales of micrometers (e.g., the presence of inert coarse grains may promote the persistence of microregions with higher or lower dry densities). Other potential sources of discrepancy include the anisotropy in the experimental samples developed during the one-dimensional compaction of bentonite powders (which can be potentially corrected for as suggested in eq A14 in the Supporting Information117) and the unsaturated state of our simulated bentonite at high dry densities (whereas hydraulic conductivity experiments are generally carried out using fully saturated samples). The agreement between simulation and experimental results at ambient temperature in Figure 8 is noteworthy in light of these potential sources of discrepancy. Finally, our results confirm that the hydraulic conductivity of Na-bentonite is extremely small at high dry densities such that fluid and solute transport in bentonite barriers is likely dominated by molecular diffusion except in conditions with very high suction gradients.

Conclusions

Bentonite in the isolation of HLRW is subjected to coupled THMC processes. The thermal, hydraulic, and mechanical properties of bentonite are not static in the post-closure period but instead evolve as a function of bentonite hydration. Reliable predictions of bentonite performance require accurate knowledge of the evolution of bentonite properties when subjected to a variety of conditions of temperature, compaction, and saturation state. Our results demonstrate that all-atom MD simulations of water–montmorillonite mixtures, on scales of tens of nanometers and nanoseconds, can help predict the THMC material properties of bentonite measured on scales of centimeters and days over a wide range of temperatures and dry densities. In short, MD simulation predictions of the properties of hydrated clay nanoparticle assemblages are consistent with a wide range of material properties measured over 106 times larger length scales and 1015 times larger time scales.

Large-scale simulators rely critically on constitutive relations representing the THMC material properties of bentonite to predict the performance of the bentonite buffers. A wide variety of empirical equations has been proposed in previous studies as compiled in Table A1 in the Supporting Information. Despite the underlying simplifications, Table A1 highlights the key parameters affecting the THMC properties of bentonite. For example, the most important factors affecting kc include dry density (or void ratio) and exchangeable cation type,100,118−120 while the key factors for λ are dry density and water saturation (or, equivalently, porosity and water mass fraction).15,60,120−124

Empirical relations proposed in previous studies were generally derived to fit individual experimental data sets. In this work, we use insights gained from our MD simulations and our compilation of previous experimental results to help constrain the form of constitutive relations. For example, experimental and simulation results presented in Figures 2 through 4 reveal that thermal properties (CP, λ, αP) are predominantly controlled by the volume or mass fractions of water, air, and clay. Constitutive relations proposed based on these insights are then parametrized based on fitting to the full database of experimental and simulation results compiled in Figures 2–8 as summarized in Table A1 and as illustrated by the gray lines in Figures 2–8. For example, the heat capacity of bentonite is related to the water mass fraction via CP(χw, T) = χwCP,w(T) + (1 – χw)CP,c(T) where CP,w(T) = 107.3(T – 298 K) + 4106.1 and CP,c(T) = 4.2(T – 298 K) + 869.4. Figures A13–A18 illustrate the agreement between the material properties of bentonite compiled in this paper and the properties predicted using constitutive relations derived in this work. Despite variability associated with the use of bentonites from different natural deposits, our constitutive relations have the potential to help constrain representations of key bentonite material properties used in large-scale THMC simulation predictions of HLRW repository performance.

More broadly, our results suggest that MD simulations have potential to help predict the fundamental properties of other materials formed by fluid-mediated self-assembly of nanoparticles, including battery and supercapacitor materials, the calcium silicate hydrate (C–S–H) phase of cement, liquid crystals, and bioinorganic materials. Although the materials listed above may not all lend themselves to the generation of realistic microstructures through the approach used here (i.e., MD simulation of spontaneous or compaction-driven self-assembly), evidence suggests that this approach holds promise, at least in certain cases. For example, experiments suggest that artificial cement created by compacting C–S–H nanoparticles has properties analogous to those of conventional cement created by growing C–S–H nanoparticles in situ.125 Similarly, coarse-grained MD simulations have contributed significant theoretical understanding of self-organization in liquid crystals and colloidal assemblages,126,127 and all-atom MD simulations of this self-organization phenomenon represent a logical next step for properties or processes that are sensitive to atomistic-level details, such as the role of water in ionic liquids or in the oriented aggregation of mineral nanoparticles.128,129 As in all MD simulation efforts, a key requirement is the existence of accurate interatomic potential models, a requirement that is fulfilled by the ClayFF model of hydrated phyllosilicate minerals used in this study. Another requirement is the feasibility of neglecting structural heterogeneities formed by nanoparticle assemblages on length scales larger than those probed by MD simulations (i.e., tens of nanometers), such as interaggregate voids. This second requirement appears to be fulfilled for Na-smectite clay assemblages possibly because of the relatively long-range interaction associated with the electrical double layer between Na-smectite clay surfaces.

Methodology

Generation of Large-Scale Hydrated Clay Structures

Bentonite is predominantly composed of montmorillonite clay, a 2:1 layer-type clay mineral in the smectite family.130 The systems simulated in this work were generated as described in Zheng et al.23 Briefly, the initial phyllosilicate structure was generated based on the pyrophyllite unit cell structure of Bickmore et al.131 Isomorphic substitutions of octahedral cations (Al3+) by lower valence cations (Mg2+) were distributed randomly but not allowed to share bridging oxygen atoms.132 Clay particles were then charge balanced by placing Na+ ions 1 nm above or below the isomorphic substitution sites. The stoichiometry of the simulated clay unit cell is Na0.8+Al3.2Mg0.8Si8O20(OH)4. Hexagonal clay particles with a diameter of 12 nm were formed by cleaving the phyllosilicate structure along the so-called B and AC edges.133 A total of 27 clay particles were created and randomly distributed, with a horizontal orientation to mimic realistic clay aggregation structures,134,135 in a 20 × 20 × 20 nm simulation cell with periodic boundary conditions. As described in our previous work, these particles were successively inserted until a total of 10,000 insertion attempts yielded no successful insertion (i.e., with no overlap between the new particle and previously inserted particles). The maximum number of clay particles that could be inserted in a 20 × 20 × 20 nm box was 27. Interatomic interactions were represented using the extended simple point charge (SPC/E) model for water,136 the Smith–Dang parametrization for sodium ions,137 and the ClayFF model for mineral particles,138,139 with clay edges represented using the ClayFF-consistent parametrization developed by Lammers et al.140 This combination of models has been extensively validated for its ability to predict the structure, dynamics, and energetics of phyllosilicate–water systems at ambient pressure.42,80,141−144 Water molecules were kept rigid using the SHAKE algorithm.145 Pairwise interatomic interactions were resolved in real space of up to 1.2 nm. Coulomb interactions beyond this distance were resolved in reciprocal space using the particle–particle–particle–mesh (PPPM) algorithm with an accuracy of 99.95%.146 Other details of our simulation methodology are presented in Zheng at al.23 and in Underwood and Bourg.42

The initial configuration was solvated using 187,131 water molecules, resulting in a system containing 766,701 atoms. Water molecules overlapping with clay particles and Na+ ions were removed. The initial solid volume fraction was 0.28, which corresponds to a dry density of 781 kg·m–3, characteristic of uncompacted bentonite powder.147 A total of 96 replicas were constructed with temperatures ranging from 298 to 373 K and equilibrated for 10 ns in the NPzT ensemble at 1 bar via the replica-exchange molecular dynamics (REMD) simulation technique, which improves the efficiency of conformational samplings.148,149 Temperature and pressure couplings were achieved via the velocity rescaling and Berendsen methods.150,151 Detailed characterization of this initial, uncompacted system as a function of temperature was presented in Zheng et al.23 In this study, we selected 10 replicas with a temperature spacing of 8.3 K and progressively dehydrated these systems to higher dry densities spanning the range observed in waste isolation applications and experimental characterization studies. The final dry density of the simulated system is 1827 kg·m–3 with a solid volume fraction of 0.64. At each dehydration step, 8911 water molecules were randomly removed from the system. Then, each replica was simulated for 2 ns at 1 bar in the NPzT ensemble, and the second half of each 2 ns simulation was used for data analysis. The dehydration process for each replica took a total of 21 steps and 42 ns to complete. Simulations were carried out with the codes GROMACS (for most calculations) and LAMMPS (for thermal conductivity calculations).

Computation of the THMC Properties of Water–Clay Mixtures

The thermal, hydraulic, and mechanical properties of water–montmorillonite mixtures during dehydration were computed by using equilibrium MD simulation results. Relations used to calculate these properties are briefly summarized below. The detailed methodologies can be found in the Supporting Information and (except for suction) in Zheng et al.23 All properties were calculated based on NPzT ensemble simulations, except for the constant volume heat capacity, CV, which was determined based on NVT ensemble simulations.

Heat capacity was computed from MD simulations using the two-phase thermodynamics (2PT) method, which utilizes the density of states with a weighting function based on quantum harmonic oscillators.152,153 The incorporation of quantum corrections is required to eliminate quantum effects due to the high phonon efficiency in clay crystals.
