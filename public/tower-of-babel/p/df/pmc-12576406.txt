Tirzepatide-Induced Syndrome of Inappropriate Antidiuretic Hormone Secretion (SIADH) Presenting With Seizures
Authors: Islam Shah, Devesh Sennik, Fahas Ali Vattiyam Veettil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTirzepatide (Mounjaro), a novel, dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist, is increasingly prescribed for type 2 diabetes and off-label weight loss. While gastrointestinal adverse effects are common, hyponatremia induced by tirzepatide is rarely reported. We report a 63-year-old woman with no significant past medical history who developed tonic-clonic seizures 4 days after starting tirzepatide for weight loss. Laboratory evaluation revealed severe hyponatremia (serum sodium 122 mmol/L), low serum osmolality, and high urine osmolality and urine sodium consistent with syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH). No other causes were identified. Discontinuation of tirzepatide and fluid restriction led to gradual normalization of sodium levels and full clinical recovery. This case highlights tirzepatide as a potential cause of SIADH and severe hyponatremia leading to seizures, even in low-risk individuals. Clinicians should monitor electrolytes when initiating GLP-1 receptor agonists, especially off-label.Keywords: tirzepatide, SIADH, hyponatremia, seizures, GLP-1 receptor agonist

Introduction

Tirzepatide is a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonist approved for type 2 diabetes management and increasingly used off-label for weight loss [1]. GLP-1 receptor agonists commonly cause gastrointestinal symptoms, but reports of electrolyte disturbances such as hyponatremia are scarce [2, 3]. Syndrome of inappropriate antidiuretic hormone secretion (SIADH) is characterized by hyponatremia and hypo-osmolality with inappropriately concentrated urine and can cause neurological symptoms including seizures [4]. There have been isolated reports linking GLP-1 receptor agonists with SIADH, but data remain limited [2, 5]. We present a rare case of tirzepatide-induced SIADH causing severe hyponatremia and seizures in a previously healthy patient.

Case Presentation

A 63-year-old woman presented to the emergency department after 2 witnessed seizure episodes characterized by tonic-clonic activity, loss of consciousness, tongue biting, and postictal confusion. She had no prior history of seizures, chronic illnesses, or medication use.

Four days prior, she began tirzepatide obtained through an online pharmacy for weight loss; her baseline weight was 79 kg, and she was not clinically overweight. She reported only nausea, but no vomiting or other gastrointestinal symptoms while on tirzepatide. On examination, she was euvolemic, with no evidence of dehydration or fluid overload. Vital signs were stable, and neurological examination showed no focal deficits. There was no fever, headache, neck stiffness, or rash.

The primary differential diagnoses for hyponatremia with seizures include SIADH, cerebral salt wasting, adrenal insufficiency, hypothyroidism, and renal failure. Normal renal, adrenal, and thyroid functions and lack of volume depletion or other underlying pathology made SIADH the most likely diagnosis. The temporal association with tirzepatide initiation suggested drug-induced SIADH.

Treatment

Tirzepatide was discontinued immediately. The patient was started on fluid restriction of 1.5 L/day. Electrolytes were monitored frequently. Sodium levels gradually improved, reaching 134 mmol/L within 5 days, with no further seizure episodes.

Outcome and Follow-up

At outpatient follow-up 2 weeks later, the patient remained seizure-free, and serum sodium was stable within the normal range. No other neurological symptoms were reported. The patient was counseled against restarting tirzepatide.

Discussion

This case highlights a rare but serious adverse effect of tirzepatide causing SIADH and severe hyponatremia leading to seizures. GLP-1 receptor agonists can cause nausea and delayed gastric emptying, which may stimulate antidiuretic hormone (ADH) secretion or alter renal water handling, contributing to SIADH [2, 5]. Thus, a plausible mechanism for tirzepatide-induced SIADH in our patient includes both gastrointestinal effects leading to ADH stimulation and potential direct incretin-mediated effects on renal and hypothalamic-pituitary regulation of water balance Although hyponatremia is a recognized complication of SIADH, it is rarely reported with tirzepatide specifically. Prior reports have documented similar electrolyte disturbances and acute kidney injury with GLP-1 receptor agonists, supporting a possible class effect [2, 6].

The temporal relationship between tirzepatide initiation and symptom onset, along with laboratory findings and clinical improvement after drug withdrawal, strongly suggests causality. Notably, this patient had no prior risk factors for hyponatremia or seizures, emphasizing the need for vigilance even in low-risk populations.

Clinicians should monitor serum electrolytes in patients starting tirzepatide, especially when used off-label for weight loss. Early recognition and management of hyponatremia are critical to prevent serious neurological complications.
