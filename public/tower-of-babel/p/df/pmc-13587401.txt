Psychometric evaluation of gender norm beliefs and attitudes among students in Ghana: evidence from the GNAS and GEM Scales
Authors: Bright Addo, Kofi Osei Akuoko, Peter Dwumah, Jonathan Mensah Dapaah
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRobust measurement of gender-related beliefs and attitudes is essential for understanding how gender norms operate in higher-education settings. However, little is known about how these constructs are structured among Ghanaian university students or whether widely used instruments retain validity in this context. This study examined the factor structure, reliability, and measurement invariance of adapted versions of the Gender Norm Attitudes Scale (GNAS) and Gender Equitable Men (GEM) Scale among Sociology and Social Work students in Ghana.MethodsA cross-sectional survey was conducted among 631 students (98.3% response rate) from two public universities, split for Explorative Factor Analysis (EFA) (n = 339) and Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA) (n = 364). EFA used principal axis factoring with oblique rotation on polychoric correlation matrices; parallel analysis guided factor retention. CFA was estimated under maximum likelihood and WLSMV estimators. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega. Measurement invariance was tested across sex, university, programme, and year level using the standard nested sequence of configural, metric, and scalar models, and was evaluated by changes in fit indices (ΔCFI, ΔRMSEA, ΔTLI, and ΔSRMR); differential item functioning (DIF) was assessed using ordinal logistic regression. Data were managed and described using SPSS Version 32; all factor-analytic, reliability, invariance, and DIF analyses were conducted in R (psych and lavaan packages).ResultsThe GNAS yielded a two-factor, 11-item structure reflecting beliefs supporting men’s privileges (α = 0.824, ω = 0.868) and equity for girls and women (α = 0.830, ω = 0.898), with strong fit (CFI = 0.960; TLI = 0.949; RMSEA = 0.057). Polychoric EFA confirmed this, indicating ordinal estimation did not alter the solution. After empirically driven item reduction, the adapted GEM Scale emerged as a modified version of the original instrument, with narrower domain coverage and near-complete loss of the Reproductive Health and Disease Prevention domain; the retained 16-item, four-factor structure covered violence, domestic chores and daily life, sexual relationships, and household authority and obedience, explaining 52.6% of variance with acceptable fit (CFI = 0.888; TLI = 0.865; RMSEA = 0.058), though the Household Authority and Obedience factor rested on only two items and the subscales showing weak Cronbach’s alpha values. The students generally rejected inequitable norms, though attitudes toward household authority and domestic roles were more variable. For the GNAS, configural and metric invariance held across sex and university (ΔCFI ≤ 0.005); scalar invariance held across university (ΔCFI = 0.006) and was only partial across sex (ΔCFI = 0.022). For the adapted GEM Scale, metric invariance held across university (ΔCFI = 0.006) but not sex (ΔCFI = 0.016), and scalar invariance was not supported in either grouping.ConclusionAlthough preliminary, the adapted GNAS shows acceptable structural validity, adequate-to-strong reliability, and metric invariance across sex and university and scalar invariance across university. In contrast, the adapted GEM Scale should be interpreted cautiously as a substantially modified measure with reduced domain coverage and less stable invariance properties, for which scalar invariance is not yet established. Further validation, confirmation of these invariance results using ordinal (WLSMV) estimators, and exploration of 5- or 7-point response formats are needed before either adapted scale is used confidently in broader research or programme evaluation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05085-1.Keywords: Gender norms, Psychometric evaluation, GNAS, GEM scale, Higher education, Factor analysis, Ghana

Background

Achieving gender equality remains one of the most pressing global development challenges. Despite significant policy commitments and institutional efforts worldwide, progress toward Sustainable Development Goal 5, which seeks to “achieve gender equality and empower all women and girls”, continues to lag. Recent global assessments indicate that many indicators of gender equality remain off-track. The UN Sustainable Development Goals Report shows that with only five years to the 2030 deadline, just 15.4% of Goal 5 indicators are on track, while 61.5% show only moderate progress and 23.1% are far off target [1]. At the current pace, it could take centuries to close critical gaps, 300 years to end child marriage, 286 years to eliminate discriminatory laws, and 140 years for women to achieve equal representation in leadership roles.

Global efforts have yielded notable gains: more girls are in school than ever before, harmful practices such as female genital mutilation have declined, and women’s participation in politics and the labor force has improved. Yet, the vision of a world where every woman and girl enjoys full equality remains elusive [1]. Ghana exemplifies this paradox. The country has demonstrated strong political will through legislative frameworks, gender policies, and institutional structures such as the Ministry of Gender, Children and Social Protection (MoGCSP). Initiatives, including the Affirmative Action Law, signal progress toward gender parity [2]. However, structural barriers persist. The World Economic Forum’s Global Gender Gap Report ranks Ghana with an overall score of 0.672, reflecting significant disparities in political empowerment and economic participation [3]. Women occupy only 14% of parliamentary seats and less than 5% of district assembly positions.

One key but insufficiently explored driver of these disparities is the influence of gender norms, i.e., deeply rooted beliefs and attitudes about appropriate roles, responsibilities, and entitlements for men and women. Studies conducted in Ghana highlight how traditional gender norms shape social expectations, reinforce inequalities, and contribute to vulnerabilities such as intimate partner violence, restricted educational opportunities, and unequal household labor divisions [4–6]. Yet, empirical research on how young adults, particularly university students, conceptualize gender norms remains limited. This gap is significant because higher-education settings serve as formative environments where future leaders acquire values, negotiate identities, and engage with discourses on equity, rights, and social change.

University students, especially those studying Sociology and Social Work, are exposed to academic content that interrogates social structures, gender relations, and power dynamics, making them both an analytically relevant and practically important population for gender-norm research. However, little is known about how gender-related beliefs and attitudes are structured within this demographic, or whether commonly used measurement tools adequately capture these constructs in the Ghanaian university context. Without valid and contextually appropriate measures, interventions, instructional practices, and gender-transformative programmes cannot be grounded in defensible empirical evidence.

Two widely used instruments, the Gender Norm Attitudes Scale (GNAS) and the Gender-Equitable Men (GEM) Scale, provide structured approaches for assessing gender‐related beliefs and attitudes. The GNAS operationalizes beliefs along a continuum from traditional to egalitarian, while the GEM Scale examines attitudes related to violence, sexual relationships, reproductive expectations, and domestic roles [7–9]. Both scales use 3-point Likert-type response formats and have been applied in diverse low- and middle-income country settings. However, past research has consistently revealed variability in the factor structures of these scales across populations, differences that may arise from several sources, including how items are interpreted and which norm domains are most salient in a given setting. For example, the original GEM Scale validation in Brazil produced a two-factor structure capturing inequitable and equitable gender norms [7], whereas an Indian application of the GEM Scale in Uttar Pradesh produced a different multidimensional structure better aligned with local gendered expectations around fertility, household decision-making, and partner communication [8]. Across Sub-Saharan African applications, violence, sexual relationships, and household roles have generally emerged as recoverable factors, while reproductive health items have shown more variable performance [10, 11]. These patterns come from a small number of studies, however, and the differences between settings may also reflect variation in sample composition, analytic methods, and the language and wording of the adapted items, rather than cultural differences alone. Similar variability has been documented for the GNAS: the original Egyptian validation supported two correlated belief domains [12]. Across these settings, items addressing reproductive health, sexual communication, and homophobia have most often shown unstable loadings, while items addressing partner violence and male decision-making authority have been comparatively stable. This pattern of cross-setting variation signals the need to evaluate, rather than assume, the factor structure of either scale before applying it in a new context. Within Ghana, no studies have systematically evaluated the psychometric properties of either scale among university students, leaving open fundamental questions about whether the original dimensional structures replicate, whether new culturally specific patterns emerge, such as those relating to household authority, domestic obedience, or respectability, and whether the measures function equivalently across groups that might be the targets of subgroup comparisons.

This last point is especially important. The intended use of adapted scales determines what validity evidence is most essential [13]. If the GNAS and GEM Scale are to be used for programme evaluation and subgroup comparisons, as proposed by prior researchers and policymakers, then evidence of structural validity must be accompanied by evidence of measurement invariance across sex, university, programme, and academic level, and by the absence of differential item functioning (DIF) that could distort group comparisons. To date, no study has examined whether these conditions hold for either scale in the Ghanaian higher-education context.

Guided by a measurement argument, the present study addresses these gaps by evaluating the dimensional structure, internal consistency, measurement invariance, and item-level functioning of adapted versions of the GNAS and GEM Scale among Sociology and Social Work students at two Ghanaian public universities.

Methods

Design and setting

This study employed a cross-sectional, non-experimental survey design to examine gender-related beliefs and attitudes among higher-education students in Ghana. Data were collected in February 2025 from two purposively selected public universities: the University of Ghana (UG), Legon, and the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology (KNUST) in Kumasi.

University selection followed a two-stage procedure designed to balance institutional diversity with feasibility. First, a sampling frame was constructed comprising all major public universities in Ghana that offer undergraduate degree programmes in the social sciences. Each eligible university was assigned an identification number, and two institutions were selected using a computer-generated random number routine, ensuring that each eligible university had an equal probability of selection. Within the selected universities, the Department of Sociology and Social Work (KNUST) and the Department of Sociology (UG) were purposively selected. Students in these departments routinely encounter academic content on social structures, gender relations, and power dynamics, making them an analytically relevant and practically important population for examining gender-related beliefs and attitudes in the Ghanaian higher-education context.

Participants

The study population comprised undergraduate students enrolled in Sociology and Social Work programmes at KNUST and UG. At the time of data collection, the accessible population totalled 2,225 students: 725 from KNUST (317 Social Work; 408 Sociology) and 1,500 regular undergraduate Sociology students at UG. Only regular, full-time UG students were included, as distance-education and sandwich students were not physically present on campus during the data collection period.

Eligibility required participants to be aged 18 years or older and currently enrolled in the identified programmes. No exclusions were made based on sex, level of study, or academic performance. A total of 631 students completed the survey out of an adjusted sample target of 642, yielding a response rate of 98.3%. Participants spanned all four academic levels across both programme areas and both universities.

Sampling procedure

Within each institution, recruitment followed a classroom-based census approach: all eligible students present during scheduled class sessions on the data collection days were invited to participate. This in-class administration maximised participation, reduced self-selection bias, and facilitated broad coverage across academic levels.

It is important to clarify a potential ambiguity in the original sampling description. The study did not attempt a full institutional census. Rather, it employed proportional stratified sampling, with class sessions serving as the primary recruitment unit within each stratum (institution and academic level). The final achieved sample (n = 631) is representative of the accessible student population within the selected departments at both universities, given the near-complete response rate (98.3%).

At KNUST, data collection was completed over three days with support from teaching assistants during scheduled class sessions. At UG, a trained research assistant collected data over 12 days, supplementing classroom sessions with recruitment in departmental libraries and informal study spaces to reach students who were not present during lectures.

Research instruments

Data were collected using the Gender Norms Beliefs and Attitudes Checklist (GNBAC), developed by adapting items from the GNAS and GEM Scale. The GNBAC comprised three sections: sociodemographic information (Section A), beliefs about gender norms (Section B: adapted GNAS items), and attitudes toward gender norms (Section C: adapted GEM Scale items).

Section A: sociodemographic characteristics

This section collected demographic and background information, including university of study, programme of study, level/year of study, sex, age, ethnicity, religion, marital status, residential dwelling, living arrangements, and selected maternal socioeconomic variables (e.g., employment status and breadwinner role).

Section B: beliefs about gender norms (adapted gnas items)

Beliefs about gender norms were assessed using 13 items adapted from the Gender Norm Attitudes Scale (GNAS). The GNAS conceptualizes beliefs along a continuum from traditional to egalitarian, and includes two domains: (1) Belief in Maintaining the Rights and Privileges of Men (traditional beliefs), and (2) Belief in and Promotion of Equity for Girls and Women (egalitarian beliefs) [12]. All 13 items were culturally relevant to the Ghanaian context and were therefore retained with no wording refinements (see Table 1 for all 13 items). Responses were recorded on a three-point Likert-type scale, with scoring directions tailored to each domain: (1) Belief in Maintaining the Rights and Privileges of Men: 1 = Agree, 2 = Partially agree, 3 = Disagree (higher scores reflect more egalitarian beliefs), and (2) Belief in and Promotion of Equity for Girls and Women (reverse-scored): 3 = Agree, 2 = Partially agree, 1 = Disagree (higher scores reflect stronger support for gender equity).

Section C: attitudes toward gender norms (adapted gem scale items)

Attitudes toward gender norms were measured using 24 adapted items from the inequitable subscale of the Gender Equitable Men (GEM) Scale. The GEM Scale assesses attitudes across four conceptual domains widely identified in global applications: (1) Violence, (2) Sexual relationships, (3) Reproductive health and disease prevention, and (4) Domestic chores and daily life [7]. All items were rated on a three-point Likert scale (1 = Agree, 2 = Partially agree, 3 = Do not agree), with higher scores indicating more gender-equitable attitudes. Most items were highly applicable to the Ghanaian sociocultural context and required virtually no adjustments for clarity. However, the homophobia-related item/domain, included in some international versions of the GEM Scale, was removed during adaptation because Ghana’s legal and policy environment criminalizes same-sex sexual relations, public discourse and socio-cultural norms make the topic highly sensitive, including such items risked discomfort, nonresponse, or biased responses, and ethical considerations required minimizing potential risk to participants. Cultural adaptation procedures

The adaptation process was guided by established cross-cultural instrument modification principles. Because many GNAS and GEM Scale items aligned naturally with Ghanaian gender-norm contexts, the adaptation required minimal content modifications. The research team conducted expert review to ensure semantic clarity, cultural appropriateness, and conceptual equivalence. To enhance item comprehensibility and cultural resonance, the adapted GNBAC underwent informal cognitive testing during the pilot test-retest exercise. Student feedback did not necessitate structural changes. This process strengthened the instrument’s relevance and acceptability without altering the theoretical foundations of either scale.

Test-retest reliability

A test-retest reliability assessment was conducted prior to the main survey to evaluate the temporal stability of the adapted GNAS and GEM Scale. The pilot involved students from the Sociology and Social Work programmes and first-year Diploma in Business Administration students at the KNUST Institute of Distance Learning. These groups were selected for accessibility and to ensure adequate variation within the target demographic of interest.

A total of 93 students completed the initial test (T1), and 53 students participated in the retest (T2) two weeks later. The two-week interval was chosen as it is sufficiently long to reduce recall bias but short enough to minimize the likelihood of real changes in gender-norm attitudes. Participants provided their phone numbers as identifiers, enabling the research team to match responses across the two time points. Using this procedure, 32 participants were successfully cross-matched as having completed both T1 and T2.

Test-retest reliability was evaluated in two complementary ways. First, Pearson correlations were computed at the item level within each scale and then averaged across items, so the reported standard deviation reflects the spread of the item-level coefficients within a scale rather than dispersion around a single value. Second, a scale-level coefficient was computed for each scale by correlating participants’ total scores at the two time points and by estimating an intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; two-way model, absolute agreement, single measures). The results indicated strong temporal stability. At the item level, the mean correlation was r = .84 (SD across items = 0.315) for the GNAS and r = .87 (SD across items = 0.271) for the GEM Scale. At the scale level, the GNAS total score showed r = .85 and ICC = 0.85 (95% CI [0.71, 0.92]), and the GEM Scale total score showed r = .83 and ICC = 0.81 (95% CI [0.65, 0.91]).

All coefficients reflect strong temporal stability, exceeding commonly accepted thresholds for adequate test-retest reliability in attitudinal and psychosocial research. Together, these findings suggest that participants’ beliefs and attitudes toward gender norms remained stable across the measurement interval and that the adapted scales demonstrate reliability when applied among higher-education students in Ghana.

Data collection

Data collection was carried out concurrently at the University of Ghana and the Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology in February 2025. A paper-based questionnaire was used for the main survey to maximize participation, following preliminary attempts at electronic distribution that yielded limited responses. This approach ensured broad coverage across academic levels and minimized non-response among students who were more likely to engage through in-class participation. At KNUST, data collection occurred over a three-day period, during which teaching assistants supported the distribution and retrieval of questionnaires during scheduled class sessions. At the University of Ghana, data collection was undertaken by a trained research assistant from the Department of Sociology across twelve days. In addition to classroom-based administration, the research assistant approached eligible students in informal departmental spaces such as libraries and reading areas to enhance participation among those not captured during lectures.

Before completing the questionnaire, students were informed about the purpose of the study, procedures, the voluntary nature of participation, and confidentiality protections. Students who agreed to participate provided informed consent and completed the questionnaire immediately. Eligibility required that respondents be at least 18 years old and enrolled in the relevant academic programmes at the time of data collection. No incentives were provided. A total of 631 questionnaires were completed, yielding a 98.3% response rate, which is sufficient for the intended psychometric analyses.

Data management

The data obtained were coded and entered into SPSS version 32 over a two-week period, with a minimum of 20 questionnaires processed daily. Upon completion, the dataset was thoroughly reviewed for accuracy. Data cleaning focused on eliminating coding errors and addressing potential processing inconsistencies. The validation tool in SPSS was employed to identify missing values and incorrect entries, ensuring the dataset was complete and error-free. No missing values were detected. Data analysis and software

Data analysis comprised descriptive statistics, exploratory factor analysis (EFA), confirmatory factor analysis (CFA), internal consistency reliability estimation, measurement invariance testing, and differential item functioning (DIF) analysis. To support cross-validation of factor structures, the full dataset (N = 631) was randomly split in SPSS Version 32, using a fixed random seed to ensure reproducibility. A 50/50 split was applied, assigning each participant an equal probability of allocation to either subsample. The dataset was randomly split using SPSS Version 32 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) into two subsamples: Subsample 1 (n = 339) for EFA and Subsample 2 (n = 364) for CFA. Both subsamples exceeded minimum sample size recommendations for their respective analytic phases.

For the EFA, two analyses were run on Subsample 1, an EFA based on Pearson correlations and an EFA based on polychoric correlations, both using Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) with Direct Oblimin rotation, so that the two solutions could be compared directly. The polychoric analyses were estimated in R Version 4.3.2 with the psych package using the polychoric() and fa() functions. Decisions about how many factors to retain were made separately from extraction: factor retention was guided by parallel analysis based on the polychoric correlation matrix (95th percentile), together with the Kaiser eigenvalue-greater-than-1.0 rule and inspection of the scree plot, all computed in the psych package [fa.parallel() and scree() functions]. CFA was estimated on Subsample 2 under both Maximum Likelihood (ML) and Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance Adjusted (WLSMV) estimators using the lavaan package in R. Reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega, computed in R via the psych package. Measurement invariance was examined for the two grouping variables with adequate group sizes, sex and university, using the standard nested sequence of configural, metric, and scalar models. These models were estimated by maximum likelihood, consistent with the primary CFA estimator, and invariance at each step was assessed using changes in fit indices (ΔCFI, ΔRMSEA, ΔTLI, and ΔSRMR).

Programme and academic year level were not subjected to formal invariance testing, because some groups (in particular Level 100 and the Social Work programme) were too small to support stable multi-group estimation; invariance across these variables is therefore left as a question for future work. The ΔCFI thresholds proposed by Cheung and Rensvold [17] were used to interpret the comparisons: |ΔCFI| < 0.010 supporting invariance, |ΔCFI| < 0.020 indicating partial invariance, and |ΔCFI| ≥ 0.020 indicating questionable invariance. Differential item functioning was assessed using ordinal logistic regression implemented in R via the lordif package. All descriptive statistics and initial data management were performed in SPSS Version 32.

Descriptive statistics

Item-level response frequencies, means, standard deviations, and the percentage of responses at the floor (category 1) and ceiling (category 3) were computed to evaluate response distributions and assess potential ceiling or floor effects. Subscale domain means were also computed, with scoring directions standardised so that higher scores reflect stronger disagreement with inequitable norms or stronger endorsement of equitable beliefs.

Exploratory Factor Analysis (EFA)

EFA was conducted on Subsample 1. The factorability of the correlation matrix was checked using the Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure of sampling adequacy and Bartlett’s test of sphericity. Principal Axis Factoring (PAF) with Direct Oblimin rotation was used as the extraction method, which is appropriate when the underlying constructs are expected to correlate. Because all items were measured on 3-point ordinal response scales, an additional EFA based on polychoric correlations was conducted using the same PAF extraction and Direct Oblimin rotation, so that the Pearson-based and polychoric-based solutions could be compared directly. Polychoric correlations account for the ordinal measurement level and give more accurate estimates of inter-item associations than Pearson correlations when response categories are few and distributions are skewed [18]. Decisions about how many factors to retain were treated as a separate step from extraction. Factor retention was guided by parallel analysis based on the polychoric correlation matrix (95th percentile criterion), the Kaiser eigenvalue-greater-than-1.0 rule, inspection of the scree plot, communalities of at least 0.30, primary loadings of at least 0.35, minimal cross-loadings (< 0.30), and theoretical interpretability. Parallel analysis, Kaiser criterion checks, and scree plots were produced in R using the psych package [fa.parallel() and scree() functions]; the Pearson PAF solutions and their scree plots were also cross-checked in SPSS Version 32. The polychoric EFA solution was compared with the Pearson PAF solution to see whether treating items as ordinal materially altered the emerging factor structures (RQ2). Item-total correlations and average inter-item correlations were computed for each subscale to supplement the factor-analytic evidence.

Confirmatory Factor Analysis (CFA)

CFA was conducted on Subsample 2 to test the factor structures identified through EFA. Two estimators were applied to each model. Maximum Likelihood (ML) estimation was used as the primary estimator for comparability with prior studies. Because ML assumes continuous indicators and approximate multivariate normality, assumptions that are violated by 3-point ordinal items, particularly those exhibiting ceiling effects, each model was also estimated using the Weighted Least Squares Mean and Variance Adjusted (WLSMV) estimator, which is specifically recommended for ordinal polytomous indicators [19]. Fit was evaluated using the chi-square statistic, Comparative Fit Index (CFI), Tucker-Lewis Index (TLI), Root Mean Square Error of Approximation (RMSEA; with 90% confidence intervals), and Standardised Root Mean Square Residual (SRMR). Conventional cut-offs were applied: CFI and TLI ≥ 0.95 for good fit and ≥ 0.90 for acceptable fit; RMSEA ≤ 0.06 for good fit and ≤ 0.08 for acceptable fit; SRMR ≤ 0.08 [20]. Standardised factor loadings, residual variances, and factor correlation matrices were reported for both estimators.

Internal consistency reliability

Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha and McDonald’s omega for each subscale and composite scale [21]. McDonald’s omega is reported in addition to alpha as it provides a less assumption-dependent estimate of reliability that does not require tau-equivalence across items [21]. Average inter-item correlations (AIC) are also reported as a complement to alpha, given that alpha is sensitive to the number of items. Values of α and ω ≥ 0.70 were considered acceptable for research purposes.

Measurement invariance

To address whether the adapted scales can support valid subgroup comparisons, a prerequisite for their use in programme evaluation, multi-group CFA models were estimated for the GNAS and the GEM Scale across the two grouping variables with adequate group sizes: sex (male/female) and university (UG/KNUST). For each, the standard nested sequence of invariance models was fitted: (i) a configural model with the same factor structure across groups but all parameters freely estimated within each group; (ii) a metric model that additionally constrained factor loadings to be equal across groups; and (iii) a scalar model that additionally constrained item intercepts to be equal across groups. The models were estimated with maximum likelihood, consistent with the primary CFA estimator. Invariance at each step was assessed using the change in fit indices between adjacent nested models, defined as ΔCFI = CFI (less constrained) – CFI (more constrained), together with the corresponding ΔRMSEA, ΔTLI, and ΔSRMR. Following Cheung and Rensvold [17], |ΔCFI| < 0.010 was read as supporting invariance, |ΔCFI| < 0.020 as suggesting partial invariance, and |ΔCFI| ≥ 0.020 as indicating questionable invariance. Programme and academic year level were not subjected to formal invariance testing, because several groups (most notably Level 100 and the Social Work programme) were too small to support stable multi-group estimation. Invariance across these variables is therefore treated as an open question for future work, ideally with larger per-group samples and ordinal (WLSMV) estimation, before strong claims about cross-group comparability of GNAS or GEM Scale scores are advanced.

Differential Item Functioning (DIF)

To assess whether individual items function differently across groups in ways that could distort comparisons, DIF analyses were restricted to the two grouping variables with sufficient sample sizes to support stable ordinal logistic regression models, namely sex (male vs. female) and university (UG vs. KNUST). DIF analyses at the programme and academic-year levels were not attempted because group sizes (particularly at Level 100 and within the Social Work programme) were too small to support reliable estimation of group-by-trait interactions for individual items. For each item, a baseline model regressing the item response on the total scale score was compared with an augmented model that additionally included group membership. A change in pseudo-R² of ΔR² ≥ 0.035 was used as the threshold for flagging an item as exhibiting meaningful DIF [22].

All relevant ethical issues related to the study were carefully addressed to ensure the protection and respect of all participants involved, adhering to the ethical principles and guidelines for the protection of human subjects of research as outlined in the Belmont Report (The National Commission for the Protection of Human Subjects of Biomedical and Behavioral Research, 1978) and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. The initial EFA of all 13 GNAS items produced an unstable three-factor solution: the iterative PAF estimation failed to reach convergence within the default 25-iteration ceiling supplied by SPSS; two items showed extraction communalities below the 0.30 retention threshold (h² = 0.014 and 0.212); and these same items also displayed cross-loadings above |0.30| on more than one factor. The default 25-iteration ceiling was retained as a conservative indicator of non-convergence rather than forcing convergence by raising the iteration limit, because persistent failure to converge within this default ceiling, together with low communalities and cross-loadings on the same two items, indicated a poorly identified solution rather than a slowly but stably converging one. Two items were identified as psychometrically problematic and removed: “Daughters should be told that an important reason not to have too many children is so they can work outside the home and earn money” (communalities: initial = 0.040, extraction = 0.014) and “A woman must have a husband or sons or some other male kinsman/relative to protect her” (communalities: initial = 0.187, extraction = 0.212). Both items also exhibited cross-loadings across multiple factors. Beyond their psychometric limitations, these items warrant a substantive note: the first item targets daughters specifically regarding childbearing decisions and may have limited local relevance, while the second item reflects a kinship protection norm that may not resonate uniformly among this academically engaged population. Their removal reduces the scale’s coverage of dependent female status norms, a limitation acknowledged in the Discussion.

A second EFA on the remaining 11 items yielded a stable, interpretable two-factor solution. Eigenvalue inspection confirmed two factors with eigenvalues greater than 1.0 (λ₁ = 3.570, λ₂ = 2.098; all subsequent eigenvalues < 1.0), and parallel analysis at the 95th percentile corroborated retention of exactly two factors (PA thresholds: λ₁ = 1.374, λ₂ = 1.263). The two factors together accounted for 44.52% of the total variance under PAF, and 51.5% under polychoric EFA (see Table 6). Item communalities ranged from 0.314 to 0.807, all exceeding the 0.30 retention threshold.

Factor correlations from the polychoric EFA were near-zero, indicating that the two dimensions of gender-norm beliefs operate largely independently within this sample. Item-total correlations for Factor 1 ranged from 0.458 to 0.581 (average IIC = 0.372), and for Factor 2 from 0.657 to 0.744 (average IIC = 0.619), indicating adequate to strong within-factor item consistency. Measurement validity and reliability of the GEM Scale

Exploratory factor analysis of the GEM Scale

EFA of the 24-item GEM Scale was conducted on Subsample 1 using PAF with Direct Oblimin rotation. The KMO was 0.837 and Bartlett’s test was significant (χ²(276) = 1942.14, p < .001). The polychoric EFA produced comparable factorability indices (KMO = 0.809; Bartlett’s χ²(120) = 1137.13, p < .001).

The initial extraction yielded six factors with eigenvalues > 1.0, accounting for approximately 44% of the total variance. However, seven items demonstrated persistent psychometric problems, i.e., low communalities (< 0.30), absence of a clear primary loading, or cross-loadings across multiple factors, and were removed. These items addressed condom use and negotiation, reproductive responsibility, disgust at male femininity, household purchasing decisions, and sexual communication. Their removal is substantial and warrants substantive reflection: the dropped items span the Reproductive Health and Disease Prevention domain almost entirely, meaning this original conceptual domain is no longer represented in the adapted scale. This construct underrepresentation is acknowledged as a limitation, and is discussed further below.

A second EFA on the remaining 16 GEM items yielded a stable four-factor solution. Factorability of the reduced 16-item matrix was again supported (KMO = 0.816; Bartlett’s χ²(120) = 1307.43, p < .001), confirming that the reduced item set remained suitable for factor analysis. Turning to the EFA solution itself, eigenvalues under PAF were λ₁ = 3.961, λ₂ = 2.001, λ₃ = 1.294, λ₄ = 1.152; all subsequent eigenvalues fell below 1.0. Parallel analysis based on the polychoric correlation matrix (95th percentile, computed in the psych package) suggested that two factors clearly exceeded the parallel-analysis threshold (λ₁ = 1.477, λ₂ = 1.360), while Factors 3 and 4 were borderline (λ₃ = 1.294 vs. PA threshold = 1.295; λ₄ = 1.152 vs. PA threshold = 1.237). Given that the four-factor structure aligns with the theoretical GEM Scale domains and that each factor was conceptually interpretable and internally consistent, retention of four factors was judged appropriate on theoretical grounds. The four factors together accounted for 52.6% of total variance under polychoric EFA (see Table 6).

The rotated pattern matrix revealed a clean structure with no cross-loadings exceeding |0.30| and all primary loadings exceeding 0.35. Factor 1: Violence, comprised six items reflecting norms that legitimize or normalize physical harm toward women (loadings: 0.413 to 0.714). Factor 2: Domestic Chores and Daily Life, comprised three items capturing traditional expectations around household labor and sexual readiness (loadings: 0.463 to 0.594). Factor 3: Sexual Relationships, comprised five items reflecting inequitable sexual-conduct and sexual-morality norms (loadings: 0.437 to 0.778). Factor 4: Household Authority and Obedience, comprised two items reflecting hierarchical gender-power relations within the home: “A man should have the final word about decisions in his home” (loading = 0.747) and “A woman should obey her husband in all things” (loading = 0.580). Communalities for retained items ranged from 0.369 to 0.755 (see Table 9).

Correction of factor labeling

The fourth factor has been relabeled from “Reproductive Health and Disease Prevention” to “Household Authority and Obedience”, which accurately reflects the content of the two retained items. This label more precisely captures the authority and obedience norms captured by this factor and avoids misrepresenting the domain as reproductive health and disease prevention, given that no reproductive health items were retained.

Confirmatory factor analysis of the GEM Scale

CFA was conducted on Subsample 2 (n = 364) to test the four-factor, 16-item GEM structure under both ML and WLSMV estimators.

Under ML, the four-factor model produced acceptable fit: χ²(98) = 251.75, p < .001; CFI = 0.888; TLI = 0.865; RMSEA = 0.058, 90% CI [0.048, 0.068]; SRMR = 0.059. Although the CFI and TLI fall marginally below the conventional 0.90 threshold, the RMSEA and SRMR both meet recommended cut-offs, and this pattern of fit is consistent with prior applications of the GEM Scale in culturally adapted contexts. The WLSMV solution produced comparable fit, confirming that conclusions are not sensitive to estimator choice.

Standardized factor loadings were all statistically significant (p < .001). Violence factor loadings ranged from λ = 0.497 to λ = 0.765 (residual variances: 0.415 to 0.753). Domestic Chores and Daily Life loadings ranged from λ = 0.618 to λ = 0.821 (residual variances: 0.327 to 0.618). Sexual Relationships loadings ranged from λ = 0.393 to λ = 0.698 (residual variances: 0.512 to 0.846), with the item “Men are always ready to have sex” having the weakest loading (λ = 0.393, R² = 0.154), suggesting it contributes minimally to the Sexual Relationships factor and may warrant reconsideration in future adaptations. Household Authority and Obedience loadings were strong and equal for both items (λ = 0.872; residual variance = 0.239; R² = 0.761), confirming this as a tightly defined two-item dimension. All loadings and residual variances are reported in Table 9.

The standardized GEM factor correlation matrix revealed moderate to moderately-strong inter-factor associations (r = .207 to 0.483), with the strongest correlations between Domestic Chores and Household Authority (r = .483) and between Violence and Sexual Relationships (r = .413). Response distributions and ceiling effects (RQ7)

Response frequency distributions were examined to assess potential ceiling or floor effects that could limit the sensitivity of the adapted measures. For the GNAS, Factor 1 (Maintaining the Rights and Privileges of Men) items showed moderate to strong ceiling effects, with 72–86% of responses at category 3 (Disagree), consistent with the high egalitarian means reported and suggesting that this sample largely rejects traditional male-privilege beliefs. Factor 2 (Equity Promotion) items showed a more balanced distribution (57–61% at category 3), indicating greater response spread for egalitarian belief items.

For the GEM Scale, Violence items showed strong ceiling effects (65–92% at category 3 = “Do not agree”), consistent with near-uniform rejection of violence-supportive norms. In contrast, Domestic Chores and Daily Life items showed considerably more distributional spread: 42–48% at category 3, with 30–41% at category 1 (Agree), indicating that endorsement of traditional domestic role norms was more prevalent in this sample. Household Authority items similarly showed a more even distribution (39–53% in category 3), confirming that these domains are more contested. The Sexual Relationships item “Men are always ready to have sex” showed a near-uniform distribution across all three response categories, which is consistent with its weak CFA loading and suggests this item may not be measuring a coherent component of sexual-relationship norms in this population.

These distributional patterns confirm that the high overall means reported across both scales partly reflect ceiling effects, particularly in the Violence and Men’s Privileges domains. The 3-point response scale limits discrimination at the upper end of both constructs; future research should consider 5- or 7-point scales to improve sensitivity.

GNAS invariance

For the GNAS, the configural model fitted well in both groupings (sex: CFI = 0.936, RMSEA = 0.070; university: CFI = 0.935, RMSEA = 0.072). Constraining factor loadings to be equal (metric model) produced only a small change in fit by sex (ΔCFI = − 0.005, ΔRMSEA = − 0.001) and by university (ΔCFI = − 0.004, ΔRMSEA = − 0.002), both within the |ΔCFI| < 0.010 threshold, supporting metric invariance. Constraining item intercepts as well (scalar model) supported scalar invariance across university (ΔCFI = − 0.006) but not fully across sex (ΔCFI = − 0.022, ΔTLI = − 0.017), indicating partial scalar invariance by sex. Taken together, the GNAS shows stable loadings across sex and university and stable intercepts across university, so that loading-based comparisons are supported across both variables, and mean-level comparisons are supported across university.

GEM Scale invariance

For the adapted GEM Scale, the configural model fitted less well (sex: CFI = 0.875; university: CFI = 0.858). Metric invariance was supported across university (ΔCFI = − 0.006) but not across sex (ΔCFI = − 0.016), and scalar invariance was not supported in either grouping (sex: ΔCFI = − 0.019; university: ΔCFI = − 0.022). These results indicate that the four-factor structure of the adapted GEM Scale does not operate equivalently across male and female students, and that item intercepts differ across both sex and university. Direct between-group comparisons of GEM subscale scores, for example comparing male and female students on violence-norm endorsement, should therefore be treated with caution until at least metric and scalar invariance can be established.

Belief in maintaining the rights and privileges of men

The domain mean for Factor 1 was 2.70 (SD = 0.41), indicating that students generally tended to disagree with statements endorsing traditional gender-hierarchical norms. Notably, 72–87% of respondents selected “Disagree” across the eight items in this domain, pointing to a strong and largely consistent rejection of beliefs that privilege men over women. The highest level of disagreement was observed for the item “Daughters should be sent to school only if they are not needed to help at home” (M = 2.80, SD = 0.55; 86.8% disagreed), followed by items on sons’ educational priority (M = 2.73, SD = 0.57; 80.4% disagreed) and the expectation that educational resources should favor boys (M = 2.67, SD = 0.65; 76.4% disagreed). Students similarly rejected beliefs concerning women’s political exclusion (M = 2.65, SD = 0.63; 73.3% disagreed), the idea that women can only rely on their sons in old age (M = 2.63, SD = 0.65; 72.1% disagreed), expectations that women should defer unconditionally to their husbands’ opinions (M = 2.70, SD = 0.61; 78.0% disagreed), the notion that children’s healthcare decisions should rest solely with fathers (M = 2.68, SD = 0.62; 76.4% disagreed), and the belief that a son’s education is more important than a daughter’s to support ageing parents (M = 2.73, SD = 0.57; 80.4% disagreed).

However, these high means should be interpreted alongside their response distributions. For all eight items, 6–10% of students agreed, and a further 6–20% partially agreed, indicating that a meaningful minority continues to endorse traditional male-privilege beliefs to varying degrees. This residual endorsement is especially notable for items addressing women’s political participation (8.4% agreed; 18.3% partially agreed) and the reliance on sons in old age (9.5% agreed; 18.3% partially agreed), suggesting that while normative rejection is dominant, traditional beliefs remain present in a non-trivial segment of this academically engaged sample.

Belief in and promotion of equity for girls and women

The domain mean for Factor 2 was 2.35 (SD = 0.73), reflecting broad but not universal agreement with statements promoting educational and economic opportunities for girls and women. Approximately 59–61% of respondents agreed with each of the three equity items, with a further 15–16% partially agreeing, suggesting that strong support for gender equity coexists with some ambivalence. Specifically, respondents endorsed daughters’ right to equal opportunities in the workforce (M = 2.33, SD = 0.86; 59.2% agreed), daughters’ right to work outside the home after having children (M = 2.35, SD = 0.85; 59.3% agreed), and the view that daughters should be supported in achieving economic self-sufficiency (M = 2.37, SD = 0.84; 60.7% agreed). The relatively higher standard deviations in this domain (SD = 0.84–0.86) compared with Factor 1 (SD = 0.55–0.65) indicate greater variability in responses, consistent with a sample in which support for gender equity is positive overall but not uniformly held.

Overall GNAS

The overall GNAS mean was 2.60 (SD = 0.38), indicating that, on average, the students expressed egalitarian orientations across both belief domains. This aggregate score masks meaningful within-sample variation: while the rejection of male-privilege beliefs was near-uniform (Factor 1 M = 2.70), support for equity promotion was more moderate and variable (Factor 2 M = 2.35). Taken together, these findings suggest that the adapted GNAS captures a meaningful gradient in gender-norm beliefs within this population, in which rejecting traditional inequalities and actively endorsing equity are empirically separable orientations, consistent with the validated two-factor structure.

However, domain-level and item-level variation revealed meaningful differences in the degree and consistency of this rejection, with some domains showing near-unanimous disagreement and others reflecting more contested attitudes. Violence

The Violence domain yielded the highest mean score among the four GEM Scale domains (M = 2.76, SD = 0.36), indicating widespread rejection of violence-supportive norms. Consistent disagreement was observed across all six items. Rejection was strongest for the statement “It is okay for a man to hit his wife if she won’t have sex with him” (M = 2.90, SD = 0.38; 92.5% did not agree), followed by “If a woman cheats on a man, it is okay for him to hit her” (M = 2.83, SD = 0.49; 87.2% did not agree), “A woman should tolerate violence to keep her family together” (M = 2.81, SD = 0.51; 85.6% did not agree), “There are times when a woman deserves to be beaten” (M = 2.78, SD = 0.55; 85.1% did not agree), and “A man using violence against his wife is a private matter that shouldn’t be discussed outside the couple” (M = 2.72, SD = 0.61; 79.9% did not agree). The only item with relatively lower, though still predominantly disagreement-oriented, responses was “If someone insults a man, he should defend his reputation with force if he must” (M = 2.53, SD = 0.70; 65.4% did not agree), which also had the highest proportion of partial agreement (22.6%) in this domain. This item reflects norms around masculine honour and reputational defence, which appear somewhat more contested in this sample than straightforward partner violence items, possibly reflecting the cultural salience of honour-based masculinity norms in the Ghanaian context.

Domestic chores and daily life

The Domestic Chores and Daily Life domain yielded the lowest mean score in the adapted GEM Scale (M = 2.08, SD = 0.59), indicating considerably more variability and greater endorsement of traditional gender-role expectations compared with other domains. Responses were distributed across all three categories for each item, reflecting genuine heterogeneity of attitudes. The item with the highest endorsement of traditional norms was “A woman’s role is taking care of her home and family” (M = 1.83, SD = 0.80; 41.8% agreed, 33.7% partially agreed), with only 24.5% disagreeing, making this the most traditionally endorsed item in the entire adapted GEM Scale. Similarly, “Changing diapers, giving a bath, and feeding kids is the mother’s responsibility” produced a mean of 2.12 (SD = 0.84), with 30.3% agreeing. The third item in this domain, “Men need sex more than women do,” yielded a mean of 2.30 (SD = 0.77; 19.2% agreed; 31.6% partially agreed), indicating moderate but meaningful endorsement of beliefs about male sexual drives as a naturalised domestic and relational expectation.

The relatively low and variable mean for this domain confirms that traditional expectations around household labor and gender-differentiated domestic responsibilities are more deeply embedded, or more slowly shifting, in this population than norms around overt violence or sexual morality. These findings are consistent with broader evidence from sub-Saharan Africa showing that domestic role divisions are among the most persistent dimensions of gender-inequitable norms, even in highly educated populations [23, 24].

Sexual relationships

The Sexual Relationships domain had a domain mean of 2.58 (SD = 0.38), indicating a general tendency toward rejecting inequitable sexual norms, though with more variation than the Violence domain. Respondents most strongly rejected the norm that a man’s identity is defined by fathering a son (M = 2.78, SD = 0.57; 86.2% did not agree) and norms equating female sexual worth with pre-marital chastity (M = 2.75, SD = 0.58; 81.9% did not agree) or confining womanhood to motherhood (M = 2.62, SD = 0.66; 72.0% did not agree). They also rejected male sexual entitlement, specifically the view that men require additional partners even in stable relationships (M = 2.73, SD = 0.59; 79.9% did not agree), and norms restricting women’s sexual agency, with the majority disagreeing that women should not initiate sex (M = 2.62, SD = 0.66; 72.0% did not agree).

The most notable item in this domain was “Men are always ready to have sex” (M = 2.02, SD = 0.79), which showed a near-uniform distribution across all three response categories (30.4% agreed; 37.4% partially agreed; 32.1% did not agree). This item also had the weakest standardized CFA loading in the Sexual Relationships factor (λ = 0.393, R² = 0.154), suggesting it is measuring something conceptually distinct from the other sexual-relationship items. Its near-even distribution and low factor loading together suggest that beliefs about male sexual readiness as a biological constant may occupy a different cognitive register than norms around sexual exclusivity, initiation, or morality, and may warrant reconsideration in future adaptations of the scale.

Household authority and obedience

The “Reproductive Health and Disease Prevention” domain in the original GEM Scale, which is relabeled here as the “Household Authority and Obedience” domain to accurately reflect the content of its two retained items, had a domain mean of 2.20 (SD = 0.71). This domain captures norms concerning men’s ultimate decision-making authority and women’s expected compliance within the home. Responses were more evenly distributed than in the Violence domain, indicating that these norms remain contested among students. The item “A woman should obey her husband in all things” had the lower mean (M = 2.07, SD = 0.84), with 32.3% agreeing and 28.7% partially agreeing, meaning 61% of respondents expressed some level of agreement with unconditional female obedience to husbands. Similarly, “A man should have the final word about decisions in his home” had a mean of 2.32 (SD = 0.80), with 20.6% agreeing and 26.3% partially agreeing. While overall disagreement was the modal response for both items, the proportions of agreement and partial agreement are notably higher than in the Violence or Sexual Relationships domains, confirming that hierarchical household authority norms are more deeply contested or more culturally normalised in this population.

This finding has important implications for gender-transformative programming: even among Sociology and Social Work students who overwhelmingly reject physical violence and sexual entitlement norms, a substantial proportion continues to accept gendered authority structures within the household. This suggests that programmatic efforts in higher-education settings should specifically target household decision-making and obedience norms as a priority domain.

Overall GEM scale

The overall GEM Scale mean was 2.51 (SD = 0.32), reflecting a general tendency to reject gender-inequitable attitudes across all four domains. However, domain-level variation ranged from M = 2.76 for Violence to M = 2.08 for Domestic Chores and Daily Life, a difference of 0.68 scale points, underscoring that the degree of normative rejection is far from uniform. Attitudes toward violence are near-universally rejected in this sample, attitudes toward sexual relationships are largely, but not unconditionally, rejected, and attitudes toward household authority and domestic roles are the most heterogeneous. This gradient is substantively meaningful and should inform the prioritisation of domains in gender-transformative interventions targeting Ghanaian university students.

Discussion

This study examined the factor structures, reliability, measurement invariance, and item-level functioning of adapted versions of the Gender Norm Attitudes Scale (GNAS) and the Gender Equitable Men (GEM) Scale among Sociology and Social Work students at two Ghanaian public universities. Guided by a measurement argument that the intended use of a scale determines which validity evidence is most essential, the analyses went beyond standard EFA and CFA to assess whether the adapted instruments support cross-group comparisons and programme evaluation purposes for which they are proposed. The results provide a substantially more complete picture of the psychometric properties of both scales in the Ghanaian higher-education context than has previously been available.

Validity and structure of the GNAS

The adapted GNAS demonstrated a two-factor structure reflecting beliefs maintaining the rights and privileges of men (Factor 1, 8 items) and beliefs promoting equity for girls and women (Factor 2, 3 items). This structure replicated cleanly across both estimation approaches: the PAF-based EFA and the polychoric EFA produced structurally equivalent solutions, directly addressing the question of whether treating items as ordinal materially alters factor solutions (RQ2). The convergence of results across ML-CFA and WLSMV-CFA further confirms that the two-factor structure is consistent with the continuous-data assumption. Fit indices under ML were strong (CFI = .960; TLI = .949; RMSEA = .057; SRMR = .047), meeting the stringent ≥ .95 threshold for CFI and TLI. All standardized loadings were significant (λ = .60–.90, all p < .001), and residual variances were well-within acceptable ranges. Reliability was strong for both subscales (Factor 1: α = .824, ω = .868; Factor 2: α = .830, ω = .898) and for the full scale (α = .775, ω = .810). These values exceed the alpha coefficients of .70 and .67 reported for the original Egyptian GNAS validation [12], indicating stronger internal coherence in this adaptation and supporting the scale's construct validity in this population.

Two items were removed prior to the final EFA, i.e., “Daughters should be told that an important reason not to have too many children is so they can work outside the home and earn money” (B12) and “A woman must have a husband or sons or some other male kinsman/relative to protect her” (B6), on the basis of very low communalities and cross-loadings. While these removals were empirically justified, they warrant substantive reflection. Item B12 targets daughters specifically regarding childbearing decisions, a framing that may not resonate uniformly with a university student population accustomed to academic discourses on reproductive autonomy. Item B6 reflects a kinship protection norm that may carry different cultural salience among educated young adults compared with broader community samples. Their removal reduces the adapted GNAS’s coverage of dependent female status norms, an acknowledged limitation discussed further below. The two-factor solution that emerges from the remaining 11 items nonetheless preserves the scale’s core theoretical distinction between endorsing traditional male privilege and actively promoting gender equity, confirming that these are empirically separable orientations in this population (RQ3). The near-zero factor correlation (r = .17) is consistent with this interpretation: rejecting male-privilege beliefs and endorsing equity beliefs are related but not redundant dimensions of gender-norm ideology.

The descriptive findings revealed that Factor 1 items showed moderate to strong ceiling effects (72-87% of responses at category 3 - Disagree), consistent with near-uniform rejection of traditional male-privilege beliefs in this academically engaged sample. Factor 2 items showed more distributional spread (57-61% at category 3 - Agree), indicating positive but less uniform support for gender equity. These distributional patterns suggest that the current 3-point scale may be insufficiently sensitive to capture the full range of variation in equity beliefs among students who are generally supportive, a limitation with direct implications for the scale’s discriminatory power in future intervention evaluations. Ceiling effects in the Men's Privileges domain align with broader evidence from sub-Saharan Africa suggesting that formal education, particularly in social science disciplines, is associated with more egalitarian gender beliefs [25, 26]. However, a non-trivial minority, 6-10% agreeing and 6-20% partially agreeing across Factor 1 items, indicates that traditional beliefs remain present in this population, and should not be overlooked in programme design.

Validity and structure of the GEM Scale

The adapted GEM Scale demonstrated a four-factor, 16-item structure corresponding to Violence, Domestic Chores and Daily Life, Sexual Relationships, and Household Authority and Obedience. It is important to note that because seven items were removed during the factor analysis, including the near-complete loss of the original Reproductive Health and Disease Prevention domain, and because one of the four retained factors rests on only two items, the present analyses do not constitute validation of the GEM Scale as originally conceived. They instead document the structural and reliability properties of a substantially modified, partially reconstructed adaptation of the GEM Scale with narrower domain coverage, calibrated to the Ghanaian higher-education context. The findings should therefore not be interpreted as evidence of full cross-contextual validity for the original instrument. CFA fit under ML was acceptable (CFI = 0.888; TLI = 0.865; RMSEA = 0.058; SRMR = 0.059), consistent with the pattern commonly observed in culturally adapted gender-norm measures, where the multidimensional and context-specific nature of attitudes tends to constrain incremental fit indices [7]. The WLSMV estimator produced a comparable solution, confirming that the marginal fit values are not an artefact of the continuous-data assumption. Standardized loadings were significant (p < .001) and substantively meaningful across most items, with the exception of item C8 (“Men are always ready to have sex”), which had the weakest loading in the Sexual Relationships factor (λ = 0.393, R² = 0.154) and a near-uniform response distribution (30.4% agreed, 37.4% partially agreed, 32.1% did not agree). These patterns together suggest that C8 may be measuring a distinct dimension, perhaps the naturalisation of male sexual drive as a biological constant, that operates somewhat independently of the other sexual-conduct and sexual-morality norms in this factor. The retention of items with relatively weak standardized loadings (e.g., C8, and items with loadings approaching 0.50 in the Violence and Sexual Relationships factors) further qualifies the structural-validity argument and indicates that the adapted scale’s factor solution should be regarded as provisional. Future adaptations should consider whether this item belongs to the Sexual Relationships factor or constitutes a separate dimension of gendered sexual expectations.

An important construct validity concern arises from the item-removal process. Seven items were removed prior to the final EFA, including all items originally comprising the Reproductive Health and Disease Prevention domain (condom use, reproductive responsibility, pregnancy avoidance). This near-complete domain loss represents significant construct underrepresentation, a dimension of gender-inequitable attitudes that was theoretically central to the original GEM Scale is absent from the adapted version. This limitation is compounded by the fact that the fourth factor, relabeled here from “Reproductive Health and Disease Prevention” to Household Authority and Obedience to accurately reflect the content of its two retained items, is a 2-item subscale with a Spearman-Brown reliability of 0.668, which falls below conventional thresholds. Two-item subscales are inherently fragile and vulnerable to measurement error. Notwithstanding these limitations, the Household Authority and Obedience factor captures a dimension of gender-norm attitudes: men’s unilateral household decision-making authority and women’s expected obedience, which proved to be one of the most contested and variable domains in the descriptive results. Its emergence as a distinct factor, even with only two items, is substantively meaningful and has theoretical grounding in Ghanaian patriarchal household structures [6].

The removal of reproductive health and disease prevention items also raises the question of whether the adapted GEM Scale retains content validity for the original construct as a whole. The original scale’s conceptualisation included reproductive norms as a core domain of gender-inequitable male attitudes [7]. Future research in Ghana should attempt to develop and validate new items that capture reproductive health and condom-negotiation norms in culturally appropriate language, rather than simply accepting the construct underrepresentation as a permanent feature of the adaptation.

Reliability estimates varied across the GEM Scale subscales. Violence (α = 0.767, ω = 0.851) and the full scale (α = 0.778, ω = 0.857) showed adequate to strong reliability. The Domestic Chores and Daily Life (α = 0.572, ω = 0.778) and Sexual Relationships (α = 0.578, ω = 0.758) subscales had weak alpha values, well below the conventional 0.70 threshold for research use, though McDonald’s omega, which is less sensitive to violations of tau-equivalence, indicated stronger composite reliability in both cases. The discrepancy between alpha and omega for these subscales likely reflects conceptual breadth and item heterogeneity rather than poor reliability per se: each subscale includes items spanning somewhat different norm dimensions, and the general factor is stronger than alpha’s assumption of equal item contributions would suggest. However, even allowing for the conceptual interpretation of the alpha-omega discrepancy, the absolute alpha values for the Domestic Chores and Daily Life, Sexual Relationships, and Household Authority and Obedience subscales remain weak, and we do not regard the omega-based estimates as fully resolving these reliability concerns. The reliability picture is further qualified by the three-point Likert response format, which restricts variance and produces substantial ceiling effects across many items, especially in the Violence and Men’s Privileges domains. Consequently, both the factor structure and the reliability estimates for the adapted GEM Scale may partly reflect limitations of the response scale itself rather than stable underlying constructs, and the reliability of the short, conceptually heterogeneous GEM Scale subscales should be regarded as provisional.

Construct interpretation and the household authority domain

The descriptive findings for the GEM Scale revealed a meaningful gradient across domains: Violence attitudes were near-universally rejected (M = 2.76), Sexual Relationships norms were largely but not uniformly rejected (M = 2.58), while Domestic Chores (M = 2.08) and Household Authority and Obedience (M = 2.20) showed the greatest distributional spread and the highest proportions of agreement or partial agreement. Notably, 61% of students expressed some level of agreement, either agree or partially agree, with “A woman should obey her husband in all things” (C24), and 47% did so for “A man should have the final word about decisions in his home” (C23). These are striking findings in a population that overwhelmingly rejects violence and sexual entitlement norms, and they suggest that hierarchical authority and obedience norms within the domestic sphere represent a qualitatively different and more deeply embedded dimension of gender inequality in this context, one that is not adequately addressed by interventions focused primarily on violence prevention or sexual health.

These findings are broadly consistent with research from Ghana and sub-Saharan Africa showing that domestic gender norms, including household authority, labor division, and sexual readiness expectations, are among the most persistent and resistant to change even as other dimensions of gender inequality shift [6, 23, 24]. The finding that Sociology and Social Work students, who are routinely exposed to critical gender discourse in their academic programmes, continue to hold heterogeneous views on domestic authority shows the durability of these norms and the need for targeted, domain-specific intervention approaches.

Measurement invariance and implications for scale use

A central contribution of this study is the systematic evaluation of measurement invariance, a prerequisite for defensible subgroup comparisons that has been largely absent from prior applications of the GNAS and GEM Scale. Using the standard nested sequence of configural, metric, and scalar models, the GNAS showed metric invariance across both sex and university (|ΔCFI| ≤ 0.005), meaning that the factor loadings operate similarly across these groups. Scalar invariance, which is needed for comparing group means, held across university (ΔCFI = − 0.006) but was only partial across sex (ΔCFI = − 0.022). These results provide reasonable evidence that GNAS loadings are stable across the main grouping variables and that mean comparisons are defensible across university, while mean comparisons across sex should be made more cautiously until full scalar invariance is established. Because programme and academic year level could not be tested with stable multi-group models, invariance across these variables remains an open question; the weaker model fit seen among Level 100 students is consistent with the idea that attitudinal consolidation strengthens with educational exposure, and warrants attention in longitudinal work.

The adapted GEM Scale invariance results present a more cautious picture. Metric invariance held across university (ΔCFI = − 0.006) but not across sex (ΔCFI = − 0.016), and scalar invariance was not supported in either grouping (sex: ΔCFI = − 0.019; university: ΔCFI = − 0.022). The configural model also fitted the GEM Scale less well than the GNAS (sex: CFI = 0.875; university: CFI = 0.858), which indicates that the four-factor structure is less stable across groups. These differences could reflect genuine variation in how gender-inequitable attitudes are organised across groups, for example, less differentiation between domestic, sexual, and authority norms among some students, or they could reflect group-specific ceiling effects that compress the correlations in some subgroups. In either case, comparing GEM Scale subscale scores across sex or institution without further invariance testing risks producing misleading conclusions.

Importantly, the absence of differential item functioning for any GNAS or GEM Scale item across sex or university groups, with maximum ΔR² values of 0.007 and 0.018, respectively, well below the 0.035 DIF threshold, indicates that the measurement non-equivalence observed in the GEM Scale invariance analyses operates at the factor level rather than the item level. No individual item functions in a systematically biased way for one group relative to another. This distinction is meaningful: it suggests that the GEM Scale items themselves are not problematic, but that the higher-order factor structure through which they are organised may differ across groups. This is a more tractable problem than item-level bias, as it may be addressed through respecification of the measurement model (e.g., partial invariance constraints, or group-specific factor structures) in future research.

Implications for programme evaluation and gender-transformative interventions

The study’s measurement argument framing, asking what validity evidence is most essential for the intended use, allows for targeted conclusions about the suitability of each scale for different purposes. The adapted GNAS, with its metric invariance across sex and university, scalar invariance across university, and clean two-factor structure with adequate loadings and reliability, shows preliminary evidence of suitability for programme evaluation and subgroup comparisons on these dimensions among Sociology and Social Work students at Ghanaian public universities, with the caveat that mean comparisons across sex should remain cautious until full scalar invariance is established. Researchers and practitioners can use GNAS scores in this population with cautious confidence. Generalising the present invariance evidence to other student populations or to community samples without further validation work is, however, not encouraged.

The adapted GEM Scale's suitability is more conditional. Its four-factor structure, acceptable overall fit, and adequate reliability support its use for describing prevailing gender-equitable attitudes within a sample and for tracking attitude change over time within a group, applications that do not require cross-group measurement equivalence. However, its use for comparing male and female students, or UG and KNUST students, on GEM subscale scores should be approached with caution until at least metric and scalar invariance are established. The absence of DIF provides assurance at the item level, but factor-level non-equivalence means that mean differences on GEM Scale subscales across these groups cannot be straightforwardly interpreted as reflecting differences in the underlying attitudes.

The descriptive and structural findings together provide specific empirical guidance for gender-transformative programming in Ghanaian higher-education institutions. The near-universal rejection of violence-supportive norms indicates that violence prevention messaging may be largely preaching to the already-converted in this population, and that programmatic resources might be more productively directed toward the contested domains of household authority, domestic labor, and the naturalisation of male sexual drives, domains where attitudes are heterogeneous and where change may be more consequential for inequality outcomes.

Strengths, limitations, and future research

This study makes several methodological contributions relative to prior applications of the GNAS and GEM Scale in Ghana. The use of split-sample cross-validation (EFAs and CFAs on independent subsamples), the parallel application of continuous and ordinal estimators, the systematic evaluation of measurement invariance across four grouping variables, and the DIF analysis collectively constitute a substantially more rigorous psychometric evaluation than has previously been reported for these scales in this context. The high response rate (98.3%) and the absence of item-level missing data further strengthen the quality of the evidence base.

Nonetheless, some key limitations should be acknowledged. First, the sample is restricted to Sociology and Social Work students at two universities, a population that is more academically exposed to gender discourse than the general Ghanaian population and most other student groups. This restricts the generalisability of both the factor structures and the descriptive norms reported here. Future research should replicate the psychometric evaluation with students from science, technology, engineering, and mathematics disciplines, as well as with community and non-student samples, to establish whether the factor structures observed here generalise beyond socially sensitised populations.

Second, the 3-point Likert response format imposes a ceiling on both item variance and factor discrimination, as evidenced by the ceiling effects documented in the current study. Although 3-point formats are commonly used in gender-norm research for their simplicity and cultural accessibility, future studies should evaluate whether 5- or 7-point formats improve response discrimination, reliability, and factor model fit without sacrificing comprehension or cultural appropriateness in the Ghanaian context.

Third, the GEM Scale adaptation resulted in the near-complete loss of the Reproductive Health and Disease Prevention domain due to poor item performance. This construct underrepresentation should not be treated as a permanent feature of the Ghanaian adaptation. Future instrument development efforts should focus on generating new items that capture reproductive and condom-negotiation norms in contextually appropriate language, and that are psychometrically viable in the target population.

Fourth, the Household Authority and Obedience factor rests on only two retained items, limiting its reliability and structural stability. While its emergence as a conceptually coherent and empirically distinct dimension is theoretically meaningful, a 2-item subscale is inherently fragile. Future adaptations should seek to identify or develop additional items that capture the breadth of household authority and obedience norms, including, for example, norms about financial control, mobility restriction, and decision-making in reproductive matters.

Fifth, the social desirability bias concern is particularly salient in this study. Data were collected in classroom settings where students were aware of their instructors’ and the institution’s positions on gender equality. The ceiling effects observed, particularly for violence items, may partly reflect socially desirable responding rather than genuine attitudinal change. Future studies should consider incorporating validated social desirability measures or using indirect questioning techniques to assess the extent to which observed responses reflect internalised beliefs rather than situationally appropriate self-presentation.

Sixth, this study did not examine the relationship between gender-norm beliefs and attitudes and behavioural outcomes, for example, experiences of intimate partner violence, academic performance, or career aspirations. Establishing criterion validity by linking GNAS and GEM Scale scores to relevant behavioural or experiential outcomes would substantially strengthen the case for the adapted scales’ validity beyond their structural properties.

Finally, the invariance analyses reported here were estimated with maximum likelihood and tested loadings and intercepts across sex and university only. Confirming these results with ordinal (WLSMV) estimators, extending formal testing to programme and academic year level with larger per-group samples, and establishing full scalar invariance for the GEM Scale are necessary next steps before either scale is used for between-group comparison. Partial invariance models, or alignment methods that allow some non-invariant parameters while still permitting meaningful comparisons, should be explored as interim solutions.

Conclusion

This study examined the factor structures, reliability, measurement invariance, and item-level functioning of adapted versions of the Gender Norm Attitudes Scale (GNAS) and the Gender-Equitable Men (GEM) Scale among Sociology and Social Work students at two Ghanaian public universities. Guided by a measurement argument, the analyses reveal a differentiated picture that carries specific implications for how each scale can and cannot be used.

The adapted GNAS demonstrated acceptable structural validity and adequate-to-strong reliability across its two-factor, 11-item structure (Factor 1: α = .824, ω = .868; Factor 2: α = .830, ω = .898; CFI = .960; RMSEA = .057). Its factor structure was confirmed by both polychoric EFA and WLSMV-CFA. Metric invariance was supported across sex and university, and scalar invariance across university, using the standard nested sequence of configural, metric, and scalar models; no differential item functioning was detected for any item. These properties collectively provide preliminary support for the use of the adapted GNAS for within-group description and cautious cross-group comparisons among Sociology and Social Work students at Ghanaian public universities, pending formal nested invariance testing and replication in other student and community populations.

The adapted GEM Scale, as evaluated, represents a substantially modified version of the original instrument rather than a straightforward cross-cultural validation. After empirically driven item reduction, the retained four-factor, 16-item structure covers Violence, Domestic Chores and Daily Life, Sexual Relationships, and a culturally specific Household Authority and Obedience domain, with the near-complete loss of the original Reproductive Health and Disease Prevention domain producing meaningful construct underrepresentation. The Household Authority and Obedience factor is moreover based on only two items, which is psychometrically fragile and conceptually limited. Although overall fit was acceptable (CFI = .888; RMSEA = .058) and full-scale reliability adequate (ω = .857), several subscales showed weak Cronbach’s alpha values well below the conventional .70 threshold, and ceiling effects associated with the three-point response format may have constrained variance and inflated apparent structural coherence. Formal nested invariance testing indicated that metric invariance held across university but not sex, and that scalar invariance was not supported across either sex or university; accordingly, GEM subscale scores should not be compared across these subgroups until metric and scalar invariance have been established. Taken together, these findings should be read as preliminary psychometric evidence for a partially reconstructed adaptation of the GEM Scale in this specific Ghanaian student population, and the adapted scale should undergo further validation in broader and more diverse samples, ideally with a 5- or 7-point response format, before being used confidently in research or programme evaluation.

Descriptively, the students generally rejected violence-supportive norms but showed considerably greater variability in attitudes toward domestic roles and household authority, a gradient that should inform the prioritisation of content in gender-transformative programs targeting Ghanaian university students. These findings, together with the documented measurement properties, provide a context-specific evidence base for gender-norm research and programme evaluation in Ghanaian higher education and demonstrate the value of ordinal estimation, invariance testing, and DIF analysis as standard components of scale adaptation and validation.
