Combining Treatment and Histopathology: Understanding Kidney Recovery in Severe Antineutrophil Cytoplasmic Autoantibody–Associated Rapidly Progressive Glomerulonephritis

Published: 2025

Rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis owing to antineutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibody–associated vasculitis (AAV) remains one of the most challenging scenarios in nephrology. Ideally, the intensity of immunosuppressive therapy should balance the chance of kidney recovery against treatment toxicity. The introduction of avacopan, a C5a receptor inhibitor, represents a promising therapeutic advancement that may help to favorably modulate this balance. The ADVOCATE trial not only demonstrated a glucocorticoid (GC)-sparing effect but also superior recovery of kidney function, especially in patients with an estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) between 15 and 20 ml/min.1,2 However, real-world evidence on the effectiveness of avacopan remains limited, particularly in patients with advanced kidney impairment (eGFR < 15 ml/min), who were excluded from pivotal trials.3

In this context, Faguer et al.4 provide valuable insights by examining kidney outcomes in patients with AAV with severe rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis (eGFR: 0–30 ml/min) treated with avacopan plus low-dose GCs compared with high-dose steroid-based regimens. They confirm that avacopan enables a reduction in GC exposure, even in patients with an eGFR < 15 ml/min. However, this retrospective case-control study did not confirm a superior effect of avacopan on kidney recovery compared with GC regimens. Consequently, the treatment choice between GCs or avacopan was not a significant predictor of achieving an eGFR > 30 ml/min. Similarly, the choice of induction therapy (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, or their combination) did not correlate with improved outcomes.

Instead, histopathological features demonstrated stronger associations with kidney recovery.4 Therefore, they raise the important question on how baseline histopathologic differences might have influenced kidney recovery in the ADVOCATE study. Although this question is intriguing, their observation of comparable kidney recovery between avacopan and GCs must be interpreted cautiously because of the small patient numbers, the substantial heterogeneity in baseline eGFR (0–30 ml/min) and comparison with unmatched, historic controls, all restricting statistical power to detect meaningful differences. Nevertheless, these findings underscore the complex interplay between treatment and histopathology in determining renal prognosis and suggest a need to identify which patients are most likely to benefit from avacopan and which might not benefit.

Although histopathology has long been recognized for its prognostic value, its role in guiding clinical decision-making remains limited. Despite decades of research, histopathological scoring systems remain largely descriptive, informing prognosis, but are not yet actionable in real-time therapeutic decisions. This gap becomes increasingly problematic as treatment options diversify. Both the ant-neutrophil cytoplasmic autoantibody renal risk score and the Berden classification consistently demonstrate that the presence of unaffected glomeruli is associated with the most favorable prognosis, whereas sclerosis and fibrosis correlate with the poorest outcomes in terms of kidney survival and recovery.5,6 Faguer et al.4 confirm that sclerotic features are unlikely to be modifiable by avacopan, as might be expected.

More interesting from a clinical point of view is the high variability in kidney recovery among patients with a high percentage of crescents, seen both in the study of Faguer et al.4 and the study of Berden et al.5 In addition, Faguer et al. showed several patients exhibiting significant declines in kidney function between 6 and 12 months, despite the absence of clinical relapses.4 Notably, these declines occurred only in the GC-treated group, raising questions about smoldering disease activity, the role of baseline histopathological features, and whether avacopan might mitigate such progression. Understanding why in some patients, kidney function recovers despite extensive crescents, whereas in others, kidney function deteriorates later, may be key to making histopathological findings actionable.

Therefore, complement depositions in biopsies may offer a promising lead to explain variability in kidney recovery. As portrayed by the development of avacopan, the role of complement activation in AAV has gained increasing attention over the past decade. Several studies have demonstrated that the presence of complement deposits, particularly C3, in kidney biopsies is associated with poorer renal outcomes.7,8 This raises the possibility that complement activation within crescents may impair recovery, and that complement inhibition via avacopan could offer the greatest benefit in this subgroup. Of note, the effect of combining rituximab and cyclophosphamide on renal recovery also remains to be clarified.9 Addressing these issues will help advance personalized medicine by improving patient stratification and minimizing the risks of underteatment or overtreatment.

The urgency of better patient stratification to help identify patients most likely to benefit from avacopan is highlighted by the pronounced variability in kidney recovery trajectories observed by Faguer et al.4 As studied by Faguer et al.,4 a combination of histopathological features, induction therapy, GC dosing and/or avacopan treatment may contribute to these differences. However, the small number of patients, particularly in the avacopan group, limited their ability to detect meaningful associations. This limitation is not unique to this study. Real-world evidence on avacopan remains fragmented, with most reports comprising small, single-center case series or cohorts with heterogenic AAV populations.3 To move beyond descriptive observations, the vasculitis community needs collaborative efforts to pool real-world data across centers and countries, such as the ongoing AVAC-EUR study initiative (NCT06794827). Large, harmonized datasets would enable robust, focused analyses that account for histopathological features, treatment combinations (rituximab, cyclophosphamide, or both), and steroid dosing. Such an approach is essential to determine whether avacopan provides incremental benefit in the most severe cases and to identify which patients are most likely to benefit and which most likely will not.

In conclusion, the study by Faguer et al.4 shows us avacopan is a safe, GC-sparing treatment option even in the severest rapidly progressive glomerulonephritis patients. However, kidney recovery depends not only on treatment but also on histopathological context. The variability in outcomes underscores the need for better patient stratification, supported by large-scale real-world data. Integrating histopathology and biomarkers such as complement deposition into therapeutic decision-making will be essential to realize the full potential of avacopan and advance personalized care in AAV.
