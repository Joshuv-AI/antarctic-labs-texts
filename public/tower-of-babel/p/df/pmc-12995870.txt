Functional customization of peptide linkers in fusion proteins through multimodal deep learning approach
Authors: Zhong Li, Jiaxi Lu, Jingsong Cui, Gaili Cao, Jiawei Li, Zhujun Ye, Yingchen Wang, Hao Qi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Peptide linkers are critical modulators of function in fusion proteins, a foundational technology in modern bioengineering. However, the rational customization of linkers for specific applications remains challenging, hindered by an insufficient understanding of the relationship between linker sequences and fused protein function. In this study, we systematically characterized 370 diverse linkers, generated from random 18–amino acid sequences with no homology to known proteins, fusing sfGFP to a nanobody. Although sfGFP fluorescence exhibited no clear correlation with canonical linker properties like flexibility or rigidity, we identified a correlation between amino acid composition and functional output. Furthermore, AlphaFold-predicted substructures encompassing the linker and adjacent sfGFP regions revealed considerable structural diversity while maintaining the overall sfGFP fold. Notably, in silico structural features derived from the Cα–Cα distance matrix of these predicted substructures correlated with fluorescence, providing a structural rationale for the functional variation. By training on both sequence representations and in silico substructural features, we developed a multimodal deep learning framework to quantitatively customize linker sequences for high sfGFP fluorescence in special fusion constructs. This work presents a generalizable framework for engineering peptide linkers to assemble highly functional fusion proteins.

Introduction

Peptide linkers, typically ranging from 5 to 50 amino acids, function as dynamic molecular hinges between protein domains and are critical for modulating the functional properties of fusion proteins [1]. This technology underpins diverse bioengineering applications, including protein tracking, antibody engineering, and therapeutic agent development. Despite their importance, the construction of optimal linkers remains a significant challenge. Current strategies rely on a limited set of empirically validated ‘off-the-shelf’ linkers, such as the flexible (GS)n and rigid (EAAAK)n motifs, chosen primarily based on precedent rather than rational design [2].

However, growing evidence indicates that custom-designed linkers can outperform these generic options for specific fusion proteins [3]. This demonstrates that the linker function cannot be predicted solely by simplistic biophysical properties such as flexibility or rigidity. The rational design of custom linkers is hampered by several fundamental challenges: (1) the vast combinatorial sequence space, which is intractable for experimental screening; (2) the multiscale complexity of modeling linker conformational dynamics, from local dihedral angles to global solvent interactions [4]; (3) the lack of predictive frameworks for establishing sequence-structure-function relationships [5]; and (4) the context-dependent nature of linker performance, which necessitates customization for each unique protein pair. Consequently, the empirical development of functional fusion constructs suffers from low success rates [6].

Deep Learning (DL) offers a powerful potential solution by identifying intricate patterns within complex biological datasets. DL techniques have proven indispensable in modeling multifaceted biological processes, such as predicting protein structures, simulating epigenetic landscapes, and accelerating drug discovery. A landmark example is AlphaFold, which captures deep evolutionary constraints on protein structure by leveraging information from multiple sequence alignments (MSAs). Its performance is highly dependent on the depth and quality of this evolutionary data; a well-conserved protein domain with many homologs will yield a highly reliable prediction [7].

This reliance on evolutionary information constitutes a fundamental limitation for designing de novo fusion proteins. A fusion protein is an artificial construct in which two unrelated functional domains are connected by a synthetic linker peptide. While AlphaFold may generate high-confidence structures for the well-conserved parent domains, the artificially designed linker has no evolutionary homologs from which to learn. Its structure is therefore predicted with low confidence, leaving its actual behavior uncharacterized. Thus, the custom design of optimal peptide linkers remains a critical unsolved problem, lacking both robust computational models and extensive experimental data. This underscores the urgent need for novel, few-shot learning approaches capable of generating effective linkers for specific protein fusions based on limited experimental datasets.

Here, we present a multimodal framework for the rational customization of peptide linkers to optimize fusion protein function. We began by characterizing a library of 370 distinct 18–amino acid linker peptides, generated from random sequences, to fuse sfGFP to a nanobody targeting the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of SARS-CoV-2. This model system was chosen for its practical potential in antibody-fluorescent fusions for diagnostics [8] and the well-defined structure of the nanobody component [9]. E. coli colonies exhibiting high sfGFP intensity were collected, enabling the selection of functional variants. Despite the consistent nanobody-sfGFP architecture, the fluorescence per unit of expressed protein of these fusions varied widely across the different linker sequences. Notably, these linker sequences were novel, showing no homology to documented peptides in major protein databases. Furthermore, their performance exhibited no clear correlation with traditional biophysical descriptors such as flexibility or rigidity. Intriguingly, AlphaFold predictions of the substructures, encompassing the linker and its flanking regions from sfGFP, revealed significant structural diversity at the junction while preserving the overall sfGFP fold. It showed that fluorescence function correlated with the predicted in silico structural features, particularly the Cα–Cα distance matrix within this substructure. This suggests that AlphaFold's algorithm captured functionally relevant folding dynamics localized to the linker region, potentially by mitigating the overwhelming dominance of the highly conserved sfGFP fold in the global structure prediction. This correlation provides a novel structural rationale for the observed functional variation. By training on both encoded sequence representations and in silico structural embeddings, we developed a multimodal deep learning framework to quantify linker sequences that maximize sfGFP fluorescence in the fusion construct. Given that sfGFP is a ubiquitously used reporter for localization, imaging, and quantification, its optimization is of broad utility. This work establishes a methodological pipeline for linker design that, in principle, can be adapted to other fusion targets. It demonstrates the powerful synergy of deep learning and structure prediction for advancing protein engineering and provides a framework that could be generalized to future applications where high-throughput screening data and structural modeling are combined for rational protein design.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

The E. coli strain BL21 Star (DE3) [F−, ompT, hsdSB (rB−, mB−), gal, dcm, rne131, (DE3)], which was purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific (cat. no. C601003), was used for the expression of fusion proteins. Strains were propagated at 37 °C for desired time while shaking at 1000 rpm. The strains were grown in liquid LB medium (10 g/L tryptone, 5 g/L yeast extract, 10 g/L NaCl). Ampicillin (100 μg/mL, Sangon Biotech #A430258, for plasmid maintenance) and isopropyl-β-d-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) (0.5 mM, Solarbio #I8070, for the induction of T7 RNA polymerase transcription) were added to the medium when required.

Plasmid construction

The Nanobody (NbH11-D4, PDB: 6YZ5_F) [9] and the DOPARS (GenBank: QLK97558.1) [10] selected as the N-terminal proteins of the fusion protein (Table S1) were gene-synthesized by BGI Tech Solutions (Beijing Liuhe) Co., Ltd., while the C-terminal sfGFP gene was amplified from the pUC19-wt-sfGFP plasmid constructed by our laboratory. The target genes were amplified using PrimeSTAR Max DNA Polymerase (Takara #R045) with specific primers synthesized by Azenta Life Sciences (Table S2). To construct the initial linker recombinant plasmid, the N-terminal protein and the C-terminal sfGFP were cloned into the pUC19 vector backbone linked by the GS linker (GGGGGSGGGGSGGGGSGGGGSLDPGGGGSG, we define it as the experimental wild-type linker) [11] using a ClonExpress MultiS One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme #C113-01). The recombinant product was transformed into E. coli XL10-Gold Competent Cell.

Construction of the randomized linker library

The linker randomized libraries were constructed by seamless cloning methodology drawing on our lab's previous experimental approach [12]. Specifically, to amplify the random library fragments, the upstream primer 54N–F and downstream primer 54N–R (where N unbiasedly stands for any one of the bases A/C/G/T) were annealed and amplified using the Primer STAR Max premix. The PCR was performed with one cycle of 98 °C (10 s), 55 °C (5 s), and 72 °C (1 h) followed by cooling to 10 °C. Next, to amplify the vector fragment, the original plasmid pUC19-nanobody-GS linker-sfGFP was used as template for PCR amplification with the primers V–F and V–R, to further circumvent the effect of the original plasmid on downstream reactions, the vector fragment PCR product was digested overnight at 37 °C with DpnI (TransGen Biotech Co., Ltd. #GD111-01). All PCR products were analyzed by electrophoresis on a 1% agarose gel to confirm the right size of the DNA bands. Then, the PCR product was purified using either the TIANquick Midi Purification Kit (Tiangen Biotech (Beijing) Co., Ltd. #DP204) or the SPARKeasy Gel/PCR Purification Kit (Shandong Sikejie Biotechnology Co., Ltd. #AE0101), and the concentration of the DNA was measured by the Nanodrop (Allsheng, Hangzhou Allsheng Instruments Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, China). Furthermore, the random library fragments and the vector fragment were assembled using the ClonExpress II One Step Cloning Kit (Vazyme Biotech Co., Ltd. #C112) and recombinant products were transformed into E. coli BL21 Star (DE3) Competent Cell, the original plasmid pUC19-nanobody-GS linker-sfGFP was also transfected to serve as a positive control for subsequent studies. Finally, the cells were cultured on LB plates containing ampicillin overnight at 37 °C and the plates were wrapped in tin foil and placed at 4 °C for subsequent activity assay.

Measurement of the fluorescence activity

The 96-well high-throughput assay for cell growth and fluorescence measurements was adapted from a previous study with appropriate adjustments [12]. Specifically, Fluorescent single colonies were marked at the corresponding positions on the plates by visualization with a blue LED transilluminator (LABGIC, Beijing Labgic Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), and then picked into LB medium with 100 μg/mL ampicillin at 37 °C, 1000 rpm in a shaking incubator (MBR-022UP, TAITEC, Tokyo, Japan) for overnight culture, the positive control colony (containing pUC19-nanobody-GS linker-sfGFP) grown on a separate plate underwent the same operation as above and became one of the 96-well samples. Then, overnight cultured cells were seeded into a 96-well deep-well plate (VIOX Scientific) at a 1:50 ratio with 300 μL LB medium containing 100 μg/mL ampicillin and 0.5 mM IPTG per well, and incubated for about 5∼6 h at 37 °C, 1000 rpm. Subsequently, 200 μL of culture solution was pipetted into a 96-well plate (Corning #3762). The optical density (OD600) and fluorescence intensity (Ex 485 nm/Em 535 nm) of each well were measured simultaneously using a microplate reader (Tecan Spark Multimode Microplate Reader, Waltham, USA), overnight cultures were subjected to PCR and Sanger sequencing to obtain the linker genotype. During the measurement process, we measured each sample three times and calculated its average value, including linker variants of the same genotype. The fluorescence intensity of each cell was homogenized by OD600, calculated as follows:(1)FLUPCsample=FLUsampleODsample(2)FLUPCGS=FLUGSODGSWhere FLUPCsample (or GS) represents the fluorescence per cell of the sample (or GS) linker variant, FLUsample (or GS) represents the fluorescence intensity of the sample (or GS) linker variant, while the ODsample (or GS) represents the OD600 of the sample (or GS) linker variant.

Here, we defined the single-cell relative fluorescence activity of fusion proteins containing different linker sequences as the normalized values of the relative fluorescence value per cell concentration of the sample and the relative fluorescence value per cell concentration of the positive control (the cell containing the initial GS linker variant) according to the formula:(3)NFA=FLUPCsampleFLUPCGSWhere the normalized fluorescence activity (NFA) stands for the relative fluorescence activity of each sample linker variant normalized to the positive control (GS linker variant).

Western blot and quantification of protein production

For each of the three parallel experiments, 66.6 μL of the bacterial solution was transferred to a 96-well PCR plate, which was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 10 min, and the supernatant was carefully discarded. The bacterial pellet in each well was resuspended with 80 μL ddH2O. Then the resuspension solution was mixed 3:1 with 4 × sample Buffer (250 mM Tris-HCl pH 6.8, 40% (v/v) glycerol, 2.86 M β-mercaptoethanol, 10% (w/v) SDS, 8‰ (w/v) bromophenol blue) and heated at 98 °C for protein denaturation. Each denatured sample was run in 10% SDS-PAGE with SDS-PAGE running buffer (25 mM Tris-base, 200 mM glycine, 0.1% (w/v) SDS) to separate protein bands (120 V, about 4 h) and transferred to PVDF membranes (Merck #IPVH08100) with the semi-dry blotting procedure (20 V, 30 min). The membranes were blocked with blocking buffer (5% (w/v) skin-milk, 10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 157.5 mM NaCl, and 0.02% (v/v) Tween 20) overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, the membranes were incubated with primary antibodies ((anti-flag (ABclonal #AE005), anti-Twin strep (ABclonal #AE066)) diluted 5000 times by blocking buffer at room temperature for 1 h. Next, the membrane was washed with Blocking Buffer (2 × 20 min). Then, the membranes were incubated with the 5000 times diluted secondary antibody (Thermo Fisher #31430) at room temperature for 1 h and washed twice (20 min for each time) with wash buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 157.5 mM NaCl, 0.02% (v/v) Tween 20). Finally, the membrane was treated with SuperSignal™ West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate (Thermo Fisher #34580, MA, USA) for imaging by Azure Biosystems C300 imager (Azure Biosystems, Inc., Dublin, USA).

To estimate the expression level of the fusion proteins and quantify the variability in protein yield across linker variants, the grayscale values are obtained using image analysis software (AzureSpot v13.2) by measuring the density of the bands for each fusion protein variant in the Western Blotting. Here, we defined the relative protein production of fusion proteins containing different linker sequences as the normalized values of the relative gray scale value of the sample and the relative gray scale value of the positive control (the cell containing the initial GS linker variant) according to the formula:(4)NPP=GraysampleGrayGSWhere the normalized protein production (NPP) stands for the relative gray scale values of each sample linker variant normalized to the positive control (the initial GS linker variant).

Statistics on physicochemical properties of linkers

To effectively characterize the physicochemical properties of linker amino acid sequences, we introduced several common physicochemical property indicators, including hydrophobicity, charge, molecular weight (MW), volume, polarity, and isoelectric point. These properties can be obtained from existing literature or standard databases, and a set of fixed values is assigned to each standard amino acid [13,14]. Based on these physicochemical indicators, we focus on the physicochemical properties carried by each amino acid at every position in the sequence. For each amino acid in each sequence, a corresponding 6-dimensional physicochemical vector can be extracted and used for subsequent analysis. To assess the impact of physicochemical properties at different positions on activity, we calculated the Spearman correlation coefficient between the physicochemical values at each amino acid position and the corresponding activity. Let the vector of the jth physicochemical property at the kth amino acid across all sequences be {Ppos,k(j)}. The correlation is calculated as the Spearman correlation coefficient for the variable pair {Ppos,k(j)}.

Comparison of sequence similarity between different linkers

To measure the differences between various linker sequences, we employ a method based on one-hot encoding and cosine similarity. Specifically, each 18-amino acid sequence is mapped to a one-hot vector composed of the 20-letter amino acid alphabet, which is then flattened into a fixed-dimensional representation. The similarity between any two sequences is calculated using the cosine similarity formula:(5)CosineSimilarity=∑i=1nAi×Bi∑i=1nAi2×∑i=1nBi2Where A and B represent the vector representations of the two sequences, and Ai and Bi denote the values at position i in sequences A and B, respectively. The resulting symmetric similarity matrix shows that values closer to 1 indicate greater sequence similarity. This matrix is further visualized as a heatmap to intuitively display the global distribution of sequence differences.

Complementary to the vector-based cosine similarity, we also utilized the Levenshtein Distance to evaluate the structural divergence between linker sequences from an editing perspective. Levenshtein Distance is defined as the minimum number of single-character editing operations (insertions, deletions, or substitutions) required to transform one amino acid sequence into another. For two sequences A and B with lengths |A| and |B|, the Levenshtein distance LevA,B(i,j) between the first i characters of A and the first j characters of B is given by:(6)LevA,B(i,j)={max(i,j)ifmin(i,j)=0,min{LevA,B(i−1,j)+1LevA,B(i,j−1)+1LevA,B(i−1,j−1)+I(Ai≠Bj)otherwise.where I(Ai≠Bj) is the indicator function equal to 1 if the characters Ai and Bj are different, and 0 otherwise. The final distance between the two full sequences is LevA,B(|A|,|B|). This metric provides a direct quantification of the "mutation cost," where a lower value indicates higher sequence conservation.

Comparison of structural similarity between different domains

To further investigate the impact of different truncation methods on the structural prediction results of fusion proteins, we performed structural predictions on a total of 370 Linker fusion proteins using RoseTTAFold (RF) and AlphaFold2 (AF2). To systematically evaluate the stability and rationality of predicted structures, we designed downstream sfGFP protein fragments of varying lengths and employed a sliding window approach for truncation. Specifically, each fusion protein consisted of “upstream protein + linker + downstream sfGFP fragment,” with sfGFP fragment lengths of 8 aa, 18 aa, 68 aa, 88 aa, and 217 aa, respectively. Subsequently, we compared the predicted structures under different truncation conditions with the reference structure of wild-type sfGFP, used “1/RMSD (Root Mean Square Deviation)” as the metric for structural divergence. After comprehensively comparing prediction results under different truncation conditions, we ultimately selected the 88 aa sfGFP fragment as the downstream fusion region to balance both the accuracy of predicted structures and the distinguishability among different fusion proteins.

Details of predictive framework

Sequence embeddings

In the predictive framework proposed in this paper, we adopted one of the most advanced models, ProtT5 [15], as the protein language model (PLM). ProtT5 is a large-scale pre-trained model based on the Transformer architecture. Through self-supervised learning on millions of natural protein sequences, it can capture the evolutionary relationships, structural features, and functional information contained in protein sequences. Specifically, given a protein sequence S=(a1,a2,…,aL) of length L, after calculation by the ProtT5 model, we obtain the embedded representation of each sequence:(7)ProtT5_Embi=PLM(Si)∈RL×d

To capture the physicochemical environment, the input sequence S extends beyond the linker to include fixed upstream and optimized downstream flanking regions.

After the protein large language model generates high-dimensional embedding vectors for each protein linker sequence, we use principal component analysis (PCA) for dimensionality reduction to lower computational complexity and extract the most informative features. PCA is a classical linear dimensionality reduction method whose core principle involves projecting the original high-dimensional data into a low-dimensional space via orthogonal transformation while maximizing the variance of the projected data, thereby preserving the primary structural information of the data. Ultimately, the reduced-dimensional vectors are:(8)Seq_Embi=PCA(ProtT5_Embi)∈RL×kWhere k is the target dimension, selected to retain sufficient feature variance while reducing computational redundancy.

Mapping embeddings to activity and protein production

To map the learned representations to the target properties, we employ a multi-layer perceptron (MLP). In the initial sequence-based phase, we input the sequence embedding Seq_Embi∈RL×k into this network. The MLP acts as a function RL×k→R, mapping the high-dimensional embedding to a continuous space representing the target values (i.e., activity or NPP):(9)yˆi=f(Ei)=MLP(Ei)Where Ei represents the input embedding (e.g., Seq_Embi). The MLP consists of fully connected layers, using the SiLU (Sigmoid Linear Unit) activation function to capture complex nonlinear relationships. Through this architecture, the model aggregates the feature information and ultimately outputs the quantitative prediction for fusion protein activity or NPP.

Model training

We built and trained the proposed model based on the PyTorch framework. The Adam optimizer was used during training, with a batch size of 128. To ensure a robust evaluation of the model's performance and generalization ability on the limited dataset, we adopted a 5-fold cross-validation strategy. Specifically, the dataset of 370 samples was randomly partitioned into five non-overlapping subsets. In each iteration, four subsets (approximately 80% of the data) were used for model training, while the remaining subset (20%) served as the validation set. This process was repeated five times, ensuring that every sample was used for validation exactly once. The final performance metrics were reported as the average of the five iterations. The maximum number of training epochs was set to 3,000, and an early stopping strategy was adopted: if the validation set performance did not show significant improvement over 200 consecutive epochs, training was terminated early to prevent overfitting (Fig. S5). The loss function uses Mean Squared Error (MSE) to measure the deviation between predicted and actual values, defined as follows:(10)MSE=1N∑i=1N(yi−yˆi)2Where yi is the ground truth label (activity or NPP value), yˆi is the model prediction value, and N is the sample size.

Considering that both protein activity and NPP typically exhibits a certain degree of linearity in numerical terms, we ultimately adopted the Pearson correlation coefficient as the primary evaluation metric, as this metric more accurately reflects the model's ability to fit the target properties. Pearson correlation coefficient, used to measure the linear correlation between the predicted value and the actual value:(11)r=∑i=1n(xi‐x‾)(yi‐y‾)∑i=1n(xi‐x‾)2∑i=1n(yi‐y‾)2

Atomic pair distance

We used AlphaFold2 to predict the structures of 370 fusion protein sequences. Due to the high computational cost of predicting the full-length fusion protein sequences in the entire library using AlphaFold, each sequence was truncated to include 18 amino acids upstream, an 18-residue linker region, and 88 amino acids downstream, totaling 124 residues (18, 18, 88). To investigate the relationship between structural features and protein activity, we focus on the spatial distance characteristics between amino acids. Specifically, we selected the Cα (alpha carbon) atom in each amino acid as the representative for spatial representation. The reason for choosing the Cα atom rather than any other atom pair is that different Linker sequences contain different types of amino acids, leading to inconsistencies in atom types and numbers, which prevent alignment calculations. In contrast, the Cα atom is present in all standard amino acids, offering good comparability and universality, making it suitable for cross-sequence consistency analysis. For each structural prediction result, we calculate the pairwise Cα atom distances between all amino acids. Let the Cα atom coordinates of the ith and jth amino acids be ci and cj, respectively. Then, their spatial distance is:(12)di,j=‖ci‐cj‖2,i≠j

Subsequently, we calculated the Spearman correlation coefficient between the distance and activity at each (i,j) position for all samples to measure the monotonic relationship between structural distance and activity. Finally, we plotted the correlation results of all atomic pairs into a heat map.

Pairwise distances based on secondary structure feature points

To further explore the potential association between protein structural features and activity, we used the DSSP tool to extract secondary structure information from the upstream and downstream regions (excluding the Linker region) of each fusion protein based on the structural prediction results from AlphaFold2. The DSSP output includes eight structural types: α-helix (H), β-sheet (E), β-bridge (B), 3/10 helix (G), π-helix (I), β-turn (T), bend (S), and no structure (N). To construct an analytical perspective with universality and structural specificity, we proposed the definition of “relative secondary structure feature points”: using the middle residue of the linker as the reference origin, the residues upstream and downstream with explicit secondary structure annotations are sorted according to the three-dimensional Euclidean distance between their Cα atoms and the center of the linker. The kth residue after sorting is defined as the kth “relative structural feature point”. We selected the Cα–Cα distances between all Linker segment residues and their upstream and downstream relative structural feature points as the subject of our study. Compared to the distance calculation method for all residue pairs, this strategy has the following two major advantages: (1) Feature purification: Focusing on key sites with clear structural functions helps to enhance the signal strength between structure and activity. (2) Cross-protein generalizability: Structural feature points (such as α-helices, β-sheets, etc.) exhibit good structural commonality and are independent of absolute position coordinates, thereby supporting the simulation of different protein backgrounds. Let i′ and j′ represent residues in the Linker region or relative structural feature points, respectively, with Cα atom coordinates ci′ and cj′, respectively. Their spatial distance is defined as:(13)di′,j′=‖ci′‐cj′‖2,i′≠j′

Finally, we calculated the Spearman correlation coefficient between the distances di′,j′ of all selected atom pairs and the corresponding protein activities, and plotted a correlation heat map to visualize the potential regulatory effects of structural features on activity.

Details of multimodal DL architecture

Structure embeddings

In the original predictive framework, we used ProtT5 to extract sequence embeddings (Sequence Embedding) of Linkers and utilized their primary structure information for activity prediction. However, sequence features alone are insufficient to comprehensively capture the complex spatial structural relationships between Linkers and upstream and downstream proteins, especially when faced with different background protein migration tasks, which limits their generalization ability. To this end, we further introduced spatial structural information of proteins and proposed a structural module (SM) to explore the complementary representation of structural hierarchy on activity. Specifically, based on the results of the secondary structure feature point analysis described above, we selected the top K Cα atom pairs significantly related to activity (i.e., the top K highly correlated structural distances). Subsequently, we performed nonlinear encoding on the spatial distance di of each selected atom pair as a structural input feature. To enhance the expressive power of distance features, we use a radial basis function (RBF) consisting of 16 Gaussian kernels to encode the distance:(14)RBF(di)=[exp(‐γ·(di‐μj)2)]j=116Where μj is the center of the jth Gaussian kernel, and γ is the bandwidth parameter. To integrate the multi-scale spatial information into a compact representation, we aggregate the high-dimensional kernel responses. We calculate the mean of the 16-dimensional RBF vector to obtain the final scalar structural embedding:(15)Stru_Embi=116∑j=116exp(‐γ·(di‐μj)2)

This embedding encapsulates the non-linear spatial characteristics captured by the Gaussian kernels into a unified feature. By condensing the distance distribution into a dense scalar representation, this approach effectively simplifies the model input while retaining critical structural information for the multimodal framework.

Multimodal embeddings

After obtaining structural embeddings and sequence embeddings, we further fuse the information from the two modalities to construct multimodal representations, with the aim of jointly modeling the effects of sequence and spatial structure on activity. Specifically, we concatenate the sequence embeddings and structural embeddings in the feature dimension:(16)Linker_Embi=Seq_Embi‖Stru_EmbiWhere Seq_Embi∈RL×k represents the sequence embeddings extracted and reduced in dimension by ProtT5, andStru_Embi∈RK represents the structural embeddings corresponding to the selected Top-K distance features. The final multimodal vector Linker_Embi∈RL×k+K integrates both sequence semantic and structural distance information. To perform the final prediction tasks, we input this fused representation into the regression model described in Section 2.9.2. Specifically, the prediction is calculated as:(17)yˆi=f(Linker_Embi)=MLP(Linker_Embi)

Finally, we compared the prediction performance of the original model and the multimodal DL framework with the structural module on a benchmark dataset of 370 fusion proteins.

Activity discriminator based on multimodal DL framework

In practical applications, transferring a model trained on a specific protein background to a completely new background protein for activity prediction often poses significant challenges. Especially when training data is limited or the activity distribution is skewed, traditional regression models usually struggle to maintain prediction accuracy and stability. To address these challenges, we introduced an Activity Discriminator module, which converts the original continuous regression task into a more robust binary classification task. Rather than directly predicting specific activity values, the discriminator focuses on determining the direction of activity change (increase or decrease) of the new fusion protein relative to the Initial fusion protein containing the Initial GS linker. This task is more discriminative and robust, particularly suitable for low-data or cross-library transfer scenarios. The discriminator adopts a simple fully connected neural network structure, with the input being the fused multimodal embedding vector Linker_Embi and the output being the binary classification probability. During training, we use the initial fusion protein containing the initial GS linker variant as a reference, mark the mutant with increased activity as the positive class (High Activity), and mark the mutant with decreased activity as the negative class (Low Activity), and use cross-entropy loss for training:(18)Lcls=−ylog(yˆ)−(1−y)log(1−yˆ)

Benchmarking of different models

Several representative protein pre-trained models were selected as baselines for comparison and extension:

ProtBert [16]: Utilizes a version trained on the UniRef100 database (approximately 217 million protein sequences) to learn general representations across large-scale sequence data;

ProtT5 [15]: Employs a version with approximately 3 billion parameters, pre-trained on the UniRef50 (approximately 45 million sequences);

ESM2 [17]: Utilizes a 650M-parameter version trained on the UniRef series databases (primarily UniRef50 and UniRef90 subsets);

Ankh [18]: Pre-trained on UniRef50 data per official configuration, featuring an optimized attention mechanism that better captures functional sites and structurally associated regions in sequences.

The experimental training and validation dataset consists of 370 fusion protein sequences collected independently. It should be noted that publicly available linker datasets are virtually nonexistent, with existing data scattered across fragmented studies or unpublished private repositories. This scarcity was a key factor in our decision to independently collect fusion protein sequences for experimental training. Following the representation layer of all models, we introduced the proposed structural module to explicitly model the underlying structural information of sequences. This was subsequently connected to a three-layer MLP classifier (with hidden layer sizes of 100) as a unified architecture for downstream prediction and classification tasks. All other hyperparameters were kept at default values. To comprehensively evaluate the performance of the proposed framework, we compared it against these baselines across two downstream tasks: (1) continuous value prediction, primarily evaluated using Pearson correlation coefficients; (2) classification discrimination, comprehensively assessed using accuracy, F1 score, and AUC score.

Construction of DOPARS-linker-sfGFP validation library

In the aforementioned analysis, we proposed the concept of “relative secondary structure feature points” and constructed structural features based on the Cα–Cα spatial distance between them and the Linker segment. The core of this design lies in the fact that relative structural position relationships have stronger cross-protein generalization capabilities than absolute residue numbers, making them particularly suitable for modeling different background proteins in structural transition tasks. Traditional structural modeling methods typically rely on fixed residue positions, but when transferred to new protein contexts, absolute sites often lack comparability and consistency. However, the representative structural feature points we have identified exhibit good structural commonality across different proteins, and their spatial relationship with the linker segment can more reliably reflect potential structure-function coupling mechanisms. Based on the above principles, we used an activity discriminator based on multimodal DL framework to predict the cross-background activity of 10 randomly selected DOPARS-linker-sfGFP fusion proteins, further validating the generalization ability and predictive effectiveness of the proposed structural feature design. Details of the activity validation of DOPARS-linker-sfGFP are provided in the following subsection.

Wet-lab experimental testing of multimodal DL framework predictions

For wet-lab experimental testing of the nanobody-linker-sfGFP, the method for constructing the nanobody-linker-sfGFP random library and activity detection is the same as the method described. For wet-lab experimental testing of the DOPARS-linker-sfGFP, the method for constructing the DOPARS-linker-sfGFP random library is the same as the Nanobody-linker-sfGFP library. Ten DOPARS-random linker-sfGFP monoclonal colonies were randomly selected from solid plates for cultivation. The PCR products from the bacterial cultures were sequenced using Sanger sequencing to determine the sequence of the fusion protein. Subsequently, AlphaFold was used for structural prediction of the fusion protein, and the multimodal DL framework was employed to assess the activity of sfGFP. Finally, the activity of the N-terminal DOPARS and C-terminal sfGFP in DOPARS-linker-sfGFP was validated. During the validation process, DOPARS-GS linker-sfGFP was used as a positive control for concurrent testing.

The method for determining the activity of C-terminal sfGFP is the same as that described above for Nanobody-linker-sfGFP. For the activity assay of N-terminal DOPARS, we primarily referenced experimental methods previously published by our laboratory [19]. Specifically, the cell-free protein synthesis (CFPS) system was established in BL21 Star (DE3) strain, while the cell-free unnatural protein synthesis (CFUPS) system was successfully established through the construction of the Class I release factor-free BL21(DE3) S-AAF3BF3 strain and the preparation of the corresponding extract. The normalized fluorescence activity (NFA) revealed an approximate range of 0.80 to 1.60 and a mean value of 1.15 (Fig. 1C and S1A), indicating that most linker variants have comparable fluorescence activity, with some exhibiting either significantly higher or lower activity than the standard GS-linker. In contrast, the normalized protein production (NPP) exhibited a right-skewed distribution (range: ∼0.50–2.00, mean: 1.39; Fig. 1C and S1B), implying that enhanced expression was limited to a minority of linker variants, while the vast majority conferred lower expression levels. These observations highlight the differential impact of linker sequences on fluorescence activity and protein production, consistent with previous studies emphasizing the critical role of linker composition and flexibility in modulating fusion protein function [1]. Fluorescence activity and protein expression were largely uncoupled, exhibiting only a weak negative correlation (Pearson's r = −0.24) (Fig. 1D). This functional decoupling indicates that the linker sequence's primary effect is on the folding efficiency or structural integrity of the mature sfGFP chromophore, rather than on gross protein production. The data indicate that linker sequences can create a functional trade-off, favoring high protein yield while compromising fluorescence. This functional decoupling implies that activity is regulated in a complex manner, where linker-specific sequence features and their consequent structural effects are critical determinants beyond mere expression levels.

By analyzing the sequence similarity within these linkers and reported linkers in the literature (Fig. S2A) [1], we found extremely low similarity among all linker sequences (average cosine similarity = 0.07, Fig. 2A; average Levenshtein distance = 16.17, Fig. S2B; median Levenshtein distances of each reported linker = 15∼17, Fig. S2C). This indicates that the random generation approach was effective in creating high sequence diversity and novelty. Notably, amino acid composition analysis revealed distinct patterns between the high-activity linkers (NFA >1.8), the total linkers and low-activity linkers (NFA <0.4) (Fig. S2D). Specifically, high-activity linkers showed a statistically significant increased presence of glycine (G; p = 1.55 × 10−6) and glutamate (E; p = 0.044) compared to low-activity linkers, suggesting that these amino acids contribute to an improved structural conformation that enhances fluorescence activity. Glycine, in particular, is a highly flexible residue that increases linker flexibility and reduces steric hindrance, which is crucial for allowing proper folding and optimal orientation of fused protein domains [21,22]. Studies on protein solubility and linker design indicate that hydrophilic linkers improve protein stability and prevent undesirable intra- or inter-molecular interactions that could quench fluorescence or interfere with domain orientation [23], which is particularly relevant for fluorescence-based assays, where solvent exposure of chromophores is critical for maintaining high signal intensity. Conversely, the proportions of phenylalanine (F), isoleucine (I), and leucine (L) were significantly lower (p < 0.05) in high-activity variants, suggesting that hydrophobic residues may have a detrimental effect on fluorescence activity. A possible explanation for this trend is that aromatic (F) and aliphatic hydrophobic residues (I and L) can promote local rigidity in the linker region, thereby restricting the conformational dynamics required for efficient intramolecular interactions between fusion protein domains. Structural flexibility has been identified as a key determinant in optimizing fluorescence resonance energy transfer (FRET)-based proteins and enzyme activity [24]. To further identify sequence-activity relationships specific to functional outcomes and to mitigate potential confounding from aggregated data (e.g., Simpson's paradox), we performed a stratified correlation analysis comparing the high-activity (NFA >1.8) and low-activity (NFA <0.4) linker subgroups. An initial analysis of the unstratified, total linkers served as a background reference, revealing predominantly weak correlations (ρ between −0.20 and 0.20) for all biochemical features across all positions (Fig. S2E). Critically, upon stratification, distinct and stronger correlations emerged within each functional class. For instance, in high-activity linkers, amino acid charge at position 11 showed a positive correlation with activity (ρ = 0.51, p = 0.01, Fig. S2F), whereas in low-activity linkers, this correlation was absent (ρ = −0.01, p = 0.94, Fig. S2G). Similarly, the amino acid charge at position 3 exhibited a negative correlation in high-activity linkers (ρ = −0.42, p = 0.04) but a positive correlation in low-activity linkers (ρ = 0.40, p = 0.04). This opposing trend suggests that a positive charge at this position likely disrupts a conserved electrostatic network essential for a functional conformation [26]. Furthermore, properties such as isoelectric point (pI) at position 3 and volume at positions 7 showed stronger negative correlations in the high-activity linkers (ρ = −0.49, p = 0.01; ρ = −0.47, p = 0.02) compared to no significant correlations in the low-activity linkers (ρ = 0.28, p = 0.17; ρ = 0.16, p = 0.42). These intergroup variabilities indicate that the activity of fusion proteins may be modulated by local physicochemical changes in the linker through a complex interplay of microscopic factors, including conformational dynamics, flexibility, and electrostatic distributions, all of which are contingent on the specific structural environment.

We applied the same stratified analytical principle to evaluate local conformational propensities (see Methods). Across the total linkers, local conformational propensity showed only weak correlations with activity, as all correlation coefficients fell within the range of −0.2 to 0.2 (Fig. 2B). However, this relationship was moderately enhanced and more position-specific within the subset of high-activity linkers (Fig. S2H). β-sheet propensity at positions 7 and 8 correlated negatively with activity (ρ = −0.47 and −0.41, respectively), whereas β-turns at positions 16 and 17 correlated positively (ρ = 0.45 and 0.43, respectively). However, random coils at these positions were negatively correlated (ρ = −0.37 and −0.32, respectively). In contrast, in the subset of low-activity linkers (Fig. S2I), the β-turn at position 3 was significantly negatively correlated with activity (ρ = −0.44, p = 0.02) and the random coil at position 18 showed no significant correlation.

Conventionally, the rigidity or flexibility of a linker peptide, often associated with secondary structures such as helices/sheets or random coils, respectively, is considered a primary factor influencing fusion protein function. Flexible linkers, typically rich in small, disordered amino acids like glycine and serine, confer high conformational freedom to accommodate necessary movements between protein domains [27]. This has led to the common practice of testing canonical linkers, such as the flexible (GS)n or rigid (EAAAK)n motifs, based on their empirical impact on the function of the fused protein. However, our results demonstrate an insufficient correlation between local conformational propensity and sfGFP function in this specific assembled fusion protein. This conclusion aligns with our previous study [3], suggesting that customized linkers, which are not dominated by strong rigid or flexible conformations, can be optimal for fusion protein performance. This indicates that linkers are not defined by a single structural feature. Consequently, selection should move beyond empirical rigid/flexible classifications and be tailored to the specific properties of the target protein, the functional requirements of the fusion, and the intended biological context.

For exploring the correlation among linker sequence, protein function, and protein expression, we developed a deep learning model comprising three key components: (i) a PLM pretrained on millions of natural protein sequences to learn evolutionary and functional patterns embedded in sequence data; (ii) a dimensionality reduction step using Principal Component Analysis (PCA) to distill informative features from the high-dimensional embeddings generated by PLM, serving as a necessary regularization strategy to mitigating the curse of high dimensionality with small samples (1024 vs 370) [28]; and (iii) a multilayer perceptron (MLP) module to perform regression and classification tasks based on the reduced feature set (Fig. 2C, see Methods), considering as a prudent choice for capturing highly nonlinear sequence-to-function mappings. This model leverages the rich information embedded in PLM-derived representations to characterize diverse linker sequence features. By integrating such representations into downstream learning, the model is able to capture the subtle sequence-function dependencies of linkers. This model was trained on the 370 sfGFP-fusion linker peptides. The model achieved a Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.62 on the training set and 0.46 on the validation set between predicted and experimentally measured activity (Fig. 2D and E), indicating a moderate predictive accuracy. By contrast, the model performs less accurately in protein expression prediction than in activity prediction (Fig. S3A and S3B), which may be due to that protein expression represents a complex phenotype involving more diverse factors that are difficult to capture directly from sequences, including translational efficiency, protein folding dynamics, intracellular degradation rates, and post-translational modification networks in host cells [29,30]. These factors may not be adequately reflected in the sequence embeddings (PLM Embedding) currently employed, which limits model learning. Therefore, we have temporarily shifted the focus of subsequent research to activity prediction. In summary, we demonstrate that both the physicochemical and local conformational features of the linker sequence are essential for modulating protein activity, albeit in a complex and interdependent manner. While a meaningful correlation exists between linker sequence and GFP function, these results indicate that one-dimensional sequence-based analysis alone is insufficient for optimal sequence-function prediction, underscoring the necessity of incorporating structural features.

Structural diversity underlies functional variability in fusion proteins

The structural impact of linker peptides was further investigated by predicting the tertiary structures of 370 fusion proteins in silico using AlphaFold (see Methods). The sfGFP (99.15% sequence identity to PDB: 2B3P) and nanobody (100% sequence identity to PDB: 6YZ5) structures are both well-characterized (Fig. 3A and S4A). Accordingly, in our predictions, the sfGFP domain scored a high average pLDDT (predicted Local Distance Difference Test) of 89.44, and the nanobody domain scored 79.39. In contrast, the randomly generated linker peptides, which share no significant homology with known proteins, obtained a relatively poor average pLDDT of approximately 50. This gradient in prediction confidence is consistent with the availability of structural templates for each region. Consequently, the sfGFP domains in all predicted fusion structures exhibited minimal RMSD values (average RMSD = 0.39), indicating a near-perfect match to the reference sfGFP structure (2B3P) despite the variation in linker sequences (Fig. S4B). We infer that the high sequence identity of sfGFP with a well-known reference structure provides an overwhelmingly strong evolutionary signal in the Multiple Sequence Alignment (MSA). As the core AlphaFold algorithm is designed to leverage such strong signals, it confidently recalls and imposes the known, stable sfGFP fold. This process may ignore the local context provided by the various linker peptides. This structural conservation in silico, despite the observed functional variation in vitro, likely explains why sfGFP exhibits a range of fluorescent activities across different fusion constructs while being predicted to have a nearly identical structure.

For exploring the structural diversity of protein variants with known functional differences, interestingly, we found that AlphaFold predictions of the substructures, encompassing the linker and its flanking regions from sfGFP, revealed significant structural diversity at the junction while preserving the overall sfGFP fold (Fig. 3B). Specifically, we generated over 1500 in silico substructures, each comprising a segment of the upstream nanobody, the linker, and a fragment of the downstream sfGFP. This large-scale analysis required more than 1270 GPU hours of computing time. Notably, substructures containing a long sfGFP fragment (217 aa, ∼92% of the full length) exhibited very high structural similarity to the full-length sfGFP reference, with a low median RMSD of 0.42 Å and slight variation between individual predictions. This indicates that a 217-aa GFP segment provides a sufficiently strong evolutionary signal to dictate its native fold, effectively overwhelming the potential influence of the adjacent linker sequence. In contrast, shorter GFP fragments (8 aa and 18 aa) displayed poor structural conservation, with high median RMSDs of 5.56 Å and 2.94 Å, respectively, and significant variation across different linker contexts. This confirms that excessively short fragments cannot independently fold into a stable native structure and are highly susceptible to their local sequence environment. Strikingly, an 88-aa GFP fragment demonstrated an optimal balance, maintaining moderate structural fidelity (RMSD of 0.53 Å) while retaining substantial conformational diversity across different linkers. This suggests that this fragment length is sufficient to preserve the core sfGFP fold yet remains responsive to the modulatory influence of the linker, making it an ideal candidate for capturing sequence-structure-function relationships.Fig. 3Structural characteristics analysis of the linker variants. To elucidate how distinct linker sequences modulate protein structure and function, we characterized inter-domain atomic spatial relationships by analyzing these in silico substructures comprising an 18-aa nanobody N-terminal fragment, the 18-aa linker, and an 88-aa sfGFP C-terminal fragment. We calculated pairwise Cα distances between representative residues across these three fragments and correlated these spatial measurements with sfGFP function (Fig. 3C and Methods). The correlation between Cα distances and sfGFP function revealed a distinct pattern, indicating that specific inter-residue distances are associated with functional output (Fig. S4C). This correlation pattern became more pronounced and structurally defined when we focused exclusively on the high-activity subset of linkers (Fig. 3D), suggesting that optimal function is linked to a precise atomic spatial configuration of the domains.

Analysis of the atomic distance correlation landscape (Fig. 3D, lower half) revealed strong positive correlations between specific Cα pairs in the sfGFP domain, including S27–R29 (ρ = 0.63) and K25–T48 (ρ = 0.59). These long-range correlations demonstrate that conformational changes induced by distinct linker sequences propagate from the linker interface to distal sites, effectively tuning fusion protein function. Notably, we also observed significant correlations involving residues critical to the chromophore triad (T64-Y65-G66) [31]. These included T64–F82 (ρ = 0.55), T64–F83 (ρ = 0.54), Y65–S85 (ρ = 0.51), and Y65–A86 (ρ = 0.50). The participation of Y65 in the chromophore's π-conjugated system is particularly crucial, as sub-angstrom fluctuations in its position are known to directly impact fluorescence quantum yield [32]. This strongly implies that linker-derived structural perturbations allosterically regulate the chromophore environment, establishing a direct mechanistic link between linker sequence and sfGFP's functional output. Furthermore, a strong negative correlation for the T37–F70 pair (ρ = −0.64) with activity suggests an alternative mechanism of functional impairment, potentially through a structural perturbation that misaligns essential domains or occludes a critical functional site. We further characterized local conformation features using the DSSP algorithm (Define Secondary Structure of Proteins) [33], which classifies structure into eight types (H: α-helix, E: β-sheet, G: 310-helix; I: π-helix, T: β-turn, S: bend, see Methods). Our analysis identified specific secondary structure features whose presence significantly correlated with biological activity (Fig. 3D, upper half). Particularly, the correlation between a 310-helix at residue G50 and an α-helix at H80 within the sfGFP domain exhibits a strong correlation (ρ = 0.55). Notably, the resulting local secondary structure correlation landscape closely mirrors the atomic-distance correlation landscape but exhibits a sharper and more defined distribution of significant features. Therefore, the correlation of both atomic distances (Cα–Cα) and local conformation features with sfGFP fluorescence provides an in silico structural rationale for the functional variation observed across the linker variants.

Customization of functional peptide linker by multimodal DL framework

To model the customization of linker peptides in fusion protein, we built a multimodal deep-learning framework that integrates encoded sequence representations with AlphaFold2-predicted in silico structural features (Fig. 4A). Fusion protein sequences were embedded using a protein language model. Structural features were extracted from the atomic distance and secondary structure correlation landscapes of AlphaFold2-predicted substructures. To effectively capture the structure determinants, we first calculated the pairwise Cα distances, and selected the top-K atomic pairs that were most significantly correlated with activity. The distances of these selected atomic pairs are non-linearly encoded using a Radial Basis Function (RBF) with 16 Gaussian kernels to create powerful in silico structural embeddings (see Methods). Therefore, we effectively integrated sequence representations with structural embeddings to capture complementary sequence and in silico structure knowledge. We then evaluated several pre-trained protein language models—including ProtBert [16], ESM2 [17], Ankh [18], and ProtT5 [15], all of which were trained on large-scale protein sequence datasets—and different model combinations and the effect of PCA dimensionality reduction using two metrics: Pearson correlation coefficients for regression performance (predictors) and classification accuracy for classifier performance (Fig. 4B and C). To ensure a robust and unbiased evaluation given the limited sample size, we adopted a 5-fold cross-validation strategy. During the training, we implemented an early stopping strategy (patience = 200 epochs) to strictly prevent overfitting (Fig. S5). Comprehensive ablation studies regarding hyperparameter choices, including with or without using PCA (Table S3 and Table S6), downstream predict module (Table S4), K-value selection (Table S5), and input sequence context length (Fig. S6), are detailed in the Supplementary Materials. Among all evaluated models, ProtT5 demonstrated the best baseline performance. Consequently, based on an optimal input context length of 18 aa upstream +18 aa linker+ 88 aa downstream, we employed ProtT5 for sequence embedding (with optimal dimensions reduced to 128 via PCA, accounting for 95% of the cumulative variance, effectively capturing the vast majority of information) and utilized a Multi-Layer Perceptron (MLP) as the downstream task layer. The incorporation of the structural module led to a consistent improvement in model robustness. While the absolute gain of the Pearson correlation coefficient was modest, the multimodal model demonstrated stable superiority over the sequence-only baseline under a variety of conditions. This was evidenced by its performance across different protein language model backbones (e.g., Prot-T5, Prot-Bert, Fig. 4B) and when assessed using different evaluation metrics (e.g., ACC, MSE, Spearman's correlation coefficient, Fig. 4C and Table S7). This consistent enhancing effect suggests that the in silico structural embeddings provided a complementary signal that systematically enhanced the model's reliability, rather than merely fine-tuning its performance on a single, specific metric. The robustness observed across these varied assessments validates the soundness of integrating sequence and structural information within our multimodal framework. In addition, the optimal multimodal model (ProtT5 + structural module combination) achieved an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.83 and a strong F1 score of 0.82 (Fig. S7A and S7B). A F1 score above 0.70 is generally considered indicative of reliable classification [34], our Multimodal DL Framework significantly exceeded this benchmark. Among the 15 validated fusion proteins, 13 exhibited fluorescence activity levels equal to or higher than the GS linker, ranging from 1.00 to 2.01, while two variants showed slightly reduced activity (0.39 and 0.88). Among these, the model correctly predicted higher activity than the GS linker variant for 8 variants and lower activity than the GS linker variant for 2 variants. The top-performing variant with the highest activity (2.01), TOP1 (linker sequence: KQRNHNVGWMNTRYHKED), showed an increased proportion of glycine residues (5.56%) and a complete absence of phenylalanine (F), isoleucine (I), and leucine (L), in contrast to the lowest-performing variant, BTM1 (sequence: TPYFLLKTINEFVKKKVL), which had 11.11% F, 5.56% I, and 16.67% L. To assess whether the linker design influenced the function of the N-terminal DOPARS, we also conducted functional assays specifically targeting its enzymatic activity. Using Western blot quantification of target protein expression (see Methods), we evaluated the efficiency of DOPA incorporation as a proxy for DOPARS activity (Fig. S7C and S7D). All 10 fusion proteins retained measurable DOPARS activity, with normalized values ranging from 0.96 to 1.79, further underscoring the impact of linker design on enzyme function. To evaluate the performance of the linker identified in our study, we directly compared our highest-activity linker variant (Top0 linker) against four commonly used linkers derived from independent literature, each representing distinct design philosophies and application contexts. These positive controls included: the rigid linker Ref1 [35], often used in bacterial systems; the classic flexible (G4S)n-type linker Ref2 [36]; the optimized flexible linker Ref3 [37] designed to improve solubility and stability; and the rigid α-helical spacer linker Ref4 [38]. All linkers were constructed within the identical Nb-sfGFP fusion framework and assessed for fluorescence activity in parallel. As shown in Fig. S8, the top0 linker obtained in this study conferred the highest normalized fluorescent activity to the fusion protein (defined as 100%). Among all controls, the rigid linker Ref1 performed best, reaching 85.14% of the Top0 linker's activity. The classic flexible linkers Ref2 and Ref3 showed lower activities, at 57.41% and 55.74%, respectively. The rigid helical linker Ref4 yielded the relatively lowest activity in this system, at 43.11%. all indicate that for the specific parental protein fusion, common general-purpose linker designs—whether rigid or flexible—were not outperformed by a custom linker identified through reasonable design and construction.

These results underscore that custom linker design is critical for maintaining the functional integrity of terminal domains in fusion proteins. Our findings establish that linker engineering directly tunes interdomain communication. By integrating sequence representations with predicted structural features, our multimodal deep learning framework successfully identifies high-functioning sfGFP fusion proteins, demonstrating its broader utility for the rational design of custom linkers.

Conclusions

Our results demonstrate that the peptide linker is a critical determinant in modulating the fluorescent function of sfGFP within a fusion protein. Although off-the-shelf flexible and rigid linkers are widely used in fusion protein construction, optimal performance for specialized functions, often requires linkers tailored beyond these conventional options. Despite the broad application of fusion protein technology in bioengineering, particularly in synthetic biology for oncotherapy, the selection of linkers remains largely restricted to standard, non-specialized peptides. Furthermore, fully artificial linker sequences designed to connect structured domains often exhibit low homology to known functional proteins, presenting a challenge for rational design.

Our findings indicate that AlphaFold captured local conformational variability pertinent to protein function. While the global sfGFP fold was conserved across all variant constructs in the dataset, the junctional region between sfGFP and the linker exhibited in silico substructural diversity. This variability, particularly evident in the Cα–Cα distance matrix, correlated with fluorescence output, suggesting that AlphaFold predictions of substructure reflect subtle, functionally relevant folding dynamics. Our results align with the capability of tools like RFDiffusion [39] and AlphaFold3 [40] to provide functional insight by resolving precise local conformations through atomic-level modeling. This underscores the potential of computational structural tools to guide linker evaluation by resolving local conformations that global fold prediction may overlook. These in silico structural features are compatible with modern protein language models, enabling a substructure-enhanced multimodal approach.

Consequently, we demonstrate that a deep learning model trained on both encoded sequence representations and predicted in silico substructural features can evaluate sfGFP function in fusion proteins, even when high functional activity is required. Thus, the customization of linkers for specific fusion architectures can be achieved using a relatively small, empirically validated dataset. This approach is uniquely advantageous for de novo linker sequences that lack evolutionary homology, as the model excels in exploring the vast sequence space and high diversity inherent to synthetic protein design. This approach is effective particularly for linker sequences generated de novo, which possess minimal evolutionary homology to entries in current protein databases, as the model is particularly well-suited to the high linker diversity and expansive design space characteristic of complex synthetic and evolutionary design scenarios [41,42].
