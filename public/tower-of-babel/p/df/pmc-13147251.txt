Applications of coronary computed tomography angiography: a clinical cardiologist’s point of view
Authors: Leonardo Bolognese, Marco Chioccioli, Matteo Rocco Reccia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractCoronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) has evolved into a cornerstone modality for the non-invasive assessment of coronary artery disease (CAD). Advances in detector technology, image reconstruction, and functional post-processing have enabled high-fidelity visualization of coronary anatomy, plaque morphology, and flow dynamics at radiation doses well below 1 mSv. CCTA now plays a pivotal role not only in the diagnosis of CAD but also in prognostic risk stratification and management guidance. Techniques such as CT-derived fractional flow reserve (FFR-CT), quantitative plaque analysis, and artificial intelligence-driven image interpretation are expanding its role towards comprehensive anatomical and functional evaluation. This review summarizes the technical developments, clinical applications, prognostic value, and emerging innovations of CCTA, highlighting its integration in modern patient management and its potential within precision cardiovascular medicine.Keywords: Coronary Computed Tomography Angiography, Photon-counting CT, Invasive Coronary Angiography, Coronary Atherosclerosis, Coronary Intervention Planning

Coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) has undergone remarkable evolution since its introduction in the early 2000s. Initially limited by motion artefacts, high radiation dose, and modest spatial resolution, continuous improvements in scanner hardware and software algorithms have established CCTA as a reliable and widely available modality for the evaluation of coronary artery disease (CAD) which has rapidly become an essential tool in the modern cardiologist's diagnostic and management arsenal.1 From a clinical perspective, CCTA's versatility and non-invasive nature allow it to be applied across a wide spectrum of cardiovascular care from the assessment of stenosis, ischaemia, and plaque characterization to preprocedural planning for complex coronary and structural interventions, as well as surgical guidance.1 CCTA's expanding role is reflected in current guidelines, where it is increasingly recommended as a first-line diagnostic modality for patients with low- to intermediate-risk, stable chest pain and suspected CAD.2 This review briefly summarizes the technical developments, clinical applications, prognostic value, and emerging innovations of CCTA, highlighting its integration into modern patient management and its potential within precision cardiovascular medicine.

Technical development in CCTA

An impressive array of technological developments has contributed greatly to expanding the clinical role of CCTA. Modern CCTA systems employ multidetector CT scanners with up to 320 detector rows, enabling complete heart coverage in a single rotation. Dual-source and dual-energy systems improve temporal resolution to <70 ms.3 Spatial resolution has improved from 0.6 mm to approximately 0.25 mm with ultra-high-resolution detectors, allowing more precise delineation of small vessels and plaque components. Radiation exposure has dramatically declined: typical effective doses now range from 0.5 to 2.0 mSv, representing a 70–90% reduction compared with early-generation systems.3 Finally, low-kVp imaging and optimized iodine delivery protocols enhance luminal opacification while allowing reduced contrast doses. Automated vessel segmentation, centreline extraction, and plaque quantification tools have streamlined workflow, while artificial intelligence (AI)-based denoising further refines image quality.3

The advent of photon-counting CT (PCD-CT) has further enhanced spectral separation, improving contrast-to-noise ratio and suppressing calcium blooming.4 By directly converting photon energies into electronic signals, PCD-CT offers significant benefits over conventional detectors, such as improved spatial resolution and decreased blooming artefacts. These advances allow a substantial reduction in detector pixel size and a consequent more accurate and detailed evaluation of coronary arteries and atherosclerotic plaques.

Detection of CAD

CCTA provides excellent diagnostic accuracy for the detection of obstructive CAD, with pooled sensitivity and specificity of approximately 95% and 85%, respectively, compared with invasive coronary angiography (ICA).1 The negative predictive value exceeds 95%, making CCTA particularly valuable for ruling out significant CAD in low-to-intermediate-risk patients.1,2 Recent technological advancements, such as PCD-CT, may further enhance image resolution and provide inherent spectral information.1,4 Sakai et al.5 retrospectively compared the diagnostic performance of PCD-CT and conventional CT (EID-CT) in 7833 patients undergoing CCTA in a single centre. PCD-CT resulted in fewer referrals for ICA compared with EID-CT (9.9% vs. 13.1%, P < 0.001). Among patients referred to ICA, revascularization was more frequently performed in the PCD-CT group (43.4% vs. 35.5%, P = 0.02). Vessel-level analysis revealed higher specificity (98.0% vs. 93.0%), positive predictive value (83.3% vs. 63.0%), and overall diagnostic accuracy (97.2% vs. 92.8%) with PCD-CT (P < 0.001), whereas sensitivity (90.9% vs. 90.7%) and negative predictive value (98.9% vs. 98.7%) were comparable.

Conventional CT scanners cannot accurately image stents with thick struts or small diameters, due to blooming artefacts, metallic interference, beam-hardening, and reduced spatial resolution. PCD-CT also has the potential to overcome these limitations, demonstrating improved visibility of the stent lumen, reduced noise levels, fewer blooming artefacts, and overall improved image quality expanding the ability to detect in-stent restenosis even in stented segments with diameters <3.0 mm.6

Functional assessment

While CCTA has proven valuable for visualizing coronary anatomy, its restriction to anatomical assessment alone has driven the development of CT-derived fractional flow reserve (FFR-CT), an innovation that integrates both anatomical and functional insights, enhancing the ability to evaluate the physiological significance of coronary artery stenoses. FFR-CT algorithms have been developed by multiple companies. A CTCA generates an anatomical image of the coronary arteries; the image is processed into three-dimensional models of the arteries, and computational fluid dynamics is applied to quantify the pressure proximal and distal to a lesion so FFR can be calculated.1 The calculation of FFR-CT is computationally intensive, meaning many healthcare providers outsource this to specialist off-site services. Recent meta-analyses suggest that the overall diagnostic accuracy of FFR-CT is higher than that of CTCA.7-9 A 2017 meta-analysis, however, found that the diagnostic accuracy of FFR-CT varies with CAD severity and is low for FFR-CT values around 0.8.8 Directly appraising the relationship of FFR-CT to invasively measured FFR may provide a better understanding of the validity of the test than diagnostic accuracy alone. One meta-analysis, encompassing 43 studies and over 7000 vessels from 5236 patients, provides the most extensive evaluation to date of the diagnostic performance and clinical applicability of FFR-CT compared with invasive FFR.9 This analysis found a moderate correlation between FFR-CT and invasive FFR (Spearman coefficient 0.67). Overall, FFR-CT demonstrated robust diagnostic accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity (82.2%, 80.9%, and 83.1%, respectively). The diagnostic performance of FFR-CT varied by value range: values >0.90 had a 93% likelihood of ruling out haemodynamically significant CAD, while an FFR-CT <0.49 had nearly 100% accuracy in identifying physiologically significant lesions. This suggests that at either end of the FFR-CT spectrum, the modality performs exceptionally well and can guide confident clinical decision-making. In contrast, diagnostic uncertainty increases within the intermediate range or ‘grey zone’ of 0.75–0.80, where accuracy drops significantly, potentially requiring additional testing before deciding on revascularization or deferral.

FFR-CT, combined with risk stratification, effectively acts as a gatekeeper to the catheterization lab, reducing the number of unnecessary invasive procedures without compromising safety10

Clinical outcomes were investigated in the large prospective ADVANCE (Assessing Diagnostic Value of Noninvasive FFR-CT in Coronary Care) registry, which enrolled 5083 clinically stable symptomatic patients diagnosed with CAD on CTA. No death or myocardial infarction occurred within 90 days in patients with an FFR-CT >0.80 (n = 1529). The prognostic value of FFR-CT has been confirmed for up to 7 years.11

It is important to recognize that most trials to date have enrolled highly selected patient populations, often excluding individuals with arrhythmias, high coronary calcium scores, obesity, renal dysfunction, prior revascularization, or other common comorbidities. These exclusions limit generalization to real-world settings.

Finally, dynamic and static CT perfusion (CTP) allows direct visualization of myocardial blood flow (MBF). Dynamic CTP provides quantitative MBF but at higher radiation cost, while static CTP offers qualitative MBF detection with lower dose.1 Combined CCTA–CTP approaches improve specificity for ischaemia compared to CCTA alone.1 CTP requires specific image acquisition protocols, including two consecutive scans, higher radiation doses, and infusion of a hyperaemic agent, and is not used as a routine diagnostic tool in current clinical practice.

Plaque characterization

CCTA has emerged as an ideal modality to visualize, characterize, and quantify specific plaque components. Adverse plaque characteristics (APCs), such as positive remodelling, low computed tomographic attenuation, spotty calcification, and the napkin-ring sign, have been shown to confer an increased risk for major adverse cardiac events (MACE) during mid-term follow-up.12 Furthermore, plaque volume quantification using semiautomated software or AI algorithms provides incremental prognostic value beyond APCs and stenosis severity during mid-to long-term follow-up13

Imaging of the peri-coronary adipose tissue (PCAT) is emerging as a valuable biosensor of dynamic inflammatory processes within the coronary vasculature. Averaging the attenuation within the PCAT provides a crude but still useful marker of coronary inflammation, offering an opportunity for risk stratification of epicardial CAD by capturing inflammatory risk.14 Although, currently, there are no invasive validation methods available for PCD-CT-based plaque characterization, several studies highlight the advanced tissue characterization capabilities of PCD-CT, leading to improved visualization of different plaque components potentially improving overall risk stratification.4

Finally, serial CCTA studies have advanced our understanding of vascular biology and atherosclerotic disease progression allowing investigation of the impact of medical therapy and dynamic changes in plaque phenotypes over time.4

Procedural planning for complex PCI and surgical guidance

Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) planning is highly valuable to inform operators about case complexity, guiding catheter selection, determining optimal fluoroscopic angles, assessing plaque composition—including calcium assessment to guide device selection—estimating lesion and stent length, and evaluating luminal dimensions. Interventional cardiologists focused on CAD would benefit from learning CCTA and mastering it sufficiently to perform procedural planning for PCI with the same accuracy that is now the standard of care before TAVI.15

Recently, the feasibility of using CCTA rather than ICA to plan coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) have been investigated. In the Safety and Feasibility Evaluation of Planning and Execution of Surgical Revascularization Solely Based on Coronary CTA and FFR-CT in Patient with Complex Coronary Artery Diseases (FASTTRACK CABG) study, patients with three-vessel disease and/or left main CAD underwent CABG procedures planned based on CCTA without prior knowledge of ICA.16 CABG guided by CCTA showed to be feasible with an acceptable safety profile. These intriguing findings should be confirmed in a large, randomized trial on the revascularization outcome by comparing patients who underwent a novel non-invasive vs. a traditional invasive roadmap.

From CV risk assessment to a personalized care strategy

At a population level, coronary atherosclerosis is more prevalent in the presence of cardiovascular risk factors; however, the association between risk factors and atherosclerosis shows considerable imprecision and high variability at the individual level.3

Coronary artery calcium score (CACS) has demonstrated incremental value and may be considered in asymptomatic individuals near established risk thresholds. CCTA, by identifying subclinical obstructive atherosclerosis and plaque burden and morphology, can further enhance risk stratification in asymptomatic patients.3

Temporal changes in plaque burden and morphology provide additional refinement for risk stratification, supporting the implementation of personalized medicine: patients can receive tailored therapy based on their individual atherosclerosis phenotype.

In future, an all-in-one coronary CTA AI-based evaluation of lumen, plaque, myocardial, and PCAT characteristics may provide an integrated approach to overall risk prediction. This framework could then be interrogated by clinicians and disaggregated into individual lesion characteristics or specific combinations of characteristics that have distinct prognostic timeframes.3,13

Integration with AI and emerging technologies

AI algorithms have been integrated across multiple stages of the CCTA workflow—from image reconstruction and noise suppression to automated coronary segmentation. Deep learning-based reconstruction reduces noise and artefacts, allowing dose reduction without quality loss. Supervised and convolutional neural network models enable automated identification and classification of plaque components, achieving accuracy comparable to expert readers. AI-based analysis accelerates workflow and standardizes quantification across centres.17

Radiomics is a rapidly evolving field of research that seeks to extract quantitative metrics from medical images. Unsurprisingly, the application of radiomics has also reached cardiac CT research. In recent years, radiomics transforms pixel-level data into high-dimensional quantitative features reflecting texture, shape, and intensity. When combined with clinical and laboratory data, these features have been shown to independently predict MACE beyond conventional metrics.12,13

Limitations, uptake, and barriers to wider adoption

Cardiac imaging is experiencing a rapid expansion, playing an increasingly prominent role across all facets of cardiology. CCTA leads this growth as a versatile modality with multiple applications. However, technical, clinical, and economic barriers limit its widespread utilization.

The diagnostic reliability of CCTA is highly dependent on image quality and interpretation. Access to advanced CT scanners with qualified readers is uneven across regions and represents one of the most significant limiting factors. Conventional scanners face challenges such as limited spatial resolution for resolving small structures, image degradation due to electronic noise, and blooming artefacts. Thus, despite its high diagnostic performance in large-scale trials, ‘the real-world performance of CCTA when interpreted by nonexpert readers is less sanguine’.18

Additionally, the dependency of FFR-CT on high-quality image acquisition remains a critical limitation. In real-world practice, non-diagnostic scan rates ranging from 10% to 25% raise valid concerns regarding reproducibility, particularly in high-volume centres managing patients with common comorbidities or suboptimal scan conditions. Traditional FFR-CT solutions also require an off-site supercomputing infrastructure. This setup imposes limitations related to processing time, cost, and scalability, making it less accessible in time-sensitive or resource-limited settings. Until improvements in scanner technology, acquisition protocols, and motion correction algorithms become more widely adopted, integrating FFR-CT into routine clinical workflows must be approached cautiously, with a realistic understanding of its current technical constraints.

As the cardiology community increasingly adopts CCTA, it is imperative to define appropriate use criteria, integrate CCTA findings into guideline-driven management, and ensure cost-effectiveness and patient safety. Cardiologists must understand its limitations and ensure that CCTA is embedded within a comprehensive patient-centred workflow in collaboration with imaging colleagues, particularly radiologists.

Addressing these concerns requires involvement of various stakeholders, clinicians, radiologists, cardiologists, and healthcare institutions management.

Conclusions

CCTA has evolved from a primarily anatomic imaging tool to a multiparametric modality integrating structural, functional, and prognostic information. Continuous innovations in scanner design, image reconstruction, and computational analysis have established CCTA as the first-line test for stable chest pain and a powerful instrument for risk stratification and management guidance in CAD. Functional extensions, including FFR-CT and CT perfusion, combined with AI-driven quantification and photon-counting detectors, are redefining non-invasive coronary imaging. As evidence grows, CCTA is poised to play a central role in precision cardiovascular medicine, bridging anatomy, physiology, and predictive analytics within a single, comprehensive examination.
