Harnessing natural killer cell-related genes for prognostic and therapeutic advances in lung adenocarcinoma: a predictive model for survival and immunotherapy outcomes
Authors: Minqi Zhu, Likang Wang, Fang Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), the most common subtype of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC), is characterized by high mortality rates and complex immune evasion mechanisms. Natural killer (NK) cells play a crucial role in tumor immune surveillance, with their activity regulated by specific genes. Recently, biomarkers derived from NK cell-related genes have garnered significant attention for their potential in predicting cancer prognosis. This study aimed to evaluate the clinical utility of a prognostic model based on NK cell-related genes in patients with LUAD.MethodsIn this study, gene expression data and clinical information from LUAD patients were obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) databases, and differentially expressed genes associated with natural killer cells (DENKCRGs) were identified. A prognostic risk score model was developed using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression and Cox regression analysis. The model’s performance was subsequently validated through Kaplan-Meier survival curves, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, enrichment analysis, and immune infiltration analysis. Additionally, its predictive capacity for immune therapy response and drug sensitivity was evaluated.ResultsA total of 493 LUAD patient datasets were retrieved from TCGA, 857 from the GEO database, and 244 NK cell-related genes were identified based on prior studies. The filtered data were then partitioned into training and testing sets. Through LASSO regression and Cox regression analysis, eight genes (PAK1, PLCG2, SHC3, SHC1, TOX, ARRB2, SERPINB4, and NLRC4) were identified as significantly associated with prognosis. A prognostic model based on these genes was developed, categorizing patients into high-risk and low-risk groups. Strong predictive performance was observed in the training set, testing set, and GEO dataset. Immune infiltration analysis revealed notable differences in immune cell distribution and immune response intensity between the high-risk and low-risk groups, with low-risk patients demonstrating greater responsiveness to immunotherapy. Furthermore, drug sensitivity analysis indicated that the high-risk group exhibited increased sensitivity to Axitinib, while the low-risk group showed higher responsiveness to drugs such as cisplatin.ConclusionsThe prognostic model developed in this study, based on NK cell-related genes, demonstrates considerable value in assessing the prognosis of LUAD. It not only serves as a predictor of patient survival but also provides a theoretical foundation for personalized immunotherapy and drug selection. Future research should focus on further validating the clinical applicability of this model and exploring its potential in the context of immunotherapy and targeted therapies.Keywords: Lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), natural killer cells (NK cells), prognostic model, immune infiltration, drug sensitivity

Highlight box

Key findings

• This study developed an natural killer (NK) cell-related gene prognostic model for lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), stratifying patients into high- and low-risk groups based on survival and immunotherapy responses. Key predictors include PAK1, SHC1, and immune infiltration patterns, offering potential for personalized treatment strategies.

What is known and what is new?

• LUAD represents a prognostically challenging and prevalent non-small cell lung cancer subtype, where NK cells mediate innate immune surveillance and their functional activity correlates significantly with patient survival outcomes.

• We developed a novel prognostic signature incorporating eight NK cell-related genes that effectively stratifies LUAD patients into distinct risk categories, uncovering significant intergroup differences in immune microenvironment characteristics and therapeutic vulnerabilities, thereby providing a framework for personalized treatment strategies.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• This study established an NK cell-related gene signature for accurate prognostic prediction in LUAD, guiding personalized immunotherapy and drug selection. Future work should focus on clinical validation, mechanistic research, and exploring combination therapies for high-risk patients.

Introduction

Lung cancer remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) being the most prevalent subtype, accounting for approximately 85% of all lung cancer cases (1). Early clinical manifestations of NSCLC are often subtle or absent, and as the disease progresses, 90% of patients in the advanced stages experience symptoms such as fatigue, persistent cough, chest pain, shortness of breath, hemoptysis, and unexplained weight loss (2). In 2023, the United States reported 238,340 new lung cancer cases, resulting in approximately 127,070 deaths (3). With significant advancements in diagnostic techniques and surgical interventions, the five-year survival rate for NSCLC patients has gradually improved. However, between 2010 and 2017, the overall survival rate for cancer patients in the United States remained approximately 26.4%, with a five-year survival rate of only 5.8% for those diagnosed at stage IV (4).

NSCLC includes several major histological subtypes, such as lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD), squamous cell carcinoma, and large cell carcinoma. Among these, LUAD is the most common subtype, accounting for approximately 40% to 50% of all NSCLC cases. Its incidence has been steadily increasing in recent years, especially among non-smokers and female patients. LUAD exhibits considerable heterogeneity, characterized by complex molecular mechanisms, a high propensity for early metastasis, and marked variability in therapeutic responses (5,6). Current therapeutic approaches primarily include surgery, chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy (7). The advent of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as pembrolizumab and nivolumab, has revolutionized treatment by targeting pathways that suppress anti-tumor immune responses (8,9). These agents primarily function by activating cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) within the tumor microenvironment, leading to improved clinical outcomes. However, a subset of patients continues to exhibit either primary or acquired resistance to these therapies (10,11).

Natural killer (NK) cells, as a fundamental component of the innate immune system, play a pivotal role in immune surveillance and tumor suppression (12). NK cells can rapidly identify and eliminate malignant cells during the early stages of tumor development. Unlike CTLs, they can recognize stressed or transformed cells independently of antigen presentation. These distinctive features position NK cells as critical factors in overcoming resistance mechanisms in cancer therapies, thereby significantly impacting cancer prognosis, particularly in LUAD (13). Studies have shown that cytokine-based therapies [e.g., interleukin (IL)-15, IL-15] or engineered NK cells can promote NK cell proliferation and activation, enhancing their anti-tumor efficacy (14).

In recent years, advancements in bioinformatics have facilitated the development of models to assess the impact of NK cell-related gene targets on the prognosis and survival outcomes of LUAD patients. The construction of such models is expected to improve the accuracy of prognostic predictions, enabling personalized and precise treatment strategies, and ultimately allowing more patients to benefit from NK cell-targeted therapies. We present this article in accordance with the TRIPOD reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-380/rc).

Methods

Data collection and processing

The RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data, microarray data, clinical information, and mutation data related to LUAD patients were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) (GSE30219, GSE31210, GSE74777, and GSE157010), totaling 857 samples (15-18). Data were also screened from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/), excluding samples with missing follow-up data, records with a time interval of less than 30 days, missing outcome data records, and duplicate records, resulting in 493 samples. Additionally, 244 natural killer cell-related genes (NKCRGs) were obtained from relevant existing studies (19). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Differential gene analysis

The “limma” package in R was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between normal lung tissue and LUAD tissue in the TCGA-LUAD dataset, applying thresholds of “false discovery rate (FDR) <0.05” and “log2fold change (FC) ≥0.585” (20). The resulting DEGs were intersected with NKCRGs to identify NKCRG-associated DEGs (DENKCRGs). To assess the prognostic significance of DENKCRGs in LUAD, univariate Cox regression analysis was performed on the TCGA-LUAD dataset (P<0.05). Differential co-expression network analysis of DENKCRGs was carried out using the “igraph” package in R. Protein-protein interaction analysis of the proteins encoded by DENKCRGs was conducted using STRING (https://string-db. database). Finally, survival analysis and log-rank tests were performed to validate the prognostic value of DENKCRGs.

Clustering analysis of NKCRGs

To further investigate the role of NKCRG expression in LUAD, consensus unsupervised clustering analysis was performed using the “ConsensusClusterPlus” package in R, identifying distinct molecular subgroups of patients based on expression profiles. The clustering process was guided by criteria such as a smooth and consistent increase in the cumulative distribution function curve, prevention of small sample sizes, and a balance between inter-group and intra-group correlations (21,22).

Exploration of molecular subtypes

Kaplan-Meier curves were generated using the “survival” and “survminer” packages in R to compare the prognostic outcomes across different subtypes. By analyzing the expression patterns of NKCRGs across the various clusters, distinct regulatory patterns of NKCRGs were identified. The relationships between molecular subtypes, clinical characteristics, and prognosis were further explored to assess the clinical relevance of the two clusters. Finally, the activation status of Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathways within each NKCRG cluster was evaluated using the Gene Set Variation Analysis (GSVA) algorithm, and the top 30 enrichment results for each group were visualized as heatmaps (23,24).

Construction of prognostic model

The “caret” package in R was used to randomly partition the TCGA samples into training and testing sets in a 6:4 ratio. A 10-fold cross-validation approach was applied in least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression analysis to further refine the DENKCRGs associated with prognosis (25,26). A multivariable Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was then conducted to construct the prognostic model. The risk score for each sample was calculated using the following formula: Risk score = coefficient1 × gene1 expression + ... + coefficientN × geneN expression, with samples categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median risk score (27).

Validation of prognostic features

Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis and the chi-square test were used to assess survival differences between the high-risk and low-risk groups in the model. The test set, validation set, and datasets from the GEO database were used to validate the model’s stability. The “time ROC” package in R was used to evaluate the predictive performance of the model and clinical features through time-dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (28,29). Survival differences among risk score groups within each subgroup were compared to evaluate the model’s applicability to patients with different clinical characteristics. Univariate and multivariate Cox analyses, incorporating risk scores and clinical features, were conducted to determine whether the model could independently predict patient prognosis. The concordance index (C-index) was used to calculate the accuracy of the features. The relationships between the groups and clinical features were analyzed to assess the model’s clinical relevance, and a nomogram integrating the model with clinical features was constructed to predict 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival probabilities for patients.

Enrichment analysis and somatic mutations

Visualization analysis was performed using the “maftools” package in R to examine somatic mutation differences between risk groups (30). Differential expression of risk genes was identified based on the criteria of “|logFC| >1” and FDR <0.05. To explore the molecular mechanisms and pathway differences among risk groups, Gene Ontology (GO) functional enrichment analysis and KEGG pathway enrichment analysis were performed (31).

Immune infiltration analysis

Immune cell infiltration and immune function analyses were performed on the high- and low-risk groups using the Cell-type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) algorithm and single-sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) (32,33). The Wilcoxon test was used to assess differences in immune checkpoint gene expression levels between the groups. The Tumor Immune Dysfunction and Exclusion (TIDE) score was calculated and compared between groups to predict the response to immunotherapy in the high- and low-risk groups (34).

Drug sensitivity analysis

Drug sensitivity was predicted using the “calcPhenotype” function from the R package “oncoPredict”, based on data from the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database (35).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using R (v4.2.1). DEGs were identified with the “limma” package (FDR <0.05, |log2FC| ≥0.585). Prognostic NK cell-related genes were screened using univariate Cox, LASSO, and multivariate Cox regression to build a risk model. Survival differences were evaluated by Kaplan-Meier and log-rank tests. Model performance was assessed by time-dependent ROC and C-index. Chi-squared and Wilcoxon tests were used for comparing clinical features, immune infiltration, and drug sensitivity. Immune analysis employed CIBERSORT, ssGSEA, and TIDE. A nomogram was constructed for individualized prognosis. P<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Data preprocessing and differential gene analysis

A total of 16,642 genes were extracted from 493 samples selected from the TCGA-LUAD dataset (Table 1). Using the criteria of “FDR <0.05 and log2FC ≥0.585”, 5,011 DEGs were identified, including 2,249 upregulated and 2,762 downregulated genes (Table 1, Figure 2A). The differential expression of each sample is depicted in a heatmap (Figure 2B). The 5,011 DEGs from the TCGA-LUAD dataset were intersected with 244 NKCRGs, resulting in 73 common genes (Figure 2C). Univariate Cox regression analysis identified 16 DEGs, including ITGAL, PAK1, CD247, and PLCG2, with P<0.05, among which the expression of SHC1 and KNSTRN was particularly significant (P<0.001) (Figure 2D). Differential co-expression analysis of the identified genes revealed that most gene interactions were positively correlated. Genes such as ITGAL, PTPRC, and PRKCB were identified as central nodes with multiple connections, indicating their potential key roles in the biological processes (BP) under investigation (Figure 2E). Protein-protein interaction networks were constructed using STRING for the encoded proteins of DENKCRGs, with SHC1, PLCG2, CD247, and PRKCB positioned centrally and exhibiting multiple connections with surrounding nodes (Figure 2F).

Clustering analysis and subtype exploration of NKCRGs

To investigate the relationship between the expression patterns of NKCRGs and LUAD, clustering analysis was performed using the R package “ConsensusCluster”, based on the input gene expression profile, to elucidate the role of NKCRG-related genes. When K=2, the correlation between patients assigned to group 1 (201 cases, 40.7%) and group 2 (292 cases, 59.3%) was higher than that at other values of K (Figure 3A, Figure S1). Kaplan-Meier survival curve analysis demonstrated that the survival rate of group B patients was significantly higher than that of group A (Figure 3B, P<0.001). Further analysis of the two groups, considering variables such as age, gender, and cancer staging, revealed correlations ranging from red (positive) to blue (negative). Group A exhibited more negative correlations with the 16 identified genes, whereas group B showed more positive correlations (Figure 3C). Analysis of the expression of ITGAL, CD247, PLCG2, PIK3CG, SHC3, SHC1, PRKCB, PTPRC, TOX, CRTAM, CADM1, KNSTRN, and NLRC4 in groups A and B revealed significant expression differences for all genes. Only SHC1 expression was higher in group A than in group B, while the expression of the other genes was higher in group B. Furthermore, the expression level of SHC1 was relatively higher than that of the other genes in both groups A and B, and the expression of TOX and CADM1 exhibited greater variability (Figure 3D). The GSVA algorithm was employed to assess the activation of KEGG pathways in each NKCRG cluster. The heatmap revealed that the top 15 pathways were expressed at low levels in group A and at high levels in group B, while the remaining 15 pathways exhibited the opposite trend (Figure 3E).

Construction of a prognostic model related to DENKCRGs

ROC analysis was performed to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) values for each DENKCRG, and the values were subsequently screened. The selected genes were further analyzed using LASSO regression, leading to the identification of the most strongly correlated genes: PAK1, PLCG2, SHC3, SHC1, TOX, ARRB2, SERPINB4, and NLRC4. Multivariable Cox regression analysis was subsequently conducted to construct a prognostic model (Figure 4A,4B, Figure S2). The risk score for each sample was calculated using the following formula: Risk score = (0.4089 × PAK1) + (−0.1242 × PLCG2) + (−0.1949 × SHC3) + (0.2496 × SHC1) + (−0.0513 × TOX) + (−0.1586 × ARRB2) + (0.0815 × SERPINB4) + (−0.0759 × NLRC4).

Stratified analysis of DENKCRGs related to clinical features in LUAD patients

Patients in the cohort were categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the median value. The TCGA dataset was divided into a training set and a testing set, while the GEO dataset was utilized to evaluate the constructed model. Analysis of the three groups revealed that the survival rate of the low-risk group was higher than that of the high-risk group. Significant differences were observed between the high- and low-risk groups in the training set (P=0.001), testing set (P=0.03), and GEO dataset (P<0.001). A heatmap was generated to illustrate the expression of eight selected genes in the training set, testing set, and GEO dataset, showing that PAK1, SHC1, and SERPINB4 were highly expressed in the high-risk group (Figure 4C-4E). ROC curve analysis of the three datasets indicated that the AUCs for predicting 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates were 0.754, 0.653, and 0.654, respectively, in the training set; 0.682, 0.617, and 0.614, respectively, in the testing set; and 0.706, 0.633, and 0.627, respectively, in the GEO dataset, demonstrating the model’s strong predictive performance (Figure 5A-5F). Data from four GEO databases (GSE30219, GSE31210, GSE74777, GSE157010) were used to assess survival, confirming the aforementioned results. All four groups indicated that the survival rate in the high-risk group was lower than that in the low-risk group, with significant statistical differences between the groups, consistent with the results from the training and testing sets (Figure 6). Stratification analysis based on factors such as age, gender, Tumor, Node, Metastasis (TNM) stage, and disease stage revealed significant differences between the low-risk and high-risk groups for age (>65 vs. ≤65 years) (P=0.002), gender (female, P=0.01; male, P=0.001), disease location (left lung, P=0.002; right lung, P=0.02), stage I–II (P=0.002), primary tumor size and extent (T1-2, P=0.007; T3–4, P<0.001), and lymph node involvement (N0, P=0.006). However, no significant differences were found between the low-risk and high-risk groups in stages III–IV (P=0.12) and lymph node involvement (N1–3) (P=0.051) (Figure 7).

Cox regression analysis was conducted to assess whether the risk score could serve as an independent prognostic factor for LUAD patients. This suggests that the risk score may serve as an independent predictor of prognosis in LUAD patients (Figure 8A).

Nomogram of DENKCRGs in LUAD patients

A visual nomogram was constructed based on multivariate Cox regression analysis to predict the survival of LUAD patients at 1-, 3-, and 5-year following diagnosis, incorporating variables such as risk score, age, gender, stage, and tumor location (left/right lung). The nomogram displays the score for each variable in the first row, with the total score being the sum of individual variable scores. The corresponding survival probability is predicted by drawing a vertical line downward to the total score. The accuracy of the clinical characteristics was assessed using the concordance index (C-index), with higher values approaching 1 indicating better predictive ability. The curves for risk score and stage were positioned higher on the plot, nearing 0.7, indicating strong predictive performance within a specific time frame; however, their predictive ability declines after 15 years. In contrast, gender, age, and site of onset demonstrated lower values, mostly below 0.5, reflecting poor predictive capacity for LUAD prognosis. Overall, the predictive ability of these factors diminishes over time. In the short term, risk score and stage exhibited strong predictive potential, but long-term predictions of LUAD prognosis were less stable (Figure 8B,8C). Finally, a calibration curve (Figure S3) was generated to validate the nomogram’s accuracy, and the results indicated that the observed and predicted survival probabilities at 1, 3, and 5 years were consistent.

Mutation analysis and enrichment analysis of NKCRGs

Somatic mutations in the high- and low-risk groups were analyzed separately, and the top 10 most frequently mutated genes identified were TP53, TTN, MUC16, CSMD3, RYR2, LRP1B, ZFHX4, USH2A, KRAS, and XIRP2. TP53 exhibited the highest mutation frequency in both the high- and low-risk groups (Figure 9). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of DENKCRGs were conducted for both groups. In the low-risk group, BP analysis indicated that NKCRGs were predominantly involved in leukocyte-mediated immune responses, microtubule movement, and ciliary movement. Cellular component (CC) analysis revealed strong localization of NKCRGs to the cell membrane and extracellular matrix. Molecular function (MF) analysis showed that NKCRGs were primarily associated with receptor-ligand interactions (Figure 10A). KEGG pathway analysis of the low-risk group highlighted significant enrichment in pathways related to hematopoietic cell lineages, the PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, and cytokine-cytokine receptor interactions (Figure 10B). In the high-risk group, NKCRGs were mainly implicated in epidermal development, with similar CC distributions to the low-risk group, predominantly in the extracellular matrix. MF analysis identified a predominant role in oxidoreductase activity (Figure 10C). The major KEGG pathways in the high-risk group were significantly enriched in diseases such as amebiasis, steroid hormone biosynthesis, complement and coagulation cascades, and cofactor biosynthesis (Figure 10D).

Immune infiltration analysis of high and low-risk groups

CIBERSORT was used to predict tumor-infiltrating immune cell scores for each sample, with the abundance and distribution of immune cells in the high- and low-risk groups shown in Figure 11A,11B. Significant differences in immune cell types and their distribution were observed between the risk groups. Specifically, naive B cells, CD8+ T cells, and naive CD4+ T cells were more abundant in the low-risk group, suggesting that these immune cell types may play a more prominent role in the immune response within this group. In contrast, their proportions were lower in the high-risk group, indicating a reduced role in immune responses. Macrophages M1, macrophages M2, monocytes, activated dendritic cells, and activated NK cells were more abundant in the high-risk group compared to the low-risk group, suggesting that these immune cells are more active and contribute significantly to inflammation or immune responses in the high-risk group. Furthermore, the high-risk group exhibited greater immune cell diversity, reflecting more complex immune system regulation. A Spearman correlation matrix was constructed to analyze the relationship between immune cell types and risk scores. Memory B cells, plasma cells, macrophages M0, monocytes, activated dendritic cells, resting mast cells, and activated mast cells showed significant correlations with risk scores (orange lines, Spearman’s P<0.01). A strong correlation was observed between activated CD4+ memory T cells and resting CD4+ memory T cells, suggesting potential synergistic effects or a common functional pattern. Moreover, significant correlations were identified between resting mast cells, macrophages M0, activated dendritic cells, and macrophages M1, indicating that these cells may collectively contribute to specific immune responses (Figure 11C). ssGSEA was used to assess immune function scores in both risk groups. The low-risk group exhibited higher immune scores than the high-risk group, with statistically significant differences between the groups, except for MHC_class_I (P<0.001) (Figure 11D). Additionally, the Wilcoxon test was used to investigate the expression patterns of immune checkpoint genes. Except for CD276 and TNFSF9, immune checkpoint expression was higher in the low-risk group compared to the high-risk group, indicating lower immune marker expression in the high-risk group (P<0.05, Figure 12A). TIDE scores were calculated, revealing that the low-risk group had lower TIDE scores than the high-risk group. This suggests that tumors in the high-risk group are more likely to evade immune system attack through immune dysfunction or exclusion, potentially resulting in poorer responses to ICIs. Conversely, the lower TIDE scores in the low-risk group indicate greater sensitivity to immunotherapy and potentially better outcomes (Figure 12B). Similar trends were observed in survival curves, where patients with higher TIDE scores had lower survival rates. In the high-risk group, patients with high TIDE scores experienced the lowest survival rates, whereas in the low-risk group, patients with lower TIDE scores showed significantly higher survival rates (Figure 12C,12D).

Drug sensitivity analysis

Six drugs were selected from the Cancer Drug Sensitivity Genomics (GDSC) database: Axitinib, Cisplatin, Oxaliplatin, AZD5363, PAK_5339, and YK-4-279. Drug sensitivity predictions were made using the “calcPhenotype” function from the R package “oncoPredict”. The analysis revealed that the high-risk group exhibited greater sensitivity to Axitinib, while the low-risk group showed higher sensitivity to the other drugs (Cisplatin, Oxaliplatin, AZD5363, PAK_5339, and YK-4-279). Significant statistical differences in drug sensitivity between the groups were observed for all drugs, except Oxaliplatin. These findings suggest that patients with different risk levels exhibit varying drug sensitivities, which is crucial for optimizing clinical treatment decisions (Figure 13).

Discussion

LUAD, the most common subtype of NSCLC, continues to pose significant challenges in early diagnosis and treatment. Despite significant advancements in current therapeutic approaches, including chemotherapy, targeted therapy, and ICIs, many patients continue to experience resistance and poor prognosis during treatment (6,36). Therefore, identifying novel biomarkers and developing predictive models are of paramount clinical importance for accurately forecasting patient prognosis and guiding personalized treatment strategies. NK cells play a crucial role in anti-tumor immunity in LUAD. As a central component of the innate immune system, NK cells exert cytotoxic effects by directly targeting tumor cells without requiring antigen presentation. Previous studies have demonstrated a significant association between NK cell infiltration in the immune microenvironment of LUAD patients and prognosis, with higher NK cell activity often correlating with better outcomes. NK cell-related genes are typically highly expressed in low-risk patients, which are associated with enhanced anti-tumor immune responses. Conversely, these genes are often underexpressed in high-risk patients, suggesting either NK cell suppression or the presence of tumor microenvironment factors that hinder their activation (37,38). This study, utilizing NK cell-related genes and comprehensive bioinformatics methods combined with RNA sequencing data from LUAD patients, established a prognostic risk model. The model effectively identifies NKCRGs that are crucial for predicting patient prognosis and treatment responses, providing valuable insights into their clinical application in LUAD. The findings highlight the significant clinical potential of this model for both prognostic prediction and immune therapy response evaluation.

In this study, RNA-seq data and clinical information from LUAD patients were initially obtained from the TCGA database. After analyzing the expression patterns of NK cell-related genes in LUAD patients, we identified DENKCRGs associated with NK cell function. A prognostic model consisting of eight core genes was subsequently constructed using LASSO regression and Cox regression analysis, which stratified patients into high-risk and low-risk groups. The study identified NK cell-related genes—PAK1, PLCG2, SHC3, SHC1, TOX, ARRB2, SERPINB4, and NLRC4—whose differential expression significantly affected patient survival. For example, PAK1 and SHC1 were highly expressed in the high-risk group and were strongly associated with poor prognosis in LUAD patients. These findings are consistent with previous studies that highlighted the role of these genes in lung cancer. PAK1 has been implicated in tumor metastasis and invasion (39,40), while SHC1 is involved in tumor signal transduction and cell proliferation (41). Furthermore, based on the NKCRG risk model, patients were categorized into high-risk and low-risk groups, and gene expression patterns were analyzed, revealing distinct clinical outcomes. In the high-risk group, most NK cell-related genes were expressed at low levels, indicating suppressed NK cell activity. This suggests that these genes may either promote tumor progression or reflect a reduced immune response to effectively combat the tumor. Conversely, the low-risk group exhibited higher expression levels of these genes, which were associated with enhanced anti-tumor immune responses. Analysis of the survival curve further revealed that the survival rate of patients in the high-risk group was significantly lower than that of those in the low-risk group, with a statistically significant difference (P<0.05). The AUC of this model demonstrated strong performance in predicting the 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates, indicating its high accuracy in clinical prognosis prediction. This model exhibits high prognostic accuracy and underscores the pivotal role of NK cells in tumor immune surveillance.

Further analysis of somatic mutations revealed an overall mutation rate of 94.29% in the high-risk group, significantly exceeding the 86.97% observed in the low-risk group. These findings indicate that tumors in high-risk patients may possess increased genomic instability and a more complex mutational landscape, potentially driving more aggressive biological behavior and poorer clinical outcomes. Although a higher mutational burden is generally associated with increased neoantigen production and enhanced immunogenicity, a profoundly immunosuppressive microenvironment may impede effective immune clearance and instead facilitate immune evasion. In contrast, despite a relatively lower mutation rate, the low-risk group may benefit from a more active immune status, enhancing tumor surveillance and clearance and ultimately contributing to better survival outcomes. These findings further validate the utility of the risk score model in capturing immune status and informing prognostic stratification. NK cells mediate early immune clearance by detecting aberrant molecules or stress-induced signals on tumor cell surfaces. This function is particularly critical in LUAD, a malignancy marked by a distinctly immunosuppressive microenvironment. Moreover, NK cell functionality is modulated by both the tumor microenvironment and related regulatory genes. The differential expression of DENKCRGs suggests their substantial potential in regulating NK cell activity and facilitating immune evasion. Given the critical role of NK cells in early tumor immune surveillance and their modulation by both the tumor microenvironment and regulatory gene expression, we next investigated key signaling pathways that may underpin the observed differences in NK cell activity between risk groups. KEGG pathway analysis revealed significant enrichment of the hematopoietic cell lineage, PI3K-Akt signaling, and cytokine–cytokine receptor interaction pathways in the low-risk group. These pathways are closely associated with immune cell development, activation, and signaling, and are particularly important for NK cell function. The enrichment of the hematopoietic cell lineage pathway suggests a more complete development and differentiation of NK cells within the immune system. The PI3K-Akt signaling pathway plays a central role in regulating NK cell survival, proliferation, and cytotoxicity, including interferon (IFN)-γ production. The cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction pathway is essential for NK cell activation, with cytokines such as IL-2, IL-15, and IL-12 enhancing their effector function. In contrast, the high-risk group exhibited enrichment in pathways associated with amebiasis, steroid hormone biosynthesis, complement and coagulation cascades, and cofactor biosynthesis. Although the amebiasis pathway is primarily associated with infection, its enrichment may indicate chronic inflammation and immune dysregulation within the tumor microenvironment. Upregulated steroid hormone biosynthesis may indicate increased glucocorticoid activity, which is known to suppress NK cell cytotoxicity and compromise immune surveillance. Moreover, excessive activation of complement and coagulation cascades is thought to contribute to pro-inflammatory signaling and immune evasion, potentially impairing NK cell function. In summary, the low-risk group likely harbors a more active and functionally competent NK cell–mediated immune microenvironment, whereas the high-risk group may display varying degrees of immune suppression and impaired NK cell function.

This study further investigated the relationship between risk scores, immune therapy responses, and drug sensitivity through immune infiltration analysis and drug sensitivity assessments. Patients in the high-risk group exhibited a diminished immune response, with tumor-infiltrating immune cells displaying characteristics indicative of immune evasion. Evaluation of the TIDE score, which predicts immune therapy responses, revealed that the high-risk group had significantly higher scores than the low-risk group. This suggests that patients in the high-risk group may be more susceptible to immune evasion, reducing their responsiveness to ICIs and diminishing the potential benefits of such therapies. In contrast, patients in the low-risk group exhibited lower TIDE scores, suggesting greater sensitivity to immune therapies. Immune infiltration analysis corroborated these findings, revealing distinct differences in the abundance and distribution of immune cell subsets between the high- and low-risk groups. Specifically, low-risk patients exhibited higher levels of immature B cells, CD8+ T cells, and immature CD4+ T cells, reflecting a more robust immune response. Conversely, high-risk patients demonstrated an increased presence of M1 and M2 macrophages, monocytes, activated dendritic cells, and activated NK cells, suggesting a more complex immune environment that may exhibit functional impairment. Drug sensitivity analysis further highlighted differences in the sensitivity of patients in different risk groups to various chemotherapy and targeted drugs. High-risk patients were more sensitive to Axitinib, whereas low-risk patients exhibited higher sensitivity to cisplatin, oxaliplatin, AZD5363, PAK_5339, and YK-4-279. These findings provide theoretical support for the personalized selection of therapeutic drugs in clinical practice, highlighting the importance of precision treatment plans based on individual risk assessments to enhance treatment efficacy, which is crucial for determining LUAD treatment strategies. Additionally, our study performed stratified analyses of clinical features (such as age, gender, and TNM staging), demonstrating that the DENKCRGs risk score independently predicts patient prognosis, irrespective of these traditional clinical features, and holds substantial clinical relevance. Nomogram analysis further facilitated personalized prognosis predictions for individual patients, helping clinicians more accurately evaluate survival probabilities and treatment responses, thereby improving decision-making in clinical practice.

This study underscores the critical role of NK cells in the prognosis and treatment outcomes of LUAD. By developing a prognostic risk model based on NKCRGs, distinct patient subgroups with varying immune environments and treatment sensitivities were identified, providing novel approaches for prognosis assessment and treatment strategy selection in LUAD. These findings not only facilitate the effective prediction of patient survival but also provide valuable insights into the role of NK cells in LUAD pathogenesis. Furthermore, this study establishes a theoretical foundation for personalized immune therapy and drug selection, with the ultimate aim of improving patient survival rates and quality of life.

There are several limitations in this study. First, although we validated the model using multiple datasets, further validation in larger clinical cohorts is required to confirm the generalizability and accuracy of this prognostic model. Second, although the study presents a prognostic model for LUAD based on NK cell-related genes, it lacks a comprehensive investigation of the specific mechanisms through which these genes function within the tumor microenvironment. Future research should aim to validate our findings in larger, more diverse patient populations and include experimental investigations to further elucidate the roles of these genes in the tumor immune microenvironment, while exploring their potential as therapeutic targets.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study underscores the critical role of NK cell-related genes in the prognosis and treatment of LUAD. By developing a prognostic risk model based on the expression of NKCRGs, we have successfully identified patient subgroups with distinct immune profiles and treatment sensitivities, thereby providing a framework for guiding personalized therapeutic strategies. The findings emphasize the importance of NK cell-mediated immune surveillance in LUAD and offer valuable insights into the potential integration of NK cell-related biomarkers into clinical decision-making. Moreover, our results suggest that NK cell-related gene expression significantly impacts immune therapy responses and drug sensitivities in LUAD patients, offering a promising avenue for precision medicine in LUAD treatment. While additional validation and mechanistic studies are required, this research establishes a foundation for future investigations into NK cell-targeted therapies and the optimization of treatment approaches to enhance patient survival and quality of life.
