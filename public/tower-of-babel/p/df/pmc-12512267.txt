Quantitative and spectroscopic assessment of early-stage enamel erosion induced by popular acidic beverages
Authors: Fajer AlHelal, Zakiya AlHomaizi, Maryam AlOmair, Lama W. Yousef, Muawia A. Qudeimat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate the initial effects of Coca-Cola, Red Bull, their sugar-free variants, and bottled orange juice on enamel surface roughness and mineral content.MethodsFifty-five sound human premolars were sectioned, mounted in resin, and polished to expose standardized enamel surfaces. After quality control, 75 samples (15 per group) were randomized and immersed in artificial saliva before submersion in acidic beverages (Coca-Cola, Red Bull, their sugar-free variants, and bottled orange juice) for 5 min. Surface roughness (Ra, Rq, Rz, Rv) was measured using optical profilometry, while Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis assessed mineral composition. The pH, titratable acidity, and buffering capacity of the beverages were also evaluated. Statistical analysis included paired t-tests, Wilcoxon signed-rank, Kruskal-Wallis, and ANOVA tests with p < 0.05 considered significant.ResultsCoca-Cola showed the lowest pH (2.36 ± 0.05) while bottled orange juice had the highest (3.68 ± 0.03). Red Bull exhibited the highest titratable acidity (TA) (10.93 ± 1.17 mL NaOH) and buffering capacity (β) (2.97 ± 0.36), followed by orange juice (TA = 10.00 ± 0.40 mL; β = 2.97 ± 0.05). All beverages significantly increased surface roughness parameters (Ra, Rq, Rz; p < 0.05) except Rv for Coca-Cola (∆Rv= -0.23 ± 0.50, p = 0.094) and Coca-Cola Light (∆Rv=-0.34 ± 0.78, p = 0.114). Red Bull caused the greatest roughness changes (∆Ra= -0.12 ± 0.07, p < 0.001). FTIR revealed that Coca-Cola significantly elevated the PO4 signal (ΔPO4 = − 9.996 ± 17.305, p = 0.050), while Coca-Cola Light (∆Amide I = + 9.88 ± 17.39, p = 0.045) and Sugar-Free Red Bull (∆Amide I = + 50.25 ± 46.14, p = 0.001) decreased Amide I FTIR signal. Red Bull altered both PO4 (∆PO4= -1.72 ± 2.15, p = 0.008) and Amide I signals (∆Amide I = + 26.21 ± 37.80, p = 0.018). Orange juice showed no significant changes in any parameter (p > 0.05). Kruskal-Wallis tests confirmed significant differences between-group in post-exposure Amide I, Peak PO4, mineral-to-matrix, and CO3/PO4 ratios (p < 0.05).ConclusionThis study demonstrates that all tested beverages increased enamel surface roughness within minutes, with energy drinks causing the most severe effects. While colas and energy drinks altered both surface and chemical properties, bottled orange juice affected only surface topography without detectable mineral changes. These distinct erosion patterns suggest that different beverages may require tailored preventive strategies.

Introduction

Erosive tooth wear (ETW) is a form of tooth wear caused by the dissolution of mineralized tooth structure due to exposure to non-cariogenic acids. These acids, classified as intrinsic (e.g., gastric acid from reflux or vomiting) or extrinsic (e.g., dietary sources), play a key role in ETW [1, 2]. While enamel demineralization in caries occurs below pH 5.5, erosion lacks a fixed critical pH. As Barbour & Lussi demonstrate, even beverages with pH >5 can be erosive if undersaturated with mineral ions, while calcium-rich acidic products (e.g., yogurt) may inhibit enamel dissolution despite low pH due to supersaturation effects [3].

Erosive demineralization initially leads to enamel softening, with early lesions exhibiting smooth, glossy surfaces above the cemento-enamel junction [1, 4]. If untreated, ETW progresses to irreversible enamel loss, altering tooth morphology, including flattened surfaces, cupping of cusps, and increased dentinal hypersensitivity. Advanced cases may involve aesthetic concerns, loss of vertical dimension, and pulp exposure [5]. The severity of erosion depends on factors such as acid type, pH, frequency of exposure, and personal habits such as oral hygiene and dietary patterns. Protective mechanisms like saliva composition and buffering capacity, as well as tooth anatomy, influence susceptibility to and severity of ETW [6].

Erosive tooth wear affects approximately 30% of the global population, with males and adolescents identified as high-risk groups [7]. This increased risk is linked to lifestyle factors, particularly the consumption of acidic beverages. Among these, fruit juices, soft drinks, and energy drinks are major contributors to ETW [8, 9]. Adolescents are a high risk group for ETW, as they represent the primary consumers of energy drinks, attracted by their perceived energy-enhancing effects [10]. Carbonated beverages further increase the risk, as they contain erosive carbonic, citric, phosphoric, and tartaric acids, which contribute significantly to enamel erosion [11, 12].

In response to health concerns, governments and industries have promoted low- or no-sugar alternatives. Although these changes address sugar-related issues, the acidic nature of these beverages remains a concern for enamel surface integrity [12]. Surveys indicate a decline in traditional soft drink consumption but an increase in reduced-sugar options, especially among adolescents [13].

While numerous studies have examined the impact of erosive beverages on enamel microhardness and surface roughness, critical chemical changes during early erosion—such as phosphate (PO4) and carbonate (CO3) depletion from hydroxyapatite dissolution, or protein matrix degradation (Amide I, representing C = O stretching vibrations in enamel proteins)— remain poorly characterized in human enamel. Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) is uniquely suited to detect these molecular alterations, as it identifies vibrational shifts in inorganic phosphate (1030–1100 cm−1) and carbonate (1410–1450 cm−1) bands, as well as organic matrix changes (Amide I, ~ 1600–1700 cm−1), which precede measurable surface damage [14, 15]. Prior studies relying solely on surface techniques (e.g., profilometry) or non-human samples [14] fail to capture these initial chemical changes that precede macroscopic erosion. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the initial demineralizing effects of popular acidic beverages on enamel using optical profilometry alongside FTIR to provide complementary chemical insights. The null hypothesis posits no significant differences in enamel surface roughness or chemical composition among enamel samples exposed to five commonly consumed acidic beverages.

Methods

Sample preparation

Ethical approval was received from the Health Science Center Ethical Committee, Kuwait University (reference number: 240). Informed consent was obtained from all participants to take part in this study. A total of 55 sound human premolars, freshly extracted for orthodontic purposes, were collected. After initial visual inspection excluded teeth with caries, restorations, cracks, hypomineralization, fluorosis, or other enamel defects, the remaining teeth were processed. Residual soft tissue was removed using hand scaling instruments. The roots were removed with an abrasive cut-off wheel (Struers, Accutom-10, Ballerup, Denmark), and the crowns were sectioned mesiodistally to create buccal and lingual samples. All samples were stored in a 0.1% thymol solution at 4 °C until processing. Each sample was then individually mounted in round molds using Epofix resin (Buehler, Lake Bluff, Illinois, USA), leaving the buccal or lingual enamel surface exposed.

Gritting and polishing procedures were performed to standardize the enamel surfaces using the Buehler Beta Grinder-Polisher Machine (Buehler, Lake Bluff, Illinois, USA). First, the resin base was flattened using 240-grit silicon carbide grinding paper. Exposed enamel surface of each sample was then sequentially gritted with 240-, 400-, 600-, and 1000-grit sandpaper. Polishing followed, using a polishing cloth and a 3 μm, then a 6 μm, polycrystalline diamond suspension (Buehler MetaDi Supreme, Buehler, Lake Bluff, Illinois, USA), and finally a colloidal silica suspension. After polishing, all samples were assessed under an optical microscope (Axio Imager M2m, Zeiss AG, Germany) to identify microcracks, previously undetected enamel abnormalities, or dentin exposure; this quality control process resulted in 75 qualified samples (15 per group) for experimental use.

The prepared samples were randomly assigned to five groups (n = 15) using computerized randomization (https://www.random.org/sequences/). Samples in groups 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 were assigned to KDD 100% Bottled Orange Juice (Kuwait Dairy Company, Kuwait), Coca-Cola (The Coca-Cola Company, Atlanta, Georgia, USA), Coca-Cola Light (The Coca-Cola Company, Atlanta, Georgia, USA), Red Bull (Red Bull GmbH, Fuschl am See, Austria), and Sugar-free Red Bull (Red Bull GmbH, Fuschl am See, Austria), respectively. Each sample was placed in 50 mL of laboratory-prepared artificial saliva and stored in an incubator at 37 °C for at least 24 h prior to acid submersion.

Pilot study and sample size calculation

An initial pilot study was conducted on sound molars to validate the study methodology. Results showed that submersion of prepared samples for 5 min resulted in sufficient roughening without surface destruction. The sample size was determined based on Rz values reported by Barac et al. [16] after 30-minute exposures (Coca-Cola: 5.62 ± 0.01 μm; orange juice: 5.37 ± 0.15 μm), we calculated a Cohen’s d of 2.36. While this large effect size would theoretically require only n = 4 per group for 90% power (α = 0.05, two-tailed t-test), we selected n = 15 per group for our five-group study (total N = 75) to accommodate potential smaller effects in other beverage comparisons, account for multi-group ANOVA requirements, and align with established methodologies in erosive tooth wear research [16].

Chemical parameters

For each beverage, measurements of pH were taken to determine whether the solution was acidic, neutral, or alkaline [17]. Titratable acidity (TA), defined as the amount of base required to raise the pH of a solution to 7.0, was also measured [18]. The buffering capacity (β) indicates a solution’s ability to resist changes in pH when strong acids or bases are added. A higher β value reflects greater resistance to pH changes [19]. The buffering capacity (β) was calculated by dividing the total amount of base required to raise the initial pH to 7.0 (ΔC) by the change in the solution’s pH (ΔpH) [18].

To ensure the accuracy of pH readings, the pH meter (WR Symphony, VWR International LLC, Pennsylvania, USA) was calibrated using standardized solutions with pH values of 7.00 and 4.01 (Orion Application Solution, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Massachusetts, USA). For titratable acidity (TA), micropipettes (Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany) were used to dispense 0.2 mL increments of 0.5 M NaOH into each beverage until a pH of 7.0 was reached. The buffering capacity of each drink was then calculated using the initial pH and the change in pH [18]. Each parameter was measured three times, and the mean value was calculated.

Baseline measurements

Baseline measurements included optical profilometry (Leica DCM8, GmbH, Wetzlar, Germany) and Fourier Transform Infrared Spectroscopy (FTIR) analysis using the Bruker Tensor 27 spectrometer (Bruker Corporation, Massachusetts, USA). To ensure measurement consistency across techniques, FTIR analysis was performed first to establish reference points. Each sample received two orientation marks: (1) a permanent ink dot on the resin base marking the exact FTIR measurement location, and (2) colored nail varnish on the side for spatial alignment. Optical profilometry was then performed using these reference marks to guarantee identical region analysis pre- and post-exposure. All specimens were measured in a maintained numerical sequence (1–15 per experimental group) for both baseline characterization and post-treatment evaluations.

Optical profiler images were acquired at 10x magnification. Each sample was measured three times, and results were averaged to provide a mean value for each parameter. Amplitude parameters included Ra (average arithmetic height), Rq (root mean square deviation of the roughness profile), Rz (maximum height), and Rv (maximum depth of the valleys). Ra reflects the average surface roughness and indicates general changes in texture due to enamel erosion. Rq provides a more sensitive measure by emphasizing larger deviations, capturing both minor and severe surface changes. Rz represents the average difference between the five highest peaks and five deepest valleys, highlighting localized enamel loss. Rv denotes the maximum depth of the valleys and is particularly useful for assessing the extent of subsurface demineralization or erosive penetration [20].

FTIR absorbance spectra were acquired by scanning the specimens in the 600–4,000 cm−1 range with a resolution of 4 cm−1. FTIR measurements included the amide I, PO4, and CO3 bands. The integrated absorbance values (peak areas) for each spectral region were calculated using Omnic software (Thermo Scientific Nicolet™ iS™ 5, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). The mineral-to-matrix ratio (M/M ratio) was determined by dividing the PO4 band area by the amide I band area, while the carbonate-to-phosphate ratio (CO3/PO4) was calculated by dividing the CO3 band area by the PO4 band area [21].

Intervention and experimental measurements

To ensure standardization, all beverages were stored at 4 °C. Samples were incubated in artificial saliva in an incubator at 37 °C for at least 24 h before the intervention. Baseline and experimental measurements were taken on the same day, and samples were not stored in artificial saliva after the acidic attack to prevent potential re-mineralization. Each sample was immersed in 50 mL of the beverage for 5 min, rinsed with artificial saliva for approximately 10 s, and then dried. Optical profilometry and FTIR experimental results were collected. Carbonated beverages were degassed by stirring before the intervention. Degassing prevented bubble adhesion during immersion that could create uneven acid exposure patterns, while ensuring standardized erosion conditions despite modifying the beverage’s native carbonation state. Both optical profilometry and FTIR measurements were taken at the same positions as the baseline measurements.

Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using the Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) (Version 27, IBM, Armonk, New York, USA). Normality was assessed using Q-Q plots and the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Based on these assessments, data consistent with a normal distribution were analyzed using parametric tests, while data not consistent with a normal distribution were analyzed using non-parametric equivalents. Specifically, most data from the optical profilometry were normally distributed; therefore, a paired t-test was used for within-group pre-post comparisons and Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) was used for between-group comparisons. Conversely, most FTIR results were not normally distributed, so the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used for within-group comparisons and the Kruskal-Wallis test was used for between-group comparisons. For significant Kruskal-Wallis results, Dunn’s post-hoc test with Bonferroni correction was applied to identify specific pairwise differences between beverage groups. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. In addition, effect sizes (Cohen’s d) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated to quantify the magnitude of differences between baseline measurements and post-exposure values for both optical profilometry and FTIR parameters.

Results

Chemical parameters

The pH, titratable acidity (TA), and buffering capacity (β) of each beverage were recorded and calculated (Table 1). The lowest pH was observed in Coca-Cola, with a pH value of 2.36 ± 0.05. In contrast, Orange Juice was the least acidic, with a pH of 3.68 ± 0.03. The highest TA value was recorded for Red Bull (10.93 ± 1.17 mL), followed closely by Orange Juice (10.00 ± 0.40 mL). Both beverages also exhibited the highest β values at 2.97. Coca-Cola had the lowest β value, calculated to be 1.12 ± 0.06. Interestingly, sugar-free beverages (Coca-Cola Light and Sugar-Free Red Bull) were less acidic than their sugar-containing counterparts (Coca-Cola and Red Bull).

FTIR

FTIR analysis (Table 4) revealed distinct beverage-induced alterations in enamel’s mineral and organic components. The significant negative ∆PO4 values for Coca-Cola (-9.996, p = 0.050) and Red Bull (-1.72, p = 0.008) indicate increased phosphate signal intensity. Concurrently, the significant positive ΔAmide I values for Coca-Cola Light (+ 9.88, p = 0.045), Red Bull (+ 26.21, p = 0.018) and Sugar-Free Red Bull (+ 50.254, p = 0.001) demonstrate progressive organic matrix exposure, suggesting that these beverages selectively degrade the mineral phase to reveal underlying collagenous components. The Amide I increase in sugar-free variants suggests particularly aggressive removal of surface mineral, consistent with their high buffering capacity. Bottled orange juice showed no significant enamel changes (p > 0.05). The Kruskal-Wallis test (Table 5) showed significant between-group differences in post-exposure peak PO4 (χ² =11.28, p = 0.024), peak Amide I (χ² =16.45, p = 0.002), M/M ratio (χ² =13.87, p = 0.008), and CO3/PO4 ratio (χ² =18.94, p < 0.001), confirming that beverages differentially affected enamel’s organic and mineral components. Dunn’s post-hoc tests revealed that sugar-free beverages, particularly Sugar-Free Red Bull, were consistently among the most aggressive, often showing significantly greater erosion than their regular counterparts or orange juice.

Discussion

Enamel is susceptible to structural changes and physical alterations when exposed to acidic beverages, leading to softening and tissue loss [9]. In this study, optical profilometry was used to assess the surface roughness of enamel following exposure to erosive beverages. Our findings indicated that all the beverages tested led to a statistically significant increase in the amplitude parameters of surface roughness. FTIR spectroscopy revealed mixed results regarding the effect of these beverages on the enamel’s chemical composition. Accordingly, the null hypothesis was rejected due to statistically significant changes in surface roughness, and FTIR data revealed beverage-specific differences in enamel chemical profiles.

The observed increase in enamel surface roughness aligns with previous studies. For example, Al-Amri et al. demonstrated a significant increase in surface roughness after 15 days of acidic exposure from soft drinks, compared to control groups [22]. Similarly, Trivedi et al. reported significant differences in enamel surface roughness after exposure to carbonated beverages and high-energy sports drinks for a 14-day period [23]. Silva et al. also noted increased surface roughness and lesion depth following a 30-minute exposure to carbonated and energy drinks [10]. Our results were consistent with these studies, showing similar findings after a single exposure to Coca-Cola and Red Bull. Barac et al. also found significant increases in surface roughness after exposing enamel to soft drinks and orange juice, particularly after 15 min of exposure to Coca-Cola [16]. However, no significant difference was observed between Coca-Cola and orange juice after 30 and 60 min of exposure. It is important to emphasize that our study design differs substantially from those involving longer-term or repeated exposures. In prior work, authors assessed extended acid contact times that led to bulk mineral loss and macroscopic erosion [10, 16, 22, 23]. In contrast, our study focused on a single 5-minute exposure, which primarily reflects the initial softening stage of enamel rather than irreversible tissue loss. This early phase of erosion is characterized by proton diffusion into the enamel surface, leading to partial demineralization without collapse of the enamel structure [4]. It is dominated by ion exchange at the outermost layers, particularly affecting phosphate and carbonate ions, before significant calcium release occurs [4]. These distinctions help explain why our findings may differ from those in the literature using longer experimental durations.

The erosive potential of a beverage is influenced by several chemical parameters, particularly pH and TA [24]. In this study, both pH and TA were measured for each beverage, and buffering capacities were also calculated. When acidic substances contact enamel, the acid diffuses through the acquired enamel pellicle (AEP), a biofilm composed of proteins, enzymes, lipids, and carbohydrates. The hydrogen ions (H+) from the acid dissolve enamel crystals, leading to surface demineralization and loss of enamel hardness [4, 9]. Interestingly, the pH of a beverage in this study did not always correlate with its TA or buffering capacity. For instance, Coca-Cola, despite having the lowest pH, required the least amount of NaOH to neutralize (TA = 5.27 mL NaOH). On the other hand, orange juice and Red Bull had the same buffering capacity (β = 2.97), even though orange juice had a higher pH. This discrepancy is likely explained by the lower TA in orange juice (10.00 mL NaOH) compared to Red Bull (10.93 mL NaOH). These findings align with Jensdottir et al. who noted that extended exposure (24 h) to soft drinks can correlate erosion with both pH and TA [25]. However, after short exposures (3 min), they found that pH was a more significant indicator of erosion than titratable acidity [18, 25]. In contrast, Hara et al. argued that TA only showed a low-to-moderate correlation with enamel demineralization, suggesting pH as the more reliable predictor of erosive damage [26]. In addition, it has previously been concluded that during early-stage erosion, pH and acid type exert a stronger influence on surface ion loss than TA alone [4]. Weak acids such as citric and phosphoric acid, even at low concentrations, have been shown to remove phosphate and carbonate groups from the enamel surface by direct dissolution and chelation [4]. In contrast, longer exposures and higher pH values tend to favor calcium loss and deeper structural changes. Our findings, based on short exposure, reflect these initial interactions—rather than later-stage erosion where mineral loss is more extensive and irreversible.

Although the surface roughness changes observed after a single 5-minute exposure in our study ranged between 0.06 and 0.12 μm, these values represent early-stage alterations in enamel topography. In a clinical context, individuals may experience repeated exposures throughout the day, often with variable durations and oral clearance rates. The cumulative effect of these episodes, compounded by mechanical forces such as brushing, could result in progressive and clinically relevant tissue loss over time. Thus, even seemingly minor surface changes can serve as an early marker of erosive risk under repeated acid challenge [1, 27].

In addition to the morphological surface changes observed, FTIR analysis was used to assess potential alterations in the chemical composition of the enamel structure. FTIR provides insights into mineral and matrix composition changes after exposure to acidic environments [14, 21]. In our study, FTIR was used to evaluate the chemical changes in enamel caused by five acidic beverages, with measurements taken before and after exposure.

The FTIR data reveal that Coca-Cola induces a statistically significant increase in the phosphate (PO4) peak (ΔPO4 = − 9.996, p = 0.050), indicating augmented phosphate signal post-exposure, most likely due to selective superficial demineralization that uncovers deeper hydroxyapatite-rich enamel strata. This inference is bolstered by the absence of significant changes in carbonate (CO3) and CO3/PO4 ratios, which suggests that the phosphate elevation is not driven by carbonate loss but rather reflects depletion of external mineral layers [28, 29]. In contrast, both Red Bull and sugar-free Red Bull elicited marked Amide I peak increases (ΔAmide I = + 26.21 and + 50.25, respectively; p < 0.05), signifying profound organic matrix exposure consequent to extensive mineral dissolution. However, it has been noted that Amide I peaks in FTIR can also reflect the presence of exogenous organics from beverage constituents—such as flavor additives or proteins—rather than true exposure of endogenous enamel matrix [4]. These findings align with previous studies demonstrating that energy drinks, owing to their high titratable acidity and chelating agents (e.g., citric acid), exert greater erosive effects on enamel than cola beverages [30].

The intermediate increase in Amide I for Coca‑Cola Light (ΔAmide I = + 9.877, p = 0.045) indicates moderate erosive potential without overt phosphate changes, consistent with beverages exhibiting lower buffering capacities. Bottled orange juice produced no statistically significant alterations in PO4, Amide I, or M/M ratios, suggesting that industrial processing—possibly including calcium fortification and thermal denaturation of native enzymes—mitigates its erosive capacity relative to fresh variants [25, 31]. Contrary to this finding, a previous study found no difference in erosive potential between processed and fresh orange juice on human enamel. Both types caused significant enamel erosion under experimental conditions [32]. Lastly, the Kruskal–Wallis analysis confirms significant variation among groups in peak PO4, Amide I, M/M ratio, and CO3/PO4 outcomes (p < 0.05), underscoring differential effects on enamel organic–inorganic balance. Together, these findings support a model in which beverage-induced enamel changes are governed by a combination of pH, buffering capacity, acid type, and organic content, particularly during the early softening phase [4, 33].

The chemical and structural alterations observed in this study highlight the need for beverage-specific preventive strategies. Coca-Cola, with the lowest pH (2.36) and buffering capacity (β = 1.12), induced notable phosphate signal changes (ΔPO4= − 9.996, p = 0.050), suggesting surface demineralization that could benefit from immediate neutralization via rinsing with water and surface-remineralizing agents such as CPP-ACP-containing varnishes [34, 35]. Red Bull and Sugar-Free Red Bull, both exhibiting high titratable acidity (TA = 10.93 mL) and buffering capacity (β = 2.97), caused significant Amide I increases (p < 0.05), indicating deep mineral loss and organic matrix exposure. For such beverages, prolonged buffering using calcium-rich chewing gums or stannous fluoride rinses may offer enhanced protection [34, 36]. Sugar-Free Red Bull showed the greatest organic matrix alteration (ΔAmide I = + 50.25, p = 0.001), highlighting the potential need for adjunctive therapies targeting organic component preservation—though further research is warranted. Bottled orange juice caused no statistically significant enamel changes after a single 5-minute exposure, though this does not preclude its erosive potential under repeated or prolonged conditions.

A key limitation of this in vitro study is its inherent simplification of the oral environment. Clinically, enamel erosion is modulated by numerous protective factors including: (1) salivary clearance and buffering capacity, (2) the diffusion-limiting properties of the acquired pellicle (containing mucins, statherin, and proline-rich proteins), (3) plaque-derived mineralization, (4) salivary calcium-phosphate saturation enabling remineralization, (5) individual variations in drinking patterns (e.g., swishing, retention time), and other factors [37, 38]. The static 5-minute immersion model of this study provides controlled conditions for comparative analysis but differs from clinical reality in two aspects: (1) it excludes salivary clearance and pellicle protection, and (2) it uses continuous exposure rather than intermittent sipping. However, this approach is justified for quantifying intrinsic beverage erosivity, as it aligns with established protocols detecting early tissue loss [14, 39]. Despite these limitations, this study provides valid comparative data on beverage erosivity, though absolute values may differ from clinical reality. Future studies should incorporate dynamic flow systems and natural saliva to better model oral conditions.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, Coca-Cola, Red Bull, their sugar-free variants, and bottled orange juice significantly increased enamel surface roughness (Ra, Rq, Rz, Rv; p < 0.05) after just 5 min of exposure, with Red Bull showing the most pronounced effects (ΔRa= -0.12 ± 0.07). FTIR spectroscopy revealed beverage-specific chemical alterations: Coca-Cola preferentially increased PO4 signal intensity (ΔPO4= -9.996, p = 0.050), suggesting superficial demineralization, while Red Bull and its sugar-free variant caused marked Amide I elevation (ΔAmide I = + 26.21 to + 50.25, p < 0.05), indicating organic matrix exposure. Notably, bottled orange juice induced no significant FTIR changes, likely due to industrial processing. These findings highlight that erosive potential depends not only on pH but also on buffering capacity and acid type, with energy drinks posing unique risks due to their high titratable acidity. Clinically, this underscores the importance of educating patients about the potential risks of frequent acidic beverage consumption. General preventive advice—such as limiting exposure time, using straws, rinsing with water afterward, and maintaining regular dental visits—can help reduce enamel erosion. Future research should aim to confirm these findings under real-life conditions and support the development of targeted preventive strategies.
