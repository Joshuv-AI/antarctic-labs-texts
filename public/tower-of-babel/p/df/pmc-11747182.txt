Home death as a negative indicator of end-of-life care in Brazil: author's response to Hong et al.
Authors: Jessé Lopes da Silva, Lívia Costa de Oliveira, Lucas Zanetti de Albuquerque, Luiz Claudio Santos Thuler, Andréia Cristina de Melo
Published: 2024

Our investigation into population-based databases has sparked a timely discussion on the quality of end-of-life cancer care in Brazil, as detailed in the article “Exploring Factors and Trends in Place of Death by Cancer: A Population-Based Study in Brazil”1 published in The Lancet Regional Health—Americas. The availability of public datasets has enabled a comprehensive analysis of this critical issue. In response to points raised by Hong et al.,2 we clarify that the databases in Brazil are de-identified and anonymised, and there is no integrated national electronic health records system for detailed patient information. Although obtaining patient-specific data from the 27 Brazilian states is theoretically possible, it was not the objective of our study.

Our study employed a cross-sectional epidemiological design with nationwide data. The influence of sociocultural factors and patient preferences on the place of death is acknowledged, but individualised patient interviews across Brazil are deemed beyond the scope due to time and resource constraints. Big data in oncology is essential for improving public visibility of cancer data and exploring key areas in the field.

Furthermore, using multi-stage sampling methods and incorporating additional survey data would significantly raise both the complexity and cost of the research, potentially making the project unfeasible. Given the potential for data imputation to reduce bias into estimates and the assumption required that missing data occurs at random, this study chose to analyse only complete cases (listwise deletion).3

Additionally, the absence of a national home-based palliative care program poses significant barriers to accessing appropriate medical care for patients with end-stage cancer. This situation has been further worsened by the COVID-19 pandemic, which has redirected hospital resources to COVID-19 patients, consequently reducing the number of available beds for those with terminal cancer.4

A recent implementation by the Brazilian Ministry of Health was to integrate the National Palliative Care Policy into the Health Care Network and include it as a component of Primary Health Care. With the provision of creating multidisciplinary teams to disseminate standard practices, promoting qualified information and education, and ensuring access, we hope that its implementation will contribute to improving the current national scenario.5

In conclusion, effectively leveraging the potential of big data is vital for addressing the national challenges associated with end-of-life cancer care. By transforming this data into actionable insights, we can create a strong foundation for national policies to improve palliative care and address Brazil's pressing healthcare concerns.
