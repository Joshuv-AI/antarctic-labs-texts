Polychlorinated dibenzo-p-dioxins/dibenzofurans (PCDD/Fs) concentrations in the environment and blood of residents living near industrial waste incinerators in South Korea
Authors: Mira Yoon, JunYoung Chang, Sang-Yong Eom, Young-Seoub Hong, SueJin Kim, Boeun Lee, Yong-Dae Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesIndustrial waste incinerators (IWIs) are the main sources of polychlorinated dibenzo-p-dioxins/dibenzofurans (PCDD/Fs), a group of persistent organic pollutants that include known carcinogens. However, data on PCDD/Fs exposure levels in the environment and among residents living near these facilities are insufficient. We aimed to identify PCDD/Fs exposure concentrations in the environment and blood of residents living near IWIs and examine factors affecting PCDD/Fs exposure.MethodsPCDD/Fs concentrations were systematically measured at the outlets of IWIs, as well as in the atmosphere, soil, and blood.ResultsPCDD/Fs concentrations at the IWIs outlets (0.00108–0.36039 ng I-TEQ/ Sm3) were below the emission standards established by the Persistent Organic Pollutants Control Act in Korea. The average PCDD/Fs concentration in the atmosphere of the exposure area (0.0242 pg I-TEQ/Sm3) was significantly higher (p < 0.05) than that in the control area (0.0073 pg I-TEQ/Sm3). The blood PCDD/Fs concentrations in residents from the exposure area (3.13 pg WHO-TEQ/g lipids) were higher than those from the control area (2.74 pg WHO-TEQ/g lipids). However, this difference was not statistically significant. In the exposure area, blood PCDD/Fs concentrations were significantly associated with recent meat consumption (p < 0.05) and showed a borderline increase with longer residence duration (p = 0.07).ConclusionThis study confirmed that the current concentrations of PCDD/Fs in the environment and the blood of residents living near IWIs were not high. However, given the potential health impacts of PCDD/Fs, even at low concentrations, sustained efforts to mitigate exposure are essential through continuous environmental monitoring and effective source control.

Introduction

Waste generation is an unavoidable consequence of daily life and industrial activities. In 2021, Korea produced 197.4 million tons of waste, representing an increase of approximately 140% compared with that in 2000, with waste volumes continuing to rise (Ministry of Environment 2000, 2021a). Implementing effective waste treatment processes is critical to safeguarding public health and preserving the environment. Currently, 87% of waste is recycled, while 5.3% is disposed of in landfills and 5.0% is incinerated, with landfill and incineration rates remaining relatively comparable (Ministry of Environment 2022). Due to limited land availability for new sites and nearly saturated domestic landfill capacity, unlike in the United States and China, Korea relies heavily on recycling and incineration for waste management (Korea Environment Institute 2019).

Despite the necessity of waste incinerators, securing new sites for these facilities is challenging due to perceived environmental and health risks. Furthermore, due to stricter environmental regulations in the Seoul Metropolitan Area (SMA), following the Special Act on the Improvement of Air Quality in the Seoul Metropolitan Area (Act No. 7041, enacted on December 31, 2003), many waste incinerators contracts were established in smaller provincial cities. In particular, Cheongju City has a high density of such facilities due to its proximity to the SMA and convenient transportation infrastructure, facilitating waste transport.

Waste incinerators in South Korea are classified into public, consigned, and on-site treatment facilities based on the responsible managing entity. Public treatment facilities are managed by state or local governments, while approximately 83% of waste treatment facilities are consigned to private companies licensed to handle the waste. As of 2021, 64 consigned waste incinerators operated in Korea, with a combined throughput of 2,285,029 t/year. Three of these consigned IWIs, two located in Bugi-myeon, Cheongju, and one near the Bugi-myeon border, collectively incinerate 179,291 t of waste annually, which is approximately 8% of the total throughput of the nation (Ministry of Environment 2021a). These IWIs in Bugi-myeon are centrally located within a distance of only 3–5 km. Consequently, the entire Bugi-myeon area is substantially influenced by increased incineration activities, leading to increased emissions from these facilities compared with those in the other areas. Therefore, further planned expansion of these facilities is challenging, as it elicits strong opposition from local residents, who, in 2019, submitted a petition requesting a survey on environmental pollution and the potential health effects caused by emissions from these IWIs.

IWIs are the main sources of polychlorinated dibenzo-p-dioxins/dibenzofurans (PCDD/Fs), a group of persistent organic pollutants that include known carcinogens. In particular, 2,3,7,8-tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD), 2,3,4,7,8-Pentachloro dibenzofuran among the 17 toxic congeners of PCDD/Fs, is classified as a Group 1 carcinogen “known human carcinogen” by the International Agency for Research on Cancer of the World Health Organization (Themba et al. 2023; IARC 2012). Previous studies have reported an increased risk of non-Hodgkin lymphoma and soft tissue cancers associated with PCDD/Fs exposure in residents living near incineration facilities (Floret et al. 2003; Zambon et al. 2007). In addition, analysis of PCDD/Fs concentrations in environmental media, such as air, soil, and the blood of residents living near incineration facilities in other countries, including Taiwan, Japan, and Italy, revealed that the concentrations vary from 20–40 pg I-TEQ/g lipid, depending on the size of the incineration facility, its proximity, and the study population (Felip et al. 2008; Fierens et al. 2007; Chen et al. 2006; Fréry et al. 2007). In South Korea, dioxin emissions were detected in municipal waste incinerators in 1987 (Chae et al. 2020). Although national efforts and stricter regulations to assess dioxin emissions from these facilities and monitoring pollution levels in environmental media and food products over the past decades have successfully reduced the dioxin concentrations in the atmosphere, soil, and water, residents living near IWIs remain concerned about potential health effects. Moreover, conflicts between local governments and residents over the operation of IWIs have emerged not only in Bugi-myeon but also in other regions.

Data on dioxin exposure levels in the environment and among residents living near these facilities are insufficient. Therefore, this study aimed to quantify PCDD/Fs exposure levels in the environment and blood of residents living near IWIs and identify the factors affecting PCDD/Fs exposure.

Materials and methods

Study area

The present study was carried out in the Bugi-myeon area in Cheongju City, Chungcheongbuk-do, South Korea. The study area is bordered by rivers to the north, west, and south, with mountainous terrain to the east. The area benefits from convenient transportation, with a national highway running vertically through its center. The study area covers 47.46 km2 and had a population of approximately 4,500 in 2022. Two IWIs (A and B) located within Bugi-myeon and a third IWI (C) situated in Ochang-eup, which borders Bugi-myeon to the northwest (Fig. 1), were included in this study. IWI A, operational since 2001, incinerates 352.3 t of industrial waste (workplace general waste) daily. IWI B, in operation since 1999, processes 99.8 tons of industrial waste (workplace general waste) daily. IWI C, operating since 2010, handles 90 t of industrial waste (workplace general waste and regulated waste, including medical waste) per day.Fig. 1Location of the IWIs A, B, and C, exposure area, and control area in Bugi-myeon area, Cheongju City, Chungcheongbuk-do, South Korea

This study utilized CALPUFF modeling to assess the areas affected by pollutants emitted from the three IWIs (six outlet stacks). The modeling period spanned from January 1 to December 31, 2018. Meteorological data were sourced from the ASOS (Cheongju Meteorological Observatory) and several AWS stations (Uamsan, Jincheon, Sejongyeonseo, Eumseong, Jeungpyeong, Cheongwon, and Ochang) operated by the Korea Meteorological Administration. Additional data included topographic details and specifications for the incinerator chimneys, such as height, top diameter, exhaust gas temperature, and emission rate. Measurement points were strategically selected in 5 of the 51 villages in Bugi-myeon anticipated to be directly affected by IWIs, considering other emission sources, such as livestock housing, roads, and wood stoves. Miwon-myeon, a typical agricultural area located approximately 16 km southeast of Bugi-myeon in Cheongju, was selected as the control area for environmental measurements due to the absence of pollution sources such as IWIs.

Study subjects

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Chungbuk National University (approval no. CBNU-202012-HRBR-0207), and prior consent was obtained from all study participants. Volunteers from Bugi-myeon, aged 20 years and older, were selected as study participants. Two additional control areas with similar gender, age demographics, and lifestyles to those of Bugi-myeon were chosen for the health survey: Miwon-myeon, which also served as the control area for the environmental survey, and Iwol-myeon, located approximately 23 km northwest of Bugi-myeon. Iwol-myeon has agricultural industrial complexes but no IWIs.

A total of 1,456 residents participated in the health survey and questionnaire, with 1,224 from the exposure area and 232 from the control area. Of them, 212 individuals (168 from the exposure area and 44 from the control area) volunteered for blood PCDD/Fs analysis. Pregnant women and the very elderly were excluded, considering the required blood volume.

Health survey

The health survey included a questionnaire, physical measurements (e.g., height, weight and blood pressure), blood and urine analyses and respiratory tests, and was performed in both the exposure and control groups. The questionnaire was developed through a review of previous studies on PCDD/Fs exposure. There were 43 main questions concerning the general characteristics of the participants (including sex, age, duration of residence and living environment), lifestyle habits (including smoking and alcohol consumption), dietary habits (consumption meat within 3 days, seafood within 7 days, locally grown food such as rice, vegetables, fruit, meat), disease history, occupational history.

From July to August 2020, trained surveyors conducted one-on-one interviews to gather information. To prevent COVID-19 infection, all researchers and participants wore masks, and the survey was conducted in compliance with quarantine measures, including fever checks, handwashing, and maintaining a distance of over 2 m.

PCDD/Fs concentrations in the environment

The concentrations of PCDD/Fs in the stack gas emitted, atmosphere, and soil were measured in the exposed and control areas to assess environmental levels. PCDD/Fs stack emissions were measured once during the summer of 2020 at all six outlets of the three IWIs, including three, two, and one outlet at facilities A, B, and C, respectively. Atmospheric PCDD/Fs concentrations were measured over 3 consecutive days during each season (spring, summer, and autumn) in 2020 at five sites in the exposure area and one in the control area. Soil PCDD/Fs concentrations were measured once during the summer at seven sites, with two additional sites added to the atmospheric measurement locations. Exhaust gas samples were collected using a stack sampler, while atmospheric PCDD/Fs were sampled with a high-volume air sampler (HV-RW, Shibata Scientific Technology Ltd., Japan). For soil sampling, a five-point mixing method was used to collect samples with a diameter greater than 5 cm and a depth of 5 cm from one central point and four surrounding points, each located 5–10 m from the center in different directions. A total of approximately 150 g of sample collected. The samples were then analyzed using high-resolution gas chromatography–high-resolution mass (HRGC–HRMS, JMS-800D, JEOL Ltd, Tokyo, Japan), following the Environmental Standard Test Method in Korea (NIER 2020).

The analysis focused on 17 congeners of PCDD/Fs isomers with toxic equivalency factors (TEFs): 2,3,7,8-TeCDD; 1,2,3,7,8-PeCDD; 1,2,3,4,7,8-HxCDD; 1,2,3,6,7,8-HxCDD; 1,2,3,7,8,9-HxCDD; 1,2,3,4,6,7,8-HpCDD; OCDD; 2,3,7,8-TeCDF; 1,2,3,7,8-PeCDF; 2,3,4,7,8-PeCDF; 1,2,3,4,7,8-HxCDF; 1,2,3,6,7,8-HxCDF; 2,3,4,6,7,8-HxCDF; 1,2,3,7,8,9-HxCDF; 1,2,3,4,6,7,8-HpCDF; 1,2,3,4,7,8,9-HpCDF; and OCDF. The TEF values for each isomer were multiplied by their respective concentrations, and the results are presented as TEQ values. Concentrations were reported using both the World Health Organization TEF (WHO-TEF 2005) and the international TEF (I-TEF, 1994), as stipulated by the Persistent Pollutants Act in Korea (Kutz et al. 1990; EPA 2010; Berg et al. 2006).

PCDD/Fs concentrations in blood

Blood samples were collected from voluntary participants. About 50 ml blood were withdrawn from each participant and centrifuged to obtain serum. The specimens were transported by refrigeration them at 4 °C and stored at –70 °C until analysis. Blood PCDD/Fs analysis in serum (15 g) was performed using the same equipment for the same items at the environmental sample analysis. Blood lipid levels were adjusted based on total cholesterol and triglyceride values, with results expressed as WHO-TEQ, following the WHO-TEF (2005) guidelines.

Statistical analysis

Categorical demographic and lifestyle variables, such as gender, smoking habits, and alcohol consumption, were compared between the exposure and control group using the chi-square test. Continuous variables, including age, residence duration, and blood PCDD/Fs concentrations, were analyzed using the Student’s t-test or analysis of variance. PCDD/Fs concentrations in the atmosphere of the exposure and control areas were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test, a nonparametric statistical method. For the blood PCDD/Fs concentrations, data below the detection limit were replaced with half of the detection limit value. As the data were not normally distributed, a natural log transformation was applied before analysis.

Multiple regression analysis was conducted to identify factors influencing blood PCDD/Fs concentrations. Variables such as gender, age, diet, body mass index (BMI), smoking, and drinking—factors previously shown to affect blood PCDD/Fs concentrations (including organic chlorinated pesticides)—were adjusted using covariates (Devine et al. 1990; Ibarluzea et al. 2011). Additionally, residence duration was included as a continuous variable to further assess its impact on PCDD/Fs concentrations. Statistical significance was defined as a p-value of less than 0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

The general characteristics of the participants are presented in Table 1. In the exposure group, 90 participants were male (53.6%) and 78 were female (46.4%), while the control group consisted of 29 males (65.9%) and 15 females (34.1%). The mean ages were 61 and 66 years for the exposure and control groups, respectively. Participants aged 60 and older represented 62.5% (105 individuals) of the exposure group and 79.5% (35 individuals) of the control group, indicating significant differences in both mean age and age distribution between the two groups (p < 0.05). Regarding the residence period, 16.1% (27 individuals) of the exposure group and 29.5% (13 individuals) of the control group had lived in the area for less than 20 years. Conversely, 83.9% (141 individuals) and 70.5% (31 individuals) of the exposure and control groups had resided in their respective areas for 20 years or more, reflecting a significant difference in residence period between the two groups (p < 0.05). Smoking status also differed significantly between the exposure and control groups, with 61.9% (104 individuals) non-smokers and 37.5% (63 individuals) smokers in the exposure group compared with 43.2% (19 individuals) non-smokers and 56.8% (25 individuals) smokers in the control group (p < 0.05). However, the proportion of non-drinkers was similar in both groups, with 51.2% (86 individuals) in the exposure group and 45.5% (20 individuals) in the control group being non-drinkers. Three participants (1.8%) in the exposure group and none in the control group had BMIs < 18.5, while 71 (42.3%) and 18 (40.9%) participants had BMIs of 18.5 to < 25, respectively. Additionally, 94 participants (56.0%) in the exposure group and 26 (59.1%) in the control group had BMIs ≥ 25. Regarding diet, the proportion of participants who ate meat within the past 3 days was similar between the two groups, with 81 participants (48.2%) and 21 participants (47.7%) in the exposure and control groups, respectively. Comparable PCDD/Fs concentrations were detected in the control area (Point 1) soil, with an average of 0.4556 pg I-TEQ/g (0.2849 pg WHO-TEQ/g).

PCDD/Fs levels in the blood of residents of the exposure and control areas (pg WHO-TEQ/g lipid)

Within the exposure group, PCDD/Fs concentrations were similar between males (3.10 WHO-TEQ/g lipid) and females (3.17 WHO-TEQ/g lipid). The control group showed a similar pattern, with mean levels of 2.86 and 2.52 WHO-TEQ/g lipid in males and females, respectively. Comparison of PCDD/Fs concentrations by age revealed similar results in the exposure group, with average levels of 3.14 WHO-TEQ/g lipid for individuals aged under 60 years and 3.12 WHO-TEQ/g lipid for those aged 60 years and above. The control group also showed comparable levels across age groups. In the exposure area, the average PCDD/Fs concentration was 2.63 WHO-TEQ/g lipid for residents who had lived in the area for less than 20 years and 3.23 WHO-TEQ/g lipid for those with a residence period of 20 years or more (p = 0.070). In the control group, although PCDD/Fs concentrations were higher among residents with a residence period of 20 years or more than those with a residence period of less than 20 years, the difference was not significant (p = 0.256). Furthermore, in the control group, PCDD/Fs concentrations were higher in smokers and drinkers, with a significant difference observed based on drinking habits (p < 0.05). No significant difference in PCDD/Fs concentrations according to BMI was observed in either the exposure or control group (p = 0.199 and p = 0.488, respectively). Concerning diet, the exposure group showed a significant difference (p < 0.05), with higher blood PCDD/Fs concentrations in individuals who consumed meat within the past 3 days (3.47 WHO-TEQ/g lipid) than in those who did not (2.84 WHO-TEQ/g lipid). In the control group, PCDD/Fs concentrations were similar regardless of meat intake. In the exposure group, PCDD/Fs concentrations were markedly higher in individuals who consumed locally grown rice (3.18 WHO-TEQ/g lipid) than in those who consumed purchased rice (2.98 WHO-TEQ/g lipid); however, this difference was not statistically significant. In the control group, no difference in PCDD/Fs concentrations was observed between residents who consumed locally grown rice (2.74 WHO-TEQ/g lipid) and those who consumed purchased rice (2.72 WHO-TEQ/g lipid). Additionally, PCDD/Fs concentrations did not differ between residents consuming locally grown vegetables and those consuming purchased vegetables in either the exposure or control group.

Multiple regression analysis showed that gender, age, BMI, and smoking and drinking habits had no significant effect on blood PCDD/Fs concentrations in the exposure group (Table 7). However, meat intake within the past 3 days significantly affected PCDD/Fs concentrations (p < 0.05). Blood PCDD/Fs concentrations also tended to increase with longer residence periods in the exposed areas, with borderline significance (p = 0.07). Contrastingly, no significant factors influencing blood PCDD/Fs concentrations were identified for residents in the control area. Discussion

In Korea, PCDD/Fs emissions from waste incinerators have been regulated under the Wastes Control Act since 1997, even before the ratification of the Stockholm Convention. Further institutional support has been provided since 2008 through the Persistent Pollutants Act, which strengthened the emission standards for industrial facilities emitting dioxins. National monitoring initiatives for dioxin measurement and emission surveys also showed a significant reduction in emissions into the atmosphere, decreasing from 1,004 g I-TEQ/year in 2001 to 78.2 g I-TEQ/year in 2019. Despite these improvements, many IWIs located near residential areas in Korea remain a source of public concern owing to the concerns about the environmental and health impacts of the emissions (Lee et al. 2012). In Bugi-myeon, the close proximity of three IWIs has led to conflicts among residents, local governments, and companies since the facilities began operations in 1999. Among the pollutants emitted by IWIs, residents are particularly concerned about dioxins, which are known carcinogens, and their potential health effects. This study aimed to evaluate PCDD/Fs exposure levels in the Bugi-myeon environment and among local residents and investigate the factors influencing blood PCDD/Fs concentrations.

The average PCDD/Fs concentration in the atmosphere of 5 villages near the studied IWIs, out of the 51 villages in Bugi-myeon, was found to be 0.0242 pg I-TEQ/Sm3 (range: 0.0176–0.0285 pg I-TEQ/Sm3). This concentration is below the atmospheric environmental standard (annual average) of 0.6 pg I-TEQ/Sm3 specified in the Persistent Pollutants Act in Korea. The environmental concentration of PCDD/Fs may differ depending on the characteristics of incinerator facilities, such as its size, incineration material, incineration amount, and prevention facilities, as well as various conditions such as analysis timing, number of samples, and analysis method. Therefore, direct comparisons between studies should be interpreted with caution. However, the concentration of PCDD/Fs in the atmosphere in the exposure area was similar to that measured in industrial complexes (0.357 pg I-TEQ/ Sm3, 0.063 pg I-TEQ/ Sm3), residential/commercial areas (0.007–0.122 pg WHO-TEQ/ Sm3), and agricultural areas (0.020–0.074 pg WHO-TEQ/ Sm3), as well as around relatively well managed public urban resource recovery facilities (0.011–0.12 pg I-TEQ/ Sm3) (Heo et al. 2014; Kim. 2009; Seo et al. 2011, Seoul Metropolitan Government 2012). These levels were also lower than those reported in other countries, such as in Taiwan (0.103 pg TEQ/Sm3), Germany (0.052 pg TEQ/Sm3), Spain (0.55 pg TEQ/Sm3), Japan (0.25 pg TEQ/Sm3), Hong Kong (0.07 pg WHO-TEQ/Sm3), and Turkey (0.0233 pg TEQ/Sm3) (Cheng et al. 2003; Choi et al. 2008; Bakoglu et al. 2005). Furthermore, our study showed no difference in PCDD/Fs concentrations in the soil between the exposure and control areas, with their concentrations below the national average of 1.310 pg I-TEQ/g, as surveyed in 2021 (Ministry of Environment 2022). Additionally, although only one-time measurements were performed, PCDD/Fs concentrations at the three incineration facility outlets in the exposure area complied with the emission standards. These findings suggest that currently, environmental PCDD/Fs concentrations in the exposure area are not as high as the residents’ concern.

However, residents in the exposure area remain concerned about potential health effects from PCDD/Fs exposure, as several IWIs operate nearby, with some having exceeded emission standards during inspections in 2017. Although the present study found low PCDD/Fs concentrations in the outlets, atmosphere, and soil, the assessment of PCDD/Fs concentrations in the environment of the exposure area was limited in the past due to a lack of data, aside from those derived from inspections and self-reported measurements.

Some congeners of PCDD/Fs are known carcinogen. Previous Korean studies have examined blood PCDD/Fs concentrations in waste incinerators workers and nearby residents; however, existing monitoring data for the general population are insufficient. Previous studies have estimated the blood PCDD/Fs concentrations in waste incinerators workers to be in the range of 3.14–41.57 pg WHO-TEQ/g lipid and that in residents living around a waste incinerators to be 6.60–53.420 pg I-TEQ/g lipid (Leem et al. 2003; Moon et al. 2005; Park et al. 2009). In contrast, long-term monitoring of residents near resource recovery facilities in Seoul revealed declining average levels of blood PCDD/Fs with a concentration of 9.41 pg WHO-TEQ/g lipid from 2001 to 2011 and 6.9 pg WHO-TEQ/g lipid (median: 6.49 pg WHO-TEQ/g lipid) in 2022 (Seoul Metropolitan Government 2022). In Waegwan-eup, Gumi, Gyeongbuk, Korea, an investigation of blood PCDD/Fs concentrations among 113 residents prompted by concerns over the suspected burial of barrels marked “Agent Orange,” used during the Vietnam War at Camp Carroll showed an average level of 2.27 pg WHO-TEQ/g lipid, despite no housing of waste incinerators in the area (Bae et al. 2016). International studies have shown similar variabilities in blood PCDD/Fs concentrations in residents near waste incinerators. For instance, it ranged from 8.6 to 11 pg WHO-TEQ/g lipid in Italy, 6.8 pg I-TEQ/g lipid in Spain, and a higher level of 37.9 pg WHO-TEQ/g lipid in Belgium (Felip et al. 2008; Nadal et al. 2019; Fierens et al. 2007). However, surveys conducted in Japan, Slovakia, and Taiwan reported levels below approximately 10 pg WHO-TEQ/g lipid (Arisawa et al. 2011; Chovancová et al. 2012; Hsu et al. 2009).

In this study, the average blood PCDD/Fs concentrations of residents in the exposed area (3.13 pg WHO-TEQ/g lipid) did not differ significantly from those in the control area and were lower than the concentrations reported in many domestic and international studies. Consistent with previous studies’ findings, no significant difference in blood PCDD/Fs concentrations was observed between areas surrounding waste incinerators and non-exposure areas (Felip et al. 2008; Parera et al. 2013). These findings support previous assertions that dietary intake, rather than ambient air, is a more critical contributor to blood concentration (Srogi 2008). Other studies have also shown that diet is the primary pathway of dioxin exposure in the body (Marinkocić et al. 2010). In Japan, fish consumption is a key dietary factor influencing blood dioxin levels (Muzembo et al. 2019), while a Korean study showed a positive correlation between blood dioxin levels and the intake of meat, milk, and eggs (Ministry of Food and Drug Safety 2021). In our analysis, meat consumption within the past 3 days showed a positive association with blood PCDD/Fs concentrations in residents of the exposure area. This association was confirmed using multiple regression analysis. However, no such difference was observed in the control area, indicating inconsistencies in the impact of diet.

Several studies have reported that blood PCDD/Fs concentration is positively related to age and is higher in women than in men (Harden et al. 2007; Gonzalez et al. 1998; Chen et al. 2005; Kreuzer et al. 1997; Fromme et al. 2015), likely due to a positive association between half-life of PCDD/Fs and body fat (Flesch-Janys et al. 1996). In this study, univariate analysis revealed higher PCDD/Fs concentrations in women in the exposure area than in women in the control area. However, this difference was not observed in multiple linear regression (data not shown). In addition, the length of residency in the exposed area was related to blood PCDD/Fs concentrations. Residents who lived in the area for 20 years or more had slightly higher PCDD/Fs concentrations (3.23 pg WHO-TEQ/g lipid) than those who lived there for less than 20 years (2.63 pg WHO-TEQ/g lipid), with this difference approaching borderline significance (p < 0.07). The multiple regression analysis showed a borderline significant trend, indicating that blood PCDD/Fs concentrations increased with longer residence periods in the exposure area. In contrast, no relationship between blood PCDD/Fs concentrations and residence period was observed in the control area. We were unable to find comparable data for the general population exploring the relationship between the duration of exposure and PCDD/Fs concentrations. However, one study on waste incinerators workers showed that blood PCDD/Fs concentrations increased with longer working periods, with an elevation from 6.2 pg I-TEQ/g lipid after 1 month to 78.7 pg I-TEQ/g lipid after 144 months (Kim et al. 2005). These findings suggest that longer residence in exposed areas may be a modest factor. However, further studies with larger sample sizes are required to validate these speculations.

This study has some limitations; therefore, caution is required when interpreting its results. First, the analysis of blood PCDD/Fs concentrations was restricted to approximately 200 people among 1,456 health survey participants due to budget constraints. The participants were carefully selected, considering villages, residence periods, and age, to avoid disproportionate participation from specific areas. However, there was a limitation in ensuring representativeness. In this study, the proportion of individuals aged 60 or above was higher than that of the general population in Bugi-myeon. However, it is unlikely that the PCDD/Fs concentrations in this study were underestimated, given that previous studies have reported higher PCDD/Fs concentrations with increasing age. Second, although the study aimed to recruit participants considering age and gender distribution in the two groups, the mean age in the control group was higher than that in the exposure group. Accordingly, multiple regression analysis was conducted to adjust for, confounding factors including age but the possibility of residual confounding cannot be ruled out. Third, due to the lack of historical environmental data, past dioxin exposure levels in the area could not be considered, and the comparison was based solely on the concentration levels currently measured from agricultural roads. Fourth, diet is a significant pathway for dioxin exposure in the body. However, we were unable to perform a detailed analysis of intake frequency and quantity. Additionally, questions about dairy products, which are known to be associated with dioxin concentrations, were not included in the questionnaire. The investigation into meat consumption also focused on short-term intake, limiting its ability to represent typical dietary habits.

A key strength of the present study is that it represents the first nationwide investigation conducted by a government institute to assess PCDD/Fs exposure levels in the environment and among residents living near IWIs in Korea. This study confirmed that PCDD/Fs concentrations in the environment (such as air and soil) and in the blood of local residents were similar to or lower than those reported in previous studies. However, the results of this study should not be taken as an indication of the safety of IWIs. Hazardous substances, including PCDD/Fs, have been emitted from IWIs, and their potential effects on the environment and public health cannot be ruled out. Therefore, continued and systematic monitoring of both the environmental conditions and the health status of residents in Bugi-myeon is necessary, considering the proximity of the three IWIs in small operational areas.

Conclusion

This study examined the exposure levels of PCDD/Fs, including carcinogenic substance such as 2,3,7,8-TCDD, 2,3,4,7,8-PeCDF, both in the environment and in the blood of residents living near IWIs, as well as the factors influencing PCDD/Fs exposure. Although atmospheric PCDD/Fs levels were significantly higher in the exposure area than in the control area, PCDD/Fs concentrations in other environmental media and blood samples of residents were similar to or lower than those reported in other domestic and international studies. Considering the potential health impacts of PCDD/Fs, even at low concentrations, sustained efforts are essential to mitigate exposure through continuous environmental monitoring and effective source control. The results of this study are expected to provide valuable data for risk communication with residents near incineration facilities and for the development of community-level environmental health policies.
