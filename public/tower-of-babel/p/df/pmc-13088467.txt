Machine learning-based prediction model for fall risk among individuals with arthritis in China: an analysis using the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) database
Authors: Minglun Xiao, Ting Tan, Mao Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFalling has become a global public health problem. Individuals with arthritis have a higher risk of falling because of joint pain, poor muscle strength, and decreased proprioception; however, there is no all-inclusive risk assessment model for fall risks in the Chinese population based on multi-health data.MethodsThis study utilized data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) to develop a machine learning (ML)-based predictive model for fall risk among adults with arthritis. Multidimensional health data were integrated to identify key risk factors. Nine ML models, including XGBoost, were employed to assess predictive performance. The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a test set (30%). Model performance was evaluated using AUC, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis, among other metrics.ResultsAmong the 4,536 participants, 927 fall incidents were recorded, with a fall incidence rate of 20.44%. LASSO regression identified six key risk factors: age, sleep time, diabetes, depression, grip strength, and ADL score. The XGBoost model demonstrated the best performance, with AUC values of 0.746 (95% CI: 0.726–0.763) in the training set and 0.734 (95% CI: 0.702–0.768) in the test set. Calibration curves showed good agreement between predicted and observed probabilities, and decision curve analysis indicated significant clinical benefits.ConclusionThe ML-based predictive model developed in this study effectively identifies fall risk among adults with arthritis, providing a valuable tool for clinical management and public health strategies. Further validation in external datasets is needed to confirm the model’s generalizability and clinical utility.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s41043-026-01283-0.Keywords: Fall, Arthritis, CHARLS, XGBoost, Machine learning

Introduction

Falls have become a public health problem in the world. The data showed that 28–35% of community-dwelling older adults aged 65 years old and above will fall each year; the proportion increased with age, up to 32–42% for those over 70 [1–4]. In the United States, more than one-third of middle-aged and older adults experience at least one fall per year [5, 6]. In China, the incidence rate of falls among middle-aged and older adults ranges from 20% to 30% [7, 8]. Falls can not only result in direct injuries such as fractures and soft tissue damage but may also lead to long-term functional impairments, psychological fear, and a decline in quality of life, while increasing medical expenses and societal caregiving burdens [9]. Furthermore, the mortality and disability rates associated with complications following falls, particularly hip fractures, are high, further exacerbating the disease burden [10]. Therefore, it is crucial to identify high-risk groups early and implement targeted preventive measures.

Individuals with arthritis, particularly those with knee osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis, are at a significantly higher risk of falling due to joint pain, diminished muscle strength, and impaired proprioception [11, 12]. Studies indicate that individuals with end-stage hip knee osteoarthritis experience a notably increased incidence of falls within a year, and their dynamic balance ability (e.g., center of pressure shift) is significantly correlated with a history of falls [13, 14]. Additionally, the inflammatory response and muscle atrophy associated with arthritis further compromise motor control, increasing the risk of gait instability and imbalance [15]. Although existing research has focused on the association between arthritis and falls, a comprehensive risk assessment model that integrates multidimensional health data for this specific sub-population in China remains scarce. Many existing models, often derived from Western cohorts, focus on a limited set of predictors and have not been extensively validated or compared in the context of a Chinese national cohort using a wide array of machine learning (ML) techniques. Notably, ML has demonstrated significant utility in predicting musculoskeletal health outcomes, such as osteoporosis, from clinical data [16–18], showcasing its potential to identify high-risk individuals for targeted management.

Building upon this concept, we hypothesize that a ML model, leveraging the high-dimensional data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), can effectively identify a robust set of predictors and achieve superior performance in stratifying fall risk among Chinese adults with arthritis. Accordingly, this study has the following primary objectives: (1) To develop and compare the performance of multiple ML models for predicting fall risk in a Chinese arthritis population; (2) To identify the most influential risk factors from a comprehensive set of sociodemographic, behavioral, clinical, and laboratory variables; and (3) To provide an interpretable, clinically applicable tool for risk assessment.

Methods

Study design and population

The data for this study were derived from the CHARLS database (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en/). [19] This database aims to establish a high-quality, nationally representative micro-database of Chinese residents aged 45 and older. The project is a prospective cohort study employing Probability Proportional to Size (PPS) sampling methodology, initiating baseline surveys in 2011, with subsequent biennial face-to-face follow-ups and periodic physical examinations and blood tests interspersed between each visit. The scope of the study spans across 150 counties (or districts) and 450 communities (or villages) across 28 provinces (including municipalities and autonomous regions) in China. The CHARLS protocol has been approved by the Institutional Review Board of Peking University (IRB No. IRB00001052-11014), and all participants provided written informed consent prior to their involvement in the study. This study is observational in nature, and our findings are presented in line with the STROBE guidelines. The study was conducted in full accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

This study utilized baseline and follow-up waves from the CHARLS in 2011. After applying specific exclusion criteria, a total of 4,536 eligible participants were included: (1) those under the age of 45 years (n = 777); (23) absence of arthritis at baseline (n = 11,242); (3) individuals with missing fall records (n = 1,060); (4) those diagnosed with cancer at baseline (n = 93). Definition of arthritis

This study operationally defined “arthritis” as knee joint disorders, specifically including osteoarthritis and rheumatoid arthritis. Diagnostic criteria were derived from the CHARLS core questionnaire item: “Has a doctor ever diagnosed you with arthritis or rheumatism?” Participants were classified as having “confirmed knee arthritis” only if their affirmative response rate to this question exceeded 40% across all available follow-up waves.

Assessment of fall and candidate predictive variables

In this study, a fall is defined as the occurrence of at least one incident within the past 24 months. This definition serves to specifically identify participants who have experienced a fall during the study period. Candidate predictive variables encompassed four dimensions, including sociodemographic characteristics, behavioral patterns, health status, and laboratory indicators, totaling 38 metrics.

Specifically, sociodemographic characteristics include age, gender (male or female), living area (urban or rural), educational (below high school or high school and beyond), and marital status (married or unmarried). Marital status is defined as follows: participants who are currently married and cohabiting with their spouse are categorized as married; those who are not living with their spouse due to work or other reasons, are separated, divorced, widowed, or have never been married are classified as unmarried. Regarding behavioral patterns, this study took into account smoking status (yes or no), drinking status (yes or no), and sleep time. Sleep time data were derived from participants’ self-reported average hours of sleep per night over the past month. Regarding laboratory indicators, this study encompassed a range of measurements including white blood cell count (WBC), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), platelet, blood urea nitrogen (BUN), glucose, creatinine (CREA), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), C-reactive protein (CRP), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), uric acid, hematocrit, and hemoglobin.

Health status was assessed based on body mass index (BMI), waist circumference, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), pulse, subjective health perception, cognitive function, depressive symptoms, grip strength, and activities of daily living (ADL) and history of chronic diseases (hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, stroke, or asthma). Subjective health perception was categorized based on responses to the question “How would you rate your health status?” with “excellent,” “very good,” and “good” classified as “healthy,” and “fair” and “poor” as “unhealthy.” Cognitive function was evaluated through the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS-10), visual-spatial skill tests, and episodic memory tests; higher composite scores indicate better cognitive function [20]. Depressive symptoms were assessed using the Center for Epidemiological Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10), which includes 10 items scored from 1 to 4 based on actual circumstances, with a total score ranging from 0 to 30 points, and a score of 10 or more indicating depression [21, 22]. Grip strength was measured based on the average of four grip strength tests (both left and right hands). The ADL assessment in this study was conducted based on the presence of difficulties in seven areas: walking, dressing, bathing, eating, getting in and out of bed, toileting, and controlling urination. This approach is conceptually similar to the Katz ADL index, which also evaluates these fundamental activities of daily living. However, our study did not use a specific standardized instrument such as the Modified Barthel Index (mBI) or the Katz ADL index directly. Instead, we adopted a simplified version tailored to the CHARLS survey design, which is more feasible for large-scale epidemiological studies. This method allows for a comprehensive yet straightforward evaluation of functional status, which is essential for identifying individuals at risk of falls. While the mBI provides a more detailed and interpretable score, our approach ensures consistency with the overall study design and data collection framework of CHARLS. The history of chronic diseases was defined as “yes” or “no” based on self-reported diagnoses.

Machine learning model development and hyperparameter tuning

In this study, participants were categorized into faller and non-faller groups based on the occurrence of fall events. To ensure the robustness of the analysis, the dataset was randomly allocated into a training set (70%, n = 3,175) and a test set (30%, n = 1,361).

The distribution of the outcome variable in the training set was imbalanced, with fallers constituting 21.29% (676/3,175) of the sample. To prevent the ML models from developing a bias towards predicting the majority class (non-fallers), we investigated several data-level resampling techniques applied exclusively to the training set. We evaluated Synthetic Minority Over-sampling Technique (SMOTE), SMOTE combined with Edited Nearest Neighbours (SMOTE-ENN), Adaptive Synthetic Sampling (ADASYN), and Random Under-Sampling (RUS) [23]. The selection of the final resampling method was based on the performance of the XGBoost model, measured by the Area Under the Curve (AUC) on a held-out validation set. SMOTE-ENN was chosen as it yielded the highest AUC; its ENN component removes noisy majority class instances, potentially improving class boundaries and model generalizability. After applying SMOTE-ENN, the final training set comprised 4,387 participants (Non-Fallers: 2,301; Fallers: 2,086). The test set (n = 1,361), with 1,110 Non-Fallers and 251 Fallers, remained untouched to provide an unbiased performance evaluation.

Initially, multicollinearity among variables was assessed in the training set using the variance inflation factor (VIF) and correlation matrices. Subsequently, univariate statistical comparisons were conducted between faller and non-faller groups in the training set to identify variables with significant differences. Following this, Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator (LASSO) logistic regression was applied for automatic feature selection. The optimal penalty parameter λ was determined through ten-fold cross-validation, and the “λ.1se” rule was adopted to balance model parsimony and predictive accuracy.

To comprehensively evaluate the predictive performance of the selected features, nine ML classification models were employed: Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost), K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN), Gradient Boosting Decision Tree (GBDT), Artificial Neural Network (ANN), Random Forest (RF), Logistic Regression (LR), Decision Tree (DT), Support Vector Machine (SVM), and Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM). The nine ML models were implemented using Scikit-learn and XGBoost libraries in Python. To ensure a robust comparison and mitigate overfitting, hyperparameter tuning was conducted for each model using a 5-fold cross-validated Grid Search (or Random Search for models with large parameter spaces like RF and XGBoost) on the training set, with the AUC as the optimization metric. The specific hyperparameter search spaces and the final parameters for the best-performing version of each model are comprehensively listed in Table S1. The model with the set of hyperparameters yielding the highest mean cross-validation AUC was selected as the final model for each algorithm. A tripartite evaluation framework was employed to comprehensively assess the predictive performance of the model: First, the discriminative ability was evaluated by constructing the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and calculating the AUC along with its 95% confidence interval (CI); secondly, calibration was assessed by plotting calibration curves to verify the consistency between predicted and observed probabilities; Lastly, decision curve analysis (DCA) was utilized to quantify the net clinical benefit across a range of threshold probabilities. Additionally, classification-based metrics including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, Youden’s index, F1 score, and Brier score were derived to fully evaluate the diagnostic performance of the model. Among these, the AUC serves as one of the most critical metrics for gauging overall model performance [24].

Additionally, SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) interpretability analysis was conducted. SHAP quantifies the contribution of each feature to the prediction outcome and ranks the importance of predictive factors [25]. Individualized waterfall plots were also generated to demonstrate the dynamic impact of feature combinations, and this interpretable output significantly enhances clinicians’ trust in the model’s predictive logic. To further explore the relationship between the top predictors identified by the SHAP summary plot, a post-hoc LR analysis was also conducted to test for interaction effects.

Statistical analysis

This study employed SPSS 23.0, R 4.3.3, and Python 3.7 for statistical analysis. Categorical data were presented as counts (percentages) and compared using the chi-squared test. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent samples t-test, whereas non-normally distributed variables were summarized as median [interquartile range] and compared with the Wilcoxon rank-sum test. Variables with missing values exceeding 30% were excluded from the analysis. To address remaining missing data, we utilized the KNN ML algorithm for intelligent imputation. This method was chosen over simpler approaches as it estimates missing values based on the feature similarity of the k-nearest neighbors, thereby better preserving the multivariate distribution and internal correlations of the dataset. While this approach is most suitable for data Missing Completely at Random (MCAR), it is also considered robust under the Missing at Random (MAR) mechanism, which is a more reasonable assumption for most variables in our study [26]. All statistical tests in this study were two-tailed, and results with a P-value less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Multicollinearity diagnosis and univariate analysis

As detailed in Table 2, participants who fell were significantly older and more likely to be female. Clinically, fallers exhibited a profile suggestive of greater frailty and comorbidity burden, including significantly weaker grip strength, higher ADL disability scores, and a greater prevalence of diabetes and depressive symptoms. These univariate associations highlight the multifactorial nature of fall risk and justify the inclusion of these broad domains in our predictive model. Figure 2 illustrates the correlation matrix among continuous variables, where color intensity and circle diameter encode the magnitude and direction of the coefficients. Results indicate a positive correlation between LDL-C and TC, hematocrit and hemoglobin, and an inverse correlation between age and grip strength (all P < 0.01). Univariate analysis identified 21 variables significantly associated with the incidence of fall: age, gender, living area, education, marital status, sleep time, subjective health perception, cognitive function, grip strength, ADL score, hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, asthma, depression, MCV, CREA, HDL-C, HbA1c, uric acid, and hemoglobin (all P < 0.05). Table 3 presents the multicollinearity diagnostics for these candidate variables; all VIF were less than 5, and tolerance values were greater than 0.1, indicating no significant multicollinearity issues.

Screening for predictors indicating fall risk

To further identify key factors affecting the risk of falls and to optimize model parameters, we applied two-step LASSO regression to the training set after addressing class imbalance using the SMOTE-ENN technique (see the ‘Methods’ section for details). The tuning parameter λ was selected through cross-validation to minimize predictive error. The optimal λ values were determined as 0.005 for minimizing mean squared error and 0.031 for minimizing the standard error of the minimum distance. This process resulted in the identification of six variables significantly impacting the model: age, sleep time, diabetes, depression, grip strength, and ADL score. Models development and evaluation

Figure 4A and B present the AUC values of the nine models in the training and test sets, where an AUC value of 1 signifies perfect prediction, while an AUC value of 0.5 indicates prediction equivalent to random guessing. Additionally, Tables 4 and 5 list six other evaluation metrics, including sensitivity, specificity, accuracy, Youden’s index, F1 score, and Brier score. Among the nine prediction models, the XGBoost model demonstrated the best performance in both the training and test sets and was therefore selected as the final model for further interpretation and clinical application. Specifically, the AUC values of the XGBoost model in the training and test sets were 0.746 (95% CI: 0.726–0.763) and 0.734 (95% CI: 0.702–0.768), respectively. To statistically validate its superior discriminative ability, we conducted DeLong tests for paired ROC curves on the training set. The XGBoost model’s AUC was significantly higher than that of the second-best performing model, RF (AUC: 0.743, DeLong test P = 0.047) and the traditional LR model (AUC: 0.718, DeLong test P = 0.021). In the training and test sets, the sensitivity of the model was 0.737 and 0.717, respectively, the specificity was 0.658 and 0.645, respectively, the accuracy was 0.675 and 0.658, respectively, the Youden’s index was 0.395 and 0.362, respectively, and the F1 score was 0.491 and 0.436, respectively. Calibration curve analysis revealed that the predicted probabilities of the XGBoost model were in good agreement with the observed probabilities, with the curve closely approximating the 45° line. The Brier scores of the model in the training and test sets were 0.219 and 0.223, respectively (Fig. 4C and D). Furthermore, decision curve analysis (Fig. 4E and F) indicated that the XGBoost model provided significant clinical benefits in both the training and test sets.

SHAP analysis for XGBoost model interpretation and importance ranking

We employed SHAP analysis to enhance the interpretability of the XGBoost model. The global feature importance ranking (Fig. 5B) confirmed that depression, ADL score, and grip strength were the top three predictors of fall risk. Beyond overall importance, SHAP dependence plot (Fig. 5A) revealed the directional and non-linear impact of these key features: grip strength exhibited a clear negative linear relationship with fall risk, whereas ADL score showed a positive, non-linear increase in risk beyond a score of 2. To delve into specific predictions and potential errors, we performed a case-based analysis using individual force plot (Fig. 5C). For instance, one false negative case (a faller incorrectly predicted as low-risk) was an individual with exceptionally high grip strength for their age, which the model weighted heavily, overshadowing their moderate ADL limitations. Conversely, a typical false positive case (a non-faller predicted as high-risk) was an individual with poor subjective health perception and long sleep time, factors the model associates with frailty, despite them not having experienced a fall in the recall period. These examples illustrate the model’s decision logic and its potential pitfalls when strong protective and risk factors are in conflict.

Interaction between depression and ADL score

Given that depression and ADL score were identified as the two most important predictors, we formally tested their interaction. The interaction term was statistically significant (OR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.70–0.92, P = 0.001) (Table S2). As visualized in Figure S1, the effect of depression was most pronounced in individuals with low ADL scores (preserved physical function), whereas its relative contribution to fall risk diminished in those with severe functional impairments (high ADL scores), who already exhibited a high baseline risk.

Discussion

This study provides a complete picture on developing and assessing the ML-based predictive model for fall risk among adults with arthritis based on CHARLS database. By combining many dimensions of health information, we identified several significant variables: age, sleep time, diabetes, depression, grip strength, and ADL score. Compared with other models, XGBoost had better performance; it possessed excellent discriminative ability and clinical practicability. This result indicated that clinicians and public health workers should pay particular attention to identifying and managing multifactorial risk factors in order to lower the risk of falls in people with arthritis.

As individuals age, organs and systems undergo varying degrees of age-related changes (e.g., reduced muscle strength, decreased activity, impaired balance, and slowed basal metabolism). Reduced sensitivity to sensation caused by aging may lead to poor eyesight and hearing loss, and fat accumulation may appear in various parts of the body [27], which are often accompanied by some special age-related diseases. Additionally, aging may precipitate certain age-related diseases, such as hypertension, diabetes, and osteoporosis, all of which can increase the risk of falls. Many previous studies have demonstrated that old age is an independent risk factor linked to the occurrence of hypertensive falls [28, 29]. Although falls can occur at any age, the incidence and associated risks of falls significantly rise among the elderly as life expectancy extends [30, 31]. Likewise, diabetes, another variable proven to be significant in this study, has also been reported to play a vital role in increasing the risk of falls [32].

Arthritis patients with comorbid depression are at a significantly higher risk of falls compared to those with arthritis alone. A meta-analysis showed that there was an association between depressive conditions and falls, suggesting a potential bidirectional relationship between depression and falls [33]. Motor disturbances, gait retardation, and cognitive delays due to typical depression symptoms all increase the chance of falling [34]. Furthermore, even if a fall does not actually occur, patients may develop a fear of falling due to depressive symptoms, which in turn may exacerbate depressive symptoms [35, 36]. This highlights the importance of depressive conditions in falls among arthritis patients. This study also found that impaired activities in arthritis patients because of poor ADL experience a significant increment in their fall risk; this may happen because pain, muscle wasting, and a significant loss in dynamic balance ability (i.e., stand-walk time length) induce changes such as gait abnormality and loss of postural control. These changes may be much more severe in those with knee osteoarthritis who already possess weaker quadriceps muscles and damaged proprioception [37, 38]. Additionally, depression-related lack of motivation and social withdrawal also reduce the amount of activity undertaken by patients, leading to more significant functional decline [39]. Therefore, in clinical management, it is necessary to integrate anti-inflammatory treatment with psychological interventions (such as exercise therapy combined with cognitive-behavioral therapy) to improve physical function and break the negative cycle of psycho-physiological interactions.

The findings of this study also indicate that an increase in grip strength is positively correlated with a reduction in fall risk. Wen et al. demonstrated that among individuals with diabetes, a 5 kg increase in grip strength is associated with a 16% decrease in fall risk [40]; a study from Taiwan also found that in the older population, a 1 kg increase in grip strength corresponds to a 5% reduction in fall risk [41]. Moreover, numerous studies have confirmed the association between lower grip strength and a higher risk of falling [42, 43]. The fall risk in patients with arthritis is closely related to a decrease in grip strength, primarily due to chronic inflammation and pain leading to reduced activity, which in turn causes muscle atrophy and weakened strength [44]. Inflammatory factors, such as TNF-α and IL-6, promote muscle protein breakdown, while joint dysfunction affects neuromuscular coordination, making it difficult for patients to maintain body balance [45]. These factors collectively lead to a decrease in muscle mass and impaired motor control, thereby increasing the risk of falling. Furthermore, grip strength is one of the simplest and most accessible indicators for assessing sarcopenia and frailty, directly reflecting the condition of upper limb skeletal muscle and overall muscle mass [46]; better muscle quality is associated with a lower incidence of falls, and research by Van Ancum et al. has also elucidated the link between reduced muscle strength and falling [47].

Insomnia, hard to fall asleep and early awakening are common in old population with sleep disorder, resulting in short night time sleeping time [48–50]. In this study, shorter sleep duration was related to more fall incident. Piovezan assumed that due to sleep disorder caused by imbalance of catabolic hormone and anabolic hormone, it would increase inflammatory response and decrease energy consumption, which explained why the older with poor sleep had a higher risk of falling [51]. After adjusting for cognitive ability, age, physical activity, BMI, anxiety, and depressed mood, Stone et al. found that both subjective and objective sleep disorders were significantly associated with increased fall incidence [52]. George et al. also found that in Australian middle-aged and older adults, those who slept less than six hours had a higher risk of falling [53]. Although evidence about the relationship between long sleep time and falls is not enough, Helbig et al. even found that in German middle-aged and old adults, sleeping nine hours or more per day was significantly positively correlated with the probability of falls [54]. But some previous studies reported there was no relation between sleep duration and falls [55, 56]. Because there lacks relevant research on the relationship between sleep duration and falls at present, we should pay attention to the relationship between objective sleep duration and the occurrence of falls in the future.

ML algorithms have powerful functions to find out data patterns and solve nonlinear relationships, which can improve the prediction accuracy effectively. Particularly in automated risk assessment and the formulation of personalized treatment strategies, ML algorithms have gradually gained widespread clinical acceptance [57, 58]. Prediction models based on ML could provide clinicians with earlier warnings of high-risk events so they can perform individualized early interventions. In this study, the XGBoost model outperformed various predictive models, achieving an AUC of 0.734 (95% CI: 0.702–0.768) on the untouched, non-resampled test set. This performance metric is crucial as it best reflects real-world conditions where the model would be applied. The superior performance of the XGBoost model can be attributed to its ensemble nature, which combines multiple weak learners (decision trees) sequentially to correct errors from previous trees. This makes it particularly effective at capturing complex, non-linear relationships between the multidimensional risk factors and fall outcomes, as evidenced by its highest AUC among all models tested. In comparison, the LR model achieved an AUC of 0.728 (95% CI: 0.693–0.762) on the test set. While LR serves as a traditional and interpretable baseline model, it is important to note that other candidate models such as KNN, GBDT, ANN, RF, DT, SVM, and LightGBM were also evaluated. The XGBoost model demonstrated superior performance not only over LR but also across these other models, further confirming its robustness and suitability for clinical applications. The selection of LR for detailed comparison in the discussion was primarily to highlight the improvement over a well-established and commonly used model. However, the comprehensive evaluation of all models in the study supports the conclusion that XGBoost is the most effective model for predicting fall risk among adults with arthritis.

To provide a comprehensive overview of the current state-of-the-art in using ML to predict falls or related outcomes among adults, we have compiled a comparative table (Table S3) summarizing key aspects of our study and related works. This table highlights the cohort characteristics, sample size, outcome definition, algorithms used, resampling methods, and missing-data strategies applied in these studies. This table demonstrates that our study contributes to the field by focusing on a specific clinical population (adults with arthritis) and integrating a wide range of multidimensional health data. The use of advanced ML algorithms and robust resampling methods further enhances the predictive accuracy and clinical applicability of our model. Additionally, our study highlights the importance of addressing missing data through intelligent imputation techniques to preserve the integrity of the dataset.

While this study provides a proof-of-concept for a ML-based risk stratification tool, several steps are necessary before clinical implementation. First and foremost, external validation in diverse clinical settings (e.g., rheumatology clinics, community health centers) is essential. Following successful validation, a prospective study or a cluster-randomized trial would be the ideal next step to assess whether using this model to guide targeted interventions (e.g., referrals to depression management or physical therapy for those identified as high-risk) actually leads to a reduction in fall rates. Integrating the model into electronic health records as a clinical decision support tool represents a long-term goal, but would require addressing challenges related to real-time data availability and workflow integration.

Despite the robust findings, this study has several limitations. First, the data were derived from a single longitudinal study, which may limit generalizability to other populations. Second, while we included a wide range of variables, other potential risk factors, such as detailed gait analysis or environmental factors, were not available in the CHARLS dataset. Additionally, the CHARLS database lacks detailed data on Vitamin D levels and physical activity, both of which have been shown to be strongly associated with fall risk in older adults. The absence of these variables may introduce bias and potentially affect the model’s discrimination and predictive performance. Third, the use of self-reported data for some variables (e.g., fall incidents and chronic disease history) may introduce recall bias. Finally, although the XGBoost model showed strong performance in our test set, further validation in external, independent datasets is essential to confirm its generalizability and clinical utility. The performance observed here may be subject to optimism bias, and external validation is the definitive step to assess the model’s true discriminative ability and calibration in new patient populations. This should be a primary focus of future research.

Conclusion

In summary, this study developed and internally validated a ML-based predictive model for fall risk among adults with arthritis using the CHARLS database. The identified risk factors and the high-performing XGBoost model represent a promising proof-of-concept tool for identifying high-risk individuals. This model provides a valuable foundation for guiding the development of targeted interventions, but its clinical utility must be confirmed through prospective and external validation before it can be implemented in real-world practice. Future research should follow a clear trajectory to translate these findings into clinical impact: First, multi-center external validation in diverse clinical and community settings is essential to assess generalizability. Second, prospective studies are needed to evaluate the model’s performance in predicting future falls and to test its efficacy within a clinical intervention framework. Finally, model performance could be enhanced by integrating novel data sources, such as objective gait measurements from wearable sensors, detailed medication records, and assessments of home environmental hazards.
