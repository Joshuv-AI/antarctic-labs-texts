Multifunctional Selenium–Sulfur-Doped Carbon Dots Nanozymes with Thioredoxin Reductase Activity for Regenerative Therapy of Infected Wounds
Authors: Jie Huang, Na Zhou, Mengmeng Cao, Xiaohan Zhang, Shujian Zhang, Xinrui Luan, Xuancheng Wei, Licheng Wang, Wenxuan Zhang, Zansheng Huang, Xinhua Shao, Li Gao, Ying Li, Han Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Bacterial-infected wounds exhibit delayed healing due to numerous pathological factors, including excessive inflammatory responses, reactive oxygen species (ROS) accumulation, and impaired angiogenesis. Current clinical therapies predominantly employ antimicrobial monotherapy, failing to address the complex interplay among the other pathogenic factors. Herein, we synthesized multifunctional selenium–sulfur-doped carbon dots (SE/S-CDs) nanozymes using selenocystine and cysteine as precursors to simultaneously target multiple pathological hallmarks of infected wounds. Primarily, SE/S-CDs exerted potent antibacterial activity by compromising the integrity of bacterial cell membranes, thereby achieving robust pathogen clearance in infected wounds. Furthermore, owing to the presence of Se–S dynamic bonds, they can specifically activate the thioredoxin reductase (TrxR) pathway, efficiently scavenging ROS and alleviating the inflammatory response. In addition, SE/S-CDs upregulated the hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) signaling pathway, promoting the expression of proangiogenic genes and accelerating neovascularization and tissue repair. Collectively, SE/S-CDs modulated the infected wound microenvironment through a coordinated “antibacterial–anti-inflammatory–antioxidant–proangiogenic” cascade, demonstrating excellent, integrated therapeutic outcomes. This integrated therapeutic strategy not only exhibits efficacy in infected wound treatment but also holds translational potential for other infection-related and oxidative stress-driven diseases.

Introduction

Infected wounds and the difficulties associated with their treatment represent a global public health concern and medical challenge. The skin functions as the body’s inherent defensive barrier against exogenous pathogens. When the skin is affected by trauma, burns, surgical incisions, or chronic illnesses such as diabetes, its defensive function is impaired, allowing the infiltration of pathogens such as bacteria and fungi. , According to recent statistics from the World Health Organization, millions of patients worldwide are affected by infected wounds every year. In addition to substantially delaying the healing process, these wounds may lead to major consequences such as sepsis, posing a considerable threat to human health. − Therefore, exploring effective treatment strategies for infected wounds has become an essential requirement in the medical community.

Bactericidal antibiotics are often the primary choice for managing wound infections. However, the overuse of antibiotics in clinical practice has engendered the growing issue of antibiotic resistance, substantially impeding the appropriate management of infected wounds. Skin injury repair is a highly intricate and dynamic regenerative process consisting of four stages: hemostasis, inflammation, proliferation, and tissue regeneration. − This regenerative process is often hindered by synergistic interactions among various pathological factors. For example, colonization by pathogenic microorganisms incites chronic inflammatory responses, which in turn trigger the excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), disrupting extracellular matrix remodeling. Meanwhile, insufficient angiogenesis results in the establishment of a hypoxic microenvironment. These interconnected detrimental phenomena establish a vicious loop, further aggravating deficiencies in wound repair. Thus, multimodal interventions that concurrently address infection, inflammatory microenvironment dysregulation, and vascular regeneration deficiencies are necessary for overcoming challenges in managing infected wounds.

Thioredoxin reductase (TrxR) is a crucial enzyme in the cellular redox system and a key member of the selenoprotein family. TrxR is essential for sustaining intracellular redox equilibrium, modulating inflammatory responses, and promoting cell proliferation in physiological processes associated with wound healing. − The utilization of natural enzymes as therapeutics faces several obstacles, such as inadequate stability, limited targeted delivery efficacy, elevated prices, and large-scale manufacturing challenges. To overcome these constraints, researchers have increasingly focused on the development of nanozymes. The catalytically active site of TrxR is formed through dynamic selenium–sulfur interactions between selenocystine and cysteine residues at the C-terminal region. In addition to being crucial for the enzyme’s redox catalytic function, this structural feature offers a template for developing TrxR-like enzymes with similar catalytic activity by engineering dynamic Se–S interactions. −

Carbon dots (CDs) are a unique and versatile nanomaterial that have exhibited considerable promise in bioimaging, nanomedicines, and drug delivery owing to their small size (1–10 nm), exceptional optical properties, excellent biocompatibility, and high stability. − Heteroatom doping can be used to modulate the electronic structure, surface properties, and active sites of CDs, thereby endowing CD-based nanozymes with catalytic activity and offering a viable strategy for fabricating CD-based nanocatalysts. Meng et al. engineered and produced copper-doped CDs exhibiting catalase-like and peroxidase-like activities for the management of oral infectious diseases. Dai et al. fabricated zinc single-atom nanozymes demonstrating superoxide dismutase activity for the treatment of diabetic wounds. Compared with metal-doped CDs, selenium-doped CDs exhibit superior biocompatibility, catalytic properties, and stability. Moreover, the introduction of selenium confers multiple biological activities upon them, including antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and antibacterial activities. These properties together place selenium-doped CDs in a unique position with regards to disease treatment and tissue repair. ,

Herein, inspired by the catalytic principle of the Se–S dynamic bond in TrxR and the diverse biological activities of selenium, we fabricated multifunctional SE/S-CDs via a hydrothermal approach using selenocystine and cysteine as precursors. This study aims to elucidate the multifaceted mechanisms underlying the therapeutic effects of SE/S-CDs on infected wounds. We found that selenium doping of CDs enhances their antimicrobial efficacy, enabling them to inhibit bacteria such as Staphylococcus aureus, Escherichia coli, and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA). At the mechanistic level, the Se–S dynamic bonds act as a primary switch that activates the TrxR pathway, facilitating efficient ROS clearance through the augmentation of cellular reductive capacity. This process not only repairs cells damaged by inflammation but also promotes fibroblast proliferation and migration. Moreover, it regulates the polarization of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, thereby suppressing inflammation at its origin. Furthermore, we found that SE/S-CDs can activate the hypoxia-inducible factor 1α signaling pathway, markedly upregulating angiogenesis-related genes and, consequently, accelerating the formation of new blood vessels, thus creating a nutrition delivery network for wound healing. In vivo experiments in a mouse full-thickness infected skin defect model verified that SE/S-CDs can swiftly mitigate inflammatory responses, enhance collagen deposition and the development of granulation tissue, and facilitate tissue regeneration and repair substantially. In conclusion, the antibacterial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and proangiogenic activities of SE/S-CDs act synergistically to provide a novel, multimodal therapeutic approach for infected wounds.

Materials and Methods

Materials

l-Cysteine and l-selenocystine were purchased from Shanghai McLean Biochemical Technology (China) and Shanghai Haohong Biomedical Technology (China), respectively. Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) and fetal bovine serum (FBS) were obtained from Gibco (U.S.A.). The Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) was purchased from Shanghai Taoshu Biotechnology (China). The thioredoxin reductase activity detection kit was acquired from Solarbio Technology (China). RIPA lysis buffer, ROS assay kit, cell viability and cytotoxicity (calcein/PI) staining kit, methyl green solution, hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining kit, DAPI staining solution, and bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit were obtained from Beyotime Biotechnology (China). Primary and secondary antibodies for immunohistochemistry (IHC), immunofluorescence (IF), and western blot (WB) analysis were purchased from Aibotai Biotechnology (China).

Synthesis of SE/S-CDs

80 mg of selenocystine and 60 mg of cysteine were dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water, followed by vigorous vortexing. An appropriate amount of NaOH solution was added to adjust the pH to approximately 8.5–9. The reaction mixture was subsequently heated to 60 °C for 48 h under magnetic stirring at 300 rpm. The resulting orange-red solution was purified by centrifugation (8000 rpm, 15 min) and filtration through a 0.22 μm microporous membrane to remove impurities, followed by dialysis (dialysis membrane with a molecular weight cutoff of 1000 Da) against ultrapure water for 72 h to eliminate unreacted small molecules. The obtained orange SE/S-CDs solution was lyophilized to obtain SE/S-CDs powder. The powder was reconstituted in deionized water and stored at 4 °C for subsequent use.

Characterization of SE/S-CDs

The morphological characteristics of SE/S-CDs were examined with a transmission electron microscope (TEM, JEOL JEM-2100F, Japan), and images were captured for analysis. Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Thermo Fisher, U.S.A.) and ultraviolet–visible spectroscopy (UV–vis, Hitachi, Tokyo, Japan) were employed to characterize the surface functional groups of SE/S-CDs, as well as those of pure Cys and Sec. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, Thermo Scientific K-Alpha, U.S.A.) was employed to characterize the surface elemental composition of SE/S-CDs, specifically carbon (C), nitrogen (N), oxygen (O), sulfur (S), and selenium (Se). Photoluminescence spectroscopy (PL, Edinburgh FS5, U.K.) was utilized to characterize the optical properties of SE/S-CDs. The crystalline structure of SE/S-CDs was characterized using an X-ray diffractometer (XRD, Rigaku SmartLab SE, Japan).

TrxR Activity

Cellular TrxR Activity Assay

L929 cells were plated into 6-well plates (3 × 105 cells per well) and incubated for 24 h. The cells were subsequently grown in medium containing different concentrations of SE/S-CDs for an additional 24 h. After 60 s of trypsin treatment, the cells were washed with PBS following centrifugation at 1000 rpm at 4 °C for 4 min. The collected cells were lysed with TrxR assay buffer and sonicated for 5 min on ice. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 8000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C to remove insoluble debris, then mixed with TrxR inhibitor solution (50:1) and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. The reaction solution was prepared using the corresponding assay kit, and the absorbance of the solution was measured at a wavelength of 412 nm at 10 s and 10 min, respectively. The TrxR activity was calculated from the absorbance difference between the two time points.

TrxR Activity Assay of SE/S-CDs

SE/S-CDs solutions with different concentrations were mixed with TrxR inhibitor solution at a ratio of 50:1 and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, the TrxR activity of this mixed system was measured using the same reaction solution preparation and absorbance detection method as described in section .

In Vitro Antibacterial Activity

Plate Count Method

Different samples were added to bacterial suspensions of S. aureus and E. coli (initial bacterial concentration: 1 × 106 CFU/mL), followed by co-culture at 37 °C for 24 h under aerobic conditions. After incubation, each bacterial suspension was diluted 103 fold with PBS, and 50 μL of the diluted suspension was inoculated onto nutrient agar plates. The plates were further incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, after which images of the colonies were captured. The antibacterial efficiency of the samples was calculated by enumerating the number of colonies on each plate.

Biofilm Formation Inhibition Assay

Before biofilm formation, S. aureus suspensions (initial concentration: 1 × 106 CFU/mL) were mixed with different samples in a 24-well plate, and co-cultured at 37 °C for 24 h under aerobic conditions. After co-culture, the medium was discarded, and the biofilm in each well was gently washed three times with PBS to remove non-adherent bacteria. The remaining biofilm was fixed with 4% PFA for 15 min, and subsequently stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 30 min. Excess crystal violet dye was washed away with deionized water, and the stained biofilm was dissolved in absolute ethanol. The absorbance of the ethanol solution at 590 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad, Japan).

Live/Dead Bacteria Staining

S. aureus and E. coli were resuspended in PBS to an initial concentration of 1 × 106 CFU/mL, then mixed with different samples and co-cultured at 37 °C for 24 h under aerobic conditions. After co-culture, the bacterial suspensions were stained with a Live/dead staining kit (DMAO and PI) in the dark. After 15 min of staining, excess dye was removed by centrifugation (1000 rpm, 5 min) and resuspended in PBS. Finally, the stained bacteria were observed and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany).

Bacterial Morphology Observation

S. aureus and E. coli were co-cultured with various samples at an initial density of 1 × 106 CFU/mL for 24 h at 37 °C in aerobic culture conditions. The bacteria were then harvested via centrifugation (1000 rpm, 5 min) and fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde at 4 °C overnight. The fixed bacteria were treated with gradient ethanol solutions (30, 50, 70, 90, and 100%, v/v) for 15 min per gradient. After dehydration, the bacteria were transferred to a silicon wafer and dried using a critical point dryer. Finally, the dried samples were sputter-coated with a thin gold film and observed under a scanning electron microscope (SEM, HITACHI, Tokyo, Japan).

Internalisation and Biocompatibility of SE/S-CDs

Mouse connective tissue fibroblast cells (L929) and mouse leukemic monocyte macrophage cells (RAW264.7) were obtained from Pronosai company (Wuhan, China). Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) were acquired from Cell Bank/Stem Cell Bank, CEMCS, CAS. Unless otherwise specified, all experiments were performed using the following default concentrations: pbs, cysteine (50 μg/mL), selenocystine (50 μg/mL), SE/S-CDs (50 μg/mL), and lipopolysaccharide (LPS, 1 μg/mL).

Cell Uptake

L929 cells, HUVECs, and RAW264.7 cells were seeded into a 24-well plate at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well, and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a cell culture incubator until complete adherence. Subsequently, 50 μL of SE/S-CDs solution was added to each well, and the cells were further incubated for 24 h under the same culture conditions. After incubation, the culture medium was discarded and the cells were rinsed three times with PBS to eliminate uninternalized SE/S-CDs. The cells were subsequently fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature. Finally, the cells were observed and imaged with a laser scanning confocal microscope.

In Vitro Cytocompatibility of SE/S-CDs

L929 cells, HUVECs, and RAW264.7 cells were plated into 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 cells per well, and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a cell incubator. The initial medium was removed, and the cells were co-cultured with SE/S-CDs solutions (0, 25, 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL, prepared in complete DMEM medium) for 24 h under identical culture conditions.

For live/dead cell staining assay: After co-culture, the culture medium was aspirated, and the cells were rinsed twice with PBS. A calcein AM/PI working solution (1:1000 dilution in PBS) was supplemented into each well, and the cells were incubated for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Fluorescent images were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (Nikon Eclipse Ti, Japan).

For CCK-8 assay: After 24 h of co-culture, the original medium was removed, and 100 μL of fresh complete DMEM medium containing 10% CCK-8 reagent was added to each well. The plate was incubated for 2 h at 37 °C in the dark, and the absorbance at 450 nm was determined using a microplate reader.

Hemolysis Assay

Whole blood was collected from the retro-orbital venous plexus of mice, immediately transferred to heparin-coated tubes and mixed thoroughly. A 0.5 mL aliquot of the whole blood was gently mixed with 4.5 mL of 0.9% NaCl solution, and the mixture was centrifuged at 1500 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min. The supernatant was discarded, and the remaining red blood cells (RBC) were retained. The RBCs were washed with 0.9% NaCl solution (repeated 3–4 times) until the supernatant became clear. A 4% RBCs suspension was prepared by mixing 200 μL of washed RBCs with 5 mL of 0.9% NaCl solution. Aliquots of the suspension (1 mL each) were mixed with SE/S-CDs solutions of different concentrations (0, 25, 50, 100, and 200 μg/mL) in centrifuge tubes. Two control groups were set up: a negative control (1 mL RBC suspension + 1 mL 0.9% NaCl solution, no hemolysis) and a positive control (1 mL RBC suspension + 1 mL 1% Triton X-100 solution, complete hemolysis). All tubes were incubated for 2 h at 37 °C in a thermostatic shaking incubator (80 rpm). After incubation, the mixtures were centrifuged at 2000 rpm and 4 °C for 5 min, and 100 μL of supernatant from each tube was transferred to a 96-well plate. The absorbance at 540 nm was determined with a microplate reader.

Biological Functions of SE/S-CDs

In Vitro Assessment of Antioxidant Stress

Intracellular ROS levels were measured using the 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) fluorescent probe. L929 cells and RAW264.7 cells were plated into 24-well plates at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well, and incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a cell culture incubator. To establish an oxidative stress injury model, LPS was added to each well, and the cells were preincubated for 6 h. After LPS pretreatment, SE/S-CDs solutions were added, and the cells were further cultured for 24 h under the same conditions. The culture medium was discarded, and fresh medium containing DCFH-DA (diluted at a volume ratio of 1:1500 with serum-free DMEM) was added to each well. The cells were incubated for 20 min at 37 °C in the dark to avoid fluorescence quenching. Fluorescence images of the cells were captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope, and the fluorescence intensity were quantitatively analyzed using ImageJ software.

Scratch Assay

L929 cells and HUVECs were inoculated into a 6-well plate at a density of 3 × 105 cells per well, and were incubated overnight at 37 °C with 5% CO2 in a cell culture incubator. When the cells had fully adhered and reached 90% confluence, a sterile 200 μL pipet tip was used to make uniform scratches perpendicular to the horizontal axis of the well. The scratched confluent cell monolayers were gently rinsed three times with PBS buffer to remove non-adherent cells and cellular debris. Serum-free DMEM medium containing different samples was added to each well, and the cells were cultured continuously under the same incubator parameters. Images of the scratch wounds were captured at 0 and 24 h using an inverted optical microscope.

Tubule Formation Assay

A 96-well plate was prechilled on ice, with 50 μL Matrigel added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h for full solidification. HUVECs resuspended in serum-free DMEM were seeded onto solidified Matrigel at 2 × 104 cells per well. The plate was incubated at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 4 h to induce tubule formation. Tubule network images were captured via an inverted microscope, and tubule formation efficiency (tubule number, length, and branch points) was quantified using ImageJ software.

RNA Extraction and Quantitative qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from cells using a total RNA extraction kit (Aikrui, Changsha, China). The extracted total RNA was reverse-transcribed and subsequently amplified with a quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) system (Agilent Mx3005p, CA, U.S.A.) for fluorescent signal collection. The primer sequences employed in the experiment (General Biotechnology, Anhui, China) are detailed in Table S1. Relative gene expression levels were calculated by means of the 2–ΔΔC t method.

In Vivo Wound Healing Experiments

Infectious Wound Model

Twenty-four female C57BL/6 mice, aged 10 weeks, were utilized in this study. They were procured from the Animal Experiment Center of the Second Affiliated Hospital of Harbin Medical University. The mice were anesthetized using abdominal anesthesia, and a circular wound (6 mm) was created on the back of each mouse. These wounds were subsequently infected with S. aureus (100 μL, 107 CFU/mL, on day 1). The mice were randomly divided into four groups and treated with PBS, cysteine, selenocystine, and SE/S-CDs on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 post-infection. Throughout the treatment period, body weight and wound area changes were measured and recorded every other day.

Histology Analysis

Mice were euthanized on days 4 and 10 post-surgery. Skin tissues from the wound sites were collected for histological examination to assess the healing process. Concurrently, major organs were excised and subjected to HE staining to evaluate the biocompatibility/toxicity of SE/S-CDs. The tissues were fixed with 4% PFA for 24 h, followed by dehydration and paraffin embedding. Subsequently, HE and Masson staining were performed on the sections. Images were captured under a microscope and analyzed quantitatively using ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemical Analysis

This study utilized IHC staining techniques to assess wound healing. First, skin injury sections were subjected to routine dewaxing and hydration, followed by antigen retrieval via a high-pressure heating method. Following the blocking of endogenous peroxidase activity and non-specific antigens, the sections were incubated with primary antibodies (Ki67, VEGF, CD31, IL-6, and TNF-α, diluted at 1:100) at 4 °C overnight. Subsequently, secondary antibody incubation, DAB staining, and nuclear staining were performed sequentially. Finally, the slides were observed under a microscope, images were captured, and analysis was conducted using ImageJ software.

Statistical Analysis

GraphPad Prism software (version 10.1.2) was employed for statistical analyzes in this study. Data are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). For statistical comparisons, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA) and t tests were utilized. A p value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant, whereas “ns” denoted no significant difference.

Results and Discussion

Synthesis and Characterization of SE/S-CDs

In this study, SE/S-CDs with Se–S dynamic covalent bonds were synthesized via a hydrothermal reaction carried out at 60 °C for 48 h with selenocystine and cysteine as precursors.

The elemental composition of SE/S-CDs and the mechanism underlying Se–S bond formation were elucidated using XPS. After deconvolution of the C 1s spectrum, three characteristic peaks were identified: one at 284.6 eV corresponding to C–C bonds in the carbon skeleton, one at 285.7 eV corresponding to C–O/C–N bonds, and one at 287.7 eV corresponding to the CO bond (Figure S1). These findings indicate an abundance of oxygen/nitrogen functional groups on the surface of SE/S-CDs. In the Se 3d spectra (Figure a–c), the characteristic Se–Se peak (55.9 eV) derived from selenocystine was found to split into two peaks, corresponding to Se–Se (54.7 eV) and Se–S (56.9 eV), confirming the success of the Se–S metathesis reaction. The Se–S bond (56.9 eV) was clearly detected in the final product SE/S-CDs, verifying the successful construction of Se–S dynamic covalent bonds. On the basis of the deconvolution and integration of the Se 3d X-ray photoelectron spectra, the atomic ratio of Se–S to Se–Se bonds was calculated to be 1:4.22. Regarding the S 2p spectrum (Figure S2), the characteristic peak of the C–S bond in cysteine (162.9 eV) shifted to 163.4 eV in SE/S-CDs. This binding energy shift was closely related to the formation of C–S–Se bonds. On the basis of peak area integration, the atomic ratio of S–Se to S–S was calculated to be 1:3.41. Furthermore, SE/S-CDs were primarily composed of C, N, O, Se, and S (Figure d). Based on the ratio of peak areas to their respective sensitivity factors, the Se/S atomic ratio of SE/S-CDs was determined to be 1:2.48. Collectively, these results confirm the successful incorporation of Se–S bonds into SE/S-CDs.

The morphology and structure of SE/S-CDs were characterized using TEM and XRD. TEM revealed that SE/S-CDs formed uniformly dispersed spherical nanoparticles with an average particle size of 3.4 ± 0.1 nm (Figure e and f), confirming the ability of the hydrothermal method to enable the controllable synthesis of SE/S-CDs. Dynamic light scattering measurements showed that SE/S-CDs had a hydrodynamic diameter of 107.07 ± 3.52 nm in PBS. Further stability evaluations under acidic and alkaline conditions confirmed that the hydrodynamic diameter of SE/S-CDs remained stable, with no obvious aggregation or size variation observed (Figure S3). In the XRD pattern (Figure g), the broad diffraction peak at 2θ ≈ 25° corresponds to the (002) crystal plane of the graphite structure, and the interlayer spacing was calculated to be 0.36 nm, slightly higher than the standard graphite interlayer spacing (0.34 nm). This expansion is attributed to the lattice expansion of the carbon framework, induced by the introduction of Se and S as dopant atoms.

FTIR results (Figure h) revealed a broad absorption peak at 3042 cm–1, corresponding to the stretching vibrations of the N–H and O–H bonds. This indicates surface enrichment of hydroxyl and amino groups, providing a structural basis for the water solubility and functionalization of SE/S-CDs. The absorption peaks at 1042 and 1399 cm–1 were attributed to the stretching vibrations of C–Se and C–S bonds, respectively, confirming that the characteristic functional groups of the raw precursors were retained during CD synthesis. Zeta potential measurements (Figure i) indicated that cysteine exhibited a charge of +1.2 ± 1.0 mV, selenocystine exhibited a charge of −1.7 ± 0.6 mV, and SE/S-CDs exhibited a pronounced negative charge of −15.3 ± 1.5 mV. The negative charge of SE/S-CDs is primarily attributed to the enrichment of surface anionic functional groups during synthesis and the synergistic effects of heteroatom doping with selenium and sulfur. , This negative charge characteristic endows SE/S-CDs with exceptional stability and excellent biocompatibility.

The fluorescent properties of CDs offer considerable potential for biomedical applications. We used PL and UV–vis to examine the optical properties of SE/S-CDs. The results of PL (Figure j) indicated that at an excitation wavelength of 370 nm, the most intense emission peak of SE/S-CDs was observed at approximately 460 nm. In addition, the emission spectrum of SE/S-CDs underwent a redshift as the excitation wavelength increased (Figure k), exhibiting typical excitation-dependent fluorescence behavior. This characteristic is associated with the quantum confinement effect and surface energy trap states of CDs. UV–vis (Figure l) revealed that the moderate-to-low-intensity absorption band at 350–400 nm arises from n to π* transitions of sp2 conjugated structures, which corresponds to the characteristic absorption of oxygen-containing and heteroatom-doped functional groups on the surface of SE/S-CDs. Combined with the XRD results, these data confirm that the graphitized sp2 conjugated framework constitutes the core structure for fluorescence emission. Additionally, Se and S doping further enhances the tunability of the optical response of SE/S-CDs by modulating the electron cloud distribution and surface state density. FTIR and UV–vis measurements of SE/S-CDs under both different pH values and H2O2 treatment demonstrated their structural stability, corroborating the results of dynamic light scattering experiments (Figure S4).

Antibacterial Activity of SE/S-CDs

Persistent bacterial infection at the wound site may trigger severe pathological reactions, such as excessive inflammatory responses and vascular damage. Timely elimination of pathogenic bacteria facilitates the reconstruction of the dysregulated skin microenvironment and markedly enhances wound healing efficiency. In this study, we assayed the in vitro antibacterial activity of SE/S-CDs against S. aureus and E. coli using the agar plate colony counting method (Figure a and f). Abundant bacterial colonies were observed in the group treated with PBS, confirming that PBS exerted almost no bactericidal effect against these two pathogenic bacteria. By contrast, SE/S-CDs exhibited potent antibacterial activity, with antibacterial inhibition rates of 82.9 and 93.1% against S. aureus and E. coli, respectively. Live/dead bacterial staining assays corroborated the results of agar plate colony counting experiments by demonstrating that treatment with SE/S-CDs resulted in a significant increase in the number of dead bacteria (Figure c, d, and h).

To elucidate the underlying antibacterial mechanism, we conducted scanning electron microscopy to observe morphological changes in the surface structures of S. aureus and E. coli. Bacteria in the PBS and cysteine groups maintained intact cell membranes with smooth surfaces and typical morphological features (Figure e). By contrast, bacteria treated with selenocystine and SE/S-CDs presented severely damaged membranes, characterized by cytoplasmic membrane wrinkling, shrinkage, and rupture, which ultimately led to cytoplasmic leakage. ATP release assays further confirmed membrane integrity. Compared to the PBS group, both selenocystine and SE/S-CDs caused significant ATP elevation, indicating membrane disruption and subsequent ATP leakage (Figure S5). These results suggest that SE/S-CDs may exert bactericidal activity by disrupting the structural integrity of bacterial cell membranes.

The extensive clinical misuse of antibiotics has led to the emergence of multidrug-resistant bacterial strains, including MRSA. We found that SE/S-CDs exhibited pronounced concentration-dependent antibacterial activity against MRSA. The antibacterial inhibition rate against MRSA increased from 49.6 to 99.2% corresponding to an increase in SE/S-CDs concentration from 12.5 to 100 μg/mL (Figure S6).

The formation of bacterial biofilms contributes majorly to the persistence of drug-resistant bacterial infections. The physical contact between antimicrobial agents and bacterial cells is hindered by the presence of biofilms, which delays the healing of infected wounds. Previous studies have indicated that selenocystine can inhibit the growth of planktonic bacteria and biofilm formation, while also effectively eradicating preformed mature biofilms. Quantitative analysis of S. aureus biofilms using crystal violet staining revealed that SE/S-CDs reduced biofilm formation by approximately 50% compared with the PBS group (Figure b and g), demonstrating the ability of SE/S-CDs to effectively inhibit bacterial biofilm formation.

Biocompatibility of SE/S-CDs

TrxR is a well-characterized selenoprotein that plays key roles in diverse cellular processes, including promoting cell proliferation and regulating intracellular redox homeostasis. , The TrxR-like enzymatic activity of SE/S-CDs was evaluated using the reduction reaction of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate and 5,5′-dithiobis­(2-nitrobenzoic acid). The results showed that TrxR activity increased progressively with SE/S-CDs concentration (Figure S7).

Cellular uptake by endocytosis is a prerequisite for nanoparticles to exert their therapeutic efficacy. Fibroblasts, vascular endothelial cells, and immune cells are essential for vascularization and tissue healing in skin wounds. Therefore, we observed the endocytosis of SE/S-CDs by L929 cells, HUVECs, and RAW264.7 cells using confocal microscopy. SE/S-CDs emitted green fluorescence under blue light excitation (450 nm) and blue fluorescence under ultraviolet light excitation (365 nm). The fluorescence signals were uniformly distributed throughout the cytoplasm and negligibly detected in the nucleus (Figure e).

The evaluate the biocompatibility of SE/S-CDs, cells were co-cultured with SE/S-CDs (0–200 μg/mL), and cell viability was assessed using the Cell Counting Kit-8 assay. The findings revealed that SE/S-CDs did not impair the viability of L929 cells at concentrations up to 100 μg/mL and in fact, enhanced cell proliferation at 25–50 μg/mL (Figure a). To elucidate the mechanism underlying the promotion of cell proliferation, we examined the TrxR activity of the cells. The results showed that TrxR activity was significantly enhanced when the concentration of SE/S-CDs was in the range of 50–100 μg/mL. Accordingly, we propose that SE/S-CDs promote cell proliferation by enhancing TrxR activity (Figure d). By contrast, selenocystine and cysteine did not enhance cell proliferation. Notably, 50 μg/mL selenocystine markedly reduced cell viability to 59.2% (Figure S8a and b). Cytotoxicity assessments conducted on HUVECs and RAW264.7 cells yielded similar results (Figure b and c). Moreover, 50 μg/mL SE/S-CDs did not impair the viability of cells in a long-term culture spanning 5 days (Figure S8c). Live/dead staining further demonstrated the favorable biocompatibility of SE/S-CDs (Figure f and g). Additionally, the results of Live/dead staining revealed a considerable decrease in cell numbers in the selenocystine group, further supporting the inhibitory effects of selenocystine on cell growth (Figure S9).

In clinical translation, hemocompatibility, as a key aspect of biocompatibility, represents a core prerequisite for the safe in vivo use of nanomaterials. Therefore, we conducted a hemolysis assay to verify the compatibility of SE/S-CDs with blood cells. Compared with the positive control group (Triton X-100), the hemolysis rate of all SE/S-CDs groups (different concentrations) remained below 5% (Figure h), demonstrating the excellent hemocompatibility of SE/S-CDs.

Antioxidant and Anti-inflammatory Activities of SE/S-CDs

Bacterial infection can lead to excessive accumulation of ROS, which majorly contributes to delayed wound healing. Most existing studies have focused on the antibacterial properties of materials, often overlooking the inflammatory cascade triggered by oxidative stress. Excessive ROS can damage biomolecules, causing cell necrosis and exacerbating inflammatory cell infiltration. Therefore, the effective removal of ROS is essential for the healing of infected wounds.

The intracellular ROS scavenging activity of SE/S-CDs was evaluated using the DCFH-DA fluorescent probe. L929 cells and RAW264.7 cells were pretreated with SE/S-CDs and subsequently stimulated with LPS to mimic an oxidative microenvironment. Fluorescence microscopy analysis revealed that the LPS group displayed markedly increased green fluorescence intensity, corresponding to an increase in ROS levels by 79.1% compared with the prestimulation group. By contrast, the fluorescence intensity of the group pretreated with SE/S-CDs was only 5.3% (Figure a and e), demonstrating the outstanding ROS scavenging capacity of SE/S-CDs.

Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) is a core transcription factor that regulates the cellular antioxidant response. It activates the transcription of a series of antioxidant genes, such as those encoding NAD­(P)­H:quinone oxidoreductase 1 (Nqo1) and heme oxygenase 1, by binding to the antioxidant response element. The transcription factor Forkhead box o1 (Foxo1) can also indirectly scavenge intracellular ROS by upregulating the expression of antioxidant enzymes. Furthermore, Foxo1 interacts synergistically with the Nrf2/antioxidant response element pathway through TAZ protein-mediated regulatory mechanisms, jointly enhancing cellular antioxidant capacity. Using qPCR, we demonstrated that SE/S-CDs upregulated the mRNA expression of Nrf2, Nqo1, and Foxo1 in a dose-dependent manner (Figure S10). This effect is attributed to the ability of SE/S-CDs to alleviate oxidative stress damage by enhancing TrxR enzyme activity and restoring intracellular redox homeostasis.

Under inflammatory conditions, the migratory capacity of L929 cells is typically inhibited. The results of scratch wound healing assay showed that LPS stimulation reduced the migration rate to 56.3% after 24 h, significantly lower than the 83.8% migration rate observed in the blank control group. This result suggests that ROS impedes cell migration and delays scratch healing. After treatment with SE/S-CDs, the cell migration rate recovered to 87.6% (Figure b and f), further confirming that SE/S-CDs reverse the oxidative stress–induced inhibition of cell migration by efficiently scavenging ROS. Furthermore, we investigated the anti-inflammatory effects of SE/S-CDs on L929 cells under inflammatory conditions. SE/S-CDs downregulated the expression levels of the pro-inflammatory cytokines interleukin 1β (IL-1β) and interleukin 6 (IL-6) in a concentration-dependent manner, while promoting the secretion of the anti-inflammatory cytokine interleukin 10 (IL-10) (Figure S11).

During the inflammatory phase, macrophages are predominantly polarized toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype. Therefore, to precisely evaluate the therapeutic potential of SE/S-CDs in this phase, we established an LPS-induced M1 macrophage polarization model and examined the capacity of SE/S-CDs to induce macrophage polarization toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype. After 24 h of LPS stimulation, macrophages co-cultured with SE/S-CDs showed a significant decrease in tumor necrosis factor α (TNF-α) fluorescence intensity and an increase in mannose receptor c-type 1 (CD206) fluorescence intensity, indicating a substantial phenotypic shift from the M1 to the M2 phenotype (Figure c and d). IF staining results were corroborated by the findings from qPCR (Figure g). Compared with the LPS group, macrophages in the SE/S-CDs group exhibited lower mRNA expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including inducible nitric oxide synthase (INOS) and IL-1β, and higher mRNA expression levels of anti-inflammatory cytokines, such as IL-10 and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β).

To gain mechanistic insights into the anti-inflammatory effects of SE/S-CDs, we further investigated the regulatory role of nuclear factor κB (NF-κB), a well-characterized master regulator of inflammatory responses and macrophage polarization. The results showed that SE/S-CDs could inhibit the activation of NF-κB (Figure S12). Collectively, these findings indicate that SE/S-CDs exert their anti-inflammatory effects through a dual regulatory mechanism involving the activation of Nrf2 and the inhibition of NF-κB.

In Vitro Proangiogenic Activity of SE/S-CDs

Vascular regeneration is a critical hallmark in the shift from the inflammatory phase to the repair phase of wound healing. It involves the proliferation and migration of endothelial cells and the cooperative action of proangiogenic factors. The impact of SE/S-CDs on the migration of HUVECs was evaluated using the scratch assay and Transwell migration assay. The results of the scratch assay indicated that the cell migration rate in the group treated with SE/S-CDs reached 65.6% within 24 h, significantly higher than the 33.4% migration rate observed in the blank control group (Figure a and d). In the Transwell assay, the number of migrated cells in the group treated with SE/S-CDs was 141.1% of that in the control group within 24 h, with cells densely distributed on the lower surface of the membrane (Figure b and g). Both experiments demonstrated the capacity of SE/S-CDs to significantly enhance the migration of HUVECs.

Angiogenesis is a pivotal process during wound healing, involving the formation, extension, and branching of tubular structures, as well as the formation of interconnected networks. In the vascular tube formation assay, the SE/S-CDs group exhibited a significantly higher number of branches and longer total branch length compared with the control group after 6 h (Figure c and e), demonstrating the proangiogenic capacity of SE/S-CDs. Additionally, SE/S-CDs exerted their proangiogenic effects in a dose-dependent manner (Figure S13).

To further assess the angiogenic potential of SE/S-CDs, we analyzed the expression of vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) and platelet endothelial cell adhesion molecule-1 (CD31) using IF staining. The results demonstrated a marked increase in the fluorescence intensities of VEGF and CD31 in HUVECs following treatment with SE/S-CDs (Figure f and Figure S14), indicating that SE/S-CDs promote the expression of angiogenic proteins in endothelial cells. qPCR analysis further showed that the mRNA expression levels of proangiogenic genes, including VEGF, fibroblast growth factor (FGF), CD31, and platelet-derived growth factor (PDGF), were significantly elevated in HUVECs treated with SE/S-CDs (Figure h). Furthermore, this upregulation was dose-dependent (Figure S15), which is consistent with the dose-dependent proangiogenic activity observed in the vascular tube formation assays.

In Vivo Wound Healing Efficacy of SE/S-CDs

After establishing the antibacterial, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and proangiogenic activities of SE/S-CDs in vitro, we evaluated the therapeutic efficacy of SE/S-CDs in a mouse model of infected cutaneous wounds on the dorsal skin. The experimental workflow is depicted in Figure a. Figure b–d shows representative photographs of the wound on days 1, 3, 5, 7, and 10, along with a quantitative analysis of wound closure rates. On day 1, no significant differences in initial wound area were observed among the treatment groups. During the healing period from days 3 to 10, all groups exhibited a dynamic decrease in wound area, but the group treated with SE/S-CDs exhibited a faster healing rate than the other treatment groups. IHC staining was performed to evaluate the expression of Ki67, a marker of cell proliferation. Compared with the control group, the SE/S-CDs group exhibited a larger Ki67-positive area, which was consistent with the observed wound healing trend (Figure S16).

Before conducting pathological skin sectioning, we evaluated the in vivo safety of the treatments. The major organs in all the treatment groups presented normal structures without any significant pathological alterations (Figure S17). Moreover, no notable changes were recorded in the body weight of the mice during the treatment period, suggesting that SE/S-CDs display good in vivo biocompatibility (Figure e). Subsequently, we evaluated the histological status of the wound tissues on days 4 and 10 using hematoxylin and eosin staining and Masson staining. Hematoxylin and eosin staining revealed that the control group exhibited a relatively high number of neutrophils owing to persistent bacterial infection-induced inflammation. By contrast, the SE/S-CDs group exhibited the fewest neutrophils, demonstrating the potent anti-inflammatory properties of SE/S-CDs and their ability to effectively facilitate wound healing (Figure a and Figure S18). Masson staining indicated that collagen deposition was minimal on day 4 but apparent in all groups by day 10 postwounding. Collagen fibers appeared loose and disorganized in the control group, whereas they appeared thicker, denser, and more regularly arranged in the SE/S-CDs group (Figure b and g and Figure S19).

Figure c–f illustrates the results of IHC staining to detect the expression of VEGF, CD31, IL-6, and TNF-α in wound tissues. Compared with the control group, the SE/S-CDs group featured larger VEGF-positive and CD31-positive areas and smaller IL-6 and TNF-α-positive areas (Figure h–k). Taken together, these results demonstrate that SE/S-CDs can alleviate inflammatory responses and promote vascular regeneration in infected wounds.

Transcriptomic Analysis of SE/S-CDs-Treated Wound Tissues

We compared the gene expression profiles of the control and SE/S-CDs groups using transcriptomic sequencing, identifying 464 differentially expressed genes (DEGs), of which 418 were upregulated and 46 downregulated (screening criteria: p < 0.05 and |log2(fold-change)| > 1; Figure a and Figure S20).

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) revealed significant enrichment of the oxidative phosphorylation pathway and HIF-1α signaling pathway in the SE/S-CDs group (Figure b and e). Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that an inflammatory microenvironment impairs oxidative phosphorylation by inhibiting the activity of mitochondrial complex I, leading to electron leakage and excessive ROS production. , ROS oxidatively modifies thioredoxin, impairing its binding to thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP). This promotes thioredoxin-interacting protein to bind to the NOD-like receptor pyrin domain-containing three inflammasome, triggering the release of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and forming an “inflammation–oxidative damage” vicious cycle. , To validate whether SE/S-CDs can regulate oxidative phosphorylation, we first constructed a functional inhibition model using rotenone, a complex I-specific inhibitor. Subsequent gene expression analysis revealed that the expression of anti-inflammatory mediators (including TGF-β and Arg-1) was upregulated in the SE/S-CDs group compared with the SE/S-CDs + rotenone cotreatment group, suggesting that SE/S-CDs may exert anti-inflammatory effects through the oxidative phosphorylation pathway (Figure g). We further investigated the effects of SE/S-CDs on mitochondrial function. Under inflammatory conditions, the LPS group exhibited a significant increase in mitochondrial membrane potential depolarization, which was attenuated by SE/S-CDs treatment. Additionally, the expression of genes related to mitochondrial respiratory chain complex I genes was upregulated following SE/S-CDs treatment (Figure h, k, and m). Notably, the upregulation of the HIF-1α signaling pathway appears to contradict oxidative phosphorylation activation, but this may reflect the ability of SE/S-CDs to regulate HIF-1α via non-hypoxic mechanisms; for example, low levels of ROS or metabolic byproducts may stabilize HIF-1α protein by inhibiting prolyl hydroxylase or activating the PI3K/AKT signaling pathway, without the accompanying classic inhibition of oxidative phosphorylation. This synergistic regulation may promote inflammatory repair by enhancing mitochondrial function and angiogenesis. The regulatory effect of SE/S-CDs on the HIF-1α signaling pathway was validated in vitro using WB and IF staining. The results of IF staining showed that the fluorescence intensity of HIF-1α was elevated in the SE/S-CDs group (Figure c and j). Consistent with this, WB analysis demonstrated that the expression levels of both HIF-1α and VEGF were upregulated (Figure i and l).

The identified DEGs were subjected to Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis. The results of this analysis showed that DEGs were significantly enriched in the interleukin-17 (IL-17) signaling pathway and the TGF-β signaling pathway (Figure d). IL-17 is a multifunctional pro-inflammatory cytokine that plays a crucial role in key biological processes, including host immune defense, tissue repair, and the pathogenesis of inflammatory diseases. The TGF-β signaling pathway plays a core regulatory role in the initiation, maintenance, and resolution of inflammation by dynamically balancing pro-inflammatory and anti-inflammatory effects. WB was performed to detect the protein expression levels of inflammation-related markers. The results showed that the SE/S-CDs group exhibited significantly decreased expression of pro-inflammatory markers and increased expression of anti-inflammatory markers (Figure S21). These findings suggest that SE/S-CDs promote tissue repair by exerting anti-inflammatory effects, which is consistent with our previous in vitro and in vivo experimental findings.

Gene ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that in the “biological process” category, the DEGs were predominantly enriched in tissue regeneration and cellular structure regulation; in the “cellular component” category, they were enriched in epithelial cell–specific structures and extracellular microenvironment regulation; in the “molecular function” category, they were enriched in metabolic enzyme activity, redox activity, and ion homeostasis regulation (Figure f). Hair follicle stem cells (HFSCs) play the crucial role of key “seed cells” in skin tissue regeneration. The skin tissue regeneration–promoting effects of SE/S-CDs were further validated through in vitro experiments. 5-Ethynyl-2′-deoxyuridine (EdU) staining indicated that the proliferation rate of HFSCs was significantly higher in the SE/S-CDs group (47.0%) than in the control group (25.4%; Figure S22), confirming that SE/S-CDs can promote the proliferation of HFSCs. Furthermore, the mRNA levels of pluripotency and proliferation markers (Lef1, Klf4, Sox2, Ki67, Pcna, and Ctnnb1) were elevated in HFSCs, as assessed by qPCR (Figure S23).

Finally, through protein–protein interaction network analysis using the STRING database (Figure S24), we identified key targets associated with wound healing, including IL-17, CCL17, FGF5, FGF22, and Lfng. Among these, FGF5 and FGF22 are implicated in hair follicle development and angiogenesis; IL-17 plays a critical role in the regulation of inflammatory responses; and Lfng modulates stem cell differentiation through the Notch signaling pathway. These core node proteins and their corresponding genes provide novel insights and therapeutic avenues for exploring the potential of SE/S-CDs in the treatment of infected wounds.

Conclusion

This study successfully fabricated a multifunctional SE/S-CDs nanozyme. This nanozyme not only exhibits the catalytic activity of TrxR but also integrates the multifaceted biological activity of selenium, effectively promoting the healing of infected wounds. Specifically, the nanozyme exhibited outstanding antibacterial and ROS-scavenging activities, promoted the polarization of pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages toward the anti-inflammatory M2 phenotype, and significantly enhanced endothelial cell proliferation, migration, and angiogenesis. Through topical administration, the nanozyme significantly accelerated the healing of infected wounds in a mouse model. Transcriptomic analysis revealed that SE/S-CDs upregulate the expression of oxidative phosphorylation and HIF-1α signaling pathways. Therefore, this multifunctional nanozyme provides an innovative strategy for developing novel multifunctional nanomedicines for treating infections and oxidative stress–related diseases.
