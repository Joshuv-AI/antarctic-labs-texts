Comorbidity Between Recurrent Major Depressive Disorder and CADASIL: A Case Report With a Brief Review of Published Cases
Authors: Yi‐meng Wang, Hong‐ye Yu, Ling Zhang, Chen Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) may underlie mood disorders by directly affecting cerebral vascular function and neural networks, thereby contributing to or worsening mood symptoms. The co‐occurrence of CADASIL and major depressive disorder (MDD) in clinical practice complicates both diagnosis and treatment. We report a case of a 42‐year‐old woman with an 11‐year history of recurrent MDD who presented with atypical depressive symptoms that required hospital admission. Subsequent assessment during hospitalization revealed persistent headaches and abnormal findings on cranial MRI. Given her familial predisposition and previous genetic analysis, a diagnosis of CADASIL was established. After a 3‐week course of psychiatric intervention, notable amelioration was observed in both affective and neurological manifestations. The patient demonstrated limited tolerance to psychotropic medications during the treatment. Timely intervention can improve prognosis; however, emotional symptoms may persist, requiring continuous monitoring and management. We also review previously reported CADASIL cases with psychiatric manifestations to provide context.Keywords: atypical depressive symptoms, CADASIL, major depressive disorder, NOTCH3 gene, white matter lesions

Summary

In clinical practice, cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) should be considered in individuals presenting with mood disturbances, particularly those with a family history of the condition and significant white matter lesions.

Introduction

The NOTCH3 gene encodes a transmembrane receptor protein essential for the function of vascular smooth muscle cells [1]. Mutations, altered expression, or dysregulation of its activity have been extensively associated with various human diseases [2]. Previous studies have demonstrated that mutations in the NOTCH3 gene are a major cause of cerebral autosomal dominant arteriopathy with subcortical infarcts and leukoencephalopathy (CADASIL) [3, 4, 5, 6]. CADASIL is a genetically and phenotypically diverse condition affecting molecular pathways in endothelial, smooth muscle, and neuronal cells, essential for cellular homeostasis, nutrient transport, and vascular reactivity [7]. Clinically, CADASIL is characterized by transient ischemic attacks and recurrent ischemic strokes, cognitive decline, migraine with aura, mood disturbance, apathy, and diffuse white matter lesions and subcortical infarcts on neuroimaging, whose onset and overall survival may vary based on the type of pathogenic variant and its location within NOTCH3 [8, 9, 10]. In adult patients with early‐stage CADASIL, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) typically demonstrates white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), primarily in the anterior temporal lobe, external capsule, and superior frontal gyrus [7, 11]. Several studies have reported an increase in WMHs in patients with bipolar and major depressive disorder (MDD) [12, 13]. A meta‐analysis suggested that individuals with bipolar disorder have a higher risk of developing WMHs and cerebral small vessel disease, which may share common pathophysiological mechanisms, compared to healthy controls [14].

Because neurological symptoms predominate in CADASIL, its psychiatric symptoms are often overlooked. Approximately 20%–41% of CADASIL patients exhibit psychiatric disorders, with mood disorders being the most prevalent, affecting about 20% of individuals [15, 16]. MDD is a severe mental illness characterized by persistent low mood, loss of interest, and decreased energy, and is one of the leading causes of disability worldwide. Some patients exhibit atypical depression, characterized by mood reactivity, hyperphagia, hypersomnia, leaden paralysis, and heightened sensitivity to interpersonal rejection [17]. Structural and functional brain abnormalities in MDD patients have been widely reported [18]. One study reported that individuals with atypical depression exhibited reduced white matter integrity in specific brain regions [17, 19].

The comorbidity of CADASIL and mood disorders suggests complex interactions, though it remains unclear whether mood disturbances are a direct consequence of CADASIL or an independent comorbid condition. CADASIL may impair vascular function and neural networks, potentially contributing to or exacerbating mood symptoms. Notably, psychiatric manifestations may even serve as the initial clinical presentation in some patients with CADASIL [20]. Currently, research on mood disorders associated with CADASIL remains limited. The comorbidity of CADASIL with MDD increases diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. We report a case of CADASIL initially manifesting as MDD, aiming to advance understanding of mood disorders in a cerebrovascular context and to provide insights for therapeutic strategies and mechanistic investigations.

Case History

A 42‐year‐old woman had a history of mood disorder for up to 11 years. She first experienced a postpartum depressive episode in 2013, which remitted after 6 months. Since then, she has had intermittent depression, anxiety, and insomnia, but has not received standardized psychiatric treatment. The patient has a long history of headaches and a family history of cerebrovascular disease. Her mother suffered a cerebral infarction at the age of 40 and experienced a gradual cognitive decline at the age of 66. In 2020, genetic testing confirmed NOTCH3 mutations in both the patient and her mother.

On day 1, the patient presented with mood instability, anxiety, insomnia, dizziness, and recurrent headaches persisting for more than 1 month. Physical examination revealed no obvious abnormalities, and the headaches fluctuated with emotional state and sleep disturbance. Cognitive decline was also observed, with deficits in processing speed, episodic memory, and visuospatial abilities. The 17‐item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD) score was 19, indicating moderate depression. Genetic testing identified two NOTCH3 deletion variants: c.586_606del (p.N196_A202del) and c.512_583del (p.G171_C194del), both within the EGF‐like repeat domain (Figure 1). According to the report, these variants were classified as variants of uncertain significance (VUS) under ACMG/AMP criteria (PM2 + PM4). Although pathogenicity was not definitively established, their location within a conserved domain, absence from population databases, and co‐segregation with disease in the family support their possible clinical relevance. A head MRI conducted on day 3 found lacunar infarctions in the right basal ganglia and multiple white matter lesions (Figure 2).

Sanger sequencing validation of familial variants. (A) A heterozygous variant was identified in the patient's mother; (B) The same heterozygous variant was confirmed in the patient.

The patient's brain magnetic resonance imaging, performed with T2‐weighted Turbo Spin Echo (T2 TSE) and T2‐weighted Turbo Inversion Recovery Magnitude (T2 TIRM, dark‐fluid) sequences, showed multiple patchy hyperintense lesions in the right basal ganglia, bilateral radial crowns, bilateral semiovoid centers, and bilateral parietal lateral ventricles, consistent with lacunar infarctions in the right basal ganglia and multiple white matter lesions.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis primarily considered recurrent major depressive disorder (rMDD), consistent with the diagnostic criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5), characterized by emotional reactivity, interpersonal sensitivity, and psychomotor fatigue. The patient also experienced persistent headaches that worsened during episodes of mood instability, suggesting a close association with affective fluctuations rather than a typical migraine pattern. Because of cognitive deficits, extensive WMHs on MRI, and a strong family history of cerebrovascular disease, vascular depression was also considered. The diagnosis of CADASIL was supported by the presence of NOTCH3 variants within a conserved EGF‐like repeat domain, a positive family history, and characteristic neuroimaging findings of lacunar infarctions and diffuse white matter lesions.

Mood disturbances are part of the neuropsychiatric manifestations of CADASIL, and distinguishing them from primary mood disorders or other disease‐related symptoms is clinically complex. In this patient, the recurrent, temporally earlier depressive episodes that fulfilled DSM‐5 criteria, together with headaches linked to affective instability, support a diagnosis of rMDD comorbid with CADASIL, rather than a purely CADASIL‐related affective presentation.

Other neurodegenerative disorders, such as Alzheimer's disease and frontotemporal dementia, were excluded based on clinical presentation and neuroimaging findings, leading to a final diagnosis of rMDD comorbid with CADASIL.

Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

On day 1, the patient began treatment with lithium carbonate, starting at a dose of 0.25 g, with a maximum daily dose of 0.5 g, and the blood lithium concentration was 0.89 mmol/L by day 19. On day 3, quetiapine 50 mg was added. However, the patient developed dizziness and lower limb ataxia after quetiapine administration. Despite reducing the dose, the symptoms persisted, suggesting an intolerance to quetiapine, and the medication was subsequently discontinued. On day 6, aripiprazole 5 mg was initiated, with a maximum daily dose of 10 mg (blood concentration within the therapeutic range by day 19). The patient's symptoms showed significant improvement, including mood stability, a reduction of crying, and the complete resolution of headaches. However, she developed acute extrapyramidal symptoms, likely as a side effect of aripiprazole. Consequently, the dose was reduced to 7.5 mg, and 2 mg of biperiden was added to manage the extrapyramidal symptoms. Due to intermittent mood fluctuations, valproate 250 mg was initiated on day 16. The patient developed mild lower limb ataxia following valproate administration, which resolved over time.

During the treatment, the patient also received lorazepam (1.0 mg–1.5 mg per day) and zopiclone (7.5 mg per day) to alleviate anxiety and improve sleep. A comprehensive treatment approach was implemented, integrating transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), humanistic psychotherapy, group psychotherapy, and various traditional Chinese medicine techniques, including herbal patch application to acupuncture points, ear acupoint pellet therapy, and foot soaking with Chinese herbal decoctions. The patient's mood symptoms gradually improved, and her HAMD score decreased to 3 at the time of discharge. She was discharged on day 24 in stable condition.

However, clinical observations revealed that the patient was more prone to side effects compared to other patients. Two months after initiating combination therapy with lithium carbonate, valproate, and aripiprazole, the patient developed medication‐related adverse effects, including photophobia, epiphora, hair loss, and akathisia. Following therapeutic regimen adjustment, the patient subsequently transitioned to a maintenance phase with valproate (0.25 g per day) in combination with benzodiazepines. The patient we reported is in the early stages of CADASIL and has a comorbid mood disorder. Following comprehensive and systematic psychiatric treatment, the patient's mood showed significant improvement in the short term; however, residual emotional symptoms persist.

Discussion

We conducted a focused literature review of previously published CADASIL cases presenting with psychiatric manifestations up to January 12, 2025, identified through searches in PubMed, Web of Science, Embase, and Cochrane, supplemented by a manual review of the reference lists from the retrieved articles. The search strategy included combinations of the following keywords: (NOTCH3 OR “Notch‐3” OR “NOTCH3 gene” OR CADASIL) AND (“mood disorder” OR “mental disorder” OR “affective disorder” OR “bipolar disorder” OR “depression” OR “mania”). A total of 17 case reports specifically detailed mental disorders associated with CADASIL, as shown in Table 1. Among the 19 patients reported with mental disorders linked to CADASIL, 11 were male and 8 were female. These patients exhibited various psychiatric symptoms, including pathological gambling (1 case), schizophrenia spectrum disorders (3 cases), bipolar disorder (4 cases), hypomanic episode (1 case), and MDD (10 cases). Across the reported cases, various mutations in the NOTCH3 gene have been reported, including p.Arg133Cys, p.Tyr1021Cys, p.Arg90Cys, p.Arg159Cys, p.Arg61Pro, p.Arg587Cys, and p.Arg61Trp. In these cases, various psychiatric medications were used across patients, including aripiprazole, quetiapine, clozapine, olanzapine, risperidone, flupentixol decanoate, amitriptyline, sertraline, St John's Wort, valproate, lithium carbonate, and bromazepam. Additionally, one case involved noninvasive physical therapy.

Although CADASIL is classically neurological, mood disorders are increasingly recognized as important and may precede stroke events in some cases [19]. This case report described a patient with rMDD and CADASIL, who presented with atypical depressive symptoms accompanied by affective fluctuation–related headaches. Notably, the patient carried two novel deletion mutations in the NOTCH3 gene: p.N196_A202del and p.G171_C194del, which had not been previously reported in the literature. These findings may provide novel insights into the genetic link between mood disorders and CADASIL, and further studies are needed to understand the specific role of these mutations in the manifestation of psychiatric symptoms.

Interestingly, the clinical features of the patient's mother included recurrent dizziness, cerebral infarction, and cognitive deficits, which are common in CADASIL. Although the patient carried the same NOTCH3 gene mutations, her initial presentation was with mood symptoms. This difference suggests that the highly heterogeneous clinical manifestations of mutation carriers within a family could reflect the diverse effects of mutations in CADASIL [34].

According to the Human Gene Mutation Database (HGMD), more than 300 NOTCH3 mutations have been identified in CADASIL patients, including missense mutations (approximately 95%), splice mutations, and deletion mutations [32]. A recent review reported six common NOTCH3 missense mutations globally: p.R75P, p.R133C, p.R141C, p.R169C, p.R182C, and p.R544C, with p.R75P, p.R141C, p.R182C, and p.R544C genotypes associated with WMHs on MRI, of which the p.R141C mutation was associated with increased disease severity [3]. To further explore the relationship between genetic mutations and psychiatric manifestations in CADASIL, we conducted a literature review of 19 published case reports (summarized in Table 1). These cases involved various NOTCH3 mutations associated with major depressive disorder [24, 25, 30, 32, 33], bipolar disorder [11, 26, 29], schizophrenia‐spectrum disorders [20, 21], and pathological gambling [28].

These reports suggest a potential association between specific NOTCH3 mutations and particular psychiatric manifestations. However, the relationship between genetic factors and psychiatric disorders in CADASIL is complex, and further research is needed to clarify the precise role of individual mutations in the onset of psychiatric symptoms. Additionally, the interaction between vascular changes and genetic mutations in the progression of psychiatric symptoms remains an area of active investigation. For instance, one study using MRI hyperintensity as an endophenotype and NOTCH3 gene analysis in a family with a high incidence of bipolar disorder found no association between NOTCH3 and bipolar disorder, indicating that bipolar disorder likely has multifactorial mechanisms [35].

Mutations in the NOTCH3 gene may impair the function of vascular smooth muscle cells, potentially leading to chronic cerebral hypoperfusion, pathological vascular remodeling, and progressive white matter damage [2]. CADASIL resulting from NOTCH3 gene mutations could primarily manifest as mood disorders in some patients, including depression and bipolar disorder [11, 23, 33]. CADASIL is responsible for widespread white matter lesions associated with lacunar infarctions in various subcortical areas [16]. The severity and progression of CADASIL correlate closely with neuroimaging findings, reflecting the extent of brain involvement [36]. Numerous neuroimaging studies have confirmed extensive alterations in the structure and function of gray and white matter in patients with mood disorders [18, 37, 38, 39]. One study suggested that WMHs may be significantly associated with depression [40]. Another study reported that individuals with atypical depression exhibited reduced white matter integrity in specific brain regions [17, 19]. Despite significant research, the neurobiological mechanisms linking CADASIL to mood disorders remain not fully understood.

Notably, the patient's first depressive episode occurred after parturition. Although this temporal association raises the possibility that physiological or vascular changes during the postpartum period may have contributed to mood symptoms, the available evidence is insufficient to establish causality. Structural and functional changes in the brain during the postpartum period may increase the risk of psychiatric disorders in individuals [41]. Neuroimaging studies have identified that postpartum women with depression exhibited abnormalities in the default mode network, amygdala, and other brain structures and functions [42]. A documented case revealed that the prothrombotic state of pregnancy might precipitate early cerebrovascular manifestations in CADASIL patients presenting with postpartum depression as the initial symptom [31]. Additionally, a small number of reports have described patients presenting with schizophrenia‐like symptoms [21, 27]. These observations indicate that the postpartum period may represent a period of increased neurovascular vulnerability, but in the present case, this association remains speculative and should be interpreted with caution.

However, in the absence of typical CADASIL clinical manifestations, such as neurologic signs, the condition may be misdiagnosed or missed and remains poorly understood in the literature [43]. Therefore, MRI findings and family history are critical for diagnosis. When psychiatric symptoms are accompanied by neurological symptoms or a relevant family history, along with evident white matter lesions, a diagnosis of CADASIL should be considered [22, 23].

The treatment should follow best general clinical practice [44]. Treatment of mood symptoms might significantly improve the quality of life in small vessel disease [45]. The patient exhibited atypical features and agitation that had a risk of developing bipolar disorder, so antidepressants should be used with caution during the treatment. In this case, the patient presented with clinical symptoms of atypical depression, including irritability, impulse control, and inattention, and received a comprehensive treatment. We used mood stabilizers, antipsychotic medications, and anxiolytics for drug treatment. Aripiprazole has partial agonism toward the dopamine 2 receptor (D2) and does not impair cognitive function, which is approved by the US Food and Drug Administration (US FDA) for the adjunctive treatment of MDD [46]. Lithium carbonate, as a mood stabilizer, is commonly used as an adjunctive treatment for MDD patients and possesses neuroprotective effects [47]. The anticonvulsant valproate, which may robustly activate the extracellular signal‐regulated kinase (ERK) mitogen‐activated protein kinase cascade, might exert cytoprotective effects on vascular smooth muscle cells through the Notch3/cellular Fas‐associated death domain‐like interleukin‐1‐beta‐converting enzyme‐inhibitory protein (c‐FLIP) pathway [48].

Conclusion

This case report presented a patient with rMDD and CADASIL, who predominantly exhibited atypical depressive symptoms and was at risk of manic conversion. The patient's first depressive episode occurred postpartum; however, this association should be regarded as a clinical observation rather than evidence of causality. Further studies are needed to clarify potential links between perinatal factors and mood manifestations in CADASIL. The patient carried novel deletion mutations in the NOTCH3 gene, p.N196_A202del and p.G171_C194del, which may provide new insights into the genetic mechanisms underlying mood disorders in CADASIL. Additionally, the patient exhibited widespread white matter degeneration in the brain and lacunar infarcts in the right basal ganglia. These changes in brain structure and function likely represented the underlying neurological pathology. In clinical practice, CADASIL should be considered in the diagnosis of patients presenting primarily with mood symptoms, especially when there is a family history of the condition and significant white matter lesions. The patient was treated with comprehensive psychiatric care but showed poor tolerance to the medications. Although early intervention can improve prognosis for patients with comorbid CADASIL and MDD, residual emotional symptoms persist, highlighting the need for ongoing management and individualized care.
