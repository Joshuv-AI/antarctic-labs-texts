Associations between cardiac adipose tissue volume, measures of coronary calcification, myocardial microvascular function and systemic inflammation in individuals with type 2 diabetes free of overt cardiovascular disease
Authors: Morten Steen Jensen, Ida K. B. Rasmussen, Anne-Cathrine Skriver-Møller, Philip Hasbak, Martin B. Blond, Adam Chebli, Victor S. Wasehuus, Mats C. H. Lassen, Morten Lindhardt, Allan Kofoed-Enevoldsen, Urd L. Kielgast, Emilie H. Zobel, Lene Holmvang, Tor Biering-Sørensen, Peter Rossing, Rasmus S. Ripa, Andreas Kjaer, Tine W. Hansen

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPeri- and epicardial adipose tissue (PEAT) is a metabolically active fat depot surrounding the heart and coronary arteries that may contribute to vascular dysfunction and inflammation in type 2 diabetes (T2D). We examined associations between PEAT volume, coronary calcification (CACS), myocardial flow reserve (MFR), and systemic inflammation (hsCRP).MethodsCross-sectional analysis of adults with T2D without overt CVD recruited 2020–2023. PEAT was quantified on non-contrast CT; CACS was calculated by the Agatston method; MFR was measured by rubidium-82 PET/CT; hsCRP assessed systemic inflammation. Associations were tested using multivariable regression adjusting for cardiovascular risk factors.ResultsAmong 887 participants (mean age 65 years; 70% male; mean BMI 30 kg/m2; median PEAT 199 mL [IQR 133–283]), higher PEAT (per 100 mL) was associated with lower MFR (β − 0.04; 95% CI − 0.09 to 0.00; p = 0.041) and higher hsCRP, corresponding to a 38% increase (95% CI 28% to 48%; p < 0.001) in age- and sex-adjusted models, but not with CACS > 300 (OR 1.01; 95% CI 0.89–1.15). After further adjustment for diabetes duration, BMI, LDL cholesterol, systolic blood pressure, HbA1c, kidney function, smoking, and albuminuria, only the association with hsCRP remained significant at 14% (95%; CI 4% to 25%; p = 0.005).ConclusionIn individuals with T2D without overt CVD, PEAT volume was not independently associated with CACS or MFR after multivariable adjustment. Higher PEAT volume was associated with higher hsCRP, suggesting a relationship with systemic inflammation; however, this exploratory finding should be interpreted in the context of established links between adiposity and inflammatory burden.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12933-026-03298-7.

Background

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is the leading cause of morbidity and mortality among people with type 2 diabetes (T2D). As the incidence of T2D continues to rise, the burden of CVD is also increasing, leading to a growing number of individuals living with CVD-related complications [1–3]. A key factor in improving outcome is an early identification of individuals at the highest risk of CVD, enabling timely intervention and reducing the progression of vascular complications [3, 4].

Pericardial and epicardial adipose tissue (PEAT) is a metabolically active subtype of visceral fat located between the myocardium and visceral pericardium surrounding the coronary arteries. A significant body of evidence has established a correlation between an increased volume of PEAT and an elevated risk of cardiovascular disease [5, 6]. PEAT may play a physiological role through paracrine secretion of adipokines, regulation of cardiac energy metabolism as well as pro- and anti-inflammatory modulation [7, 8]. In individuals with T2D the enlargement of PEAT may perturb these functions, leading to a pro-inflammatory state that could promote coronary atherosclerosis and compromise myocardial perfusion [9].

Other markers of cardiovascular risk include coronary artery calcium score (CACS) and myocardial flow reserve (MFR). CACS is a validated quantification of subclinical coronary atherosclerosis and an independent risk marker of cardiovascular events and mortality in people with and without T2D [10]. MFR reflects coronary vascular function at both the macro- and microvascular level [11]. MFR can be quantified using cardiac rubidium-82 positron emission tomography/CT (PET/CT) and reduced MFR is a risk marker for cardiac outcomes even in asymptomatic individuals [12].

It has been hypothesized that increased PEAT volume may promote both coronary calcification and impaired myocardial perfusion through local inflammatory and metabolic effects. However, evidence supporting this hypothesis is limited and conflicting.

This analysis aimed to investigate the association between PEAT volume, CACS and MFR. As an exploratory objective, we also examined the relationship between PEAT volume and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), as a marker of systemic inflammation.

Methods

Population and design

The population included in this study has previously been described [13]. The cohort includes Danish individuals with T2D, according to WHO-criteria, free of overt or symptoms of CVD. The age range for inclusion was set at 40 to 85 years. For individuals aged 40 to 50 years, inclusion also required at least two of the following risk factors; active smoking, dyslipidemia, family history of CVD and hypertension. Criteria for exclusion were history of CVD including coronary artery disease, previous stroke, non-diabetic kidney disease and contraindications for 82Rb PET/CT scans.

Study participation involved a clinical visit at the recruiting center, during which written informed consent was obtained, clinical assessments were performed, and blood samples were collected. All cardiac rubidium-82 PET/CT scan were performed at Rigshospitalet Copenhagen, Denmark.

The study was conducted from January 2020 through August 2023 in accordance with the WMA Declaration of Helsinki. The Danish National Committee on Health Research Ethics (H-19063311) approved the study protocol, and all participants provided written informed consent.

Clinical characteristics

The demographics, including age, sex, diabetes duration and comorbidities (e.g., hypertension, dyslipidemia), were collected through interviews and electronic medical health record review. Weight and height were measured, and body mass index (BMI) calculated. Blood pressure was measured three times in a seated position after five minutes of rest. Smoking status was assessed and defined as current smoking if the participant reported daily use of at least one cigarette, cigar, or pipe. Blood samples were collected and analyzed using standardized procedures to determine levels of HbA1c, serum creatinine, total cholesterol, low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-cholesterol, and hsCRP. Estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) was calculated using the 2009 CKD-EPI Eq. [14]. The urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) was calculated as the geometric mean of three consecutive morning urine samples, analyzed using an enzyme immunoassay.

Imaging protocol

The cardiac rubidium-82 PET/CT scan was performed using an activity dose of 1,100 MBq 82Rb using a hybrid PET/CT scanner in 3D mode (Siemens Biograph mCT, Siemens) as well as Bracco infusion system. Scanning was initiated with the individual at rest, followed by pharmacologically induced stress using an infusion of 140 µg/kg/min over a period of 6 min, to achieve maximal myocardial hyperemia. Participants were instructed to discontinue the use of phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors for 72 h and dipyridamole-containing medications for 36 h, and to abstain from caffeine for 18 h prior to the scan.

The adipose tissue volume was quantified using the cardiac software Syngo.via Frontier—Cardiac Risk Assessment (Siemens AG, Healthcare Sector, Germany). The software identifies and delineates adipose tissue surrounding the heart by segmenting the myocardium and adjacent fat based on predefined radiodensity thresholds for adipose tissue (− 150 to − 50 Hounsfield units) not including the paracardial fat. Previous validation studies have demonstrated high agreement between automated and expert manual segmentation, supporting the reproducibility and reliability of this method [15, 16]. It was however not possible to differentiate between pericardial adipose tissue and epicardial adipose tissue, hence the combined volume pericardial and epicardial adipose tissue PEAT volume is used throughout.

The CACS was assessed using the method originally described by Agatston et al. [15], summing calcified plaques in the three major coronary arteries. Quantification was performed using Syngo.via software (Siemens Healthineers, Germany). In accordance with established guidelines, CACS values were categorized into two groups: ≤ 300 and > 300 [17].

The MFR was determined using the Siemens Syngo MBF 2.3 software (Siemens Medical Solutions, Malvern, PA, USA), applying a one-compartment tracer kinetic model for 82Rb and the extraction curve proposed by Lortie et al. [18]. MFR was calculated as the ratio of myocardial blood flow during pharmacological stress to myocardial blood flow at rest, based on global myocardial assessment. An MFR value ≤ 2 and < 1.7 was considered indicative of impaired and severely impaired coronary microvascular function, respectively [19–21].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize the study population overall and across tertiles of PEAT volume. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation if normally distributed, or as median with interquartile range if non-normally distributed. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and percentages.

Group comparisons across PEAT tertiles were performed using one-way analysis of variance when model residuals met the distributional assumptions and the Kruskal Wallis test when these assumptions were not met. The Pearson’s chi-squared test was used for comparisons of categorical variables. Missing data were reported if > 2% per variable and managed by listwise deletion in regression analyses.

Multivariable regression models were applied to assess the associations between PEAT volume (per 100 mL increase, as a continuous variable) and each outcome variable. CACS was analyzed as a binary variable (> 300 vs. ≤ 300 Agatston units). MFR was normally distributed and analyzed as a continuous variable; in secondary analyses it was also categorized according to established clinical cutoffs (≤ 2.0 and ≤ 1.7) [20]. Since model residuals showed right skewness and heteroscedasticity, hsCRP was log transformed using the natural logarithm to meet regression assumptions. Standardized beta coefficients were calculated for continuous outcomes to allow direct comparison across variables; for hsCRP, standardized estimates correspond to ln-transformed values. For hsCRP results were transformed and presented as percentage difference per 100 mL PEAT increase for interpretability.

For each outcome, two models were constructed. Model 1 included age and sex. Model 2 was additionally adjusted for markers of cardiovascular risk routinely collected in the care of individuals with T2D: diabetes duration, BMI, LDL cholesterol, current smoking status, systolic blood pressure, HbA1c, eGFR, and UACR.

In a sensitivity analysis, we additionally adjusted for treatment with metformin, sodium–glucose cotransporter-2 inhibitors (SGLT-2is), and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1 RAs) to account for potential pharmacological confounding.

Restricted cubic spline analyses were performed to evaluate potential non-linear associations between PEAT volume and both MFR and CACS. The primary spline models were fitted using four knots. To assess the robustness of the findings, sensitivity analyses were additionally conducted using three- and five-knot models were added during review.

To explore which covariates contributed to the attenuation of the association between PEAT volume and MFR, additional linear regression analyses were performed during review. First, separate linear regression models were fitted in which the association between PEAT volume and MFR was adjusted individually for each covariate. Subsequently, a targeted model including age, sex, eGFR, and log-UACR was fitted, incorporating the covariates that had the greatest impact on the association when examined individually.

All statistical analyses were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, with not adjustment for multiplicity. Analyses were performed using R version 4.3.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) in RStudio (Posit Software, Boston, MA; version 2024.12.1 build 563). Code development and troubleshooting were assisted by a large language model (ChatGPT, OpenAI, USA.) All analyses were independently verified and validated by the authors.

Results

Clinical characteristics

A total of 901 participants were initially included; however, 14 were excluded due to suboptimal CT imaging quality that prevented quantification of the PEAT volume. Thus, 887 participants were included in the final analyses presented in this paper. Among the 887 participants, 623 (70%) were male, mean age was 65 years (SD ± 8.9), average duration of diabetes was 14 years (± 8.4), mean BMI was 30 kg/m2 (± 5.5), and median EAT volume was 199 mL (IQR 133 to 283).

Table 1 summarizes clinical characteristics stratified into tertiles of PEAT volume. Participants in the highest PEAT tertile were more likely to be men and had significantly higher BMI and lower eGFR compared to the lower tertiles. Median hsCRP did not differ significantly across PEAT tertiles. Moreover, the prevalence of severely increased albuminuria (UACR > 300 mg/g) was higher in the highest compared to the lower tertiles. No other clinical characteristics differed significantly among tertiles.

Cardiac PET/CT measures

Median CACS increased across the tertiles from 76 (5 to 350) in the lowest to 247 (23–801) in the highest tertile (p < 0.001) (Table 2). Likewise, the proportion with CACS > 300 increased from 27% in the lowest to 46% in the highest tertile (p < 0.001).

Both rest and stress myocardial blood flow declined across PEAT tertiles (p = 0.030 and p < 0.001, respectively). Mean MFR was higher in the first tertile 2.60 (± 0.70) than in the 3rd tertile 2.48 (± 0.77) (p = 0.026). The proportion with impaired MFR (< 2) increased from 20% in the first to 26% in the third tertile, although this difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.13). The prevalence of severely impaired MFR (< 1.7) rose from 8.5% to 14% (p = 0.018).

Associations between PEAT volume and CACS, MFR and hsCRP

Model 1 showed that higher PEAT volume (per 100 mL) was associated with lower MFR (β = − 0.04, 95% CI; − 0.09 to 0.00; p = 0.041) as well as higher hsCRP, corresponding to a 38% increase per 100 mL increase in PEAT (95% CI; 28% to 48%; p < 0.001) (Table 3 and Fig. 1). The association with CACS (> 300 vs. ≤ 300) was not statistically significant (OR = 1.01; 95% CI; 0.89 to 1.15; p = 0.84).

Discussion

While PEAT has been implicated in the pathogenesis of CVD, our results did not support a consistent association with CACS or MFR after adjustment for conventional risk factors. This may indicate that PEAT does not provide incremental information beyond established risk factors but could still act as a mediator within the same underlying pathophysiological pathways.

Previous studies have reported that higher PEAT volume is associated with subclinical coronary atherosclerosis, although not always independently of established risk factors. For instance, Mahabadi et al. and Yerramasu et al. observed independent associations between PEAT volume and progression of coronary calcification in asymptomatic individuals [22, 23]. In contrast to these findings, our analysis of a much larger cohort study did not confirm an independent association. The lack of an association with CACS after adjustment for age and sex suggests that these factors, particularly sex, may explain the crude association observed in unadjusted analyses.

We tested nonlinear associations between PEAT and CACS with exploratory spline analyses within the observed data range. Although prior studies have proposed that the association between PEAT and coronary calcification may follow a threshold pattern, we did not observe statistically significant differences across CACS categories and are therefore unable to confirm this hypothesis in our cohort [24, 25].

Although higher PEAT volumes were associated with lower MFR in unadjusted analyses, this association was fully explained by established cardiometabolic risk factors. This indicates that PEAT does not provide information on microvascular function beyond what is already captured by conventional clinical markers.

Our findings contrast with a recent study by Abusnina et al., reporting a significant association between PEAT volume indexed for body surface area and invasively measured coronary microvascular function in a small cohort of symptomatic individuals referred for coronary assessment [26]. However, their analysis was univariate without adjustment for demographic or clinical covariates, which limits comparability with our multivariable models. While the study highlights a potential link between PEAT and coronary microvascular dysfunction, it was limited by sample size, selected referral population, and invasive methodology. In contrast, our study was based on a larger, asymptomatic T2D cohort examined with standardized non-invasive imaging. Collectively, the attenuation of the associations after adjustment suggests that established cardiometabolic risk factors may lie on the causal pathway linking PEAT to coronary disease. When such intermediates are adjusted for, any underlying relationship may be obscured, making it difficult to determine whether the absence of an independent association reflects mediation or a true lack of effect. This pattern is consistent with the interpretation that, in this population, PEAT primarily mirrors overall cardiometabolic burden, and that any direct effects on coronary microvascular function or coronary calcification are likely closely intertwined with other cardiometabolic risk factors.

The only statistically significant association independent of routinely collected clinical markers of cardiovascular risk was observed between higher PEAT and higher hsCRP, supporting its role as a metabolically active fat depot contributing to systemic low-grade inflammation [26, 27]. Whether inflammation contributes to the development of PEAT, or vice versa, cannot be determined from cross sectional analysis. These findings align with a longitudinal study by Christensen et al., demonstrating that EAT was an independent predictor of CVD and mortality in T2D. In addition, elevated PEAT volume measured by echocardiography in over 1,000 individuals with T2D was associated with incident cardiovascular events and all-cause mortality over a median follow-up of 4.7 years, independent of traditional risk factors [27].

Together, these results underscore PEAT’s potential relevance as an early biomarker of cardiometabolic dysregulation, even in the absence of overt structural cardiac abnormalities.

Modern non-contrast computed tomography (CT) allows for observer-independent quantification of PEAT volume using automated segmentation software, enabling its use as a potential cardiovascular risk marker in both research and clinical settings [28].

Supporting this concept, a large MRI-based study by Ayton et al. evaluated 215 individuals with T2D and found that higher PEAT volume and lower CT attenuation, reflecting metabolically dysfunctional EAT, were associated with early myocardial alterations assessed by cardiac MRI, including increased left ventricular mass/volume ratio and reduced global longitudinal strain. These findings support the hypothesis that increased, and metabolically dysfunctional PEAT may influence cardiac structure and function in individuals with T2D, thereby underlining the relevance of exploring the relationship between PEAT volume and both coronary atherosclerosis and microvascular function [29].

Strengths and limitations

This study includes one of the largest cohorts to date with simultaneous assessment of PEAT volume, CACS, MFR and hsCRP using standardized imaging protocols. PEAT volume was quantified using automated segmentation on non-contrast CT which provides high spatial resolution and is highly reproducible. MFR was measured using validated rubidium-82 PET/CT techniques enabling sensitive evaluation of myocardial microvascular function [3]. The observed association between PEAT and hsCRP provides insight into the potential inflammatory component of PEAT.

An important methodological limitation is that epicardial and pericardial adipose tissue could not be distinguished on the non-contrast CT imaging. Consequently, the quantified adipose tissue represents the combined volume of PEAT rather than isolated EAT. Although EAT is considered the most biologically active compartment due to its direct contact with the myocardium and coronary arteries, CT-based quantification of total PEAT has been widely applied in previous studies and is considered a reasonable surrogate of cardiometabolic and inflammatory risk [22]. A further limitation is that peri-coronary or local vascular inflammation could not be assessed, precluding mediation analyses of local inflammation as a potential mechanistic link between PEAT and the observed cardiovascular outcomes.

However, this cross-sectional design makes causal inference impossible. Despite extensive adjustment for relevant clinical covariates, residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as lifestyle and medication adherence remains possible. The study population consisted primarily of individuals of European descent, which may limit generalizability. Although the study included a relatively large sample and was likely powered to detect moderate associations, it was not formally designed or powered a priori for the specific hypotheses tested here and should therefore be considered exploratory.

Conclusion

In this large cross-sectional study of individuals with T2D without overt CVD, higher PEAT volume was not independently associated with CACS or MFR after multivariable adjustment. PEAT volume was associated with higher hsCRP, suggesting a link with systemic inflammation; however, this finding should be interpreted in the context of the established relationship between adiposity and inflammatory burden. These findings suggest that PEAT largely reflect overall cardiometabolic burden and that any direct impact on coronary structure or function is likely modest and closely linked to already established risk factors. Future longitudinal studies are warranted to clarify whether PEAT contributes to cardiometabolic dysregulation increasing the risk of CVD.
