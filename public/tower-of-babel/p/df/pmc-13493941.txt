Mediterranean outflow waters supply North Atlantic convection sites
Authors: Elena Calvo, Paola Malanotte-Rizzoli, Milena Menna, Riccardo Martellucci, Enrico Zambianchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe northward-flowing branch of the Mediterranean Outflow Water (MOW) carries warm and saline water into the Subpolar North Atlantic, where deep convection contributes to the formation of water masses that feed the lower limb of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation. Due to its distinctive thermohaline characteristics, the MOW presence may act as a preconditioning factor for the formation of these waters. However, the complex dynamics of the subpolar regions make tracing its pathway beyond ~50°N using conventional tracers and datasets challenging. We use an extensive dataset of Argo float salinity and oxygen profiles acquired over the North Atlantic during more than two decades to track the northward-flowing MOW and characterize its evolution along the way. Here we show that these observations allow for a multi-tracer approach, providing robust evidence of MOW’s presence in the subpolar basins, including the Irminger Sea, which has recently been identified as a basin where major deep convective processes occur. These findings highlight the potential role of the MOW in influencing the large-scale overturning circulation.Subject terms: Ocean sciences, Climate sciences

Introduction

The inflow of Mediterranean Outflow Water (MOW) into the North Atlantic plays a key role in shaping the physical and biogeochemical structure of the intermediate ocean layers1. As a warm, saline, and oxygen-poor water mass, the MOW modifies the thermohaline characteristics of the eastern North Atlantic, contributing to the salt and heat budgets at mid-depths2. It influences large-scale circulation patterns, enhances stratification, and impacts the distribution of chemical tracers such as nutrients and dissolved oxygen3,4. Its importance lies also in its potential influence on climate-relevant processes at higher latitudes, including the preconditioning of dense water formation5.

In the Gulf of Cadiz, the warm and saline Mediterranean water (CT = 12.91 °C, AS = 38.58 g/kg) (Θ = 13 °C, S = 38.4 psu in ref. 6) flowing out of the Strait of Gibraltar entrains the overlying North Atlantic Central Water and mixes with other local water masses to form the MOW7. As it flows downstream from Gibraltar, this water mass gradually loses part of its high salinity content and becomes neutrally buoyant at a depth of approximately 1200 meters near Cape St. Vincent (CT = 11.79 °C, AS = 36.57 g/kg, σθ = 27.6 ± 0.1 kg/m3) (T = 12 ± 1 °C, S = 36.4 ± 0.1 psu in refs. 7,8).

From this region, two distinct cores of MOW have been identified: one spreads westward into the open North Atlantic Ocean, forming a characteristic high salinity tongue (Fig. 1a), while the other flows northward along the Iberian continental slope7. Further north, most of the flow of MOW is deflected into the Bay of Biscay, where part of it is recirculated while the remaining continues to spread northward along the upper continental slope, detected as far north as 50°N, in the region of Porcupine Bank6,9. Here, the northward-flowing boundary current is divided into two branches: a branch is deflected westward until it converges with the North Atlantic Current (NAC), while the other is observed to penetrate into the Rockall Trough6,9.

The pathway of the MOW, especially its northward penetration into the eastern North Atlantic basin, has drawn much interest due to the hypothesis that this water mass could be a source of heat and salt to high-latitude waters, thus contributing to the preconditioning for the formation of deep waters10. However, the fate of the MOW past the region of Porcupine Bank remains largely unexplored.

The importance of the northward branch of this water mass was first recognized by J. Reid in his pioneering studies11–13, where he proposed a scenario in which the MOW is directly advected into the Labrador Sea and Nordic Seas by an eastern boundary current, preconditioning their convective cells and thus contributing to the formation of the North Atlantic Deep Water (NADW) that would then constitute the main body of the southward flowing branch of the Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC)14,15.

A second scenario was later proposed in refs. 16,17. These papers suggested that the influence of MOW in the Subpolar North Atlantic (SPNA) is realized through diffusive fluxes and mixing with the waters of the NAC, rather than through its direct northward advection.

These two theories were later unified in a paper18 in which the authors argue that the penetration of MOW into the subpolar gyre, and its subsequent spreading toward the northern regions of the Atlantic basin, is influenced by the position of the subpolar front, which fluctuates with the North Atlantic Oscillation, i.e., the teleconnection pattern dominating atmospheric variability across the Northern Atlantic region (NAO19,20).

In the above cited paper18, the northward pathway of the MOW along the Iberian Peninsula and up to Porcupine Bank is well established. However, the further northward progressions are still left undetermined. Investigating whether the MOW reaches the subpolar basins of the North Atlantic is particularly important given the key role of these regions in the formation of NADW.

In this context, recent studies have brought new insights into deep water formation in the SPNA. While earlier studies identified the Labrador Sea as the primary site for deep water formation, newer findings emphasize the Irminger Sea and the Iceland Basin as the dominant regions for this process21,22.

Previous investigations of MOW pathways and their potential relevance for the AMOC have relied primarily on traditional hydrographic datasets, which are often limited in spatial and temporal coverage. In this work, we use 22 years of Argo salinity and dissolved oxygen observations to track the complete northward pathway of the MOW from the Gulf of Cadiz into the SPNA. Here, we investigate the relationship between the NAO and the northward propagation of the MOW, and we show that MOW reaches and branches into both the Iceland Basin and the Irminger Sea. By characterizing its evolution along a series of sampling regions spanning the eastern and subpolar North Atlantic (Fig. 1b), we provide observational evidence for the extension of the MOW pathway into the SPNA.

Results

Influence of the North Atlantic Oscillation on the MOW’s northward extension

The northward penetration of the MOW into the SPNA is modulated by the phases of the NAO, primarily through changes in the wind stress, which determine a shift in the position of the subpolar front18.

During persistent NAO+ periods, stronger westerly winds cause the subpolar gyre to expand and shift the subpolar front eastward, which can limit the northward flow of the MOW into the SPNA. In contrast, during persistent NAO- phases, weaker winds lead to a westward shift of the subpolar front, facilitating a greater penetration of the MOW beyond Porcupine Bank into the Rockall Trough and the subpolar gyre18,23.

To investigate this mechanism, we analyzed a dataset of Argo float salinity profiles collected in the Eastern SPNA between 2001 and 2022 (see “Methods”). The first twelve years of this period are characterized by an average NAO index of −0.17, while the last decade shows a transition to a predominantly positive NAO phase, with an average index of +0.19 (Fig. 2).

A complementary analysis of salinity anomalies within the MOW density layer (Fig. S3) further highlights the spatial heterogeneity of this variability. These anomalies were computed relative to the SeaDataNet climatology24 by extracting, for each profile, the salinity maximum within the characteristic MOW density range (27.47–27.67 kg m−3). The southeastern subpolar boxes (A–C) broadly follow the pattern proposed by ref. 18, exhibiting more positive anomalies during NAO− periods and weaker negative anomalies during NAO+ conditions. In contrast, the northwestern boxes (D–F) display positive anomalies during both phases, suggesting that here the NAO-driven displacement of the subpolar front alone is insufficient to account for the observed regional variability.

Importantly, maximum salinity within the MOW density layer should be interpreted as a tracer-based indicator of the MOW-associated salinity signal, rather than as a quantitative measure of MOW transport or intensity. This distinction is especially important north of Porcupine Bank, where the MOW signal becomes progressively diluted and overlaps with other saline water masses, reducing the specificity of salinity as a tracer. Accordingly, the analysis presented here provides a qualitative assessment of the relative variability of the MOW-associated salinity signal within the SPNA. Restricting the analysis to the characteristic MOW density layer helps focus on the intermediate-depth signal associated with the MOW and minimizes the influence of shallower salinity structures, although it does not fully remove the effects of mixing and regional hydrographic variability.

Overall, the observed patterns are consistent with the interpretation that the NAO may modulate the relative distribution of the MOW-associated signal in the eastern SPNA, while the more complex behavior observed farther northwest suggests that additional processes may contribute to shaping the observed signal distribution, potentially involving basin-scale circulation variability and time-dependent chaotic advection, as suggested by recent Lagrangian analyses25. Resolving the relative contribution of these mechanisms would require dedicated dynamical analyses beyond the scope of the present observational study.

Because MOW-associated signals can be identified in the SPNA during both NAO phases, the following sections analyze the complete Argo dataset (2001–2022) to provide a statistically robust characterization of the mean spatial distribution and northward occurrence of the MOW in the SPNA.

Mediterranean Outflow Water’s pathway up to Porcupine Bank

To investigate the northward progression of the MOW, we selected twelve regions along its hypothesized pathway, as shown in Fig. 1b. These regions are strategically placed to sample key stages of the MOW’s evolution. The six green boxes 1–6 are located along the well-established southern segment of the path, where the MOW has been detected in previous studies6,9,17,18. In contrast, the six magenta boxes (A–F) are positioned farther north in the SPNA, along a proposed extension of the MOW pathway. Their placement is guided by the large-scale circulation at intermediate depths, as derived from the ANDRO dataset26, with a detailed description of the selection criteria provided in the “Methods” section.

In this section, we identify the northward pathway of MOW all the way to Porcupine Bank, analyzing its climatological hydrographic properties derived from the Argo float profiles collected between 2001 and 2022 within boxes 1-6 (see “Methods”). The results of this analysis are reported in Table 1.

Overall, the climatological values extracted from the Argo dataset fall within the density range of 27.47–27.67 kg/m³, which corresponds to the upper core of MOW as defined by ref. 9. The only exceptions are found in Boxes 1,2 and 3, located in the Gulf of Cadiz and along the Iberian margin, where the salinity maxima correspond to slightly higher densities because they are associated with the denser lower core of MOW27.

As the MOW advances northward, its hydrographic properties undergo a progressive transformation due to the entrainment of the overlying Eastern North Atlantic Central Water (ENACW, see below) and mixing with surrounding water masses3,27. In particular, Argo float data reveal a decrease in absolute salinity of approximately 1.14 g/kg, alongside a reduction of the conservative temperature of about 3.6 °C and a progressive thinning of the MOW layer, which gradually reduces from roughly 400 m in the source region to 150–200 m near Porcupine Bank.

Mediterranean Outflow Water’s presence in the Subpolar North Atlantic

Following the analysis of the climatological characteristics of the MOW along its already established pathway up to Porcupine Bank, we now focus on its evolution in the SPNA. In particular, we investigate the fate of the core of MOW that is deflected westward beyond Porcupine Bank, until it converges with one of the northeastward-flowing branches of the NAC9.

We now focus on Boxes A–C, located in the Iceland Basin, and Boxes E-F, situated in the Irminger Sea (Fig. 1b). The SPNA is characterized by a series of processes that complicate the detection and tracking of MOW. In particular, the presence and seasonal formation of Subpolar Mode Waters (SPMWs), characterized by relatively high salinity values (35.26–35.56 g/kg or 35.1–35.4 psu) and occupying the 27.3–27.5 kg/m3 density range28–30, adds ambiguity to the identification of MOW as their properties can closely resemble those of the diluted northward flowing MOW.

Additionally, in this region, the upper core of MOW interacts with the NAC and undergoes enhanced mixing, which progressively weakens its distinct salinity signature6,9, making its detection even more challenging. Consequently, to trace the path of MOW in the SPNA, we used a combination of subsurface salinity maxima and oxygen minima, which are well-established markers of MOW’s presence4,31,32.

In regions where mixing processes attenuate the thermohaline signature of water masses, oxygen serves as a valuable complementary tracer. Although oxygen is a non-conservative property that can also be affected by biological processes and air-sea gas exchange33, these influences are limited at intermediate depths where the MOW spreads. At these depths, the direct influence of atmospheric forcing is strongly reduced, and water mass properties are primarily shaped by ocean interior processes and mixing34–36. This means that the low oxygen content of the MOW, relative to the well-ventilated surrounding subpolar waters, can persist even after mixing modifies temperature and salinity, making it a more reliable tracer37,38.

Isopycnals are overlaid on all sections (thin black lines in Fig. 4b–d), with the 27.47 and 27.67 kg/m³ isopycnals highlighted as ticker dashed lines to mark the density range of the upper core of the MOW, following ref. 9. Throughout most of the pathway, these isopycnals are found in the 600–1000 m depth layer, progressively sloping upward as the section extends into the Irminger Sea (Fig. 4b–d).

The characteristic high-salinity tongue of MOW is clearly visible in the southern part of the section (Fig. 4b). As MOW progresses northward, this salinity signal follows the isopycnals corresponding to its upper core (27.47–27.67 kg/m³) and remains well distinguishable up to Porcupine Bank (Fig. 4a, b). Beyond this region, as MOW enters the Hatton Basin and the SPNA, the salinity signal gets strongly attenuated due to mixing processes.

In contrast, the oxygen section (Fig. 4c) shows that the well-defined oxygen minimum associated with MOW is observed more clearly as it progresses northward into the SPNA. The signal remains aligned with the density layer of MOW and is particularly evident up to the tip of the Reykjanes Ridge (Fig. 4a, c).

Beyond the ridge, the sections extend into the Irminger Sea, a region well-known for deep winter convection22,39–41. In the climatological sections presented here, the hydrographic signature of this convective activity is evident. Elevated oxygen concentrations (O2>280μmol/kg) (Fig. 4c) and low potential vorticity values (PV<4×10−11s−1m−1) (Fig. 4d) indicate a well-ventilated, homogenized water column reaching depths of approximately 1400 m.

The PV section also provides valuable insight into the identification of the regions of SPMW formation. These water masses are characterized by very low potential vorticity values, in particular where PV<4×10−11s−1m−128,29.

In the southern portion of the section, extending up to the region of Porcupine Bank, the 4×10−11s−1m−1 PV isolines (highlighted in red in Fig. 4d) indicate the presence of ENACW, a mode water formed through winter convection in the Bay of Biscay1,42,43. In this area, the ENACW predominantly occupies the 27.1–27.2 kg/m³ density range and remains clearly separated from the isopycnals that define the core of MOW. Farther north, in the Iceland Basin, the PV isolines reveal the formation of denser SPMW, which resides within the 27.3–27.5 kg/m³ layer (Fig. 4d)28–30. Notably, in the region of the Reykjanes Ridge, where the isopycnals begin to slope upward, the PV isolines associated with the SPMW intersect the isopycnals corresponding to the MOW core. This overlap might introduce an ambiguity in the distinction between the two water masses, which we resolve in the next section.

In winter (Fig. 5a), the Iceland Basin is characterized by a homogeneous mixed layer extending from the surface to an average depth of ~400 m. This low PV layer (PV<4×10−11s−1m−1) marks the formation of SPMW, driven by intense air-sea fluxes and strong convective mixing28,29,44. The PV isolines show that SPMW extends into the Irminger Sea, where similarly low PV values are observed throughout the water column, reflecting the presence of a deep convective cell21,45. In the region of the Reykjanes Ridge, isopycnals begin to slope upward and outcrop, and from this point onward, SPMW occupies the same density range as the MOW core.

In spring (Fig. 5b), surface stratification is re-established, as shown by the increased PV values. In the Iceland Basin, the 4×10−11s−1m−1 PV isolines define a thick SPMW layer between ~100 and ~500 m, extending beyond the Reykjanes Ridge into the Irminger Sea. Notably, this layer entirely overlaps with the 27.47–27.67 kg/m3 isopycnals associated with the core of the MOW.

In summer (Fig. 5c), remnants of SPMW are still present in the Iceland Basin. The 4 × 10−11 s−1 m−1 isolines highlight a residual core of this water mass intersecting the upper bound of MOW’s core density range, particularly along the eastern flank of the Reykjanes Ridge, where the isopycnals slope upward.

In autumn (Fig. 5d), the surface mixed layer begins to deepen in both the Iceland Basin and the Irminger Sea. Although the 4×10−11s−1m−1 PV isolines no longer clearly delineate the presence of SPMW in the Iceland Basin, a small residual of this water mass may still be present along the eastern flank of the Reykjanes Ridge, as suggested by localized low values of PV in that region.

Overall, autumn appears to be the season when the influence of SPMW is least evident, offering a better opportunity to detect potential evidence of the presence of MOW in the subpolar basins. Additionally, autumn is the season in which preconditioning for the winter convective activity occurs. Therefore, identifying the presence of MOW during this season (when SPMW is less dominant) would assert the key role of the MOW in the winter deep convective processes.

To assess the impact of seasonality, we extended the analysis to include seasonal climatological sections of salinity and dissolved oxygen along the MOW pathway (Figs. S4, S5). These sections were constructed using all available Argo profiles for each season over the period 2001–2022 for salinity and 2003–2022 for dissolved oxygen. The results show that the large-scale structure of the MOW within its density layer is broadly consistent across seasons, with limited seasonal variability along most of its pathway. The main seasonal differences are observed in the northern subpolar regions, where dissolved oxygen reflects the influence of wintertime deep convection and ventilation. These findings support the use of autumn data in the main analysis (see next section), as it minimizes ambiguity with the SPMW while remaining representative of the overall MOW structure.

Detection of Mediterranean Outflow Water’s signal along its complete northward pathway in the Subpolar North Atlantic

Based on these considerations, we tracked the northward pathway of the MOW by detecting its presence within the selected boxes (Fig. 1b), specifically targeting the 27.47–27.67 kg/m3 density layer that defines its core9, and using salinity maximum and oxygen minimum as tracers4,37 (see “Methods”).

The top panel (Fig. 6a) shows a sharp decline in the salinity maximum along the southern portion of the pathway, particularly up to box 6 near Porcupine Bank, indicating strong mixing between MOW and the Atlantic waters. In this region, MOW is still characterized by high salinity values, and the strong gradient with the surrounding water masses enhances mixing, leading to a rapid erosion of its salinity signature6,13,46,47. Beyond this point, as MOW progresses northward into the subpolar basins, strong mixing persists but the salinity contrast diminishes, leading to a more gradual decrease of the salinity maximum (see salinity section, Fig. 4b), consistent with the progressive dilution of intermediate water masses along their advective pathways47–50. In contrast, oxygen concentrations begin to increase gradually in the southern portion of the pathway, where waters are generally less ventilated, and then increase more steeply in the subpolar basins, where the surrounding waters are substantially more oxygenated (see oxygen section, Fig. 4c50–54).

The bottom-left panel (Fig. 6b) shows the depth evolution of the detected salinity and oxygen peaks. Up to the Hatton Basin (Box A), the salinity maximum lies slightly deeper than the oxygen minimum. However, farther north in the subpolar basins, the salinity signal rises above the oxygen minimum. This transition coincides with the gradual shoaling of the isopycnals in the Iceland Basin and their steeper slope in the Reykjanes Ridge region (see sections, Fig. 4). Here, the salinity maximum appears to be lifted upward and entrained into shallower layers following the evolution of the isopycnals, influenced by the gyre circulation and a weakened stratification. In contrast, the oxygen minimum, although it also rises following the sloping isopycnals, persists at deeper levels in SPNA, likely confined in a less ventilated intermediate layer which remains isolated from the surface, consistent with the slower ventilation and longer residence times of intermediate waters13,55,56.

This vertical separation is also evident in density space (bottom-right panel, Fig. 6c), where salinity maxima are found at denser levels than oxygen minima up to Box B, but shift to lighter densities farther north. The evolution of the density values of the two tracers remains tightly aligned up to Box B and then diverges as MOW enters the SPNA. This decoupling suggests that salinity is more easily mixed upward along sloping isopycnals, while the oxygen signal remains trapped in denser, less ventilated layers.

Despite this divergence, both tracers remain broadly coherent in their depth evolution, suggesting that they continue to track the same water mass. The observed separation in density space reflects the distinct sensitivities of salinity and oxygen to mixing and is probably associated with complex tracer-specific behaviors.

In particular, the persistence and smoother evolution of the oxygen minimum suggest that it may be less affected by mixing or may reside in more isolated layers, making it a reliable tracer for the MOW. This is consistent with previous studies highlighting the usefulness of dissolved oxygen as a tracer of intermediate water mass properties and mixing processes4,37,38,47,49. Its clear and consistent signal, co-located with a salinity maximum in the density layer of the core of the MOW, offers strong evidence for the presence of this water mass across all the analyzed boxes, effectively tracing its northward pathway.

To assess the robustness of the extrema-based tracking, we performed a complementary analysis based on the mean of the salinity maxima and oxygen minima identified within the MOW density layer (Fig. S6). While the extrema-based approach targets the most pronounced and least mixed remnants of the MOW signal, the mean diagnostic is inherently more sensitive to mixing with surrounding water masses and therefore provides a more integrated representation of the evolving properties of the MOW.

This complementary analysis exhibits a consistent large-scale evolution of both tracers along the MOW pathway, closely resembling that obtained from the extrema-based approach, but with smoother variations, particularly in the density evolution in the subpolar regions. This behavior reflects the increasing influence of mixing and ambient variability, which progressively dilute and redistribute the MOW signal along its pathway.

The agreement between the two diagnostics supports the robustness of the identified pathway and confirms that, despite significant transformation, persistent traces of the MOW signature remain detectable across the SPNA. Fig. 6c, d further support the validity of the obtained results, showing that the identified salinity and oxygen values generally correspond to actual peaks in the respective profiles. The only exceptions that emerge for the salinity data are given by boxes 3 and D.

In Box 3, the detected salinity maximum falls within the correct high-salinity layer but does not precisely correspond to the absolute peak. This inconsistency likely arises because, in this region, the salinity peak occurs at slightly higher densities than the selected MOW core density range (see Table 1 for climatological reference values).

Box D presents a more interesting exception: here, no clear salinity peak is observed within the MOW core density layer. This box is located in a region characterized by complex dynamics, as it includes the Reykjanes Ridge, where the isopycnals slope most steeply. In this context, the MOW signal is likely advected along sloping isopycnals and subject to enhanced mixing, which can lead to a partial redistribution of its properties across density surfaces. As a result, part of the high-salinity signal may be displaced outside the selected density range, making it less detectable within the MOW core layer. As the isopycnals flatten beyond the ridge, the density surfaces become more stable, and the salinity maximum becomes detectable again within the expected range in boxes E and F.

Additional features in the subpolar boxes further highlight the value of combining salinity and oxygen as complementary tracers of the MOW. In Boxes A–C, relatively saline overlying waters reduce the vertical contrast between the MOW signal and the surrounding water masses, making the maximum salinity associated with the MOW less distinct in these regions. This is consistent with the progressive dilution and modification of the MOW signature along its northward pathway.

In this context, the oxygen profiles provide significant complementary information. In particular, Box F shows low oxygen concentrations between approximately 200 and 600 m, whereas elevated salinity is mainly confined to shallower depths, between approximately 100 and 300 m. This vertical separation suggests that oxygen can strengthen the identification of the intermediate-depth MOW signature in regions where its salinity signal becomes less distinct. More generally, the detected oxygen minima align with well-defined extrema in the oxygen profiles, supporting the reliability of oxygen as a tracer, together with salinity, to identify the MOW signal along its northward pathway.

It is important to emphasize that, although the presence of the MOW can be robustly identified through salinity and oxygen extrema, these diagnostics do not provide a direct quantitative measure of its strength or transport. Rather, they allow us to trace its spreading and assess its presence in the SPNA, which is the primary objective of this study. The analysis of salinity anomalies within the MOW density layer (Fig. S3) offers additional insight into the variability of its influence in the subpolar basins; however, a more rigorous quantification of MOW intensity would require dedicated analyses that explicitly resolve circulation and water mass transformation processes.

While our results demonstrate that traces of the MOW can be detected in the vicinity of the subpolar convection regions, the present analysis does not allow us to directly assess its role in deep convective processes. Addressing this question would require a targeted, region-specific investigation of the convective sites, aimed at resolving the transformation of MOW properties and their interaction with local water masses during wintertime convection. Such an analysis is beyond the scope of the present study but represents a natural direction for future work.

Overall, this study provides strong evidence for the presence of MOW in the SPNA and introduces a tracer-based methodology to identify its pathways in regions previously considered ambiguous. Moreover, these findings suggest that the MOW may play a broader role in the preconditioning of the SPNA for winter convection. Future work combining high-resolution models and physical and biogeochemical tracers could further clarify MOW’s contribution to the structure of subpolar water masses and climate-relevant processes.

Methods

The influence of NAO on the MOW

To assess the influence of the North Atlantic Oscillation (NAO) on the northward penetration of Mediterranean Outflow Water (MOW) into the Subpolar North Atlantic (SPNA), we first analyzed the NAO index time series provided by NOAA (updated from ref. 57). Monthly values spanning from January 1950 were available, but we focused on the period from 2001–2022 to match the temporal coverage of the Argo float dataset. Annual averages of the NAO index were computed to produce the time series shown in Fig. 2. As previously documented58,59, the first decade of the 2000s was dominated by a predominantly negative NAO phase, followed by a transition to a generally positive phase in the subsequent decade. To assess the impact of this shift on MOW’s northward spread, we examined Argo float salinity profiles collected during years representative of each NAO phase.

In particular, we selected the Argo float salinity profiles collected during six NAO- peak years (2005, 2006, 2008, 2009, 2010, 2012) and six NAO+ peak years (2013, 2015, 2017, 2018, 2020, 2022) in the eastern SPNA. For each profile, we extracted the maximum salinity value recorded within the 27.47–27.67 kg/m3 density layer, which has been identified as the density range typical of the core of MOW near its source region9. These in-situ salinity values were converted to absolute salinity using the TEOS-10 toolbox60. Finally, we mapped the spatial distribution of these maxima separately for NAO− and NAO+ phases to assess whether NAO-driven variability affects the northward spread of MOW (Fig. 3a, b).

Definition of analysis regions (boxes) for the proposed extension of the MOW pathway into the SPNA

The placement of the northern boxes (A–F, Fig. 1b) was designed to investigate a proposed extension of the MOW pathway into the SPNA. Their location was guided by the mean velocity field at 1000 m depth, derived from the ANDRO dataset26, which captures the large-scale circulation at depths corresponding to the MOW layer. In particular, these boxes were positioned along areas of persistent northward and northwestward flow along the eastern boundary and across the Iceland Basin toward the Irminger Sea, following the pathways suggested by the mean circulation. This approach allows us to sample the potential pathway of the MOW as it propagates into the SPNA, while accounting for the influence of the subpolar gyre circulation and regional bathymetric features such as the Reykjanes Ridge (Fig. 1a, b).

This configuration is consistent with previous observational studies documenting traces of MOW spreading northwest of Porcupine Bank6,9,61,62, suggesting a potential extension of its influence toward higher latitudes. It is further supported by recent Lagrangian simulations analysis showing that a fraction of MOW-origin water parcels can follow northward pathways into the subpolar gyre, reaching latitudes beyond the Rockall Ridge25. Together, these elements guided the selection of the boxes so as to capture the largest possible spatial extent of the proposed MOW pathway into the SPNA.

The spatial and temporal distribution of Argo observations within the selected regions is not uniform and may potentially affect the robustness of the analysis. To evaluate the observational coverage, we examined the sampling distribution of salinity and dissolved oxygen profiles within each box (Figs. S1, S2). Heatmaps of profile density and seasonal histograms normalized by region were used to evaluate both the spatial and temporal coverage of the dataset.

Salinity observations from the core Argo program provide extensive coverage across all regions throughout the study period. Dissolved oxygen measurements from the BGC-Argo program are less numerous because this program began more recently; nevertheless, they provide substantial coverage, particularly within the SPNA regions (boxes A–F; Fig. 1b), which are critical for detecting the northward penetration of MOW. In contrast, the southern regions (boxes 1–6; Fig. 1b), located between the Gulf of Cadiz and Porcupine Bank, contain fewer oxygen observations. However, these regions correspond to the well-established pathway of the MOW in the eastern North Atlantic, where its presence has been extensively documented by previous studies.

Overall, the available Argo sampling provides sufficient spatial and temporal coverage to robustly characterize the MOW pathway investigated in this study63.

The retrieval of the climatological properties of MOW up to Porcupine Bank

In order to determine the climatological hydrographic properties of the MOW along its established pathway from the Strait of Gibraltar to Porcupine Bank, we analyzed the Argo float profiles collected between 2001 and 2022 within boxes 1–6 (Fig. 1b).

In each box, in-situ observations of temperature and salinity were converted to conservative temperature and absolute salinity using the TEOS-10 toolbox60, from which potential density profiles were subsequently derived. We focused on the 600–1300 m depth layer, identified as the typical depth range of MOW in these regions31,64.

Within this depth layer, we extracted the annual maximum values of absolute salinity from each profile, along with their corresponding temperature, depth, and potential density. For each region, we then computed the climatological mean and standard deviation of these annual values over the 22-year period. The resulting values are summarized in Table 1.

The pathway of the MOW in the SPNA

To investigate the large-scale distribution and evolution of MOW properties across the North Atlantic, we constructed three composite sections using Argo float data: absolute salinity, dissolved oxygen concentration, and potential vorticity (PV) (Fig. 4b–d). Each section is aligned with the expected pathway of MOW (Fig. 4a), beginning with its well-established northward progression up to Porcupine Bank, and extending into the SPNA following the hypothesized path outlined by Boxes A–F, in the Iceland Basin and Irminger Sea (Fig. 1b).

In this study, we neglect the core of MOW which flows into the Rockall Trough, as this region is characterized by complex hydrographic and dynamical conditions, dominated by tidal flows and interactions with bathymetry65–68 that require a more detailed, dedicated analysis. Therefore, the evolution of this core of MOW will be addressed in future work.

To obtain the sections, Argo float profiles were vertically interpolated onto constant depth levels at 10 m intervals from the surface to 2000 m. The profiles were then spatially binned on a regular 2° × 2° grid covering the entire North Atlantic and averaged over the duration of the dataset: 2001–2022 for salinity and temperature, and 2003–2022 for oxygen.

MOW detection algorithm

To track the northward flow of MOW along its pathway, we analyzed salinity and dissolved oxygen data from Argo float profiles located within the boxes shown in Fig. 1b, focusing on the density range that defines the core of MOW (27.47–27.67 kg/m3).

For each box, we identified all Argo profiles collected between 2001 and 2022 for salinity and between 2003 and 2022 for oxygen. In-situ salinity and temperature values were converted to absolute salinity and conservative temperature using the TEOS-10 toolbox60, and potential density was calculated from the corresponding temperature and salinity profiles.

Each profile was then analyzed to co-locate the maximum absolute salinity and minimum dissolved oxygen concentration within the specified density range. For each identified peak and for each box, the corresponding depth and potential density were also extracted. In the case of boxes D, E and F, located in the Irminger Sea and including the Reykjanes Ridge (Fig. 1b), the analysis was limited to data collected during autumn (October–December) to minimize the interference from the SPMW.

For each box, the primary diagnostic is defined as the maximum of all salinity maxima and the minimum of all oxygen minima identified across the profiles. These extrema represent the most pronounced and least mixed remnants of the MOW signal within each box. The results of this detection are presented in Fig. 6a, which shows the spatial progression of these extrema across the North Atlantic, together with the evolution of their depths and potential densities.

To assess the sensitivity of this diagnostic to mixing and ambient variability, we performed an additional analysis in which we computed, for each region, the mean of all salinity maxima and the mean of all oxygen minima, along with the corresponding mean depths and potential densities (Fig. S6). This complementary diagnostic is more sensitive to mixing processes and provides a smoother representation of the evolution of MOW properties.

To better visualize the detected peaks, for each box, we also identified the maximum salinity and minimum oxygen values at each depth level, constructing representative vertical profiles of absolute salinity maxima and dissolved oxygen concentration minima. Finally, to assess the robustness of the detection, the identified salinity maxima and oxygen minima are marked with black crosses on the respective vertical profiles (Fig. 6b).
