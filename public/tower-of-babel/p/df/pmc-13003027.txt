Time to Treatment Discontinuation and Cost Effectiveness of Third-Line Therapies in Advanced Colorectal Cancer: Real-World Evidence from the NIH All of Us Research Program
Authors: Patrick J. Kiel, Mark W. McGiffin, Todd C. Skaar, Michael A. Preston
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTreatment options for patients with advanced colorectal cancer who progress after standard therapies remain limited, and real-world evidence on treatment durability and economic outcomes in later lines of therapy is needed to inform clinical and policy decision making. This study evaluated real-world outcomes, healthcare utilization, and cost effectiveness of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) compared with regorafenib or trifluridine/tipiracil (Rego/Tri) in third-line (3L) advanced colorectal cancer (CRC), leveraging the nationally distributed NIH All of Us Research Program.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort cost–utility analysis using All of Us Version 8 Curated Data Repository. Eligible adults (≥ 18 years) with advanced CRC initiated ICI (pembrolizumab, nivolumab ± ipilimumab) or Rego/Tri during 2017–2023. Outcomes included time to treatment discontinuation (TTD), healthcare utilization, costs, and quality-adjusted life years (QALYs; base-case utility = 0.75). Costs were estimated from CMS 2023 schedules (Part B ASP for infused agents, Part D unit costs for orals; inpatient/outpatient fee schedules for non-drug services), applied per cycle and proportionally for partial cycles. Analyses were conducted from the US Medicare payer perspective, with the time horizon limited to observed TTD and no discounting applied (< 1 year). Incremental cost-effectiveness ratios (ICERs), incremental net monetary benefit (INMB), and cost-effectiveness acceptability curves (CEACs) were estimated using nonparametric bootstrapping (1000 replicates). Sensitivity analyses varied utility weights (0.6–1.0).ResultsAmong 82 patients (ICI n = 50; Rego/Tri n = 32), median TTD was significantly longer with ICI (8.0 vs 1.4 months; HR 0.49, 95% CI 0.30–0.79; p = 0.0037). Non-drug utilization costs were not significantly different, but ICI drug costs were higher (US$207,558 vs US$119,805; p = 0.001). Mean total costs were US$476,729 for ICI versus US$264,225 for Rego/Tri (p = 0.018). QALYs were higher for ICI (0.84 vs 0.43; p < 0.001). The ICER was US$518,000 per QALY gained. CEAC analysis demonstrated ~ 0% probability of cost effectiveness at US$150,000/QALY, ~ 50% at US$500,000/QALY, and ~ 80% at ≥ US$1,000,000/QALY. Sensitivity analyses yielded consistent findings.ConclusionsIn this exploratory analysis using the novel NIH All of Us Research Program, ICI therapy was associated with longer treatment duration and greater QALYs but substantially higher costs compared with Rego/Tri. ICI was unlikely to be cost effective at conventional US thresholds but may be favorable at higher willingness-to-pay values. These findings highlight both the promise of leveraging the All of Us Research Program for nationally distributed, demographically diverse pharmacoeconomic research and the challenges of interpreting real-world cost effectiveness in small cohorts. As the dataset matures and sample sizes increase, broader application of All of Us may provide valuable insights to inform precision oncology policy and value-based pricing strategies.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s40801-026-00539-9.

Introduction

Colorectal cancer (CRC) remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality in the United States, with many patients progressing to advanced or metastatic disease despite standard first- and second-line therapies [1–3]. For patients requiring third-line treatment, options such as multikinase inhibitors (e.g., regorafenib) or oral cytotoxic agents (e.g., trifluridine/tipiracil [TAS-102]) offer only modest improvements in survival (median overall survival ~ 6.4 to 7.1 months) and quality of life [4, 5]. More recently, the emergence of immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI), particularly for patients with microsatellite instability–high (MSI-H) or mismatch repair deficient (dMMR) tumors, has reshaped the therapeutic landscape by offering the potential for durable responses. However, the clinical and economic implications of ICI in broader, real-world CRC populations remain poorly characterized [6–11].

Real-world data (RWD) provide critical insights into patterns of care, outcomes, and costs that extend beyond the scope of randomized clinical trials [12–16]. The National Institutes of Health (NIH) All of Us Research Program is one of the largest, most diverse RWD repositories, integrating electronic health records (EHRs), surveys, genomics, and social determinants of health (SDoH) across more than 800,000 participants [17]. The curated data model enables analyses of treatment exposures, utilization, and outcomes, while also exposing challenges in oncology data completeness. Prior work using All of Us has demonstrated the feasibility of identifying biomarker testing and targeted therapy use in lung, colorectal, and prostate cancers, but also documented significant under-reporting of biomarker results, imaging, and molecular diagnostics across cancers [17, 18]. These gaps underscore the importance of both leveraging All of Us for broad population-level insights and acknowledging the limitations inherent in real-world documentation.

In metastatic CRC, biomarker testing (e.g., KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, MSI status) is essential to guide the use of targeted therapies and immunotherapies [3]. Despite coverage mandates and clinical guidelines supporting next-generation sequencing (NGS), uptake and documentation remain inconsistent in real-world settings [13–16].

Incomplete biomarker data introduce both clinical and economic uncertainty, complicating assessments of whether ICI use aligns with precision oncology principles.

This study leverages the NIH All of Us Research Program to evaluate clinical outcomes, healthcare utilization, and cost effectiveness of ICI compared with Rego/Tri in patients with advanced CRC treated in the third-line setting. We quantify time to treatment discontinuation (TTD), estimate quality-adjusted life years (QALYs), and conduct incremental cost-effectiveness analyses from the US Medicare perspective. By integrating RWD from a diverse national cohort, this work provides pragmatic insights into the value of ICI in routine clinical practice. Our findings provide real-world evidence from a nationally distributed and demographically diverse research cohort on the value of ICI in CRC, while underscoring persistent documentation gaps and highlighting policy challenges that must be addressed to enable effective implementation of precision oncology at scale.

Methods

Research Patient Data Repository

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using Version 8 of the Curated Data Repository from the NIH All of Us Research Program. The All of Us program integrates electronic health records (EHRs), participant surveys (including social determinants of health), genomics, physical measurements, and data from mobile and wearable devices. As of February 2025, the program included over 800,000 participants, of whom approximately 45% identified as a racial or ethnic minority. More than 440,000 participants had linked EHRs and more than 568,000 contributed biosamples. The provider network comprises regional academic medical centers, federally qualified community health centers, and US Department of Veterans Affairs facilities. The NIH All of Us Research Program is a large, nationally distributed research cohort designed to enhance demographic and geographic diversity, including the intentional oversampling of populations historically underrepresented in biomedical research [17]. While national in scope, the cohort is not population-weighted and therefore is not statistically representative of the US population.

For this analysis, we extracted demographic characteristics, diagnoses, types of visits, laboratory tests, administered medications, stage at initial diagnosis, histology, biomarker testing status, and death dates. All data were de-identified and harmonized into the Observational Medical Outcomes Partnership (OMOP) common data model. Additional methodological details, including the dataset cover sheet and supplementary tables, are provided in the Supplementary Appendix, see electronic supplementary material (ESM).

Defining and Characterizing Population

Eligible patients were adults (≥ 18 years) with advanced colorectal cancer (CRC), defined as stage IV or metastatic disease, who initiated third-line systemic therapy between January 2017 and October 2023 (Supplement Fig. A1, ESM). Third-line or later (3L+) therapy was defined pragmatically as the initiation of an ICI or regorafenib/trifluridine–tipiracil (Rego/Tri) following prior documented fluorouracil exposure. Because fluorouracil doublets (with oxaliplatin or irinotecan) constitute the universal backbone of first- and second-line metastatic CRC therapy, documented fluorouracil exposure was required to ensure that subsequent ICI or Rego/Tri initiation reflected ≥ 3L therapy. Additional prior therapies (oxaliplatin, irinotecan, bevacizumab, anti-epidermal growth factor receptor [EGFR]) were captured as binary indicators using OMOP drug_concept_ids and are summarized in Table 1. Patients without evidence of fluorouracil exposure were excluded. Where earlier therapy documentation was incomplete, patients were retained if fluorouracil exposure was present, recognizing under-documentation in All of Us while minimizing risk of misclassification. Patients were excluded if they had (a) more than one documented primary cancer, (b) treatment dates preceding the CRC diagnosis, (c) therapy or diagnosis inconsistent with CRC, or (d) < 21 days of continuous drug exposure. The final exclusion criterion was applied to reduce bias from early discontinuation unrelated to therapeutic effectiveness (e.g., intolerance, rapid clinical decline, or administrative miscoding), thereby ensuring that treatment duration more accurately reflected therapeutic effect. A 21-day minimum exposure threshold was selected as a pragmatic cutoff aligned with common systemic therapy scheduling in routine oncology practice, including the standard 21-days-on dosing period for regorafenib within a 28-day cycle and historical every-3-week administration intervals for immune checkpoint inhibitors.

Biomarker testing was identified using structured EHR data from the NIH All of Us Research Program, harmonized to the OMOP Common Data Model. Testing events were captured across the procedure (CPT), measurement (LOINC), and OMOP concept ID domains to ensure standardized ascertainment across health systems. CPT Codes included both panel-based and single-gene molecular pathology codes (e.g., MSI analysis 81301; KRAS 81275–81276; NRAS 81311; BRAF 81210; EGFR 81235; targeted genomic sequencing panels 81445, 81450, 81455, 0037U; and tiered molecular pathology codes 81400–81408). Unlisted codes (81479, 81599) were also included to capture laboratory-developed tests (Supplement Table A5, ESM). LOINC Codes including laboratory test result concepts were mapped for MSI, KRAS, NRAS, BRAF, and related molecular markers. OMOP Mapping was conducted with diagnoses and procedures were standardized to OMOP condition_concept_id and procedure_concept_id fields. Measurements were captured via measurement_concept_id, with mapping to CPT/LOINC ensuring consistency across health systems. Patients were classified as having documented biomarker testing if one or more relevant CPT or LOINC code was present; however, we anticipated under-documentation given the prevalence of free-text or scanned genomic reports in All of Us, and therefore interpret findings as reflecting gaps in documentation fidelity as well as testing utilization.

Time to Treatment Discontinuation

TTD was defined as the number of days from initiation of third-line systemic therapy (Rego/Tri or ICI) to treatment discontinuation. Discontinuation was defined as the earliest occurrence of either (1) the last documented administration followed by no subsequent administration within 60 days, or (2) initiation of a subsequent systemic therapy, which was treated as a discontinuation event for the index regimen. This included switches between immune checkpoint inhibitors and regorafenib or trifluridine–tipiracil, or vice versa, consistent with standard oncology real-world evidence conventions [19].

Participants who remained on treatment within 60 days of the database cutoff (October 1, 2023) were considered censored at their last observed administration date. TTD distributions were estimated using Kaplan–Meier methods, with censoring applied at the date of last known treatment for patients still on therapy or with follow-up shorter than 60 days.

Cost-Utility Analysis Framework

Health Economic Outcomes

Health economic outcomes were assessed using a cost–utility analysis from the US Medicare payer perspective [20–27]. Drug acquisition costs were based on 2023 CMS benchmarks: Medicare Part B ASP Payment Limits (reflecting statutory ASP + 6% reimbursement) for infused immune checkpoint inhibitors (nivolumab, pembrolizumab, ipilimumab) and Medicare Part D national average spending for oral agents (regorafenib, trifluridine/tipiracil) (CMS Part B ASP Pricing Files, CMS Part D Spending Dashboard) [23, 26, 27]. Costs were applied per cycle, adjusted for each patient’s observed treatment duration, and proportionally for partial cycles (e.g., 0.5 cycle = 50% of cost). Dosing followed FDA/NCCN label recommendations without modeling dose reductions or adherence adjustments; combination regimens (e.g., nivolumab plus ipilimumab) were based on pivotal trial protocols and prescribing information (Supplement Table A1 & A4, ESM). No adjustments were made for rebates, confidential discounts, or vial wastage, representing gross Medicare spending.

Non-drug healthcare utilization was captured at the patient-event level using OMOP-standardized concepts and grouped into clinically relevant categories: laboratory tests (CEA), colonoscopy, imaging (MRI, CT, PET/CT), emergency room visits, inpatient admissions, and outpatient visits. Costs were applied using 2023 Medicare national average reimbursement rates from the Physician Fee Schedule, Inpatient Prospective Payment System, and Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule (Physician Fee Schedule, IPPS, CLFS) [24, 25]. Only patients with documented utilization were included. For each category and treatment group, we calculated number of patients, mean, standard deviation (SD), median, interquartile range (IQR), and 95% bootstrap confidence intervals (1000 resamples). Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test where utilization occurred in both groups; events unique to one cohort were summarized descriptively.

Baseline Demographics and Clinical Characteristics

Baseline variables included age, race, ethnicity, sex at birth, and prior exposure to oxaliplatin, irinotecan, bevacizumab, cetuximab, and panitumumab.

Quality-Adjusted Life Years (QALYs) and Economic Evaluation

Health outcomes were expressed as QALYs, calculated as treatment duration (in years) multiplied by a utility weight of 0.75, derived from published estimates for patients with advanced colorectal cancer using validated preference-based instruments, consistent with prior CRC health economic literature [4, 21]. A single, fixed utility value was applied uniformly across treatment groups. Utilities were not varied by treatment response, health state, or adverse events, as the All of Us dataset does not systematically capture longitudinal patient-reported outcomes (e.g., EQ-5D) or health-state transitions aligned with treatment exposure. Accordingly, QALYs in this analysis represent duration-adjusted survival on treatment rather than treatment-specific quality-of-life differences.

Unweighted treatment duration (years) was reported descriptively as a secondary outcome to distinguish differences in treatment persistence from utility-adjusted outcomes, but was excluded from incremental cost-effectiveness ratio (ICER) and cost-effectiveness accessibility curve (CEAC) calculations. Sensitivity analyses varied utility weights from 0.6 to 1.0.

ICERs were calculated as incremental cost divided by incremental QALYs. Incremental net monetary benefit (INMB) was computed as (ΔQALY × WTP) − ΔCost across willingness-to-pay (WTP) thresholds ranging from US$0 to US$1,000,000 per QALY [28]. Total costs included both drug acquisition and non-drug utilization. Analyses were conducted from the US Medicare payer perspective, with a time horizon limited to observed treatment duration (TTD) and no discounting applied (< 1 year).

Decision Uncertainty and Value Frameworks

Uncertainty was evaluated using nonparametric bootstrap resampling (1000 stratified replicates preserving group sizes), a widely accepted approach for handling skewed cost data and estimating uncertainty around ICERs [28, 29]. Incremental costs and QALYs were recalculated for each replicate, and INMB was derived across the full range of WTP thresholds. CEACs were constructed as the proportion of replicates with positive NMB. Thresholds included US$50,000, US$100,000, US$150,000, US$500,000, and US$1,000,000 per QALY, reflecting conventional US benchmarks and higher values debated in oncology policy, situating the analysis within a value-based pricing framework [30].

Statistical Analysis

Baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were summarized using descriptive statistics. Group comparisons were conducted using Student’s t tests for continuous variables and chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables, depending on cell counts. Statistical significance was defined as two-sided p < 0.05 or non-overlapping 95% confidence intervals.

Time to treatment discontinuation (TTD) was analyzed using Kaplan–Meier survival methods, with unadjusted group comparisons performed via the log-rank test. The proportional hazards assumption for Cox models was tested using Schoenfeld residuals. Given the limited sample size (N = 82), multivariable adjustment for TTD and cost outcomes was not performed, and results should be interpreted as descriptive associations rather than causal effects.

Cost outcomes were summarized using means, medians, and interquartile ranges. Nonparametric methods were used because healthcare cost data are typically right-skewed, and bootstrapping avoids distributional assumptions that may bias variance estimates. Uncertainty in incremental costs and QALYs was evaluated via nonparametric bootstrap resampling (1000 replicates) using the boot package, with INMB and CEAC constructed from the bootstrap replicates.

All analyses were performed in R version 4.4.0 using the survival and survminer packages for survival analyses, the boot package for resampling, the table1 package for baseline characteristics, and the tidyverse suite (dplyr, stringr, lubridate, tidyr, ggplot2) for data management and visualization.

The NIH All of Us Institutional Review Board determined that access to the de-identified participant dataset qualifies as non-human subjects research and does not require additional IRB review. In accordance with All of Us policies, all counts are rounded and results representing fewer than 20 participants are suppressed to protect privacy.

Results

Demographics

Among 82 patients included (ICI, n = 50; Rego/Tri, n = 32), median age was slightly higher in the ICI group compared with Rego/Tri (p = 0.038). Sex distribution and race/ethnicity were generally balanced between groups, with masked counts reported for categories with < 25 patients to maintain privacy. Prior systemic therapy exposure differed markedly with patients in the Rego/Tri group more likely to have received oxaliplatin, irinotecan, bevacizumab, and anti-EGFR agents compared with ICI patients (all p < 0.001). Biomarker testing was infrequently documented, and MSI testing was not observed in any patient.

Time to Treatment Discontinuation (TTD)

Median follow-up time, defined as time from third-line treatment initiation to treatment discontinuation, was 4.1 months (95% CI 2.8–6.4) overall. Median follow-up was 8.0 months (95% CI 4.6–17.0) in the ICI cohort and 1.4 months (95% CI 1.0–2.8) in the regorafenib/trifluridine cohort.

Among the 82 patients included in the study, median TTD was significantly longer for patients receiving ICI compared with those receiving regorafenib or trifluridine/tipiracil (Rego/Tri). Median TTD was 8.0 months for the ICI group versus 1.4 months for the Rego/Tri group (p = 0.0037). The hazard ratio (HR) for treatment discontinuation in the ICI group compared with Rego/Tri was 0.49 (95% CI 0.30–0.79), indicating a 51% reduction in the risk of discontinuation for patients treated with ICI (Fig. 1). Formal testing using Schoenfeld residuals suggested deviation from the proportional hazards assumption (p = 0.009). Discussion

This study leveraged the NIH All of Us Research Program to evaluate real-world treatment patterns, TTD, healthcare utilization, and economic outcomes among patients with advanced CRC treated with third-line or later therapies. To our knowledge, this represents one of the first exploratory pharmacoeconomic analyses of ICI versus regorafenib/trifluridine–tipiracil using a large, nationally distributed EHR-linked cohort. The analysis reflects real-world practice during a historical and transitional treatment period (2018–2023), rather than contemporary first-line treatment paradigms. In this analysis, ICI therapy was associated with substantially longer treatment duration and greater QALYs, but also markedly higher drug costs, resulting in an ICER of approximately US$518,000/QALY. CEAC and INMB analyses indicated that ICI was unlikely to be cost effective at conventional US thresholds, though cost effectiveness improved at higher WTP values.

The novelty of this analysis lies in the use of All of Us, a unique dataset designed to capture diverse populations often underrepresented in oncology clinical trials [17]. Although sample size was modest and biomarker documentation incomplete, this work demonstrates the feasibility of conducting pragmatic pharmacoeconomic analyses within All of Us. By integrating OMOP-coded diagnoses, CPT and LOINC biomarker codes, and CMS cost benchmarks, we provide a transparent framework for real-world economic evaluations that complements, but does not replace, clinical trial evidence. Given the heterogeneity and under documentation inherent in EHR-derived RWD, findings should be interpreted as exploratory signals that generate hypotheses rather than definitive estimates.

Consistent with prior oncology RWD studies, we employed TTD as a pragmatic proxy for treatment benefit, acknowledging incomplete mortality capture in All of Us [19]. TTD has been increasingly used in real-world oncology research as a practical surrogate for progression-free survival, particularly in EHR-derived datasets where radiographic progression is not routinely or consistently captured. Prior observational studies have demonstrated that TTD correlates with progression-free survival and reflects clinically meaningful treatment durability, supporting its use for comparative effectiveness analyses outside of randomized trial settings [32, 33]. Median TTD was substantially longer for ICI compared with Rego/Tri, paralleling the survival advantages observed in pivotal ICI trials of MSI-H/dMMR CRC [6–10]. This suggests that RWD-derived TTD, while imperfect, may reflect relative differences in therapeutic durability. Even when proportional hazards assumptions are not strictly met, comparisons of Kaplan–Meier-derived TTD remain informative for assessing relative treatment persistence in real-world settings, and the observed separation between treatment groups supports the robustness of the qualitative TTD findings [31]. Both ICI and Rego/Tri are administered until disease progression or unacceptable toxicity, rather than for a fixed course of therapy. Accordingly, discontinuations are most often a reflection of treatment failure or intolerance, not completion of a planned regimen, reinforcing the relevance of TTD in this setting. Importantly, use of TTD may avoid bias introduced by incomplete death reporting and aligns with recent literature advocating its use as a pragmatic RWD endpoint in health economic modeling.

Drug acquisition and total healthcare costs were significantly higher for ICI compared with Rego/Tri, resulting in an ICER exceeding conventional WTP. These results underscore the ongoing challenge of balancing clinical value with elevated costs in oncology [34, 35]. While trial-based analyses rarely integrate US payer benchmarks, our real-world approach using CMS pricing offers policy-relevant insights. Exploratory CEAC further illustrate that ICI therapies may only achieve favorable cost effectiveness under higher thresholds (≥ US$500,000 per QALY), highlighting the need for value-based pricing and innovative reimbursement models [24, 25].

Several key limitations merit discussion around our findings. Importantly, biomarker testing was incompletely documented, and MSI/dMMR status was not reliably captured, precluding restriction of the analysis to a biomarker-defined population or direct comparison within MSI-H/dMMR subgroups. Molecular testing information in clinical practice is commonly stored in non-structured formats such as scanned pathology reports or uploaded PDF files, which cannot be parsed into CPT, LOINC, or OMOP fields. Even when orders were present in structured tables, corresponding results were frequently missing or inconsistently linked. This likely led to underestimation of biomarker testing and underscores the broader informatics barrier in oncology RWD [12–16]. Second, incomplete coding of prior oxaliplatin/irinotecan/biologic exposure necessitated reliance on documented fluorouracil therapy as the minimum anchor for 3L+ definition, which may introduce misclassification. Participants in the Rego/Tri cohort were substantially more likely to have received prior systemic therapies compared with those in the ICI cohort. These imbalances likely reflect real-world sequencing patterns but introduce confounding that limits causal interpretation. Although propensity score matching or multivariable adjustment can balance measured covariates between treatment groups, such approaches cannot address unmeasured confounding, which remains a fundamental limitation of real-world oncology datasets. In the All of Us Research Program, key clinical variables such as ECOG performance status and disease stage at treatment initiation are not systematically captured, precluding robust adjustment and reinforcing the exploratory nature of the analysis. The exclusion of patients with < 21 days of therapy may preferentially omit individuals who discontinued very early due to intolerance, rapid clinical decline, or incomplete documentation, which could bias estimates toward patients with longer treatment persistence. Healthcare utilization events were variably coded across sites, and costs reflect CMS benchmarks rather than net prices inclusive of rebates or discounts. Additionally, the small analytic cohort (N = 82) limits precision, even with bootstrap resampling. Collectively, these limitations highlight the exploratory nature of the findings and the importance of viewing results as hypothesis generating rather than confirmatory. As the All of Us dataset matures and incorporates structured genomic results, future analyses will be able to replicate this comparison within MSI-H/dMMR-defined populations to reflect current clinical guidelines.

Microsimulation or Markov modeling approaches were considered; however, due to the small sample size, lack of complete biomarker stratification, and limited real-world follow-up data on progression and survival, we opted for a patient-level cost-effectiveness analysis using observed treatment duration. Future work may incorporate simulation modeling to project lifetime costs and outcomes, particularly in biomarker-defined subpopulations.

Conclusion

This exploratory analysis provides novel real-world evidence on the relative effectiveness, costs, and cost effectiveness of ICI versus Rego/Tri in advanced CRC. Findings demonstrate longer treatment persistence but substantially higher costs with ICI, yielding ICERs above conventional thresholds. These results reflect real-world practice patterns during a period of evolving biomarker testing and immunotherapy adoption and should not be interpreted as guidance for contemporary first-line treatment selection. While preliminary, these results underscore both the opportunities and challenges of using All of Us for pharmacoeconomic research and highlight the need for improved data infrastructure, biomarker documentation, and value-based frameworks to guide ICI adoption in recurrent advanced CRC.
