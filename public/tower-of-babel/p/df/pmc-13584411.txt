Strengthening patient safety competencies among physician interns through a structured quality and safety course
Authors: Muhammad Fazal Hussain Qureshi, Fozia Asif, Shahmeer Raza Khan, Munazza Akhtar, Muzna Shah, Ahmed Altaf, Asad Latif
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPatient safety is a fundamental principle of healthcare, yet adverse events remain a major cause of preventable morbidity and mortality worldwide. Human error is a leading contributor to these events. Patient safety education has been shown to improve healthcare providers’ knowledge, attitudes, and competencies, but evidence from LMICs remains limited. This study evaluates the effectiveness of a patient safety course implemented among medical interns.MethodsA one-arm quasi-experimental study was conducted among postgraduate medical interns at a private tertiary care hospital. Participants completed a structured Quality and Patient Safety (QPS) module consisting of pre-module assessment, two days of interactive didactic and applied learning sessions, and a post-module assessment. Quantitative data were analysed using paired statistical tests, while qualitative reflections underwent thematic analysis.ResultsA total of 166 medical interns participated, most without prior patient safety training. The intervention led to significant improvements in knowledge test scores, systems thinking, and knowledge efficacy across all cohorts (p < 0.001). Gains were particularly pronounced among interns without previous training. Qualitative reflections highlighted improved confidence, teamwork, and systems-oriented perspectives.ConclusionThis study provides evidence that a structured patient safety course introduced into the intern’s curriculum significantly increases knowledge, systems thinking and knowledge efficacy regardless of previous patient safety education exposure. The findings support that course should be formally included for early career clinical staff in LMICs where preventable harms remain a major patient safety concern.

Background

Primum non nocere is part of the Hippocratic oath every healthcare provider takes. The concept of ‘‘First, do no harm’’ came to attention after early studies conducted in the United States of America revealed that at least 44,000 Americans die each year as a result of an adverse event (AE) [1–4]. An adverse event is an unintended injury that is caused during medical management (rather than the underlying disease). In addition to increased length of stay and disability or death at the time of discharge [5], it strains the healthcare system financially [1]. Approximately one in ten patients are affected by an adverse event during the course of hospitalization [6]. A study revealed that the adverse event rate is even higher in low- and middle-income countries, in LMIC the rate was reported to be between 2.5% and 18.4%, of which 83% were judged to be preventable [7].

One of the most common reasons for preventable adverse events according to recent literature is human error [8]. Strengthening patient safety education can reduce preventable adverse events, but requires a comprehensive, system-centred approach addressing multiple dimensions of patient care [9]. Globally in the developed and developing countries Patient Safety education and courses has been formally integrated into the medical and nursing curriculum [10]. A recent meta-analysis by Park et al., synthesizing evidence from 15 studies conducted in the United States, South Korea, and Turkey, examined the impact of patient safety courses on nursing students’ knowledge, attitudes, and competencies. The analysis found that patient safety education significantly improved nursing students’ knowledge, attitudes, and competencies [11].

Evidence from low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) remains scarce in this regard, with very limited data available. A study from Tunisia reported that a patient safety educational intervention was effective among gynaecology and obstetrics healthcare professionals, highlighting staff commitment as a cornerstone for achieving and sustaining improvements [12]. Evidence from South Asia is extremely limited, with only three studies previously exploring this topic, all conducted by authors from the same institution as the present study. These studies introduced a patient safety course for undergraduate nursing and medical students and demonstrated its effectiveness [13–15].

Despite increasing global recognition of the importance of patient safety education, important gaps remain in the existing body of evidence. Much of the published literature originates from high-income settings, while data from low- and middle-income countries are limited, fragmented, and often confined to single institutions. In South Asia, the available studies are few and have primarily examined patient safety training among undergraduate medical and nursing students. There is limited empirical evidence evaluating such interventions at the level of medical internship, a formative stage of training during which graduates transition to supervised clinical practice and assume substantial responsibility for patient care. Given the heightened exposure to clinical decision-making and procedural tasks during internship, understanding the impact of structured patient safety education at this stage is particularly relevant. The present study seeks to address this gap by evaluating the effectiveness of a modified patient safety course implemented for medical interns, thereby contributing context-specific evidence to inform curricular development and strengthen patient safety initiatives in similar settings.

Methods

A quasi-experimental study design was employed for this study. The study population comprised postgraduate medical interns who had entered the workforce in a private tertiary care hospital following completion of their Bachelor of Medicine and Bachelor of Surgery (MBBS) degree.

Study population and inclusion criteria

Eligible participants were medical interns who had successfully obtained their MBBS degree and were enrolled in the one-year internship programme at the study institution during the data collection period (2023–2025). Interns were included regardless of their prior exposure to patient safety training. No exclusion criteria were applied, as the QPS module was a compulsory component of the internship curriculum and all enrolled interns were approached for participation with a response rate on 100%. Written informed consent was obtained from each of the participants prior to data collection, and the confidentiality of the data was ensured throughout the study.

Intervention

The intervention consisted of a structured Quality and Patient Safety module delivered to postgraduate medical interns. The module began with a pre-module online assessment administered through the institutional Virtual Learning Environment, followed by two consecutive in-person teaching days (13 contact hours). The in-person component comprised interactive didactic sessions and applied learning activities. Morning didactic lectures covered core topics including an introduction to quality and patient safety, the science of safety, error disclosure, safety culture, and conflict management. These lectures were followed by facilitated, group-based activities designed to reinforce and apply the concepts introduced. The applied learning components included case-based learning exercises on learning from defects, communication lab, and structured case-based discussions. The module concluded with a post-module assessment to evaluate participants’ knowledge acquisition and learning outcomes.

Evaluation

The pre and post test were modified and adapted from the Health Professional Education in Patient Safety Survey (H-PEPSS) [16], the Systems Thinking Scale (STS) [17], and Johns Hopkins PS course evaluation [18]. Data analysis

The data were paired at the individual student level and anonymized prior to analysis. All data were cleaned, coded and analysed using STATA (Version 17). Descriptive statistics were calculated, with means and standard deviations reported for continuous variables, including test scores, the Systems Thinking Scale (STS), and knowledge-efficacy scores. Paired t-tests were used to assess differences between pre- and post-test results, with a p-value of < 0.05 considered statistically significant. Frequencies and percentages were calculated for categorical demographic variables. Additionally, a subgroup analysis was conducted to examine differences in pre- and post-test scores between interns with prior patient safety training and those without, using paired t-tests.

A thematic analysis was conducted for the qualitative component of the study using NVivo Version 15. Each reflection was independently reviewed and coded by two reviewers. The assigned codes were then compared, and any discrepancies were discussed and resolved by a third reviewer before finalizing the coding framework. The finalized codes were subsequently grouped into overarching themes. Verbatim quotations are presented to preserve the authenticity and depth of participants’ perspectives, and the frequency of recurring themes is also reported. In instances where a participant referred to the same theme multiple times within a single reflection, it was counted once to avoid overrepresentation of individual responses in the overall thematic frequency.

Results

The study included 166 participants, predominantly female (67.5%), with most born in Sindh (76.2%). Nearly all participants reported proficiency in Urdu (98.7%) and English (96.8%), alongside regional and other languages (Shina, Balochi, Korean, Balti, Persian, Arabic, Gujrati). A slight majority graduated from private medical colleges (56.1%), and most had graduated recently, primarily between 2023 (42.1%) and 2024 (34.9%). Only 13.9% reported prior training in patient safety, most obtained through the Institute for Healthcare Improvement (56.5%), with training largely delivered via online modalities (60.8%). Overall systems thinking capacity improved significantly following the intervention, with the mean System Thinking Scale (STS) score increasing from 3.96 ± 0.22 to 4.09 ± 0.14 (mean difference = 0.13; p < 0.001). Significant gains were observed across several domains, particularly in seeking diverse perspectives, viewing problems as interconnected issues, recognizing system change over time, proposing system-level solutions, and accounting for historical and cultural influences (p < 0.05). The thematic analysis of 151 participant reflections described in Table 6 revealed strong positive engagement with the course. The most frequently reported theme was appreciation of the session (n = 87), highlighting the value of its interactive format and practical relevance. Discussion

This study demonstrates that a structured patient safety and quality course included in the internship curriculum resulted in significant improvements in patient safety knowledge. The participants showed increased knowledge acquisition, systems thinking capacity, and knowledge efficacy in this diverse cohort of recent medical graduates. Additionally, these knowledge improvements were observed consistently throughout different graduation years and independent of prior exposure to patient safety training. It shows the importance of broad educational gain and scalability of this intervention. These findings support the integration of proper patient safety and quality education module early in postgraduate clinical training, especially in context of low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) settings where patient safety education remains underdeveloped.

Moreover, a significant improvement was noted in post-test scores across three different graduating cohorts highlighting the effectiveness of the intervention. A comparable baseline exposure to patient safety modules during undergraduate medical education was noted across all three cohorts. The low baseline exposure to these modules raises concerns regarding systematic inclusion of patient safety modules in existing medical school curricula. The consistent improvement observed across cohorts indicate the appropriation of content for early years of clinical training. These findings align with prior studies showing patient safety and quality modules produce meaningful gain in knowledge regardless of participant’s seniority when baseline exposure is limited [13, 15, 19, 20].

This study has demonstrated an improvement of systematic thinking as measured by the System Thinking Scale (STS). The overall increase in STS scores reflect recognition of healthcare delivery as a complex system rather than isolated clinical actions. These improvements were particularly evident in domains including identifying interconnections between system components, recognizing temporal change, seeking diverse perspectives and system-level solutions. These findings are relevant in LMICs where patient harm is frequently caused by individual negligence rather than systematic gaps [21]. A systematic thinking process incorporated by this course may contribute towards individual error prevention strategies and emphasize learning. The betterment of systems thinking observed in this study is consistent with prior interventions in both high and resource-limited settings [22]. The ability to identify errors in context of system failures is a core competency for healthcare professionals and is identified as a determinant of patient safety [5]. The relatively small change in some individual STS components may be due to the short duration of the intervention as systems thinking is known to develop through repeated clinical exposure and practice [19]. However, overall improvement in STS score suggests that even brief interventions can induce meaningful patient safety cognition amongst interns.

Similarly, knowledge efficacy showed the highest improvement after intervention. The significant gains were seen across all assessed domains with most pronounced improvements seen in inter-professional communication, speaking up for patient safety, identifying and managing safety risks, understanding contributors to adverse events and knowledge of hospital error reporting system. These domains represent important behavioural competencies that are often under-prioritized in traditional medical training but are responsible for safe clinical practices. There was substantial improvement noted in error reporting knowledge of the participants which is important in LMICs context due to lack of reporting because of fear of blame, lack of awareness and vague reporting mechanisms [23]. This finding suggests that patient safety curricula may benefit from allocating greater time to less familiar domains such as systems analysis and communication. Knowledge on personal protective equipment showed least knowledge gain as it was one of the topics not discussed in the course due to time constraints.

Moreover, it was noted that interns with no prior patient safety training showed substantially greater improvements in test scores compared with those who had previous training. It provides further insight into the effectiveness of this intervention and the capacity for gain with low baseline knowledge. Additionally, both groups showed comparable improvements in systems thinking and knowledge efficacy. Even though interns with previous training in patient safety had a higher baseline score in all three domain they also gained a significant knowledge gain from this course. It indicates and aligns with previous studies [24] that this course reinforces existing competencies while addressing introductory framework for novice learners.

The qualitative findings complement the quantitative results and provide evidence of high learner engagement and perceived course relevance. Participants’ responses emphasized on the appreciation of the session, the use of real-life clinical scenarios and focused on teamwork and communication. These themes present similar findings from previous qualitative studies which have showed that participants valued discussion-based learning more than complete didactic approaches [13, 15, 25, 26]. The increase in confidence reported by 33 participants suggests that the course increased knowledge along with influence on professional attitude. Notably, several reflections revealed a reduction in fear associated with medical error, a dimension that extends beyond knowledge acquisition into professional identity and emotional resilience. Participants who described feeling less self-doubt when minor errors occur articulated a shift in how they relate to the occurrence of errors in the complexity of modern healthcare systems, a shift the course appears to catalyse by reframing error as a systems phenomenon rather than a personal failing. This has potential implications for the broader literature on psychological safety in clinical training, which identifies fear of blame as a significant barrier to error reporting and open communication [27]. The ‘second victim’ phenomenon, whereby clinicians experience distress, self-doubt, and professional crisis following adverse events is well documented and disproportionately affects early career practitioners [28]. Curricula that address the emotional and identity dimensions of error, not only the technical, may therefore have protective effects on trainee wellbeing alongside their educational value. The participants identified gaps between institutional policies and practices along with suggestions to extend the course duration and inclusion of all patient care stakeholders. It underscores the acceptability and application of patient safety content to real clinical environments. Incorporating such approaches into future curricula will have an amplified impact especially in error prone settings.

Regardless of this study’s strengths, there are some limitations noted. The one-arm quasi-experimental design, while appropriate for an implementation evaluation within a mandatory curriculum, precluded the use of a concurrent control group and limits causal attribution of observed gains. Future studies should consider a waitlist-control design or comparison with a concurrent cohort receiving standard internship training without the structured QPS module, to allow more robust causal inference. Furthermore, self-reported measures overestimate actual competence. As patient safety competencies are better evaluated through behaviour observation and patient outcomes, it is important to consider future studies based on longitudinal designs. Future evaluations of patient safety courses should consider measuring psychological safety and emotional responses to error as distinct outcomes.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that a structured patient safety course introduced into the intern’s curriculum significantly increases knowledge, systems thinking and knowledge efficacy regardless of previous patient safety education exposure. The findings support that this course should be formally included for early career clinical staff in LMICs where preventable harms remain a major patient safety concern. This course helped interns gain important system perspectives, communication skills and confidence to resolve conflicts to implement in everyday clinical settings.
