Psychosocial mediators of a physical activity and dietary intervention in pregnant women with overweight or obesity
Authors: Meghan Baruth, Sara Wilcox, Jihong Liu, Rebecca Schlaff
Published: 2026

Background

Despite the established guidelines for physical activity [1, 2], dietary intake [3], and weight gain [4], many women do not meet these recommendations during pregnancy [4]. Only about one-third of women gain within the recommended range; 48% gain more [5]. Less than one-quarter of pregnant women meet physical activity guidelines [6]. Further, the average Healthy Eating Index Score among pregnant women, which measures diet quality, is 63 (on a scale from 0 to 100 where 100 is best) [3]. These data support the need for behavioral interventions aimed at promoting physical activity, healthy dietary behaviors, and appropriate weight gain in pregnant populations.

Behavioral interventions have been shown to be effective in improving physical activity and dietary behaviors in pregnant populations [7–10]; there is also evidence showing they can promote healthy weight gain [11]. Although it is important to understand the effectiveness of an intervention in changing the intended outcomes, perhaps just as important is understanding how the program exerted its effects. Mediation analyses estimate the extent to which an intervention affects an outcome through a potential causal mechanism [12]. In behavioral interventions, we often examine psychosocial variables as potential mediators. Findings from these analyses can be used to advance theory, optimize interventions, and facilitate the implementation of interventions in public health practice [12].

The most recent review of mediation studies targeting physical activity outcomes found 51 published studies in non-clinical populations [13]. Results found that overall, psychosocial constructs (including those from the Social Cognitive Theory) were significant mediators of change in physical activity outcomes; however, the effects were very small and it didn’t appear that any specific construct was more important than the others [13]. This points to the need for additional studies to really understand how interventions change outcomes and which theoretical constructs may be of most importance. Also of note, none of the mediation studies included in this review were in pregnant populations.

Mediation studies in pregnant populations are lacking. The single published study available was conducted in a sample of pregnant Latino women [14]. Results found that none of the hypothesized mediators (healthy food beliefs, intention, perceived control) mediated changes in a variety of dietary outcomes (i.e. dietary intake of vegetables, fiber, added sugar, total fat) following a healthy lifestyle intervention. No mediation studies focused on physical activity outcomes in pregnant populations were found.

To better understand how interventions work (or perhaps why they do not work), it is important that mediation analyses are conducted and published. This will allow for the creation of more targeted and effective interventions. The mechanisms by which interventions work may differ based on the intervention approach, the population included, and the behavioral outcomes that are targeted. Therefore, the purpose of this study is to examine the psychosocial mediators of physical activity and dietary outcomes in a sample of pregnant women with overweight or obesity participating in the Health in Pregnancy and Postpartum (HIPP) randomized controlled trial [15].

Methods

The methods of HIPP are described in detail elsewhere [15–17]. HIPP was a randomized controlled trial that recruited pregnant African American and white women with overweight or obesity to participate in a behavioral lifestyle intervention aimed at promoting appropriate gestational weight gain. The institutional review boards of the three participating healthcare centers and the University of South Carolina approved this study.

Participants

Participants were primarily recruited to take part in the study through obstetrics and gynecology clinics in South Carolina. In order to participate, women had to be 18–44 years of age, white or Black/African American, able to read and speak English, had no plans to move from the area in the next 18 months, ≤ 16 weeks gestation, had a pre-pregnancy body mass index ≥ 25 kg/m2, a pre-pregnancy weight ≤ 370 pounds, regular access to a telephone, and were willing to participate in weekly telephone calls. Exclusion criteria included uncontrolled hypertension, use of insulin, uncontrolled or untreated thyroid disease, hospitalization for mental health or substance use disorder in the past 6 months, multiple gestation, persistent bleeding in the first trimester, physical disabilities that prevent exercise, physician advice to not exercise during pregnancy, and history of > 3 miscarriages, eating disorder or malnutrition, or incompetent cervix [16]. All participants provided written informed consent prior to study enrollment.

Behavioral intervention

Participants were randomized to receive the behavioral intervention or standard care. The behavioral intervention has been described in great deal elsewhere [15, 16]. To summarize, the primary goals of the behavioral intervention, guided by the Social Cognitive Theory [18], were to improve diet, increase physical activity, and to promote healthy gestational weight gain. Participants received an in-depth counseling session followed by brief telephone counseling, 10 behavioral podcasts, and access to a private Facebook group. Participants in the standard care group attended their scheduled visits with their prenatal care provider. They also received monthly mailings (n = 6) and links to podcasts (n = 10) that aligned with the timing of the intervention group’s podcasts. The resources focused on a healthy pregnancy and fetal development, however, they did not discuss physical activity, diet, or weight gain.

Measures

Data were collected during early pregnancy (≤ 16 weeks gestation, prior to randomization) and late pregnancy (32 weeks gestation).

Physical activity

The SenseWear Armband was used to objectively measure physical activity [19, 20]. Participants were asked to wear the armband on the upper left arm for 8 days. Data were used in the study for participants who wore the armband on ≥ 5 days, ≥ 1 weekend day, ≥ 21 h/day.

Participants not meeting these criteria were able to wear the armband again. Moderate to vigorous intensity physical activity was classified as ≥ 3 metabolic equivalents (METS), and sedentary behavior (≤ 1.5 METS). The percentage of time spent in sedentary and moderate to vigorous intensity physical activity was calculated and used in this study.

Diet

Participants completed two randomly assigned dietary recalls using the Automated Self-Administered 24-h dietary recall (ASA24) [21, 22]. One recall was conducted for a weekday and one for a weekend. The first recall was completed at the measurement session; the second within the next 7 days. Data were used in the study for participants who completed one weekday and one weekend recall (which could include Friday). Total calories consumed was calculated by taking the average of the two recalls.

Diet quality was measured by scoring the two dietary recalls with the National Cancer Institute’s Healthy Eating Index-2015 algorithm [23]. The HEI-2015 includes 13 component that determined diet quality relative to the 2015–2019 Dietary Guidelines for Americans [24]. Scores can range from 0 to 100, where a higher score indicates a higher diet quality.

Social support

Social support for exercise was assessed using 13 items from the validated Friend Support for Exercise Habits Scale and the Family Support for Exercise Habits Scale [25]. Participants rated how frequently family and friends (separately) did each activity in the past month on a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (none) to 5 (very often). Higher scores indicated more support. Internal consistency reliability was α = 0.89 (friends) and α = 0.86 (family).

Social support for diet was assessed using 10 items from the validated Friend Support for Diet Scale and the Family Support for Diet Scale [25]. Participants rated how frequently family and friends (separately) did each activity in the past month using a 5-point scale ranging from 1 (none) to 5 (very often). Higher scores indicated more support. Internal consistency reliability was α = 0.67 (friends) and α = 0.74 (family).

Self-efficacy

Self-efficacy for overcoming barriers to exercise was assessed using the 5-item validated Marcus et al. scale [26]. Participants rated their confidence to overcome common barriers related to exercise on a scale from 1 (not at all confident) to 7 (very confident). Higher scores indicated higher self-efficacy. Internal consistency reliability for the measure was α = 0.74.

Self-efficacy for overcoming barriers to eating a healthy diet was assed using the 10-item validated Self-efficacy for Diet Questionnaire [27]. Participants rated their confidence to eat a healthy diet in challenging situations on a scale from 1 (not at all confident) to 4 (very confident). Higher scores indicated higher self-efficacy. Internal consistency reliability for the measure was α = 0.86.

Self-regulation

Self-regulation for exercise was assessed using the 10-item validated Exercise Goal-Setting Scale and the 10-item Exercise Planning and Scheduling Scale [28]. Participants rated the extent to which each item described them on a scale from 1 (does not describe) to 3 (describes moderately) to 5 (describes completely). A higher score indicated higher use of self-regulation skills. Internal consistency reliability was α = 0.91(goal setting) and α = 0.84 (planning).

Self-regulation focused specifically on self-monitoring for diet was assessed using 6 items from the HealthStyles survey [29]. Participants rated (yes/no) whether they used each of the strategies to control their weight. A higher score indicated higher use of self-monitoring activities. Internal consistency reliability for the measure was α = 0.56.

Demographic / health-related characteristics

Age, education, marital status, income, employment status, race/ethnicity, baseline weight status, weeks gestation, and parity were collected at baseline.

Statistical analysis

Analyses only include participants with complete pre/post outcome data (n = 167 for PA outcomes; n = 191 for dietary outcomes). Basic descriptive statistics described the study sample. Change scores were calculated for each potential mediator and outcome variable by subtracting the variable at baseline from the variable at 32 weeks; they were used in all analyses. The SPSS PROCESS macro (Model 4) was used to examine whether changes in social support, self-efficacy, and self-regulation mediated changes in the physical activity and diet outcomes. All mediators for a given outcome variable were entered into the model simultaneously (parallel mediation model). The mediation model is shown in Fig. 1. Bias corrected bootstrapping with 10,000 resamples was used to generate 95% confidence intervals for direct and indirect effects. Confidence intervals that did not include zero indicated significant mediation [30]. All models controlled for baseline gestation, age, parity, education, race, and pre-pregnancy weight status. For both self-regulation (goal setting) and self-regulation (planning), the intervention group reported increases whereas the standard care group reported decreases. Mediation – dietary outcomes

Table 5 shows the results from the mediation analyses in dietary outcomes. Results showed that being in the intervention group was associated with significant increases in self-regulation (self-monitoring (p = 0.005). Increases in self-regulation (self-monitoring) were associated with an increase in diet quality (HEI score) (p = 0.000). Results showed that self-regulation (self-monitoring) was a significant mediator of increases in diet quality (HEI score) (95% CI: -3.59, -0.51) and decreases in total caloric intake (95% CI: 0.51, 72.60).

Discussion

This study examined the psychosocial mediators of a behavioral intervention targeting physical activity and dietary behaviors in a sample of overweight and obese pregnant women. Although lacking, studies examining mediators of change in behavioral interventions targeting pregnant women are important to understand how an intervention changes the targeted behavior(s). Mediation analyses can also shed light on the potential reasons an intervention may or may not have worked. Such an understanding would allow for researchers to develop interventions that can be most effective in changing the targeted mediators, and thus outcomes.

Results from this study found that self-regulation, and more specifically self-monitoring, was a significant mediator of changes in diet quality (HEI score) and total calories consumed. Several activities in the HIPP behavioral intervention targeted self-regulation skills. For example, participants monitored their weight using a bathroom scale, tracked their weight on a personalized weight gain tracking chart, set goals related to dietary behaviors, and problem solved any barriers encountered. Process evaluation showed that some of the self-regulation skills (i.e. setting healthy eating goals, weighing themselves, using problem solving strategies) were reported as being the most frequently used and/or were perceived as being the most helpful to participants [31]. Similar to other studies [32, 33], our findings support the role of and importance of self-regulation in changing dietary outcomes in various populations. Future behavioral interventions in pregnant populations should continue to incorporate self-regulation activities like self-monitoring, goal setting, rewards, and problem solving.

The overall results from the HIPP intervention showed no significant changes in pregnancy physical activity and dietary outcomes [16]. Results from these mediation analyses allow us to look more closely at potential explanations for the lack of findings. Findings from our study showed that the behavioral intervention was not successful in changing many of the mediators that were hypothesized to change the behavioral outcomes. This path of the ‘causal chain’ was broken; therefore, it may not surprising that the intervention didn’t change the outcomes as intended. Although intervention activities were developed to target these mediators, it is possible that they were not robust enough to change them or robust enough to detect change given the sample size. Further, process evaluation data found that some of the mediators that did not change as a result of the intervention (e.g. asking people for support and help with eating healthy and exercising, using an exercise log to track exercise) were among the lowest rated in frequency of use and perceived helpfulness by participants [31]. These findings can be used to improve/build upon this intervention in the future where more and/or different activities focused on building support and self-efficacy, for example, could be incorporated. Another possible explanation is that the exposure to, delivery method of (e.g. phone vs. in person, podcasts), and/or length of the intervention activity doses (e.g. 20-minute phone calls) may not have been intense enough to elicit changes in the mediators in this population. Regardless, these findings provide insight into how interventions can more intensely and effectively target these mediators in future interventions.

Mediation studies are lacking in behavioral interventions with pregnant populations. We were only able to find one other mediation study in a pregnant population [14]. This study examined mediators of dietary change in a sample of pregnant Latina women participating in a culturally tailored behavioral intervention based on the Theory of Planned Behavior and the social support theory. Results found that none of the hypothesized mediators were found to be significant mediators of changes in dietary outcomes [14]. No mediator studies targeting physical activity outcomes in pregnant populations have been published. These findings, along with ours, indicate that the mechanisms by which these interventions exert their effects in this population are unclear.

Conclusions

This study is contributing to the very limited literature examining psychosocial mediators of change in physical activity and dietary outcomes in pregnant women. Although there are many strengths, we also recognize some limitations to this study including the smaller than anticipated sample size. A recent review found that overall, interventions were linked to changes in the theoretical mediators. However the size of the effects were small [34]; our small sample size may have limited our ability to detect changes, especially if the changes were small. There may also be limitations related to the generalizability of our findings given all women were from a single region in one state.

Perhaps as important as publishing the impact of an intervention on changing the targeted outcomes is publishing the results of mediation analyses. Mediation analyses provide insight into both ‘pieces’ of the mediation chain; they allow us to look into the ‘black box’ that is behavior change. They allow researchers to better understand the ‘how’ of an intervention’s effects and/or the ‘why’ behind why an intervention worked or did not work. The lack of publications in pregnant populations highlights the need for additional studies to be conducted and published. Such efforts will help to understand how interventions exert their effects and will ultimately allow for the creation and implementation of more targeted and effective behavioral interventions in pregnant women.
