Outcomes, survival, mortality, and major adverse events in aortic valve replacement surgeries in Africa: A systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Victory Bassey Effiom, Victor Oluwafemi Femi-Lawal, Atharv Vivek Oak, Oluwasegun John Oyesola, Fatma Kamal Ahmed, Samuel Ghislain Junior Fodop, Michael James Anayo, Achanga Bill-Smith Anyinkeng, Jonas Paul Ibekwe, Kolawole Victor Olalekan, Samuel Mesfin Girma, Daniel Brabi, Ahmet Kilic
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesAortic valve replacement (AVR) is the definitive treatment for severe aortic valve disease, yet outcome data from Africa remain scarce. This systematic review and meta-analysis assessed survival, mortality, and major adverse events after AVR across Africa.MethodsAdhering to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines, we searched PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science for studies reporting AVR outcomes in African populations, covering randomized trials, cohort studies, case series, and case reports. Subgroup analyses were conducted by AVR modality: surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR), minimally invasive surgery (MICS), and transcatheter aortic valve implantation. Study quality was assessed using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool 2 and the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Outcomes included survival, mortality, echocardiographic improvements, and valve- or procedure-related complications.ResultsOf 2547 studies screened, 21 studies involving 1303 patients across 6 African countries were included; 15 (71.4%) studies were from Egypt. Mean age was 60.4 years; 43.3% were female. Aortic stenosis (70.4%) and regurgitation (64.0%) predominated. Modalities included SAVR (45.1%), minimally invasive surgery (27.8%), and transcatheter aortic valve implantation (27.1%). Technical success rate was high (97.5%-100%). Complications included arrhythmias (16.0%), postoperative bleeding (15.0%), paravalvular leak (5.0%), pacemaker requirement (6.0%), stroke (5.0%), and myocardial infarction (3.0%). Reoperation rate was 4.4%. Thirty-day mortality was 5.0% (95% CI. 3.1%-8.1%), with 1-year and 5-year survival of 93.2% and 91.0%, respectively.ConclusionsAVR outcomes in Africa show favorable success and survival rate although below optimal benchmarks despite resource constraints. However, results are not generalizable continent-wide, given the heavy geographic skew. Complications including arrhythmias and postoperative bleeding necessitate enhanced perioperative protocols.

Perspective

This study synthesizes evidence on the outcomes of aortic valve replacement (AVR) in the African continent.

Aortic valve replacement (AVR) is the definitive surgical treatment for individuals with a severely diseased aortic valve and involves replacing the damaged valve with a mechanical or biological prosthesis.1 This procedure is the standard treatment for most young adults with severe aortic stenosis (AS) and/or regurgitation. The causes of aortic valve disease are diverse and include aortic insufficiency, endocarditis, and congenital diseases such as a bicuspid aortic valve (BAV).1,2 In developing countries, particularly within the African continent, rheumatic heart disease remains the major cause of aortic valve disease, and surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) through full sternotomy is the conventional method.3,4 Because of the nature of the underlying disease, aortic valve repair options are limited, making SAVR the treatment of choice for the majority of patients with symptomatic rheumatic aortic valve disease.5 AS is known to have a long latent period that is often followed by fast deterioration once patients develop symptoms,6 with a mortality rate of 50% documented in the first 2 years if left untreated.7 For several years, SAVR has been the standard treatment for symptomatic patients with severe AS. In patients without serious comorbidities, SAVR often carries a reduced operative risk of mortality.8 However, in clinical practice, approximately 30% to 50% of patients with severe AS are not offered surgical replacement.9

AVR has also been reported to be the surgery of choice for patients with severe aortic regurgitation.10 Despite good long-term outcomes, it can involve significant risks of complications and impairment of quality of life for various valid reasons.11 Although AS is often the result of degenerative processes and does not progress to severe disease until later in life,12,13 aortic regurgitation is usually as a result of congenital aortic valve anomaly or acute infective endocarditis.14 These patients often present early in life. Hence, surgical treatment is the major intervention method in this young group of patients.15 Aortic insufficiency is associated with increasing age in developed countries but is reported to be greatly associated with infectious etiologies like rheumatic heart disease and infective endocarditis in developing countries.16,17 AVR is an indispensable treatment option for those with aortic valve disease. Aortic valve replacement surgery is a key treatment option in the management of these pathologies, ensuring improved survival rates and quality of life for patients.18 This procedure has become routine in recent times, with significant advances and excellent outcomes, yet it continues to carry various complications associated with the underlying heart condition and the use of cardiopulmonary bypass.5 Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aim at examining the most up-to-date published literature within the African continent to draw conclusions on the outcomes, survival rates, mortality rates, and major adverse events in AVR surgeries in Africa.

Methods

Study Design

This study was a systematic review and meta-analysis of published studies evaluating the outcomes of aortic valve replacement surgery in Africa. This study was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines.19 We registered this study on the International Prospective Register for Systematic Reviews, with ID number CRD420250651350.

Inclusion/Exclusion Criteria

We used the PICOS (population, intervention, comparator, outcome, and study design) framework to define our inclusion criteria for this study. We included human studies conducted in any of the 54 African countries, involving either pediatric or adult populations. These studies included original research papers, including randomized controlled trials, case reports, and cohort studies. We excluded papers not in English, systematic reviews, commentaries, meta-analyses, abstracts, and studies involving non-African populations.

Intervention

Patients underwent AVR as a treatment for any aortic valve disease. Interventions could be conventional SAVR, minimally invasive SAVR (minimally invasive surgery, or MISC), or transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI).

Control

All types of controls/comparators were included.

Outcomes

We included studies that reported outcomes including mortality, survival, and adverse events after aortic valve replacement surgery.

Search Strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted to identify relevant studies assessing the outcomes, survival rates, mortality rates, and major adverse events in aortic valve replacement surgeries in the different African countries from PubMed (MEDLINE), Embase, and Web of Science from January 1, 2000, until November 31, 2024. The systematic literature review was conducted by 2 independent investigators, who used a comprehensive search strategy that incorporated terms related to “Aortic valve replacement,” “Transcatheter aortic valve implantation,” “Surgical aortic valve replacement,” AND “Africa.” Search terms were combined with Boolean operators, Medical Subject Heading terms, field tags, and other relevant operators relevant to each database.

Study Selection

Search results were exported to Rayyan.ai, a systematic review software, for deduplication and detailed screening. Initially, titles and abstracts were reviewed by 7 independent authors, followed by a full-text screening of potentially relevant studies. The full texts of papers included from the title and abstract screening phase were assessed for eligibility by four independent reviewers, with conflicts resolved by a third reviewer (V.O.F.). Articles that were included were transferred to a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet for data extraction.

Data Extraction

A structured data collection form was created in Google Sheets to systematically extract relevant information from each included study. The extracted data covered multiple key aspects, including the study title, publication year, first author's name, study year, country of origin, patient characteristics, comorbidities, valve pathology, type of intervention performed or surgical approach, valve-related complication, systemic complications and the outcome measures assessed. Data extraction was performed independently by 6 investigators to ensure accuracy. Subsequently, the lead investigator reviewed the data.

Data Synthesis

Data analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel and R, version 4.4.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing). Pooled analyses were calculated using the random effects model. Improvements in echocardiographic parameters such as left ventricular ejection fraction, aortic valve area, and aortic valve mean pressure gradient were calculated using standardized mean difference. Values less than 0 indicated that the effect was nonsignificant. Values between 0 and 0.19 meant a negligible effect, values between 0.20 and 0.39 indicated a small effect, values between 0.40 and 0.59 were taken as moderate, and values between 0.60 and 0.79 were taken as moderately large. Values greater than 0.80 indicating a large effect. For outcomes such as mortality, survival rates, and complications, we calculated pooled prevalences. Heterogeneity among the studies was assessed using the I2 statistic, with values between 0% and 25% indicating low heterogeneity, values between 26% and 50% indicating moderate heterogeneity, values between 51% and 75% indicating substantial heterogeneity, and values greater than 75% indicating high heterogeneity. We also used the χ2 test in assessing heterogeneity. Because of the relatively small number of studies reporting quantitative data, we did not perform subgroup analyses.

Quality Assessment

To assess the quality of the included randomized controlled trials, the Cochrane Risk of Bias assessment tool 2 was used. For cohort studies, we evaluated study quality using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale. Four independent reviewers evaluated the risk of bias for each study. Any disagreements between the reviewers were resolved through discussion or by the lead investigator.

Results

Study Selection

The databases searches yielded 2547 citations from PubMed, Embase, and Web of Science. After deduplication, we obtained 2201 unique citations for further assessment. We excluded 1866 articles based on title and abstract screening. After full-text evaluation, we included 21 studies in this review with the complete selection process detailed in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses flow diagram provided in Figure 1.Figure 1Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses flowchart of the study selection process.

The large number of records excluded following full-text review (n = 2526) reflects the breadth of the initial search strategy, which was intentionally sensitive to minimize the risk of missing eligible studies. The majority of excluded records were duplicates, did not report outcomes specifically for African populations, involved non-AVR cardiac procedures, or were systematic reviews, conference abstracts, or commentaries per our prespecified exclusion criteria.

Study Characteristics

Of the studies included in this review, there were 18 cohort studies, 1 randomized controlled trial, 1 case series, and 1 case report. The studies were published between 2012 and 2024 and conducted in 6 countries. Fifteen studies (71.4%) were conducted in Egypt,E1, E2, E3, E4, E5, E6, E7, E8, E9, E10, E11, E12, E13, E14, E15 2 studies were conducted in South Africa,E16,E17 and 1 study each was conducted in Tunisia,E18 Morocco,E19 Senegal,E20 and Cameroon.E21 The total sample size across the publications that reported on sample size was 1303. There was a male predilection, with the participants being 43.3% female. The average age of participants was 60.36 ± 8.78 years.

Patient Characteristics and Preoperative Echocardiographic Parameters

The overall prevalence of AS (pure and mixed) was 70.4%; the overall prevalence of aortic regurgitation (pure and mixed) was 64.02%. The mean preoperative European System for Cardiac Operative Risk Evaluation was 5.735 ± 3.93, and the mean preoperative Society of Thoracic Surgeons score was 3.063 ± 3.77. Mean preoperative left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) was 59.1 ± 9.54%. The mean preoperative aortic valve area was 0.90 ± 0.31 cm2, and the mean preoperative aortic valve pressure gradient was 49.18 ± 14.31 mm Hg. The mean preoperative annulus diameter was 22.86 ± 2.6 mm.

Surgical Characteristics

In total, 550 patients (45.1%) underwent conventional surgery, 339 patients (27.8%) underwent MISC, and 330 patients (27.1%) underwent TAVI. Technical success rate was high across all studies, ranging from 97.5% (Soliman and colleaguesE4) to 100% (reported in most studies).

Operative Outcomes

Improvements in echocardiographic parameters and functional status

There was no statistically significant improvement in LVEF (standardized mean difference [SMD], 0.63; 95% CI, −0.08 to 1.34) or aortic valve area (SMD, 4.47; 95% CI, −0.86 to 9.80). However, there was a significant improvement in aortic valve mean pressure gradient (SMD, −3.64; 95% CI, −5.05 to −2.23). There were no significant subgroup differences for these outcomes.

Complications

Valve-related complications

Paravalvular leaks occurred in 5% (95% CI, 2%-17%) of patients. Subgroup analysis showed that there was a paravalvular leak prevalence of 2% (95% CI, 1%-9%) in TAVI, 5% in conventional AVR (95% CI, 0%-25%), and 10% (95% CI, 1%-61%) in the MICS group. Endocarditis had a prevalence rate of 1.9% (95% CI, 0.5%-7.7%). No subgroup analysis was conducted for endocarditis as only studies involving open heart surgery reported this outcome.

Systemic complications

From the meta-analyses, transient ischemic attacks occurred in 3% (95% CI, 1%-12%) of patients. No subgroup analysis was conducted for this outcome because of the limited number of studies. Stroke occurred in 5% (95% CI, 3%-8%) of patients. The frequency of postoperative strokes was 6% (95% CI, 3%-14%) in TAVI, 5% (95% CI, 3%-10%) in conventional AVR, and 3% (95% CI, 0%-10%) in MICS. Myocardial infarction was recorded in 3% (95% CI, 1%-9%) of patients. From subgroup analysis, the frequency of postoperative myocardial infarction was 2% (95% CI, 0%-13%) in TAVI and 4% (95% CI, 1%-13%) in conventional AVR. Permanent pacemakers were required in 6% (95% CI, 4%-10%) of patients. The frequency of this was 5% (95% CI, 3%-9%) in conventional AVR and 6% (95% CI, 2%-14%) in TAVI. In a study involving minimally invasive cardiac surgery, Holzamer and colleaguesE17 reported a 20% pacemaker requirement rate. Arrhythmias were recorded in 16% (95% CI, 9%-28%) of patients, the most common complication. The frequency of arrhythmias was 19% (95% CI, 7%-40%) in conventional AVR, 16% (95% CI, 1%-75%) in MICS, and 10% (95% CI, 1%-54%) in TAVI. Postoperative bleeding was the second commonest complication and was recorded in 15.1% (95% CI, 9%-23%) of patients. The frequency of postoperative bleeding was 15% (95% CI, 8%-27%) in conventional AVR, 12% (95% CI, 3%-42%) in TAVI, and 17% (95% CI, 4%-41%) in MICS.

Reoperation

The pooled reoperation rate was 4.4% (95% CI, 2.4%-7.8%).

Intensive Care Unit (ICU) Length of Stay

The overall mean length of stay in the ICU was 2.58 ± 2.25 days. For conventional AVR, the mean ICU length of stay was 3.03 ± 2.42 days. For minimally invasive cardiac surgery, the mean ICU length of stay was 1.62 ± 1.41 days.

Hospital Length of Stay

The overall mean length of stay in the hospital was 9.71 ± 6.15 days. For conventional AVR, the mean hospital length of stay was 10.03 ± 6.72 days. For patients who underwent minimally invasive surgery, the mean hospital length of stay was 9.22 ± 4.80 days.

Mortality

Overall, the 30-day mortality rate was 5% (95% CI, 3.1%-8.1%).

One-Year Survival

The pooled 1-year survival rate was 93.2% (95% CI, 86.9%-96.5%).

Discussion

Across the included African cohorts, aortic valve disease was predominantly severe and often complex: AS was present in approximately 70% of patients, and aortic regurgitation in approximately 64%. These figures highlight the high burden of mixed lesions in this region, which differs significantly from that seen in high-income countries on other continents, such as Italy and the United States, where age-related calcific AS predominates and mixed lesions are relatively uncommon.20,21 This difference is likely because, in Africa, rheumatic heart disease still accounts for the major burden of disease in patients needing heart valve interventions, particularly affecting marginalized populations in rural and underserved communities, where health care access remains constrained.

Our study found encouraging survival rates with the 1-year survival at 93.2%, and 5-year survival remaining high at 91%. These findings were consistent with those of a previous study conducted in the United States, where it was shown that a 5-year survival rate of isolated SAVR in low-risk patients was 92.9%.22 Another meta-analysis study of 5498 Japanese patients with 5-year follow-up also revealed a trend towards improved survival for SAVR in patients deemed low risk.23 These excellent early and midterm results reinforce the role of AVR in the treatment of aortic valve disease, especially in the younger low-risk patient population, with long life expectancy and lower operative risk. Although there have been several studies showing good survival rates after AVR, a study from Sweden found that survival was lower after AVR than in the general population and that the difference in survival became more pronounced after approximately 6 years.24 The differences between the studies can be explained by the fact that our study was of patients who had a severely diseased aortic valve only, whereas their study included patients who were undergoing coronary artery bypass grafting. Another difference between the studies can be explained by the larger number of patients in their study.

It is also interesting to see that there is a low 30-day mortality rate of approximately 5%. This is no different from a cohort study carried out by Husso and colleagues25 on 75 propensity score–matched patients, where it was found that the 30-day mortality rate of patients with BAV undergoing SAVR was 5.3%. Although Husso and colleagues went further to show the 2-year mortality rate as 18.7%, our study did not reveal any long-term mortality rate. The 30-day mortality rate from this current study was inconsistent with what was reported by Chen and colleagues26 in Australia, where patients with BAV undergoing transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) had a 30-day overall mortality of 2.1%. It is possible that the lower mortality rate may have been caused by the fact that the study highlighted just patients with BAV who underwent TAVR, which is in contrast with our study, which was focused on all patients with aortic valve disease who underwent AVR surgery. Furthermore, Australia is a developed country; hence, there may be better health care systems and facilities for this kind of surgery, which is not the same in Africa. The greater mortality rate in our study signals a need for strengthened perioperative care pathways, more consistent early referral, and better access to intensive care resources.

It has been reported that AVR involving either implantation of biological or mechanical aortic valve prostheses comes with the inevitable risk of short- and long-term complications, such as bleeding, stroke, prosthetic valve endocarditis, and valve degeneration and subsequent reoperation.27,28 Hence, the results of our study reflect low risks for adverse events such as transient ischemic attacks, stroke, and myocardial infarction after AVR surgeries in Africa, with arrhythmias and postoperative bleeding being the most common complications. Bleeding complications in patients who undergo AVR in this region are likely attributable to the invasiveness of open-heart surgery, because most studies reported this surgical approach as what was being used. The observed occurrence of stroke, although less expected, highlights the fact that it remains an important issue in aortic valve intervention even in low-risk patients, and the long-term effect on patients should not be discounted despite its low occurrence. This reflects that AVR is a safe procedure; however, these low risks can also reflect underreporting because most included studies did not report adverse event occurrence.

As noted by our study, the incidence of device-related complications, particularly permanent pacemaker implantation and paravalvular leak, was approximately 6% and 5%. This observation mirrors outcomes from the FRANCE 2 registry and other observational cohorts, which were mainly comparing TAVR with SAVR.29, 30, 31, 32 The increased rate of conduction abnormalities requiring pacemaker placement is mostly linked to the anatomical proximity of the aortic valve annulus to the cardiac conduction system, especially when using self-expanding valves.

One final observation from our meta-analysis relates to the notable technical success rates which were uniformly high (≥97.5%) reporting significant improvements in hemodynamic parameters, particularly a fall in the mean aortic valve pressure gradient by 3.64 mm Hg. However, there were no statistically significant differences in the echocardiographic parameters of LVEF and aortic valve area, which likely reflect the chronic nature of rheumatic pathology and underline the importance of early surgical referral before irreversible myocardial changes occur. This findings suggest that, in the specific centers studied in Africa, outcomes achieved are favorable, indicating that high-quality care is possible even in resource-limited settings. Caution is warranted in interpreting these results as representative of Africa broadly, given the geographic concentration of included data. Nevertheless, this does not overlook the challenge of inadequate infrastructure and limited access to essential surgical tools and postoperative care facilities across the continent.

Study Strengths and Limitations

This review is strengthened by its comprehensive inclusion of African data, which is often underrepresented in global meta-analyses, as well as by systematic searching across multiple databases with quality assessment of included studies. However, several limitations exist. Eighteen of the 21 included studies were observational, and 1 case report, which might have introduced bias to our study as low-level evidence. Also, there is a heavy geographic skew toward Egypt and South Africa, limiting generalizability across the continent. Furthermore, the heterogeneity found in surgical technique, patient selection, and perioperative care limits the precision of pooled estimates and necessitates narrative synthesis for many endpoints. The type of prosthesis used in the surgical interventions was also not included in this study because of the data gap and inconsistent reporting in the primary studies. Also, long-term follow-up beyond 5 years is scarce, with potential loss-to-follow-up biasing survival estimates.

Recommendations

On the basis of our systematic review and meta-analysis of AVR surgeries in Africa, these recommendations are critical to enhance patient outcomes and improve access to this life-saving intervention:1.Health care infrastructure development: African health care systems require substantial investment in cardiac surgical infrastructure. The predominance of studies from Egypt (71.4%) highlights geographic disparities in cardiac surgical capacity. African countries must prioritize establishing specialized cardiac centers with adequate facilities as well as invest in training programs for required staff to address the critical shortage of skilled personnel. Intracontinental collaborations offer a cost-effective training pathway, especially with established programs in Egypt and South Africa.2.Technology and equipment access: The high cost of TAVI devices presents barriers in Africa. Governments and international organizations should negotiate bulk-purchasing agreements for prosthetic valves and establish regional centers to maximize resource use. Public-private partnerships could also facilitate local manufacturing capabilities.3.Financial accessibility: Substantial cost of AVR surgeries represents a significant barrier in low- and middle-income countries. National health insurance schemes should include coverage for cardiac surgical interventions. Establishing cardiac surgical funds through international aid, charitable organizations, and domestic health care budgets could improve access to the surgery.4.Prevention-focused health care policies: Given the predominance of rheumatic heart disease, investment in primary and secondary prevention programs offers greater population health impact than tertiary interventions which are not cost-effective. Strengthening primary health care systems for early detection and treatment of streptococcal infections, alongside prophylactic programs, could significantly reduce future valve disease burden.

Conclusions

The predominance of rheumatic heart disease as the underlying etiology reflects Africa's unique epidemiologic profile and inadequate primary health care systems that fail to prevent and treat streptococcal infections, necessitating targeted interventions. The significant improvement in aortic valve pressure gradients postoperatively confirms the intervention's clinical efficacy. However, notable complications including arrhythmias (16%) and postoperative bleeding (15.1%) require continued attention to perioperative management protocols.

The geographic concentration of studies, with 71.4% conducted in Egypt, highlights substantial disparities in cardiac surgical capacity across the continent hence preventing a generalizable assessment from this study. This underscores the urgent need for infrastructure development, training programs, and financial mechanisms to eliminate barriers to AVR surgery access, requiring coordinated efforts from governments, international organizations, and health care stakeholders. Future research should focus on expanding cardiac surgical programs across underrepresented African countries, developing cost-effective treatment algorithms, and establishing robust outcome databases to monitor progress, drive research, and guide quality improvement initiatives.
