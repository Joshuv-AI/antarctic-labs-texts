Tumor microenvironment-activated ROS enhancers for effective inhibition of osteosarcoma
Authors: Ke Li, Yufan Huang, Chunmei Xiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractReactive oxygen species (ROS) in the tumor microenvironment (TME) are key factors involved in inhibiting tumor cell proliferation. This study developed a hollow mesoporous silica-Prussian blue (HMSNs-PB) nanozyme delivery platform loaded with metformin (HPB@MET), which enhances ROS levels within tumor cells through peroxidase-like (POD) activity. Moreover, in the acidic microenvironment of tumors, HPB@MET disintegrates, and the released MET causes mitochondrial dysfunction and increased electron leakage, ultimately promoting the abnormal accumulation of ROS in the TME. In vitro experiments demonstrate that HPB@MET achieves peak cellular uptake at 72 h and significantly inhibits tumor cell proliferation through its exceptional ROS-generating capacity. In the K7M2 osteosarcoma model (initial tumor volume 80 mm3), HPB@MET increased the tumor inhibition rate from 55% with HPB to 76.5%, with median survival extended to 57 days, indicating that HPB@MET synergistically suppresses tumor cell proliferation via POD activity and mitochondrial dysfunction, effectively suppressing osteosarcoma progression. These results indicate that this nanozyme-drug delivery platform can synergistically promote ROS generation in the TME, effectively inhibiting OS and providing a key technological approach for the efficient treatment of this disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12896-025-01057-2.Keywords: ROS, Nanozyme, POD activity, Mitochondrial dysfunction, Tumor microenvironment

Introduction

Osteosarcoma represents the most prevalent primary malignant neoplasm of bone, constituting approximately 20% of all primary bone tumors, and predominantly affects adolescents between the ages of 10 and 25 years [1, 2]. The typical clinical presentations include progressive osseous pain, localized tumorous masses, and pathological fractures [3, 4]. The standard therapeutic approach involves surgical resection in conjunction with both preoperative and postoperative chemotherapy [5–7]. Despite these interventions, the five-year survival rate remains suboptimal, underscoring the critical need for the development of innovative and efficacious treatment strategies.

Within the realm of oncology, reactive oxygen species (ROS) play a dual regulatory role: at low concentrations, ROS promote tumor proliferation, invasion, and drug resistance via redox signaling pathways [8, 9]; however, when intracellular ROS levels surpass the antioxidant capacity, they induce lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial membrane disruption, and DNA fragmentation, thereby rapidly initiating ferroptosis, apoptosis, and immunogenic cell death [10–12]. As a result of metabolic reprogramming and the persistent activation of oncogenes such as KRAS and MYC, the basal ROS levels in tumor cells approach their oxidative tolerance threshold, rendering them vulnerable to oxidative stress, which can be exacerbated by exogenous stimuli. Metformin (MET) induces electron accumulation and leakage, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction, which positions it as a promising therapeutic agent for cancer treatment [13]. Nevertheless, small molecular compounds application is limited by low effective accumulation concentrations in tumor tissues and significant systemic side effects associated with conventional oral administration routes [14–16].

With the advancement of nanotechnology, nanoparticles have attracted considerable attention as delivery carriers because of their superior targeting capabilities, low systemic toxicity, and controlled drug release mechanisms [17, 18]. Hollow mesoporous silica nanoparticles (HMSNs) are characterized by uniformly adjustable mesoporous channels, a high specific surface area, and large internal cavities [19, 20]. In the acidic microenvironment of tumors, the HMSNs structure can undergo protonation-induced collapse, facilitating the release of its contents. Furthermore, the tumor microenvironment (TME) is characterized by mitochondrial electron leakage and elevated expression of NADPH oxidase, leading to hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) concentrations that are 5–10 times higher than those in normal tissues [21, 22]. Under these H₂O₂-rich conditions, peroxidase (POD) can catalyze the formation of substrate-induced ROS, thereby enhancing oxidative stress within the tumor. Prussian blue (PB) nanozyme, renowned for their superior POD-like enzyme activity, have emerged as optimal catalytic platforms for enhancing oxidative stress and selectively eradicating tumor cells [23, 24]. Thus, the modification of HMSNs with PB (HPB) to precisely augment ROS within the TME while concurrently inducing mitochondrial dysfunction through MET represents a promising strategy for the targeted elimination of tumor cells.

The nanozyme delivery platform HPB@MET developed in this study exhibited POD-like activity, serving as a ROS amplifier within the TME. This platform facilitates the release of MET within the acidic microenvironment of tumors, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction. Concurrently, PB effectively catalyzes the conversion of H₂O₂ into •OH within tumor cells, thereby increasing ROS levels. This results in a cascade of oxidative stresses, culminating in abnormal ROS accumulation and subsequent tumor cell elimination.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and animals

The murine osteosarcoma K7M2 cell line (mouse origin) was obtained from Zhong Qiao Xin Zhou Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) and maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum. Female BALB/c mice (8 weeks old, 20 g body weight) were purchased from the Comparative Medicine Center of Yangzhou University. The animals were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions with six mice per cage, provided with ad libitum access to water, and kept on a 12-hour light/dark cycle (7:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m.) at a constant ambient temperature of 25 °C. All experimental procedures involving animals were reviewed and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Yangzhou University (No. YXYLL-2025-97).

Synthesis and characterization of HPB and HPB@MET

HMSNs were synthesized using a reverse microemulsion method. Specifically, 10.02 mg of HMSNs was dispersed in 10.0 mL of deionized water under sonication. Then, 16.22 mL of an aqueous FeCl3 solution (6.43 mg, 0.40 mmol) was introduced under continuous stirring for 1 h. Subsequently, an aqueous solution of K4[Fe(CN)6]·3H2O (8.45 mg, 0.20 mmol) was added dropwise to the mixture, followed by stirring at 25 °C for 0.5 h. The resulting HPB were collected via centrifugation and dried.

To prepare the HPB@MET complex, 1 mg of HPB was mixed with 0.2 mg of MET and gently stirred overnight at 4 °C. The final product was isolated by centrifugation. The morphology and size distribution of the nanoparticles were characterized using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Elemental mapping was performed to visualize the spatial distribution of constituent elements, and X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was employed for further compositional analysis. Drug loading capacity (DLC, wt%) was determined by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA).

Measurement of POD-like activity and drug release

The POD-like activities of HMSNs and HPB were evaluated by incubating them with TMB (3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine) and H2O2 in acetate buffer (pH 4.0) at 37 °C for 15 min, followed by measuring the absorbance at 652 nm. To investigate pH-responsive drug release, HPB@MET was dispersed in PBS at different pH values (6.5 and 7.4) under constant shaking at 37 °C. At predetermined time intervals, the supernatants were collected and analyzed via HPLC to quantify the amount of released MET.

Results and discussion

Preparation and characterization of the HPB@MET nanozyme-drug delivery platform

HPB was obtained via a hydrothermal method, and HPB@MET was prepared by mixing HPB with MET. HPB and HPB@MET were observed as homogeneously dispersed spheres in TEM images, with average diameters of 36 nm and 40 nm, respectively (Figs. 1A-D). The increased size of HPB@MET compared with that of HPB may be attributed to the incorporation of the drug. Elemental mapping provided evidence of the successful integration of PB with the HMSNs framework, demonstrating a homogeneous distribution of the key elements C, O, Si and Fe throughout the architecture (Fig. 1E). Furthermore, XPS analysis confirmed the presence of Fe within the resulting HPB nanostructure, supporting the formation of the target composite (Fig. 1F). Additionally, POD activity assays demonstrated that HPB possesses significant POD-like activity, effectively promoting the accumulation of ROS in the TME (Fig. 1G). For tumor cells, positively charged particles have superior cell membrane penetration properties, whereas MET alone has a negative charge. After being loaded with highly positively charged HPB, the HPB@MET nanomaterial system still exhibited a positive charge, which facilitated the delivery of HPB@MET to tumor cells (Fig. 1H). The DLC of HPB@MET was subsequently determined via TGA. The results found taht HPB@MET exhibited a dominant derivative thermogravimetry (DTG) peak at ~ 260 °C, with a mass loss of 23.1% in the 200–350 °C interval, HPB showed 0.9% loss over the same range. After baseline subtraction, the calculated DLC was 22.2 wt% (Fig. 1I). Notably, under conditions mimicking the acidic TME (pH 6.5), HPB@MET was found to release substantially more MET than under normal physiological conditions (pH 7.4) did (Fig. 1J). This may be due to increased protonation of HMSNs in an acidic environment, leading to the release of MET. These findings establish a foundation for MET to synergistically increase ROS generation in the acidic TME by inducing mitochondrial damage.

Efficient intracellular delivery, superior ROS generation, and significant anti-tumor cell proliferation effects of HPB@MET

Effective inhibition of tumor cell proliferation is a fundamental prerequisite for cancer therapy. We evaluated the cytotoxicity and intracellular ROS-enhancing properties of HPB@MET in mouse OS K7M2 cells. A Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay revealed that HPB@MET at 0.6 mg/mL exhibited significant cytotoxicity against tumor cells (Fig. 2A, Figure S1A-B), which was further confirmed by live/dead cell staining results (Fig. 2B, C). Consequently, subsequent in vitro experiments were conducted by 0.6 mg/mL HPB@MET. Subsequently, CY5.5-labeled HPB@MET (HPBCY5.5@MET) was prepared to monitor cellular uptake. As shown in Fig. 2D and E, the uptake of HPB@MET by K7M2 cells peaked at 72 h. Additionally, similar results were observed when HPB@MET was injected into subcutaneous tumor-bearing mice (Fig. 2F, G). Both in vitro and in vivo experiments demonstrated significant tumor cell uptake of HPBCY5.5@MET over time, which contributed to the suppression of tumor progression.

Further investigation of the effects of HPB@MET nanocatalysts on mitochondrial function via JC-1 staining. JC-1 is an ideal fluorescent probe for detecting mitochondrial membrane potential. At high mitochondrial membrane potential, JC-1 forms red-fluorescent polymers; at low mitochondrial membrane potential, JC-1 transforms into green-fluorescent monomers [25]. The results revealed that HMSNs alone did not interfere with the cellular mitochondrial potential. In tumor cells treated with HPB, JC-1 was predominantly present in its monomeric form (green fluorescence), whereas the fluorescence intensity of polymerized JC-1 was lower than before (red fluorescence), indicating that HPB effectively reduces the mitochondrial membrane potential. However, free MET only caused a slight decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential, closely related to the limited cellular uptake of free drugs. Furthermore, HPB@MET loaded with MET further promoted this phenomenon, as the release of MET induced mitochondrial electron accumulation and leakage, leading to further functional impairment (Fig. 2H, I). Previous studies have shown that damage to the mitochondrial electron transport chain and increased electron leakage can lead to the excessive production of ROS, such as superoxide anion radicals, triggering oxidative stress and cellular damage [26]. Therefore, dihydroethidium (DHE) was used as a fluorescent probe to detect intracellular ROS levels. 1 × 105 K7M2 cells were seeded into per well of 6-well plates, followed by the addition of HMSNs, HPB, or HPB@MET at a concentration of 0.6 mg/mL for co-culture over 72 h. The medium was then replaced with a solution containing 5 µM DHE, and ROS levels was observed after 0.5 h of incubation with K7M2 cells. The results revealed that ROS production in the HPB@MET group was significantly greater than that in the HPB and MET group (Fig. 2J, K). This enhancement was attributed to the efficient cellular uptake of nanoparticles and the intracellular MET-induced mitochondrial dysfunction. In summary, HPB@MET can be effectively taken up by tumor tissues and inhibit tumor cell proliferation through a synergistic mechanism involving POD-like activity and further induction of mitochondrial dysfunction, both of which synergistically promote the accumulation of ROS in the TME, thereby eliminating tumor cells.

Obvious Inhibition of osteosarcoma by ROS accumulation in the tumor microenvironment

The efficiency of nanoparticle accumulation in tumor regions within the body is a prerequisite for effective tumor suppression. To investigate the tumor targeting of HPB@MET, mice were euthanized at 6, 12, and 24 h post-intravenous injection of HPBCY5.5@MET. Major organs and tumors were thoroughly exposed for fluorescence imaging. Results showed that fluorescence intensity in tumors of HPBCY5.5@MET-injected mice progressively increased over time, indicating tumor-targeted accumulation via the EPR effect (Fig. 3A). Additionally, substantial fluorescence signals were observed in the liver and lungs, indicating that HPBCY5.5@MET nanoparticles were primarily metabolized in these organs (Fig. 3A). Over time, the fluorescence signals of HPBCY5.5@MET nanoparticles in the organs gradually diminished and were progressively degraded. Additionally, given the high iron content in HPB nanozymes, iron ICP analysis was performed on organs and tumors from each group of mice to observe the biodistribution of nanoparticles. We found significantly elevated iron levels in tumors from the HPB@MET group compared to both the control and MET groups, indicating that HPB@MET exhibits strong tumor targeting properties (Fig. 3B).

Tumor Targeting of HPB@MET. (A) Biodistribution at different time points following intravenous injection of HPBCY5.5@MET. (B) Iron levels in organs and tumors 72 h after intravenous injection of HPB@MET

To further validate the antitumor effect of HPB@MET in vivo, physiological saline, HPB, and HPB@MET were administered intravenously to K7M2 subcutaneous tumor-bearing mice for treatment. The initial tumor volume was 80 mm3, with treatment administered intravenously every 4 days at a constant dose of 100 mg/kg over a 14-day cycle (Fig. 4A). As shown in Fig. 4B, the tumor volume in the MET and HPB group was significantly lower than that in the CON group, whereas the HPB@MET group presented a greater reduction in tumor volume than other groups. The tumor volume curve revealed that the tumor volume in the CON group approached 500 mm³ by day 6 after successful modeling, after which it rapidly increased. In the HPB group, the tumor volume remained at 300 mm³ by day 8 posttreatment but subsequently grew rapidly. In contrast, the tumors maintained slow growth in the HPB@MET group (Fig. 4C). The tumor tissue was subsequently weighed and analyzed, and the tumor weight was significantly lower in the HPB@MET group than in the CON, MET and HPB groups (Fig. 4D). The following formula was used to calculate the tumor inhibition rate: tumor inhibition rate (%) = (1-average volume of the treatment group/average volume of the control group) × 100%. By calculating the tumor inhibition rates across treatment groups, the results demonstrated that the HPB@MET group exhibited a significantly greater tumor-suppressing effect compared to other groups, achieving a tumor inhibition rate of 76.5% (Fig. 4E).

To further validate the potential mechanism of HPB@MET in tumor treatment, the mitochondrial status in tumor regions across all groups was examined via transmission electron microscopy. The CON group exhibited neatly aligned mitochondrial cristae, whereas MET and HPB treatments resulted in swollen mitochondria with cloudy contents. Notably, the HPB@MET-treated group showed complete disappearance of mitochondrial cristae and leakage of mitochondrial contents (Fig. 4F). Subsequent histopathological staining confirmed that tumor cells in the CON group exhibited vigorous growth with regular morphology, whereas tumor cells in the HPB@MET group demonstrated extensive necrosis (Fig. 4G). To verify whether tumor cell necrosis was caused by elevated ROS levels resulting from mitochondrial dysfunction, we performed DHE staining on tumor tissue sections. Compared with the CON, MET and HPB groups, the HPB@MET group presented the greatest amount of red fluorescence, indicating the presence of abundant ROS in the tumor region. In summary, we validated the tumor-suppressing mechanism of HPB@MET in vivo, where HPB@MET generates ROS through POD-like enzyme activity on the one hand and releases MET in the acidic TME to induce mitochondrial dysfunction, further causing locally elevated ROS levels to kill tumors on the other hand.

Superior biosafety of HPB@MET

The assessment of the biocompatibility of biomaterials is critical prior to clinical translation. Therefore, we performed histopathological examinations of the major organs of the mice from each treatment group. The results revealed no adverse conditions, such as necrosis or atrophy, in the heart, liver, spleen, lung, or kidney sections of any of the treatment groups (Fig. 5A). Additionally, monitoring the body weights of the mice throughout the entire treatment period revealed that the body weights of all three groups fluctuated within a reasonable range, with no significant rapid weight loss occurring (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, to further validate the potential of these nanoenzymes to extend the survival period of tumor-bearing mice, we continued the previous treatment strategy and found that mice in the HPB@MET group exhibited significantly extended survival compared to other treatment groups. (Fig. 5C). These results further validated the efficacy and safety of the constructed HPB@MET for tumor treatment. On the basis of these results, we conclude that the constructed HPB@MET has good biosafety, good tumor therapeutic efficacy, and promising potential for clinical translation.

Conclusion

This study developed a novel ROS enhance system, HPB@MET. Leveraging POD activity, this platform first catalyzes the generation of ROS within tumor cells. The acidic microenvironment of tumors subsequently triggers protonation of HPB@MET, enabling the controlled release of MET, which further leads to mitochondrial dysfunction and elevated ROS levels. In vitro experiments demonstrated that HPB@MET exhibited significant ROS generation efficiency and effectively inhibited tumor cell proliferation. In a mouse osteosarcoma model, POD activity synergistically amplifies oxidative stress with mitochondrial damage, significantly blocking tumor progression. Notably, this study still has certain limitations. The research did not fully replicate the microenvironmental characteristics of human primary osteosarcoma, and thus further validation in patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models is still necessary. The specific molecular mechanisms underlying the synergistic effects of HPB@MET, particularly the spatiotemporal cooperative relationship between MET-induced mitochondrial electron leakage and the catalytic reaction of PB nanozyme require more in-depth investigation. It is believed that this strategy will provide an efficient and safe novel paradigm for the precise treatment of osteosarcoma in the future.
