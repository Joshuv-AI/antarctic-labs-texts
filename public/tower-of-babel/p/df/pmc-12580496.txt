Influence of Temperature Regimes on the Production of 2‐MIB and Geosmin by Pseudanabaena galeata and Phormidium ambiguum
Authors: Mudalige Don Hiranya Jayasanka Senavirathna, Mohoppu Arachchillage Dasun Dineka Jayasekara
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe temporal variation of 2‐Methylisoborneol (2‐MIB) and Geosmin (GSM) production in two filamentous cyanobacteria species, Pseudanabaena galeata and Phormidium ambiguum , was tested under three temperature regimes (20°C, 30°C, and 40°C). The 20°C is considered the ideal cultural temperature for both species. The 2‐MIB, GSM, cellular H2O2, pigmentation, and total protein were quantified every other day for a 10‐day period. Both species showed temporal variation in the measured parameters, but the variations were distinct between the two species. P. galeata showed significant changes in odor compound production at 40°C compared with 20°C and 30°C, recording a maximum 2.4‐fold increase of 2‐MIB and an 8.2‐fold increase of GSM. P. ambiguum showed significant changes at 30°C and 40°C. 2‐MIB of P. ambiguum increased a maximum of 13‐fold at 30°C and 19‐fold at 40°C, and GSM increased by 17‐fold at 30°C and 4.6‐fold at 40°C. The total chlorophyll content, H2O2 contents, and protein contents were also recorded with significant changes. No significant relationships were observed between odor compounds and other parameters.

Introduction

The contamination of the water environment with cyanobacteria has caused unpleasant odors in drinking water apart from the toxification of drinking water. The two main odor compounds that can be found in contaminated water, 2‐Methylisoborneol (2‐MIB) and Geosmin (GSM), are increased worldwide with the increased cyanobacterial growth despite the 2‐MIB and GSM being produced by several organisms, including fungi, actinomycetes, phytoplankton, and higher plants (Suurnäkki et al. 2015; Machado et al. 2017; Perkins et al. 2019; Senavirathna et al. 2021). Among those, the cyanobacteria are contributing largely to the odor compounds in water recently (Perkins et al. 2019). The 2‐MIB and GSM are both volatile organic compounds (VOCs), which cause the widely known musty odor in water that most humans can easily detect at very low threshold concentrations of 2–15 ng/L and 4–10 ng/L for 2‐MIB and GSM, respectively (Liato and Aïder 2017; Pochiraju et al. 2021). Therefore, water contamination with 2‐MIB and GSM has become a serious issue, and testing and removal of 2‐MIB and GSM have become practices to maintain water quality standards (Jüttner and Watson 2007; Mustapha et al. 2021). Further, the increased odor in urban water bodies has also negatively affected the livability in urban cities (Waajen et al. 2014; Grogan et al. 2023).

The production of 2‐MIB and GSM by the cyanobacteria is bound with environmental factors, including light and temperature. It is a known fact that each species has a temperature‐light response range for 2‐MIB and GSM production (Wang and Li 2015; Oh et al. 2017; Alghanmi et al. 2018). In general, high temperature promotes total 2‐MIB production, whereas an ideal temperature is preferred for GSM production (Shen et al. 2021). Therefore, the increasing global temperature should influence the 2‐MIB and GSM production. In general, the increasing temperature is promoting the production of musty odor in water environments, despite the 2‐MIB and GSM production increasing at different temperatures. The increased musty odor in the water environment during the summer is recorded, evidenced by the impact of high temperatures (Sun et al. 2013).

The temperature‐driven physiological changes in the cells should have contributed to the change in 2‐MIB and GSM production at different temperatures. The response of the physical parameters of cyanobacteria to temperature can vary according to the type, species, and habitat preference of each species. When considering two filamentous cyanobacterium types, the benthic and planktonic habitat preferences determine the physiological responses to environmental factors (Muhetaer et al. 2020). Confirming the varied physiological response bound odor compound production, the production of 2‐MIB and GSM varied between habitat preference, with planktonic Pseudanabaena galeata and benthic Phormidium ambiguum showing different diurnal variation in 2‐MIB and GSM production under light stress (Senavirathna and Jayasekara 2023). When considering the temperature factor, it is evident that temperature plays a significant role in the production of odor compounds by cyanobacteria. There is evidence of a temporal change in 2‐MIB production of Pseudanabaena species in both long‐term cultures and short‐term cultures (Kakimoto et al. 2014; Park et al. 2024). Further, increasing temperatures have been found to promote 2‐MIB production by Pseudanabaena species in natural environments and laboratory cultures (Wang and Li 2015; Zhang et al. 2016). However, the research evidence on the temporal variation of both 2‐MIB and GSM production of Pseudanabaena sp. under extreme temperature regimes remains limited.

In this study, we investigated the effect of temperature on the temporal variation in the production of the odor compounds 2‐MIB and GSM by Pseudanabaena species and Phormidium sp. under different higher temperature regimes. The planktonic P. galeata (NIES 512) and the benthic P. ambiguum (NIES 2119) were exposed to 20°C, 30°C, and 40°C temperature regimes, and changes in 2‐MIB and GSM production over time were observed in a laboratory setting. The results showed that temperature influenced the production of 2‐MIB and GSM in both species; however, the temporal response patterns differed between the two species.

Materials and Methods

Cyanobacteria Culture and Experiment Samples

P. galeata (NIES‐512; also known as Pseudanabaena foetida var. intermedia) and P. ambiguum (NIES‐2119) were obtained from the National Institute for Environmental Studies (NIES), Tsukuba, Japan. Both species had been cryopreserved in the NIES culture collection and were precultured before being provided as isolated cultures. After receiving, the species were cultured separately in disinfected incubators in the laboratory. All cultures were maintained in Erlenmeyer flasks (250–300 mL), sealed with sponge foam caps equipped with silicone sponge filters to prevent airborne contamination. More than 10 flasks per species were prepared. Contamination was monitored microscopically, and any contaminated cultures were immediately discarded. As cultures matured, they were subdivided, and fresh BG‐11 medium was added to increase the number of cultures. The incubation temperature was maintained at 20°C, with a light intensity of 20–25 μmol m−2 s−1 PAR under a 12:12 light–dark cycle. Illumination was provided by full‐spectrum dimmable LED panel lights (VBL‐SL150, Valore, Kyoto, Japan). After more than 3 months of growth and multiplication of cultures, healthy cultures were selected and used for the experiments.

Temperature Treatment

Healthy cultures were selected and mixed to maintain the uniformity and used for the preparation of experiment samples. The temperature treatment was done by preparing 18 treatment samples in 100‐mL conical flasks containing 60 mL of cell culture. The treatment samples were incubated for 5 days in an incubator under the same conditions as the cultures. After the incubation period, experiments were conducted for three temperature regimes: 20°C, 30°C, and 40°C. Samples were collected from the starting day and every other day for 10 days. For each species, triplicates were maintained for each temperature regime.

Sample Collection and Cell Pallet Preparation

The 2‐mL samples from each treatment flask were collected in 4‐mL glass vials containing 750 mg of NaCl for 2‐MIB and GSM analyses. The collected samples were refrigerated at −80°C until analysis. The cell pellet was prepared from the remaining cell suspensions in flasks for oxidative stress, pigment, and protein content analysis. From each treated flask, 18 cell pellets (a total of 324 cell pellets per temperature condition) were prepared. Each cell pellet was prepared from 1 mL of the samples; samples were put in 1.5‐mL microcentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min at 4°C (Tomy MX‐105, Digital Biology, Japan). The supernatant was removed from the centrifuged tubes and stored at −80°C until analysis.

Total 2‐MIB and GSM Quantification

The headspace gas chromatography/mass spectrometry (GC/MS) method was used to quantify 2‐MIB and GSM (Table 1). Shimadzu, GC/MS QP2010 was used for the analysis (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). The headspace extraction of MIB and GSM was performed using a solid‐phase microextraction fiber (57293‐U, SPME fiber, Sigma, Japan) attached to a manual fiber holder/plunger (57330‐U, Sigma, Japan). Samples were homogenized using an ultrasonic tissue homogenizer (Astrason XL2020, New York, USA) for 1 min, and then the vials were placed on a hot vial stirrer (HSH‐10VA, As One, Japan). The SPME fiber was inserted into the headspace through the vial septum, and the fiber was exposed 2 mm above the sample surface. The vials were heated to 70°C with stirring at 700 rpm (vials contained PTFE‐coated magnetic stir bars (Z328898‐10EA, Sigma, Japan) inserted at the time of sampling). After the 30‐min extraction duration, the SPME fiber was removed from the vial and inserted into the injection port of the GCMS system. The monitoring ions, 95 and 107, were used for 2‐MIB detection, whereas 112 and 182 were used for GSM detection.

The quantification of the 2‐MIB and GSM was performed using calibration curves obtained using standard 2‐MIB and GSM solutions. Concentrated 2‐MIB and GSM solutions (CRM47523, Sigma, Japan) and GSM solution (CRM47522, Sigma, Japan) were used to prepare the standard solutions. 2‐MIB and GSM concentrations 0, 0.5, 1, 2.5, 5, and 10 ng/L were used for the standard curves.

Cellular H2O2 Quantification

The H2O2 content was quantified using the method described by Jana and Choudhuri (1984) with modifications. The cell pellets were mixed with 1 mL of 50 mM phosphate buffer (pH 7) and vortexed. The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min at 4°C, and the supernatant was separated for H2O2 quantification. A 750‐μL aliquot of a reaction mixture containing 0.1% titanium sulfate in 20% H2SO4 (v/v) was added to 250 μL of the supernatant. The color formation was measured after a 1‐min incubation period, and the optical absorbance was measured at 410 nm using a spectrophotometer (UVmini‐1240, Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). A standard curve was used to determine H2O2 content in samples.

Chlorophyll‐a (Chl‐a) and Total Protein Quantification

Chl‐a content was quantified by extracting cellular pigments into 95% ethanol. Cell pellets were incubated in 1 mL of 95% ethanol for 24 h in the dark at 25°C. After 24 h, they were vortexed and centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 15 min. The optical absorbance of the supernatant was measured at 665 and 649 nm using a spectrophotometer (UVmini‐1240). The Chl‐a content was calculated using Equation (1) below and expressed in micrograms per milliliter (APHA 2017). (1) Chl=13.95A665−6.88A649 where Chl is the chlorophyll content, A665 is the absorbance at 665 nm, and A649 is the absorbance at 649 nm.

The cell pellets obtained after removing the supernatant from the Chl‐a measurement were used for total protein quantification. The total protein content was measured using the Bradford method (Bradford 1976). Pellets were vortexed with 500 μL of 0.5 M NaOH and incubated for 10 min at 70°C. After reaching room temperature, the mixture was centrifuged at 10,000 × g for 10 min, and the supernatant was separated. The supernatant was mixed with Bradford reagent (Wako Chemical) and incubated at room temperature (25°C) for 10 min. The optical absorption of the reaction mixture was measured at 595 nm using a spectrophotometer (UVmini‐1240), and the protein content was determined using a standard curve (microgram per milliliter). The standard curve was prepared using protein standard (BSA) solutions (P0914, Sigma, Japan). Known concentrations of BSA solutions were prepared, and the Bradford method color development was measured spectrophotometrically to plot the standard curve.

Data Analysis

One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Duncan's post hoc test was used to compare the data statistically (p‐value less than 0.05 is considered significant). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 25 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive statistics and data visualization were performed using Microsoft Excel 2001 (Microsoft, Washington, USA).

Discussion

The biological materials respond even to a slight temperature change (Janni et al. 2023; Vadboncoeur et al. 2023) and therefore, a broad change in temperature regimes can shift the physiological conditions into broadly different statuses. The outcome of the present research is also not deviated from such phenomena, and physiology was broadly changed between the different temperature regimes. The exposure to 20°C, 30°C, and 40°C temperature regimes caused different responses in the measured parameters. The 20°C temperature is considered optimal for the laboratory cultures of P. galeata and P. ambiguum . Consequently, changes in physiological parameters observed under 20°C conditions were relatively minor compared with those under 30°C and 40°C exposures. It is also important to note that the cyanobacterial cultures used in this study were in the rapid growth phase; thus, the continuous variations observed at 20°C can be attributed to natural growth‐associated dynamics. In contrast, the physiological responses observed under 30°C and 40°C conditions likely reflect a combination of growth‐related and temperature‐induced stress responses.

When P. ambiguum and P. galeata are concerned, the physiological responses and odor compound production in each of the temperature regimes are not always similar. Such deviated responses are obvious because of the species difference, despite the filamentous nature of the two species. P. ambiguum is a benthic type, and P. galeata is a planktonic type of cyanobacteria; therefore, in the natural environment, even under the same atmospheric temperature, further deviation in the response of the two species can be expected because of temperature attenuation with depth. However, in this experiment, as the samples are exposed to the temperature from the surroundings and the media depth is only about 3 cm, a temperature difference cannot be expected. Thus, the differences observed in the responses are solely due to the difference between the species. In a natural environment, the responses of the two species are driven by lifestyle difference and species difference.

The higher temperature exposure obviously affected the production of 2‐MIB and GSM of both species. The P. galeata produces more GSM than the 2‐MIB at lower temperatures, and when the temperature is increasing, at 30°C and 40°C, the 2‐MIB level was increased. In the case of P. ambiguum , at 20°C, the 2‐MIB was at an undetectable level, but at 30°C and 40°C, both odor compounds were detected. Further, as the higher temperature continued, the amount of 2‐MIB and GSM production of both species increased. The temperature level and the temperature exposure duration‐dependent odor compounds production of cyanobacteria species was also reported in previous studies (Li et al. 2012; Shen et al. 2021), which is consistent with the present findings. Because of global warming, exceeding 40°C and more consecutive hotter days in the summer are recorded recently in many countries; therefore, the finding of the present research suggests that it is expected to have higher odor compounds released into water in the future when P. galeata and P. ambiguum are present.

Odor compound production in cyanobacteria has been reported to increase because of physiological changes triggered by adverse environmental conditions (Zuo 2023). Shen et al. (2021) also reported a positive correlation between geosmin/2‐MIB concentrations and Chl‐a in P. galeata . However, in the present study, no significant relationships were found between odor compounds and Chl‐a or other measured parameters in either species (see Data S1). The freshly prepared samples used in this experiment contained fresh BG‐11 medium. This BG‐11 medium refreshment, combined with low initial cell densities, allowed the cyanobacteria to re‐enter a growth phase, which may have altered the physiological state of the cyanobacteria (Hense and Beckmann 2006; Zhang et al. 2015). This is supported by the observed temporal changes in the parameters, even in the 20°C treatment, despite 20°C being the optimal growth temperature for both species. Thus, the measured parameters in this study reflect both growth‐related and temperature‐induced changes. As a result, the lack of significant relationships between physiological responses and odor compound production may be due to this overlapping influence. Future studies should consider using cultures in the stationary growth phase to better isolate and assess the relationship between temperature and odor compound production.

In the present study, sampling was conducted on alternate days to examine the temporal dynamics of 2‐MIB and GSM production in P. galeata and P. ambiguum , and to facilitate comparison between the two species. However, existing evidence indicates that the physiological responses of P. ambiguum to external stimuli such as light intensity can occur within a few hours and may fluctuate in accordance with diurnal light cycles (Muhetaer et al. 2020). In natural environments, both light and temperature exhibit diurnal variation, which can potentially induce corresponding fluctuations in odor compound production in these species. Accordingly, it is recommended that future research investigate the diurnal patterns of 2‐MIB and GSM production in P. galeata and P. ambiguum under experimental conditions that replicate the natural diurnal cycles of light and temperature.

Conclusions

The differing responses observed between the two species, despite their filamentous morphology, may be attributed to species‐specific traits. These differences are likely to be further amplified under natural conditions because of temperature attenuation along the water column and variations in ecological lifestyle (benthic and planktonic), which will be investigated in future studies using a vertical tank system. In the present study, cultures were in the early rapid growth phase, during which physiological parameters are subject to rapid changes associated with high growth rates. Therefore, it is recommended that further investigations utilize cultures in the stationary growth phase and increase the number of replicates to enhance the accuracy of the results. Additionally, in natural environments, light intensity also influences species‐specific physiological responses. Therefore, future studies integrating both temperature and light as experimental variables are essential to understand odor compound production dynamics in these cyanobacterial species.
