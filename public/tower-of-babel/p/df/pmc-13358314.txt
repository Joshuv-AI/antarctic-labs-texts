Area deprivation and hepatitis C infection in Korea: a multilevel case–control study using the National Health Insurance Database
Authors: Hwa Young Choi, Dahye Baik, Moran Ki
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionHepatitis C virus (HCV), a major cause of chronic liver disease, shows decreasing prevalence in Korea, yet regional disparities persist. This study aimed to examine the impact and temporal changes in individual characteristics and regional health inequalities on HCV infection to provide a framework for HCV elimination and management.MethodsThis nationwide population-based multilevel case–control study analysed 252 993 patients with HCV and 4 000 000 controls using National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) and population census data from 2005, 2010, 2015 and 2020. The Area Deprivation Index (ADI) was calculated for each area (region/town) using population census data. Sex-standardised and age-standardised HCV prevalences were estimated for each area using NHIS data. The association between area-level HCV and ADI was examined using correlation and linear regression analyses. Individual-level and area-level HCV infection risks were estimated using logistic regression and multilevel logistic regression models incorporating sex, age, HCV prevalence and ADI.ResultsThe HCV prevalence (per 100 000) decreased from 151 to 117 (2005–2020). The maximum inter-area ADI gap decreased modestly (34.64 to 32.50). Although the correlation between ADI and HCV prevalence was weak across all areas, it was markedly stronger in high-prevalence areas (≥200/100 000). In logistic regression, residents of the most deprived areas had elevated risks vs the least deprived (2020 adjusted OR 1.37). Multilevel models showed age (individual level) and area HCV prevalence (area level) were dominant determinants. Deprivation increased an individual’s odds of HCV case status in ADI-only models; however, this association was not statistically significant after adjusting for area-level HCV prevalence and individual factors.ConclusionsEffective HCV elimination requires tailored screening and treatment strategies that prioritise socioeconomically vulnerable areas among the high-prevalence areas.Keywords: Public Health, Epidemiology, Community Health

Introduction

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection is a major cause of liver diseases, including liver cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. In 2022, an estimated 50 million people worldwide were living with chronic HCV infection, with approximately 0.9 million new infections occurring that year. The WHO aims to eliminate viral hepatitis as a major public health concern by the year 2030. However, recent data suggest that Korea is unlikely to achieve complete viral hepatitis elimination by 2030.1 According to Korea Disease Control and Prevention Agency, the newly reported HCV cases in South Korea decreased from 10 811 in 2018 to 5627 in 2025.2 Korea’s HCV prevalence shows distinct regional disparities,3,5 and to expedite HCV elimination, it is necessary to analyse the characteristics of high-prevalence areas and manage the risk factors in these regions.

The Area Deprivation Index (ADI) is a comprehensive indicator that represents the degree of socioeconomic deprivation in an area and has been used in various health inequality studies. Previous studies have shown that a higher ADI is significantly associated with increased tuberculosis mortality rates,6 and that COVID-19-related risks also vary according to the degree of area deprivation.7

Studies from Denmark and the Netherlands have reported that lower socioeconomic status was associated with higher HCV infection risk and prevalence,8 9 while Japan reported a significant correlation between higher levels of area socioeconomic deprivation and increased HCV prevalence.10 However, despite the persistent regional disparities in HCV prevalence in Korea, there is a lack of empirical research examining the association with area socioeconomic characteristics. Additionally, as ADI reflects the unique sociocultural context of each country, research using an ADI tailored to Korea’s HCV profile is needed.

While area-level prevalence effectively identifies geographic hotspots of disease burden, it does not capture the underlying socioeconomic context of those areas. Incorporating the ADI provides insight not only into where interventions are needed but also into how they should be designed. Even among high-prevalence areas, socioeconomic conditions vary considerably; those with greater deprivation require fundamentally different public health strategies than less deprived areas. By considering both area-level prevalence and ADI together, it becomes possible to simultaneously identify where to intervene and how, enabling evidence-based prioritisation and the development of tailored prevention strategies for each area.

Research evaluating HCV infection risk in Korea by incorporating individual-level risk factors and area-level variables, such as regional HCV prevalence and ADI, could provide a framework for establishing national strategies for effective HCV elimination. The remaining undiagnosed and untreated burden of HCV is increasingly concentrated in socioeconomically vulnerable and underserved populations, which may contribute to persistent regional health disparities in Korea’s rapidly ageing society. This study aimed to provide the scientific evidence necessary for HCV management policies by jointly evaluating individual-level factors (sex, age and income) along with area-level risk factors (HCV prevalence and ADI) using the ADI calculated from the census data of Korea’s population spread across approximately 250 areas.

Materials and methods

Study participants

This study used National Health Insurance Service (NHIS) claim data for each year (2005, 2010, 2015 and 2020). Patients with HCV were defined as individuals diagnosed with and treated for acute or chronic hepatitis C (ICD-10: B17.1, B18.2) at medical institutions in the respective year. The control group (non-HCV) comprised one million individuals per year, selected through simple random sampling from those who had never been diagnosed with acute or chronic hepatitis C from 2002 to 2020.

Variable definition and analysis unit

Individual-level variables

Individual-level variables included the sex, age and income of HCV patients and controls for each year.

Area-level variables

The area-level analysis unit was Korea’s administrative zones (regions/towns). The total number of nationwide areas was 250 in 2005 and 2020, 251 in 2010 and 252 in 2015.

Area deprivation index

The ADI was calculated using the population census data provided by Statistics Korea for 2005, 2010, 2015 and 2020. The population census is a basic national statistical survey conducted by the United Nations. It encompasses the entire process of collecting, evaluating, analysing and providing demographic, economic and sociological data related to households and dwellings for all people in a country or a specific region at a particular point in time.11 Korea’s population census is conducted every 5 years and consists of complete enumeration and sample surveys depending on the items. The complete enumeration surveys the overall characteristics, such as the size of the population, households and housing, whereas the sample survey investigates commuting and school attendance, domestic population mobility, activity limitations, women and children, elderly people, social activities and economic activities that are difficult to identify in the complete enumeration. Until 2010, a 10% sample survey was conducted; this was later changed to 20%.12 13

Our study uses the same components as the Korean ADI (KorDep_2010), which measures individual socioeconomic indicators (income, occupation and education levels) at the area level. KorDep consists of an elderly population, female-headed households, single-person households, non-apartment households, low educational attainment, divorced or widowed households, lower social class households, substandard housing conditions and no-car households14 (online supplemental table 1). While completely enumerated data were used to calculate the component indicators, sample survey data were used for lower social class and no-car household variables.

Because the ADI components have different absolute value magnitudes, standardised scores (z-scores) were calculated for each component and summed. Korea’s ADI ranged from −16.96 to 17.71, where higher values indicate higher levels of deprivation and lower values indicate lower levels of deprivation.

Area-level HCV prevalence

The NHIS claims data contain medical information of National Health Insurance subscribers, including demographic information (sex, age, residential area), individual health insurance premiums that can identify income levels and medical institution treatment details (diagnostic codes, surgeries, procedures, prescribed medications).15 Korea’s National Health Insurance coverage rate is 97.1% as of 2023.16 For all age groups in 2005, 2010, 2015 and 2020, sex-standardised and age-standardised HCV prevalence per 100 000 population was calculated by area (region/town) using the number of HCV prevalent patients who were diagnosed and treated for acute or chronic hepatitis C at medical institutions in the respective year as the numerator and the year-end resident registration population of the respective year as the denominator. The 2020 year-end resident registration population was used as the standard population. This variable was intended to reflect the local epidemiologic context of HCV infection. Because HCV is a transmissible disease, area-level prevalence serves as a proxy for the community reservoir of the virus and the ecological risk of exposure, rather than a mere aggregation of individual statuses.

Statistical analysis

Stepwise analyses were conducted to examine factors associated with HCV infection, considering both individual-level and area-level variables (ADI and area-level HCV prevalence). First, correlation and linear regression analyses were performed by year. Additionally, we compared how these associations changed when the analysis was limited to areas with high HCV prevalence (top 20% level, ≥200/100 000). Subsequently, the association between variables and HCV infection was analysed using a logistic regression analysis that included both individual-level and area-level variables. A multilevel analysis was also performed to conduct an integrated analysis at both individual and area levels (figure 1).

Area-level analysis

The association between area-level HCV prevalence and ADI was examined using Pearson’s correlation and linear regression analyses. Correlation analysis was used to identify the correlation between two variables, and linear regression analysis was used to evaluate the association between the two variables. The coefficient of determination was calculated to assess the explanatory power of the regression model, thereby confirming the extent to which the ADI explained the variability in HCV prevalence.

Individual-level analysis

Logistic regression analysis was performed after adjusting for sex, age and income, which can affect the HCV infection, ADI and area-level HCV prevalence. The association between individual HCV infections and ADI quartiles was calculated using ORs.

Integrated individual and area-level analysis

A multilevel logistic regression analysis was performed to account for both individual-level and area-level variation, with area-level HCV prevalence included as a contextual variable rather than as a direct individual-level exposure. Four sequential models were constructed to estimate the fixed effects of individual-level and area-level factors and random intercepts representing inter-area variation. Four models were compared: (1) a null model including only area-level random effects; (2) an individual-level model; (3) an area-level model and (4) a full model including both individual-level and area-level variables. In the area-level model, the area-level HCV prevalence and ADI were analysed separately to examine the detailed effects of area-level variables. To evaluate the degree to which inter-area variation was explained by changes between each model, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) and percentage change in variation (PCV) were calculated and compared with the null model. ICC represents the proportion of variation in HCV infection explained by inter-area differences out of the total variation, and PCV represents the percentage reduction in inter-area variation when variables were added to the model. Although this study used a case–control design, the outcome was relatively rare, and controls were selected from the source population using an unbiased random sampling approach; therefore, the estimated ORs can be interpreted as close approximations of relative risks. All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS Enterprise Guide V.8.1 (SAS Institute) and R V.4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with the significance level set at p<0.05.

Risk stratification for targeted interventions

To develop region-specific HCV elimination strategies, we stratified the 250 districts into three risk tiers based on 16 combinations of HCV prevalence quartiles (Q1–Q4) and ADI quartiles (Q1–Q4). The ‘low-risk’ tier comprised six groups: districts with HCV prevalence in Q1, or those with prevalence in Q2 combined with an ADI in Q1 or Q2. The ‘high-risk’ tier comprised five groups: districts with HCV prevalence in Q4, or those with prevalence in Q3 combined with an ADI in Q4. The remaining five groups were classified as the ‘moderate-risk’ tier.

Patient and public involvement

None.

Results

Characteristics of the study subjects

The ADI was calculated for each study year (250, 2005, 251 in 2010, 252, 2015 and 250 in 2020). Temporal changes in the components of the ADI showed that the proportion of the elderly population and single-person households continuously increased, whereas the proportions of low educational attainment, lower social class and substandard housing conditions showed a decreasing trend. Specifically, the proportion of single-person households increased by 48.5% in 2020 compared with 2005, while the proportion of low educational attainment decreased by 55.2% during the same period (online supplemental figure 1). The standardised ADI decreased from a maximum of 17.68 points (2005) to 16.48 points (2020), and the minimum value also decreased from −16.96 points (2005) to −16.02 points (2020), resulting in a gap reduction from 34.64 points (2005) to 32.50 points (2020), representing a 6.2% decrease.

When examining the characteristics of subjects in the area-level analysis, significant differences were observed between the HCV patient group and non-HCV group across all variables (χ² test, p<0.001), and these differences were consistently maintained throughout the study period. The HCV patient group was concentrated in the 50–60 age range, whereas the non-HCV group showed the highest frequency in those under 30 years of age. In terms of income, the proportion of HCV patients in the lowest income quartile (Q4) increased substantially over time, from 15% in 2005 to 30% in 2020, whereas the non-HCV group hovered around 17%–19% over the same period. Regarding area-related variables, both the HCV patient and non-HCV groups showed the highest frequency in areas with a low ADI. However, in the areas with the highest ADI (Q4), the HCV patient group showed a higher frequency and mean ADI than the non-HCV group. Additionally, the HCV patient group was concentrated in areas with the highest HCV prevalence (Q4), and the HCV prevalence per 100 000 in areas where HCV patients resided was higher than the national HCV prevalence per 100 000, adjusted for sex and age. These patterns were consistently observed across all years (table 1).

Area-level analysis results

The age-adjusted, sex-adjusted and area-adjusted HCV prevalence per 100 000 showed a decreasing trend from 151 in 2005 to 117 in 2020.17 However, as the level of deprivation increased, the mean HCV prevalence gradually increased, and in the highest deprivation areas (Q4), the distribution of HCV prevalence showed a wider range and frequent upper extreme values compared with other areas. This trend indicates that in areas with high HCV prevalence, the mean HCV prevalence increased sharply as the level of deprivation increased (online supplemental figure 2).

The Pearson’s correlation analysis between the ADI and area-level HCV prevalence revealed statistically significant positive correlations across all years (r=0.14, 2005; r=0.25, 2020), although the correlation coefficients were generally low. Because most areas were concentrated at low levels of HCV prevalence (below 200), additional analysis was conducted targeting areas with an HCV prevalence of 200 or more. The results showed that the correlation coefficients for areas with HCV prevalence of 200 or more were higher than those for all areas combined (r=0.41, 2005; r=0.51, 2020), confirming that areas with high HCV prevalence have many socioeconomically vulnerable areas, and the association between HCV prevalence and area deprivation was established (online supplemental figure 3, online supplemental table 2).

Linear regression analysis between area-level HCV prevalence and ADI showed statistically significant positive correlations across all years. In areas with HCV prevalence of 200 or more, the regression coefficients ranged from 7.62 to 11.83, which were 2.3 to 3.5 times higher than the regression coefficients for all areas combined, and the coefficient of determination (R-squared; R²) was also substantially higher than that for all areas. However, even in areas with a high HCV prevalence, the coefficient of determination remained below 30%, confirming that factors other than area deprivation had a considerable influence on HCV prevalence (figure 2).

Individual and area-level integrated analysis

Logistic regression analysis revealed that, at the individual level, males had higher odds for HCV infection than females. By age group, due to cohort effects, the highest risk age group shifted from those in their 60 s in 2005 (adjusted OR (aOR): 10.69, 95% CI 10.36 to 11.04) to those in their 70s in 2020 (aOR: 40.65, 95% CI 38.25 to 43.20), with the magnitude of HCV infection odds also increasing. Regarding income, the lowest income group (Q4) showed increasing odds of HCV infection over time, shifting from a protective association in 2005 (aOR: 0.73, 95% CI 0.71 to 0.74) to significantly elevated odds by 2020 (aOR: 1.61, 95% CI 1.57 to 1.64). Among area-level variables, for the ADI, the OR for HCV infection risk in areas with the highest level of deprivation showed an increasing trend from 1.17 times (aOR: 1.17, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.21) in 2005 to 1.33 times (aOR: 1.33, 95% CI 1.28 to 1.37) in 2020. Area HCV prevalence emerged as the critical risk factor for HCV infection, with odds for HCV infection increasing stepwise as the regional HCV prevalence increased. However, the odds for HCV infection in areas with the highest HCV prevalence showed a decreasing trend over time, contrary to area deprivation levels, from 4.21 times (aOR: 4.21, 95% CI 4.08 to 4.36) in 2005 to 3.43 times (aOR: 3.43, 95% CI 3.33 to 3.54) in 2020 (figure 3).

Through multilevel logistic regression analysis of individual and area-level factors affecting HCV infection, the ICC values for individual, area, combined individual and area models compared with the null model ranged from 0.24 to 0.73 across all years, which were substantially greater than 0.1. This confirmed that there were considerably large inter-regional differences in HCV infection status and that multilevel analysis was appropriate (table 2, online supplemental table 3).

The PCV representing the explanatory power of model 1, which included only individual-level variables of sex, age and income ranged from 6.25% to 16.29% and showed an increasing trend over time. Among the individual-level variables, age consistently showed strong associations across all years, with HCV infection odds increasing dramatically with age. As of 2020, the OR for those in their 70s compared with those under 30 was highest at 40.06 times (aOR: 40.06, 95% CI 37.67 to 42.61). The association between income and HCV infection reversed over the study period. While lower income was associated with reduced odds in 2005, the lowest income group exhibited progressively higher odds of infection in subsequent years (aOR: 1.18, 1.39 and 1.60 in 2010, 2015, and 2020, respectively) (table 2, online supplemental table 3).

Examining the independent effect of the ADI (model 2), the model’s explanatory power (PCV) increased approximately 5 times, from 4.07% in 2005 to 20.27% in 2020. The HCV infection OR for areas with the highest deprivation level (Q4) compared with areas with the lowest level (Q1) continuously increased over time from 1.39 times (aOR: 1.39, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.72) in 2005 to 2.13 times (aOR: 2.13, 95% CI 1.74 to 2.61) in 2020 (online supplemental table 3).

The explanatory power of the model, including only the area-level HCV prevalence (model 3), decreased from 78.85% in 2005 to 70.67% in 2020. Although it acted as a powerful risk factor across all years, unlike other factors, the model’s explanatory power decreased over time. Areas with the highest HCV prevalence (Q4) had an OR of 4.09 times (aOR: 4.09, 95% CI 3.62 to 4.63) compared with areas with the lowest prevalence (Q1) in 2020 (table 2). The explanatory power of the model including all area-level variables (model 4) was similar to the sum of the explanatory power of the individual models (models 2 and 3) in 2005 but showed a decreasing trend over time. The HCV infection ORs in model 4 also decreased slightly compared with those in the individual models (online supplemental table 3).

The explanatory power of the final model, including all variables (model 7), ranged from 84.03% in 2005 to 82.62% in 2020, showing the highest explanatory power among all the models. For individual-level variables, the ORs for HCV infection were higher in males than females. While the highest OR was observed in those in their 60s in 2005, from 2010 onwards, it was highest in those in their 70s, with the HCV infection OR for those in their 70s showing a gradually increasing trend over time. Additionally, lower income was associated with higher HCV infection odds over time. In contrast, among the area-level variables, the HCV infection OR for the ADI was not significant in any year, but a higher regional HCV prevalence was associated with increased HCV infection odds (table 2, online supplemental table 3).

Distribution of regional risk tiers

Based on the combined matrix of HCV prevalence and ADI quartiles in 2020 (figure 4A), the 250 districts were categorised into three risk tiers for targeted interventions. A total of 72 districts were classified as high-risk, 79 as moderate-risk and 99 as low-risk. Discussion

Using the Korean population census data and NHIS claims data from 2005 to 2020, we conducted association analyses between area-level variables (ADI and HCV prevalence) and integrated risk analyses that combined individual and area-level variables. A study of the associations between area-level variables confirmed that higher levels of area deprivation were associated with increased HCV prevalence, and this association strengthened over time. Notably, the regional HCV prevalence was found to have a much stronger influence on individual HCV infection risk than the ADI. Korea’s ADI decreased from 34.64 points in 2005 to 32.50 points in 2020; however, among the components of the ADI, the proportions of elderly households and single-person households increased over time. Rather than relying on external this pattern driven by the increasing proportions of elderly and single-households observed in our data suggests ongoing changes in family structure and the socioeconomic environment during the study period. While the overall HCV prevalence decreased, the gap in HCV prevalence between deprivation levels actually widened, indicating that health inequalities were deepening.

Correlation analysis between ADI and HCV prevalence revealed that the correlation coefficients in areas within the top 20% of HCV prevalence were approximately 2–3 times higher than those in all areas combined. This suggests that the association between socioeconomic vulnerability and HCV prevalence is not uniform, but rather particularly pronounced in areas with the highest infectious burden. In the linear regression analyses, the regression coefficient was 2.3–3.5 times higher in areas with an HCV prevalence of 200 or more than in all areas combined, confirming that socioeconomic vulnerability in high-prevalence areas tends to further exacerbate health inequalities. This finding suggests that the impact of area-level deprivation on HCV transmission may depend on the size of the existing viral reservoir. In low-prevalence areas, limited sources of infection might restrict overall transmission, making the effect of deprivation less apparent. Conversely, in high-prevalence areas, socioeconomic deprivation could more actively facilitate the spread of the virus among vulnerable populations. However, the fact that the explanatory power of the model remained below 30%, even in areas with HCV prevalence in the top 20%, suggests that individual-level risk factors and unmeasured area characteristics, in addition to the ADI, work in complex combinations.

Logistic regression analysis integrating individual and area levels showed that the adjusted odds of HCV infection for individuals living in areas with the highest deprivation levels (ADI Q4) after adjusting for sex, age, income and area-level HCV prevalence increased from 1.17 (95% CI 1.13 to 1.21) in 2005 to 1.33 (95% CI 1.28 to 1.37) in 2020. However, the OR for areas with the highest regional HCV prevalence was 4.09 times as of 2020, which is approximately twice the risk of ADI. This demonstrates that, while the influence of area deprivation level on HCV prevalence is strengthening over time, regional prevalence itself is a crucial determining factor.

The multilevel logistic regression analysis results clearly demonstrated the complex influence of individual-level and area-level factors on HCV and the importance of interregional disparities. The ORs for age in the multilevel analysis model, including both individual and area levels (model 7), showed the highest OR in the 60s age group in 2005, which shifted to the 70s age group from 2010 onwards, displaying a typical cohort effect. Furthermore, because HCV is predominantly a chronic infection, the strong association observed between older age and HCV infection should be interpreted with caution; it likely reflects not only the age-specific risk of acquiring the infection but also the cumulative persistence of chronic cases over time. However, the 30s age group consistently maintained high ORs (2005, aOR: 3.24; 2020, aOR: 3.85), suggesting that new infections continue to occur in younger age groups. Additionally, the non-significant effect of the ADI in the final model (model 7) indicated that the independent effect of the area deprivation level was relatively weakened with the addition of powerful variables, such as individual-level variables and regional HCV prevalence. In contrast, HCV prevalence consistently showed a higher HCV infection risk in high-prevalence areas across all models and maintained significance in the final model. In our multilevel model, area-level HCV prevalence was consistently associated with higher odds of individual HCV infection. It is important to note that because HCV is a transmissible infectious disease, high area-level prevalence represents a larger infectious reservoir within the community. Thus, the significant ORs observed do not merely reflect a tautological aggregation of cases, but rather indicate a genuinely elevated individual risk of exposure and transmission (ie, higher transmission pressure) in these regions. This suggests that area-level HCV prevalence is a critical ecological risk factor that can help identify areas warranting targeted public health interventions.

This study showed results consistent with those of previous studies. A US study reported that HCV prevalence increased by 25% per one-unit increase in the ADI,18 and our study also found that areas with high deprivation levels had 33% higher odds of HCV infection compared with areas with low deprivation levels as of 2020 (aOR: 1.33 CI 1.28 to 1.37). A study from Japan, which is socioeconomically similar to Korea, analysed the association between area-level and individual-level socioeconomic factors and HCV prevalence, and found that HCV prevalence increased with higher area deprivation and lower individual income levels.10 This suggests the possibility of common socioeconomic determinants in HCV prevalence patterns.

Our study had certain limitations. First, we only included sex and age as individual-level variables, and did not consider individual factors, such as education level and health behaviours, and additional area factors, such as healthcare accessibility. Second, because this study relied on the NHIS claims database, only individuals with diagnosed HCV who generated healthcare claims could be identified. Undiagnosed infections and cases among individuals who did not seek medical care were therefore not captured, which may have led to underestimation of the true HCV prevalence. If underdiagnosis is more common in socioeconomically vulnerable areas with limited access to screening and care, the observed associations may underestimate the true concentration of HCV burden in underserved populations. Third, due to the inherent limitations of the claims database and the social stigma associated with injection drug use in Korea, we could not identify and adjust for individual-level injection drug use, which is a major risk factor for HCV infection. Finally, owing to the cross-sectional study design, causal relationships between the ADI and HCV prevalence cannot be established. Future research needs to clearly identify the causal relationships through longitudinal studies that include more diverse variables.

Despite these limitations, this study has several strengths. First, this is the first study in Korea to analyse the relationship between the ADI and HCV prevalence over a period of 15 years. Second, we enhanced the validity of the research results by applying various statistical methods that included both area and individual variables. Third, the generalisability of the outcomes was increased by the use of large-scale nationwide data. While geographic variation in HCV prevalence alone highlights the need for localised approaches, our findings suggest that integrating the ADI provides a more comprehensive framework for public health planning. Relying solely on prevalence mapping may overlook socioeconomically vulnerable areas where transmission pressure is amplified. Furthermore, our results demonstrated a strong and clear association between advancing age and increased individual risk of HCV infection.

Building on these findings, we suggest a potential conceptual framework for HCV elimination that combines two regional factors (viral reservoir and socioeconomic vulnerability) with one crucial individual factor (age). By stratifying the 250 districts into three risk tiers based on the combination of HCV prevalence and ADI, policymakers could consider tailoring secondary and tertiary interventions such as financial support for HCV screening and treatment according to both regional risk and individual age.

For example, while primary prevention efforts like public awareness campaigns could be universally implemented, subsidised screening and treatment might be initiated at younger ages in higher-risk environments (eg, ages ≥40 years in high-risk areas, ≥50 years in moderate-risk areas and ≥60 years in low-risk areas). This targeted approach is presented as an illustrative model to demonstrate how public health resources might be efficiently directed toward populations with the highest ecological and individual risks, thereby supporting progress toward national HCV elimination.

In conclusion, this study highlights the need for region-specific HCV elimination strategies that account for both geographic and socioeconomic factors. By evaluating area-level variables (HCV prevalence and ADI) alongside individual characteristics (sex, age and income), we demonstrate how regional and individual vulnerabilities jointly affect HCV infection risk. These findings suggest that targeting high-prevalence areas alone is insufficient; the socioeconomic context of both regions and individuals should be integrated into policy design. As Korea introduces HCV antibody testing for 56-year-olds in the national screening programme in 2025, future screening strategies should incorporate area deprivation and individual economic vulnerability as core targeting criteria. Specifically, developing community-based programmes tailored to socioeconomically vulnerable populations, even within areas of similarly high HCV prevalence, will be a critical component of successful HCV elimination.
