Emergence From Minimally Conscious State Following Addition of Zolpidem to Amantadine Therapy in a Patient With Preserved Cortical Metabolism After Subarachnoid Hemorrhage: A Case Report
Authors: Jiwoong Yoo, Nayeon Ko
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report the case of an 80-year-old female patient with subarachnoid hemorrhage who demonstrated marked improvement in the Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRS-R) score following the addition of zolpidem to amantadine therapy. Brain positron emission tomography–computed tomography demonstrated diffuse cerebral hypometabolism with relatively preserved fronto-parietal cortical uptake, suggesting preserved cortical metabolic activity. After initiating zolpidem 10 mg and escalating amantadine to 200 mg/day, the patient’s CRS-R score increased from 12 to 19 within three days, corresponding to emergence from a minimally conscious state. This case suggests that preserved cortical metabolic activity may be associated with the additive or synergistic effects of pharmacological arousal therapy in disorders of consciousness in non-Traumatic Brain Injury patients.

INTRODUCTION

Disorders of consciousness (DOC) include coma, unresponsive wakefulness syndrome, and minimally conscious state (MCS). Emergence from minimally conscious state (eMCS) is defined by recovery of functional communication or object use. Pharmacologic agents such as zolpidem and amantadine have been investigated to enhance arousal in DOC [1].

Amantadine, a dopamine agonist and N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor modulator, increases metabolism in fronto-parietal regions through dopaminergic activation [2]. Zolpidem, a non-benzodiazepine hypnotic with α1-selective gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-A receptor activity [3], has been paradoxically shown to restore consciousness, arousal, cognition, and motor functions in patients with DOC.

We report a non-traumatic subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH) patient who showed eMCS following the addition of zolpidem to amantadine therapy, with brain positron emission tomography–computed tomography (PET-CT) findings suggesting residual cortical metabolic activity. The requirement of informed consent was waived by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Soonchunhyang University Hospital Seoul (IRB No. 2025-12-021).

CASE DESCRIPTION

An 80-year-old woman with hypertension and diabetes mellitus was admitted for sudden loss of consciousness. Brain computed tomography revealed SAH with intraventricular hemorrhage, and she underwent emergent decompressive craniectomy with external ventricular drainage (Fig. 1). Postoperatively, she remained comatose with spontaneous eye opening and intact brainstem reflexes (Fig. 2).

After transfer to the rehabilitation department, neurological examination revealed a Coma Recovery Scale-Revised (CRS-R) score of 7 (A1 V1 M3 O0 C0 Ar2), consistent with an MCS. Manual muscle testing showed severe quadriplegia with poor-grade strength in all extremities. Laboratory findings were unremarkable.

Amantadine 100 mg daily was initially initiated on post-stroke day 61 as a dopaminergic and NMDA-modulating trial to enhance arousal. No clinical improvement was noted after several days of treatment. Routine electroencephalography was performed to exclude seizure activity, and no epileptiform discharges were noted. Brain PET-CT was performed on post-stroke day 65 to evaluate residual cortical metabolic activity as the patient showed no clinical response to initial amantadine therapy. PET-CT showed diffuse hypometabolism in left parietal and right cerebellar regions but preserved fronto-parietal cortical uptake, suggesting residual functional networks (Fig. 3). These findings are compatible with preserved cortical network activity despite widespread diaschisis.

Given the lack of response to low-dose amantadine and the presence of residual cortical metabolism on PET-CT, zolpidem 10 mg was administered once daily in the morning, and the amantadine dose was escalated to 100 mg twice daily. Considering the short half-life of zolpidem, the CRS-R was assessed 2 hours after administration daily by two independent physicians, using standardized bedside examination. Within 24 hours, her CRS-R score markedly increased to 12 (A3 V3 M3 O0 C1 Ar2), and to 19 (A4 V3 M4 O3 C2 Ar3) at day 3, indicating eMCS. The patient began to follow simple commands and communicate reliably through gestures such as blinking and nodding.

The patient showed improved cognitive responsiveness in the morning after medication administration; however, during afternoon rehabilitation sessions, a decline in arousal level was observed, with a tendency toward somnolence. At the time of discharge, the CRS-R score was 19, and motor function improved, particularly in the right upper limb (from poor grade to fair grade). No adverse events occurred, and the patient remained hemodynamically stable with preserved responsiveness. A detailed summary of temporal interventions and CRS-R scores is presented in Table 1.

DISCUSSION

This case suggests that residual cortical metabolic activity may be associated with responsiveness to the addition of zolpidem during ongoing amantadine therapy. PET-CT findings revealed decreased but not absent cerebral metabolism, suggesting preservation of residual cortico-thalamic network integrity. In addition, a combination of amantadine 200 mg with zolpidem 10 mg may have contributed synergistically to the observed improvement of consciousness.

Although spontaneous recovery cannot be completely excluded, the patient's neurological status had remained relatively stable for more than two months after stroke onset before a marked improvement in consciousness was observed following the addition of zolpidem and escalation of amantadine therapy. Notably, increased arousal was consistently observed after morning medication administration, whereas reduced responsiveness was noted during afternoon rehabilitation sessions. This temporal pattern may be compatible with a zolpidem-responsive phenotype; however, causality cannot be established because of the concomitant amantadine dose escalation and the possibility of spontaneous neurological recovery [4].

Previous theories emphasized diaschisis as the main mechanism of reversible network dysfunction. However, our findings suggest that preserved residual cortical metabolic activity may be associated with responsiveness to pharmacologic arousal therapy. Zolpidem’s α1-selective GABA-A receptor modulation may inhibit globus pallidus activity, leading to thalamo-cortical disinhibition and paradoxical cortical activation when residual metabolism persists. These observations align with prior reports demonstrating transient restoration of consciousness and metabolism in zolpidem responders [56]. Our case extends these findings to non-traumatic brain injury, highlighting the importance of neuroimaging to identify recoverable brain networks. Furthermore, previous studies have reported relatively low response rates of approximately 5%–7% to zolpidem in DOC [47]. The present case may underscore the clinical rarity and potential significance of paradoxical arousal responses in elderly patients with non-traumatic brain injury.

Additionally, the combination therapy of zolpidem-amantadine could suggest a plausible mechanism with the mesocircuit fronto-parietal model; amantadine enhances fronto-parietal brain metabolism as a dopamine agonist and NMDA agonist, while zolpidem suppresses globus pallidus as a GABA agonist, which consequently stops the inhibitory signal from globus pallidus to thalamus and subordinate motor cortices [18].

This report has several limitations. First, as a single-case study, it is subject to limited generalizability and cannot establish causality. The observed improvement may have been influenced by several factors, including spontaneous neurological recovery, cumulative rehabilitation effects, delayed therapeutic effects of amantadine, or potential synergistic interactions between amantadine and zolpidem. Second, no controlled zolpidem washout or rechallenge protocol was performed, precluding assessment of the independent contribution of zolpidem. Third, PET-CT findings were based on qualitative interpretation without quantitative metabolic analysis (e.g. standardized uptake value measurements, region-of-interest analysis), which limits establishment of metabolic thresholds predictive of zolpidem responsiveness. Finally, although behavioral fluctuations following medication administration were observed, no additional evening CRS-R assessments were performed, and formal blinded or multi-rater validation was not conducted, limiting the objectivity of the observed response pattern.

This case suggests that residual cortical metabolic activity may be associated with responsiveness to the addition of zolpidem during ongoing amantadine therapy, although the independent contribution of zolpidem cannot be determined from a single-case observation. The findings also raise the possibility of synergistic effects between amantadine and zolpidem in selected patients with DOC. As evidence accumulates, neuroimaging findings may provide additional information when considering pharmacologic arousal therapies beyond traumatic brain injury.
