Sensitivity of current Tropilaelaps mercedesae monitoring methods in Apis mellifera colonies
Authors: Rogan Tokach, Dan Aurell, Bajaree Chuttong, Geoffrey R Williams
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe parasitic mite Tropilaelaps mercedesae (Anderson & Morgan) has recently been confirmed in western honey bee (Apis mellifera L.) colonies in western Asia and eastern Europe, marking a range expansion that necessitates effective monitoring methods to rapidly detect introductions into new regions, as well as promote integrated pest management where it is established. This study compared the sensitivity of current T. mercedesae monitoring methods, using the nearly globally distributed A. mellifera parasitic mite Varroa destructor (Anderson & Trueman) for comparison. Methods tested were bottom board sticky trap, worker brood cell uncapping, brood frame bump test, alcohol wash, and powdered sugar shake. Additionally, detection thresholds (cf. ED50) were determined using dose-response analyses for the brood frame bump test, alcohol wash, and powdered sugar shake methods to understand how T. mercedesae worker brood cell infestation density influences the ability of different methods to detect T. mercedesae. Unlike for V. destructor, adult bee-based methods (alcohol wash and powdered sugar shake) had low sensitivity to detect T. mercedesae. The most sensitive method was bottom board sticky traps, followed by worker brood cell uncapping and brood frame bump tests. The adult bee-based methods (alcohol wash and powdered sugar shake) were the least sensitive methods of detecting T. mercedesae. Similarly, the alcohol wash and powdered sugar shake methods had a higher detection threshold than brood frame bump tests. This work provides valuable insights into T. mercedesae monitoring, especially as surveillance efforts increase in T. mercedesae-free regions considering the mite’s recently detected range expansion.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46467-0.Subject terms: Ecology, Ecology, Zoology

Introduction

Tropilaelaps mercedesae (Anderson & Morgan) is a parasitic mite of honey bees (Apis mellifera L.) that damages colonies by feeding on developing larval and pupal honey bees (brood)1,2. Parasitism reduces honey bee emergence weight, causes deformities, and transmits viruses3,4. Despite initial uncertainty about whether T. mercedesae mites could establish in temperate areas where pauses in brood rearing occur5, T. mercedesae has been confirmed spreading westward into temperate areas of western Asia and eastern Europe1,6–8. To prevent this damaging pest from establishing in new areas, and to promote effective integrated pest management strategies in areas where it is established, appropriate monitoring techniques are urgently needed.

When monitoring for invasive pests, a fundamental concern is test sensitivity – the probability of correctly detecting a pest if present in a colony9–11. It is also important to know if tests can detect the pest at low densities12. The relationship between the density of organisms and the probability of detecting them can be examined with dose-response analyses, yielding measures such as ED50 (hereafter referred to as a detection threshold), which is an estimate of the parasite density required for detection probability to reach 50%.

The most sensitive methods for T. mercedesae monitoring are based on quantifying mite infestations within capped brood cells and on hive-bottom sticky boards9,13,14; however, these monitoring methods are time-consuming and labor-intensive, which makes them impractical for large-scale monitoring programs9. Meanwhile, more time-efficient methods (alcohol wash and powdered sugar shake) are used to monitor the globally distributed parasitic mite Varroa destructor (Anderson & Trueman), a leading cause of colony losses in many global regions15–17. However, research has yielded conflicting results on the sensitivity of these V. destructor monitoring methods when used to monitor T. mercedesae, signaling that simply repurposing V. destructor monitoring methods may not be sufficient for T. mercedesae detection. While these methods – the alcohol wash and powdered sugar shake – are faster on a per colony basis than current T. mercedesae monitoring methods9, it is still unclear if they offer sufficient sensitivity to make them viable for large-scale monitoring programs. For example, Gill et al.18 recently reported that the powdered sugar shake, but not alcohol wash, yielded sensitivity statistically similar to bottom board sticky trap and worker brood cell uncapping. Contrarily, Pettis et al.9 demonstrated the soapy water wash to be significantly less sensitive than bottom board sticky traps, worker brood cell uncapping, and brood frame bump tests. Presently, monitoring programs in the United States commonly use the brood frame bump test that is based on dislodging T. mercedesae from brood frames rather than collecting mites from adult honey bees. Although this method is relatively practical because it can be performed in the field in about 15 min per colony, it provided only moderate sensitivity of 50% when universal infestation was assumed9. This leads to the need to test the suitability of commonly employed, practical monitoring methods that sample live mites from adult honey bees or outside of capped honey bee brood cells (i.e., brood frame bump test) to compare the effectiveness of monitoring methods. Overall, effective and efficient detection methods are crucial to identifying new incursions of T. mercedesae quickly. This study therefore aims to compare the sensitivity of current T. mercedesae and V. destructor monitoring methods and to understand how T. mercedesae worker brood cell density influences detection.

Results

All 26 colonies in the study were infested by T. mercedesae as they were detected by mite fall on sticky boards from each colony. Visually estimated adult bee population ranged from 1.5 to 5.5 frames of bees (mean = 3.9; SD = 1.1).

For T. mercedesae, bottom board sticky trap was the most sensitive, followed by worker brood cell uncapping, brood frame bump test, alcohol wash, and powdered sugar shake. All methods were significantly different from one another, except for alcohol wash and powdered sugar shake that were statistically similar. The detection threshold for the brood frame bump test was 1.15% T. mercedesae cell infestation (SE = 0.46; 95% CI: 0.26–2.04); for the alcohol wash it was 5.20% (SE = 2.32; 95% CI: 0.65–9.76) and for the powdered sugar shake it was 5.67% (SE = 2.05; 95% CI: 1.65–9.69) (Fig. 1).

An initial investigation of the spatial distribution of T. mercedesae mites was possible because data on worker brood cell infestation were partitioned into four groups of 50 cells per colony (groups 1 and 2 usually being the opposite sides of the first frame; groups 3 and 4 usually being the opposite sides of the second frame). The correlation between the T. mercedesae infestation of capped brood cells was stronger when comparing the adjacent cohorts that usually represented the opposite sides of each frame (group 1 vs. 2, 3 vs. 4; R2 = 0.74; R2 = 0.88 if an influential point was removed). The correlation was somewhat weaker when comparing the same number of non-adjacent cohorts that usually represented comparisons patches on different brood frames (group 1 vs. 3, 2 vs. 4; R2 = 0.67). For scatterplots of these correlations, see Supplementary Figure S3.

Because 200 worker brood cells were uncapped per colony in four groups of 50 cells, it was also possible to determine the effect of uncapping a smaller number of cells. Overall, uncapping of 200 worker brood cells detected T. mercedesae infestations in 92% of colonies (24 of 26). If 100 worker brood cells had been uncapped, T. mercedesae would have been detected in 85% of colonies (21–23 of 26 for different subsets; mean = 22.2). If only one group of 50 worker brood cells had been uncapped, T. mercedesae would have been detected in 70% of colonies (17–19 of 26 for different subsets; mean = 18.25).

Discussion

With continued geographical expansion of T. mercedesae6,7, there is a need to assess existing monitoring methods to allow inspection programs to use methods that balance sensitivity and time efficiency. The findings in this study resolve discrepancies in the literature about the relative sensitivity of various T. mercedesae monitoring methods9,18, and provide an understanding of what T. mercedesae brood infestations allow different methods to become viable. Combined with published information on the time required for existing T. mercedesae monitoring methods9, this will assist monitoring programs in selecting appropriate methods.

Results showed that bottom board sticky trap, worker brood cell uncapping, and the brood frame bump test had significantly higher sensitivity to detect T. mercedesae than the alcohol wash or powdered sugar shake. This contrasted with results for detection of V. destructor, for which alcohol wash and powdered sugar shake were more sensitive than worker brood cell uncapping. When comparing number of detections per monitoring method for T. mercedesae, results are similar where bottom board sticky traps had the highest mean number of detections followed by worker brood cell uncapping and brood frame bump test. The alcohol wash and powdered sugar shake again proved to be inferior with significantly fewer T. mercedesae detections per sample. This correlation between results is unsurprising since naturally more detections within a sample corresponds with a higher sensitivity of detection. These results confirm the previous findings that bottom board sticky trap and worker brood cell uncapping are sensitive T. mercedesae detection methods9,18. Additionally, our results support the conclusion that the brood frame bump test is a viable method for T. mercedesae detection that balances sensitivity and efficiency9.

Comparing other monitoring methods, our results showed both alcohol washes and powdered sugar shakes to be less sensitive than bottom board sticky traps, worker brood cell uncapping, and brood frame bump tests. This aligns well with previous studies that have documented that T. mercedesaes mites preferentially infest brood compared to adult bees5,9. Whereas Gill et al.18 found powdered sugar shakes performed better than alcohol wash and concluded that it is a reliable T. mercedesaes monitoring technique, we found powdered sugar shakes to be less sensitive than other methods tested and equal to alcohol washes. Physical, adult honey bee-based methods, like the alcohol wash and powdered sugar shake, provided detection in only about a third of colonies, even though all colonies were confirmed to be infested with T. mercedesae mites. This is likely because T. mercedesae mites spend minimal time outside of capped brood cells and have limited presence on adult honey bees19. We found high agreement between powdered sugar shake and alcohol wash methods regarding the presence of T. mercedesae (81%; 21 of 26 colonies); we believe this likely reflects the fundamental similarity of these methods, which sample adult honey bees from the part of the colony containing brood. The reasons for our conflicting results with Gill et al.18 regarding the powdered sugar shake are unclear. However, the T. mercedesae infestation rate was lower in their study (mean of 3.3 T. mercedesae mites detected per 100 cells, compared to mean of 6.0% of worker brood cells infested in our study). This lower infestation could potentially minimize differences seen between methods. Alternatively, their study might have been conducted during conditions that promote the presence of T. mercedesae mites on adult honey bees resulting in the powdered sugar shake performing relatively well. In this sense, colony conditions could have been favorable for increased phoresy of T. mercedesae mites, and drivers of this phoresy should be further studied. Regardless, our results and previous work5,9 indicate these adult honey bee-based methods are usually inferior.

By estimating detection thresholds, we provide context for when the adult honey bee-based methods become more viable. This is important as T. mercedesae mites can reproduce rapidly14 and have been found exiting highly infested colonies on adult honey bees20. Adult honey bee-based monitoring methods were estimated to predictably detect T. mercedesae with 50% confidence only when brood infestations exceeded approximately 5–6% – a level that may be adequate to promote dispersal of T. mercedesae20. In contrast, we estimated that the detection threshold of the brood frame bump test was 1.15%, allowing earlier detection by this method.

Of the faster sampling methods – brood frame bump test, powdered sugar shake, and alcohol wash – our results suggest the brood frame bump test should still be the preferred option, as it can detect T. mercedesae at lower brood infestations and is more time efficient than bottom board sticky trap and worker brood cell uncapping9. Although bottom board sticky traps had 100% sensitivity and provided the highest number of detections per sampling, they require multiple apiary visits and take longer to inspect than brood frame bump test cloths limiting their feasibility for widespread adoption at a larger scale. Similarly, worker brood cell uncapping is also limited by sample processing and is not as practical for large scale adoption.

When V. destructor was being introduced to new regions globally, there was a need for rapid monitoring and detection methods to detect new introductions of V. destructor. Current monitoring needs are shifting to T. mercedesae due to continued expansion of its global range6,7. The findings in this study highlight that simply recycling monitoring methods that are effective for V. destructor will not result in successful detection of T. mercedesae. While the alcohol wash and powdered sugar shake demonstrated superior sensitivity and produced a higher number of mean detections compared to worker brood cell uncapping for V. destructor, those methods were inferior to bottom board sticky trap, worker brood cell uncapping, and the brood frame bump test for T. mercedesae. This is likely primarily attributable to the fact that V. destructor females feed on the fat bodies of honey bee adults while in the dispersal phase, and that this is a necessary part of their life cycle21,22. In comparison, T. mercedesae is thought to be incapable of feeding on adult honey bees5,23, and therefore has a limited need to be on adult bees. This fundamental difference in mite biology is likely the primary cause of differences seen in monitoring method sensitivity between the mite species.

This research highlights the limitations in current T. mercedesae monitoring methods. While bottom board sticky traps and worker brood cell uncapping remain sensitive methods, the time to conduct them limits their scalability and adoption for present day monitoring programs. Furthermore, a faster but still moderately sensitive monitoring method, the brood frame bump test, still takes ~ 15 min per colony including sample collection and inspection9. One potential solution to this issue is to adapt the highly sensitive brood uncapping method while increasing its efficiency. A new, recently developed method of rapidly uncapping worker brood cells using wax strips has shown promise in a small scale test by allowing for visual confirmation of T. mercedesae quicker than the traditional method of uncapping using forceps24. This method simulates worker brood cell uncapping but relies on uncapping a patch of worker cells in a particular area rather than strategically opening cells over multiple frames, markedly reducing the individual time taken per colony24. Additionally, the speed of uncapping opens the possibility of using this method to more rapidly understand T. mercedesae brood infestation to make informed management decisions. Whereas beekeepers use an alcohol wash to determine V. destructor infestation rate on adult honey bees to assist in making decisions regarding treatment control need and timing25, because of the time intensive nature of T. mercedesae monitoring, no current economic thresholds exist to assist beekeepers in making management decision to control T. mercedesae. The practicality of this method provides the possibility of future use to create T. mercedesae economic thresholds. However, one potential drawback of this method is the latent variability in distribution of T. mercedesae within the brood. Results from our study correspond with a previous observation which suggests T. mercedesae may be variably distributed between frames within a colony5. Although our research did not specify the locations of each frame within the colony, our results showed there was more variation between T. mercedesae infestation on frames compared to frame side. Therefore, non-randomness of T. mercedesae infestation distribution on frames and in colonies could have an outsized impact since rapid worker brood uncapping as currently practiced is limited to a singular patch potentially over or underestimating T. mercedesae infestation levels while also introducing the opportunity for more false negatives in detection. While the rapid worker brood uncapping method was not published until after our study was conducted, it has shown promise in a limited sample size and should be further investigated and refined. Furthermore, more work understanding T. mercedesae distribution should be done to allow for further use of theory to design sampling protocols26.

A final limitation for T. mercedesae monitoring is that, currently, all available methods for T. mercedesae detection still rely on visual confirmation, which means detection programs can be restricted by personnel who have experience with visual identification of T. mercedesae. Additionally, T. mercedesae is about 1/3rd the size of V. destructor, which heightens the difficulty of detection by methods that require visual confirmation. A scalable monitoring solution could involve the use of environmental DNA (eDNA) as a tool to detect T. mercedesae, which could limit the need for time-intensive physical sample collection27. Overall, further research is needed to develop sensitive detection methods that can be employed on a broad scale to allow timely intervention when T. mercedesae mites expand into new regions.

Materials and methods

Study colonies

This work was conducted in late January 2024 in Chiang Mai, Thailand on the campus of Chiang Mai University using 26 western honey bee (Apis mellifera) colonies that were housed in single-deep Langstroth boxes containing four to eight deep frames. Colonies had not been treated for T. mercedesae two months prior to monitoring to ensure moderate levels of T. mercedesae infestation. Additionally, prior to any monitoring, all colonies were inspected and determined to be queenright via presence of eggs or visual confirmation of a queen, and colony populations were measured by visually looking between frame spaces to determine frames of adult honey bees28. All sampling occurred over the course of ten days. Worker brood cell uncapping was done first on all colonies within a three-day period. Subsequently, sticky boards were inserted one day following completion of the final worker brood cell uncapping. The remaining sampling for the brood frame bump test, alcohol wash, and powdered sugar shake were performed over the following five days (exact dates provided in the dataset). All sampling methods are described in detail below.

Tropilaelaps mercedesae and Varroa destructor mite monitoring methods

For worker brood cell uncapping, frames were inspected by uncapping a total of 200 cells with 50 worker cells inspected on each side of two frames14. For each side of the frame, five lines of ten cells were uncapped on different areas of each frame. If colonies did not have two frames with 50 capped brood cells on each side, one frame was used and 100 cells were uncapped on each side, but this was only necessary for a small minority of colonies. Uncapping 200 worker brood cells was chosen to follow established methods from Pettis et al.9(who uncapped 100 worker cells on one frame) but to expand sampling to two frames in case infestations vary substantially between frames. Percent cell infestation was calculated as the number of infested cells per 100 cells. Bottom board sticky trapping was conducted by inserting petroleum-jelly covered boards (with a mesh barrier) into colonies for ~ 3 days (70 h) before sticky boards were collected and T. mercedesae mites were counted to determine the total number of mites fallen in the period14. The brood frame bump test was conducted by finding a frame containing open honey bee larvae. Honey bees were then shaken and brushed off the frame. The frame was rapped over a plastic tub two times on each end and side, for eight total strikes9. Water was then used to rinse and strain the contents from the tub through a fine cloth which was then placed in a 120 mL specimen container in 35% ethanol. Later, the cloth was removed and inspected for presence of T. mercedesae. For the alcohol wash and powdered sugar shake, adult honey bees were shaken from a frame containing larvae and a sample of ~ 300 honey bees was collected into respective jars after waiting 10 s for foragers to fly away. Alcohol wash samples were assessed with a triple-rinse alcohol wash29 using 35% ethanol instead of isopropyl alcohol. The sugar shake was performed following Dietemann et al.16, except jars were left standing for two minutes with powdered sugar before shaking for one minute. For both monitoring methods the total number of mites was counted.

Statistics

Data preparation, analysis, and plotting were performed in R 4.2.230 using the packages tidyverse, lme4, emmeans, DHARMa, drc, and ggplot231–36. Sensitivity of monitoring methods and the mean number of T. mercedesae and V. destructor detections were mathematically calculated, and statistical analyses were used to formally assess the effect of monitoring method and conduct post-hoc analyses. The sensitivity of each monitoring method was determined by calculating the percentage of sampled colonies (total n = 26) that were positive by each method. The mean number of detections was calculated based on the number of infested cells detected per colony (worker brood cell uncapping) or the number of mites detected per colony for each individual sample (all other methods). Generalized linear mixed effects models37 were used to compare the sensitivity and number of detections among monitoring methods, using a binomial distribution and a negative binomial distribution, respectively. A random effect of colony ID was included as a random effect to account for the multiple observations per colony (i.e., one observation per monitoring method)31. Separate analyses were performed for T. mercedesae and V. destructor. As monitoring method was a significant predictor of detection for both mite species in both analyses, Tukey-corrected post-hoc tests were performed between methods (all pairwise comparisons) using the emmeans package35,37. Model fits were checked using the DHARMa package36.

To determine how worker brood cell density of T. mercedesae mites in colonies affected the performance of monitoring methods, the detection by the more time efficient methods (brood frame bump test, powdered sugar shake, and alcohol wash) as determined by Pettis et al.9 was assessed against detection by a well-established method that measures mite density in the worker brood cells (worker brood cell uncapping)9. A dose-response analysis was performed using the drc package32, fitting two-parameter binomial log-logistic models with detection predicted by percent T. mercedesae infestation of brood cells. Models were fitted separately for each monitoring method. A detection threshold for each method was estimated, and confidence intervals were calculated using the delta method32. This threshold represents the T. mercedesae worker brood infestation required for a 50% probability of detection (cf., ED50).

A corresponding analysis of detection thresholds for V. destructor sampling methods was intended but was omitted after reviewing the data. For T. mercedesae, it was sensible to use percent infestation from worker brood cell uncapping (a sensitive method) to predict detection by brood frame bump test, alcohol wash, and powdered sugar shake. For V. destructor, however, worker brood cell uncapping was less sensitive than either alcohol wash and powdered sugar shake (see Results) and was therefore not useful as a reference method to determine detection thresholds for adult bee-based V. destructor sampling methods.

To determine whether there was more variability in T. mercedesae worker brood infestation between frames or between sides of the same frame, we took advantage of the data collection that was partitioned into data on 50-cell groups, each of which represented the 50 cells uncapped on one side of a frame (except for a small number of colonies where lack of brood complicated data collection as described above). Given the small number of exceptions, we determined the Pearson correlation between “adjacent” groups in the data (groups 1 and 2; 3 and 4) to determine the consistency of infestation between opposite sides of a frame; we determined the Pearson correlation between “non-adjacent” groups in the data (groups 1 and 3; 2 and 4) to determine the consistency of infestation between frames.

To determine the effect on T. mercedesae detection of uncapping smaller numbers of cells, we assessed the proportion of colonies where mites were detected when different subsets of collected data were retained. We considered the proportion of detections that occurred when all 200 cells were included; when two groups of 50 cells were included (group 1 and 2; 1 and 3; 1 and 4; 2 and 3; 2 and 4; 3 and 4); and when only one group of 50 cells was included (group 1; 2; 3; 4).
