Economic burden of recurrence in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma after curative-intent treatment in Spain
Authors: M Varela, J Feliu, M Garrido-Siles, MA Gómez-Bravo, S González, J Irurzun, L Ruiz de Alda, P Vieitez, D Carcedo, M Crespo, R Vera, M Reig
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDespite potentially curative-intent treatments such as resection, ablation, or liver transplantation, a significant proportion of patients with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) experience recurrence. This study aims to quantify the economic burden of HCC recurrence in patients initially diagnosed with a BCLC-0 or BCLC-A tumor, who received curative-intent treatment and achieved an initial radiological complete response.MethodsA decision-tree model was developed to simulate the management of HCC recurrence over a 1-year time horizon. As a secondary analysis, the time horizon was extended to 5 years to capture the cost of a potential second recurrence. Clinical parameters included HCC stage (0, A, B, C, and D, per Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer [BCLC] staging), treatment modalities, adverse events and routine follow-up, were provided by a multidisciplinary panel of experts. The analysis was conducted from the Spanish National Health System perspective, considering only direct medical costs (expressed in 2025 euros). Deterministic sensitivity analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the base case results.ResultsThe target population was estimated to be 1,250 patients with HCC recurrence after curative-intent treatment of the first HCC in Spain. The total cost of managing these 1,250 recurrences over one year was estimated at €33 million, with an average cost per recurrence of €26,426. When the time horizon was extended to 5 years, the total cost increased to €43.3 million (+€10.3 million), primarily due to 228 additional recurrences. The average cost per recurrence over 5 years rose to €34,637, and a hypothetical patient experiencing two recurrences over this period would cost €59,542. Sensitivity analyses confirmed the robustness of these results.ConclusionsThe literature on the economic burden of HCC is scarce. This study is the first to estimate the cost of HCC recurrence after curative-intent treatment in Spain. Further research is needed to better understand the full economic impact of this complex disease.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13561-026-00796-y.

Background

Liver cancer is the sixth most commonly diagnosed cancer and the third leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide. Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the most common type of primary liver cancer, accounting for approximately 90% of cases. The main risk factors associated with HCC include chronic hepatitis B and C virus infections, non-alcoholic fatty liver disease, alcohol consumption and tobacco smoking [1].

The Barcelona Clinic Liver Cancer (BCLC) staging and treatment algorithm is the most widely adopted in clinical practice for HCC management. It stratifies patients into five stages [very early (0), early (A), intermediate (B), advanced (C) and terminal (D)] with different prognoses and allocates different treatment strategies based on disease extension, liver function, and patient performance status [1, 2].

According to the 2022 BCLC update, BCLC-0 and BCLC-A patients are candidates for potentially curative-intent treatments such as liver transplantation (LT), surgical resection or ablation. When curative treatments are not feasible, locoregional therapies (LRTs) such as transarterial chemoembolization (TACE) or transarterial radioembolization (TARE) may be considered. For BCLC-B patients, recommended options include LT, TACE or systemic therapy. BCLC-C patients receive subsequent lines of systemic therapy, including immunotherapy combinations and tyrosine kinase inhibitors. However, when these options are not feasible, clinical trials have to be considered. Finally, BCLC-D patients are managed with best supportive care (BSC) [2].

HCC recurrence remains a major challenge in disease management. Despite the availability of curative-intent treatments, up to 70% of patients experience recurrence within five years after surgical resection or ablation [1, 3, 4]. This high recurrence rate significantly contributes to the poor prognosis and high mortality of the disease. Currently, there are no approved adjuvant therapies, making close postoperative surveillance essential, particularly during the first two years after treatment when recurrence risk is higher [1, 2]. Additionally, identifying risk factors for recurrence is essential for improving patient outcomes after the first HCC treatment [5].

Studies on the economic burden of cancer have become an important area of research, as they provide valuable insights into the costs faced by healthcare systems and the allocation of resources, thereby contributing to the sustainability of health systems. While the costs associated with other cancers are well documented, there is a gap in the literature concerning the cost of HCC, particularly in the context of recurrence.

The aim of this study is to estimate the economic burden of HCC recurrence in patients with a first HCC at BCLC-0 or BCLC-A stage, who underwent curative-intent treatment and achieved an initial radiological complete response.

Methods

Data collection

A multidisciplinary panel of 8 clinical experts from various regions of Spain (2 oncologists, 2 hepatologists, 1 surgeon, 1 radiologist and 2 hospital pharmacists) completed a questionnaire on the management of HCC recurrence in Spain, based on their routine clinical practice. This enabled gathering information on the key clinical and resource-use parameters required for the development of the model. Following this initial phase, a series of meetings were organized between January and May 2024 to present the questionnaire results to the expert panel and reach consensus on the final parameters to be included in the model.

Model design and target population

A decision-tree model was developed to estimate the cost of disease recurrence after curative-intent treatment of first HCC via resection, ablation or LT (Fig. 1). Such models are particularly well suited to short-time horizons. The hypothetical cohort of patients enters the model at the time of HCC recurrence. Depending on the stage of recurrence, different treatment options may be offered in accordance with the latest European Association for the Study of the Liver (EASL) and European Society for Medical Oncology (ESMO) clinical guidelines [1, 6], as validated by a panel of experts:

BCLC-A or BCLC-B stage HCC recurrence: Very early or early-stage patients may receive potentially curative-intent treatments (resection, ablation or LT) or LRTs (TACE, TACE plus ablation, TARE or stereotactic body radiotherapy [SBRT]). However, if these options are not suitable, patients may instead receive systemic therapy or BSC.

BCLC-C stage HCC recurrence: Patients with advanced disease receive subsequent lines of systemic therapy, depending on treatment response or until unacceptable toxicity occurs. If systemic therapy is not feasible, BSC is provided.

BCLC-D stage HCC recurrence: Patients with terminal disease receive BSC focused on symptom control and/or management of cirrhosis complications.

Diagram of the model

The primary analysis adopted a 1-year time horizon, corresponding to the year in which HCC recurrence occurs. Additionally, a secondary analysis extended the time horizon to 5 years to estimate the cost of a potential second HCC recurrence.

The target population was defined as patients with HCC recurrence after receiving curative-intent treatment (resection, ablation or LT) for their first HCC at BCLC-0 or BCLC-A stage, and achieving a complete radiological response. The estimated number of such patients in Spain was derived from epidemiological data reported in the literature [1, 3, 4, 7, 8] and validated by the expert panel (Table S1, Additional file 1). This yielded a target population of 1,250 hypothetical patients with HCC recurrence in Spain.

The analysis was conducted from the Spanish National Health System perspective, so only direct medical costs were considered.

Patients with BCLC-C recurrence, as well as those with BCLC-A or BCLC-B who are not eligible for curative-intent treatments or LRTs, receive either first line (1 L) systemic therapy (84.5%) or BSC (15.5%). Among those receiving 1 L systemic therapy, 53.85% go on to a second line (2 L) treatment. To simplify the model, subsequent lines of therapy beyond 2 L were not included, as only a small proportion of patients currently receive a third line (3 L), and no specific treatments are approved for this line in Spain.

Systemic therapies included in the analysis were limited to those approved and available in Spain at the time of expert panel validation (January-May 2024). 1 L therapies included atezolizumab–bevacizumab (78.13%), sorafenib (14.58%), and lenvatinib (7.29%). In the 2 L setting, sorafenib (90.80%) and lenvatinib (9.20%) were considered.

For the calculation of drug acquisition costs, the expert panel agreed to assume a uniform duration of treatment (DoT) of 6 months for all treatments in the base case. This standardization ensured that more effective treatments with longer durations were not penalized. Nonetheless, alternative scenarios were considered in the sensitivity analysis, incorporating specific DoTs or progression-free survival (PFS) data from randomized clinical trials or real-world studies [9–13] (Table S7, Additional file 1).

The model also accounted for the use of LRTs following, or concurrently with, systemic treatment: TACE (12%), TARE (11%), and SBRT (5%).

Finally, grade ≥ 3 treatment-related adverse events (Tr-AEs) reported in clinical trials with a frequency > 3% were included as one-off cost in the model [9, 10] (Table S2, Additional file 1).

Direct medical costs

The model included the following direct medical costs, expressed in 2025 euros: drug acquisition costs, costs of curative-intent treatments and LRTs, costs of Tr-AEs, BSC costs and costs related to healthcare resource use for recurrence diagnosis, management and follow-up.

Healthcare resource utilization

The diagnosis and characterization of HCC recurrence involves the use of various healthcare resources. According to the expert panel, all patients require a complete blood test and a visit to the specialist. In addition, 82% of patients undergo a computed tomography (CT) scan, 18% require magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), 30% receive ultrasound, 10% undergo a full-body positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) and 20% require a liver biopsy.

Once the diagnosis and characterization of recurrence is made, patients with BCLC-A, BCLC-B or BCLC-C stage disease undergo routine follow-up, which entails additional healthcare resource use. The frequency of follow-up procedures is detailed in Table 2 on an annualized basis.

Furthermore, within the course of advanced stage BCLC-C, in addition to the disease management described above, there is an additional use of resources that varies depending on the location of metastases. The expert panel estimated that 30% of patients with BCLC-C recurrence develop bone metastases, which require additional resource consumption that includes surgery (2% of patients, once per year) and palliative radiotherapy (65% of patients, once per year).

For patients with BCLC-D recurrence, an end-of-life cost was applied.

Unit costs

Drug acquisition costs were expressed as the ex-factory price considering the corresponding deductions according to Royal Decree-Law (RDL) 08/2010 when applicable [14].

Unit drug costs were obtained from official Spanish sources [15, 16] while unit costs of curative-intent treatments, LRTs, healthcare resources and Tr-AEs were obtained from the literature [17] and from Spanish healthcare databases [18, 19] (Tables S3, S4 and S5, Additional file 1).

Finally, the model included a one-off cost of €5,252.51 [18, 20, 21] associated with BSC (end-of-life) for patients with BCLC-D recurrence.

5-year recurrence follow-up

In addition to the primary analysis with a 1-year time horizon, the model incorporated a 5-year follow-up scenario. During this period, patients who experienced a first recurrence in BCLC-A or BCLC-B stage and were treated with curative-intent treatments or LRTs, could either develop an additional recurrence (second recurrence) or remain disease-free (in remission). The remaining patients are not included in the 5-year follow-up scenario.

After the first recurrence there is a resource consumption associated with patient follow-up which, according to expert panel, includes a visit to the specialist, a complete blood test and imaging studies (computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging) every six months during the first 2 years and annually thereafter. In the case of an additional recurrence, the model applied the same cost estimated for the first recurrence in the base-case analysis.

Five-year survival rates (disease-free survival [DFS] and survival with recurrence) were obtained from the health technology assessment (HTA) report of the Unidad de Evaluación de Tecnologías Sanitarias [22]. This source provided the survival rates after ablation and surgical resection (in surgical and non-surgical tumors, which were assimilated to BCLC-A and BCLC-B recurrences in our model, respectively). For patients whose first HCC was treated with LT, these rates were obtained from the expert panel, as these were not available in the HTA report. Finally, these survival rates were weighted according to the treatment distributions for BCLC-A or BCLC-B recurrences shown in Fig. 2. These two survival variables enable us to identify which patients remain disease-free and which experience a second relapse during the five years of follow-up. A detailed summary of survival parameters is provided in Table S6 of the Additional file 1.

Sensitivity analysis

Deterministic sensitivity analyses were conducted to assess the uncertainty of parameters and to determine the robustness of the base case results.

A one-way sensitivity analysis (OWSA) was performed by varying key model parameters individually by ± 20% from their base case values. In contrast, a multi-way sensitivity analysis (MWSA) applied a simultaneous ± 20% variation across all relevant parameters.

Results

Base case

Total cost of 1,250 recurrences in Spain

The decision-tree model showed that, in a hypothetical cohort of 1,250 patients in Spain experiencing HCC recurrence, 617 would be diagnosed at BCLC-A stage, 421 at BCLC-B, 177 at BCLC-C and 35 at BCLC-D.

The total cost of managing these 1,250 recurrences amounted to €33,033,390 during the year of recurrence. This figure includes treatment costs, healthcare resource use costs and Tr-AEs costs. Among these, treatment costs represented the largest component, regardless of whether the curative approach for the first HCC was resection, ablation, or LT. Moreover, most of the economic burden was attributed to patients initially treated with resection or ablation, since the number of recurrences after LT were very low (only 107 cases) and thus, contributed less to the total cost (Table 3).

Average cost of recurrence

Overall, the average cost of HCC recurrence over a 1-year time horizon (the year of recurrence) was estimated at €26,426.

When categorized by type of treatment by first HCC, the average cost of HCC recurrence was €45,029 after LT, €27,241 after resection and €21,284 after ablation.

Finally, for the description of the mean costs according to the stage of recurrence, we obtain that the average cost of BCLC-A and of BCLC-B recurrences was €23,220 and €20,082 respectively, while the average cost of BCLC-C recurrence rose to €51,172. Finally, the average cost of BCLC-D recurrence was €5,253, reflecting the absence of active treatment at this terminal stage, where patients receive only BSC focused on symptom relief.

Cost after 5 years of follow-up

When the time horizon was extended to 5 years, the total cost increased to €43,297,202, representing an additional €10.26 million compared to the 1-year analysis. This increase was due to the 751 patients with BCLC-A or BCLC-B recurrence who received curative-intent treatment or LRT, after which routine surveillance is carried out (+ €2.87 million) and 228 additional recurrences (second recurrence) occurring within the follow-up period (+ €5.83 million). The remaining cost increase corresponds to recurrence diagnosis and characterization costs and end-of-life costs.

The average cost of recurrence over the 5-year period increased to €34,637, reflecting the added burden of long-term follow-up and second recurrences.

When the analysis was restricted to patients who experienced a second recurrence, the mean cost per patient rose to €59,542, which includes the cost of managing two recurrences and all associated follow-up.

Sensitivity analysis

The results of the MWSA showed that the cost of the 1,250 recurrences ranged from €30,302,458 to €33,412,350; likewise, the average cost of recurrence ranged from €24,241 to €26,729.

The results of the OWSA showed that the parameters with the greatest impact on the average cost of HCC recurrence were: the percentage of patients with BCLC-A recurrence (after first HCC resection, ablation or LT) eligible for curative treatments or LRTs, the unit costs of all healthcare resources, the percentage of patients with BCLC-B and BCLC-C recurrence who received a 1 L of systemic therapy, and the percentage of patients with BCLC-B recurrence after first HCC ablation or resection eligible for curative-intent treatments or LRTs. These results were represented by a tornado diagram (Fig. 3).

Discussion

At the national level, several studies have assessed the economic impact of different types of cancer, however, to date, no published study has specifically addressed the economic burden of HCC in Spain. This gap in the literature underscores the relevance of the present study, which provides the first estimates of the costs associated with HCC recurrence in the Spanish healthcare system.

According to our analysis, the overall cost of HCC recurrence in Spain (33 million euros) is primarily attributable to patients who underwent resection and ablation for their first HCC, as these patients are more likely to experience recurrence compared to those treated with LT. Conversely, although recurrences after LT are less frequent, their management is more expensive, resulting in a higher average cost per recurrence (€245,029) for this group. Despite successful management of the first recurrence, an estimated 50–70% of patients may experience a second recurrence [1, 23]. To reflect this, the time horizon of the analysis was extended to five years to capture the economic impact of potential second recurrences, even though this was not the primary objective of the analysis and should be interpreted with caution.

It should be noted that our work is a cost-of-illness analysis focused specifically on HCC recurrence after curative-intent treatment of early-stage tumor, and therefore does not include an assessment of the survival of these patients or the efficacy of treatments. In any case, to put our economic results into context, it is important to remember that the prognosis of HCC is closely correlated with tumor stage. Patients diagnosed at an early stage can achieve 5-year survival rates of up to 70% with LT, local ablation or surgical resection; whereas those diagnosed at an advanced stage are only eligible for BSC and have a median survival of 1–2 years [1, 4, 6, 24]. This highlights the need for new effective life-extending treatments for HCC patients and for future complete economic evaluations (including both cost and health outcomes) of such new interventions. These future economic evaluations will be able to draw on the evidence generated by our study of the economic burden of HCC. This will provide healthcare decision-makers with comprehensive information to support their decision-making processes.

In addition, another interesting topic that was beyond the scope of our study, but which would be interesting to explore in future work, is quantifying the impact of HCC screening and the potential savings that could result. The current cancer screening programs implemented in Spain focus on breast cancer, colorectal cancer, and cervical cancer [25]. To date, no study has evaluated the economic implications of HCC screening in Spain. Although our cost analysis focuses on the context of recurrence and does not include the screening or initial diagnosis phases, it may serve as a useful tool for future research assessing the overall economic burden of HCC.

There is limited literature on cost studies focusing on HCC recurrence. To date, the LIVERPOOL study, conducted in France, is the only European study that has specifically examined the costs associated with HCC recurrence. This study described the substantial economic burden of HCC recurrence after curative resection or ablation. During the period between the inclusion and the initiation of a new treatment or death, total costs were reported as €18,956 for ablated patients and €16,496 for resected patients. These costs increased significantly in the subsequent phase, from treatment initiation to death, rising to €35,911 and €38,971, respectively. The study highlighted the significant economic burden of HCC recurrence after curative resection or ablation and suggested that novel post-treatment management strategies may help mitigate this burden [26]. Despite these findings, differences in study populations, designs and methodologies hinder the comparison with our analysis.

Broadening the focus beyond recurrence, some studies have been identified in international literature that quantify the costs associated with HCC. A study in the United States estimated an average cost of $154,688 during 3 years of follow-up [27], while a previous study reported annual HCC costs of $454.9 million, with per-patient costs of $32,907 [28]. Another study in this country revealed that the cost of HCC patients treated with LRTs was $20,316 per patient per month [29]. A retrospective study revealed that the cost of HCC for the national health service in England over 5 years was £245 million [30]. In Canada, a study showed that the mean 5-year net cost of HCC care was $77,509 and the 5-year aggregate net cost of care was $106 million [31]. In the Greek healthcare setting, the average cost per patient was estimated at 12,119.1 euros for a 14.2-month follow-up period [32]. These findings highlight the economic burden of HCC on the healthcare system, underscoring the need to continue evaluating its impact and consider strategies to optimize treatment costs.

Although many studies analyze the economic burden of different types of cancers in Spain, there are not many studies specifically focused on the cost of recurrence. Our estimates in HCC are lower than those reported in other cost analysis for other types of cancers in Spain. A recent study assessing the cost of non-small cell lung cancer recurrence after resection, revealed an average cost of €25,194 for locoregional recurrence and €127,167 for metastatic recurrence [33]. Similarly, another study reported that the average cost of breast cancer recurrence was €36,367 for local recurrences and €124,657 for metastatic recurrences [34].

Although the sensitivity analyses performed showed that the results are quite robust, our study is not exempt from some limitations. First, the rigid structure of deterministic models makes it difficult to comprehensively represent common clinical practice in HCC, requiring generalizations to be made in complex clinical situations that take into account individual patient characteristics. These simplifying assumptions were necessary to ensure the tractability of the model and were validated by the clinical expert panel. Also, there is the lack of published data on healthcare resource utilization for HCC management in Spain. Consequently, our study relied on a questionnaire completed by 8 clinical experts who provided information on current treatment practices, which may introduce biases due to variability in clinical practices, potentially leading to an over- or underestimation of resource utilization and associated costs. In addition, the panel of experts can be considered relatively small compared with conventional Delphi studies, which may have limited the diversity of perspectives captured and, consequently, the full representativeness of the findings across the country. Future research with a prospective methodological design would be needed to accurately quantify the healthcare resource consumption in HCC patients directly from representative samples to enhance the robustness of findings.

Among the limitations related to costing, our study does not consider recurrent patients enrolled in clinical trials, whose treatments are typically funded by sponsoring pharmaceutical companies and therefore do not generate costs for the health system. However, this subgroup likely represents a small proportion of the overall population, so its inclusion would slightly reduce the estimated cost of recurrence. Regarding the duration of systemic therapies, median durations (6 months in the base case and PFS/DoT in the sensitivity analysis) were used to estimate drug costs in order to simplify the analysis. Additionally, the study focused exclusively on direct healthcare costs and did not include indirect costs such as productivity losses, which may lead to an underestimation of the total economic impact of the disease. Another limitation is that the model reflects current clinical practice and does not consider potential future changes to the therapeutic landscape. For example, the introduction of new systemic therapies such as durvalumab-tremelimumab, or updates to clinical guidelines, could significantly impact the cost of managing HCC recurrence. Furthermore, the 5-year recurrence cost estimates are based on modeled assumptions derived from published survival data, which are not specific to patients who have already experienced a first recurrence. This may affect the precision of the long-term cost projections.

Lastly, our findings are specific to the Spanish healthcare setting and may not be directly applicable to other countries with different healthcare systems, resource availability, or cost structures.

Conclusions

This study is the first to estimate the economic burden of HCC recurrence after curative-intent treatment in Spain. To date, only a limited number of studies in Europe have addressed this issue. Given the complexity involved in estimating the cost of HCC recurrence, due to the heterogeneity of clinical pathways, treatment strategies, and patient outcomes, further research is needed to better quantify the actual economic burden of this disease.
