Can we ensure a safe and effective integration of language models in oncology?
Authors: Chiara Corti, Leo A Celi
Published: 2024

The article published in this issue of The Lancet Regional Health–Europe by Verlingue et al.1 raises a pivotal question: can we ensure a safe and effective integration of artificial intelligence (AI), particularly large language models (LLMs), in modern oncology?

As the authors point out, over 80% of hospital medical content is in narrative format. This makes natural language processing (NLP) critical for extracting valuable insights from unstructured data like electronic health records.2 Examples include the NYUTron model, which reliably predicts in-hospital mortality and 30-day readmission risk,1,3 and the use of real-world data (RWD) to complement clinical trial evidence in oncology.4

Yet, when LLMs are used in clinically-related administrative tasks—such as summarizing clinical notes, drafting plain-language letters to patients, and editing insurance letters to remove medical jargon—they can produce inaccurate and potentially harmful outputs.5,6 This risk is even greater when using LLMs directly in clinical practice for tasks like prognosis estimation in oncology, clinical trial matching, and decision-support tools.7 These applications require a deeper understanding of ethical implications and current limitations, as hidden biases in LLMs can significantly affect patient outcomes.3,8

For instance, NYUTron's efficiency in estimating prognosis of patients with cancer was lower than in the general population, highlighting the need for specialized NLP models in oncology.1 Similarly, a recent study fine-tuned a transformer-based LLM to automate the curation of clinical notes for detecting immune-related adverse events (irAEs) and compared the irAE detection rates with those identified using predefined billing codes, finding discrepancies between the study's AE rates and existing literature.4

This last example highlights a key issue: the use of labels not verified by experts for model development.4 More broadly, the training data used by LLMs can perpetuate biases related to race, gender, language, and culture.8 While ensuring diverse patient representation in training datasets is crucial, it is not sufficient. The social patterning of data generation can still result in AI that maintains or even amplifies existing disparities in care.8 Adding to the complexity, in oncology more than in other medical subspecialties, the ground truth (medical evidence) on which models are trained is constantly evolving and difficult to keep up with. The medical knowledge system itself, which provides the ground truth, is not immune to bias. The knowledge base often comes from well-funded institutions in affluent, English-speaking countries, leading to significant underrepresentation of perspectives from other parts of the world. This results in health and disease models biased towards the understanding prevalent in high-income countries. Finally, current models fail to assess the quality of their outputs or provide a measure of uncertainty.8

Patients may also be concerned about healthcare providers using LLMs when the collection, use, and potential dissemination of sensitive data are poorly regulated, exposing this information to companies and hackers.8 Given the lack of transparency in how companies handle and store data, it is crucial for institutions to ensure the protection of sensitive health information submitted to LLM interfaces and to maintain transparency in the data handling process.8 In this context, GPT4DFCI stands out as a hospital-initiated initiative promoting ethical and fair LLM use. It is HIPAA compliant and currently used for administrative and research purposes, not for clinical applications.9

Other important aspects to consider are authorship and payment methods for LLM interfaces.8 If in clinical practice physicians' signatures are important due to the legal responsibilities of medical documentation, in cancer research, authorship is crucial because of the high level of trust placed in authors, who are responsible for the quality and integrity of the research.8,10 If LLMs are used, it raises the question of where to draw the line between an assistive tool and the actual author.10 On the positive side, LLMs can enhance productivity and promote equal opportunities in academia for individuals with limited English proficiency.8 However, training a model may involve tiered payment systems based on performance and usage volume, while other LLMs may require upfront subscription fees for access, use, or training. This raises concerns that the cost of access could widen existing digital divides.8

In conclusion, integrating LLMs into oncology practice and research raises significant ethical concerns regarding bias, trust, authorship, equity, and privacy. Although this technology has the potential to revolutionize healthcare, it is crucial to anticipate its downstream effects on health disparities. Rather than banning its use, it is essential to establish guidelines for the responsible and effective implementation of LLMs, and continuous monitoring of their impact on patient outcomes.
