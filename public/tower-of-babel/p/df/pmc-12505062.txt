Long-term maintenance of mycophenolate mofetil in anti-NMDA receptor encephalitis (LEARN): a multicentre, open-label, blinded-endpoint, randomised controlled trial
Authors: Xue Gong, Yue Liu, Yaru Ma, Bo Yan, Dongmei An, Yonghua Guo, Xu Liu, Xingjie Li, Linjun Cai, Xiaolin Deng, Dong Zhou, Jin-Mei Li, Zhen Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor encephalitis (NMDARE) is a severe autoimmune disorder with high morbidity and mortality. Current treatments have limitations including relapse, highlighting the need for effective maintenance therapy. This study evaluates the efficacy and safety of mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) as long-term adjunctive therapy to first-line treatment in newly diagnosed patients with NMDARE.MethodsWe conducted a prospective, randomised, open-label trial in four academic centres in China. Patients aged 14 and older with acute NMDARE, who received first-line treatments within 2 weeks of presentation to the hospital and had a modified Rankin scale (mRS) score of 2 or more, were recruited. Participants were randomly assigned to receive first-line treatment with or without MMF (0.5 g two times per day for 24 months). Primary outcomes included relapse rates and time to relapse, with secondary outcomes including cognitive deficits, treatment response (the proportion of patients with≥1 point improvement in mRS within 4 weeks) and adverse events (AEs).ResultsOf 100 patients (52% female; median age 27), those in the MMF group had fewer relapses (5.9% vs 26.5%; p=0.006) and better treatment response (84.3% vs 65.3%; p=0.03). No significant difference was found in long-term functional prognosis at 12 and 24 months. However, MMF patients had less fatigue, cognitive impairment, depression and seizures. AEs were mild-to-moderate, with no deaths or anaphylactic reactions.ConclusionsThis study provides Class II evidence that long-term adjunctive treatment of MMF to first-line treatment of NMDARE resulted in a lower risk of relapse and was well tolerated beyond the 24 months of treatment.Trial registration numberChiCTR2100044362.Keywords: AUTOIMMUNE ENCEPHALITIS

Introduction

Anti-N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor encephalitis (NMDARE) is the most prevalent and severe type of autoimmune encephalitis,1 with substantial morbidity and mortality rates, characterised by relapse rates of 25∼30%, often necessitating intensive care unit (ICU) admission in more than 50% of cases.2 It affects 1.5 per million individuals yearly and imposes a heavy economic and social burden on people and the healthcare system.3 4 The disorder most commonly manifests by psychosis, cognitive impairments, epileptic seizures, movement disorders, autonomic dysfunction, coma or even death.5

In NMDARE, the highly specific pathogenic autoantibodies (Abs) bind to neuronal surface-expressed NMDARs and induce the reduction of synaptic NMDAR currents and impaired long-term potentiation.6 7 This process involves the dysregulation of autoreactive T and B lymphocytes.8 Therefore, current first-line treatment for patients with NMDARE includes intravenous immunoglobulin (IVIg), high-dose glucocorticoids or plasma exchange (PLEX), but these treatments have several disadvantages.9 The most important problem with first-line immune treatment is the heterogeneity of responses, with approximately 20–35% or even 50% of patients having a relapse at some point after immunotherapies have been reduced or stopped.10,13 Given that relapses pose a risk of permanent and severe neurological disability, it is essential that a patient’s maintenance therapy remains highly effective, safe and tolerable for long-term use.

Although mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) is widely used in autoimmune diseases due to its immunosuppressive properties and selective inhibition of lymphocytes,14 its specific action in neurological immune disorders remains a subject of interest. Despite its potential efficacy in refractory cases, including those with neuronal surface antigen-mediated encephalitis,14,16 there is a lack of definitive randomised controlled trials assessing its role in the treatment of such conditions. Therefore, this study aims to investigate the efficacy and safety of MMF maintenance therapy in new-onset patients with NMDARE to address this gap in clinical evidence. The hypothesis of our study posits that adding long-term MMF to first-line treatment is a more effective treatment strategy than using first-line treatment alone in patients with NMDARE.

Methods

Trial design and participants

This trial was a randomised, multicentre, prospective, open-labelled, blinded endpoint study (LEARN, ChiCTR2100044362). Between 15 March 2021 and 30 June 2022, patients were identified and enrolled at four academic hospital centres in China. The trial protocol with the statistical analysis plan is in the Protocol (pp 5–46). The study was done in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice. An independent ethics committee at each site approved the study protocol, if required, and any subsequent amendments. The study sponsor, West China Hospital, provided the trial drug, monitored the trial and analysed the data with oversight from the study. The LEARN is terminated when all participants have reached at least 24 months of follow-up.

Written informed consent was obtained from all patients or their legal representatives before the study began. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) new acute onset of one or more of the following eight major groups of manifestations within less than 3 months: psychosis, memory deficit, speech disturbance, seizures, movement disorder, disturbance of consciousness, autonomic dysfunction and central hypoventilation; (2) positive cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) tests for NMDAR Abs (cell-based assay); (3) patients older than 14 years of age; (4) patients had received one or more first-line treatment options for their initial therapeutic approach within 2 weeks of hospitalisation before randomisation (baseline visit), adhering to international consensus recommendations; (5) patients were also required to have a modified Rankin scale (mRS) score of 2 or more for at least 24 hours before assessment.

First-line treatment dosing was as follows: (1) intravenous methylprednisolone (IVMP) treatment was initiated at 1000 mg daily for 5 days. Subsequently, IVMP was transitioned to daily prednisone, commencing at 1 mg/kg body weight during the first 2 weeks and gradually tapering to 0 over a period of at least 3–6 months, as determined by the treating neurologist; (2) IVIg: 0.4 g/kg daily for 5 days; (3) PLEX: 5–7 PLEX over 7–10 days should be equivalent to 1–2 PLEX.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) pregnancy and breastfeeding; (2) patients of reproductive potential did not agree to use a highly effective method of birth control from screening to 24 months after final dose of the experimental drugs; (3) current evidence or known history of clinically significant infection (bacterial, fungal, parasitic, mycobacterium tuberculosis infection, herpes simplex virus, varicella-zoster virus, cytomegalovirus, Epstein-Barr virus, HIV, hepatitis viruses, syphilis, etc); (4) encephalopathy secondary to sepsis or systemic inflammatory response syndrome; (5) pre-existing diagnoses of epilepsy, cerebral trauma and/or other nervous system diseases prior to the onset of encephalitis; (6) positive serum and/or CSF laboratory tests for other neuronal surface or intracellular antigens; (7) presence of any untreated teratoma before randomisation; (8) previous history or current presence of cancer or malignancy; (9) underwent any surgical procedure within 4 weeks prior to baseline but excluding teratoma resection; (10) insufficient heart, liver, kidney or bone marrow function (white blood cell<3.0 ×10∧9/L, absolute neutrophil count<2.0 ×10∧9 /L, absolute lymphocyte count<0.5 ×10∧9 /L, platelet count<100 ×10∧9 /L, aspartate transaminase or alanine transaminase>1.5 × upper limit of normal); (11) previous treatment with any anti-CD20 (eg, rituximab, ofatumumab, obinutuzumab), anti-CD19 (eg, inebilizumab), anti-IL6R (eg, certolizumab, tocilizumab), T-cell depletion therapy, complement inhibitors, Fc receptor antagonists, anti-B-lymphocyte stimulator monoclonal Abs (eg, belimumab), cyclophosphamide, azathioprine or bortezomib; (12) lack of key clinical data; (13) contraindications to MMF; (14) inability to give informed consent; (15) participation in another interventional trial within the last 3 months. Detailed inclusion and exclusion criteria are available in Protocol (pp 13–15).

Randomisation and masking

Eligible participants were randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to receive either first-line treatment plus long-term adjunctive MMF (MMF+) or first-line treatment regimen only (MMF−). Randomisation was done centrally by an independent statistician at West China Hospital using a computer-generated randomisation list. This process was overseen by the chair of the Statistician Panel, as detailed in online supplemental 1. To ensure an even distribution of patients across time, randomisation was performed in block sizes of 4 to maintain concealment. Participants, caregivers and investigators were aware of the treatment group assignment. Outcome assessors and site staff were masked to treatment assignment. The personnel who conducted the efficacy analyses were different from those who recorded the safety results and all were masked to the treatment assignments. The success of masking was not assessed.

Trial procedures

At each study site, the investigator was responsible for assessing patient eligibility, supervising the administration of study medication, recording and managing adverse events (AEs) and assessing relapses. During the screening visit (V1), all candidates underwent CSF Ab testing, MRI scanning and blood analyses to check liver and renal function, electrolytes and blood glucose. Participants were further evaluated by an enrolment authorisation committee for suitability and eligibility to participate. After approval by the enrolment authorisation committee, participants were randomly allocated to either MMF maintenance or non-MMF maintenance. Patients assigned to receive MMF plus group began treatment with 500 mg two times per day. After 24 months of MMF therapy, the dose for all patients who had a complete response to MMF (mRS score≤2) was reduced by 250 mg each month and gradually withdrawn the MMF.

The scheduled study visits included a treatment period (24 months) and an extended follow-up period. During the treatment period, patients were assessed at intervals of 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18 and 24 months by the investigators and raters. The clinical follow-up at each visit included a full neurological examination, blood pressure and heart rate measurements, routine blood tests and blood chemistry. ECG, MRI, CSF/blood sampling for laboratory tests, including NMDAR-IgG analysis, were performed at 6 and 12 months. For neurological deficit assessment, the following key questionnaires were measured: (1) mRS, (2) clinical evaluation scale for autoimmune encephalitis (CASE), (3) Modified Fatigue Impact Scale (MFIS), (4) Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MOCA), (5) Beck Depression Inventory-II (BD-II) score. A seizure diary was provided for the subject or caregiver to complete which included data on seizure attacks, frequency, type, duration and possible triggers. For non-responsive patients who are unable to cooperate in completing the self-assessment questionnaires (eg, significantly impaired consciousness, inability to express oneself), self-assessment scales (BD-II score, MFIS) were not performed. During the extended follow-up period (every 6 months), we recorded primary outcome events (relapses), adverse effects and concomitant medications.

If patients reported clinical worsening or possible relapse, an extra visit was scheduled within 72 hours. The investigators identified relapses which were subsequently confirmed by the central review committee. Relapse was defined as new onset or worsening of symptoms occurring after initial improvement or stabilisation of at least 2 months with elevated CSF/serum Ab titres at relapse.2 Thorough clinical and laboratory examinations were conducted to rule out other aetiologies and to validate the diagnosis of relapse. The patients were assessed using a relapse questionnaire designed for this study. The relapse questionnaire included the frequency of relapses, number of events, time to relapse, clinical symptoms, NMDAR-IgG positivity and titre in CSF and serum and treatment. Relapses or clinical worsening could be treated with rescue therapy at the investigators’ discretion. Rescue therapy was defined as due to lack of improvement/worsening of the disease, clinicians increased or failed to taper the oral corticosteroids dose according to the tapering schedule or changed to another immunosuppressant (such as rituximab, cyclophosphamide, azathioprine) in patients with NMDARE. Any increase in the dose of symptomatic medications (including antiepileptic drugs, antipsychotics and analgesics) during the study period will not be considered as rescue therapy. Follow-up visits were conducted in person to check for drug tolerability and the occurrence of AEs (Protocol: pp. 15–18).

Outcomes measures

The primary endpoint was the number of patients with relapse and the difference in the time to relapse, assessed in the intention-to-treat analysis. Secondary endpoints were as follows: (1) response rates, defined as the proportion of patients achieving a≥1 point improvement in the mRS score within 4 weeks after randomisation; (2) the proportion of patients achieving a≥2 point improvement in the mRS score within 4 weeks after randomisation; (3) the median time to achieve a 1-point improvement in the mRS score after randomisation; (4) the proportion of patients who reached a favourable outcome during 24 months of follow-up. A favourable functional outcome was defined as an mRS score of≤2 and a poor functional outcome as an mRS score of>2 at the last follow-up; (5) the duration of prednisone tapering; (6) the extent of NMDAR1-IgG reduction; (7) the proportion of patients need for rescue therapies; (8) the longitudinal profile of disease outcomes (change in scores of mRS, CASE, MFIS, MOCA and BD-II scale score at 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18 and 24 months); (9) the proportion of patients achieving seizure freedom during the follow-up period.

Safety was assessed based on AEs reported by study participants or investigators. Safety outcomes comprised assessment of serious AEs—defined as hospitalisation for any reason—and AEs. The severity of AEs was evaluated using the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (V.5.0). Serious AEs were adjudicated by the Event Committee. We also assessed the causal relation between AEs and the use of MMF or the study procedures. Relapses were considered severe AEs if they led to hospital admission for reasons beyond routine NMDARE treatment, such as requiring an extended treatment course or prolonged hospital stay.

Statistical analysis

The estimated proportion of relapse patients at 24 months with first-line treatments only was found to be 25–35%, according to a literature review. However, we lack prior knowledge about the relapse rate with MMF. A retrospective study reported that the relapse rate for corticosteroids plus MMF in patients with neuronal surface Ab-mediated encephalitis was approximately 9.1% at 24 months.15 Assuming a 1:1 randomisation, we calculated that 118 patients with 20 relapse events would provide 80% power to determine the prespecified between-group difference on the basis of a two-sided log-rank test with a probability of type I error of 5%, assuming a 10% dropout rate. Enrolment was planned to stop when 118 participants had been randomly allocated and at least 20 relapse events had occurred. The sample size was calculated using PASS software.

Categorical variables were summarised using frequency and percentage, while continuous variables were presented as mean (SD), median values and IQRs or n (%). Baseline demographic characteristics, disease symptoms, treatment modalities and examination factors were compared using Fisher’s exact test for categorical outcomes and Student’s t-test for continuous variables, as appropriate. The primary analyses of efficacy and safety outcomes were conducted using an intention-to-treat approach which included all participants who were randomly assigned to treatment groups. The primary outcome analysis used Kaplan-Meier plots, with statistical significance assessed using the log-rank test. Prespecified sensitivity analyses for the primary efficacy outcome included variables such as age (<30 vs ≥30 years), sex, clinical severity (mRS=5, CASE score≥20), acute manifestations (ICU admission), auxiliary examinations and baseline treatments. All secondary outcomes were evaluated under the intention-to-treat protocol. The relative treatment effect size is expressed as a risk ratio (RR) with 95% CIs. Repeatedly measured continuous variables were analysed using a mixed-effects model for repeated measures. Changes in scores relative to baseline are reported as mean (SE). All statistical analyses were conducted using R software, V.4.3.1 (R Group for Statistical Computing). Detailed statistical procedures can be found in Protocol (pp. 21–22).

Results

Eligible participants were screened between March 2021 and June 2022. Of the 255 participants who were screened, 155 were ineligible. eTable 1 online supplemental 2 shows the detailed reasons for screening failure. A total of 100 participants were randomised into treatment groups, with 51 assigned to the MMF+ group and 49 to the MMF− group. One participant in the MMF+ group withdrew due to death (motor vehicle accident) 16 months after randomisation. The follow-up time used for analysis of relapse was truncated to the last follow-up time (at least 24 months) and further truncated by the date of relapse, if applicable. This resulted in a median follow-up time of 30 months in the MMF+ group and 36 months in the MMF− group. Three participants were partially lost to follow-up. Missing data accounted for no more than 5% of outcomes over the 24 months for the 100 participants, as illustrated in figure 1.

In this study, the investigators recorded a total of 18 relapse events in 16 patients from randomiSation to the end of follow-up, all of which were further confirmed by central review in 100% of the patients. Of the relapse patients: 3 were in the MMF+ group (5.9%) and 13 were in the MMF− group (26.5%, 2 patients with subsequent relapse events). Time-to-event curves plotted on a Kaplan-Meier graph are shown in figure 2. The HR, estimated with the use of a Cox proportional hazards model, is 4.2 (95% CI, 1.5 to 11.6; p=0.006 (p value derived from the Cox model)). The lower relapse rate in the main analysis was supported by all sensitivity analysis. We further found that patients with onset age less than 30 years, female sex, CASE score greater than 20, timely first-line treatment or a higher CSF/serum NMDAR-IgG titre seemed more responsive to MMF (eFigure 1 in the online supplemental 2).

Patients assigned to the MMF+ group were also more likely to have better treatment response, with 84.3% of patients achieving a≥1 point mRS score improvement within 4 weeks after randomisation (relative RR, 1.8; 95% CI, 1.1 to 3.4; p=0.03) compared with 65.3% in the MMF− group. In the MMF+ group, 5.9% of patients received rescue immunotherapy after randomisation, as compared with 26.5% in the MMF− group (RR: 0.3; 95% CI, 0.1 to 0.7; p=0.006). Only 27.4% of patients assigned to the MMF+ group had ≥2 points mRS score improvement within 4 weeks after randomisation, as compared with 22.5% in the MMF− group (p=0.8). Data concerning longitudinal motor disability, fatigue deficit, cognitive impairment, depressive symptoms and seizure outcomes over a 24-month follow-up period in patients with NMDARE are presented in figures 4. Patients in the MMF+ group did not demonstrate a significantly better functional prognosis (the proportion of patients with mRS scores≤2) at 12 months (92.1% vs 79.6%; p=0.08, figure 3) and 24 months (98.0% vs 89.7%; p=0.11, figure 3). Nonetheless, those receiving long-term add-on MMF treatment exhibited lower scores on the CASE (figure 4A), MFIS (figure 4C) and BD-II (figure 4D) scales compared with the MMF− group. Moreover, improved cognitive outcomes were observed using the MOCA assessment (figure 4B), along with a higher rate of seizure freedom (76.5% vs 53.1%; p=0.02) at 6 months postrandomisation in patients undergoing MMF maintenance treatment. Subgroup analysis stratified by patients with a non-relapsing course showed no treatment benefit from MMF maintenance. (eFigure 3 in the online supplemental 2).

The incidences of treatment AEs, serious AEs, infections and serious infections in the MMF+ treatment periods were consistent with those in the MMF− groups during the study periods. Rates of serious infections were low and the rates of infections, serious infections, neutropenia and tumour did not increase over time. Most AEs in the MMF+ group were mild or moderate in severity and there were no reported deaths or anaphylactic reactions related to MMF (table 2 and eTable 2 in the online supplemental 2).

Discussion

To our knowledge, the LEARN study is the first randomised trial to compare MMF maintenance for the treatment of NMDARE. We found that long-term add-on therapy with MMF was safe, well-tolerated and showed a signal of efficacy for preventing relapse and neurological deficits compared with patients receiving first-line treatments only.

In our study, nearly 25.6% of the patients not receiving MMF suffered relapse which is consistent with previous reports (15–30%).2 11 17 We found that long-term MMF therapy reduced the risk of relapse by 94.2% with a median follow-up time of 30 months compared with first-line treatment only. Unlike the relatively standard therapeutic regimen for patients with multiple sclerosis (MS)18 19 and neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD),20,22 few studies have evaluated MMF, rituximab and cyclophosphamide as long-term treatments for patients with NMDARE.11 15 16 These studies have reported varied or controversial outcomes, thus providing little evidence of therapy efficacy. For example, in an analysis of long-term outcomes for treatment in patients with relapse NMDARE from a Chinese registry cohort, no significant difference was reported in the occurrence of subsequent relapse between MMF-treated patients (4/18) and other patients (8/20).11 However, our study confirms the efficacy of MMF reported by previous retrospective studies involving small patients (n=6) during the second or third disease episode treated with MMF.16 Furthermore, a systematic review of patients with paediatric-onset NMDARE treated with MMF, based on 41 articles reporting a total of 45 patients, suggests MMF may have a role in managing refractory disease and preventing relapse.23

Previous studies on reducing relapse risk in NMDARE were all limited to non-randomised studies and the benefit of sequential immunotherapy remains controversial. An analysis of long-term outcomes from rituximab treatment, involving 358 patients with NMDARE from a German registry cohort, reported relapses in 19% of patients, with 54% receiving induction therapy (defined as treatment duration less than 6 months) and 46% receiving maintenance therapy with rituximab.24 Their study did not find a significant reduction in relapses associated with rituximab use. However, a recent study from UC San Diego and Rady Children’s Hospital demonstrated the total relapse rate in an NMDARE cohort was 23% in paediatric and 30% in adult patients. They observed a substantial benefit of continued rituximab infusions for reducing relapse rates in autoimmune encephalitis, especially for the adult population.17 A meta-analysis using multivariable logistic regression models in a cohort of 410 patients highlighted that rituximab (OR 0.17, 95% CI 0.05 to 0.42) and IVIG use for 6 months or longer (OR 0.16, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.33) were associated with a lower likelihood of disease relapse.12 Inconsistent findings across these studies may be attributed to: (1) different rituximab therapy patterns (acute induction therapy or continued infusions) may significantly impact clinical outcomes; (2) the non-randomised nature of these studies and variations in inclusion/exclusion criteria, leading to potential selection bias. As the optimal long-term therapy remains inconclusive, our study suggests MMF maintenance treatment as a new approach to effectively prevent relapse in adult patients with NMDARE. Furthermore, MMF is less expensive than many immunosuppressive and biologic drugs in China, potentially offering an economic benefit.

In previous studies, predictors of poor functional outcome and relapse included young age, delayed treatment, ICU admission, disturbance of consciousness, cognitive deficit and high CSF and serum titres.2025,29 Thus, in this study, a sensitivity analysis was performed involving the entire sample and controlling for age, sex, symptoms, treatment and baseline score. The lower relapse rate in the main analysis was supported by all sensitivity analyses. Specifically, we further found that patients with onset age less than 30 years, female sex, CASE score greater than 20, timely first-line treatment or a higher NMDAR-IgG titre seemed more responsive to MMF. These data might emphasise the importance of tailoring treatment for different populations. However, the relatively small sample size in this study may have limited our ability to detect associations between these potential confounding factors and relapse.

We also provided evidence that the MMF+ group attained a better treatment response (84.3% of MMF+ patients achieving≥1 point improvement in mRS score vs 65.3% in the MMF− group within 4 weeks after randomisation). MMF is generally considered a relatively slow-acting drug14 and detecting clinical effects as early as 4 weeks postrandomisation is unexpected. We hypothesise that the improvement in the mRS score within 4 weeks may not be solely attributed to immunological changes. Early symptom improvements, such as reduced pain or improved functional ability, might have contributed to the observed early changes in the mRS score. However, it is important to note that no significant differences were observed in the CASE score at 4 weeks nor the proportion of patients showing a≥2-point improvement in mRS score between the two groups within this time frame. These findings suggest that MMF may not lead to significant improvements in cognitive, behavioural, psychosocial aspects or overall quality of life in the short term.

Furthermore, MMF typically exhibits a delayed onset of action, often requiring weeks to months to achieve significant therapeutic effects.14 In contrast, cyclophosphamide can start showing effects within days to weeks due to its potent immunomodulatory action,30 while rituximab typically induces B-cell depletion within 2–4 weeks after a single intravenous infusion.31 Thus, for patients with NMDARE with highly active disease or at the acute phase of relapse, rituximab and cyclophosphamide might be more beneficial to rapidly control disease activity. In contrast, MMF’s advantages lie in its ability to maintain long-term disease stability and prevent relapse, making it more suited for maintenance therapy rather than acute intervention.

In our study, 98.0% of MMF+ and 89.7% of MMF− patients had attained favourable neurological functional outcomes (mRS of 0–2) at 24 months follow-up. Compared with Western countries, where general favourable clinical outcomes (mRS score≤2) at 70–96 months range from 71% to 78.6%.2 28 We speculate two reasons account for this discrepancy: (1) we excluded patients with severe infections, the presence of tumour and overlapping neuronal surface or intracellular autoantibodies, which were previously reported to be related to disease severity and clinical outcomes;32 (2) in our prospective cohort, all patients with relapse received timely and aggressive rescue immunotherapies; (3) over 80% of patients at new disease onset in our study received timely treatment (timely treatment was defined as the initiation of immunotherapy within 30 days of onset) as compared with 40% delayed treatment in previous studies.2 24 28 33 As reported from previous and our studies, delayed immunotherapies at onset are a significant risk factor for long-term relapse and worse functional outcomes.2 12 33

Previous studies have shown that NMDARE is associated with fatigue and persistent cognitive and neuropsychological symptoms28 34 35 which from the patient’s perspective can be of greater concern than simply mRS improvement. This trial incorporated the use of validated questionnaires to assess depressive, cognitive, fatigue and seizure symptoms. Nearly all patients (96 of 100 (96%)) completed the questionnaires at follow-up points. Over 24 months of follow-up, patients who received MMF reported lower levels of fatigue and depressive symptoms. We found no evidence of differences between the groups in cognitive disorder after 6 months by using MOCA cognitive assessment, possible reasons included the sensitivity and specificity of MOCA assessment being unstable under different circumstances. To better assess and detect prolonged cognitive deficits, using a more detailed evaluation such as Rey Auditory Verbal Learning Test or Wechsler Memory Scale is necessary for further study.

When we evaluated the treatment side effects in patients during follow-up, those related to glucocorticoids were the most common and included metabolic disorders, difficulty sleeping and weight gain. In contrast, the side effects expected with MMF were similar in the two groups, with both groups having the same number of gastrointestinal issues and infections. Neutropenia and tumour developed in no patients in the MMF add-on group. These results are similar to those of a previous trial of first-line treatment for other autoimmune diseases.36 37

Overall, our study indicated that early add-on therapy with MMF may be particularly valuable for patients with NMDARE in terms of relapse avoidance and neurological deficit improvement, either from the patient’s perspective or on clinical grounds. However, this study has several limitations. First, the study was an open-label trial, which can lead to observer bias and placebo effects, requiring cautious result interpretation and confirmation through blinded trials. However, the endpoints of relapse in our study were based on laboratory Ab measurements and adjudicated by outcome assessors who were blind to the treatment assignment. Furthermore, the rate of loss to follow-up was low and treatment compliance was high and well-balanced between treatment groups; this suggests that the risk of investigator and participant bias would be small. Second, specific exclusion criteria set for safety reasons, such as excluding the youngest paediatric (under 14 years of age) and those with severe infections, might have further limited the representation of the real-world NMDARE population. This could potentially introduce unconscious reporting bias, thereby limiting the ability to draw causal inferences about long-term treatment effects and safety. Third, the study was conducted among patients in China and so caution should be used when generalising findings to other populations. Fourth, while the study may be considered underpowered based on the initial sample size calculation, which required 118 patients with 20 relapse events, several factors contributed to the enrolment shortfall. First, the study demonstrated a significant effect in preventing relapse following an interim analysis which led to the decision to cease further patient enrolment. Additionally, the relatively low prevalence of NMDARE and the relatively low relapse rate in the recruitment regions, as well as the strict eligibility criteria designed to ensure the reliability and integrity of the results, further constrained patient recruitment. Despite not reaching the planned sample size, the study’s key findings remain both statistically significant and clinically relevant. Finally, despite our research showing that MMF maintenance therapy may reduce the relapse rate of NMDARE, the overall relapse rate of NMDARE is significantly lower than that of MS and NMOSD. Thus, long-term immunosequential therapy may provide limited benefits for non-relapsing patients, as shown in our subgroup analysis. This underscores the need for further research into the necessity and cost-effectiveness of prolonged therapy. Individualised treatment plans are essential, with MMF being more beneficial for high-risk patients. Future studies should focus on identifying those who are most likely to benefit from long-term treatment, optimising outcomes and minimising unnecessary treatment costs.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that long-term add-on therapy with MMF was safe, well-tolerated and showed efficacy in preventing relapse and reducing neurological deficits compared with first-line treatments alone in individuals with new-onset NMDARE. This supports the use of MMF as a safe and effective immunosequential therapy for patients with NMDARE at high risk of relapse.
