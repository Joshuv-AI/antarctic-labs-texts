Efficacy of ultrasound localization and fiberoptic bronchoscopy-guided percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy in the intensive care unit
Authors: Xiao-Lu Zhang, An-Qi Liu, Ya-Lei Chen, Hu-Nan Liu, Hong-Yi Kou, Pei-Hong Zhao, Zhong-Dong Li, Nian-Fang Lu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the clinical efficacy and safety of ultrasound localization with fiberoptic bronchoscopy-guided percutaneous tracheostomy (US-B PDT) in the intensive care unit (ICU) setting.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted on 122 patients who underwent tracheostomy between August 2016 and December 2024 at the Department of Critical Care Medicine, Beijing Electric Power Hospital. Based on the surgical technique employed, patients were divided into two groups: the ultrasound localization and fiberoptic bronchoscopy-guided percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy group (US-B PDT group, n = 62) and the conventional percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy group (PDT group, n = 60). Surgical parameters and intraoperative and postoperative complications were compared between the two groups.ResultsPatients in the US-B PDT group demonstrated statistically significant improvements in several parameters compared to the PDT group, including shorter surgical duration (8.30 ± 0.51 vs. 10.42 ± 1.29 min, respectively), reduced intraoperative blood loss (9.91 ± 0.97 vs. 13.92 ± 0.82 ml, respectively), lower tracheal tube cuff leakage rate (0% vs. 13.3%, respectively), higher first-attempt intubation success rate (96.8% vs. 90.2%, respectively), and decreased sputum overflow rate at the tracheostomy site (3.2% vs. 18.3%, respectively), with p-values < 0.05 for all comparisons. Additionally, the incidence of complications in patients of the US-B PDT group (9.7%) was significantly lower than that in the PDT group (45%) (P < 0.05). Notably, no cases of damage to the posterior tracheal wall or tracheoesophageal fistula were observed in either group.ConclusionThe use of US-B PDT significantly shortened surgical duration, reduced intraoperative blood loss, lowering tracheal tube cuff leakage, enhancing first-attempt intubation success rate, and preventing complications such as posterior tracheal wall injury and tracheoesophageal fistula, ensuring superior safety. These findings highlight the safety and efficacy of this surgical approach for widespread use in ICUs equipped with the necessary facilities.

Background

Surgical tracheotomy (ST) is one of the most common invasive procedures in intensive care units (ICUs), aiming to provide effective airway management for patients with respiratory failure who require long-term mechanical ventilation support [1]. Tracheotomy can reduce the incidence of ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP), shorten the duration of mechanical ventilation, decrease ICU hospitalization time, and lower the risk of death. The main reasons for this are that tracheotomy can reduce the need for sedatives, improve patient comfort, facilitate oral hygiene care, help enhance communication skills, and enable operations such as swallowing, drinking, and eating. In addition, it can provide another airway option for patients with upper airway obstruction. In summary, tracheotomy, by reducing respiratory muscle work and minimizing the occurrence of airway dead space, is beneficial for mechanical ventilation and suctioning, and is an important means for establishing a reliable artificial airway [2]. Percutaneous dilatational tracheotomy (PDT) was first introduced by Ciaglia et al. [3] in the 1980s. It has since become the preferred method for tracheotomy in ICUs due to its minimally invasive nature, bedside feasibility, shorter procedure duration, and relatively fewer complications [4]. It has been reported that approximately 10% of cases in comprehensive ICUs involve tracheotomy. [5, 6]

With the rapid advancement of minimally invasive techniques, bedside PDT—performed by trained senior ICU physicians—has gradually replaced traditional ST [7]. However, unguided PDT carries inherent risks, including inaccurate puncture depth leading to posterior tracheal wall injury, failed first-attempt punctures due to improper positioning, and repeated punctures causing tissue trauma around the trachea. Additionally, the absence of guidance during the procedure can increase the likelihood of vascular injury, which in severe cases may result in massive hemorrhage and suffocation. The fiberoptic bronchoscopy technique enables real-time observation by using a bronchoscope with a light source. The flexible design allows for access to various branches of the airway. Combined with saline irrigation and specimen collection functions, it can directly monitor inflammatory, tumor, foreign body and other pathological conditions as well as perform interventional treatments. With the development of endoscopic technology, the use of fiberoptic bronchoscopy for guiding PDT is becoming more common. By visualizing the trachea through the bronchoscope, the entire process of tracheotomy can be easily monitored, making the entire surgical procedure safer and reducing the risk of complications [8].

Meanwhile, with the popularization of bedside ultrasound technology in the ICU, preoperative ultrasound localization not only helps us to clearly identify the specific location of the thyroid and the distribution of surrounding blood vessels, but also enables us to accurately determine the positions of the thyroid cartilage, cricoid cartilage, and tracheal cartilage rings, as well as any deformities or anatomical abnormalities, and precisely locate the puncture point, thereby reducing the risk of complications. Several studies have indicated that fiberoptic bronchoscopy-guided percutaneous tracheostomy can provide clear visual guidance and enable real-time monitoring of the internal condition of the airway, minimizing damage to the surrounding tissues and reducing the risk of posterior wall injuries from over-penetration [9–11]. Additionally, the integration of bedside ultrasound allows for precise identification of the puncture point, thereby enhancing the first-attempt puncture success rate. By offering physicians an intuitive and accurate procedural interface, this combined approach improves the safety, control, and efficiency of complex tracheostomy procedures.

Clinical comparative studies examining conventional PDT and ultrasound localization combined with fiberoptic bronchoscopy-guided percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (US-B PDT) are exceedingly rare. In this study, the aim was to investigate the clinical outcomes and surgical complications associated with these two ST approaches to offer clinical evidence and practical insights for effectively preventing and reducing procedural complications.

Materials and methods

Study participants

A total of 122 patients who underwent tracheostomy between August 2016 and December 2024 in the Department of Critical Care Medicine at Beijing Electric Power Hospital, were included in this retrospective study. Patients were divided into two groups based on the surgical technique: the US-B PDT group comprising those who underwent US-B PDT, and the PDT group consisting of those who underwent conventional PDT.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Indication for ST due to inability to achieve ventilator weaning in the short term; and (2) signed informed consent forms obtained from the patient or their families prior to surgery.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) Patients with severe bleeding tendencies, coagulation dysfunction, or the presence of uncontrollable hemorrhagic diseases; (2) patients with a history of neck surgery or anatomical abnormalities in the neck, such as goiter; (3) aged < 18 years; (4) the presence of local soft tissue infection or malignant infiltration in the tracheotomy area; and (5) patients with a history of neck fracture or prior neck surgery. This study was conducted with approval from the Ethics Committee of State Grid Beijing Electric Power Hospital (20230830080102). This study was conducted in accordance with the declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Surgical methods

Preoperative Preparation

Patients underwent preoperative fasting for at least six hours, with continuous monitoring of vital signs. Airway secretions in the oral cavity and respiratory tract were cleared. Analgesia and sedation were administered to achieve a critical-care pain observation tool (CPOT) score of zero and the Richmond Agitation Sedation Scale (RASS) score: (−4 to −5). Ventilator parameters were adjusted to synchronized intermittent mandatory ventilation (SIMV) with a tidal volume of 6 ml/kg, fraction of inspired oxygen (FiO2) set to 100%, and positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) reduced to 0 cmH2O. Equipment, including a bedside ultrasound (Mindray), a fiberoptic bronchoscope, and a tracheotomy intubation kit, was prepared to ensure procedural readiness.

Observation indicators

The clinical data of all patients were recorded, including general baseline characteristics and changes in vital signs before and after surgery.

Intraoperative metrics that were recorded included blood loss (subtract the weight of the dry gauze from the weight of the gauze after all the blood has been wiped away), surgical duration (defined as the time from local infiltration anesthesia of the puncture site with 2% lidocaine to the successful placement of the tracheostomy cannula; both the ultrasonographic examination and bronchoscopy insertion were completed prior to the formal commencement of the surgery and were therefore excluded from the measurement of surgical duration), length of the incision, distance from the tracheal tube to the incisors, tracheal tube cuff leakage rate, first-attempt intubation success rate.

Postoperative complications that were observed included sputum overflow at the tracheostomy site, posterior tracheal wall injury, tracheoesophageal fistula, hypoxemia, infection at the incisional surgical site, subcutaneous emphysema, and airway stenosis.

Postoperatively, the surgeon assessed the difficulty of the surgical procedure for each patient using the Froga method [10] as follows: Grade I: No difficulty encountered; Grade II: minor difficulty encountered but successfully overcome; Grade III: Surgery was terminated due to excessive difficulty. Additionally, the extent of bleeding during surgery was classified as follows: Degree I: No or minimal bleeding, requiring no intervention; Degree II: Moderate bleeding, managed with sterile gauze compression hemostasis or ligation of small bleeding vessels; Degree III: Severe bleeding necessitating surgical intervention.

Routine chest X-ray examination was conducted postoperatively to assess for complications such as pneumothorax or subcutaneous emphysema.

Results

General information

A total of 122 patients were included in the study, with 62 cases in the US-B PDT group and 60 cases in the conventional PDT group.

General Information: Comparison between the two groups revealed no statistically significant differences in sex, age, the Acute Physiology and Chronic Health Evaluation II (APACHE II) score, types of primary diseases, or preoperative coagulation function (P > 0.05). However, there was a statistically significant difference in the body mass index (BMI), with a significantly higher BMI in the US-B PDT group than in the PDT group (P = 0.03).

Procedural Difficulty: Difficulty during the procedure was encountered in seven patients of the PDT group, whereas some difficulty was encountered in only one patient in the US-B PDT group. This difference was statistically significant.

Bleeding Complications: There were 10 patients in the PDT group with moderate bleeding requiring intervention, compared to two in the US-B PDT group. This difference between the two groups was also statistically significant (P < 0.05) (Table 1).

Comparison of surgical parameters and complications between the two groups

Surgical Outcomes: Percutaneous tracheotomy was successfully completed in all 122 patients. Compared to the conventional PDT group, patients in the US-B PDT group had significantly better surgical outcomes, including shorter surgical durations, less intraoperative blood loss, lower tracheal tube cuff leakage rates, higher first-attempt intubation success rates, and lower sputum overflow rates at the tracheostomy site (all P < 0.05). There were no significant differences between the two groups with respect to incision length or the distance from the tracheal tube to the incisors (P > 0.05).

None of the 122 patients experienced severe complications such as posterior tracheal wall injury, tracheoesophageal fistula, airway stenosis, tracheal cartilage fracture, or arrhythmia.

Complications: There were 2 cases of subcutaneous emphysema and pneumothorax each in the conventional PDT group, but these differences were not statistically significant when compared to the US-B PDT group (P > 0.05). Hypoxemia occurred in 3 cases in the conventional PDT group and 1 case in the US-B PDT group. The cause of hypoxemia was related to the aspiration of oropharyngeal secretions during the procedure and was not associated with bronchoscopy. Moreover, in the US-B PDT group, the fiberoptic bronchoscope allowed for timely suction of sputum and blood. Therefore, bronchoscopy not only did not contribute to hypoxemia but may actually reduce its incidence.; however, the difference between the two groups was not significant (P > 0.05).

Postoperatively, there were 4 cases of bleeding in the conventional PDT group and 1 case in the US-B PDT group. In all cases, the bleeding was limited to incision site oozing and was managed effectively with local medication and compression hemostasis; no significant differences were observed between the two groups. Similarly, incisional surgical site infections that occurred within 2–3 days postoperatively were noted in 5 cases in the routine PDT group and 2 cases in the US-B PDT group. Microbiological cultures of the wound exudate yielded no positive results, and no cases of septic shock ensued. All patients recovered with anti-infection therapy and routine daily dressing changes; these differences between the two groups were not significant.

Overall Complications: Patients in the conventional PDT group exhibited higher rates of complications, including intraoperative hypoxemia, postoperative bleeding, infection, subcutaneous emphysema, and pneumothorax, compared to those in the US-B PDT group. However, these differences between the groups were not statistically significant (P > 0.05) (Table 2). The overall complication rate was significantly higher in the conventional PDT group (45%) compared to the US-B PDT group (9.7%) (P < 0.001).

Discussion

Critically ill patients in the ICU often present with severe conditions, including respiratory distress and retained airway secretions [12, 13], which can readily lead to respiratory failure. Tracheostomy is therefore required to reduce dead space in the artificial airway, maintain airway patency, and facilitate oxygen therapy. As an emergency procedure, tracheostomy has become an essential supportive measure in the management of patients in respiratory, neurological, emergency, and intensive care units [14]. Conventional surgical tracheostomy (CST), characterized by longer operative time, greater invasiveness, and logistical challenges in patient transport, is associated with various complications and may even result in mortality in severe cases [15]. Consequently, its use has gradually declined. Percutaneous dilatational tracheostomy (PDT), as a minimally invasive technique, offers several advantages including reduced trauma, feasibility for bedside performance, shorter procedure time, fewer complications, and favorable cosmetic outcomes with improved healing after decannulation. It has been widely adopted in critically ill patients both domestically and internationally [16, 17]. However, PDT carries certain blind risks [18]. Although technical refinements have been continuously proposed by researchers, there remains a scarcity of studies on ultrasound localization combined with fiberoptic bronchoscope-guided PDT [19].

Research indicates that bleeding related to PDT is frequently caused by injury to the thyroid isthmus or the anterior jugular venous system. Congenital anatomical variations, such as abnormal innominate arteries or inferior thyroid arteries, may also contribute to procedural hemorrhage [18]. Preoperative bedside neck ultrasound can clearly identify the position of the thyroid gland and the distribution of peripheral vessels, thereby avoiding injury to the thyroid isthmus and major vessels that could lead to significant bleeding. It also enables accurate localization of the puncture site, reducing the risk of postoperative tracheal stenosis due to an improperly high incision [19]. Additionally, ultrasound guidance minimizes repeated puncture attempts and associated damage to the tracheal wall, improving the first-attempt success rate of cannulation [20–22]. This study demonstrated that the operative time in the US-B PDT group (8.30 ± 0.51 min) was shorter than that in the conventional PDT group (10.42 ± 1.29 min). The amount of intraoperative blood loss and the incidence of wound secretion leakage were also lower in the US-B PDT group. Moreover, the first-attempt success rate of cannulation was higher in the US-B PDT group (96.8%) than in the conventional PDT group (90.2%), consistent with reports by international researchers [23]. Fiberoptic bronchoscopy allows real-time tracking and adjustment of the puncture needle’s direction and depth, ensuring the puncture site is located at the midline of the tracheal wall [24]. This helps avoid deviation-induced injury to the lateral tracheal wall or adjacent vessels, thereby reducing bleeding risk, and also prevents excessive penetration that could damage the posterior tracheal wall and lead to tracheoesophageal fistula [20]. In this study, all incisions in the US-B PDT group were midline, with no instances of posterior tracheal wall injury or tracheoesophageal fistula. The endotracheal tube cuff leakage rate was also lower in the US-B PDT group than in the conventional PDT group, consistent with previous domestic and international literature [19]. Furthermore, related studies have shown that ultrasound-guided combined with bronchoscope-assisted PDT can shorten operative time, reduce bleeding, and decrease complication rates in the ICU setting [1, 7, 9]. In the present study, the total number of complications in the US-B PDT group was 6 (9.7%), significantly lower than the 27 cases (45%) in the conventional PDT group, and the difference was statistically significant (P < 0.05).

Additionally, in this study, no posterior tracheal wall injuries were observed in either group of patients undergoing PDT, likely because such injuries would remain undetectable after incision unless severe enough to result in a tracheoesophageal fistula. This further underscores the role of bronchoscopy-guided percutaneous tracheotomy in minimizing damage to the posterior tracheal wall. Furthermore, no instances of tracheoesophageal fistulas or airway stenosis were observed in either group, demonstrating the higher safety profile of minimally invasive PDT compared to conventional ST and reflecting the surgical team’s in tracheotomy techniques.

Patients in the US-B PDT group also demonstrated lower rates of intraoperative hypoxemia, postoperative bleeding, infections, subcutaneous emphysema, and pneumothorax compared to those in the conventional PDT group. While these differences did not reach statistical significance, probably due to the small sample size, future studies with larger cohorts may yield more definitive results.

The comparison of surgical difficulty between the two groups in this study revealed a significant difference, with challenges during the procedure encountered in one patient in the US-B PDT group and seven in the conventional PDT group. This disparity is likely due to the absence of ultrasound localization and fiberoptic bronchoscopy guidance in the PDT group, which may have resulted in tracheostomy tube misplacement into a false passage. These findings suggest that US-B PDT offers a more straightforward approach to the procedure.

Additionally, the incidence of moderate bleeding requiring intervention was significantly lower in the US-B PDT group (two cases), compared to the PDT group (10 cases). This finding further underscores the safety advantages of US-B PDT, as the integration of ultrasound guidance and bronchoscopy enhances surgical precision and reduces complications.

In this study, a statistically significant difference was observed in BMI between the two groups, with the BMI in the US-B PDT group being significantly higher than that in the PDT group (P = 0.03). This discrepancy in BMI likely arose due to the retrospective nature of the study, which did not allow for the random allocation of patients. Given the increased challenges and heightened surgical risks associated with performing conventional PDT in patients with obesity, surgeons frequently opted for either traditional ST in the operating room or the US-B PDT, rather than conventional PDT for this patient subgroup. Previous studies showed that for obese patients, using ultrasound to guide fiberoptic bronchoscopy for tracheotomy can effectively shorten the operation time, reduce intraoperative blood loss, and improve the treatment outcome, which is worthy of promotion and application [25]. In the present study, nine obese patients with a BMI exceeding 30 kg/m2 underwent US-B PDT and all procedures were completed successfully.

The results of this study demonstrated that US-B PDT offers several advantages, including shorter surgical duration, reduced intraoperative blood loss, and a lower the tracheal tube cuff leakage rate. Additionally, the technique also improved the first-attempt intubation success rate and complications such as posterior tracheal wall injury and tracheoesophageal fistula were successfully avoided. Importantly, US-B PDT proved to be a safe and effective option for performing ST in patients with obesity.

This study had certain limitations: (1) It was a single-center study rather than a multi-center one, which limits the generalizability of the findings due to the relatively small sample size; (2) there were no long-term follow-up data as the focus of the study was primarily on perioperative patient management. Consequently, factors such as tracheostomy tube retention time and long-term complications such as wound scars, hoarseness of voice, or tracheal stenosis were not documented; (3) Variability among operators performing the ultrasound and fiberoptic bronchoscopy procedures may have introduced discrepancies in the collected data; (4) As this was a retrospective study conducted over an extended period, it was not feasible to ensure the same operators of both techniques, which lack of operator consistency may introduce a potential source of bias.

Conclusion

US-B PDT might be a safer and more effective technique than conventional PDT. It may maximize patient safety during the procedure and significantly reduce the incidence of postoperative complications, making it well-suited for widespread use in ICUs equipped with the necessary resources.
