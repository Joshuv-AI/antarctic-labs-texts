Engineering Insights into Tailored Metal–Organic Frameworks for CO2 Capture in Industrial Processes
Authors: Hamidreza Mahdavi, Alice Robin, Nathan T. Eden, Abdollah Khosravanian, Muhammad Munir Sadiq, Kristina Konstas, Stefan J. D. Smith, Xavier Mulet, Matthew R. Hill
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Despite the known impacts on climate change of carbon dioxide emissions, the continued use of fossil fuels for energy generation leading to the emission of carbon dioxide (CO2) into the atmosphere is evident. Therefore, innovation to address and reduce CO2 emissions from industrial operations remains an urgent and crucial priority. A viable strategy in the area is postcombustion capture mainly through absorption by aqueous alkanolamines, which focuses on the separation of CO2 from flue gas, despite its limitations. Within this context, porous materials, particularly metal–organic frameworks (MOFs), have arisen as favorable alternatives owing to their significant adsorption capacity, selectivity, and reduced regeneration energy demands. This research evaluates the engineering insights into tailored MOFs for enhanced CO2 capture, focusing on three series of MOFs (ZIF, UiO-66, and BTC) to investigate the effects of organic ligands, functional groups, and metal ions. The evaluation encompassed a range of aspects including adsorption isotherms of pure gases [CO2 and nitrogen (N2)] and mixed gas mixture (CO2 and N2 with 15:85% ratio), along with utilization of the ideal adsorbed solution theory (IAST) to simulate multicomponent gas adsorption isotherms. Moreover, the reliability of IAST for mixed gas adsorption prediction has been investigated in detail. The research offers valuable insights into the correlation between the characteristics of MOFs and their effectiveness in gas separation and how these characteristics contribute to the differences between IAST predictions and experimental results. The findings enhance the understanding of how to enhance MOF characteristics in order to reduce CO2 emissions and also highlight the need for advanced models that consider thermodynamic nonidealities to accurately predict the behavior of mixed gas adsorption in MOFs. As a result, the incorporation of MOFs with enhanced predictability and reliability into CO2 capture industrial processes is facilitated.

Introduction

On-going economic development relies on the production of dispatchable energy, which is still produced from fossil fuel sources despite the penetration of renewable energy such as wind and solar. In essence, the combustion of fossil fuels for power leads to carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions into the atmosphere and climate change.1,2 While there is undoubtedly a transition to renewable, there are significantly hard-to-abate or slow-transitioning industries that require methods to decrease CO2 emissions; these are attracting attention from both industrial and academic aspects.3 Power plants and other industrial sectors, such as cement production plants, refineries, and petrochemicals, are the dominant sources of CO2. There are four major processes, namely, precombustion, oxyfuel-combustion, membrane filtration, and postcombustion for CO2 mitigation, among which postcombustion removal is considered the most practical.4 The postcombustion of generated flue gas mainly entails N2 (73–77%) and CO2 (15–16%). Thus, postcombustion capture aims to separate CO2 from N2 with a 15% content of CO2.1 Different methods, including adsorption, absorption, and cryogenic separation, are employed for postcombustion CO2 capture. Among these methods, however, absorption by aqueous alkanolamine solutions is the most developed process.5,6 Absorption is a relatively inexpensive process with technical benefits such as remarkable water solubility, high capacity, and fast kinetics. Yet, some disadvantages include amine degradation, amine renewal, extensive energy requirements, and, more importantly, the high energy supplies for CO2 separation from rich amines, which could be up to 80% of the operation cost.7

These drawbacks redirected attention toward alternate processes, such as adsorption using porous materials. Porous adsorbents demonstrated high versatility, significant gas uptake, excellent reusability, and diverse applicability.8 Porous solids such as zeolites, activated carbons, calcium oxides, hydrotalcites, and supported amines are used for CO2 separation.9,10 However, they may still need relatively high regeneration energy requirements or exhibit poor selectivity, translating to low productivity and high operational costs.11,12 This leads to the exploration of new, energy-efficient porous solids. Metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) are considered promising porous solids for achieving high CO2 separation with low regeneration energy requirements.13,14 MOFs have exceptional properties, including high adsorption capacity, selectivity, surface areas, stability, reusability, and require minimal energy for regeneration.15−17 For example, several MOFs18−22 demonstrate capacity and selectivity that outperform commercial zeolites23 and activated carbons.24,25 Zeolite 13× has a gravimetric CO2 absorption of 2.41 mmol·g–1 at 0.1 bar and a CO2/N2 selectivity of 500 at 1 bar (with CO2 and N2 with the 10:90% ratio).26 Conversely, Maxsorb activated carbon has a CO2 uptake of >0.2 mmol·g–1 and a selectivity of >10 under similar conditions. Whereas Mg-MOF-74 demonstrates a CO2 absorption of 5.1 mmol·g–1 and a selectivity of 182,27,28 SiFSIX-3-Cu obtains a CO2 uptake of 2.4 mmol·g–1 and a selectivity of <2000,29 and SiFSIX-3-Zn provides a CO2 uptake of 2.39 mmol·g–1 and a selectivity of 1700,26 with the same conditions. In recent years, many synthetic pathways have been explored to synthesize new MOFs. These new frameworks interact differently with gas species and consequently possess different selective adsorptions of CO2.30−32 Some important features of MOFs that offer a route to satisfy the MOF selection criteria are the modular nature of the organic ligands, functional groups, and metal ions.33

From a MOF structure perspective, three groups of MOFs can be considered for evaluating the effects of organic ligands, functional groups, and metal ions on adsorption properties. One MOF series that can be synthesized with different organic ligands is the ZIF series. The ZIF series are microporous materials based on a 3-D structure of zinc metal ions and imidazole ligands.34 Another MOF that can be synthesized with different functional groups is UiO-66. The UiO-66 series are microporous materials based on a 3-D structure of zirconium metal ions and benzene dicarboxylate ligands. Integrating functional groups into the UiO-66 structure is possible through electrophilic aromatic substitutions. The modified dicarboxylate ligand induces different functional groups to the UiO-66 series.35 BTC series, as another microporous material, can possess different metal ions. BTC series are based on a 3-D structure of different metals and benzene-1,3,5-tricarboxylic acid ligands.36 Regarding MOF performance assessment, several factors can be considered when evaluating the adsorption behavior. Single-component adsorption isotherms serve as the primary initial screening tool for relative gas adsorption capabilities.4 However, single-component adsorption isotherms do not consider competitive adsorption among different gas components. Therefore, it is essential to evaluate mixed gas adsorption isotherms to accurately estimate MOFs’ gas capture ability.37 Furthermore, the ideal adsorbed solution theory (IAST), which assumes that the gas mixture behaves as an ideal solution, fits two models to single-component adsorption isotherms. Utilizing these fitted models, IAST enables the prediction of mixed gas adsorption isotherms and selectivity.38,39

In this work, the ZIF series (ZIF-7, ZIF-8, and ZIF-62), UiO-66 series [UiO-66, UiO-66-NH2, UiO-66-SO3H, and UiO-66-(COOH)2] and BTC series (AlBTC, ZrBTC, and CuBTC) have been synthesized. All the MOFs have been characterized via several techniques, including X-ray diffraction, Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy, scanning electron microscopy, thermogravimetric analysis, and Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) analysis. Experimental gravimetric single-component adsorption isotherms of CO2 and N2 gases and a mixed gas mixture containing CO2 and N2 with 15:85% ratio were measured. The IAST modeling used the pyGAPS code to investigate the effect of organic ligands, functional groups, and metal ions on adsorption properties. A route to selecting the optimal MOF for different criteria is presented. This leads to an engineering perspective, which can fill the gap between chemistry and chemical engineering of applying MOFs in industrial CO2 capture processes. It also helps to resolve how the features of MOFs affect their capacity to capture CO2 emissions and explore new frameworks with the greatest likelihood of contributing to the global push of decreasing CO2 emissions. Moreover, the reliability of the IAST for mixed gas adsorption prediction has been investigated to provide insights into its applicability in real-world scenarios.

Theory of Gas Adsorption in MOFs

Some factors impact the MOF’s adsorption, including surface area, pore volume, steric hindrance near the metal cluster, and surface binding sites. In general, higher surface area and pore volume could provide more active sites for the interaction between the adsorbent and adsorbate, resulting in higher gas adsorption capacity.40 However, strong adsorbate–adsorbent interactions are another important factor that can affect the gas adsorption capacity. First, these interactions result in strong adhesion of gas molecules, leading to greater surface coverage and thereby facilitating gas adsorption. Furthermore, these interactions increase the heat of adsorption, which increases the energy released during the adhesion of gas molecules; thus, adsorption is made more efficient and desirable.40,41

Gas adsorption with MOFs follows several mechanisms: physisorption, chemisorption, and size-exclusion separation mechanisms.42 Physisorption is the adsorption of gas molecules on the surface of MOFs via physical forces. The comparative adsorption capacity depends on factors such as surface polarizability and dipole, magnetic susceptibility of the surface and gas, and/or MOF adsorbents quadrupole moment. The process is called chemisorption if the adsorption occurs by the chemical interactions between adsorbates and MOFs. The chemical interactions naturally correspond to either the interactions of MOF functional groups and gas molecules or the targeted gas’s connections to unsaturated metallic sites of MOFs. Adsorption of the targeted gas due to size variations is also vital since the kinetic diameters of the adsorbates move toward the MOF’s pore size, where larger gas molecules are blocked at the entrance of the pores. In contrast, smaller gas molecules can easily enter the pores.43 Besides, gas adsorption in all MOFs can be categorized into two separate pressure regimes: low and high. The low-pressure adsorption regime mainly depends on the surface area and presents a remarkable increase in adsorption with the pressure increments. Whereas, at the high-pressure regime, the favorable adsorption sites are occupied, and adsorption is because of the available pore volume. The increasing trend reaches a plateau and approaches adsorption saturation.40

Selectivity is a fundamentally important issue for gas separation. Nevertheless, high gas adsorption capacity does not necessarily mean higher selectivity over other gases.40 Indeed, the adsorption gate-opening pressure depends on the binding affinity and molecular sieving (matching of size and shape).44 Since the kinetic diameter of CO2 is small in comparison to N2 (3.3 and 3.8°A for CO2 and N2, respectively), in porous materials with smaller pore diameters, superior CO2/N2 selectivity of CO2 gas molecules can be observed. Additionally, thanks to the greater quadrupole moment, which in turn causes significant polarizability, CO2 gas molecules exhibit intense electrostatic interactions with MOFs leading to higher selectivity.14

The flexibility of the MOF adsorbents is established by the flexibility of the employed organic ligands.45 Therefore, flexible MOF adsorbents can employ all the existing mechanisms simultaneously and go through a structural change to distinguish the variances of guests, hence improving the uptake capacity.46 In stark contrast, inflexible MOFs differentiate the guest molecules by the differential binding or molecular sieving procedures.47 In addition, it is established that the organic ligand characteristics govern some crucial features such as construction, topology, and binding selectivity of the fabricated MOF adsorbents.45,48,49 Accurately controlling these features can result in more optimized pore sizes, shapes, internal surface traits, and active sites on the adsorbent’s surface where the synthesized MOFs can exhibit improved intermolecular interactions.48,49 Interestingly, MOFs’ exceptional attributes like high surface area which leads to greater pore volume and lower density, pore structure, molecular recognition, stable structure, and so forth can be optimized by organic ligand manipulation and design.48

Modeling Approach

IAST was used to develop a methodology to understand the relation between mixed gas adsorption and the properties of MOF’s properties. IAST can predict multicomponent gas mixture adsorption from the experimental single-component adsorption isotherms at the same temperature and for the same adsorbents.50 It is essential to have a wide range of MOFs with different properties to screen the structure–property relationships. Therefore, as presented in Table S2, 10 frameworks with different organic ligand groups, functional groups, and metals have been considered. Experimental gravimetric adsorption isotherms of pure gases including CO2 and N2 and a mixed gas mixture containing CO2 and N2 with 15:85% ratio were also measured using an Intelligent Gravimetric Analyzer (IGA-001). The modeling used the pyGAPS code (Python General Adsorption Processing Suite) to predict the single-component and mixed gas adsorption isotherms and selectivity.38,51 For this purpose, the single-component adsorption isotherms should be fitted precisely with the appropriate model to perform the required IAST integrations. The best-fit models were selected by fitting the experimental single-component gas adsorption isotherms with several adsorption isotherm models for CO2 and N2 of MOFs at 298 K. As a result, the BET and Jensen–Seaton (JS) models were found to be the best fit for the experimental data for N2 and CO2, respectively. Table 1 presents the goodness of fit for each MOF, characterized by the root-mean-square error (RMSE) and R-square error (R2) values. As presented, the BET and JS equations fit the single-component isotherms extremely well. The R2 values for nearly all of the fitted adsorption isotherms were over 0.99. Therefore, the fitted adsorption isotherm parameters were applied to perform the necessary integrations in IAST. Besides, the model coefficients for each MOF are also demonstrated in Table 2.

Results and Discussion

Effects of Organic Ligands on Adsorption Properties

Specifically, in the low-pressure regime, ZIF-62 exhibits higher CO2, N2, and mixed gas adsorptions than does ZIF-7. This is attributed to ZIF-62’s intricate pore structure and higher flexibility than ZIF-7. Indeed, ZIF-62 presents gate-opening behaviors at a lower pressure compared to ZIF-7. However, in the high-pressure regime, ZIF-7 exhibits a higher adsorption capacity rate increase than ZIF-62 attributed to its higher affinity and ability to mitigate the limitations of low flexibility through gradual gate-opening behavior as the pressure increases, though the overall adsorption capacities’ trends remain the same. On the other hand, in low-pressure regimes, ZIF-8’s CO2 and mixed gas adsorptions are initially lower than those of ZIF-7 and ZIF-62 due to lower affinity despite ZIF-8’s higher flexibility. However, with increasing pressure, the adsorptions increase owing to ZIF-8’s flexibility, which plays the dominant role, allowing it to adsorb larger quantities readily. Furthermore, ZIF-8 exhibits higher N2 adsorption in low-pressure regimes due to the larger surface area. However, ZIF-8’s N2 adsorption at high pressure regimes is lower owing to faster pore saturation despite its flexibility and initially higher adsorption at lower pressures.52

By examining the interaction between gases and adjacent aromatic rings, it can be deduced that these behaviors are governed by the aromaticity of the organic ligand, which causes the intensity of π–π interactions to increase.53 Therefore, ZIF-7, where benzimidazole serves as the ligand, has a higher affinity for CO2 in comparison to ZIF-62 which possesses a mixture of benzimidazole and imidazole as the ligand. Similarly, ZIF-62 has a higher affinity for CO2 when compared to ZIF-8, featuring 2-methylimidazole as its ligand. However, the bulkier organic ligands can result in less MOF flexibility,52 leading to a smaller pore aperture and lower easily accessible surface area, as observed in Figure S2 (BET analysis using N2 gas: 500, 1606, and 233 m2·g–1 for ZIF-7, ZIF-8, and ZIF-62, respectively), leading to less adsorption.54 These structural variations resulting from organic ligands lead to distinct adsorption characteristics. Therefore, at low-pressure regimes, ZIF-62 has a higher IAST selectivity, whereas at higher-pressure regimes, more CO2 molecules can penetrate the ZIF-7 pores, leading to higher IAST selectivity, as seen in Figure 4.

Effects of Functional Groups on Adsorption Properties

To investigate the effects of functional groups, it is essential to analyze the size, caused variations in structural properties, and conferred affinity of the functional groups that provide the most noticeable impact on MOF behaviors.14 As presented in Figures 1–3, UiO-66-NH2 exhibits adsorption enhancement over the UiO-66. It can be assigned to the higher surface area, larger pore volume, and stronger surface binding sites imparted from the –NH2 groups. Meanwhile, the UiO-66-SO3H and UiO-66-(COOH)2 represent a significantly lower adsorption capacity than UiO-66. It can be ascribed to the lower surface area, smaller pore volume, and weaker surface binding sites induced by the –SO3H and –(COOH)2 functionalities. Although the adsorption isotherms of UiO-66-SO3H and UiO-66-(COOH)2 are similar, the -(COOH)2 group has a severe impact compared to the –SO3H group, possibly due to less flexibility. Although CO2, N2, and mixed gas mixture follow similar trends, the CO2 adsorption is higher than N2 adsorption due to UiO-66’s strong surface or chemical interactions with CO2 in comparison to N2 as a probe molecule which does not have strong surface or chemical interactions. Thus, N2 adsorption isotherms have a nearly linear nature with a smaller adsorption capacity. Introducing functional groups brings about the potential for optimizing the size of the cavities to be more attractive to the size of the CO2 gas molecule size. More significantly, it proliferates the adsorption hot spots, which trigger the CO2 adsorption capacity. However, this can extend the N2 adsorption capacities as well, yet the decrease in the surface area could govern it because the incorporated functionalities occupy some spaces. The light –NH2 group has significantly higher ideal selectivity for CO2 over N2 than unfunctionalized UiO-66, as seen in Figure 4. This difference is most likely due to the enhanced interaction of the –NH2 group with the –NH2 group with CO2. The bulky –SO3H and –(COOH)2 groups reduce the pore window but with increasing pressure, the ability for CO2 to enter the pores increases, resulting in higher CO2/N2 ideal selectivity. Thus, at high pressures, –NH2, –SO3H, and –(COOH)2 functionalization will exhibit a greater affinity for CO2 over N2.14

Effects of Metal Ions on Adsorption Properties

To study the effects of metal ions, it is important to study the metal’s coordination preference, which determines the number and orientation of organic ligands that bind to the metal. This binding process forms a crystalline solid with periodic reticulation, rather than an amorphous phase with special pores’ size and shape.55,56 In addition, the functionality and activity of a MOF depends on the metal center and its charge used in its synthesis.57 MOFs with the presence of unsaturated metallic sites (UMSs) or open metal sites (OMSs) can inherently provide Lewis acidity behavior. MOFs with this property exhibit more CO2 adsorption.58,59 Thus, defect inclusion allows for obtaining UMSs with Lewis acidity.60 In the context of the BTC series, the performance comparisons should be regarded as almost stoichiometric rather than differences that are influenced by structural or crystallographic trends, as the structures of CuBTC, AlBTC, and ZrBTC are significantly different due to the varying charges on the metal centers.61−63 As depicted in Figures 1–3, the CuBTC’s CO2, N2, and mixed gas adsorptions are higher than other materials across all pressures due to the high surface area, more available OMSs, and flexibility. CuBTC is a well-known OMS material that contains weakly bound water molecules on the axial coordination sites of each Cu2+ metal center. The water molecules can be easily removed through vacuum heating to afford open Cu2+ sites pointing toward the interior of the cavity for binding of guest molecules. Whereas ZrBTC possesses small and hard ions (Zr4+) with high charge density, which can bond strongly to the organic ligand and create a robust structure. Moreover, AlBTC can also be made from Al3+ which leads to stable MOF formation due to strong interactions with organic ligands and forms a relatively robust structure. Both cases reduce the presence possibility of OMSs.64 AlBTC shows a higher capacity for CO2 compared to ZrBTC, potentially due to its higher surface area, more number of OMSs, and higher flexibility. However, AlBTC has a lower N2 adsorption capacity than ZrBTC, possibly due to its smaller pore size. Similarly, this behavior was also observed for mixed gas adsorption. As exhibited in Figure 4, the larger quadrupole moment and greater polarizability of CO2 compared with N2 leads to a stronger electrostatic interaction with an exposed metal site which leads to higher selectivity for CuBTC at lower pressures compared to AlBTC and ZrBTC.58,59 On the other hand, the AlBTC has a lower interaction energy with the metal center compared to CuBTC, but greater compared to ZrBTC. As a consequence, AlBTC has lower selectivity than CuBTC but higher than ZrBTC at low pressures. By an increase in pressure, the selectivity of CuBTC and ZrBTC exhibits a slight improvement. However, ZrBTC still maintains the lowest selectivity owing to its robust structure and fewer OMSs. However, AlBTC reveals higher selectivity than CuBTC at higher pressures owing to its relative flexibility.65

Reliability of the IAST for Mixed Gas Adsorption Prediction

The prediction of mixed gas adsorption using IAST is based on two principles: (i) the uniform distribution of adsorbates within the pores, leading to fair competition for adsorption sites, and (ii) the surface area occupied by an adsorbate in the mixture is the same as in mixed adsorption, indicating the absence of clustering with other partners.66 Nevertheless, within MOF materials like the ZIF, UiO-66, and BTC series, due to the existence of pore architectures containing small apertures, functional groups providing affinity, or open metal sites inducing preferential attraction, they can selectively draw CO2 molecules.

MOFs have pore architectures that selectively prevent the penetration of specific gas molecules depending on their size and shape. For example, the ZIF series has pores that can more easily contain CO2 molecules compared to N2 molecules owing to their difference in size. The selective exclusion mechanism contradicts the premise in IAST that all adsorbate molecules have equal access to adsorption sites. Moreover, functional groups or open metal sites in MOFs can induce strong interactions with specific gas molecules, which can affect how these molecules are adsorbed. Functional groups in the UiO-66 series or open metal sites in the BTC series can preferentially bind with CO2 because of their polar nature, resulting in more CO2 adsorption relative to that of N2. In this scenario, the IAST is also unable to accurately anticipate the local areas where CO2 is preferentially accommodated due to its inability to account for preferred surface interactions.

The IAST is built on ideal thermodynamic assumptions, including the absence of clustering and ideal adsorbed solution characteristics. However, thermodynamic nonidealities such as bonding, clustering of molecules, and entropy effects can substantially impact adsorption properties in real systems; IAST fails to account for these complexities. Consequently, inadequate consideration of these effects in the IAST results in differences between experimental observations and model predictions in terms of under- or overprediction for mixed gas adsorption of mixtures containing CO2 and N2 in MOFs, as shown in Figure 5. IAST often underpredicts the amount of gas adsorbed in MOFs at low pressures. This is because the model does not fully account for the effect of parameters such as limited access to the pores, blockage of the pores, or the effects of saturation. Nevertheless, MOFs that have robust structures, do not have strong binding functionality, or lack a significant amount of OMSs often provide more accurate predictions. However, even in these cases, the IAST often shows a slight level of underprediction owing to a lower extent of the aforementioned reasons. On the other hand, MOFs with significant flexibility worsen the problem of underprediction in the IAST since effectively representing the dynamic behavior of these flexible MOFs is challenging inside the static framework of the IAST. The inherent flexibility of these MOFs can give rise to variations in the size and shapes of their pores, making it challenging to accurately predict the interactions between gas molecules and the MOFs. Nevertheless, as pressure increases, the difference between experimental and IAST results of all the MOFs tends to decrease. Increased pressures promote the number of adsorption sites and intensify the interactions between gas molecules and the MOF surface, hence enhancing the capacity of adsorbate molecules to occupy the pores and correlate more closely with experimental results. Moreover, when the pressure increases, MOFs that possess a moderate level of flexibility and intricate pore structures, or those that have high-affinity functionalities or a greater number of available OMSs, often lead to overprediction. At high pressures, where there is improved access to pores and stronger favorable surface interactions, predicted adsorption capabilities are greater than what is measured from experiments. Hence, it is crucial to use advanced models that take into consideration all the effective variables to get precise predictions of mixed gas adsorption behavior.

Experimental and modeling mixed gas adsorption isotherms for CO2 and N2 with 15:85% ratio at 298 K of (a) ZIF, (b) UiO-66, and (c) BTC series results (experimental and modeling adsorption isotherms are shown with filled and empty shapes, respectively).

For example, several advanced models have been developed to reduce the deviations seen in IAST by addressing its limitations. The real adsorbed solution theory (RAST) incorporates activity coefficients to include nonideal interactions between adsorbate molecules and the adsorbent, resulting in a more accurate representation of the adsorbed phase.67 The extended Langmuir model addresses the issue of competing adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces by assuming comparable saturated adsorption capacities among mixture components.68 The osmotic framework adsorbed solution theory incorporates the thermodynamic osmotic ensemble, enabling coadsorption prediction in flexible porous materials that undergo guest-induced phase transitions.69 The vacancy solution model considers the adsorption equilibrium as a phase equilibrium between two vacancy solutions in the gas and adsorbed phases and it utilizes activity coefficients to address the nonideal behavior of the adsorbed phase and spreading pressure to ensure equilibrium is maintained.70

Conclusions

The complex relationship among organic ligands, functionalization, and metal ions highlights the need for a comprehensive strategy in tailoring MOFs for the best gas adsorption performance. The gas adsorption capacity of MOFs is considerably affected by the flexibility of the organic ligands that control their structure. ZIF-8, a kind of flexible MOF, can employ various mechanisms at the same time to adjust its structure to differentiate between different guest variations and increase its CO2 uptake ability. In contrast, larger ligands in ZIF-62 and ZIF-7 cause a drop in flexibility, a reduction in pore size, and a decrease in surface area, ultimately leading to a decrease in the CO2 adsorption capacity. Moreover, MOF functionalization offers a strategic approach to enhance features such as adsorption, selectivity, and multifunctionality by their size, caused variations in structural properties, and conferred affinity. UiO-66-NH2 demonstrates improved adsorption due to its greater surface area, bigger pore volume, and stronger surface binding sites imparted from the –NH2 groups. In contrast, UiO-66-SO3H and UiO-66-(COOH)2 have reduced adsorption capabilities as a result of their lower surface area, smaller pore volume, and weaker surface binding sites induced by the –SO3H and –(COOH)2 functionalities. Functional groups are introduced to optimize cavity diameters, resulting in improved CO2 agreement and increased adsorption hot spots. The presence of various metal ions (Cu2+, Zr4+, and Al3+) in MOF structures significantly impacts characteristics such as adsorption and activity. MOFs containing open metal sites exhibit Lewis acidity, which leads to the enhanced adsorption of CO2. Open Cu2+ sites in CuBTC result in enhanced CO2 adsorption and selectivity at lower pressures owing to its Lewis acidity. On the other hand, AlBTC and ZrBTC create more robust MOFs with fewer exposed metal sites, resulting in less sorption. However, when pressure is increased, the AlBTC exhibits more selectivity as a result of its flexibility.

Moreover, although IAST is a valuable tool for estimating mixed gas adsorption and selectivity, it is restricted by some parameters. The IAST assumes that the adsorbate is evenly distributed and that there is no clustering. However, this overlooks the complicated nature of real systems, such as preferential adsorption due to pore geometry and interactions with functional groups or metal sites. These complexities can result in differences between IAST predictions and the experimental results. This highlights the need for advanced models that consider thermodynamic nonidealities to accurately predict the behavior of mixed gas adsorption in MOFs.
