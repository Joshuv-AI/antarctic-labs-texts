Emerging strategies for treating medical device and wound‐associated biofilm infections
Authors: Chenlong Wang, Yajuan Su, S M Shatil Shahriar, Yu Li, Jingwei Xie
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacterial infections represent a significant global threat to human health, leading to considerable economic losses through increased healthcare costs and reduced productivity. One major challenge in treating these infections is the presence of biofilms ‐ structured bacterial communities that form protective barriers, making traditional treatments less effective. Additionally, the rise of antibiotic‐resistant bacteria has exacerbated treatment difficulties. To address these challenges, researchers are developing and exploring innovative approaches to combat biofilm‐related infections. This mini‐review highlights recent advancements in the following key areas: surface anti‐adhesion technologies, electricity, photo/acoustic‐active materials, endogenous mimicking agents, and innovative drug delivery systems. These strategies aim to prevent biofilm formation, disrupt existing biofilms, and enhance the efficacy of antimicrobial treatments. Currently, these approaches show great potential for applications in medical fields such as medical device and wound – associated biofilm infections. By summarizing these developments, this mini‐review provides a comprehensive resource for researchers seeking to advance the management and treatment of biofilm‐associated infections.

INTRODUCTION

Microbial infections pose a severe threat to global public health and economic stability (Carratalá et al., 2024; Tuffs et al., 2024; Uberoi et al., 2024). Pathogenic microorganisms can cause both acute and chronic diseases, potentially leading to widespread outbreaks that significantly strain healthcare systems and result in substantial economic losses (Rudbaek et al., 2024; Silverman et al., 2024). The growing issue of antibiotic resistance exacerbates this challenge, as traditional antimicrobial treatments become increasingly ineffective (Lockhart et al., 2023; Mann et al., 2024). This situation compromises patient health and exerts profound negative impacts on societal and economic systems. Consequently, the urgent need for innovative therapeutic and preventive strategies has become increasingly evident.

A critical factor contributing to the difficulty in managing microbial infections is the formation and development of biofilms (Choi et al., 2023; Uberoi et al., 2024). Biofilms are complex, self‐protective microbial communities that adhere to surfaces and are encased in a protective extracellular matrix (Costerton et al., 1999). This matrix provides the microorganisms with enhanced adherence and resistance to both host immune responses and conventional drug treatments. The presence of biofilms not only exacerbates the persistence of infections but also causes extensive contamination and damage to medical devices, implants, and natural environments (Ciofu et al., 2022; Masters et al., 2022; Rosenberg et al., 2022). Thus, controlling biofilm formation and its resistance to treatments has become a key research focus in managing microbial infections.

This min‐review explores recent advancements in combating biofilm‐associated infections, emphasizing five innovative approaches: surface anti‐adhesion technologies (aimed at preventing microbial attachment and biofilm formation), electricity (using electric current to disrupt biofilm structures and enhance antimicrobial efficacy), photo/acoustic‐active materials (leveraging unique physical and chemical properties to target and disrupt biofilms), endogenous mimicking agents (mimicking natural processes and offering targeted and efficient interventions with reduced side effects compared to traditional treatments), and innovative drug delivery systems (optimizing the release and targeting of antimicrobial agents to overcome biofilm barriers) (Figure 1). By examining these emerging technologies and their clinical potential, this mini‐review provides researchers with up‐to‐date insights and strategies for effectively addressing biofilm‐related infections, thereby, advancing further research and technological development in this critical field.

ANTI‐ADHESION

In the early stages, bacteria primarily adhere to material surfaces through weak interactions such as electrostatic forces, hydrophobic bonds, and/or van der Waals forces (Gristina et al., 1985). As bacteria release sticky substances, such as capsular polysaccharides, mature biofilms adhere more tightly and cannot be removed by standard mechanical cleaning, disinfectant spraying, or UV light. Therefore, designing anti‐adhesive surfaces to prevent bacterial adhesion is a theoretically feasible approach. Currently, the design of anti‐adhesive surfaces focuses mainly on two major directions: superhydrophobic surfaces and superhydrophilic surfaces.

Superhydrophobic surfaces

Bacteria find it difficult to form sufficient adhesion on superhydrophobic surfaces, preventing biofilm formation. Oh et al. etched micro‐nanostructures similar to rice leaves on quartz substrates and, after treatment with trimethylchlorosilane, obtained superhydrophobic surfaces with a bacterial anti‐adhesion rate of up to 99.9% against Escherichia coli and Staphylococcus aureu s (Oh et al., 2015). Inspired by the self‐cleaning superhydrophobic surface of lotus leaves, Wang and colleagues developed a nano‐layered diamond surface with self‐cleaning and super‐wear‐resistant properties, significantly reducing bacterial adhesion (Wang et al., 2020). In a different study, Han et al. discovered that cotton fabrics treated with nanogels prevent bacterial adhesion by enhancing hydrophobicity and antibacterial properties (Han et al., 2019). In another work, Liu et al. deposited a superhydrophobic coating composed of multilayer polyelectrolyte films encapsulating Ag nanoparticles on metal substrates, exhibiting excellent antibacterial and anti‐adhesion properties (Liu et al., 2012). Additionally, scientists reported the successful preparation of a dual‐functional coating with antibacterial and anti‐adhesion properties by immobilizing lysozyme in a functionalized superhydrophobic coating, retaining its superhydrophobic properties (Liu, et al., 2020).

Superhydrophilic surfaces

Superhydrophilic surfaces can easily capture water and provide a highly hydrophilic environment. Venault et al. demonstrated that superhydrophilic surfaces can resist the adhesion of positively charged bacteria, such as Bacillus subtilis (Venault et al., 2014). Current research mainly focuses on obtaining superhydrophilic surfaces by utilization of amphiphilic non‐ionic poly(ethylene glycol) (PEG) and super‐hydrophilic zwitterionic poly (carboxybetaine) (PSB) (Cao & Jiang, 2012). Chen et al. investigated the effects of short‐chain (oligoethylene glycol (OEG)4), medium‐chain (OEG8), and long‐chain (polyethylene glycol (PEG)2 K) PEGs and found that the chain length of PEG is crucial (Chen et al., 2019). Medium‐chain PEG provides excellent antifouling properties, minimizing background noise of unwanted cell adhesion and maintaining a sufficiently high density of functional peptides on the surface. This enables the peptides to perform optimally in cell adhesion, migration, and differentiation. However, amphiphilic PEG has negative effects on proteins and nanoparticles due to its hydrophobic properties, including reduced protein bioactivity, unstable nanoparticle assembly, and lipid bilayer instability (Cao & Jiang, 2012). Ultra‐hydrophilic PSB can overcome these issues. McVerry et al. developed a simple one‐step zwitterionic surface modification method that significantly reduces protein and microbial adhesion to synthetic materials. They successfully modified several clinically relevant materials (Figure 2A), including difficult‐to‐treat elastomeric polydimethylsiloxane. PSB was chosen due to its biocompatibility, ultra‐low fouling properties, and oxidative stability. PSB coatings form a thin hydrated barrier through electrostatic adsorption of water, preventing bio‐adhesion (McVerry et al., 2022).

Anti‐adhesion materials can also be combined with existing antimicrobial agents. Liu et al. functionalized thin film composite membranes with zwitterionic polymers and silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) to combat biofouling. Combining hydrophilic zwitterionic polymers with antimicrobial AgNPs endows the membranes with dual functions: anti‐adhesion and bacterial inactivation (Figure 2B) (Liu et al., 2017). Wang et al. developed a superhydrophilic DURA‐Z coating for double‐sided tapes, allowing for easy and widespread application on common substrates (Wang et al., 2017a).

Superhydrophilic and superhydrophobic surfaces each offer distinct advantages, and the release of antimicrobial agents is closely related to the wettability of the material's surface. Yohe et al. noted that superhydrophobic surfaces can delay the release of antimicrobial agents, while superhydrophilic surfaces facilitate their release more readily (Yohe et al., 2012). Controlling the conversion of hydrophilic and hydrophobic states is expected to become a new method for controlled release of antimicrobial drugs. Gui et al. designed a photo‐responsive TiO₂‐based surface capable of intelligent switching between superhydrophobic (Figure 2C) and superhydrophilic states (Figure 2D) on the same material (Gui et al., 2022). UV light irradiation can easily change the surface state (Figure 2E). The superhydrophobic state demonstrated superior anti‐bacterial adhesion properties, whereas the superhydrophilic state was more conducive to release‐killing.

ELECTRIC CURRENT

Electric currents have garnered significant attention as a biofilm treatment method due to their ease of use, non‐invasive nature, and controllable dosages. Studies have demonstrated that electric currents can enhance the antimicrobial efficacy of antibiotics against biofilms (Froughreyhani et al., 2018; Pozo et al., 2009). Costerton et al. coined this phenomenon the “bioelectric effect”, which involves using electric fields to augment the efficacy of biocides and antibiotics in killing biofilm bacteria (Costerton et al., 1994). This effect has been shown to reduce the required concentrations of antimicrobial agents to levels very close to those needed to kill planktonic bacteria of the same species. Although the mechanisms behind the bioelectric effect are still being studied, iontophoresis‐enhanced antibiotic delivery is considered a crucial contributing factor. Traditionally, it was believed that the decay of electroactive biofilms was due to the limited diffusion of substrates/metabolites within the biofilm or insufficient transmembrane redox gradients to sustain extracellular respiration (Bond et al., 2012; Chadwick et al., 2019). However, Liu et al. discovered that the genome of Geobacter sulfurreducens contains a prophage that, during the growth of electroactive organisms, spontaneously activates the “lysis” pathway, leading to the continuous decay of the electroactive biofilm (Liu et al., 2023).

While the bioelectric effect can enhance the efficacy of antibiotics and reduce biofilm bacterial density, the low intensity of the electric currents used (<0.5 mA/cm2) necessitates long treatment durations (≥24 h) (Wattanakaroon & Stewart, 2000). Pozo et al. found that increasing the electric current intensity can improve the efficiency of iontophoretic antibiotic delivery (del Pozo Jose et al., 2009). However, high‐intensity currents can cause significant changes in pH and temperature at the tissue interface, leading to severe tissue damage (Schulze et al., 2016). To address these challenges, Zhao et al. introduced an efficient electro‐antibiofilm treatment system that employs high‐intensity currents to completely clear biofilms from wound surfaces through enhancing antibiotic delivery to biofilm‐infected wounds (Figure 3A) (Zhao et al., 2023). The system combines these effects to achieve a significant reduction in bacterial counts on methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) biofilm‐infected ex vivo skin wounds within a short treatment duration (≤2 h). By utilizing a hydrogel ion circuit design, this system improves the in vivo safety of high‐intensity electric current applications compared to traditional devices.

Use of electric current to combat biofilms. (A) Hydrogel ionic circuits for treatment of wound biofilm (Zhao et al., 2023). (B) Self‐powered microneedle device for eliminating bacteria and promoting infected diabetic wound healing (Li et al., 2024).

Additionally, traditional exogenous electrical stimulation devices face several limitations that hinder their clinical application. These include: (i) their relatively large size, which makes them cumbersome to carry; (ii) inadequate mechanical compatibility with wounds; and (iii) the risk of causing psychological stress and skin inflammation with extended use. Li et al. designed a self‐powered triboelectric nanogenerator device incorporated multifunctional microneedles carrying ZnO particles (Figure 3B) (Li et al., 2024). This self‐powered triboelectric nanogenerator can convert biomechanical motion into exogenous electrical stimulation. Through the action of electric current, the device showed effective antibacterial performance against Staphylococcus aureus and Escherichia coli (antibacterial rate >99%). Notably, the materials used in electrical methods typically exhibit good conductivity. In the future, it will be feasible to develop devices that combine electrical signal input for bacterial eradication with output capabilities for recording the wound status.

PHOTO/ACOUSTIC‐ACTIVE MATERIALS

Photo/acoustic‐active materials are a class of advanced materials that respond to light (photo) and sound (acoustic) stimuli. By converting energy from light or sound into a functional response, photo/acoustic‐active materials hold significant potential for innovative technological advancements. Among these, photodynamic therapy (PDT) and photothermal therapy (PTT) materials are extensively studied. PDT involves the use of materials that generate toxic reactive oxygen species (ROS) when exposed to light (Dougherty & Marcus, 1992). Since 1993, regulatory approval for PDT involving the use of a partially purified, commercially available hematoporphyrin derivative compound (Photofrin®) has been obtained in Canada, the Netherlands, France, Germany, Japan, and the United States for patients with early and advanced stage cancer of the lung, digestive tract, and genitourinary tract (Dougherty et al., 1998). ROS can cause oxidative damage to bacterial lipids, nucleic acids, and proteins, making photodynamic nanomaterials a prominent candidate for combating biofilms. This oxidative attack leads to irreversible bacterial death (Huang et al., 2012; Jia et al., 2019). For a notable example, Yang et al. developed a highly effective near infrared light (NIR)‐responsive antibacterial TiO2/TiO2 − x superhydrophobic surface on Ti alloy implants using a novel bidirectional alkaline‐acid hydrothermal method based on topochemical transformation (Figure 4A) (Yang et al., 2022). Both in vitro and in vivo tests demonstrated excellent antibacterial effects after just 10 min of low‐power NIR irradiation. In a different study, Xia et al. developed a boronic acid‐functionalized lipophilic cationic Type I photosensitizer, ACR‐DMP, which effectively generates •OH radicals to overcome hypoxic microenvironments and eradicates MRSA and biofilms through photodynamic therapy (Xia et al., 2023). However, PDT requires substantial ROS production to eliminate bacteria, which could lead to inflammation, fibrosis, and even necrosis in normal cells.

Use of photo/acoustic‐active materials to combat biofilms. (A) A PDT‐based antibacterial nanomaterial (Yang et al., 2022). (B) An SDT‐based antibacterial nanomaterial (Sun et al., 2020). (C) An STT‐based antibacterial nanomaterial (Guan et al., 2024).

PTT employs a light source to activate a photothermal agent, which generates heat to damage bacteria (Zhi et al., 2020). Tan et al. demonstrated titanium red phosphorus, as a photothermal agent, effectively eradicates biofilms at 50°C using near‐infrared (808 nm) PTT in both in vitro and in vivo environments (Tan et al., 2018). However, PTT generally requires high temperatures to effectively kill bacteria, which can risk thermal damage to surrounding healthy tissues. Therefore, it is essential to carefully adjust parameters and optimize treatment methods to minimize potential side effects on healthy tissues while effectively targeting and eliminating bacteria.

In biological systems, bacterial and cellular respiration often involves electron transfer. External electrical signals can disrupt this natural electron transfer process in bacteria, leading to oxidative stress, increased ROS, ultimately impaired bacterial growth (Wang et al., 2017b). Tang et al. engineered a hierarchical functional structure on a titanium surface, integrating rare earth nanoparticles (RE NPs) and gold nanoparticles (Au NPs) on titanium‐loaded TiO2 nanotube arrays (NTA) (Tang et al., 2024). The anodically patterned TiO2 NTA served as an ideal scaffold to uniformly distributing functional particles and enhancing adhesion to titanium implants. Au NPs, known for their high biocompatibility, positively affect osseointegration, while RE NPs absorb light in the NIR‐I (800–1000 nm) and NIR‐II (1000–1150 nm) ranges and convert it into visible light, working in synergy with plasmonic Au NPs. The up‐conversion effect boosts the energy of photoelectrons. This innovative approach exploits the unique properties of RE NPs and Au NPs to disrupt bacterial growth through oxidative stress induced by external electrical signals, offering a promising avenue for developing antibacterial materials and enhancing the effectiveness of titanium implants.

Ultrasound offers superior tissue penetration and the safety of mechanical waves. Ultrasound‐driven sonodynamic therapy (SDT) is particularly promising for eradicating deep bacterial infections due to its non‐invasive nature, localized irradiation, and high tissue penetration capabilities. Pang et al. developed an intelligent nano‐liposome platform using maltose‐modified cholesterol and bacteria‐responsive lipid compositions to precisely deliver the potent sonosensitizer hematoporphyrin monomethyl ether (HMME) (Pang et al., 2019). In a separate study, Wang et al. proposed a multi‐immunotherapy strategy combined with SDT for treating biofilm‐associated infections in a time‐sequenced manner (Wang et al., 2024). However, the limited accumulation of sonosensitizers at infection sites, hypoxic microenvironments, and rapid oxygen consumption during SDT significantly reduce its therapeutic efficacy. To address these issues, Sun et al. introduced a switchable ultrasound nanozyme system designed for the controlled generation of catalytic oxygen and sonosensitizer‐mediated ROS during ultrasound activation (Figure 4B) (Sun et al., 2020). This approach aims to overcome hypoxia‐related barriers and improve SDT efficacy. By integrating advanced delivery platforms and strategies to mitigate hypoxia, researchers are working to optimize SDT for treating deep‐seated bacterial infections, potentially leading to more effective and targeted antibacterial treatments. Another strategy to overcome hypoxia barriers is the use of self‐supplying oxygen nanomaterials. Chu et al. developed a platform combining TiO2 nanorod arrays with oxygen‐supplying CaO2 nanoparticles, which continuously release oxygen to effectively eliminate bacteria on implant surfaces and within neutrophils (Figure 4C) (Chu et al., 2024).

Similarly, sonothermal therapy (STT) shows considerable promise for treating implant‐related infections by overcoming the limited penetration of PTT. However, achieving precise ultrasound heating at the implant surface presents significant challenges. Inspired by the noise reduction capabilities of porous acoustic metamaterials, these materials offer a novel approach to realizing STT. Guan et al. introduced an “acoustic trapping effect” that “capture” ultrasound waves using a superstructured TiO2 porous coating on titanium implants (Figure 4C) (Guan et al., 2024). This advanced coating enabled targeted sound absorption and efficient sonothermal conversion, surpassing the non‐specific heating of traditional coatings. Theoretical calculations and experimental results validated the sonothermal conversion mechanism, which was also confirmed in medical polyether ether ketone (PEEK) superstructured porous coatings, demonstrating its versatility. Notably, this acoustic metamaterial “trap” effectively captured bacteria and enhanced local sonothermal effects, achieving impressive antibacterial rates (Staphylococcus aureus, 99.69%; Escherichia coli, 99.58%) under low‐power ultrasound. Additionally, the superstructured TiO2 coating mimicked human bone and exhibited outstanding osseointegration. This study provides valuable insights into developing acoustically responsive surfaces and paves the way for new applications of acoustic metamaterials in medicine.

ENDOGENOUS MIMICKING AGENTS

Endogenous mimicking agents are synthetic molecules designed to imitate the function of naturally occurring compounds within biological systems. These agents can replicate the activity of enzymes, hormones, or other cellular components to regulate physiological processes. By mimicking natural processes, these agents can potentially offer targeted and efficient interventions with reduced side effects compared to traditional treatments. Therefore, endogenous mimicking agents are gaining momentum in the fight against bacteria. In biofilms, microorganisms are embedded within a self‐produced extracellular polymeric substance (EPS) matrix. This matrix typically consists of polysaccharides, proteins, and extracellular DNA (eDNA). As the longest molecule within the EPS, eDNA acts as a bridge, enhancing initial bacterial surface adhesion and subsequent cell aggregation, as well as linking bacteria with other EPS components to form a mature network, ultimately maintaining all components together. Swartjes et al. proposed preventing biofilm formation by cleaving this critical EPS component using DNase, thereby dismantling pre‐formed biofilms and rendering the encased bacteria susceptible to antibiotics (Swartjes et al., 2013). They demonstrated that a DNase I coating on biomaterial implants and devices provides a timely and effective new method for biofilm prevention. However, to prevent biofilm formation, due to the lack of long‐term activity and recoverability, there is a need to continuously add DNase to new generations of bacteria, leading to high costs. Moreover, the high environmental sensitivity and low operational stability of natural enzymes, coupled with the slowed infiltration and diffusion of biofilms and proteolytic extracellular enzymes, significantly impact the activity of subsequently added DNase. Inspired by the mechanism and molecular structure of natural enzymes, chemists have recently invested substantial efforts in constructing numerous inexpensive enzyme mimics with high catalytic turnover rates and stability (Ai et al., 2021; Ai et al., 2022). Herget et al. demonstrated that CeO2‐x nanorods exhibit intrinsic haloperoxidase‐like activity through the catalytic bromination of organic signalling molecules, thereby showing antibacterial capabilities (Figure 5A) (Herget et al., 2017). Chen et al. described the construction of a DNase‐mimicking artificial enzyme (DMAE) for anti‐biofilm applications (Figure 5B) (Chen et al., 2016). By confining multiple cerium (IV) complexes to the surface of colloidal magnetic Fe3O4/SiO2 core/shell particles, they obtained a robust and recyclable artificial enzyme that exhibited high cleavage capability against model substrates and eDNA. Compared to the high environmental sensitivity of natural DNase in anti‐biofilm applications, DMAE demonstrated better operational stability and easier recyclability. When DMAE was coated on substrate surfaces, biofilm formation was inhibited for extended periods, and DMAE performed exceptionally well in dispersing established biofilms of various ages. Lastly, the presence of DMAE significantly enhanced the efficiency of traditional antibiotics in killing biofilm‐encased bacteria and eliminating biofilms. Antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) are promising candidates for treating various diseases due to their multifunctional properties. With the increasing number of multidrug‐resistant (MDR) bacteria, AMPs are viewed as alternatives to antibiotics. The importance of AMPs lies in their efficacy over millions of years. In humans, there are several defensins, but only one gene encoding the defensin LL‐37. However, the natural peptide has drawbacks, such as long sequences (high cost) and instability against proteases (loss of activity). Su et al. engineered17BIPHE2 derived from LL‐37 (Su et al., 2020), which surpasses the parent molecule in many aspects, including robust anti‐biofilm and antibacterial activity under different salt, pH, and medium conditions. The hydrophilic and hydrophobic amino acids of LL‐37 are distributed above and below the helical backbone, while the hydrophilic and hydrophobic amino acids of the peptide W379 are concentrated in two regions (Figure 5C). This engineered peptide can selectively attack bacterial cell membranes, thus inducing precise and effective bacterial elimination.

NEW DELIVERY METHODS

Skin infections caused by bacteria, viruses, and fungi are challenging to treat with traditional topical drug administration due to poor permeability through the stratum corneum (Lee et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2024a). Similarly, extracellular polymeric substances, serving as physical barriers, often hinder the penetration of antimicrobial therapeutics into biofilms (Caffarel‐Salvador et al., 2015). This results in low bioavailability of drugs at the infection site and a lack of sustained release. Emerging microneedle (MN) patches have become a promising delivery method for various antibacterial, antifungal, and antiviral therapies. MN patches, composed of miniature needles, are a promising tool for penetrating the stratum corneum and delivering antimicrobial agents to the dermis in a minimally invasive manner. Because these micron‐scale projections are typically only 100 to 1000 μm in length, they can pierce the stratum corneum while having limited interaction with nerve endings in the dermis (Figure 6A). Compared to traditional needles, they cause less pain, tissue damage, and skin inflammation, making them an ideal choice for patients with chronic conditions (Jamaledin et al., 2020).

(B) Microneedles carry multiple drugs to combat biofilms through multiple mechanisms (González García et al., 2019). (C) Triggered release of antimicrobial peptide from microneedle patches (Su et al., 2023). (D) A delivery system consisting of nanofiber mats and microneedles (Su et al., 2020). (E) Co‐delivery of the engineered antimicrobial peptide W379 and the monoclonal antibody Anti‐PBP2a by microneedle (Su et al., 2024).

González reported a self‐sterilizing dissolving nanosilver microneedle patch made of carboxymethylcellulose that inhibits the growth of microbial pathogens at the insertion site (Figure 6B) (González García et al., 2019). Su et al. developed a NIR‐responsive microneedle patch designed for the on‐demand release of antimicrobial peptides to treat wound biofilms (Figure 6C) (Su et al., 2023). This innovative patch encapsulates IR780 iodide, a photothermal conversion agent, and a molecularly engineered antimicrobial peptide, W379, within dissolvable polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP) microneedles. These microneedles are then coated with the phase‐change material 1‐tetradecanol (TD). Upon being placed in a wound environment, either in vitro or in vivo, and exposed to NIR light, the embedded IR780 induces photothermal conversion, causing the TD to melt. This process leads to the dissolution of the PVP microneedles, thereby releasing the loaded W379 peptide into the surrounding area, such as the solution, biofilm, or wound bed. Compared to traditional microneedle patches, the NIR light‐responsive microneedle patch offers programmable release of antimicrobial peptides, demonstrating higher antibacterial efficacy in vitro.

Microneedle technology can also be combined with electrospinning to provide a more sustained antibacterial capability. Su et al. developed a biphasic dressing, one side of which consists of a dissolvable microneedle array and the other side of a nanofiber mat. This design effectively delivers database‐designed antimicrobial peptides to both the interior and exterior of biofilms (Figure 5D) (Su et al., 2020). The antimicrobial peptides released from the dissolvable microneedles and nanofiber mat work together to eliminate biofilms, while the peptides released from the nanofiber mat subsequently prevent biofilm recurrence. Building on this success, the team further developed a biphasic scaffold composed of dissolvable microneedles and a nanofiber membrane, capable of simultaneously delivering different combinations of three antimicrobial agents (e.g., Ag+, Ga3+, and vancomycin) (Su et al., 2021). Subsequently, the team combined the engineered antimicrobial peptide W379 and the monoclonal antibody Anti‐PBP2a and seamlessly integrated them into the microneedle system to achieve enhanced collective anti‐biofilm efficacy (Figure 5E) (Su et al., 2024). These biphasic scaffolds combine multiple antimicrobial agents with different mechanisms of action, demonstrating enhanced antibacterial efficacy. This innovative strategy holds promise for combating wound biofilms, especially those composed of multi‐drug‐resistant bacteria and pathogens. Its potential clinical translation could significantly improve the quality of wound care.

CONCLUSIONS AND FUTURE DIRECTIONS

The treatment of biofilms, which are communities of microorganisms that adhere to surfaces and are encased in a protective matrix, presents significant challenges due to their resistance to conventional antimicrobial treatments. This review examines the latest developments in tackling biofilm‐related infections, highlighting four cutting‐edge strategies: surface anti‐adhesion technologies, electrical debridement techniques, advanced nanomaterials, and novel drug delivery methods. Research on nanoparticles for biofilm‐related infections includes ROS‐generating nanoparticles that disrupt microbial cells, thermal nanoparticles that use heat to weaken biofilms, oxygen‐producing nanoparticles that address hypoxic environments, and endogenous mimetics that simulate biological defence mechanisms.

Notably, 3D printing has emerged as a widely adopted method for creating mammalian cell carriers with intricate microenvironments, particularly in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine. Moreover, engineered 3D biofilms are increasingly utilized in antimicrobial susceptibility testing (Lazarus et al., 2024). In parallel, phage‐derived lysins represent a new class of antimicrobials capable of rapidly killing bacteria, including antibiotic‐resistant strains. These engineered lysins have shown considerable promise in disrupting biofilms (Carratalá et al., 2024; Vander Elst et al., 2023). In addition, continued interdisciplinary research and development are essential to overcome current limitations and translate these innovative approaches into effective, widely used treatments for biofilm‐associated infections. For example, CRISPR‐Cas technology is well known for its gene editing capabilities, and scientists are also exploring its potential to target and dismantle biofilms (Alshammari et al., 2023; Gou et al., 2019; Nie et al., 2022). Engineered phages combined with nanoions can be designed to target specific bacterial strains within biofilms (Azeredo et al., 2021; Tinoco et al., 2016). And phage enzymes (such as lysozymes) can degrade the biofilm matrix and enhance the permeability and effectiveness of antimicrobial agents. Advanced wound dressings embedded with sensors and responsive materials can monitor the infection status and release antimicrobial agents based on specific triggers. These smart dressings can provide real‐time feedback and deliver treatment accurately when and where it is needed. Additionally, the development of various hydrogel materials is advancing rapidly (Wang et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2022; Xiong et al., 2020). Hydrogels embedded with antimicrobial agents are particularly useful as wound dressings, offering sustained antibacterial activity while promoting wound healing (Annabi et al., 2017; Li et al., 2011). Incorporating these technologies into clinical practice will hopefully revolutionize the treatment of biofilms.
