Development of a resting myocardial contrast echocardiography multi-indicator diagnostic model for coronary heart disease: a preliminary study
Authors: Hui-Fang Lu, Xiao-Yan Huo, Bing-Yu Zhu, Feng Guo, Kang Ding, Xin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStudies have demonstrated a good concordance between myocardial contrast echocardiography (MCE) and single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT) results for myocardial perfusion assessment. Moreover, when combined with multiple indicators, MCE has shown superior diagnostic ability for detecting myocardial hypoperfusion compared to SPECT. However, no MCE multi-indicator diagnostic model has been reported to date. We aimed to develop a diagnostic model using resting MCE for detecting myocardial hypoperfusion.MethodsResting MCE and myocardial SPECT examinations were performed on 163 patients with clinically suspected or confirmed coronary heart disease (CHD) based on coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) or coronary angiography (CAG). A total of 360 myocardial segments were included in the MCE quantitative analysis (180 normal and 180 abnormal segments), categorized according to SPECT results. Key parameters—time to peak intensity (TP), duration time (T), peak intensity (PI), and myocardial blood flow (A × β)—were calculated to determine cutoff values for myocardial hypoperfusion. Logistic regression and receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves were used to construct a multi-indicator MCE diagnostic model for myocardial hypoperfusion and evaluate its performance.ResultsThe TP and T values of abnormal segments were significantly longer than those of normal segments (P<0.01). Conversely, the PI and A × β values were significantly lower in abnormal segments than in normal segments (P<0.01). The cutoff values for predicting myocardial hypoperfusion were as follows: TP =6.54 s, T =13.36 s, PI =6.67 dB, and A × β =8.70 dB2/s. The diagnostic model was defined as follows: logit P = 0.769 × TP + 0.871 × T − 0.662 × PI − 0.301 × A × β − 8.464. The model achieved a sensitivity and specificity of 97.9% and 96.8% for diagnosing myocardial hypoperfusion, respectively.ConclusionsThe resting multi-indicator MCE diagnostic model demonstrated performance comparable to that of SPECT in detecting myocardial hypoperfusion. This model shows promise for CHD screening, follow-up, and prognostic evaluation.

Introduction

Coronary microcirculation dysfunction (CMD) is closely associated with myocardial ischemia (1,2), which can occur in the early stages and persist throughout the course of coronary heart disease (CHD). Although occlusion of an epicardial coronary artery can be relieved through percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) (3), some myocardial tissues may remain hypoperfused due to CMD (3,4). Therefore, evaluating CMD is essential for CHD screening, PCI follow-up, and prognostic assessment.

Noninvasive methods for CMD detection include myocardial contrast echocardiography (MCE), myocardial single-photon emission computed tomography (SPECT), and positron emission tomography (PET), among others (5). Although PET is considered the gold standard for myocardial perfusion evaluation, SPECT is more commonly used in clinical practice (6,7). Compared to SPECT, MCE is more convenient and free of radiation. Studies have demonstrated good concordance between MCE and SPECT results for myocardial perfusion assessment. Moreover, when combined with multiple MCE indicators such as A + β, A + A × β, and β + A × β, MCE has shown superior diagnostic ability for CHD compared to SPECT (8,9). However, no MCE multi-indicator diagnostic model has been reported to date. Therefore, we aimed to develop an MCE multi-indicator diagnostic model combining four variables: time to peak intensity (TP), duration time (T), peak intensity (PI), and myocardial blood flow (A × β). Our objective was to explore a convenient and noninvasive method for evaluating myocardial perfusion for clinical option. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-474/rc).

Methods

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Sixth Medical Center of PLA General Hospital (No. HZKY-PJ-2022-11) and informed consent was taken from all the patients.

Patients

We enrolled 163 patients (118 men and 45 women) with clinically suspected or confirmed CHD based on abnormal findings from coronary computed tomography angiography (CCTA) or coronary angiography (CAG). All patients underwent myocardial SPECT and resting MCE examinations at The Sixth Medical Center of PLA General Hospital between May 2022 and June 2024. The interval between the MCE and myocardial SPECT examinations was less than 15 days. The patients’ ages ranged from 31 to 75 years, with an average age of 60.35±12.23 years. General clinical data, including age, sex, body mass index, and other relevant variables, were recorded.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) allergy to the SonoVue contrast agent; (II) congenital heart disease or severe pulmonary hypertension; (III) ventricular arrhythmia; (IV) pulmonary embolism or adult respiratory distress syndrome; and (V) pregnancy.

Myocardial perfusion SPECT protocol

Each patient underwent 2-day 99mTc-MIBI SPECT myocardial perfusion imaging. On the first day, resting imaging was performed 1 h after the injection of the imaging agent. On the second day, the Bruce protocol was applied. Patients underwent step-by-step treadmill exercise, starting at 25 W and increasing by 25 W every 3 min. The myocardial imaging agent 99mTc-MIBI (25–30 mCi) was injected into the cubital vein once the termination target was reached. Subsequently, patients continued exercising for an additional 1–2 min before gradually discontinuing the exercise. Stress imaging was conducted 1 h after the injection of the imaging agent.

MCE examination

We used a Philips IE-Elite ultrasonic diagnostic instrument (probe S5-1, frequency 1.7–3.4 MHz, contrast-MCE program) for MCE. The contrast agent was prepared by mixing 59 mg of SonoVue (produced by Bracco Company, Milan, Italy) with 5 mL of saline (included in the SonoVue package), resulting in a milky-white sulfur hexafluoride suspension. Before contrast injection, preparations included antecubital vein puncture, electrocardiogram (ECG) connection, oxygen bag setup, and an emergency cart.

Routine echocardiography was performed first, and left ventricular wall motion was thoroughly observed. Standard apical four-chamber, two-chamber, and three-chamber views were obtained before switching to a low machine index real-time contrast mode. The contrast agent was then injected at a slow and steady rate of 2 mL/2 min (10). Once a clear left ventricular image without sound attenuation was achieved, the “flash” mode was activated to eliminate microbubbles. Continuous myocardial reperfusion images were acquired for 20 cardiac cycles following the completion of the flash mode.

Dynamic post-flash images of 20 consecutive cardiac cycles were then reviewed. Cases of normal and abnormal perfusion were identified based on myocardial SPECT reports. Normal and abnormal perfusion segments were selected through an independent double-blind MCE visual review conducted by two physicians. A total of 360 myocardial segments were included for quantitative analysis of MCE time-intensity curves, with 180 abnormal segments selected from 105 abnormal cases and 180 normal segments from 58 normal cases.

The Q-LAB analysis software (produced by Philips) was used to automatically generate time-intensity curves for each myocardial wall, yielding the following parameters: TP, T, PI, and calculated A × β.

Sitting blood pressure, heart rate, and left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) were routinely measured before and after contrast imaging.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS 26.0 software. TP, T, PI, and A × β were used to evaluate normal and abnormal myocardial perfusion. Receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to assess the diagnostic performance of the model, based on the area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, and specificity, as well as to calculate the cutoff values for MCE parameters distinguishing normal and abnormal myocardial perfusion. Abnormal myocardial perfusion was treated as the dependent variable (0= no, 1= yes), whereas the four MCE parameters were included as independent variables in a logistic regression model. This approach was used to develop a multi-indicator combined diagnostic model for MCE.

This study included a total of 163 participants, comprising 118 men and 45 women. CAG and CCTA data

Among the 163 patients, 97 were confirmed to have CHD based on CAG, whereas 66 were suspected to have CHD based on CCTA. Of these, 75 underwent PCI, and 12 ultimately underwent coronary artery bypass grafting.

Myocardial SPECT results

Myocardial SPECT revealed 105 abnormal cases with 369 segmental myocardial perfusion abnormalities and 58 normal cases with 986 segmental myocardial perfusion normalities.

Quantitative analysis of MCE parameters

Abnormal segments exhibited significantly longer TP (8.28±2.52 vs. 4.76±1.18 s) and T (16.44±2.91 vs. 10.92±2.13 s) but significantly lower PI (5.10±1.82 vs. 8.54±2.50 dB) and A × β (4.86±3.02 vs. 18.55±9.46 dB2/s) compared to normal segments (P<0.01, Table 2).

ROC and logistic regression analyses

The cutoff value, sensitivity, and specificity of TP, T, PI, and A × β for predicting myocardial perfusion abnormalities were 6.54 s, 74.3%, and 92.8%; 13.36 s, 86.6%, and 89.6%; 6.67 dB, 82.4%, and 78.4%; and 8.70 dB2/s, 89.8%, and 88.8%, respectively.

The regression equation based on TP, T, PI, and A × β is as follows: logit(P)=0.769×TP+0.871×T−0.662×PI−0.301×A×β−8.464.

The AUC of the combined diagnostic model was 0.993, with a sensitivity of 97.9% and specificity of 96.8% for diagnosing abnormal myocardial perfusion (Figure 1).

Discussion

Efficiency of the resting MCE multi-indicator diagnostic model

MCE reflects capillary volume through peak microbubble signal intensity while qualitatively assessing myocardial perfusion based on signal intensity. Delayed myocardial enhancement or filling defects (Figure 2), caused by reduced blood flow velocity and increased vascular resistance, explain the ability of MCE to detect CHD (11,12). In this study, PI and A × β of abnormal segments were significantly lower compared to those of normal perfusion segments. Additionally, TP and T of abnormal segments were significantly longer than those of normal segments. Therefore, we deduced that each of the four variables is valuable for detecting myocardial perfusion dysfunction. When these variables were combined, the diagnostic value in predicting hypoperfusion was higher, as ultimately verified. The sensitivity and specificity of the MCE diagnostic model for detecting myocardial perfusion abnormalities were 97.9% and 96.8%, respectively, which were equivalent to those of SPECT. Compared with SPECT, resting MCE is radiation-free, more convenient, and more economical, making it suitable for bedside examination of critically ill patients. Moreover, due to its good repeatability, MCE is preferable for preoperative diagnosis and post-therapy follow-up. It is also likely to be utilized for CHD screening in patients with chest pain, detecting myocardial no-reflow, and for prognostic determination following PCI, however, this requires multi-center clinical data support.

Applicability of the MCE diagnostic model in detecting microvascular angina

According to our preliminary research, resting MCE demonstrates prolonged TP and T in patients with microvascular angina (13). In this study, TP and T were significantly longer in segments with abnormal myocardial perfusion compared to those with normal perfusion (P<0.01). In five cases, CAG detected coronary slow flow (CSF) without myocardial perfusion abnormalities on SPECT; however, MCE revealed prolonged TP and T (Figure 3). This may be attributed to the combined effects of increased microvascular resistance and the adhesion of inflammatory vascular endothelial factors to the microbubbles (14-16). These findings suggest that the resting MCE diagnostic model, which incorporates the time variables TP and T, could potentially identify myocardial hypoperfusion in cases of CSF. Nevertheless, further research is necessary to evaluate its feasibility and clinical application.

Safety of resting MCE

In this study, no significant differences were observed in heart rate or blood pressure before and after contrast imaging (P>0.05). Moreover, no severe allergic reactions were noted. The reported incidence of severe allergic reactions to SonoVue is 0.009% (17). The present study confirmed that resting MCE, which requires only a 2-mL dose of SonoVue, is safe and clinically advantageous. Additionally, to address the possibility of delayed allergic reactions, the elbow vein channel should be preserved for 30 min after the MCE procedure.

Comparison of LVEF before and after MCE

In this study, no significant difference was observed in LVEF before and after contrast imaging (P>0.05). However, image quality significantly improved, with endocardial boundaries being distinctly displayed after contrast injection—an essential property for the accurate assessment of left ventricular systolic function in patients with CHD, and for detecting subendocardial myocardial ischemia left occurring in the early stage of CHD (18,19).

Research limitations

This study has some limitations. Firstly, the sample size was small, and the number of normal perfusion cases was relatively low. Additionally, varying degrees of interference from human factors were present during the visual inspection and quantitative analysis of MCE and myocardial SPECT images. Lastly, exercise stress echocardiography was not performed in our study. Dobutamine stress testing in conjunction with contrast echocardiography can be used to assess ischemia, myocardial perfusion, and myocardial viability, which can improve the assessment of the severity of coronary disease and enhance test sensitivity (20). Chest shape assessment was of clinical significance in the diagnosis of coronary disease and stratification of risk, but it was not included either (21).

Conclusions

To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to develop a resting MCE multi-indicator diagnostic model and confirm that its performance is equivalent to that of SPECT and superior to that of single-indicator MCE in detecting myocardial hypoperfusion. It shows substantial potential for CHD screening, PCI follow-up, and prognostic determination.
