Upper abdominal pain in a patient with a history of laparoscopic adjustable gastric banding
Authors: Jae Yong Park
Published: 2025

Quiz

A 41-year-old female presented to the emergency department with abdominal pain in the left upper quadrant (LUQ) that began earlier that day. She had undergone laparoscopic adjustable gastric banding (LAGB) 10 years previously for morbid obesity and had not been followed up for the last two years. Vital signs were notable for fever (39.1 °C) and tachycardia (125 beats per minute) with a blood pressure of 134/80 mmHg. Physical examination revealed tenderness in the LUQ area. Laboratory results were unremarkable, except for an elevated C-reactive protein level at 34.8 mg/L (reference, 0–5.0). Abdominal radiography revealed an adjustment port located in the left lower quadrant, with the connecting tube extending toward the gastric band (Fig. 1). Abdominal computed tomography (CT) showed inflammatory soft tissue encasement of the prosthetic tube in the LUQ, suggesting tractitis along the tube (Fig. 2). Subsequent upper endoscopy revealed a visible gastric band protruding into the gastric lumen surrounded by hyperemic mucosa and areas of superficial ulceration near the gastric cardia (Fig. 3A), with a connection tube penetrating the mid-body anterior side of the gastric wall (Fig. 3B, C).

Abdominal X-ray showing a radiopaque gastric band in the left upper quadrant. The adjustment port is located in the left lower abdomen, and the connecting tube between the port and the gastric band is visualized.

Abdominal computed tomography findings. (A) Intragastric location of the gastric band (arrowheads). (B) Penetration of the connecting tube through the gastric wall (arrowheads). (C) Inflammatory soft tissue encasement surrounding the prosthetic tube in the left upper quadrant abdominal wall (arrowheads).

Endoscopic findings. (A) Complete intragastric migration of the gastric band near the cardia, with surrounding hyperemic mucosa and superficial ulcerations. (B) The connecting tube is seen penetrating the anterior wall of the mid-gastric body. (C) The intragastric portion of the connecting tube forms a loop within the gastric lumen.

What was the most likely diagnosis?

Answer

The patient was diagnosed with gastric band erosion and intragastric migration. This is a recognized late complication of LAGB, reported in up to 0.2% to 11% of cases.1,2 Band erosion, also referred to as intragastric migration or band-related gastro-gastric fistula, occurs when the band gradually erodes through the gastric wall, leading to partial or complete migration into the gastric lumen.

Risk factors include microinjuries at the time of band insertion, chronic mechanical pressure with local ischemia, overinflation of the band, ingestion of large food boluses, and use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs.1-5 Patients may present with nonspecific symptoms such as epigastric pain, nausea, vomiting, dysphagia, weight regain, or port-site infection.1,3 However, some cases remain asymptomatic and are discovered incidentally during endoscopy or imaging. Endoscopic findings typically reveal a visible band within the gastric lumen that is often surrounded by mucosal inflammation or ulceration. The diagnosis is confirmed endoscopically, and imaging, such as CT or upper gastrointestinal (GI) series, may provide additional anatomical detail.6

Management should be tailored according to the extent of erosion and clinical status of the patient. Complete migration into the lumen permits endoscopic removal in selected cases, whereas partial erosion usually requires surgical intervention, typically via laparoscopy.1,2 In this case, the patient underwent laparoscopic removal of the band without complications and showed complete resolution of symptoms during follow-up. In recent years, various types of advanced endoscopic bariatric therapies have been introduced for obesity treatment, substituting LAGB.7-9 With the global decline in the use of LAGB, these complications are becoming less frequent. However, clinicians should remain vigilant when encountering patients with prior bariatric surgery and new upper GI symptoms.
