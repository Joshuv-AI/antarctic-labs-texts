Impaired antibody responses to heterologous ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 and BNT162b2 vaccination in individuals with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Wipawee Saenwongsa, Sudarat Kingpha, Phonnipha Saythanu, Sopita Sopila, Ganjana Lertmemongkolchai, Chidchamai Kewcharoenwong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The COVID-19 pandemic has caused many deaths worldwide, especially in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM). Owing to the association of type 2 DM with immune dysfunction, we evaluated the impact of DM on the humoral immune responses induced by inactivated (Sinovac, SV), viral vector (ChAdOx1 nCoV-19, Az) and mRNA (BNT162b2, BNT) vaccines, compared with those in non-DM individuals. The levels of anti-spike (S) and neutralizing antibodies (nAbs) against the S1 domain/receptor-binding domain (RBD) of severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) before and after COVID-19 vaccination were determined. The immune response in non-DM individuals heterologously immunized with the Az-BNT regimen was more robust than that in those immunized with other regimens. Notably, individuals with type 2 DM presented reduced anti-S antibody levels following vaccination with the Az-BNT regimen at 30 and 90 days post-vaccination. Furthermore, a rapid decline in nAb levels in DM individuals was noted on day 90 post-boost for all regimens. Thus, a shorter interval between boosters might be necessary for DM individuals. These findings could inform the selection of optimal vaccination protocols for individuals with or without DM and contribute to the development of an efficacious COVID-19 vaccine for individuals with underlying health conditions, particularly DM.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20324-y.

Introduction

Severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), the causative agent of coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), has spread worldwide and caused a severe pandemic1,2. The development of effective and safe vaccines is urgently needed to control and end this pandemic. Ideal for vaccine design would include good targets for the immune system and induction of both humoral and cellular immunity. Moreover, it is widely thought that vaccine-induced neutralizing antibodies (nAbs) targeting the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the SARS-CoV-2 spike (S) protein constitute a plausible mechanism of protection, as they provide nearly complete protection against rechallenge in animal studies3. However, several studies have shown that both nAbs and the T-cell response also play critical protective roles during SARS-CoV-2 infection4–7.

To date, many COVID-19 vaccines have been licenced for use, and their robust efficacy has been reported8–10. The initial COVID-19 vaccination regimens began to be implemented in February 2021 and included a two-dose primary course of a whole inactivated virus (SV, Sinovac) vaccine, a viral vector ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 (Az, AstraZeneca) vaccine, or an mRNA BNT162b2 (BNT, Pfizer–BioNTech) vaccine. To enhance the immune response to viruses with specific mutations found in concerning variants, a heterologous vaccination strategy was implemented in Thailand. In July 2021, the Thai government suspended the use of regimens with the first two doses of the SV vaccine because of inefficient protection against the SARS-CoV-2 delta variant. When people received the first dose of the SV vaccine and had an appointment date for a second dose after suspension was initiated, the second dose was promptly switched to other vaccines11. Moreover, the regimens of the first two doses of Az were suspended in September 2021 because the long interval between doses, which takes time to induce an immunity. People who received the first dose of the Az vaccine before the suspension was initiated continued to receive the same Az vaccine for the second dose. However, people who had not received the first dose before the suspension was initiated had to start with other regimens instead11.

In Thailand, a retrospective cohort study using national registration data revealed that 2-dose heterologous vaccination with either SV-Az or Az-BNT provided approximately 50% of the initial protection against severe and fatal COVID-1912. Moreover, a study in Korea revealed that heterologous booster vaccination after primary vaccination (Az-BNT) increased nAb titres and provided a greater level of protection against the omicron variant than primary vaccination alone13. However, there are multiple factors that increase or decrease COVID-19 vaccine effectiveness, such as host, vaccine access, viral variant and immune factors10,14.

In addition, several studies have shown that type 2 diabetes mellitus (DM) and the use of antidiabetic medications impair the immune response to pathogen infection or vaccination15–18. Individuals with DM are known to be at an increased risk of severe COVID-19 and there is a growing concern that their altered immune status might affect vaccine efficacy. Given the high prevalence of T2DM (WHO, 2024), understanding their immune response to different COVID-19 vaccine platforms is critical for informing personalized vaccination strategies. Moreover, immunization regimens and the effects of COVID-19 vaccines on DM patients remain unclear and require further exploration. Here, we aimed to investigate anti-S and anti-nAb levels produced in response to COVID-19 vaccination, especially in individuals with or without type 2 DM.

Results

Descriptive characteristics

The demographic data revealed no significant difference (p > 0.05) between the two groups with respect to age, sex, or body mass index (BMI). The fasting blood sugar levels of DM individuals were significantly greater than those of non-DM individuals. Individuals with type 2 DM were prescribed metformin only as frequently as they were prescribed metformin plus glipizide (46.5% and 48.8%, respectively), and a few DM individuals were prescribed metformin plus insulin (4.7%) (Table 1). The duration of antidiabetic drug use was recorded as the date when the DM individuals started taking antidiabetic drugs until the date of blood collection. In addition, the longest duration of antidiabetic drug use occurred in individuals with DM who were prescribed metformin plus insulin. Among the DM individuals enrolled in this study, no satisfactory glucose control, defined by a glycated haemoglobin (HbA1c) level < 6.5%, was reported in this study.

Impaired anti-S levels against Az-BNT at both day 30 and day 90 post-vaccination in type 2 DM individuals

In addition, current evidence on the effectiveness of COVID-19 vaccines in DM individuals is insufficient. To investigate the impact of diabetes on the effectiveness of the COVID-19 vaccine, we investigated the antibody response against various COVID-19 vaccine regimens in DM individuals compared with that in non-DM individuals at baseline and following the first and second doses of various COVID-19 vaccine regimens. Five groups of vaccine regimens were examined in this study (SV-SV, SV-Az, Az-Az, Az-BNT, and BNT-BNT). There were no significant differences in age, BMI and DM rate among the groups of vaccine regimens (Supplementary Table 1).

Anti-Spike RBD (anti-S) levels and neutralizing antibody were assessed at each time point, as indicated in Fig. 1. Both assays provide distinct, yet complementary, information about the humoral immune response to vaccination. While anti-S levels offer a broader picture of antibody production, neutralizing antibody provides a critical measure of the functional effectiveness of that response. Analyzing both in parallel allows for a more nuanced interpretation of vaccine-induced immunity and its potential to protection against infection.

Anti-S levels were evaluated via an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay (Elecsys, Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany19. For the long-term response, anti-S levels were compared between the DM and non-DM groups at baseline and at 30 days post-prime and 30 and 90 days post-boost (Supplementary Fig. 1). We found that all individuals had no anti-S response at baseline. A single dose of BNT or Az elicited an anti-S response at 30 days after vaccination, whereas 2 doses of SV were needed to elicit an anti-S response (Supplementary Fig. 1). All regimens elicited an anti-S response on day 30 after 2 doses and then waned over time at a consistent rate until day 90 post-boost (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1). Interestingly, DM individuals presented a significantly diminished anti-S response to the Az-BNT prime-boost regimen than non-DM individuals did at both day 30 (Fig. 2a) and day 90 (Fig. 2b) post-boost. However, heterologous Az-BNT and homologous BNT-BNT prime-boost regimens were the best regimens for individuals with DM in terms of the elicited anti-S response. In addition, the homologous BNT-BNT and heterologous Az-BNT prime-boost regimens were also the best for eliciting anti-S responses in non-DM individuals. For the other regimens, all the individual groups presented similar anti-S levels (Fig. 2 and Supplementary Fig. 1).

Moreover, on day 90 after 2 doses, only the Az-BNT and BNT-BNT regimens induced persistent anti-S responses at greater than 1,000 U/mL in non-DM individuals (3,490 and 1,590 U/mL, respectively), whereas only the BNT-BNT regimen did so in DM (1,530 U/mL) (Fig. 2b).

The results from the multivariate analyses revealed that vaccine regimen was a significant determinant of immune responses at both Day 30 and Day 90 post-vaccination, after adjusting for age, BMI, DM, and gender. Specifically, for Anti-S antibody levels, participants receiving AZ-AZ, AZ-BNT, and BNT-BNT regimens demonstrated significantly higher levels compared to the SV-SV group at Day 30 (Adjusted β = 857.89, 2186.08, and 2916.49, respectively; all p < 0.05). These differences persisted at Day 90, with the AZ-AZ, AZ-BNT, and BNT-BNT groups maintaining significantly elevated Anti-S antibody levels (Adjusted β = 300.81, 893.29, and 1749.25, respectively; all p < 0.05) relative to the SV-SV group. In these analyses of Anti-S levels, covariates including age, BMI, DM, and gender were not statistically significant at either time point (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3).

Taken together, these data implied that compared with non-DM individuals, DM individuals had an impaired anti-S response against the heterologous Az-BNT regimen both on day 30 and day 90 post-vaccination. Only the BNT-BNT regimen resulted in a persistent anti-S response in patients with a DM greater than 1000 U/mL at 3 months post-boosting. However, heterologous Az-BNT and homologous BNT-BNT regimens were the best for DM individuals in terms of the elicited anti-S response. Moreover, the antibody response induced by all vaccine regimens waned over time, and reboosting was thus still needed.

Impaired neutralizing antibody response in individuals with type 2 DM

Previous studies have shown that anti-SARS-CoV-2 neutralizing antibody (nAb) binds to the N-terminal domain of the S protein of SARS-CoV-2 to block the binding and fusion of this virus, and nAbs whose production is induced by vaccines or infection play crucial roles in controlling SARS-CoV-2 infection20–22. In addition, nAb levels are highly predictive of immune protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection4,7. Therefore, to the nAb response against the S1 domain/RBD of SARS-CoV-2 in type 2 DM and non-DM individuals after administration of various COVID-19 vaccine regimens, the levels of nAbs against the S1 domain/RBD of SARS-CoV-2, which mediate binding to ACE2 receptors, were determined via ELISA (SARS-CoV-2 NeutraLISA Kit, EUROIMMUN, Lübeck, Germany)23–25. The results of nAbs are presented as the percentage of inhibition of fluorochrome-labeled ACE2 binding to the coated RBD measurement in vitro (% IH). On day 30 after the 2nd dose, only the Az-Az, Az-BNT and BNT-BNT regimens elicited inhibition of measured nAbs greater than 50% in the DM group (Fig. 3a). The analysis revealed differences in nAb responses between DM and non-DM individuals, varying by vaccination regimens. DM individuals showed significantly lower nAb responses compared to non-DM individuals when receiving homologous SV-SV or Az-Az regimens. However, comparable nAb responses between DM and non-DM individuals were observed with heterologous SV-Az, Az-BNT, and homologous BNT-BNT prime-boost regimens. In addition, the nAb response of DM individuals was not different from that of non-DM individuals to the SV-Az, Az-BNT and homologous BNT-BNT prime-boost regimens at day 30 post-boost. Interestingly, the levels of nAbs in DM individuals rapidly decreased and were significantly lower than those in non-DM individuals after they were boosted with any of the vaccines at day-90 after the booster dose (Fig. 3b). In addition, only the Az-BNT and BNT-BNT regimens maintained nAb responses above 50% inhibition in non-DM individuals. In contrast, DM individuals showed nAb responses below 50% inhibition across all vaccination regimens at this time point.

Regarding % IH of nAb from the multivariate analyses, all tested vaccine regimens resulted in significantly higher % IH compared to the SV-SV group at Day 30 (p < 0.05). At this time point, BMI was positively associated with % IH (Adjusted β = 2.25), while a significant negative interaction effect between BMI and DM was observed (Adjusted β = −2.39; p < 0.05). These findings suggest that a shorter time before reboosting in the DM individuals might be needed.

Discussion

CoronaVac or SV was the most abundant available vaccine in Thailand in the early phase of the COVID-19 pandemic. Therefore, most health care workers were vaccinated with this vaccine as a baseline. Nevertheless, previous studies revealed that immunization with inactivated vaccine platforms elicited the production of substantial nAb levels in humans26–28, but in this study, SV elicited the lowest nAb response compared with those with the mRNA vaccine and viral vector vaccine platforms. Although most approved vaccines are designed for a two-dose homologous prime-boost regimen to achieve optimal protection9, various real-world factors including vaccine supply shortages, logistical constraints, and individual preferences based on perceptions of vaccine efficacy have led some individuals to receive heterologous prime-boost regimens. Our data showed that the heterologous Az-BNT prime-boost regimen elicited a significantly greater anti-S response than the other regimens did, followed by the BNT-BNT, Az-Az, SV-Az and SV-SV regimens. In addition, the heterologous Az-BNT prime-boost regimen elicited higher nAb levels than did the homologous prime-boost regimens. This finding was consistent with data from a previous study that reported a greater immune response after vaccination with a heterologous Az-BNT regimen than after vaccination with a homologous Az-Az regimen29.

While this study was not designed to definitively disentangle the effects of the prime-boost regimen versus the booster vaccine type, our findings suggest that the prime-boost regimen plays a significant role in shaping the observed immune responses. The substantial difference in anti-S antibody levels between the Az-BNT and BNT-BNT groups, despite receiving the same BNT booster, provides compelling evidence for this. This difference indicates that the priming immunization (vector vs. mRNA vaccines) significantly influences the subsequent immune response to the same booster. While the type of booster undoubtedly contributes to the overall immune response, the observed variations across different prime-boost combinations cannot be solely attributed to the booster alone. Future studies with a broader range of vaccine combinations, including homologous and heterologous prime-boost schedules, and ideally with varying intervals between doses, are necessary.

Multiple factors, including age and underlying conditions, can affect the post vaccination immune response and its long-term dynamics10,14,30. Previous studies revealed that individuals with DM have impaired immune responses to trivalent influenza vaccines16 or vaccines against other infectious diseases15,17,18, especially COVID-1931. Our results indicated that DM impaired the anti-S response against the Az-BNT regimen at both day 30 and day 90 post-boosting and that only the BNT-BNT regimen resulted in a persistent anti-S response greater than 1000 U/mL at 3 months post-boosting. In DM individuals, the anti-S level to the heterologous Az-BNT regimen was significantly lower than that in non-DM individuals at both 30 and 90 days post booster. Furthermore, when comparing the DM group, the anti-S level elicited by the homologous BNT-BNT regimen was similar to that elicited by the Az-BNT regimen at 30 days post booster; however, at 90 days post booster, the BNT-BNT regimen induced a significantly higher anti-S level compared to the Az-BNT regimen. Consistent with these findings, homologous boosting has been shown to induce a wide range of immunogenicity responses, and a heterologous boost immunization strategy provides similar or greater immune responses, depending on the type of vaccine platform used29,32.

In addition, the nAb levels at 30 days after two doses of the mRNA or viral vector were similar and significantly greater than those after administration of the SV-based formulation. Following the booster dose, all vaccination regimens showed a significant decrease in antibody levels over the first 3 months. This observation was consistent with previous studies that reported a rapid decline in nAb levels in the first 1–3 months among SARS-CoV-2-infected individuals or fully vaccinated individuals5,30,33. From a previous study, two doses of mRNA-based vaccines elicited an immune response that poorly neutralized omicron, and three mRNA vaccine doses elicited potent variant cross-neutralization, including against omicron34. The diminished neutralizing antibody observed in DM compared with non-DM individuals following the SV-SV and Az-Az regimens, persisting for at least three months post-boost, raise important questions about the underlying mechanisms of immune dysfunction in this population. This sustained, blunted response, in contrast to the effective neutralization observed with the Az-BNT and BNT-BNT regimens in non-DM individuals, suggests a potential impairment in the ability of DM patients to mount a robust and durable humoral immune response to certain vaccine platforms. Future research should investigate the specific factors contributing to this reduced immunogenicity in DM and explore strategies to enhance vaccine efficacy in this high-risk group.

Other studies have also examined short-term immune responses to COVID-19 vaccines. However, our study offers several key novel contributions within this timeframe that distinguish it from the previous work. Firstly, we directly compared humoral immune responses to five different COVID-19 vaccine regimens in individuals with and without type 2 DM specifically up to 90 days post-vaccination. While some previous studies have included DM patients, but our work provides a detailed analysis of the differential immune response in this vulnerable population within this critical early period, which is essential for understanding early protection and informing targeted booster strategies. Secondly, we specifically examined the impact of DM on the heterologous Az-BNT vaccination regimen. The early data suggests this regimen may offer advantages in healthy individuals13. Only few studies have focused on this critical comparison within this timeframe, especially in the DM individuals. Thirdly, our finding of the rapid decline of neutralizing antibodies in DM individuals across all vaccine regimens at 90 days post-boost is novel and has important implications for optimizing booster strategies in this at-risk group. This early decline in DM individuals may be missed if any study was looking only at longer term data or without this early 90-day window.

The combination of different vaccine platforms may affect the overall immune response. While the Az-BNT combination may stimulate a broader immune response in non-DM individuals13, the impact of this combination on the DM population might be more nuanced. While the similar BNT-BNT response in both DM and non-DM groups suggests a potential impairment in the innate immune response to the Az prime in DM individuals, it is also important to consider the well-documented impact of diabetes on T-cell function. Diabetic patients often exhibit diminished T-cell responses17, which could directly contribute to the lower antibody levels observed in our study. This may require further investigation into specific mechanisms underlying the impaired immune responses in DM individuals and exploring potential strategies to enhance vaccine efficacy in this vulnerable population.

Moreover, the potential immunopathological mechanisms underlying reduced vaccine responses in type 2 diabetes mellitus (Type 2 DM). These mechanisms may involve several points. Firstly, T2DM is intrinsically linked to a state of chronic low-grade inflammation and broader immune dysfunction. This persistent inflammatory environment can significantly impair both innate and adaptive immune responses17,35,36. Such an altered immune status17 likely contributes to the reduced vaccine immunogenicity observed in our study. Secondly, key metabolic factors inherent to type 2 DM, notably hyperglycemia and insulin resistance, are known to directly disrupt critical immune cell functions. These disruptions can affect antigen presentation, as well as cytokine and antibody production35. The transiently increased fasting blood sugar (FBS) levels we observed post-vaccination in individuals with type 2 DM may further exemplify this, potentially contributing to the impaired immune responses documented.

Furthermore, compromised immune signaling pathways, particularly impaired type I interferon (IFN-α/β) signaling, can directly undermine B cell activation and subsequent antibody generation37. This is particularly relevant given that metformin, a widely used antidiabetic medication, has been reported to suppress IFN-α production, a cytokine critical for robust B-cell responses to mRNA vaccines38. Indeed, the role of IFN-α/β in enhancing B cell responses is well-established39. The potential suppression of IFN-α by metformin, possibly via mTORC1 inhibition as suggested by our previous work on seasonal influenza vaccination16, could therefore contribute to the diminished antibody responses observed in individuals with type 2 DM receiving the BNT vaccine. This warrants careful consideration of metformin’s impact on vaccine efficacy, especially given its widespread use.

Finally, cellular immunity, particularly T-cell function, plays a vital role in orchestrating effective vaccine protection and supporting robust antibody production. Individuals with type 2 DM often exhibit diminished T-cell functionality and impairments in germinal center formation17. These defects in T-cell help could further contribute to the lower antibody levels observed in our study participants with type 2 DM. Collectively, these interconnected mechanisms, spanning chronic inflammation, metabolic dysregulation, impaired interferon signaling potentially exacerbated by metformin, and T-cell defects, provide strong biological plausibility for the impaired humoral responses we observed in participants with type 2 DM across different COVID-19 vaccine regimens.

Regarding the evaluation of concomitant factors such as glycemic control and medications that could affect the immune response. Only transiently increased FBS levels were observed but no correlation with antibody levels. This temporary change does not explain the antibody responses, implying that other factors beyond transient glycemic fluctuations are responsible for the diminished antibody production.

This study has several limitations. The relatively small number of participants, particularly type 2 DM individuals, limits the generalizability of the findings to a larger population. The disproportionate number of non-DM individuals compared with DM individuals may have biased the results and hindered the ability to draw definitive conclusions about vaccine efficacy in the DM population. Moreover, the absence of longitudinal data for the third and fourth booster doses restricts the assessment of long-term and re-boost vaccine effectiveness and the potential need for adjusted vaccination schedules in DM individuals. While no severe side effects presented in any of the participants in this study, the potential for long-term adverse events, such as myocarditis and pericarditis37, requires extended follow-up to establish a comprehensive safety profile. By addressing these limitations, further research could provide more robust evidence on the efficacy and safety of COVID-19 vaccines in individuals with DM and inform optimal vaccination strategies for this high-risk population.

Furthermore, EUROIMMUN NeutraLISA Kit used in this study utilizes a recombinant RBD protein to detect neutralizing antibodies. While the specific variant used to produce this RBD protein is not explicitly explained by the manufacturer, it is important to acknowledge that variations in RBD sequences across different SARS-CoV-2 variants40 may potentially affect the assay efficiency to detect neutralizing antibodies against circulating viral strains.

Non-obese diabetes is uniquely dominant in Northeastern region of Thailand and most of diabetic individuals in this study were non-obese. While a previous study of impaired antibody in diabetes in China suggested that anti-RBD IgG antibody levels were significantly lower in T2DM with higher BMI (≥ 24 kg/m2) in response to inactivated COVID-19 vaccines31. Future research is needed to elucidate the mechanistic differences in vaccine responses including immunogenicity, efficacy, and safety between non-obese and obese individuals with diabetes, which could inform the development of personalized vaccination approaches.

Defining a precise, universal clinically relevant threshold for nAbs that guarantees protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection or severe COVID-19 remains challenging. This is because such a threshold can be influenced by multiple factors, including the specific viral variant, the host’s immune status, the type of vaccine, and the time elapsed since vaccination or infection41,42. While a definitive threshold is elusive, a strong correlation between higher nAb levels and protection from symptomatic SARS-CoV-2 infection has been consistently reported in numerous studies4,7,43. In our study, levels below 50% inhibition in DM individuals suggest reduced protection and support the need for earlier booster vaccination or more frequent booster doses. Regarding the translation of our nAb findings to actual protection against severe COVID-19, particularly in individuals with type 2 diabetes, several points can be involved. Neutralizing antibody (nAb) levels have emerged as key immune correlates of protection against SARS-CoV-2 infection, with higher titers generally linked to reduced risk of symptomatic disease. However, pivotal studies have demonstrated that the nAb threshold required to prevent severe COVID-19 is substantially lower than that needed to prevent any symptomatic infection. This finding implies that even modest levels of nAbs may confer substantial protection against severe outcomes across diverse vaccine platforms and viral variants7,44.

In our study, individuals with type 2 diabetes mellitus exhibited significantly reduced anti-spike antibody titers following heterologous ChAdOx1 nCoV-19 - BNT162b2 vaccination, particularly at 30 and 90 days post-boost, as well as an accelerated decline in nAb levels across all tested regimens. Although early post-booster titers in these individuals may still exceed the threshold for protection against severe disease, the faster waning kinetics suggest a shorter window of effective immune coverage compared to non-diabetic individuals. Given the chronic immune dysregulation often present in type 2 DM17, these patterns raise concern over increased susceptibility to breakthrough infections and, potentially, their progression to severe disease in the absence of sufficient nAb-mediated or cellular immune control.

Indeed, while humoral immunity remains central to infection prevention, T-cell mediated responses are critical for viral clearance and protection against severe outcomes, particularly when antibody titers decline or when facing immune-evasive variants45. Impaired T-cell function, frequently reported in individuals with type 2 DM17, may further compromise this arm of immunity, heightening vulnerability in this group. Therefore, sustaining even moderate nAb levels through vaccination and optimally timed boosters may be especially critical for those with type 2 DM. Although our study assessed immunological surrogates rather than direct clinical endpoints, these findings are interpretable in the context of validated correlates of protection. The attenuated magnitude and durability of the antibody response in individuals with type 2 DM suggest a potentially diminished and shorter-lived protection against COVID-19. These results highlight the need for tailored vaccination strategies for this population, potentially including shorter intervals between booster doses. While quantifying the exact level of clinical protection conferred by the nAb titers observed in our type 2 DM cohort would require large-scale outcome studies, the established correlation between nAb levels and clinical protection strongly supports the clinical relevance of our findings and underscores the importance of proactive public health interventions for at-risk groups.

In conclusion, our results indicated that anti-S and nAb responses following BNT boost in non-DM individuals previously immunized with the Az vaccine are greater than those following vaccination with other regimens. Interestingly, an impaired anti-S antibody response against the Az-BNT regimen at both day 30 and day 90 post-vaccination was found in type 2 DM individuals. Similarly, an impairment in the nAb response against the SV-SV and Az-Az regimens at day 30 post-boosting was observed. Moreover, nAb levels rapidly decreased at 90 days post-boosting with all regimens in these DM individuals. However, among the currently available COVID-19 vaccines, the heterologous Az-BNT and homologous BNT-BNT regimens might be the best for eliciting anti-S response and nAb responses in type 2 DM individuals. Moreover, the adjusted analyses were shown that DM was significantly associated with lower % IH at Day 90. While a precise nAb threshold for protection against severe COVID-19 is not absolute and can be influenced by various factors, existing evidence strongly supports nAbs as a key correlate of protection. Our findings of impaired nAb responses in individuals with type 2 DM suggest a potential for reduced or less durable protection against severe outcomes. This highlights the necessity for optimized and potentially more frequent booster vaccinations in this high-risk population, alongside ongoing research into the multifaceted immune responses to vaccination in individuals with type 2 DM. Importantly, a shorter time till a re-booster shot in DM individuals might be needed. These findings might be useful for guiding the use of appropriate regimens for individuals with or without DM and aid in the development of an effective COVID-19 vaccine for people with underlying conditions, especially DM, in the future.

Methods

Ethical approval of the study protocol

The study protocol was carried out in accordance with the approved guidelines of the Chiang Mai University Ethics Committee on Human Research (AMSEC-63EX-030; Chiang Mai, Thailand), and all the subjects provided written informed consent.

Study participants and COVID-19 vaccines

All participants were defined here as those aged over 18 years. None of the participants had any signs of acute infectious disease in the prior three months and did not receive any vaccines in the six months prior to enrolment in the study. Moreover, the non-DM consisted of individuals with normal fasting blood glucose levels (< 110 mg/dL). Most individuals were health care workers (92%) recruited from hospital staff, who live in the same endemic area as office staff and farmers. All DM participants were previously diagnosed and registered as DM patients at the hospital prior to the enrolment in the study. This ensures that the study population consisted of individuals with established DM diagnosis. Both fasting blood glucose and hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) levels were measured at baseline (seven days prior to the initiation of the study). These measurements provided essential information on participants’ glycemic control prior to the study. The exclusion criteria for all participants were a history of infection with SARS-CoV-2, contact with COVID-19 patients within 2 weeks prior to enrolment, receipt of other SARS-CoV-2 vaccines, receipt of a live attenuated vaccine in the past 30 days, receipt of other inactivated or subunit vaccines in the past 30 days, having a history of allergy to any study vaccine components, having a history of immune deficiency, suffering from alcoholism and having an acute illness or signs of illness at the time of vaccination. Non of the participants had any exceptionally high levels of anti-N antibodies suggesting no SARS-CoV-2 exposure prior to the study (Supplementary Fig. 2). In this study, blood glycemic control was assessed by measuring fasting blood sugar (FBS) levels at multiple time points after vaccination in DM individuals (Supplementary Fig. 3). FBS transiently increased at day 30 following vaccination, returning to baseline by day 90.

In addition, we systematically collected data on adverse events from all participants including both DM and non-DM groups using the same standardized questionnaire and follow-up procedures. Notably, all subjects in both groups reported no observable major adverse effects following vaccination. This uniform finding indicates that, within our study cohort, the vaccine was well tolerated irrespective of diabetes status.

Individuals who were diagnosed with DM (n = 43) and individuals without DM (non-DM) (n = 133) who worked in the hospital in Ubonratchathani, Thailand, were enrolled in this study (Fig. 1). All participants were included in the evaluation of the anti-S protein RBD antibodies and nAbs against the S1 domain/RBD of SARS-CoV-2, which mediates binding to ACE2 receptors of human cells. Anti-S responses were evaluated by an electrochemiluminescence immunoassay at baseline (day − 7), 30 days after the 1st dose and 30 and 90 days after the 2nd dose. nAbs against the S1 domain/RBD of SARS-CoV-2 were quantified via ELISA at 30 and 90 days after the 2nd dose. The results are presented as the percentage of inhibition (% IH).

All the participants made their own decisions regarding whether to be primed or boosted with any COVID-19 vaccination regimen. All participants were divided into 5 groups according to their vaccination schedules as follows: SV-SV, SV-Az, Az-Az, Az, BNT, or BNT-BNT. COVID-19 vaccines were provided by the Ministry of Public Health of Thailand. Although participants were categorized based on their final completed regimens, vaccine allocation was largely determined by national policies and availability. Baseline sociodemographic characteristics, including age, gender, and BMI, were comparable across groups, minimizing the risk of major confounding (Supplementary Table 1).

Measurement of levels of antibodies against the Spike receptor-binding domain (RBD) of SARS-CoV-2 (anti-S)

Antibodies against the S-RBD of SARS-CoV-2 (anti-S) were quantified on a Cobas e411 analyser (Roche Diagnostics, Mannheim, Germany) with electrochemiluminescence double-antigen sandwich immunoassays targeting total anti-SARS-CoV-2 antibodies against the S protein (Elecsys-S) following the manufacturer’s instructions, with a cut-off value for positive results of ≥ 0.8 U/mL and linear range 0.4–250 U/mL. Anti-S antibody levels ranging from 0.40 to 250 U/mL represent the primary measurement range. The results below this range were set to 0.4 U/mL and were considered nonreactive. Samples with levels above 250 U/mL were automatically diluted into the linear range of the assay (dilutions used in this study: 1:10 or 1:100) with DiluentUniversal (Roche Diagnostics, Rotkreuz, Switzerland).

Neutralizing antibody assay

nAbs against the S1 domain/RBD of SARS-CoV-2 were quantified via the SARS-CoV-2 NeutraLISA Kit (EUROIMMUN, Lübeck, Germany). The kit was used to quantify nAbs via competitive ELISA. The principle of this test involves coating a microtiter plate with the S1 domain/RBD domain of the S antigen of SARS-CoV-2, followed by the addition of samples dissolved in ACE-2 biotinylated buffer. If nAbs are present, they compete with the binding sites of ACE-2 for the S1 domain/RBD receptors. The intensity of the colour formed is inversely proportional to the level of antibodies in the sample, and the results are presented as the percentage of inhibition (%IH). According to EUROIMMUN, this kit has a specificity of 99.7%, and its sensitivity is 95.9%46. However, a recent study suggests that the sensitivity of this kit may be lower than previously reported, and the manufacturer-recommended cutoff values may require further investigation24. According to the kit instructions, the percentage inhibition (%IH) results were interpreted as follows: % IH < 20 was considered “negative”, % IH ≥ 20 to < 35 was considered “borderline”, and % IH ≥ 35 indicated a “positive” result.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were carried out via two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). The Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to compare the nonparametric data before and after boosting in each group. Nonparametric tests of significance were performed if a normal distribution could not be assessed or if populations did not have a normal distribution. To confirm the sample size suitability and its potential impact on statistical power and generalization of the results, post-hoc power analysis was conducted with the achieved power > 80 to 100% at a 95% confidence level. PRISM 9 software (GraphPad, San Diego, CA, USA) was used for all the statistical analyses; p < 0.05 was considered to indicate significance. The generalized linear model was used for the multivariate analysis. All models were adjusted for age, BMI, DM and gender at the time of enrolment. The multivariate analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (Version 27). The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, and the 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated for the point estimates.
