On the relationship between efficiency and cooperativity in multi‐subunit molecular machines
Authors: Miki Ben‐Maimon, Amnon Horovitz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo key properties of machines are the extent of coordination between their moving parts and their efficiencies. Here, we analyze how the extent of coordination, as reflected in cooperativity between subunits in an oligomeric molecular machine, affects its efficiency. The efficiency is found to increase in a non‐linear fashion with increasing cooperativity. In the case of a given cooperativity, efficiency increases when the allosteric constant, that is, the energy difference between the two apo states of the machine, increases or when the binding energy for the high affinity state increases but not both. These results highlight design principles that can guide strategies for increasing machine efficiency.

The Cambridge Dictionary's definition of a machine is: ‘A piece of equipment with several moving parts that uses power to do a particular type of work’. This definition alludes to two features of machines that can be quantified. One feature is cooperativity, that is, the extent to which the different parts of the machine move in a random or coordinated fashion. The second feature is efficiency, that is, the extent to which the input energy (power) of the machine is converted into useful output energy that can do work. A fundamental question regarding machines concerns, therefore, the relationship between their efficiency and extent of cooperativity. This question is also very significant for biology given that virtually all cellular processes involve machines (Alberts, 1998) that consist of moving parts (protein subunits) and consume energy (ATP) to carry out their function.

Movement in biological machines occurs due to ligand binding‐promoted conformational changes. The coupling between ligand binding and conformational change can be described by a thermodynamic cycle that comprises the ligand‐bound and unbound states of a protein in two conformations, T and R, with low and high affinities for the ligand, respectively (Figure 1). Owing to free energy conservation in the cycle, one has: (1) ∆GbT+∆GN=∆GbR+∆G0 where ΔG b(T) and ΔG b(R) are the respective free energies of ligand binding to the T and R conformations of the protein and ΔG 0 and ΔG N are the respective free energies of the T to R conformational changes of the unbound and bound (with N ligand molecules) states.

Thermodynamic cycle for coupling between conformational changes and ligand binding. The protein is in equilibrium between two states, T and R, with low and high affinities for the ligand, S, of which N molecules can bind thus forming TS N and RS N . The respective free energies of the transitions T → R and TS N → RS N are designated by ΔG 0 and ΔG N . The respective free energies of binding of N ligand molecules to the T and R states are designated by ΔG b(T) and ΔG b(R).

The efficiency of a machine, η, is given by the output energy divided by the total input energy. In line with previous work (e.g., Nayak et al., 2019), the output energy is given by the difference in the free energy of conformational switching of the unbound and bound states, ΔG N − ΔG 0. The input energy is equal to the free energy of binding of N ligand molecules to the R state (ΔG b(R)), which is the high affinity state. The efficiency of the machine can, therefore, be expressed in accordance with this definition and Equation (1), as follows: (2) η=∆GN−∆G0∆GbR=∆GbR−∆GbT∆GbR=1−lnKTlnKR where K R and K T are the respective ligand binding constants for the R and T states. This definition is like that for the Carnot heat engine in assuming that all the steps in the cycle are reversible and that the free energy change around the cycle is zero. It represents an upper limit also because other steps, which may take place in the function of the machine, are not considered.

K R and K T are also parameters of the classic Monod‐Wyman‐Changeux (MWC) concerted allosteric model (Monod et al., 1965). According to this model, the protein is in equilibrium between two states: a T state with low affinity for the ligand and an R state with high affinity for the ligand. The extent of cooperativity in ligand binding is determined by the allosteric equilibrium constant, in the absence of ligand, between the T and R states (L = [T 0]/[R 0]) and by the ratio between K T and K R (c = K R /K T ). In certain cases, it is possible to express the Hill coefficient at half saturation, n 0.5, which provides a measure of the extent of cooperativity in a system, as a function of L and c (Gruber et al., 2019; Levitzki, 1978). In the simplest case of a symmetric dimer, it is possible to derive the following expression which relates n 0.5 with L and c: (3) n0.5=21+1+cLL+11+c2L2

A detailed derivation of Equation (3) can be found also elsewhere (Roy & Horovitz, 2022). Note that this derivation of n 0.5 is for the fraction of bound sites and not for the fraction of molecules in a certain state (e.g., relaxed or open (Karlin, 1967; Qin, 2010)) since machines are driven by ligand binding. Inspection of Equation (3) shows that cooperativity is absent, that is, n 0.5 = 1, when the two states are indistinguishable with respect to ligand binding (i.e., c = 1) or when L → ∞ or when L → 0, that is, when the equilibrium between the T and R states does not shift much upon ligand binding. It can be seen from Equation (2) that η = 0 when K R = K T in which case n 0.5 = 1 from Equation (3). Conversely, it can also be seen that η = 1 when K R > > K T in which case n 0.5 = 2. In other words, increased cooperativity leads to higher efficiency.

Equation (4) can be used to show the dependence of the efficiency of a machine on its cooperativity in the case of a symmetric dimer for given values of L and K R . Simulations using Equation (4) show that the efficiency increases with increasing cooperativity in a non‐linear manner that depends on the values of L and K R (Figure 2a,b). Interestingly, efficiency can be increased for a given cooperativity by increasing K R and decreasing L or vice versa (Figure 2c,d). In other words, there is a tradeoff in the contributions to efficiency of (i) the relative stabilities of the two apo states of the machine (L) and (ii) the ligand (ATP) affinity for the high affinity state (K R ). In summary, therefore, this work provides a formal link between efficiency and cooperativity, which are two key features of machines. It also indicates that the design of more efficient machines can be achieved by increasing K R or L but not both.

It may be seen that the efficiency increases when L increases and K R decreases or vice versa, that is, there is a tradeoff. The heat bars designate efficiency values from 0 to 1.

Future work should consider various complexities here neglected for simplification. The derivation we provide is for a homodimer and should be extended for larger homo‐ and hetero‐oligomers although the principles should apply to them as well. Effects of cofactors, which may be found in cells and that bind preferentially to the T or R states or stabilize some third state, should be analyzed. Future studies should also examine the implications of non‐exclusive binding of ATP to the R state in deriving Equation (1) and the existence of sequential allostery.
