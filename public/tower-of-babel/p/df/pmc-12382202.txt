Workplace mobbing as a predictor of quality of work life in early-career clinical nurses
Authors: Habip Balsak, Fatma Ayhan, Hasret Narin Balsak, İbrahim Gün
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMobbing is defined as a systematic and continuous form of psychological violence in the workplace. The healthcare sector is a field where workplace violence, including mobbing, is notably prevalent, with nurses in the early years of their careers representing a particularly vulnerable group in this context.AimsThis study aims to investigate the prevalence of workplace mobbing among early-career nurses and its relationship with their quality of work life and sociodemographic characteristics.MethodThis descriptive and prospective study employed a convenience sampling method. The study sample consisted of 392 clinical nurses who were in their first five years of professional life and were still working at the institution where they first started their career. Data were collected using a socio-demographic characteristics questionnaire, the workplace psychological violence scale (WPVS), and the Nursing Quality of work life scale (NWLQS).ResultsAmong nurses in the early years of their careers, mobbing related to professional status was the variable with the highest Beta coefficient negatively affecting work life quality (β: -0.171; p < 0.05), and together with the negative behavior subscale score, it was a significant predictor of reduced work life quality (R² Change: 16.547; p < 0.05). However, attacks on personality and workplace isolation did not make a significant contribution to the quality of work life in the hierarchical regression model (p < 0.05).ConclusionsThis study reveals that exposure to bullying in the professional status subscale during the first five years of nurses’ careers significantly negatively affects their quality of work life. It highlights the need for greater support for nurses in the early years of their careers in addressing this issue within their work environments.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Workplace mobbing, first defined by Swedish psychologist Heinz Leymann, refers to systematic bullying and harassment behaviors directed at an employee by a group or person in the workplace [1, 2]. In this study, the term “workplace bullying” is used to refer to systematic psychological violence in the workplace. Although the term “mobbing” is frequently used in several European countries, including Türkiye, the term “workplace bullying” is more commonly adopted in English-speaking academic literature [3]. Therefore, we use both terms interchangeably, with a primary emphasis on “workplace bullying” to ensure consistency with the international discourse. Characterized by intentional, repetitive, and harmful behaviors, workplace mobbing gradually exerts negative effects on the employee’s psychological and physical health over time [4–6].Large and complex structures, such as the healthcare sector, are particularly susceptible to mobbing due to their hierarchical nature and high levels of stress. Therefore, mobbing can be observed at all levels within healthcare workplaces, leading to seriously detrimental outcomes [2, 5].

Nurses play critical roles within the healthcare professionals and constitute the largest group of employees in the healthcare sector. Due to these characteristics, nurses work in nearly every area of the healthcare workforce and are affected by bullying to varying degrees. Various studies have confirmed that the rates of exposure to bullying among nurses fluctuate [7–9]. Structural issues such as heavy workload, staff shortages, and lack of managerial support (10) leave nurses without adequate organizational protection, making them vulnerable from a psychosocial perspective. Furthermore, their limited involvement in decision-making processes, lower hierarchical status, and impaired perceptions of organizational justice facilitate the emergence of mobbing behaviors (11). When combined with conflicts involving both supervisors and colleagues, these factors create an environment that threatens nurses’ psychological safety in the workplace. Additionally, a culture of organizational silence and the fact that mobbing is often perpetrated by individuals in higher positions restrict nurses’ ability to defend themselves against such behaviors, contributing to the chronic nature of mobbing (12). Collectively, these factors indicate that nurses constitute a professional group at high risk for exposure to mobbing. A systematic review examined 38 studies conducted over a span of 13 years and reported that exposure to mobbing among nurses could reach rates as high as 100% [4]. Mobbing, which is frequently observed among nurses, leads to serious negative consequences, particularly contributing to the development of psychological disorders such as anxiety and depression, and in severe cases, may even result in suicidal ideation [10, 11]. Another study conducted on clinical nurses found that factors related to workplace violence increase employees’ intentions to leave their jobs and decrease their quality of work life [12]. In conclusion, while mobbing adversely affects health, it also significantly diminishes the productivity and performance of employees in the workplace [6, 10].

Nurses in the early years of their careers (EYC nurses), in particular, face various challenges in adapting to the work environment, with negative experiences during this period directly impacting both their professional development and quality of work life [13, 14]. The absence of adequate support systems in the workplace and the neglect of this issue exacerbate the consequences of mobbing [1]. Many EYC nurses may hesitate to report incidents of violence and mobbing, which decreases the visibility of the problem and complicates the development of effective solutions [15]. As previously mentioned, EYC nurses are known to experience high levels of workplace violence [16] and mobbing [4]. These circumstances underscore the vulnerability of novice nurses to bullying. These findings highlight the necessity of addressing the prevalence and impacts of bullying specifically among EYC nurses. In the literature, numerous studies have demonstrated the negative effects of mobbing, clarified its long-term impact on both psychological and physical health [6, 12, 14]. This study aims to investigate the prevalence of workplace mobbing among early-career nurses and its relationship with their quality of work life and sociodemographic characteristics. This study examined the level of mobbing experienced by nurses in the early years of their professional careers and effect of mobbing on their work life quality. Despite numerous studies addressing workplace mobbing among nurses, there remains limited research specifically exploring the impact of mobbing on the quality of work life among nurses in the early stages of their careers. Therefore, investigating this relationship within this particular demographic group is critical for identifying targeted interventions and improving working conditions for novice clinical nurses. Method

The data for this descriptive and prospective study were collected between 01.03.2024 and 15.05.2024.

Participants

The participants of the study consist of nurses who began their professional experience by working at the hospital where they were employed for the first time and are in the EYC nurses. In the literature, EYC nurses are typically studied within a 1–3-year range, and these studies predominantly focus on topics such as workplace well-being, burnout, and job satisfaction [17, 18]. However, since mobbing is a form of systematic psychological harassment that requires a minimum of six months of continuity for its identification, nurses in the first six months of their careers were excluded from the study. Additionally, workers who were under 18 years old also excluded from the study. Moreover, considering EYC nurses may be inexperienced in recognizing mobbing, it was deemed appropriate to include nurses in the first five years of their careers in the study. The study population consists of clinical nurses with a minimum of six months and a maximum of five years of experience, who are still employed at their initial workplace. Researchers reached participants from this population using convenience sampling methods using online tools such as social media, email, and WhatsApp.

Data collection

Descriptive data concerning the nurses participating in the study were collected using a socio-demographic questionnaire developed by the researchers, which included variables such as age, gender, marital status, education level, and position in the department where they work. The level of exposure to mobbing among nurses was assessed using the Workplace Psychological Violence (Mobbing) Scale (WPV), while their work-life quality was evaluated using the Nursing Work-Life Quality Scale (NWLQS).

Workplace psychological violence (mobbing) scale (WPVS)

This scale was developed by Dilek and Yıldırım to assess exposure to mobbing in the workplace [19]. The validity and reliability of the scale was conducted in Turkish and specifically on nurses. The WPVS consists of four sub-dimensions: Isolation from Work (IW), Attack on Professional Status (APS), Attack on Personality (APD), and Direct Negative Behavior (DNB) with a total of 33 questions. Self-reported items rated on a 6-point Likert-type (0 = Never; 5 = Always). The mean score of the scale is obtained by dividing the total score by the number of questions. A higher score indicates increased exposure to mobbing. In this study Cronbach’s Alpha was 0.971.

Nursing quality of work life scale (NWLQS)

The scale was developed by Brooks (2001) to determine the quality of work life of nurses [20]. Turkish validity and reliability study was conducted by Şirin (2015). The NWLQS consists of 35 questions and five sub-dimensions: Support Services (SS), Relations with Managers (RM), Work Environment (WE), Working Conditions (WC), and Work Perception (WP). Self-reported items rated on a 5-point Likert-type (1: Strongly Disagree; 5: Strongly Agree), and a higher score obtained from the scale indicates an increased quality of work life of nurses. In this study Cronbach’s Alpha was 0.925.

Data analysis

The collected data were analyzed using the SPSS 24 software package program. Descriptive statistics such as frequency, percentage, arithmetic mean, and standard deviation were used in the analysis of the data. The sample representation power was calculated using the Epi Info package program [21]. It was determined that the expected frequency is 53.3%, and that 392 participants with a 5% margin of error correspond to the 95% confidence interval. The normal distribution characteristics of the total and sub-dimension mean scores of the NWLQS and WPVS were assessed using skewness and kurtosis values. These values being between − 2 and + 2 indicate that the data exhibit normal distribution characteristics [22]. Independent samples t-tests and one-way analyses of variance (ANOVA) were conducted to examine the associations between independent variables (such as age and gender) and the total and subscale scores of the NWLQS and WPVS. To determine the differences between groups in Anova test, the Tukey test was conducted. The relationship between scales and sub-dimension of the scales based on mean scores were assessed using Spearman correlation analysis. Correlation coefficients were interpreted as very weak ( < ± 0.20), weak (± 0.21–0.34), moderate (± 0.35–0.49), strong (± 0.50–0.69), and very strong ( ≥ ± 0.70) [23]. The association of WPVS sub-dimension scores with NWLQS was evaluated using hierarchical regression analysis. In the conducted analyses, a p-value below 0.05 was considered significant. In the study, the levels of mobbing and quality of work life were addressed.

Ethics

Initially, appropriate citations were provided for the original studies that developed the scale and its Turkish adaptation. Following this, explicit permission for the utilization of the scale was obtained from the authors responsible for the Turkish adaptation. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committee of Batman University (Date: 01.03.2024, Number: 94540). Nurses participating in the study were informed about the research, and their voluntary consent were obtained. Additionally, the confidentiality of the data was assured.

Results

A total of 392 nurses participated in the study. The participants had a mean age of 25.89 ± 2.38 years, with an age range of 21 to 34 years. Their average professional experience was reported as 2.29 ± 0.86 years (ranging from 1 to 5 years). The gender distribution was 55.1% female (n = 216) and 44.9% male (n = 176). Regarding marital status, 74.0% (n = 290) of the participants were single, while 26.0% (n = 102) were married. In terms of educational level, 87.0% (n = 341) held a bachelor’s degree, and 13.0% (n = 51) had a postgraduate degree. All nurses worked in either internal medicine or surgical clinics of hospitals, with 96.4% (n = 378) serving as clinical nurses and 3.6% (n = 14) holding clinical supervisor or managerial positions (see Table 1).

Discussion

This study highlighted that workplace mobbing negatively associated with quality of work life among early-career clinical nurses, particularly concerning professional status. These findings contribute significantly to the literature, addressing a critical research gap regarding bullying experiences specifically within early-career nurse populations. The early stages of career in nurses is a critical period which nurses establish their professional identities [24]. During this time, exposure to mobbing can significantly adversely affect nurses’ quality of work life, jeopardizing their professional motivation, job satisfaction, and overall health [16–18]. Therefore, it is crucial to identify the mobbing experiences of nurses in the early years of their professional careers. However, a review of the literature reveals a lack of studies addressing the mobbing experiences of nurses during the initial years of their work life. In this study, both the mobbing experiences of EYC nurses, and the impact of mobbing experiences on their quality of work life have been examined. It is anticipated that the findings will serve as an important guide for creating more suitable and supportive work environments in healthcare institutions.

In our study, participants reported exposure to at least one of the subscales of the mobbing scale, with rates of 45.9% for isolation from work, 54.6% for attacks on professional status, 50.5% for attacks on personal character, and 30.9% for direct negative behaviors. In a study conducted in Türkiye involving nurse and physician participants, the rate of exposure to at least one of the subscales of the mobbing scale within the past year was reported as 66.4% for work isolation, 71.8% for attacks on professional status, 78.1% for attacks on personal character, and 28.4% for direct negative behaviors [25]. A systematic review evaluating mobbing research targeting nurses in Türkiye reported that the prevalence of experiencing mobbing behavior at least once ranged from 0 to 100% [4]. Internationally, reported rates of mobbing exposure among nurses include 5.9% in Slovakia [9]; 10.5% in the Czech Republic [10]; 30.2% in Greece [8]; 34% in Northern Italy [26] and 10.3% in Thailand [7]. Although differences in mobbing rates nationally and internationally may partly stem from variations in study samples and measurement tools, findings indicate that nurses experience significant levels of mobbing within the first five years of their careers. The prevalence of mobbing to this extent points to serious structural and managerial issues in the workplace, potentially adversely affecting nurses’ mental health, job satisfaction, and job performance. Indeed, our study’s findings underscore the importance of the issue, highlighting that exposure to mobbing diminishes quality of work life. Although the mean score of mobbing score was low, this is expected given the participants’ early career stage. Mobbing typically emerges through long-term repeated exposure, and most participants had limited time in the profession. Nonetheless, even low-level exposure, particularly to status-related behaviors, was significantly associated with decreased quality of work life, underscoring the importance of early intervention.

For EYC nurses, a high perceived workload significantly and negatively impacts mobbing exposure and quality of work life. On the basis of results, employees who report excessive workloads have significantly higher total mobbing scores and subscale scores for individual isolation, attacks on professional status, and personal character compared to those with balanced workloads. In a study involving nurses in Thailand, excessive workload was highlighted as a significant factor contributing to physical violence in the workplace [7]. Nurses working under heavy workloads may feel compelled to complete tasks rapidly, which can lead to reduced job quality and a higher likelihood of malpractice. This situation also negatively impacts quality of work life, manifesting in the subscales related to work conditions and relationships with management. Similarly, it has been found that nurses working 46 h or more per week have lower mean scores on the work conditions subscale of the NWLQS compared to those working between 40 and 45 h [27]. Additionally, shift-working nurses have been reported to score lower on the NWLQS than nurses working solely daytime shifts [28]. These findings align with evidence indicating that excessive workload, physical and mental fatigue, stress, work-life imbalance, professional dissatisfaction, and health issues can negatively impact the overall quality of work life for nurses [29].

Nurses with more than one year of work experience scored higher in quality of work life on the SS subscale, as well as in the mobbing subscale for APS, compared to those with less than one year of experience. A study conducted in Saudi Arabia found that longer work experience was a significant factor contributing to higher quality of work life scores [30]. The literature suggests that the targets of mobbing are often younger nurses with less workplace experience [9, 31], while nurses with more advanced clinical skills tend to show greater resilience against mobbing [32]. The finding that nurses with less than ten years of experience are at higher risk of verbal abuse supports our results [7]. New nurses may lack the experience and confidence needed to effectively respond to mobbing situations, making them more vulnerable. These findings highlight the significance of experience deficiency among EYC nurses, indicating its combined impact on mobbing exposure and quality of work life.

In our study, nurses who evaluated their income as “partially adequate” or “inadequate” perceived higher levels of APS compared to those who found their income “adequate.” Similarly, in the study conducted by Erdoğan and Yıldırım (2017), nurses and physicians who assessed their income as low reported experiencing more mobbing than those with higher income levels [25]. Moreover, a critical finding of this study was that APS represent the mobbing subdimension that most negatively impacts quality of work life. It is well established that salary satisfaction affects not only the material aspect but also psychological and social dimensions [33]. All these findings indicate that, among EYC nurses, salary plays a significant role in shaping perceptions of quality of work life and professional status. Nurses who consider their income is sufficient tend to experience greater economic satisfaction, which enhances their job satisfaction and contributes to a more positive attitude in the workplace.

In this study, the overall and subscale mean scores of the NWLQS were higher among nurses who chose their profession willingly. This finding is consistent with existing literature [34]. A systematic review involving healthcare workers found that those who enjoy their profession tend to have higher quality of work life scores [35]. Accordingly, the choice of profession is posited to positively influence nurses’ motivation, thereby enhancing their levels of job satisfaction.

In our study, married nurses had higher mean scores on the WC and WE subscales of the NWLQS compared to single nurses. The literature presents a mix of findings, with some studies indicating that married nurses report higher quality of work life than their single counterparts [30, 36], while others show no significant differences between marital status and quality of work life [37]. Although marriage may negatively impact quality of work life among EYC nurses, individual differences can influence these outcomes.

In our study, a negative correlation was found between the quality of work life of nurses and their levels of exposure to mobbing. Similarly, a study conducted in Türkiye, revealed a negative relationship between the experiences of mobbing among nurses and physicians and their external and overall job satisfaction [25]. The literature indicates that exposure to mobbing in the workplace can lead to decreased motivation, lack of concentration, increased malpractice, and absenteeism [38, 39]. Additionally, it has been associated with a weaker organizational climate [40], lowered morale, and job dissatisfaction [25, 41], as well as emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and diminished personal accomplishment [42]. Workplace mobbing and the resultant increase in burnout are associated with a decrease in job satisfaction among nurses [4, 41]. It is emphasized that this situation can undermine nurses’ commitment to their profession and degrade the quality of care provided by nurses, potentially leading to increased patient mortality rates [38, 39, 43]. All these findings highlight the negative relationship between mobbing and quality of work life, in parallel with the results of our study.

The hierarchical regression model conducted in relation to our third research question, which explores how mobbing affects quality of work life, allows for the examination of the changes in the variance of quality of work life scores attributable to the mean scores of the subscales of mobbing. According to the analysis results, the subdimension of APS emerges as the variable with the highest Beta coefficient. This is followed by the subdimension of DNB. It was found that the subscales of APD and IW have a limited association on the determination coefficient; however, they do not provide a significant contribution to the model.

These findings indicate that APS is the strongest predictive variable among the factors of mobbing for EYC nurses. Previous studies have emphasized that nurses’ perceptions of a decline in their professional status adversely affect their roles and performance in the workplace [24]. This situation may lead to more severe consequences, particularly for EYC nurses. Supporting and empowering EYC nurses in the work environment not only positively contributes to their career development but also enhances their job satisfaction [18]. Furthermore, it has been noted that as work experience increases among young nurses, their positive professional perceptions decline [44], and their levels of burnout tend to rise [41]. Therefore, it is evident that mobbing based on professional status is a critical factor that accelerates this negative process for EYC nurses. In this context, enhancing nurses’ professional status can contribute not only to their career development but also to the prevention of other issues they may encounter in the future. Thus, a significant finding of this study is that the professional status of EYC nurses should be addressed as a critical issue by decision makers including nursing supervisors, policymakers, and healthcare administrators.

The subdimension of negative behaviors serves as another significant predictor of quality of work life among EYC nurses. Exposure to negative behaviors in the workplace has been reported to lead to anxiety, depression, emotional distress, reduced job satisfaction, burnout, and cynicism among EYC nurses [45]. It is well-documented that individuals in healthcare services frequently encounter negative behaviors, such as bullying and incivility, in their work environments [46]. Considering the sources of mobbing such as patients, patient relatives, colleagues, and hierarchical relationships in the workplace EYC nurses are highly likely to be exposed to negative behaviors. A study conducted on various employee groups has reported that daily emotional support mitigates the adverse association between exposure to negative behaviors and workplace outcomes [47]. It is anticipated that such negative experiences during the initial years of their careers may instill a sense of anxiety about the future among young employees. Providing psychosocial support in this context is crucial for the career development of EYC nurses.

In our study, despite a significant rate of participants reporting experiences of APD and IW, these factors did not appear to lead to a meaningful change in their quality of work life. These findings provide insights into the primary concerns and threats faced by EYC nurses in the workplace. APS and DNB are perceived as more prominent and concrete threats. As EYC nurses are at the beginning of their careers, reinforcing their professional roles and gaining acceptance in the workplace may be critical for their quality of work life. Threats to their professional status can directly undermine their perceived value and identity within the workplace. Therefore, it can be inferred that while attacks on professional status may have a more substantial impact, they might be less affected by personal attacks.

Given that mobbing related to professional status significantly impacts EYC nurses’ quality of work life, healthcare administrators should prioritize creating supportive and respectful work environments. Concrete steps might include establishing mentoring programs where experienced nurses support novices, developing clear and confidential reporting mechanisms for workplace bullying incidents, and offering training to management staff on recognizing and addressing mobbing behaviors.

Policymakers should consider implementing comprehensive workplace violence prevention policies specifically addressing psychological violence and mobbing. Regular audits or surveys assessing the quality of nurses’ work life, especially among early-career staff, could be institutionalized. Furthermore, policies that advocate balanced workloads, fair remuneration, and professional recognition can significantly mitigate bullying incidents and enhance overall job satisfaction.

Limitations

The study has several limitations as other studies. First, the data collection for this study was conducted via social media platforms, introducing a limitation in reaching nurses who do not engage with social media. Second, our findings on mobbing and quality of work life rely solely on nurses’ self-reports, without incorporating observational methods or in-depth interviews. This reliance on self-reported data may limit the generalizability of the results. Third, since convenience sampling method was used in the study, workers with varying characteristics (e.g., age, gender) may not have been adequately represented in the sample. This limitation could negatively impact the generalizability of the study findings. Another limitation of the study is that the WPVS used in this research does not differentiate the sources of mobbing behaviors experienced by nurses (patients, relatives, colleagues, managers). Future research could explore how mobbing experienced by nurses is shaped by different actors by using measurement tools that more thoroughly assess the sources of mobbing. Since our study was conducted with EYC nurses, a lack of experience may have hindered their ability to fully identify workplace mobbing. Inexperienced nurses might not yet recognize mobbing behaviors or could be encountering such situations for the first time, potentially leading to lower reported levels of mobbing than what may actually occur.

There are also some additional limitations that need to be acknowledged. The data on race or ethnicity were not collected. This decision was based on the relatively homogenous demographic structure of Türkiye, where such distinctions are less emphasized in sociocultural contexts. However, we recognize that this limitation may affect the generalizability of the findings to populations with more diverse racial and ethnic compositions. Future research could explore how race or ethnicity may influence mobbing experiences and quality of work life outcomes in multicultural settings. Second, the study was geographically limited to Türkiye, which may reflect specific sociocultural and organizational dynamics unique to the country. As a result, the findings might not be directly applicable to healthcare systems and work environments in other regions with different cultural norms and professional practices. Replicating this study in other geographic and cultural settings would provide valuable insights into the universality of the observed relationships. Finally, the findings of our study specifically focus on the impact of mobbing on the quality of work life among nurses in the first five years of their careers in Türkiye. However, the generalizability of these results to psychiatric nurses in the United States may be influenced by differences in healthcare systems, cultural norms, workplace dynamics, and nurses’ professional experiences between the two countries. The workplace conditions and mobbing experiences encountered by psychiatric nurses in the United States may differ significantly from those of nurses in Türkiye. Therefore, conducting a similar study that considers cultural, structural, and professional differences would be necessary to generalize our findings to psychiatric nurses in the United States.

Despite these limitations, the study provides important insights into the mobbing experiences of nurses in the early stages of their careers and their impact on quality of work life within the Turkish healthcare context.

Conclusion

Our study highlights the negative impact of mobbing on the quality of work life for nurses in the early stages of their careers. Although there is a lack of studies in the literature examining the mobbing experiences of EYC nurses, it is emphasized that exposure to mobbing during their work life leads to health issues such as stress, anxiety, headaches, insomnia, and depressive symptoms, reduces job satisfaction, and increases turnover intentions [6]. In this context, both this study and other related research highlight the negative effects of mobbing on nurses. Based on these findings, it is recommended that both individual and institutional measures be implemented to enhance nurses’ work-life quality and mitigate the adverse association of mobbing. Regular training programs should be conducted for EYC nurses to help them recognize mobbing behaviors and learn appropriate responses, aiming to raise awareness of workplace harassment. Furthermore, managers should receive training to identify mobbing signs at early stages and take prompt action. Support mechanisms must be established for nurses exposed to mobbing, including psychological services, counseling, and guidance. Secure and confidential avenues should also be provided for reporting incidents of mobbing. Balancing nurses’ workloads will be a crucial step in improving their work-life quality. Additionally, policies fostering a positive culture of respect, collaboration, and team spirit in the workplace should be developed. Further research is needed to gain a deeper understanding of how different mobbing sub-dimensions impact work-life quality. Exploring additional factors affecting nurses’ work-life quality, as well as how nurses perceive and assess mobbing in the workplace, would also be beneficial.

In addition to the recommendations provided, future research should delve deeper into the specific factors that influence workplace bullying in EYC. This includes examining the role of organizational culture, leadership styles, and support systems in either mitigating or exacerbating such experiences. Studies focusing on diverse healthcare settings and populations can offer a more comprehensive understanding of the issue. Furthermore, longitudinal research tracking nurses over time would be valuable in assessing the long-term effects of workplace bullying on their professional development and well-being. Exploring the effectiveness of interventions, such as mentorship programs and resilience training, can also provide insights into strategies that support early-career nurses in navigating and overcoming workplace challenges.
