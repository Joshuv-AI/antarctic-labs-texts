Blueberry leaf polyphenols suppress biofilm formation and restore oral microbial homeostasis for caries control
Authors: Xuanlan Wu, Heng Xiao, Yanbing Chen, Chunqing Wu, Qiyuan Xie, Yu Gu, Xiaofei Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental caries is a dysbiotic biofilm-induced disease driven by Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans). Blueberry leaf polyphenols (BLP) are plant-derived polyphenols exhibiting antimicrobial properties. However, their effects on S. mutans remain incompletely understood.MethodsThe composition of BLP was determined by ultrahigh-pressure liquid chromatography-triple quadrupole tandem mass spectrometer (UPLC-MS/MS). The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and acid suppression activity were determined. In addition, the effect on biofilm was investigated by adhesion and aggregation assay, crystal violet assay, glycosyltransferases (GTF) activity, extracellular polysaccharides (EPS) quantification, scanning electron microscope (SEM) images and qRT–PCR. Furthermore, we carried out a rat caries model, and microbiome analyzes were performed by 16S rRNA sequencing.ResultsUPLC-MS/MS analysis identified a range of compounds in BLP, including quercetin and chlorogenic acid. The MIC of BLP was 500 μg/mL, and it inhibited the acid production of S. mutans. Importantly, BLP decreased the sucrose-dependent adhesion and its auto-aggregation, inhibited the activity of GTF, resulting in reduced EPS and down-regulated genes like gtfB, comA and gbpB. In addition, it inhibited dental caries in rats and ameliorated the microbiota dysbiosis associated with caries.ConclusionsBLP combats caries by inhibiting S. mutans growth, biofilm formation and restoring the oral microbiota.

Introduction

Dental caries is a biofilm-mediated and microbiota-associated disease [1]. Untreated caries may progress to secondary pulpitis and apical periodontitis, potentially triggering inflammatory cascades in the alveolar bone and jaw, significantly compromising patients' quality of life [2,3]. This multi-factorial disease involves complex interactions between host factors, microbial agents, dietary substrates and temporal components [4]. As a pioneer species in cariogenic biofilm formation, Streptococcus mutans (S. mutans) secretes and utilizes glycosyltransferases (GTFs) to produce sucrose-dependent extracellular polysaccharides (EPS). The EPS matrix serves dual functions: acting as a microbial adhesive and providing a three-dimensional scaffold that protects bacteria from environmental stresses. Subsequent microbial encapsulation within the EPS framework enables the utilization of dietary carbohydrates (such as sucrose, glucose and fructose) through biofilm-anchored enzymatic processes. This metabolic conversion generates organic acids that facilitate microbial proliferation and establish an acidic microenvironment (pH 4.5–5.5) within the biofilm structure. The resultant acidic conditions promote hydroxyapatite dissolution, initiating the demineralization-remineralization imbalance characteristic of dental caries progression [5,6].

The oral ecosystem comprises distinct ecological niches (saliva, mucosal surfaces and hard dental tissues), each supporting specialized microbial communities through nutrient provision and optimized growth conditions [7]. Disruption of this ecological equilibrium predisposes the host to oral pathologies like caries and periodontitis. For instance, environmental shifts in the oral cavity, particularly the ecological dominance of S. mutans driven by dysbiosis, disrupt microbial homeostasis and eventually lead to dental caries [8]. Therefore, understanding and modulating this complex ecosystem, rather than merely targeting individual pathogens, has become a central focus in modern caries research. Tools such as 16S rRNA gene sequencing have become indispensable for comprehensive analysis of complex oral microbiomes [9,10].

Current preventive approaches utilizing fluorides, chlorhexidine and antibiotics face limitations due to antibacterial resistance and microbial dysbiosis risks [11,12]. In contrast, strategies aimed at ecological modulation have gained attention. For example, nanofur and the antimicrobial peptide GH12 have been shown to regulate the microbiota and inhibit acid and extracellular polysaccharide production in caries biofilms, highlighting the potential of targeted microbial community management [13]. Emerging research highlights plant-derived polyphenols as promising therapeutic alternatives, leveraging their antioxidant/anti-inflammatory properties and protein-binding abilities to suppress cariogenic virulence factors, thereby demonstrating potential for innovative caries control and treatment modalities [14–16]. For instance, catechins, the primary bioactive constituents in tea polyphenols—exhibit antimicrobial, anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. These compounds not only inhibit the growth and acid production capacity of S. mutans, but also significantly reduce bacterial adhesion to dental surfaces [17]. Proanthocyanidins, identified as active components in cranberries and grape seeds, demonstrate effective anti-S. mutans activity. They suppress the formation of insoluble polysaccharides in the extracellular matrix while preventing glycan-mediated adhesion, co-aggregation and accumulation of proteins in S. mutans. While numerous current studies focus on the effects of polyphenols on specific bacterial species, investigations into their impact on oral microbiota remain relatively scarce [18].

Blueberry leaf polyphenols (BLP), a class of natural plant-derived polyphenols with established therapeutic properties, have garnered extensive interest in the biomedical domain. Compared to berries, leaves not only have a higher polyphenol content but also represent a more stable and sustainable biomass source, making them easier to standardize into clinically applicable extracts. Previous studies proved that BLP can enhance the abundance of beneficial gut microbiota such as Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus, thereby maintaining normal intestinal function [19]. Additionally, BLP alleviates intestinal inflammatory responses by inhibiting the activation of inflammatory signaling pathways and reducing the production of inflammatory mediators, which contributes to the restoration of gut microbial homeostasis [20]. Many studies have shown that BLP has capabilities in suppressing inflammatory cytokines and hepatic inflammation [21]. However, research on the potential of BLP in oral caries management remains scarce. In this study, the biofilm formation inhibition and mechanism of BLP against S. mutans were fully investigated, and its ability to regulate oral microecology was also discovered (Figure 1). Firstly, we identified the major components of BLP using UPLC-MS/MS analysis. To assess its role in combating cariogenic biofilms, we investigated its antibacterial activity, inhibition of adhesion and aggregation and further explored the underlying mechanisms and molecular targets of BLP against S. mutans through qRT‒PCR and molecular docking. Additionally, we evaluated its efficacy in a rodent caries model and its impact on the plaque microbiota via 16S rRNA sequencing. Blueberry leaves are an abundant agricultural by-product. However, their application in the food industry is limited due to a lack of approval as food ingredients, which leads to significant waste of this valuable resource. The exploration of BLP for caries control in this study provides a new perspective for the future utilization of blueberry leaves.

Materials and methods

Materials

Blueberry leaves were sourced from the blueberry base in Majiang County, Guizhou Province. The model strain of S. mutans (UA159) was donated by the Stomatological Laboratory of Zunyi Medical University. SHI (The SHI medium is a novel culture medium developed by Shi's group by combining key nutritional components from peptone-yeast extract-glucose medium (PYG), Modified basal medium mucin (BMM) and N-acetyl muramic acid (NAM) broth. The SHI medium supports highly diverse in vitro microbial communities with profiles closely resembling original saliva-derived oral microflora [22], Purchased from Qingdao Daily Water Biology. Glutaraldehyde, crystal violet solution, sodium fluoride, potassium hydroxide and anhydrous magnesium chloride were purchased from Maclin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). The total RNA extraction kit, reactive oxygen species (ROS) test kit and glucose detection kit were supplied by Biyun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. T12 membrane integrity dye was purchased from Bestbio Co., Ltd. Quercetin and citric acid were supplied by Macklin Co., Ltd.

BLP was extracted by alcohol extraction

Blueberry leaves from Majiang County were dried in a large-scale hot-air drying oven (Yafei, CT-C-II) at 60 °C. The dried leaves were ground into a fine powder in intermittent cycles (15 s on/15 s off, 3 min total) using a traditional Chinese medicine grinder, then sieved through a 200-mesh screen. The material was extracted with 60% ethanol (pH = 4) at a 1:20 ratio and 70 °C for 2 h. Following this, the mixture was rotated and evaporated to concentrate into a black viscous liquid. The BLP concentrate was adjusted to 3 mg/mL with distilled water, pH = 2 and purified using HPD400 macroporous resin. The loading rate was 2 mL/min, and the elution was performed with 40% ethanol at a rate of 1 mL/min. The resulting eluent, an amber clear liquid, was collected and evaporated to concentrate into a black paste. This paste was pre-cooled in a refrigerator at −80 °C overnight and then lyophilized in a freeze-dryer for 12 h to produce a black powder.

Identification of BLP

Ultrahigh-pressure liquid chromatography-triple quadrupole tandem mass spectrometer (UPLC-MS/MS) was used to determine the BLP components. The BLP extract was diluted with chromatography-grade methanol and filtered with a 0.22 μm organic membrane to remove impurities. Chromatography-grade methanol was used to dissolve various standards to prepare a 1 mg/mL mother liquor, which was mixed and diluted into a standard mixture containing 67 standards at serial concentrations of 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500 ppb. Agilent Poroshell C18 Column (150 mm × 2.1 mm, 2.7 μm) was used with column temperature maintained at 35 °C, controlled injection volume: 5 μL, maintained flow rate: 0.3 mL/min, mobile phase composition: 0.1% formic acid in water, methanol. The sample solution was analyzed with Agilent 260 SFC/1290 + 6495, the electrospray ion source model was Agilent ESI-AJS, the scanning was in positive and negative ion mode, the drying gas and spray gas were nitrogen, the drying gas temperature was set to 290 °C, the control gas flow rate was 14 L/min, the spray gas pressure was 20 psi, the sheath gas temperature was 250 °C, the sheath gas flow rate was 11 L/min and the m/z was swept from 100 to 1,000. Mass spectrometry data were acquired using MassHunter (8.0) software.

Bacterial cultivation

S. mutans (UA159) was cultured overnight in brain-heart infusion (BHI) broth under anaerobic conditions with 85% nitrogen, 10% hydrogen and 5% carbon dioxide at 37 °C. The culture was then centrifuged at 5,000 rpm and 4 °C for 10 min and washed using phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and the bacterial cells were resuspended in fresh BHI broth to achieve a final concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL. This suspension served as the inoculum for subsequent experiments.

Growth curve assay

To determine the MIC of BLP against S. mutans, a broth microdilution assay was performed according to the standard protocol. Briefly, twofold serial dilutions of BLP were prepared in BHI broth in a 96-well microplate to achieve final concentrations ranging from 0 to 2,000 μg/mL, with sodium fluoride (NaF, 500 μg/mL) serving as a positive control. An equal volume of a 1:10 dilution of the initial bacterial suspension (1 × 10⁸ CFU/mL) in BHI was then added to each well. Finally, the 96-well polystyrene microplates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h, and their optical density at OD600 nm was measured every 4 h to plot the growth curves of S. mutans.

pH drop assay

The effect of BLP on S. mutans glycolysis was measured as described elsewhere [23]. Centrifuge the initial bacterial suspension with a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL at 5,000 rpm at 4 °C for 10 min, discard the liquid and leave the bacteria at the bottom to collect. Add 5 mL of saline solution (50 mM potassium chloride + 1 mM MgCl2) and discard the liquid after centrifugation to wash away excess bacterial impurities. Add BHI (containing 1% sucrose) and mix well. 4 mL of bacterial solution containing different concentrations (0, 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250 μg/mL) of BLP was used, and the initial pH of the liquid was adjusted with saturated KOH = 7.2~7.4. Within 120 min, the pH value of the culture was measured by pH meter every 20 min.

Sucrose-dependent adhesion assay

The sucrose-dependent adhesion assay was performed according to established methods with modifications [24,25]. This assay was designed to separately quantify planktonic and surface-adhered bacterial cells. Briefly, the initial bacterial suspension with a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL was supplemented with BHI medium containing 1% sucrose at different BLP concentrations (0, 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250 μg/mL). Two milliliters of each mixture were aliquoted into centrifuge tubes. The tubes were then placed at a 30° angle and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. After incubation, the bacterial suspensions were collected and the adhered bacteria on the tube walls were gently rinsed with 1 mL of PBS buffer to remove remaining bacteria adhering to the tube wall. The PBS rinse solution was also collected. The combined bacterial suspensions were mixed with 1 mL of PBS and centrifuged (4 °C, 4,000 × g, 5 min), after which the supernatant was discarded. To dissociate the biofilm, 2 mL of PBS was added to each tube and the samples were vortexed vigorously at 2,500 rpm for 5 min. The optical density at OD600 of the resulting bacterial suspension was measured by using a 200 μL aliquot (denoted as Ad). Similarly, the OD of the original planktonic suspension was measured as At.

Bacterial aggregation assay

The aggregation experiment was carried out according to the method described previously [26]. The initial bacterial suspension with a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL was centrifuged at 12,000 × g, 4 °C for 1 min. Washed twice with PBS, discarded the supernatant, suspended in BHI with 0.1% sucrose to OD600 = 0.7–0.8 with PBS, and recorded the initial OD600 value. The above bacterial solutions containing different concentrations (0, 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250 μg/mL) of BLP were incubated in 24-well plates for 2 h. The OD600 value after incubation was measured, and the OD2 h and ODBlank values were recorded.

Crystal violet assay

The effect of BLP on the biofilm formation of S. mutans was analyzed using crystal violet staining, according to the method described by He Z et al. [27] with modifications. Briefly, the initial bacterial suspension with a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL was incubated overnight in a 96-well plate containing different concentrations of BLP (0, 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250 μg/mL) and 1% sucrose. After incubation, the supernatant was discarded, and the biofilm was washed twice with sterile PBS to remove non-adherent cells. The biofilm was then fixed with 100 μL of 2.5% glutaraldehyde solution overnight, followed by two washes with sterile PBS. Subsequently, the biofilm was stained with 0.4% crystal violet for 30 min and gently rinsed with distilled water to remove excess dye. After drying at 37 °C, the bound crystal violet was dissolved in 150 μL of 95% ethanol, and the absorbance was measured at 550 nm.

GTF activity assay

The GTF activity assay was adapted from established protocols [28,29]. Briefly, the initial bacterial suspension (1 × 108 CFU/mL) was treated with various concentrations of BLP (0, 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250 μg/mL) for 24 h. After incubation, the culture was centrifuged, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was saturated with solid ammonium sulfate and placed at 4 °C overnight to precipitate proteins. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation (2,000 × g, 10 min) and then subjected to three rounds of ultrafiltration to obtain a crude GTF enzyme preparation. The total protein content of the crude GTF extract was quantified using a BCA assay. GTF activity was measured based on the amount of water-soluble glucans formed. The crude enzyme was mixed with PBS containing 1% sucrose and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. The reaction was terminated by heating in a boiling water bath for 10 min. Then, 1.25 mL of double-distilled water was added, followed by centrifugation (2,000 × g, 10 min). The resulting supernatant was mixed with chilled absolute ethanol and stored at 4 °C overnight to precipitate the newly synthesized water-soluble glucans. The precipitate was collected by centrifugation, and the amount of water-soluble glucans was determined using a Glucose Assay Kit, with activity expressed as glucose content. Specifically, 5 μL of the sample or standard was diluted to a final volume of 190 μL in a PCR tube. After vortexing and a brief centrifugation, the tubes were heated at 95 °C for 8 min and then cooled to 4 °C. A 180 μL aliquot from each tube was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 630 nm. The experiment was repeated three times independently. Water-insoluble EPS measurement

Water-insoluble EPS measurement was improved according to the method described by Liu Q et al. [30]. The initial bacterial suspension (1 × 108 CFU/mL) was centrifuged (5,000 r/min, 10 min, 4 °C), washed twice with PBS and resuspended to an OD600 of 0.3–0.5. This suspension, containing BLP (0, 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250 μg/mL) and 2% sucrose, was incubated in 24-well plates for 12 h. The supernatant was discarded, and the biofilm was washed with PBS. Then, the entire biofilm was scraped into a microcentrifuge tube. The collected biofilm was centrifuged (4,000 r/min, 10 min, 4 °C), and the resulting pellet was washed twice with PBS. To solubilize and extract the water-insoluble EPS, 1 mL of 0.4 M NaOH was added to the pellet to solubilize the water-insoluble EPS, and the mixture was incubated for 2 h at room temperature with gentle shaking. The sample was then centrifuged (6,000 r/min, 10 min, 4 °C). After centrifugation, the pellet was discarded. A 200 μL aliquot of the resulting supernatant, which contained the solubilized EPS, was mixed with 600 μL of anthrone reagent and heated at 95 °C for 8 min in a dry thermostat. After cooling, 200 μL of the solution was transferred to a 96-well plate, and the absorbance was measured at 625 nm.

Biofilm formation

Mono-species biofilms of S. mutans were formed on circular slides. The initial bacterial suspension (1 × 108 CFU/mL) was first treated with different concentrations of BLP (0, 125, 250 μg/mL) and 1% sucrose, and then incubated for 24 h to allow biofilm formation. The polymicrobial biofilm model was established according to the method described by Edlund A et al. [31] with modifications. Briefly, saliva was collected from five healthy volunteers (DMFT = 0, CPI = 0 and no use of antibiotics within 3 months, smoking, or antiseptic mouthwashes within 4 weeks) who provided written informed consent. Each donor contributed 1.5 mL, and the samples were pooled and mixed. After collection, saliva was diluted in glycerol (70% saliva and 30% glycerol), centrifuged at 2,600 × g for 10 min to remove debris. The same aliquot batch was used for all experiments within a given replicate to ensure consistency. Prior to saliva inoculation into SHI medium, within a sterile 24-well plate, 200 μL of saliva supernatant was added to each well. This pre-coating of wells allowed attached pellicle growth. The plates were then incubated at 37 °C with the lid open for 1 h to dry the saliva coating. Plates were then sterilized under UV light for 1 h before 150 μL of saliva was inoculated into a pre-coated well containing 850 μL SHI medium. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 16 h to allow biofilm formation. From day 2 to day 5, the developing biofilms were divided into the following three groups for intervention: a control group (simulating oral biofilms from caries-free individuals), a caries model group (simulating biofilms under cariogenic conditions) and a BLP group (simulating biofilms treated with BLP during caries development). Each day, the biofilms in each group underwent a 10-min immersion treatment (the treatment group with 250 μg/mL BLP solution, the other two groups with PBS). After each treatment, the processing solution was removed and replaced with fresh SHI medium to continue cultivation. The medium for the caries model group and the treatment group was supplemented with 5% sucrose to simulate the high-sugar environment associated with caries development, thereby promoting the continued formation of polymicrobial biofilms. These standardized procedures in saliva collection, processing and biofilm culture were implemented to ensure the reproducibility of the polymicrobial biofilm model across experimental replicates.

SEM

The mono-species biofilms and polymicrobial biofilms were placed in a dark condition containing glutaraldehyde (4 °C, 2%) for 4 h, and then subjected to gradient dehydration with ethanol (30%, 50%, 70%, 90%, 95% and 100% twice) and drying. The samples were placed on the double-sided adhesive of the conductive carbon film on the sample stage of the ion sputtering instrument for gold spraying, and the images were observed under the scanning electron microscope (SEM5000X, Guoyi Quantum, China).

Molecular docking

MOE is a comprehensive software platform for molecular simulation of drug design, utilizing MOE2020 for docking. Among them, the protein structures of GtfB, GtfC and GtfD of S. mutans were downloaded from the UniProt database, and the protein structure was optimized by Fast Preparation module, and all water molecules were removed as acceptors. Download the main structural substances of BLP (quercetin, citric acid, chlorogenic acid) from PUBCHEM to minimize their energy as ligands. The binding site for docking was defined based on the catalytic pocket identified in the crystal structures of S. mutans Gtfs. Specifically, we focused on the region encompassing the key catalytic residues Asp451 and Glu489 in GtfB (and their analogous residues in GtfC and GtfD) as determined by structural studies [32]. Docking calculations were performed using the Triangle Matcher placement method within MOE. These poses were then scored and ranked using the GBVI/WSA dG scoring function, which estimates the binding free energy. After the docking was completed, the highest-ranking further studies are selected according to the association between their energy and active site residues.

RNA isolation and quantitative real-time PCR (qRT–PCR)

The qRT‒PCR experiments were performed and reported following the MIQE guidelines. Expression of S. mutans biofilm-associated genes (gtfB, gtfC, gtfD, vicR, comA, comE, gbpB) [33,34] under the treatment of BLP were investigated by qRT–PCR. The sequences of all the primers used for qRT‒PCR are provided in Table S2. The initial bacterial suspension with a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL was treated with 250 μg/mL BLP for 12 h in the culture medium. After 8,000 rpm, 4 °C, centrifugation for 5 min, and then rinsed twice with DEPC water, centrifuged for 2 min each time. Then the cells were incubated with lysozyme (20 mg/mL) at 37 °C for 20 min. According to the manufacturer's instructions, total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent. The cDNA was generated by HiScript II qRT SuperMix II, and qRT‒PCR analysis was performed on 96-well plates. Reaction mixture: total volume of 10 μL, composed of 1 μL cDNA, 5 μL 2 × ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix, forward and reverse primers (0.2 μL each), 3.6 μL dd water.

Membrane integrity assay

The membrane integrity of S. mutans was assessed using the T12 membrane integrity dye. The T12 dye was diluted 200-fold with the provided diluent to prepare the staining working solution according to the manufacturer's instructions. The initial bacterial suspension with a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL was treated with various concentrations of BLP (0, 31.25, 62.5, 125, 250 μg/mL) for 24 h. The cells were then collected by centrifugation, and the supernatant was discarded. The pellets were incubated with T12 dye at 37 °C for 10 min. Following incubation, the cells were centrifuged at 5,000 r/min for 10 min and washed twice with PBS to remove unbound dye. Finally, the bacterial pellets were resuspended in PBS, and the optical density at 620 nm was measured using a microplate reader. T12 dye incorporation increases upon membrane damage, leading to increased absorbance.

ROS assay

DCFH-DA was diluted 1:1,000 in BHI broth to a final concentration of 10 μmol/L. The initial bacterial suspension with a concentration of 1 × 108 CFU/mL was treated for 24 h with various concentrations of BLP (0, 31.25, 62.5, 125 and 250 μg/mL), then collected and incubated with the diluted DCFH-DA solution at 37 °C for 20 min. During incubation, the mixture was gently agitated every 3–5 min to ensure sufficient contact between the probe and cells. Afterwards, the cells were washed three times with PBS to thoroughly remove any extracellular DCFH-DA. Fluorescence intensity was measured using a microplate reader with an excitation wavelength of 488 nm.

In vivo rodent model

A total of thirty 21-day-old male Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats were acclimatized for 3 days under standard laboratory conditions and then randomly divided into six groups using a computer-generated random number table: a negative control group (treated with sterile distilled water), three BLP treatment groups (treated with 250, 1,000 and 2,000 μg/mL BLP solutions, respectively), a positive control group (treated with sodium fluoride solution), and a blank control group (treated with sterile distilled water and fed a normal diet). The sample size of five rats per group (n = 5) was determined based on established practices in previous similar caries model studies and supported by a prospective power calculation (Cohen's f = 0.8, α = 0.05, β = 0.20). The experimental unit was a single animal. To establish the caries model, all rats except the blank control group received broad-spectrum antibiotics (carbenicillin, ampicillin and chloramphenicol) administered via drinking water from days 21 to 24 of age to suppress the indigenous oral flora. Subsequently, from days 24 to 29, they were orally inoculated daily with a suspension of S. mutans (1 × 108 CFU/mL) and continuously fed Keyes 2000 # diet until the end of the experimental period to induce severe dental caries. After the bacterial inoculation phase, topical treatments began on day 29. Under anesthesia, a cotton swab soaked with 200 μL of the respective treatment solution was applied to the rat tooth surfaces for 5 min every 48 h. General conditions, including body weight, fur color and activity levels, were monitored and recorded regularly throughout the study. At the end of the administration at 66 days of age, the rats were sacrificed and the jawbone specimens were obtained. To remove food debris, all rats had their molar surfaces wiped with sterile cotton swabs 15 h before euthanasia, followed by fasting. The dental plaque was quantitatively evaluated using the method of Shuhei Naka et al. [35]. After cleaning, the specimens were autoclaved for 15 min, and placed in a container with 0.4% violet amine solution for 12 h. The rat jaw was taken out and dried in the dark at room temperature. The unilateral rat mandible was observed under a stereomicroscope along the mesial and distal sagittal half of the occlusal surface of the jaw molar. Sulcal caries in the rat molars was scored according to the Keyes scoring system [36]. In addition, the heart, liver, spleen, lung, kidney and testis were collected, and their organ indices were recorded. Tissue samples harvested from the submandibular gland, stomach and liver were stained with hematoxylin and eosin and examined under a microscope. No data or animals were excluded from the analysis. No specific strategies were used to control for the order of treatments and measurements or the location of animal cages.

16S rRNA

The rats fed with normal diet were set as the normal group (NR), the above-mentioned 1,000 μg/mL drugs were fed as the BLP treatment group (TR) and the sterile distilled water intervention was the model group (MR). Samples were collected from the buccal mucosa, hard palate, dorsal surface of the tongue, gingiva and occlusal plaque. The samples were then lysed using the cell lysis buffer from the DNeasy PowerSoil HTP Kit (Qiagen) according to the manufacturer's instructions. After vortexing for 60 s, DNA was isolated using the same kit and quantified with a spectrophotometer. The hypervariable V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified with the universal primers 515F (5′-GTGCCAGCMGCCGCGGTAA-3′) and 806 R (5′-GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-3′). A unique 8-bp barcode was incorporated into the forward primer for sample multiplexing. PCR reactions were performed in a 50 μL system containing 5 μL of 10 × KOD Buffer, 5 μL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 1.5 μL of each primer (5 μM), 1 μL of KOD Polymerase and 100 ng of template DNA. The thermal cycling conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 2 min; 27 cycles of denaturation at 98 °C for 10 s, annealing at 62 °C for 30 s and extension at 68 °C for 30 s; followed by a final extension at 68 °C for 10 min. Each sample was amplified in triplicate. The PCR amplicons were visualized on 2% agarose gels, purified using the AxyPrep DNA Gel Extraction Kit (Axygen Biosciences, Union City, CA, USA) and quantified with the ABI StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies, Foster City, USA). Equimolar amounts of the purified amplicons from all samples were pooled together. The pooled library was then subjected to paired-end sequencing (2 × 250 bp) on an Illumina HiSeq 2,500 platform following the manufacturer's guidelines. Sequences were clustered into operational taxonomic units (OTUs) using the UCLUST method with a 97% similarity threshold. Classification assignments were obtained based on the GreenGenes 16S rRNA gene database. The Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used to compare diversity, richness and bacterial taxon abundance.

Statistical analysis

All in vitro experiments were performed with at least three independent replicates. Data were analyzed using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett's multiple comparison test, and two-factor ANOVA with Sidak's multiple comparison test. All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and each experiment was repeated at least three times for comparative testing. Images were drawn using Graphpad Prism 8.4.0, and in this study, the significance of the data was expressed as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ns = not significant.

Result

Compositional profiling of blueberry leaf polyphenols (BLP)

The chemical constituents of BLP were analyzed using UPLC-MS/MS, with representative total ion chromatograms obtained under different analytical conditions presented in Figure 2. UPLC-MS/MS analysis enabled the identification of 45 bioactive compounds, which were categorized into distinct chemical classes: 9 organic acids, 8 flavonoids, 8 phenolic compounds, 8 esters, 6 glycosides, 2 phenolic acids and 4 other compounds (Table S1). Among the bioactive constituents identified were quercetin, chlorogenic acid, citric acid, gallic acid and guava acid, along with other phytochemicals. These compounds were closely aligned with previous studies reporting similar phytochemical compositions in blueberry leaf extracts [19,37].

Growth suppression and acidogenesis attenuation in S. mutans by BLP

We evaluated the effects of BLP with different concentrations on the growth rate of S. mutans by real-time growth curve assay. It was observed that in comparison to the control, BLP treatment induced concentration-dependent growth inhibition (Figure 3a). The MIC of BLP against S. mutans was 500 μg/mL. In the meantime, the antibacterial activities of two BLP constituents quercetin and citric acid were further detected, and the results demonstrated that those bioactive constituents showed significant bacteriostatic effects in separate validation experiments, with MIC of 1.28 and 4 mg/mL, respectively (Fig. S2).

(a) Growth curves of S. mutans treated with BLP or Sodium Fluoride (NaF, positive control) (b) pH changes during sucrose metabolism following BLP treatment. Data are presented as mean ± SD (n = 3). Given that lactic acid production by S. mutans constitutes a critical cariogenic mechanism through enamel demineralization, we further investigated the anti-acidogenic ability of BLP using glycolytic pH drop assay. S. mutans was cultured in the presence of various concentrations of BLP, and the pH change was measured. As shown in Figure 3b, the pH decreased from 7.35 ± 0.07 to 4.81 ± 0.01 after 120 min of incubation in the control group. The terminal pH was raised from this acidic pH (4.81 ± 0.01) to 4.93 ± 0.06, 5.41 ± 0.03, 5.34 ± 0.10, 5.91 ± 0.21 after treatment with BLP (31.25, 62.5, 125 and 250 μg/mL).

Anti-biofilm effects of BLP

Dental caries is a classic biofilm-induced disease. The biofilm not only impairs drug penetration but also induces bacterial drug tolerance, while simultaneously enhancing the mechanical stability of the biofilm structure [38,39]. Therefore, developing more effective anti-infective therapies requires strategies that specifically target biofilm disruption. As shown in Figure 4a, treatment with 250 μg/mL BLP significantly reduced the bacterial adhesion rate to 18.03 ± 0.73%, compared to 23.51 ± 0.20% in the untreated control (P < 0.001). Additionally, BLP demonstrated dose-dependent anti-aggregation activity across concentrations ranging from 31.25 to 250 μg/mL, achieving near-complete inhibition at the highest concentration compared to the control group (P < 0.001, Figure 4b). Furthermore, crystal violet quantification confirmed biofilm suppression by BLP, revealing significant inhibition (P < 0.001) even at the minimal concentration of 31.25 μg/mL (Figure 4c). Importantly, the anti-biofilm effect was mediated through dose-dependent suppression of glucosyltransferase (GTF) activity and EPS production. Specifically, GTF activity was suppressed by 62.31 ± 12.78% at 250 μg/mL of BLP (Figure 4d). In addition, the EPS production was also significantly reduced at 250 μg/mL of BLP (Figure 4e, P < 0.001). These results indicated that BLP suppressed biofilm formation by reducing bacterial adhesion and aggregation, and diminishing GTF activity, resulting in a reduction in extracellular polysaccharide (EPS) synthesis.

SEM reveals disruption of mono- and polymicrobial biofilms by BLP

Encouraged by the prominent biofilm suppression effect of BLP against S. mutans, the morphological changes were investigated by SEM in both mono- and polymicrobial biofilms following BLP treatment. As shown in Figure 5, in mono-species biofilms, S. mutans bacteria attached and colonized to the circular slide, forming a layer of biofilm. In the control group, a large number of S. mutans formed a dense structure with an adhesive extracellular matrix-like structure between the bacteria. However, in the BLP treatment groups, the biofilms were sharply reduced and their structural disintegration was induced, which were characterized by reduced bacterial adhesion, dispersion of microbial clusters and loss of matrix integrity.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of S. mutans mono-species biofilms treated with BLP (0, 125 and 250 μg/mL). Scale bars: 4 μm.

For polymicrobial biofilms, the control group only cultured saliva to represent the biofilm of healthy individuals. The model group and the BLP group also added high concentration of sucrose to represent the biofilm during caries. At the same time, 250 μg/mL BLP was applied to the BLP group to evaluate the inhibition of BLP on biofilm formation. As shown in Fig. S2, the caries model group exhibited substantial biofilm biomass with spherical-shaped bacteria arranged in chains that resemble Streptococcus (indicated by arrows). However, BLP treatment markedly reduced biofilm biomass, achieving levels comparable to the control group.

Molecular docking showed that the main components of BLP competitively bound to GTFs

The multi-constituent nature of BLP requires systematic investigation of ligand-protein interactions to elucidate its anti-cariogenic mechanism. The above results proved that GTFs activity was inhibited by BLP. Therefore, to elucidate the molecular targets for anti-cariogenic activity of BLP, we performed molecular docking analysis targeting glucosyltransferase isoforms (GtfB, GtfC, GtfD) with representative BLP components, such as quercetin, citric acid and chlorogenic acid. Table 1 and Figure 6 reveal the highly specific interactions between those BLP components and (GtfB, GtfC, GtfD). Specifically, the docking scores of the binding strength between BLP components and GtfB, GtfC and GtfD were all lower than −4 (Table 1), indicating that the active components in BLP can stably bind to GtfB, GtfC and GtfD proteins. Specifically, quercetin, citric acid and chlorogenic acid mainly form conjugation with amino acid residues such as glutamic acid, aspartic acid and asparagine in the active pockets of GtfB, GtfC and GtfD, thereby enhancing the binding ability of BLP to GtfB, GtfC and GtfD. These interactions constitute the basis for the binding of small molecules to proteins (Figure 6).

BLP downregulates biofilm-associated genes expression in S. mutans

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying biofilm suppression by BLP, we analyzed transcriptional alterations in critical biofilm-associated genes via qRT–PCR. Among the studied genes, three genes were found to be involved in encoding glucosyltransferase (gtfB, gtfC, gtfD), two genes were found to be related to quorum-sensing system (comA, comE) and two genes were found to be related to adhesion and virulence (vicR, gbpB). As shown in Figure 7, all tested genes were significantly down-regulated in the biofilms treated with 250 μg/mL BLP compared to the control group (P < 0.001). After BLP treatment, the expressions of gtfD and comE were both significantly reduced to 0.00013-fold of the control group. And expression levels of other genes associated with biofilm formation were reduced in the range from 0.011 to 0.132 fold.

The mechanism of BLP exerting antibacterial effect by destroying cell membrane integrity

The above results indicate that BLP can inhibit biofilm formation by targeting GTFs and down-regulating biofilm-related genes. In order to discover the antibacterial mechanisms of BLP against S. mutans, the membrane integrity and reactive oxygen species (ROS) experiments were carried out. As shown in Figure 8a, quantification of membrane permeabilization using T12 dye revealed progressive damage, with OD620 values increasing from 0.12 ± 0.003 (untreated control) to 3.56 ± 0.006 at 250 μg/mL BLP. Indicating BLP exerted concentration-dependent bactericidal effects by compromising membrane integrity. Besides, ROS levels in S. mutans were assessed using 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA). Figure 8b showed that the untreated control exhibited a fluorescence intensity of 493.9 ± 4.09. No significant changes in ROS levels were observed at BLP concentrations below 125 μg/mL. Intriguingly, treatment with 125 μg/mL and higher BLP concentrations paradoxically reduced ROS production, which suggests BLP may possess ROS-scavenging activity at bactericidal doses. These trends indicate that antimicrobial action of BLP is not mediated by ROS production but rather by compromising bacterial membrane integrity.

BLP slows the progression of caries in rat models

Building upon previous mechanistic studies demonstrating inhibitory effects of BLP on S. mutans cariogenicity through multifaceted mechanisms including target binding, transcriptional regulation and cellular damage, as well as its suppression of human saliva-derived multispecies biofilm formation, we subsequently investigated its anti-caries effect using rat models. Firstly, body weight was monitored throughout the experimental period to evaluate the safety of BLP. All groups maintained comparable weight gain trajectories, indicating no treatment-related systemic toxicity (Figure 9b). In addition, post-experiment organ weight measurements revealed no significant variations in organ indices across all groups (Figure 9c). Furthermore, histopathological evaluation by hematoxylin-eosin staining confirmed the absence of pathological alterations in major organs, including hyperplastic changes, inflammatory infiltrates, or necrotic lesions (Figure 9d). Taken together, these data suggested that topical BLP treatments exhibited no in vivo toxicity and remarkable biocompatibility.

Macroscopic observation showed that dental plaque staining in rats revealed a significant reduction in plaque formation in the BLP group (Figure 10a). BLP restores dysbiosis of oral microbiota

To systematically investigate the regulatory effects of BLP on high-sugar diet-induced dysbiosis of oral microbiota, we performed 16S rRNA sequencing to analyze structural and functional shifts in the rat oral microbiome. The Venn diagram showed 1,015, 2,198 and 2,436 unique OTUs in the normal group (NR), model group (MR), and BLP treatment group (TR), while all three groups shared 189 OTUs (Figure 11a). Plateauing of rarefaction curves (Fig. S3) confirmed sufficient sequencing depth. Compared to the MR, the TR exhibited no significant differences in Chao1 indices, but in the Simpson (P < 0.001) and Shannon (P < 0.05) indices, significant differences were observed (Fig. S4). β-Diversity analysis further revealed structural changes. Bray-Curtis-based PCoA (Figure 11b) explained 83.08% of total variance (PC1 = 51.86%, PC2 = 31.22%), the TR exhibited closer clustering toward the NR along PC1 relative to the model group. NMDS results (Figure 11c) corroborated this trend (stress = 0.002). ANOSIM confirmed intergroup differences predominated over intragroup variations (R = 1, P = 0.001; Fig. S5), validating intervention-driven effects. At the phylum level (Figure 11d), Firmicutes, Actinobacteria, Proteobacteria and Bacteroidetes dominated all groups. The MR exhibited an increase in Firmicutes and a reduction in Proteobacteria versus the NR. This phenomenon was improved in the TR, so that the species structure of the treatment group was similar to that of the NR. Genus-level (Figure 11e) revealed a significant increase in the relative abundance of Lactobacillus, Allobaculum, Bifidobacterium and Enterococcus, coupled with a reduction in Rothia in the MR compared to the NR. TR reversed these trends, reducing the elevated taxa while rescuing Rothia abundance. Species-level (Figure 11f) confirmed that the abundance of S. mutans was enriched in the MR, whereas it was reduced after BLP treatment in the TR. In conclusion, a high-sugar diet induced changes in oral flora, while TR regulated the abundance of specific genera to improve the changes of oral flora induced by a high-sugar diet (Figure 11g, h, i, j). Screening of taxa with > 0.1% abundance (Figure 11k) identified Lactobacillus johnsonii, Faecalibaculum rodentium and Lactobacillus reuteri as potentially pathogenic species suppressed by BLP treatment.

In order to further explore the effect of BLP on oral flora, we used Phylogenetic Investigation of Communities by Reconstruction of Unobserved States (PICRUSt) functional analysis to analyze the functional changes of oral flora in rats. PICRUSt functional prediction (Figure 12a) revealed significant activation of folate biosynthesis and nitrogen metabolism in the TR. Discussion

Dental caries is the most prevalent oral disease, which seriously endangers public health. Dysbiosis of microbial biofilm in a sugar-rich environment mediated mainly by S. mutans plays a key role in dental caries development [40,41]. Our study reveals the potential of BLP to inhibit caries through various mechanisms. Distinct from conventional antimicrobial strategies predicated on singular bactericidal mechanisms, BLP demonstrates a unique tripartite synergy through integrated pathogen suppression, biofilm disruption and microecological remodeling.

First of all, UPLC‒MS/MS analysis identified quercetin, gallic acid, citric acid and other antimicrobial compounds as the principal constituents of BLP. These phytochemicals have been previously documented to exhibit inhibitory effects against S. mutans growth [42–44]. The concentrations of BLP used in this study were selected based on its biological activity. The in vitro range bracketed its MIC against S. mutans (500 μg/mL). Our experiment revealed that BLP demonstrated potent antibacterial activity against S. mutans and dose-dependently suppressed acidogenicity. Notably, the antibacterial activity of BLP is not attributable to a single predominant component but is likely the result of the complex mixture of phytochemicals present in the extract. This synergistic effect among constituents enhances the overall efficacy. Furthermore, the decrease of adhesion and aggregation confirmed that BLP began to exert a good biofilm inhibition effect from the initial stage of biofilm formation. Mechanistically, this inhibition correlates with suppressed glucosyltransferase (GTF) activity, which are enzymes responsible for synthesizing glucans from sucrose. These glucans constitute the structural backbone of the EPS [45], facilitating bacterial adhesion and enamel colonization [46]. EPS production decreased, directly linking GTF inhibition to attenuated EPS biosynthesis, therefore reducing the biofilm formation. In vivo models, low, medium and high doses (250, 1,000 and 2,000 μg/mL) were administered to verify the inhibitory effect on caries in rats. The results showed that BLP had a concentration-dependent inhibitory effect on the progression of caries in rats.

The molecular mechanism of BLP inhibiting biofilm formation was elucidated by qRT–PCR. BLP treatment triggered downregulation of biofilm-associated genes, implicating several kinds of pathways. First, gtfB, gtfC, gtfD collectively encode glucosyltransferases responsible for synthesizing glucans, which serve as the biomechanical backbone of the EPS [45]. The transcriptional inhibition of these genes reduces glucan production, directly destabilizing the biofilm architecture. Furthermore, molecular docking revealed successful binding interactions between primary constituents of BLP and the catalytic domains of gtfB, gtfC, gtfD encoded enzymes (GtfB, GtfC, GtfD). This evidence suggests that BLP may directly inhibit glucosyltransferase activity by occupying substrate-binding pockets, providing a structural rationale for the observed gene suppression. Secondly, BLP inhibits the expression of important comA and comE in quorum sensing (QS) system [47]. S. mutans can use the QS system to regulate the expression of a variety of phenotypic genes, which is essential for virulence and biofilm formation [48]. Therefore, BLP down-regulation of QS-related genes is beneficial to interfere with the information exchange of S. mutans, regulate biofilm and virulence. Third, gbpB gene expresses an adhesion protein named glucan-binding protein B facilitating the binding of glucan to the physical and biological surfaces. VicR binds to the promoter regions of gtfB and gtfC genes to play a responsive regulatory role, thereby activating these genes and promoting biofilm formation [49]. vicR encodes the essential virulence factors that promote biofilm formation of S. mutans. Therefore, BLP inhibits the expression of gbpB and vicR to further inhibit biofilm formation. In summary, BLP inhibits S. mutans biofilm formation by concurrently targeting genes involved in EPS synthesis, QS system and virulence.

To elucidate another antimicrobial mechanism of BLP, we investigated its effects on bacterial membrane integrity and intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS). We found that BLP can disrupt the integrity of the cell membrane of S. mutans, which is essential for energy production, pH and nutrient homeostasis and plays an important role as a cell permeability barrier [50]. Therefore, the destruction of membrane integrity by BLP is an important mechanism for its efficacy. Intriguingly, a declining trend in ROS levels was observed at BLP concentrations of 250 μg/mL, potentially attributable to the inherent antioxidant capacity of BLP. This phenomenon aligns with extensive studies documenting the robust antioxidant properties of polyphenolic compounds [51]. Given the presence of phenolic hydroxyl groups in BLP constituents, we postulate that the antioxidant properties of BLP arise from these characteristic structural features [52]. This scavenging of ROS, rather than its generation, distinguishes BLP from many conventional antimicrobial agents, such as β-lactam antibiotics [53] and silver nanoparticles [54], which exert their bactericidal effects primarily through the accumulation of intracellular ROS. An excessive burst of ROS, while effective against pathogens, is often nonselective and can indiscriminately harm beneficial commensal bacteria, thereby exacerbating dysbiosis. However, this antioxidant capacity of BLP likely contributes to a more favorable microenvironment for the recovery and maintenance of a healthy oral microbiome, suppressing cariogenic pathogens without collateral damage to the indigenous flora. In addition, this mechanistic insight confirms that the inhibition of BLP is mediated not through the oxidative stress pathway, but as described above, by disruption of membrane integrity.

The etiological understanding of dental caries has shifted from the traditional ‘specific plaque hypothesis’ to the ‘ecological plaque hypothesis’, emphasizing the critical role of oral microbiota in maintaining oral homeostasis [55]. Therefore, we explored the inhibitory ability of BLP on composite biofilm. We used human saliva as the inoculation source and SHI as the culture medium to construct a biofilm model with bacterial composition, pH changes, biochemical characteristics and complexity more similar to natural biofilms [31]. Experiments showed that BLP also had an inhibitory effect on saliva-derived caries biofilms. In addition, we analyzed the changes in oral flora in rats after a high-sugar diet and BLP intervention by 16S rRNA. The 1,000 μg/mL dose was a representative effective concentration that showed significant anti-caries effects in vivo. Selecting this intermediate concentration ensured that it was above the MIC, thereby effectively reducing S. mutans, while also allowing us to assess whether BLP would disturb the broader commensal microbiota at this treatment level. The results demonstrated that BLP counteracts oral dysbiosis by modulating taxonomic composition, restoring metabolic functionality and enriching commensal species. In terms of taxonomic composition, the high-sugar diet markedly increased the abundance of S. mutans, Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium and other species, significantly altering the composition of the oral microbiota. Among these, S. mutans is a well-established primary cariogenic pathogen. Lactobacillus contributes to caries by fermenting carbohydrates to produce lactic acid, which facilitates the formation of an acidic microenvironment. Similarly, Bifidobacterium exhibits acid-producing capabilities and enhances biofilm formation, suggesting its potential role in cariogenesis [56]. BLP suppressed the proliferation of these caries-associated strains, highlighting its therapeutic potential for dental caries control. Significantly, BLP intervention elevated the abundance of Rothia, a nitrate-reducing genus [57], which generates nitrite to locally increase pH levels, thereby counteracting the acidification caused by S. mutans. This pH modulation may serve as a pivotal trigger for shifting the relative abundance of acid-resistant bacteria, promoting the restoration of oral microbial equilibrium.

Further PICRUSt functional and LEfSe analysis revealed that BLP counteracted oral dysbiosis in terms of metabolic functionality and commensal species. Specifically, the treatment group exhibited significant enrichment in nitrogen metabolism and folate biosynthesis pathways, accompanied by increased abundances of Pasteurellaceae, Staphylococcus and Burkholderiales. A potential mechanism involves nitrogen metabolism, the intervention of BLP increases Pasteurellaceae, Staphylococcus and Streptococcus_respiraculi abundance, thereby increasing the nitrogen metabolism to increase the local pH and buffer the acid environment during caries formation [58,59]. Furthermore, the enhancement of folate biosynthesis observed in the BLP-treated group may contribute to caries control by mitigating oxidative stress. This is supported by the increased abundance of Burkholderia, a genus with known folate synthesis capabilities [60]. Elevated folate levels can reduce homocysteine accumulation and subsequent ROS production [61], thereby protecting dental hard tissues from oxidative damage and slowing caries progression [62]. Collectively, our findings demonstrate that BLP counteracts high-sugar diet-induced oral dysbiosis by modulating taxonomic composition, restoring metabolic functionality and enriching commensal species, thereby offering a microbiome-targeted strategy for caries management. Thus, BLP represents a promising antibacterial agent for caries control. Its translational potential is supported by clinical evidence, such as a trial demonstrating the efficacy of an epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) mouthrinse against cariogenic microbiota, underscoring the value of polyphenol-based oral care strategies [63].

The occurrence of dental caries often involves changes in the number and richness of oral flora, and 16S rRNA sequencing provides a comprehensive view of the microbial community, which can identify all the bacterial and archaeal species present in saliva samples. This comprehensiveness helps to understand the structure and diversity of the microbial community [64]. However it may not be enough to rely solely on 16S rRNA sequencing to study oral microecology at the species level. It is necessary to combine multi-omics techniques to study oral host‒microbiome interactions. For example, proteomics can be used to analyze protein activity, metagenomics and metatranscriptome can be used to analyze microbial functional genes and their expression activities, and metabolomics can be used to detect the effects of host and microbial metabolites on bacteria. In short, it is more valuable to comprehensively analyze BLP's effects on caries formation through multi-omics techniques. The current in vivo model mainly depends on rodents. Its advantage is that the genetic background is clear and similar to human physiological function. It can induce caries lesions similar to those in humans by adjusting the diet structure and oral microbial environment of rodents [65]. Notably, because the saliva flow, pH value and buffering capacity in the human oral cavity are also different from those of rodents, the oral microbial community of rodents may not be able to fully simulate the human situation, especially in studies involving microbial interactions and community dynamics [66]. In practical applications, species differences must be taken into account and necessary adjustments and verifications must be made. In summary, considering the culture of human saliva combined with multi-omics technology for oral microecology research may be more helpful for the study of oral microecological imbalance diseases.

Conclusion

Our findings demonstrate that blueberry leaf polyphenols (BLP) disrupt critical cariogenic processes by attenuating bacterial growth, acid production and biofilm formation. Mechanistically, BLP inhibits the expression of biofilm-related genes, destroys the integrity of bacterial membranes and scavenges reactive oxygen species against oxidative stress. Beyond direct antimicrobial effects, BLP alleviates oral dysbiosis by lowering the relative abundance of cariogenic genera such as S. mutans and Lactobacillus, while elevating the levels of health-associated Rothia. In rat models, these effects translate to significant caries reduction. These results position BLP as a promising natural alternative to conventional antimicrobials.
