Measuring antioxidant capacity directly in emulsions: A new paper-based FRAP assay and comparison with its DPPH counterpart✰
Authors: Georgia Eleni Tsotsou, Garyfallia Petraki, Foteini Chourda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A paper-based Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP) assay was developed to monitor the antioxidant potential of emulsions without prior extraction. The assay successfully quantified the antioxidant capacity of two hydrophilic antioxidants - ascorbic acid and N-acetyl-L-cysteine - showing an initially linear concentration-response relationship (R2 ≥0.934) and produced half-maximal effective concentration values consistent with literature. However, the assay exhibited significant limitations regarding lipophilic antioxidants and water-in-oil emulsions, a result attributed to the FRAP reagent’s inherent hydrophilicity. The assay met official guidelines for precision and accuracy when analyzing quality controls but showed decreased accuracy and increased variability upon analysis of commercial emulsions. Nonetheless, its level of performance remains acceptable for cosmetic and food industry applications, where comparative analysis and stability profiling are often more relevant. Comparative studies showed that its DPPH counterpart is more versatile, working with a broader range of antioxidants and with both oil-in-water and water-in-oil emulsions, all while having fewer interferents.Method outline• Analysis of antioxidant capacity of emulsions is based on measurement of reflected light intensity by a smartphone camera• An extraction-free paper-based format has been validated employing an adapted FRAP methodology.• Limitations of the FRAP-based format when compared to its DPPH counterpart are presented, relevant to the hydrophobicity of the antioxidant under study and the emulsion type.

Background

Antioxidants in food or cosmetic emulsions serve a double vital role: they prevent the breakdown of the product's ingredients due to oxidation, thereby extending shelf life and maintaining quality. They additionally offer protection to consumers, whether through ingestion of food or application to the skin, against the harmful effects of oxidative stress [1,2]. To guarantee effective and safe products for consumers, rigorous quality control is essential before market release, including antioxidant capacity measurement and antioxidant stability studies over time, given the known instability of antioxidants. Traditional such methods are spectroscopic assays performed in solutions, after antioxidant extraction from the emulsion. The two most common examples are the 2,2-Diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl (DPPH) radical scavenging assay and the Ferric Reducing Antioxidant Power (FRAP) assay, with fundamental differences in their mechanisms and reaction conditions [3]. The choice between the DPPH and FRAP assays when applied in solutions is heavily influenced by the hydrophilicity - hydrophobicity of the antioxidants being studied. Crucially, the FRAP assay is typically performed in an aqueous medium, whereas the DPPH assay is conducted in organic solvents, allowing the latter to monitor antioxidant activity across a wider range of molecular polarities [3].

While these solution-based methods are well established, their application to emulsions presents significant challenges. In these complex systems, an antioxidant's effectiveness is not solely determined by its chemical capacity. Antioxidant partitioning within the emulsion's phases and emulsion characteristics can affect antioxidant efficacy, making efficiency in inhibiting oxidation not always directly related to the antioxidant capacity, as typically measured in solution after extraction [1,4]. This phase-dependent antioxidant activity means that a highly potent antioxidant in a homogeneous solution might be significantly less effective in an emulsion, if trapped in a phase where it cannot interact with the oxidizing species.

We have already described a new assay format for measuring the antioxidant capacity directly in emulsions, after application of a thin emulsion layer on a paper strip spotted with DPPH radical [5,6]. The antioxidant capacity was assessed by capturing images of the resulting colour change through a smartphone camera. This approach eliminates the need for complex extraction processes and enables direct, extraction-free analysis of emulsions, as a cost-effective, simpler, and greener alternative to traditional extraction-based assays. The method has been tested in various emulsion formulations, both oil-in-water (O/W) and water-in-oil (W/O), and we have obtained promising results, including the accurate quantification of compounds like N-acetyl cysteine (NAC), α-tocopheryl acetate (α-TA) and ascorbic acid (AA) [5,6].

Building on previous success with the DPPH assay, we here present the adaptation of the new analytical paper-based format to the FRAP reaction. We validate this adapted FRAP assay and provide a comprehensive comparison of its applicability and limitations against the DPPH assay, with respect to the lipophilicity of antioxidants and type of emulsions. We finally identify interferents unique to the FRAP assay and propose a practical mitigation for analysis of interferent-rich matrices. As such, this article aims to provide guidance on the strengths and limitations of the paper-based methods to in-situ quality control in the cosmetics, pharmaceutical and food industries.

Method details

A video of the procedure can be found in the below link: https://youtube.com/shorts/IjmZ86KiCu8

The assay is run at room temperature

Antioxidant standards are freshly prepared in a suitable, defined Oil-in-Water (O/W) emulsion matrix. The matrix should be free from any assay interferent. An example of a suitable emulsion has the following composition: Aqua 81.7 % w/w, Paraffinum Liquidum 10 % w/w, Cetearyl Alcohol 4.0 % w/w, Glyceryl Stearate 3.0 % w/w, Phenoxyethanol 1.0 % w/w, Xantham Gum 0.2 % w/w, Ethylhexylglycerin 0.1 % w/w (Base Emulsion O/W).

To fall within the linear dynamic range, unknown samples are diluted using the same matrix as the standards. A minimum dilution factor of at least 100 is recommended to mitigate matrix interference, employing multiple dilution tiers if necessary. Precisely replicating the matrix between the standards and unknowns is, moreover, vital. If e.g. water is utilized in the standards to assist with mixing, the same relative amount must be incorporated into the unknowns.

10 μL of the FRAP reagent are spotted in duplicate on a chromatography paper strip and allowed to dry. A layer of the standard or unknown sample is then applied over the FRAP spots, using a plastic spatula. A non-metal spatula should be used, not to favorize oxidation reactions.

Immediately after, a cover glass is gently, but firmly, pressed down on the emulsion layer to spread the emulsion into a thin, even layer. Excess emulsion around the spots is cleared using the cover glass.

Pictures of the spotted paper strips are captured by a smartphone camera (12-Megapixel wide camera of an Apple iPhone 12 mini smartphone, or a 50-Megapixel camera of a Xiaomi Redmi Note 12 Pro 5G smartphone). Typically, three photos are taken with a time difference of at least 5 min, within the first 5–15 min after emulsion spreading on the spots.

To ensure consistent, accurate light readings, imaging takes place inside a white photographic cube (edge of 23 cm from Shenzhen PULUZ Technology Limited (China) which has a single row of LED light and a top centre opening for the phone’s camera). The paper strips are placed parallel to the light strip within the box.

To ensure high reproducibility, it is essential that each picture of the unknown sample spots includes the corresponding standard spots, placed side-by-side within the cube.

The photos are analyzed using software ImageJ: a homogeneous central area of the spots is selected for RGB analysis. The green channel is optimum upon image analysis of the spots of the opaque emulsions studied under the selected experimental conditions, while the average, or the luma (0.299R+0.587G+0.114 Β) component often also provides satisfactory linearity.

The linear relationship between the green component intensity and antioxidant concentration remains satisfactory for at least the first 20 min. While sensitivity increases over time, this comes at the cost of a reduced goodness of fit, in general. A reaction time of 5–15 min (after sample application) is therefore chosen to balance high sensitivity, good linearity between response and antioxidant concentration, and reasonably short reaction times.

The green channel intensity of each set of duplicate spots per picture is then plotted against the standard concentrations to generate two calibration curves per picture. The unknown concentration is calculated as the average value from resulting highly correlated calibration curves (with R2≥ 0.978), coming from the three pictures per sample analysis.

To establish a quantifiable relationship (standard curve) between the antioxidant concentration in the standards and the measured signal intensity, linear regression using Microsoft Excel is performed on the initial portion of the concentration-response data. The coefficient of determination (R2), is used to assess the linearity and goodness of fit. All quality control (QC) and unknown antioxidant concentrations are calculated using a standard curve run in parallel.

Each concentration-response curve is generated using at least 9 standards, while each linear calibration curve using at least 5 standards.

The dissolution and homogenization of these solutions were facilitated by sonication. WTW standard aqueous buffer solutions (Weilheim, Germany) were used to calibrate the pH meter.

Method validation

Monitoring the antioxidant capacity of O/W emulsions containing model antioxidants

First, the antioxidant capacity of three model antioxidants incorporated into Base Emulsion O/W was assessed using the above method. The selected antioxidants—ascorbic acid (AA, hydrophilic), N-acetyl-L-cysteine (NAC, hydrophilic), and α-tocopheryl acetate (α-TA – vitamin E acetate, lipophilic)—were chosen to represent a broad solubility range and to enable a direct comparative analysis with our previously validated DPPH paper-based assay [5,6]. Concentration-response curves for each antioxidant standard in Base Emulsion O/W were generated using the FRAP assay. Both NAC- and AA-spiked emulsions demonstrated a concentration-dependent antioxidant activity, exhibiting an initial linear correlation (Fig. 1). This linearity was confirmed across at least seven replicate curves generated on separate days, yielding coefficients R2 ≥0.934 and ≥0.938 for AA and NAC, respectively. Notably, 60 % of these curves achieved a R2 value of 0.99 or greater. This linear relationship is a prerequisite for the quantitative application of this assay. In the case of α-TA, only faint blue spots appeared, with intensities similar to the blank, regardless of antioxidant concentration. A well-defined concentration-response curve could not be obtained, likely because the water-soluble FRAP reagent has restricted access to the α-TA, which should primarily be in the dispersed oil droplets of the emulsion. Half-maximal effective concentration (EC50) values - defined as the concentration of producing 50 % of the maximal FRAP response - were calculated by fitting the full concentration-response curves to the four-parameter logistic (4PL) function (MyCurveFit.com software). Based on the obtained EC50 values of Table 1, NAC demonstrated an antioxidant capacity approximately 1.5 times lower than that of AA. While literature often reports varying EC50 values due to non-standardized study conditions, studies employing DPPH and/or FRAP assays in solution consistently report AA's potency as 1.5 to 2 times that of NAC [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. FRAP method interferences

The FRAP assay relies on the ability of antioxidants in a sample to reduce ferric iron to ferrous iron at a low pH. Reduction ability is quantified through the blue-coloured complex formed between the TPTZ reagent and the ferrous ion (Fe(II)-TPTZ). Any ligand capable of forming an alternative complex with ferrous ions acts as an interference. Such a common interfering ligand in cosmetic and food emulsions is EDTA, which is widely used as a chelating agent. Indeed, during the analysis of O/W emulsions containing both antioxidants and EDTA, we observed very significant signal fading, with responses dropping even below background levels. Increasing the concentrations of ferric chloride and TPTZ in a modified FRAP reagent only partially mitigated this effect; the resulting signal remained weak and highly unstable over time. To identify other potential interferences among common cosmetic and food chemicals, we utilized a paper-based FRAP assay to screen for color-fading effects. This was applied to Base Emulsion O/W preparations containing 0.003 % w/w AA and a fixed concentration of the tested chemical. The net color intensity (above background) relative to AA is shown in Fig. 2, demonstrating complete inhibition by EDTA, moderate inhibition by citric acid, and only little inhibition by potassium sorbate. Other tested chemicals showed minimal positive interference. In contrast, the same chemicals displayed very little interference with the DPPH assay within the assay timeframe. Monitoring the antioxidant capacity of commercial cosmetic emulsions spiked with antioxidants

To evaluate whether the most potent inhibitors - EDTA and citric acid - interfere with the FRAP assay under realistic testing conditions, we analysed commercial cosmetic emulsions spiked with antioxidants. The commercial emulsions contained either disodium EDTA dihydrate (0.1 % w/w; matrix 1) or citric acid (0.04 % w/w; matrix 2). Prior to analysis, these samples were significantly diluted into Base Emulsion O/W. The resulting relative error (RE%) and coefficient of variation (CV%) in specific cases (Table 3) exceeded the typical thresholds for analytical methodologies, suggesting the presence of both systematic and random errors. However, in most instances, an overestimation of the actual antioxidant potential was observed. In any case, this level of analytical performance is considered acceptable for quality control in the cosmetics and food industries, where the primary objective is often to compare the antioxidant capacities and stability profiles of different antioxidants, rather than to determine absolute concentrations. Such comparative applications are essential for studies designed to evaluate the antioxidant capacity of new extracts against established antioxidant standards. As an example, we previously successfully employed the DPPH format to quantify the antioxidant capacity of a natural extract from French wine shoots (Resveratrox®) within an O/W emulsion [6].

Since TPTZ readily forms coordination complexes with various transition metals [13], the presence of metal ions in the matrix could also introduce interference. Such an interaction would be observable as an alteration in the blank reading. Significant dilution of the sample in a metal-free emulsion matrix should ensure that the concentration of iron remains in substantial stoichiometric excess relative to any potential interfering metal ion, thereby minimizing competitive complexation and its subsequent impact on the assay.

Limitations

Incompatibility of the proposed FRAP assay with W/O emulsions

No measurable signal was observed when testing either lipophilic or hydrophilic antioxidants in a W/O formulation using the paper-based FRAP setup. This can be attributed to the negligible solubility of the FRAP reagent in the external oil phase, which prevents the reagent from reaching the antioxidants, regardless of whether they are dissolved in the continuous oil phase (lipophilic) or confined to dispersed water droplets (hydrophilic). Even if minor diffusion were to occur across the paper/emulsion interface, the reaction kinetics would likely be too slow to generate a detectable signal within the assay's timeframe.

Incompatibility of the proposed FRAP assay with lipophilic antioxidants

We evaluated the applicability of the proposed FRAP paper-based format, against the DPPH format, using a series of molecules of varying lipophilicity. Molecule lipophilicity was characterised by "average Log P" values obtained from the Swiss ADME software [14] (Table S1). While all tested molecules have been published to generate measurable signals in solution-based DPPH and FRAP assays [[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]], our results indicate that the paper format introduces new constraints. Fig. 3 summarises our findings, showing the net intensity of the coloured assay output for each antioxidant spot as a percentage of the net intensity of the spots of the highly water-soluble AA. These results underscore the critical importance of phase partitioning for both analytes and chromogenic reagents. The water-soluble FRAP reagents cannot access the lipophilic antioxidants in O/W emulsions, nor any antioxidants in W/O emulsions. Therefore, the paper-based FRAP format is effectively limited to monitoring hydrophilic antioxidants in O/W systems. In contrast, the DPPH assay is significantly more versatile. Since the DPPH reagent can maintain sufficient accessibility to both the oil and water phases, it can effectively monitor antioxidant capacity across a wide range of antioxidant polarities in both W/O and O/W emulsions [6].

Conclusions

Concluding, unlike traditional spectroscopic methods, this digital image colorimetric approach analyzes complex emulsions directly on paper without requiring time-consuming antioxidant extraction. It, furthermore, replaces laboratory equipment with a simple smartphone camera and a white photographic cube, all while requiring minimal reagent and sample consumption, offering a greener and more accessible alternative for quality control. The method finally accounts for how antioxidants actually behave within an emulsion's specific phases, providing a more realistic profile of "in-situ" efficacy than traditional solution-based tests [1,4]. Specific limitations of the FRAP counterpart regarding lipophilic antioxidants and water-in-oil emulsions, are attributed to the FRAP reagent’s inherent hydrophilicity and are not relevant for the DPPH counterpart [5,6].
