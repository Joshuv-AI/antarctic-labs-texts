Relationship between pre-operative central sensitization and post-operative outcome after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair—longitudinal outcomes over 2 post-operative years
Authors: Tsuyoshi Sasaki, Hitoshi Shitara, Masataka Kamiyama, Ryosuke Miyamoto, Fukuhisa Ino, Takuma Kachi, Kurumi Nakase, Tsuyoshi Ichinose, Noritaka Hamano, Kenji Takagishi, Hirotaka Chikuda
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWhether pre-operative central sensitization (CS) affects post-operative outcomes after arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) remains unclear. This study aimed to evaluate the potential association between pre-operative CS and post-operative pain and function for 2 years in patients who underwent ARCR for small-to-medium rotator cuff tears.MethodsSixty patients with small-to-medium full-thickness rotator cuff tears who underwent ARCR and were followed up for 24 months post-operatively were enrolled. The presence or absence of CS was evaluated using the Japanese version of the Central Sensitization Inventory (short form). Patients were divided into 2 groups according to the presence or absence of CS, with scores ≥10 defined as CS. Baseline demographic and radiologic factors (eg, smoking status, diabetes mellitus, occupation, tear size, and fatty infiltration) were recorded and compared between groups. Post-operative outcomes, including pain (numerical rating scale), shoulder range of motion, muscle strength, and clinical scores (Constant, American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons), were assessed at 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months. Rotator cuff integrity was evaluated by magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months post-operatively.ResultsCS was present in 45% of patients. There were no significant differences in baseline demographic, clinical, or radiologic factors, as well as in pre-operative shoulder joint range of motion, strength, or pain between the groups with and without CS. Patients with CS exhibited significantly less improvement in pain on motion, abduction strength, and clinical scores at multiple time points up to 18 months; by 24 months, however, these differences had diminished.ConclusionPre-operative CS may be associated with delayed improvement in post-operative motion-related pain and clinical scores after ARCR, although its long-term impact appears limited. Clinicians should consider pre-operative CS among factors that may affect post-operative functional recovery, particularly in the first 18 months, with no apparent difference by 24 months.Keywords: Central sensitization, Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair, Shoulder pain, Post-operative outcome, Physical function, Clinical scores

Arthroscopic rotator cuff repair (ARCR) is a well-established and effective surgical treatment option. In small-to-medium rotator cuff tears, defined by Cofield et al3 as tendon tears of less than 3 cm, clinical and anatomic results after surgical repair are generally good. However, although ARCR repairs the torn rotator cuff, resolves subacromial impingement, and reduces peripheral neuropathic acceptance, some patients complain of persistent post-operative pain and dysfunction, even in cases with a smaller tear.10 No relationship between post-operative patient satisfaction and tear size has been reported, suggesting that smaller pre-operative tear size does not necessarily indicate higher post-operative satisfaction.6

Several studies have reported that central sensitization (CS) is associated with pain and post-operative outcomes in patients with subacromial impingement and rotator cuff tears.4,14,22 CS is characterized by allodynia and hyperalgesia, in which the responsiveness of nociceptive neurons in the central nervous system to normal or below-threshold afferent input of nociceptive neurons in the central nervous system relates to persistent postsurgical pain.21 Patients with CS may have prolonged post-operative pain due to normal or minor irritation despite improved physical impingement from rotator cuff repair. However, previous reports regarding the relationship between CS and shoulder pain or post-operative outcomes are limited, as they included subjects other than those with rotator cuff tears, were cross-sectional, or had a short study period. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of CS on post-operative pain and clinical scores 2 years post-operatively in patients who underwent ARCR for rotator cuff tears. We hypothesized that patients with CS would experience more post-operative pain and have lower clinical scores than those without CS.

Materials and methods

This prospective cohort study was approved by the institutional ethical review board of Gunma University (2021/07/21, HS2021-062). During the study period, a total of 71 patients underwent ARCR for small-to-medium full-thickness rotator cuff tears at our institution. After applying the inclusion and exclusion criteria, 60 patients were ultimately enrolled in this study (see Fig. 1). Patients who underwent ARCR for rotator cuff tears after CS evaluation and whose clinical outcomes were known during the 2-year follow-up period were included. Patients with a history of trauma or surgery of the affected shoulder, or those with prior episodes of shoulder pain that had resolved before a subsequent recurrence or reinjury leading to the current diagnosis, were excluded. Patients with incomplete CS assessments were also excluded from the analysis (see Fig. 1).

Flow diagram of patient inclusion based on central sensitization status.

Baseline demographic, clinical, and imaging variables were recorded as described under Evaluated items.

CS was assessed using the Japanese version of the Central Sensitization Inventory (CSI),19 a self-administered questionnaire distributed to all patients on their first hospital visit (1-2 months before surgery). A short version of the CSI (CSI-9), which has been useful in assessing CS in patients with musculoskeletal pain, was used in this study.12 It consisted of 9 questions related to pain sensitivity and emotional or somatic symptoms in daily activities. Each question was rated on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 0 to 4 (0 = never, 1 = rarely, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often, and 4 = always) (see Supplementary Table S1). Points for each of the 9 questions were summed and grouped according to total points (0-9: no CS, 10-19: mild CS, 20-36: moderate/severe CS).11 A CSI-9 score of 10 points was adopted as the threshold to define whether patients had CS. Patients with a CSI-9 score of more than 10 points were defined as having CS.

Evaluated items

Patient demographic and baseline characteristics

Age, sex, affected side, smoking history, diabetes mellitus, occupational category (manual vs. non-manual labor), and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-based tear size and fatty infiltration (Goutallier classification) were recorded. Intraoperative long-head biceps tendon lesions were also documented.

Pain

Pain severity was assessed using a numerical rating scale from 0 to 10 at the first pre-operative visit to our hospital and 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months post-operatively. Pain at rest, during motion, and at night was assessed separately.

Physical function

Passive range of motion (ROM) and muscular strength were evaluated during physical examinations at the first hospital visit. Shoulder ROM was evaluated in flexion, abduction, external rotation, and internal rotation with the patient in a sitting position. Internal rotation ROM was assessed based on the highest spinal segment as described previously.5 Muscle strength was assessed during shoulder abduction and external rotation, as described previously.16 Peak muscle strength was evaluated using a digital myodynamometer (Micro FET; Hoggan Health Industries Inc., West Jordan, UT, USA) positioned at the distal end of the arm.

Clinical scores

Clinical scores were evaluated using the Constant and American Shoulder and Elbow Surgeons (ASES) score upon the first hospital visit and at 3, 6, 12, 18, and 24 months post-operatively. Both clinical scores have been validated and are widely used to evaluate ARCR outcomes.

Rotator cuff integrity after surgery

As rotator cuff integrity may affect post-operative pain, physical function, and clinical score after ARCR,8 it was assessed using magnetic resonance imaging at 3, 6, 12, and 24 months post-operatively. Not all patients underwent MRI at every follow-up because of logistical constraints or patient-related factors. Therefore, re-tear rates were calculated using the number of patients who underwent MRI at each time point, rather than the full cohort. The presence of a re-tear was defined as a Sugaya classification18 of ≥4.

Statistical analysis

Patient demographics and the above clinical assessment items were compared between patients with and without CS. Continuous variables are presented as means and standard deviations in the descriptive statistics and were tested for normal distribution using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test; variables that followed a normal distribution were compared using the t-test, and those that were not normally distributed were compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Chi-square tests were used to compare categorical variables between the 2 groups. SPSS (version 25.0; IBM) was used in statistical analyses. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Although a priori power analysis was not performed, effect sizes (Cohen's d) and 95% confidence intervals were calculated for key outcomes (eg, pain on motion) to assess both statistical significance and clinical relevance.

Results

CS was observed in 27 of 60 patients (45%) (Fig. 1). Baseline characteristics did not differ significantly between the CS(+) and CS(−) groups, including age (mean 66.3 vs. 65.7 years; P = .72), sex, affected side (dominant vs. non-dominant), pain duration, smoking history, diabetes mellitus, and occupational category (manual vs. non-manual labor) (Table I). Pre-operative MRI findings, including tear size and rotator cuff fatty infiltration (Goutallier stage ≥2), also showed no significant between-group differences. Additionally, the intraoperative prevalence of long-head biceps tendon lesions was comparable between groups. These findings are summarized in Table I.

Pain

Post-operatively, pain at rest and at night improved similarly in both groups, with no significant differences observed at any time point over 24 months (Fig. 2, A and C; Supplementary Table S2A and C). However, pain on motion improved significantly more in the non-CS group at 3, 6, 12, and 18 months post-operatively (P < .05). At 6 months, the change from baseline in numerical rating scale pain on motion was −5.1 ± 2.8 in the non-CS group and −2.8 ± 2.6 in the CS group (P = .003). This difference resolved by 24 months (Fig. 3, B; Supplementary Table S2B). Effect sizes and 95% confidence intervals are presented in Table II.

Physical function

Post-operative improvements were observed in both groups; however, significant differences were observed in favor of the CS (+) group for abduction and external rotation ROM at 24 months, while abduction strength was significantly great in the non-CS group from 6 months onward. For example, at 6 months, abduction strength was 4.5 ± 1.8 kg in the non-CS group and 3.1 ± 1.6 kg in the CS group (P = .005) (Figs. 3 and 4; Supplementary Tables S3 and S4).

Clinical scores

Constant and ASES scores improved over time in both groups. No significant between-group differences were observed at 3 or 24 months. Conversely, between 6 and 18 months, the CS group exhibited significantly less improvements than that exhibited by the non-CS group (P < .05). At 6 months, the change in Constant score was 18.2 ± 12.8 in the non-CS group vs. 9.2 ± 14.5 in the CS group (P = .011), and the change in ASES score was 36.3 ± 16.7 vs. 25.6 ± 20.0, respectively (P = .038) (Fig. 5; Supplementary Table S5).

Discussion

In this prospective cohort study, pre-operative CS correlated with differences in post-operative pain, physical function, and clinical scores. Specifically, CS predicted greater pain on motion for up to 18 months, reduced abduction strength between 6 and 24 months, and lower clinical scores between 6 and 18 months. In contrast, ROM in the CS (+) group was similar or slightly better at 24 months. To our knowledge, this is the first study with a 2-year follow-up demonstrating that pre-operative CS influences recovery trajectories after ARCR.

Although the CSI-9 is a shortened version of the original 25-item inventory11 and has been used in musculoskeletal research,12 the optimal cutoff value remains debated. While a score of ≥10 is commonly used,15,17 other thresholds (eg, 14 or 20 points) have also been proposed.7,20 Further research is needed to standardize CSI-9 interpretation in surgical populations. Regarding the relationship between CS and shoulder pain, Othman et al investigated sensitivity to movement-evoked pain in patients with chronic shoulder pain and showed that the chronic shoulder pain group reported significantly higher sensitivity to movement-evoked pain scores than the healthy control group.13 Their study showed that CS symptoms and signs were involved in chronic shoulder pain but was limited by being cross-sectional. Gwilym et al reported that patients with CS had worse post-operative outcomes following decompression surgery for subacromial impingement.4 However, their report included only a few cases of rotator cuff tears, and it remained unclear whether CS affects pain after ARCR. In this study, only patients who underwent rotator cuff repair were included, and the relationship between pre-operative CS and pain after ARCR was evaluated longitudinally for 2 years post-operatively. Pre-operative CS was negatively linked with improvement of pain during motion until 18 months post-operatively; CS may be associated with reduced improvement in post-operative pain on motion after ARCR and should be emphasized in predicting post-operative outcomes for >1 year. These results suggest that CS selectively influences dynamic pain (eg, pain during motion) rather than static pain at rest or at night. Movement-evoked pain involves increased central nociceptive excitability, a hallmark of CS, triggered more prominently by active stimuli. Conversely, static pain may reflect mechanisms such as local inflammation or peripheral sensitization, which are less directly linked to CS.

Baseline characteristics, including ROM measures related to potential shoulder stiffness, did not differ significantly between groups. Therefore, the influence of pre-operative stiffness on post-operative outcomes is likely limited, and the observed between-group differences are more likely ascribed to CS.

Ueki et al reported that a low post-operative pressure pain threshold was associated with low clinical scores after ARCR.22 Their report indicated that CS may be associated with clinical outcomes and pain after ARCR. However, their study involved repeated cross-sectional investigations of the association between CS and post-operative outcomes at each evaluation time point; a longitudinal evaluation was not performed. In contrast, our study longitudinally examined the association between pre-operative CS and post-operative outcomes over 2 years, offering a clearer view of how CS may influence recovery trajectories over time. Yaari et al reported that pain sensitivity influenced surgical outcomes of ARCR in a prospective cohort study.23 However, their study was performed 1 year post-operatively, meaning that post-operative outcomes after this period were unclear. In this study, CS was assessed pre-operatively, and its association with outcomes was evaluated longitudinally for 2 years post-operatively, complementing the information missing in previous studies. To date, no studies have directly examined the relationship between pre-operative CS and post-operative rotator cuff integrity using long-term, serial MRI assessments. Although CS has been linked to post-operative pain and function after ARCR, its potential influence on tendon healing and repair integrity remains unclear. Therefore, to our knowledge, this study is the first to longitudinally evaluate the association between pre-operative CS and structural outcomes, including re-tear rates, over a 2-year follow-up period. Pre-operative CS is associated with post-operative outcomes in other orthopedic surgeries, as patients with CS have been reported to have lower clinical scores after knee arthroplasty9 and strong pain after hip arthroscopy.1 Based on the results of previous studies, pre-operative CS evaluation seems to be important in predicting and managing post-operative outcomes after joint surgery, suggesting that its pre-operative evaluation in ARCR is also important. In addition to its effects on pain and clinical scores, pre-operative CS influenced physical function. Patients with CS exhibited significantly lower abduction muscle strength from 6 to 24 months post-operatively, whereas ROM in abduction and external rotation was significantly better in the CS group at 24 months. These findings suggest that while CS may hinder post-operative muscle recovery, it may not necessarily impair joint mobility and could potentially be associated with enhanced compensatory movement strategies. Therefore, CS may have a differential effect on components of physical function, and further studies are warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the evaluation of CS was based solely on the CSI-9 questionnaire, and no objective assessments such as pressure pain threshold measurements were performed. Previous reports suggest that subjective questionnaires and objective tests for CS do not always agree,2 and debate continues over the optimal CS assessment method. Furthermore, the CSI-9 has limited validation in orthopedic populations, and its utility in routine post-operative evaluations remains unclear. Including the actual CSI-9 items as a supplemental table would clarify the tool's contents and improve reproducibility.

Second, we did not collect post-operative pain data during early recovery (eg, 2 and 6 weeks), time points critical for understanding pain trajectory and CS's early impact. Their absence limits our ability to characterize CS's short-term effects.

Third, we did not assess psychosocial factors such as anxiety, depression, or catastrophizing, which influence post-operative pain and recovery. In particular, depressive symptoms were not systematically evaluated, and their potential role as confounding factors could not be examined.

Fourth, pre-operative analgesic use and physiotherapy were not documented, potentially biasing pain perception and recovery trajectories.

Fifth, we assessed CS only pre-operatively; some patients may have developed CS post-operatively, affecting their recovery. Future studies should investigate post-operative CS development and its impact on long-term outcomes.

Sixth, rotator cuff integrity was evaluated using MRI; however, not all patients underwent MRI at each follow-up time point because of logistical constraints or patient-related factors. Therefore, re-tear rates were calculated using the number of patients who received an MRI at each interval rather than the full cohort, which may have introduced selection bias.

Despite these limitations, this study provides valuable 2-year longitudinal data and offers new insights into the association between pre-operative CS and post-operative outcomes after ARCR.

Conclusions

Pre-operative CS may negatively impact post-operative recovery after ARCR, particularly in pain on motion, physical function, and clinical scores within the first 18 months. Assessing CS pre-operatively may help identify at-risk patients and guide perioperative management by anticipating delayed functional recovery. Further studies are needed to clarify mechanisms and evaluate targeted interventions.
