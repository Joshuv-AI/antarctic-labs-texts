Rapid and efficient removal of water-soluble dyes via natural asphalt oxide as a new carbonaceous super adsorbent; NA-oxide synthesis and characterization
Authors: Shabnam Rashidi, Mohammad Soleiman-Beigi, Homa Kohzadi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this study, natural asphalt was oxidized to synthesize a new nano-structure adsorbent for dye removal. The functionalization of natural asphalt by oxidation introduced new properties that influenced its activity. The process of oxidizing natural asphalt with potassium permanganate resulted in a low-cost adsorbent, which can potentially be a more affordable option compared with synthetic alternatives. Characterization analysis confirmed the enhanced surface area, improving dye interaction and adsorption. The interconnected channels and capillaries of the oxidized natural asphalt facilitated the capillary action drawing in liquids, including dyes. The distinctive porosity of natural asphalt oxide (NA-oxide) was noted, and the experimental results showed that the NA–oxide nanoadsorbent efficiently adsorbed cationic and anionic dyes in water, with maximum capacities of 14.68 mg.g−1, 17.81 mg.g−1 and 16.47 mg.g−1 for methyl orange, methylene blue and Rhodamine B, respectively. The study investigated various parameters, such as concentration, adsorption dose, pH, contact time, and temperature, affecting the dye removal process. Langmuir, Freundlich, and Temkin isotherms along with pseudo-first and pseudo-second-order kinetic equations were applied to assess the adsorption process, indicating that dyes adhered to the pseudo-first-order model and Langmuir isotherm. Analysis of MO, MB, and RhB dyes revealed conformity to Langmuir isotherm and first-order kinetics. Thermodynamic evaluations like ΔH°, ΔS°, and ∆G° displayed the exothermic and spontaneous nature of dye adsorption on the NA-oxide adsorbent. Furthermore, the absorbent displayed remarkable stability with a recovery rate of 98.45% after ten cycles, signifying its potential for enduring effectiveness in dye removal processes.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75106-9.

Introduction

Adsorbents play a crucial role in chemistry and industry by facilitating the process of adsorption. Also, they play a significant role in environmental remediation processes by adsorbing pollutants from air, water, and soil. Various substrates, such as silica1, zeolite2, activated carbon3–5, soils6, Zno Nanoparticles7, sludge8, CNTs9, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs)10, composite11,12, Fe3O4@NH2-MIL88B nanorods13, meso-CaAl2O414and carbon adsorbents15–17, have been used as absorbent. Among the adsorbents, carbonaceous adsorbents have received more attention that are widely used for adsorption applications in various industries due to their high surface area, porosity, and adsorption capacity. They are used in wastewater treatment, air purification systems, and soil remediation to remove contaminants and pollutants.

Clean water is crucial for all living organisms, but specific sources such as wastewater and the increasing use of pesticides potentially contribute to water pollution, which can threaten the environment and human health18–21. Dyes are a significant category of pollutants present in industrial wastewater and can cause toxic effects in humans and aquatic animals. It is necessary to remove dyes from industrial wastewater before discharge them into the environment due to their resistance to natural degradationy22,23. Industrial dyes can harm aquatic ecosystems by disrupting the natural balance of aquatic flora and fauna when they are discharged into water bodies. Organic dyes are resistant to natural degradation, making it necessary to remove them from industrial wastewater before discharging into the environment24,25.

There are several methods for treating dye wastewater, including photocatalysis26, photo-degradation27, membrane filtration28, oxidation29, adsorption30, ion exchange31, ozonation32, electro-dialysis and chemical precipitation33. However, each of these methods has limitations such as high cost, low efficiency, and production of pollutants and by-products. Therefore, simple and efficient new methods are required for wastewater treatment. The adsorption method is the best method for treating colored wastewater as it is low-cost, easy to design, efficient, and does not produce unwanted by-products34,35.

Dye removal occurs through the electrostatic interaction of color molecules with the absorbent surface, while in the process of color decomposition, the absorbent reacts with the color molecules and dissolves in the solution and the absorbent cannot be reused36,37.

In our earlier research, the use of natural asphalt as a nanocatalyst in different reactions was explored38,39.

Solid natural asphalt is a hydrocarbon that is often found in underground mines as a hard and black material with a relatively high melting temperature. Typically, natural asphalt contains aromatic fused rings, aliphatic chains and functional groups that contain polar heteroatoms40. One of the most important categories of natural asphalt is asphaltenes. Asphaltenes are compounds made of aromatic rings welded together. Aliphatic bridges connect these planar polycyclic structures. Expanding our research on natural asphalt application, here we introduce an innovative method of synthesizing a nano-structure adsorbent from natural asphalt. Natural asphalt, which is inexpensive and non-toxic, was first used as an absorbent after undergoing oxidation. The process of functionalizing natural asphalt through oxidation has led to a significant improvement in the properties of the adsorbent, such as increased surface area and distinctive porosity. The research findings demonstrate that the oxidized natural asphalt is highly efficient in adsorbing both cationic (methylene blue (MB), Rhodamine B (RhB) and anionic dyes methyl orange (MO) from aqueous solutions. This innovative advancement could potentially address challenges related to dye pollution, offering a sustainable and effective solution for addressing environmental contamination.

Results and discussion

A proposal mechanism for oxidation of natural asphalt

Oxidation of natural asphalt could point to the introduction of oxygen-containing functional groups, such as OH, CO2H, C = O on the NA surface. Potassium permanganate (KMnO4) is a well-known oxidizing agent that can be used to oxidize natural asphalt.

Natural asphalt reacts with KMnO4, leading to the transfer of electrons from carbon atoms to the oxidizer due to the manganese ions acting as electron acceptors. This process results in the formation of oxygen-containing groups, including hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl groups, on the surface of the asphalt (Scheme 2).

The oxidation of natural asphalt not only introduces functional groups but also disrupts the carbon-carbon (C-C) π-bond network, resulting in the formation of defects or vacancies in the lattice. These defects can increase the reactivity of natural asphalt and facilitate the incorporation of other functional groups or molecules41.

The presence of functional groups on the natural asphalt surface makes it hydrophilic and allows easy dispersion in water and other polar solvents. Additionally, the presence of oxygen-containing groups introduces electrical and chemical functionality, making natural asphalt oxide suitable for various applications. Therefore, the oxidation of natural asphalt in the presence of potassium permanganate-based oxidants involves the transfer of electrons from carbon atoms to manganese ions, resulting in the formation of oxygen-containing functional groups.

Adsorption experiment

To study the ability of dye adsorption by the synthesized NA-oxide nanoadsorbent, three dyes were selected as models for the experiment: methyl orange, methylene blue and rhodamine B. A stock solution (500 mg.L−1) was prepared separately for each color. Adsorption studies were carried out with 25 mL of solution in volumetric flask to examine the effects of various parameters, including the initial concentration of the dye solution, the amount of NA-oxide, adsorption time, temperature, and pH. The pH of the solution was adjusted with 0.1 M NaOH and HCl solutions. The NA-oxide was then separated from the solution by simple filtration, and the remaining dye concentration was estimated using a UV-Vis spectrophotometer at 464, 664, and 553 nm for MO, MB, and RhB, respectively. Adsorption of dyes by NA-oxide (a proposal mechanism)

The adsorption process is controlled by many factors, including the nature of the functional groups in the adsorbent, the textural and surface characteristics of the adsorbent, the diffusion behavior of the adsorbent towards the adsorbent, and how they interact. When it comes to the types of interactions between the absorbent surface and the adsorbed particles, several forces and mechanisms are involved. The role of hydrogen bonding in surface adsorption is significant, especially when considering the adsorption of polar molecules or those containing hydrogen atoms capable of forming hydrogen bonds. Hydrogen bonding can increase the affinity between the adsorbate and the absorbent surface. The presence of hydrogen bonds can strengthen the interaction between the molecules of the adsorbate and the surface, leading to enhanced adsorption. The role of π-π interaction in surface adsorption is also noteworthy, particularly when dealing with aromatic or conjugated systems. π-π interactions can significantly enhance the adsorption of aromatic or conjugated molecules on surfaces. The alignment of the π-electron clouds creates attractive forces, leading to a stronger overall interaction between the adsorbate and the surface. This increased affinity facilitates the adsorption process50. Another effective factor is electrostatic attraction. The electrostatic attraction in surface adsorption is crucial, particularly when dealing with charged species or polar molecules. Electrostatic forces can confer selectivity in surface adsorption processes. Since different molecules carry varying degrees of charge or exhibit different polarities, surfaces with specific charge distributions can selectively attract or repel certain adsorbates. Electrostatic interactions are often reversible, allowing adsorbates to be desorbed or detached from the surface under suitable conditions. By altering the electrostatic environment or applying external forces, such as changing pH or ionic strength, the strength of the electrostatic attraction can be modulated, causing the adsorbate to be released from the surface.

Since the synthesized NA-oxide has high porosity, it can be done by entrapping the dyes. When dyes molecule is trapped in the pores, they are effectively removed from the solution, resulting in purified or decolorized dye effluent. In fact, dye adsorption occurs through physical adsorption, chemical adsorption, or both, depending on the nature of the mutual interaction between the adsorbent and the adsorbent. Other methods are based on surface adsorption and ion binding. In the scheme 3, the mechanism of Mb adsorption is shown as an example, which has the highest amount of adsorption due to proper interactions with the adsorbent. The adsorption of MB on the NA-oxide is carried out with the involvement of π-π interaction, electrostatic attraction and hydrogen bonding51,52.

Experimental section

Apparatus and materials

The chemicals including H2SO4 (98%), KMNO4 (99%), NaNO3 (99.5%), HCl (37%), H2O2 (30%), Methyl orange, Methylene blue and Rhodamine B (99%) were purchased from Merck chemical company and natural asphalt powder (mesh 150–200) was provided by mines of Ilam. FT-IR spectra of NA-oxide Nano adsorbent were recorded with FT-IR, VERTEX 70, Bruker, Germany spectroscopy.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) scanning results were obtained by Holland Philips X and The thermal analysis (TGA) was carried out with a PL-STA 1500 device manufactured by Thermal Sciences. BET surface area, volume of pores and nitrogen absorbent porosity was investigated by nitrogen’s adsorption/rational iston using micro-criteria (Chem Bet-3000, Quantachrome C; Quantachrrome, Boynton Beach, Florida, the United States). SEM images were recorded with Mira 3-XMU. The elemental composition was determined using EDS and Mira 3-XMU. The adsorption of dyes was measured analyzed by UV–Visible Jusco spectrophotometer (Japan, Model V-570). The pH of the solutions was obtained by S-3 C model pH meter. Zeta potential was measured by a Zeta Potential Meter (HORIBA-SZ 100) at 298 K.

Oxidizing of natural asphalt (NA-Oxide synthesis)

Hummer procedure as a common method for oxidizing graphene was chosen for oxidation of natural asphalt65,66 (Scheme 4). In a 1000 mL flask, natural asphalt powder (3.0 g), H2SO4 (98%) (70 mL) were stirred under mixing in an ice bath for 30 min. Then, NaNO3 (1.5 g) was added to the mixture and stirred for 45 min. Afterwards, KMnO4 (9.0 g) was added portion wise to reaction solution over 4 h and stirred under for 45 min. It was brought to 35 ˚C and stirred under reflux for 3.5 h. Distilled water (150 mL) was then added and the temperature was increased to 90 ˚C. It was stirred for 24 h under reflux conditions, then (450 mL) distilled water and (20 mL) H2O2 (30%) were added and stirred for 19 h. Next, it was washed with 5% HCl and distilled water. Finally, NA-oxide was separated and washed several times with distilled water. Finally, it was dried to obtain 4.2 g of NA-oxide as a black solid.

Conclusion

Along with our previous studies, and in order to develop natural asphalt applications as an affordable and inexpensive hydrocarbon, the natural asphalt was oxidized by potassium permanganate under the conditions of the Hummers method. The synthesized adsorbent underwent identification using various techniques, including FT-IR, TGA, SEM, EDS, BET, Zeta potential and XRD. Analysis of N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms revealed that the synthesized NA-oxide adsorbent exhibited a specific surface area of 3.49 m2.g−1and a pore size of 0.0187 cm3. g−1. This value was lower than that of natural asphalt, suggesting successful synthesis of the NA-oxide adsorbent. Oxidized natural asphalt after oxidation reaction can be ability formation hydrogen bonding with some groups like –NH and OH groups in dyes. Also, it has many holes on its surface that can trap dyes. The oxidized natural asphalt in this study showed great potential for the efficient removal of cationic and anionic dyes, including MO, MB, and RhB, from aqueous solutions. The affordability, non-toxicity and recyclability of NA-oxide make it an environmentally friendly adsorbent for removal of dyes. Langmuir, Freundlich and Temkin isotherms as well as pseudo-first and pseudo-second order kinetic equations were used to analyze the adsorption process. The results of the adsorption experiment show the unique adsorption properties of the three dyes, and the adsorption process agrees relatively well with the Langmuir isothermal adsorption model and the pseudo-first-order kinetic equation. The optimal thermal dynamic parameters ∆G˚, ∆H˚ and ΔS˚ show that the adsorption process of MO, MB and RhB dyes on the NA-oxide adsorbent is exothermic and occurs spontaneously. The study also tested the impact of contact time on the effectiveness of dye removal, finding that the optimal contact times for MO, MB, and RhB dyes were 45, 25, and 40 min, respectively, with removal efficiencies of 96.2%, 99.73%, and 97.87%. Additionally, the results revealed that MO showed the highest removal efficiency at pH 7, while MB and RhB displayed peak efficiencies at pH 3 (96.2%, 99.8%, and 97.87%, respectively). The optimal dye removal concentrations for MO, MB, and RhB were determined to be 20, 120, and 100 mg.L−1 at 298 K, with maximum capacities of 14.68 mg.g−1, 17.81 mg.g−1, and 16.47 mg.g−1 and removal efficiencies of 96.2%, 99.57%, and 97.73%, respectively. Also, the results of recycling the adsorbent after ten times showed the high stability of the adsorbent. In summary, this study opens the possibility of using oxidized natural asphalt as an adsorbent in wastewater treatment and proposes a sustainable approach to the problem of water pollution by dyes.
