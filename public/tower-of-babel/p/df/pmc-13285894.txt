Sessile growth on human dentine is associated with ace gene expression in Enterococcus faecalis: An ex vivo study
Authors: Yara Anna Paula García-Guerrero, Gabriela E. Idrovo-Heredia, Jessica A. Jiménez-Rodríguez, Adriana Rodrìguez-Ciodaro, Catalina Méndez-de la Espriella, Hugo Díez-Ortega
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Evidence regarding the role of the ace gene in biofilms generally stems from DNA detection studies conducted under planktonic conditions, rather than from models that have assessed the functional expression of the gene.Aim:To determine whether sessile growth in a biofilm on human dentine in an ex vivo model modifies the expression of the ace gene in Enterococcus faecalis clinical strains.Materials and Methods:Seventy biofilm-forming clinical isolates were cultured in planktonic and sessile states using an ex vivo human dentine model. Their ability to form biofilm on dentine was assessed by electron microscopy. After total RNA extraction from both bacterial growth states, ace gene expression was confirmed by reverse transcriptase–polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR). Paired differences were analyzed using the exact McNemar test.Results:Detectable ace gene expression was observed more frequently in the sessile condition (85.7%) than in the planktonic condition (75.7%), with a statistically significant difference (P = 0.023). Seven isolates expressed the ace gene upon transitioning from a planktonic to a sessile state.Conclusion:Sessile biofilms showed more frequently detectable ace gene expression than in the planktonic state.

INTRODUCTION

Enterococcus faecalis is one of the microorganisms most frequently implicated in endodontic treatment failure, particularly in persistent or secondary infections that do not resolve clinically or radiographically after therapy. Within the root canal environment, its persistence has been due to several physiological adaptations and virulence mechanisms, including the ability to penetrate dentinal tubules, resist chemomechanical preparation, tolerate irrigants such as sodium hypochlorite, and survive under adverse nutritional and physicochemical conditions.[12] A key factor contributing to its persistence is the formation of biofilms, structured microbial communities embedded in an extracellular matrix that enhance bacterial survival and resistance to antimicrobial agents and host defenses.[34]

Among the adhesion determinants involved in dentine interaction, the ace gene encodes a collagen-binding adhesin that recognizes type I collagen, the predominant structural component of human dentine, and an important substrate for bacterial adhesion.[5] However, despite being a chromosomal determinant, ace gene expression is not detected in all endodontic strains. Most available evidence is based on the detection of ace DNA and statistical associations obtained under planktonic or simplified in vitro conditions, rather than on studies evaluating gene expression at the RNA level under physiologically relevant conditions.[6] These observations suggest that ace gene expression may be influenced by environmental stimuli present in the root canal system, reflecting the functional genetic plasticity of E. faecalis. Such regulation may depend on factors within the endodontic microenvironment, including local physiological conditions, nutrient availability, transcriptional regulation, and the presence of collagen-rich substrates, which may favor ace activation on dentine surfaces and during biofilm growth.[789]

Experimental models capable of reproducing both the dentine substrate and sessile growth conditions are therefore required. Human tooth-based systems, including double-chamber and ex vivo models, allow the formation of mature biofilms and enable the evaluation of bacterial gene expression under conditions that more closely approximate the intraradicular environment.[101112]

In this context, the present study aimed to determine whether E. faecalis isolates forming biofilms on human dentine in an ex vivo model exhibit detectable expression of the ace gene compared with planktonic growth conditions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Bacterial isolates

Seventy clinical isolates of mature biofilm-forming E. faecalis obtained from the Laboratory Cell Bank were included in the study. The isolates were collected between 2015 and 2020 from patients undergoing endodontic retreatment with secondary apical periodontitis who had not received antibiotic therapy within the previous 3 months and had no history of systemic disease. Samples originated from salivary ejectors, sterile paper points, K-type files, gutta-percha fragments, and oral mucosa swabs. Frozen isolates were reactivated in Enterococci Broth (Becton Dickinson Microbiology Systems, Cockeysville, MD, USA) following standardized microbiological procedures.[1314] Species identification was confirmed by MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry using the Biotype system (Bruker Daltonics GmbH, Bremen, Germany) according to previously reported protocols.[15] All procedures were performed in duplicate. E. faecalis ATCC 29212, E. faecalis ATCC 700802, and E. faecium ATCC 12030 were used as control strains.

Experimental design

To evaluate ace gene expression under different growth conditions, each E. faecalis strain was cultured in parallel under both planktonic and sessile states using an ex vivo dentine model. In both conditions, total RNA obtained from each isolate was analyzed by reverse transcriptase–polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) to determine the presence or absence of ace gene expression.

Planktonic growth

Ten microliters of a bacterial suspension (1.5 × 108 CFU/mL) were inoculated onto Chromocult® Enterococci Agar (Merck KGaA, Darmstadt, Germany) and incubated at 37°C for 24–48 h to obtain colonies typical of E. faecalis.[13]

Ex vivo model for sessile growth and biofilm formation

Sessile state growth was evaluated using a tooth-based double-chamber ex vivo model previously described for biofilm studies.[12] Premolars extracted for orthodontic reasons were standardized to a root length of 16 mm and instrumented using conventional biomechanical preparation with NaOCl irrigation followed by EDTA. External root surfaces were coated with nail varnish, leaving the apical millimeters exposed, and the roots were mounted in a modified polypropylene tube forming the lower chamber of the system [Figure 1]. The device was sterilized using ethylene oxide before experimentation.

The assembly of the ex vivo dual-chamber experimental system for the sessile growth of Enterococcus faecalis

To favor biofilm formation, the bacterial inoculum consists of 50 µL of the bacterial suspension deposited into the root canal chamber. The system was then placed in the brain–heart infusion (BHI) broth (Oxoid, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Basingstoke, United Kingdom) and incubated for 3 weeks to wait for mature biofilm development. Biofilm formation was verified by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. The teeth were cryofractured in liquid nitrogen; one fragment was coated with gold and examined using a scanning electron microscope (JSM-6490 LV, JEOL Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), whereas the other fragment served for RNA extraction.[12]

Molecular analysis

Total RNA was extracted from planktonic and sessile cultures using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen GmbH, Hilden, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were assessed using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, DE, USA). A260/A280 ratios between 1.8 and 2.0 were considered acceptable. Expression of the ace gene was evaluated by RT-PCR using the AccessQuick™ RT-PCR System (Promega Corporation, Madison, WI, USA). Primers were selected according to Duprè et al.:[16] (ace-F: 5’-AAAGTAGAATTAGATCCACAC-3’; ace-R: 5’-TCTATCACATTCGGTTGCG-3’). The 16S rDNA gene served as an internal control. RT-PCR amplification was performed under conditions optimized for the AccessQuick™ RT-PCR System: reverse transcription at 45°C for 45 min, initial denaturation at 94°C for 2 min, followed by 35 cycles of denaturation at 94°C for 30 s, annealing at 54°C for 30 s, and extension at 72°C for 1 min, with a final extension at 72°C for 5 min.

Statistical analysis

Differences in ace gene expression between planktonic and sessile states were analyzed using a paired 2 × 2 contingency table based on RT-PCR band detection (1 = expression detected; 0 = not detected). Paired differences were analyzed using the exact McNemar test, with a significance level of P < 0.05. The odds ratio (OR = b/c) and its 95% confidence interval (95% CI) were calculated. The results were expressed as the number and percentage of isolates with detectable expression in each condition, as well as the absolute difference between proportions. Analyses were performed using R software (version 4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

RESULTS

Microbiological identification

After protein profile spectra were obtained using MALDI-TOF MS (Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany) using the Bruker Biotyper system, and the isolates were compared with ATCC control strains and the laboratory database, all 70 isolates were identified as E. faecalis. Following protein extraction, identification showed 100% concordance at both genus and species levels, with scores ≥2.0, indicating reliable identification at the species level. In addition, all isolates were viable and pure.

Biofilm formation

SEM results showed that a mature biofilm had formed after 30 days of incubation. Micrographs revealed microorganisms embedded within an extracellular polymeric matrix, with structures consistent with an organized biofilm. Following SEM observation, confirmatory tests demonstrated that the isolates were viable, corresponded to E. faecalis, and showed logarithmic phase counts between 1 × 105 CFU/mL and 1 × 107 CFU/mL.

SEM analysis showed that E. faecalis formed a distinct biofilm on dentine. At ×5000, cocci measuring approximately 0.8–1.0 µm remained embedded within a polymeric extracellular matrix. Cell aggregation occurred, and dentinal tubules were no longer visible due to coverage by the biofilm. The surface displayed irregular structures consistent with glycocalyx and successive layers of extracellular material, indicative of a mature biofilm [Figure 2].

Reverse transcriptase–polymerase chain reaction ace gene

Evaluation of the ace gene expression showed that 53 (75.7%) of the 70 isolates were positive under planktonic conditions, whereas 60 (85.7%) of the 70 isolates were positive under sessile conditions. Comparison of the two growth conditions using the McNemar test showed a statistically significant difference (χ2 = 5.14; P = 0.023), indicating more frequent detectable ace gene expression in mature biofilms [Table 1]. The paired difference in proportions was 10.0% (95% CI: 3.0%–17.0%).

Evaluation of the change in the ace gene expression

Comparison of the two conditions showed that seven isolates with no detectable ace gene expression under planktonic conditions subsequently showed detectable expression under sessile conditions (0 → 1 = 7), whereas no isolates lost detectable expression when transitioning to the biofilm condition (1 → 0 = 0). Based on these discordant pairs, the continuity-corrected matched-pairs OR was 15.0, reflecting the predominance of transitions toward detectable ace gene expression under sessile conditions.

DISCUSSION

The presence of E. faecalis among the analyzed isolates reinforces its recognized role as one of the microorganisms most frequently associated with endodontic treatment failure. Identification by MALDI-TOF MS showed complete concordance at genus and species levels for the 70 isolates evaluated, supporting the reliability of this technology for identifying E. faecalis from clinical samples, including those obtained from infected root canals.[81718]

The relationship between E. faecalis and endodontic failure is closely linked to its virulence factors and its ability to form biofilms.[1920] In the present study, all selected isolates formed mature biofilms on human dentine. SEM observations revealed microorganisms embedded within an extracellular polymeric matrix, cell aggregation, glycocalyx formation, and partial occlusion of dentinal tubules. These characteristics are consistent with previous descriptions of intraradicular biofilms whose 3D architecture contributes to protection against irrigants, environmental stress, and bacterial persistence.[21] The sustained viability of the isolates and their capacity to develop mature biofilms in a dentine environment rich in collagen and phosphate support the ability of E. faecalis to persist under adverse conditions.[22] These findings provide support for the ex vivo model used, which reproduces relevant features of the intraradicular microenvironment. Such conditions may favor the persistence of E. faecalis within the root canal system and help explain its frequent involvement in refractory endodontic infections.

Expression of the ace gene was observed in both planktonic and sessile states and was consistent with previously reported findings for E. faecalis associated with endodontic infections. Although ace is a chromosomal determinant, it is not present in all strains, suggesting intraspecific variability potentially linked to adaptation to the intraradicular niche or to selective pressures within the microenvironment.[23] The gene encodes a collagen-binding adhesin with a high affinity for type I collagen, the main structural component of dentine, and plays a crucial role in bacterial adhesion to dentinal surfaces and penetration of dentinal tubules.[5]

The increased frequency of detectable ace gene expression observed under sessile growth on human dentine, together with the transition of isolates from nondetectable expression in planktonic conditions to detectable expression in the sessile state, suggests that ace expression is associated with dentine-related environmental conditions. This behavior may be influenced by interactions between E. faecalis and dentinal matrix components, particularly type I collagen, considering that ‘Ace’ refers to the protein (collagen-binding adhesin), not the gene belonging to the MSCRAMM family (Microbial Surface Components Recognizing Adhesive Matrix Molecules).[524] In this context, the ace expression may be modulated by environmental conditions associated with biofilm growth on dentine.[52425]

From a clinical perspective, the increased frequency of detectable ace expression under sessile conditions may be relevant to the capacity of E. faecalis to adhere to dentinal surfaces, penetrate dentinal tubules, and persist within the root canal system, features associated with persistent and post-treatment endodontic infections. These findings support the concept that dentine acts not only as a structural substrate but also as a biologically active environment capable of influencing the expression of adhesion-related virulence factors.[3422] This may have implications for the optimization of endodontic disinfection strategies, particularly those aimed at disrupting dentine-associated biofilms and limiting bacterial adhesion in refractory cases.

From a methodological perspective, the ex vivo model employed represents a strength of the study, as it incorporates instrumented human roots and the formation of mature sessile biofilms, providing structural and environmental conditions relevant to bacteria–dentine interactions, in contrast to planktonic systems where these stimuli are absent.[10111225] In addition, the qualitative RT-PCR approach enabled the detection of ace gene expression (presence/absence) and revealed differences in the frequency of detectable expression between growth conditions. Future studies incorporating quantitative approaches such as RT-qPCR may allow the determination of gene expression levels and contribute to a more detailed understanding of regulatory mechanisms associated with dentine-related biofilm growth.

CONCLUSION

In this ex vivo dentine biofilm model, E. faecalis showed more frequently detectable ace gene expression in the sessile than in the planktonic state, with some isolates expressing the gene only during biofilm growth. These findings indicate that dentine conditions may promote Ace-mediated adhesion associated with endodontic persistence.
