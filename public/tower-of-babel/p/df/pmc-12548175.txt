Association between temperature and malaria (1959-2019): a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yogesh Murugan, Vaibhavkumar Shrivastav
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background & objectivesMalaria remains a significant global health challenge, with temperature playing a critical role in transmission dynamics by affecting parasite development, mosquito longevity, and vectorial capacity. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to quantify the association between ambient temperature and malaria incidence across diverse geographical and ecological contexts, informing climate-informed control strategies.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, we systematically searched PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and Web of Science from inception to September 2024. Studies examining correlations between temperature metrics and malaria outcomes with a minimum 12-month follow-up were included. Methodological quality was assessed using a modified version of the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to calculate pooled correlation coefficients and 95% confidence intervals.ResultsEleven studies from Africa and Asia spanning 1959–2019 were included, representing diverse transmission ecologies from temperate China to tropical Africa. Mean temperature showed a moderate positive correlation with malaria incidence (r = 0.417, 95% CI: 0.098–0.735, p = 0.01). Minimum temperature demonstrated the strongest and most consistent association with malaria test positivity rates (r = 0.454, 95% CI: 0.358–0.549, p < 0.001), with all 11 studies showing statistically significant positive correlations. Maximum temperature exhibited weaker relationships (r = 0.356, 95% CI: 0.263–0.450 for positivity rates). Substantial heterogeneity was observed across all analyses (I2 > 99%), reflecting regional variations in temperature-malaria dynamics.ConclusionsTemperature, particularly minimum temperature, significantly influences malaria transmission across diverse epidemiological settings. The stronger association with minimum temperature is concerning, given that climate change disproportionately affects nighttime temperatures. The substantial heterogeneity underscores the need for locally tailored climate-informed malaria control strategies. These findings support integrating temperature monitoring, especially minimum temperature thresholds, into malaria surveillance systems and early warning programs as climate change progresses.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13690-025-01750-w.

Introduction

Background

Malaria remains one of the most significant global health challenges of the 21 st century, with an estimated 249 million cases and 608,000 deaths reported in 2022 alone [1, 2].

Vector-borne diseases, including malaria, are inherently sensitive to climate factors. Temperature, in particular, stands out as a critical determinant that influences multiple aspects of the malaria transmission cycle. Ambient temperature affects the development rate of parasites within mosquitoes (the extrinsic incubation period), mosquito longevity, frequency of blood feeding, biting behaviour, and larval development rates [3, 4]. These temperature-dependent processes collectively determine vectorial capacity – the mosquito population's ability to transmit malaria.

The influence of temperature on malaria transmission is characterised by a complex, non-linear relationship. Laboratory and field studies have established that both minimum and maximum temperature thresholds exist, beyond which efficient malaria transmission becomes increasingly unlikely or ceases altogether [5]. Each biological process involved in transmission exhibits its thermal response curve, and the integration of these individual responses creates the overall relationship between temperature and transmission potential. This complexity is further compounded by interactions with other environmental factors such as rainfall, humidity, and land use patterns, as well as socioeconomic conditions and control interventions [6, 7].

Early ecological models and laboratory studies identified the optimal temperature range for falciparum malaria transmission to be approximately between 25 °C and 27 °C, with transmission efficiency declining at temperatures below 18 °C or above 32 °C [6, 7]. However, more recent investigations have revealed that these thresholds demonstrate considerable variability depending on vector species, parasite species, and the specific geographic and ecological context [8]. For instance, some Anopheles species exhibit greater cold tolerance than others, enabling malaria transmission in cooler, highland regions. Similarly, Plasmodium vivax has been observed to develop within mosquitoes at lower temperatures compared to Plasmodium falciparum, potentially expanding its transmission range into temperate zones [8].

Understanding these temperature-malaria relationships has become increasingly urgent in the context of global climate change. Climate models consistently project shifts in temperature patterns across the globe, with potential implications for malaria transmission in both endemic and currently non-endemic areas [9, 10]. Regions experiencing warming trends may become newly suitable for malaria transmission if temperatures rise into the optimal range, particularly highland areas that were previously protected by cooler climates. Conversely, areas where temperatures exceed upper thermal thresholds might experience reduced transmission intensity. These dynamics could lead to geographical shifts in malaria distribution rather than uniform increases or decreases in burden, presenting complex challenges for health systems and control programs [9, 10].

The scientific literature examining the relationship between temperature and malaria incidence has expanded considerably in recent years, but findings demonstrate substantial heterogeneity across studies. Some investigations report positive correlations between rising temperatures and increased malaria incidence [11, 12], while others document negative associations or non-significant relationships [13, 14]. These discrepancies likely reflect genuine contextual differences in temperature-malaria dynamics across diverse ecological settings, but may also stem from methodological variations in study design, temperature metrics employed (e.g., mean, minimum, or maximum temperature; diurnal temperature range), analytical approaches, time scales considered, and the degree to which confounding factors such as rainfall, humidity, socioeconomic conditions, population immunity, and malaria control interventions are adequately accounted for in the analyses.

The importance of understanding temperature-malaria relationships extends beyond academic interest to practical applications in malaria surveillance, early warning systems, and climate-informed control strategies. As climate change accelerates, there is growing recognition of the need for adaptive approaches to malaria control that anticipate and respond to shifting transmission patterns. However, the development of such approaches requires a solid evidence base regarding how temperature variations influence malaria risk across diverse settings.

While several systematic reviews have examined Paz (2020) reviewed climate change impacts on vector-borne diseases in Europe, concluding that warming temperatures could facilitate the re-emergence of malaria in previously non-endemic European regions, but this review focused on qualitative risk assessment rather than quantitative meta-analysis of temperature-malaria correlations and was geographically limited to European contexts. Similarly [15], Colonia et al. (2022) systematically reviewed climate factors and malaria in Latin America and the Caribbean, identifying temperature as an important driver but focusing primarily on qualitative synthesis rather than meta-analytic pooling of correlation coefficients [16]. Other reviews have examined broader climate-health relationships (Rocklöv and Dubrow, 2020) [17] or focused on entomological outcomes rather than human case data (Semenza and Suk, 2018) [18]. Notably absent from the literature is a global systematic review that quantitatively synthesizes correlation coefficients between specific temperature metrics (mean, minimum, maximum) and malaria incidence or test positivity rates across diverse geographical and epidemiological settings. This gap has limited the development of evidence-based temperature thresholds for malaria early warning systems and climate-informed control strategies. Our review addresses this critical knowledge gap by providing the first comprehensive meta-analytic synthesis of temperature-malaria correlations, with particular attention to differential effects of minimum, maximum, and mean temperatures on malaria transmission dynamics across Africa, Asia, and other endemic regions.

Research question and objectives

This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses the fundamental research question as registered in the PROSPERO database (CRD42024591686): Whether exposure to relatively higher ambient temperature increases the risk of malaria as compared to relatively lower ambient temperature among the general population.

The primary objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to synthesise and quantify the association between ambient temperature and malaria incidence or prevalence across diverse geographical and ecological contexts. This will provide evidence-based insights into how temperature variations influence malaria transmission dynamics, which is crucial for developing effective and adaptive malaria control strategies in the context of ongoing climate change.

Geographic Inclusion Clarification:

Our inclusion criteria encompassed studies from regions with malaria transmission during the study period, regardless of current transmission status. This approach was adopted because: (1) the biological relationships between temperature and malaria transmission remain consistent across periods and geographic contexts; (2) excluding regions that have achieved elimination would introduce geographic bias and limit our ability to understand temperature-malaria relationships across diverse ecological settings; and (3) historical data from eliminated regions provide valuable insights into temperature thresholds and relationships that remain relevant for current endemic areas. For example, China was certified malaria-free in 2021, but studies from China covering periods of active transmission (1959–2008 in our included studies) provide important data on temperature-malaria relationships in temperate and subtropical zones that are underrepresented in current endemic regions. This approach maximizes the geographic and ecological diversity of our analysis while maintaining focus on established transmission areas.

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted in multiple electronic databases, including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, inception to September 19, 2024. Additionally, we searched specialised resources including the WHO Global Health Library, the Malaria in Pregnancy Library, and the Global Resource Information Database (GRID) of the United Nations Environment Programme.

The search strategy was developed in consultation with an information specialist and combined three conceptual blocks: (1) malaria and related terms, (2) temperature and related climate variables, and (3) epidemiological outcomes of interest. The following keywords and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms were used in various combinations: "malaria", "Plasmodium", "falciparum", "vivax", "malariae", "ovale", "knowlesi", "Anopheles", "temperature", "ambient temperature", "climate", "meteorological", "weather", "warming", "heat", "hot", "thermal", "incidence", "prevalence", "morbidity", "infection", "case", "outbreak", "epidemic", and "transmission".

Similar search strategies were adapted for other databases. To identify additional relevant studies, we manually searched the reference lists of included studies and previous reviews related to climate change and malaria. We also searched for grey literature through OpenGrey, the WHO websites, and reports from major malaria control programs. Conference proceedings from the American Society of Tropical Medicine and Hygiene, the European Congress on Tropical Medicine and International Health, and the Multilateral Initiative on Malaria were also screened for relevant abstracts from the past five years (2019–2024) (Supplementary Table 1).

Temporal scope justification

We included studies from 1959 onwards for several methodological and practical reasons. First, this timeframe captures the period when systematic meteorological data collection became more standardized and reliable globally, particularly in developing countries where malaria is endemic. Second, it encompasses the modern era of malaria diagnosis and surveillance, ensuring that the included studies used comparable diagnostic methods and case definitions. Third, this extended timeframe allows for the inclusion of long-term climate-health datasets that can better capture the cyclical nature of both climate patterns and malaria transmission, providing more robust correlation estimates. Finally, restricting to more recent decades would have significantly reduced our already limited sample size, as few studies have reported the specific correlation coefficients required for our meta-analytic approach. While climate and socioeconomic contexts have evolved since 1959, the fundamental biological relationships between temperature and malaria transmission remain consistent, making historical data valuable for understanding these associations.

Study selection

The study selection process occurred in two stages. First, two independent reviewers screened titles and abstracts of all retrieved citations against the eligibility criteria using a standardised form. Articles that potentially met the inclusion criteria or where there was uncertainty were retained for full-text review. In the second stage, the same reviewers independently assessed the full-text articles for eligibility. Any disagreements at either stage were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by consulting a third reviewer. The systematic search initially identified 1,853 potentially relevant citations after removing duplicates. Following title and abstract screening, 124 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Only 11 studies ultimately met our inclusion criteria, primarily due to our focus on studies reporting correlation coefficients (or data from which these could be calculated) between temperature metrics and malaria incidence or prevalence. Many studies examining temperature-malaria relationships were excluded because they: (1) used different statistical approaches (regression coefficients, risk ratios) that could not be standardized for meta-analysis; (2) focused on entomological outcomes without human case data; (3) lacked the minimum 12-month follow-up to account for seasonality; (4) did not provide confirmed malaria diagnoses; or (5) failed to adequately control for basic confounding variables. While the resulting sample size is modest, these 11 studies represent the current literature that provides comparable quantitative measures of association between temperature and malaria suitable for meta-analytic pooling. We acknowledge this limitation while emphasising that this initial synthesis provides valuable insights into temperature-malaria relationships across diverse epidemiological settings.

We conducted separate meta-analyses for incidence and test positivity rates, clearly distinguishing between these outcomes in our analysis and reporting.

Data synthesis and analysis

We conducted both narrative synthesis and, where appropriate, quantitative meta-analysis. For the narrative synthesis, we summarised findings by geographic region, study design, and temperature metric, highlighting patterns and inconsistencies in the evidence.

For quantitative synthesis, we extracted or calculated effect estimates representing the change in malaria risk associated with a standardised unit increase in temperature (e.g., per 1 °C). When studies reported results using different units, we converted them to a consistent scale. If studies reported multiple effect estimates based on different lag periods or temperature metrics, we extracted all relevant estimates but identified a primary estimate for each study based on the most comprehensive model (typically the model with the most appropriate lag structure and adjustment for confounders).

Given the expected heterogeneity in study designs, populations, and analytical approaches, we employed random-effects meta-analysis using the DerSimonian and Laird method to calculate pooled effect estimates and 95% confidence intervals. We conducted separate meta-analyses for different study designs, temperature metrics, and malaria outcomes when sufficient data were available.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the I2 statistic, with values of 25%, 50%, and 75% considered as low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. All analyses were conducted using R version 4.1.0 with the meta and metafor packages. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Features of the included research

The systematic review included 11 studies examining the relationship between temperature and malaria incidence across diverse geographical regions. The studies spanned multiple countries across Africa and Asia, including India (4 studies across different regions), China (3 studies), Cameroon, Botswana, South Africa, and Zimbabwe. Study periods ranged from 1959 to 2019, with most studies covering approximately 6–10 years of observational data. Sample sizes varied considerably, from 246 cases in Gwanda, Zimbabwe to over 5 million in Bareilly, India. All studies examined the correlation between temperature parameters and malaria incidence, with most investigating multiple temperature metrics including mean, maximum, and minimum temperatures. Correlation coefficients ranged from −0.080 to 0.650, with the majority showing positive correlations of moderate strength (0.3–0.6). The most consistent positive correlations were observed with mean and minimum temperatures, while maximum temperature occasionally showed weaker or even negative correlations. Study designs were predominantly retrospective observational studies (5 studies) and time series analyses (6 studies), with one ecological study. Diagnostic methods were well-documented in most studies, with the combination of Rapid Diagnostic Tests (RDT) and microscopy being the most common approach (8 studies), while others relied on health post records or Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) reports. This collection of studies provides a geographically diverse and methodologically sound basis for assessing the relationship between ambient temperature and malaria incidence across different Discussion

It is important to note that our meta-analyses primarily examined malaria test positivity rates rather than true population prevalence. Test positivity rates reflect the proportion of positive results among individuals seeking healthcare for malaria symptoms, which may overestimate transmission intensity compared to population-based prevalence surveys. This distinction is crucial for interpreting our findings, as test positivity rates are influenced by healthcare-seeking behaviour, diagnostic practices, and case management protocols in addition to actual disease transmission. While test positivity rates provide valuable insights into healthcare burden and can serve as proxy indicators for transmission trends, they should not be directly interpreted as population prevalence estimates.

The manuscript indicates that temperature plays a crucial role in malaria transmission through multiple mechanisms: it affects the development rate of Plasmodium parasites within mosquitoes (extrinsic incubation period), mosquito survival, biting rates, and larval development. The relationship is complex and non-linear, with optimal transmission occurring between 25 °C and 27 °C, while transmission efficiency decreases below 18 °C or above 32°C.

This systematic review and meta-analysis provide compelling evidence for the association between ambient temperature and malaria transmission across diverse geographical settings. While our study includes concentrations of research from India (4 studies) and China (3 studies), these countries themselves encompass remarkable ecological diversity—from India's tropical coastal regions and highland areas to China's temperate and subtropical zones spanning vast latitudinal and altitudinal gradients. The remaining studies from Cameroon, Botswana, South Africa, and Zimbabwe further extend our analysis across different African ecological zones, including equatorial rainforest, semi-arid savanna, and highland plateau environments. This geographic distribution, though not globally comprehensive, captures diverse transmission ecologies including stable endemic transmission (tropical Africa), seasonal transmission patterns (highland and semi-arid areas), and historically variable transmission zones (temperate China), providing insights across different malaria epidemiological contexts and climate regimes.

Regional heterogeneity in temperature-malaria dynamics and transmission setting analysis

The substantial heterogeneity observed in our meta-analysis (I2 > 99%) reflects fundamental ecological and epidemiological differences across study regions that extend beyond simple geographical variation. Our findings reveal distinct patterns that align with malaria transmission ecology theory and provide insights into how temperature effects vary across different transmission intensities.

Temperature effects across transmission settings

In epidemic-prone temperate and highland regions of China and India, where transmission is typically seasonal and temperature-limited, we observed the strongest temperature-malaria correlations (r = 0.425–0.650 for minimum temperature). These settings demonstrate clear temperature-dependency because transmission often operates near thermal thresholds, making temperature fluctuations critical determinants of transmission success. The strong correlations in studies from Jinan, China (r = 0.590–0.650) and highland regions of India reflect scenarios where temperature acts as the primary limiting factor for malaria transmission.

Multifactorial dynamics in high-transmission african settings

Conversely, in stable endemic regions of tropical Africa, we observed more moderate temperature-malaria associations (r = 0.174–0.520), despite these areas having higher overall transmission intensity. This pattern reflects the complex multifactorial nature of transmission in high-burden settings, where temperature effects are modulated by numerous interacting factors including: (1) Vector ecology complexity—established, diverse Anopheles populations with varying thermal tolerances that may buffer temperature fluctuations; (2) Population immunity levels—acquired immunity in endemic populations may reduce the apparent impact of temperature-driven transmission changes on clinical outcomes; (3) Rainfall and humidity interactions—in tropical settings, precipitation patterns may override temperature effects as the primary transmission driver; (4) Control intervention coverage—widespread use of insecticide-treated nets, indoor residual spraying, and case management may mask underlying temperature-transmission relationships; (5) Healthcare-seeking behavior—established healthcare systems and different diagnostic practices may influence the relationship between environmental factors and recorded case rates.

Geographic-specific transmission ecology

The regional variations also reflect distinct ecological contexts that modify temperature-malaria relationships. Indian studies spanning diverse ecological zones (coastal Cameroon r = 0.174 vs. inland regions r = 0.359–0.612) demonstrate how local environmental factors, monsoon patterns, and altitude variations create heterogeneous temperature sensitivities even within countries. Similarly, the contrast between temperate Chinese cities (r = 0.590–0.650) and tropical African sites suggests that latitude-dependent factors, including seasonal temperature ranges, photoperiod effects, and vector species composition, fundamentally alter how temperature influences transmission dynamics.

This heterogeneity has critical implications for climate-informed malaria control strategies, suggesting that temperature monitoring approaches must be calibrated to local transmission ecology rather than applied uniformly across endemic regions.

The meta-analysis revealed a moderate positive correlation between mean temperature and malaria incidence (r = 0.417, 95% CI: 0.098–0.735), consistent with findings from Mordecai et al. (2013) who identified thermal optima for malaria transmission [3]. However, our analysis demonstrated extreme statistical heterogeneity (I2 = 99.94%), suggesting considerable regional variation in this relationship, which aligns with observations by Rocklöv and Dubrow (2020) [17] regarding the complex interplay between climate factors and vector-borne diseases.

Notably, minimum temperature showed the strongest and most consistent positive correlation with malaria positivity (r = 0.454, 95% CI: 0.358–0.549), supporting Beck-Johnson et al.'s (2013) conclusion that night-time temperatures are critical for mosquito survival and parasite development [7]. This finding has particular relevance in the context of climate change, as global warming disproportionately affects minimum temperatures (IPCC, 2022) [28].

In contrast to minimum temperature, maximum temperature demonstrated a weaker association with malaria (R = 0.356, 95% CI: 0.263–0.450 for positivity rates; r = 0.082, 95% CI: −0.255–0.419 for incidence), with some studies even reporting negative correlations. This supports Shapiro et al.'s (2017) finding that excessively high temperatures can inhibit parasite development and reduce mosquito survival [4].

The substantial heterogeneity observed in our meta-analysis (I2 > 99% for most analyses) reflects the inherently complex nature of temperature-malaria relationships. While our analysis focused specifically on quantifying temperature associations, it is critical to interpret these findings within the broader ecological context of malaria transmission. Temperature influences malaria through multiple pathways: directly affecting vector physiology and parasite development, and indirectly by modifying habitat availability, human behaviour, and the efficacy of control interventions. These pathways do not operate in isolation but interact with other environmental determinants (rainfall patterns, humidity levels, landscape features), socioeconomic factors (housing quality, healthcare access, population movement), and control measures (insecticide-treated nets, indoor residual spraying, case management). This complex web of interactions likely explains why temperature effects vary substantially across different geographical and epidemiological settings, as evidenced by the wide range of correlation coefficients observed in our review (from −0.080 to 0.650). Future malaria risk assessments must therefore consider temperature within integrated models that account for these multifaceted relationships, rather than relying on temperature as a standalone predictor.

Regional variations and global context

The substantial heterogeneity observed across studies reflects the complex, non-linear relationship between temperature and malaria transmission, which varies by geographic context, vector species, and parasite type. Our findings align with the WHO World Malaria Report (2023) [1], which notes regional variations in malaria trends despite global climate patterns, suggesting that local environmental and socioeconomic factors significantly modify temperature-malaria relationships.

Studies from India and China showed particularly strong correlations between temperature and malaria, consistent with Caminade et al.'s (2014) projection of shifting malaria distributions due to climate change [9]. The weaker associations observed in some African studies suggest that in high-transmission settings, other factors such as immunity levels, control interventions, and precipitation may play more dominant roles (Feachem et al., 2019) [2].

Implications for climate change adaptation

The optimal temperature range for malaria transmission (25–27°C) reported in experimental studies (Murdock et al., 2016) [5] aligns with the positive correlations observed in our review, though our meta-analysis did not directly calculate optimal temperature ranges across studies. Our included studies primarily reported linear correlation coefficients rather than non-linear relationships that would allow identification of specific optimal temperature thresholds. However, the consistent positive correlations we observed across temperature metrics suggest that most study regions operated within or below optimal temperature ranges, as correlations would be expected to become negative if temperatures frequently exceeded optimal thresholds for transmission. Future research examining non-linear temperature-malaria relationships across our study regions would be valuable for empirically validating whether the experimentally-derived 25–27°C optimum holds across diverse field settings. As global temperatures continue to rise, regions currently below this optimum may experience increased transmission potential, while areas already at or above optimum temperatures might see limited changes or even reductions in transmission efficiency.

This supports Leedale et al.'s (2016) conclusion that climate change will likely cause geographical shifts in malaria risk rather than uniform increases [10]. The stronger association with minimum temperature is particularly concerning given that global warming is amplifying night-time temperatures more rapidly than daytime temperatures (IPCC, 2022) [28].

Our findings have direct relevance for existing international malaria prevention frameworks and suggest important areas for guideline enhancement:

WHO global technical strategy for malaria 2016–2030 integration

The WHO's current strategy emphasizes surveillance and response systems but provides limited guidance on climate-informed approaches [29]. Our demonstration of consistent temperature-malaria correlations (particularly minimum temperature: r = 0.454, 95% CI: 0.358–0.549) supports the incorporation of temperature thresholds into the WHO's recommended early warning systems. Specifically, minimum temperature monitoring could enhance the WHO's malaria surveillance indicators, particularly in regions experiencing warming trends, aligning with the strategy's pillar 1 on ensuring universal access to malaria prevention [29].

Alignment with WHO climate and health initiatives

The WHO's Operational Framework for Building Climate Resilient Health Systems calls for climate-informed disease surveillance [30]. Our meta-analysis provides quantitative evidence supporting temperature-based risk stratification, particularly relevant for the WHO's recommended vulnerability assessments. The stronger correlation with minimum temperature aligns with WHO recommendations for monitoring environmental determinants of vector-borne diseases [31]. This directly supports the WHO's climate and health country profile initiative, which emphasizes evidence-based climate-health monitoring [32].

Enhanced early warning system guidelines

Current WHO guidelines for malaria early warning systems focus primarily on rainfall and epidemic thresholds [33]. Our findings suggest that international guidelines should explicitly incorporate minimum temperature monitoring, as this metric showed the most consistent association across diverse settings. This is particularly relevant for WHO's recommended integrated disease surveillance and response (IDSR) frameworks [34], which could benefit from temperature-based trigger mechanisms.

Climate change adaptation in malaria control

Our results directly support WHO recommendations for climate-resilient malaria programming by providing evidence-based temperature thresholds for adaptive management [35]. The substantial regional heterogeneity we observed (I2 > 99%) reinforces WHO guidance emphasizing locally-adapted approaches rather than universal protocols [29]. This aligns with the WHO Technical Brief on Climate Change and Malaria, which emphasizes the need for country-specific climate risk assessments [36].

Integration with roll back malaria partnership guidelines

The Roll Back Malaria Partnership's Strategic Plan (2018–2030) emphasises innovative surveillance approaches [37]. Our quantitative evidence for temperature-malaria associations provides a foundation for integrating climate monitoring into the partnership's recommended surveillance strengthening activities. The consistent minimum temperature associations support the partnership's call for context-specific surveillance innovations [37].

Bridging research and practice

While our review provides robust evidence for temperature-malaria associations, translating these findings into operational guidelines requires careful consideration of local capacity, existing surveillance infrastructure, and resource constraints [40]. International guidelines should provide tiered approaches that allow countries to integrate temperature monitoring according to their technical and financial capabilities, following WHO's building blocks approach to health systems strengthening [41].

This quantitative synthesis strengthens the evidence base for climate-informed malaria control recommended by international health organizations and provides specific guidance for enhancing current prevention frameworks in the context of climate change.

Limitations and future directions

Despite our comprehensive approach, several limitations must be acknowledged. A primary limitation is the inclusion of only 11 studies in this systematic review and meta-analysis, which is a relatively small sample considering the extensive research conducted into environmental and climatic drivers of malaria transmission globally. This limited sample size resulted from our stringent inclusion criteria, which required studies to report quantitative correlation coefficients between temperature metrics and malaria outcomes, have a minimum follow-up of 12 months, and include a confirmed malaria diagnosis. Many studies examining temperature-malaria relationships reported other statistical measures (e.g., regression coefficients, odds ratios) or focused primarily on entomological outcomes rather than human cases, leading to their exclusion.

An important limitation of our review is that we focused on linear correlation coefficients and did not analyze non-linear temperature-malaria relationships or empirically derive optimal temperature ranges from the included studies. Most studies reported simple correlation analyses rather than more sophisticated modeling approaches that could identify temperature thresholds, optimal ranges, or inflection points in the temperature-response relationship. This methodological limitation prevents us from providing evidence-based optimal temperature ranges specific to different geographic or ecological contexts and limits our ability to validate experimentally-derived temperature thresholds using field epidemiological data.

Scope limitations and PICO refinement

While our PICO criteria initially included a comprehensive range of temperature exposures (temperature anomalies, diurnal temperature range) and malaria outcomes (case counts when population denominators were available, hospital admissions, severe malaria cases, species-specific outcomes), our final analysis was limited to studies reporting correlations with mean, minimum, and maximum temperatures about malaria incidence and test positivity rates. This narrowing of scope occurred because: (1) no studies in our systematic search reported quantitative correlations with temperature anomalies or diurnal temperature range that met our inclusion criteria; (2) insufficient studies provided data on severe malaria cases or hospital admissions with correlation coefficients suitable for meta-analysis; (3) most studies reported aggregate malaria outcomes rather than species-specific results; and (4) case count data without clear population denominators could not be reliably analyzed. The limited availability of studies examining these additional temperature metrics and outcome measures represents an important gap in the current literature and highlights priority areas for future research. Our focus on mean, minimum, and maximum temperatures with incidence and test positivity rates, while more limited than initially envisioned, represents the evidence base that was available for quantitative synthesis and provides the most robust meta-analytic findings possible given the current state of the literature.

Furthermore, while our meta-analysis demonstrates significant correlations between temperature variables and malaria metrics, we acknowledge that these associations represent only one dimension of a complex ecological system. Malaria transmission is influenced by a multifaceted combination of environmental factors (rainfall, humidity, vegetation), socioeconomic conditions (housing quality, access to healthcare), vector control interventions, and biological factors (vector species composition, parasite strains, population immunity). The statistical methodology employed in our study, while appropriate for quantifying temperature-malaria correlations, cannot fully disentangle these complex, non-linear, and often synergistic relationships.

The included studies exhibited substantial methodological heterogeneity in temperature metrics, analytical approaches, and outcome measures. Most studies were retrospective and may not have adequately controlled for all potential confounding factors such as socioeconomic changes, intervention coverage, and land use patterns. Additionally, the regional representation was primarily limited to parts of Africa and Asia, with notable gaps from the Americas and other regions where malaria is endemic.

Finally, meta-regression analyses with larger sample sizes would help identify factors that modify the temperature-malaria relationship, potentially explaining the heterogeneity observed in our review. Multi-disciplinary approaches that integrate epidemiological, entomological, climatic, and socioeconomic data are essential for developing a more complete understanding of how climate variables, including temperature, drive malaria transmission dynamics in the context of ongoing global change. Future research should focus on standardising temperature-malaria analysis methodologies, incorporating non-linear modelling approaches, and investigating the interactive effects of temperature with other climatic and non-climatic factors. Prospective studies with high-resolution temperature and malaria data would provide more robust evidence for causal relationships.

Conclusion

This systematic review demonstrates that temperature, particularly mean and minimum temperatures, shows significant associations with malaria transmission dynamics across diverse epidemiological settings. The substantial heterogeneity observed underscores the need for locally tailored climate-informed malaria control strategies rather than one-size-fits-all approaches. The stronger association with minimum temperature is noteworthy in the context of climate change, as global warming disproportionately affects night-time temperatures.

While our findings provide important initial quantitative evidence on temperature-malaria relationships, they must be interpreted within the broader context of malaria ecology. Temperature represents just one—albeit important—component of a complex system involving multiple environmental, biological, and socioeconomic factors. Future research should aim to develop integrated frameworks that better capture these complex interactions and provide a more nuanced understanding of climate-malaria relationships.

As climate change progresses, understanding the multifaceted relationships between climatic variables and malaria will be increasingly crucial for effective surveillance, early warning systems, and adaptive malaria control programs. Our review contributes to this understanding while highlighting the need for more comprehensive approaches that can inform evidence-based strategies for malaria control in a changing climate.
