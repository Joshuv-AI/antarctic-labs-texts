Impact of various types of heat treatment on the content of selected B vitamin and their profile in goose breast meat
Authors: Zuzanna Goluch, Gabriela Haraf, Andrzej Okruszek, Małgorzata Stryjecka, Monika Wereńska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe objective of this study was to (1) investigate the impact of commonly used heat treatment methods – water bath cooking (WBC), convection oven roasting (OCR), grilling (G), and pan frying (PF) – on the concentrations and retention of various forms of selected B vitamins (B1, B2, B3, B5, B6, and B12) in goose breast muscle, and (2) assess the extent to which a 100 g portion of goose meat, both with and without skin, contributes to meeting the Nutrient Reference Values-Requirements (NRV-R) for these vitamins in the adults. The material used in the study comprised 36 breast muscles of 17-week-old White Koluda® geese. The contents of B vitamins were determined using liquid chromatography. Significantly lower thermal losses were found in skinless meat (P ≤ 0.05). This meat contained significantly (P ≤ 0.05) more niacin, nicotinamide, and vitamin B6. Meat with skin contained more thiamine monophosphate, nicotinic acid, pyridoxal, pyridoxine, and vitamin B12 (P ≤ 0.05). Heat treatment techniques significantly affected the total content of thiamine, riboflavin, nicotinic acid, vitamin B6, pyridoxal, pyridoxamine, and vitamin B12. Significant differences in retention were observed only in niacin, nicotinamide, vitamin B6, and pyridoxine. A portion of 100 g of goose breast meat covers the largest extent NRV-R of an adult for niacin (average 31.7 %) and the smallest for thiamin (0.66 %), regardless of the presence of skin or type of heat treatment. The findings described can be helpful to consumers in making informed dietary choices and in developing nutrient composition tables and computer programs for nutritionists, food labeling, evaluation, and diet planning. However, it is not possible to identify a single heat treatment at which individual B vitamins would achieve the greatest retention.Keywords: Goose, Breast muscle, Heat treatment, Vitamins B, Nutrient reference values

Introduction

Generally, B vitamins are not stored in the human body. After fulfilling their physiological functions, they are primarily excreted in the urine and should be supplied through the daily diet. All B vitamins play a key role as coenzymes in enzymatic reactions in various biological systems. They directly impact energy levels, brain function, and cell metabolism. They are essential for the metabolism of carbohydrates, amino acids, fatty acids, and lipids, as well as the synthesis of proteins, cholesterol, neurotransmitters, S-adenosylmethionine, and nucleotide bases. These vitamins are also essential for erythropoiesis, the production of adrenal hormones, maintaining gastrointestinal function, maintaining the condition of the skin and its derivatives, and maintaining visual function. Individuals with digestive and absorption disorders, malnutrition, those following alternative diets (e.g., veganism), the elderly, pregnant women, those abusing alcohol, or taking medications with known interactions are particularly at risk of deficiency. Symptoms of B vitamin deficiencies can include problems such as anemia, a weakened immune system, fatigue, neurological disorders, metabolic disorders, and skin disorders (Sarwar et al., 2022; Hrubša et al., 2022; Ibrahim et al., 2023; Bayram-Weston, 2024). The physiological functions of individual B vitamins and the symptoms of their deficiency in the body have been thoroughly described in recent review articles (Hanna et al., 2022; Sarwar et al., 2022; Hrubša et al., 2022; Ibrahim et al., 2023). To maintain consumer health, it is essential to provide these vitamins primarily through a balanced diet, in which poultry meat can be a valuable dietary source. However, these vitamins can also be consumed by consumers through fortified foods (Alina et al., 2019; Rathee et al., 2022) and/or dietary supplements (Schellack et al., 2016; Ali et al., 2022), and in cases of apparent clinical deficiencies, even prescribed in the form of medications. However, when using these forms of vitamin supplementation, their safety should be considered to avoid hypervitaminosis and exceeding the Upper Level (for niacin, vitamin B6, and folate), which may lead to toxicity (EFSA, 2024).

It is generally recognized that poultry meat can be a good dietary source of B vitamins. Still, it is more often consumed by consumers after using various heat-processing techniques than raw (depending on environmental, economic, and cultural traditions), which is believed to ensure microbiological safety, desirable sensory qualities, better digestibility, and health-promoting nutritional value, including the retention of B vitamins (Marangoni et al., 2015; Sobral et al., 2018; Yong et al., 2019). Furthermore, poultry meat has health-promoting benefits as it prevents non-communicable diseases, including anemia, overweight, type 2 diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases, reduces the risk of developing certain cancers, increases immunity, and slows down the aging process (Jilo and Hasan, 2022; Leroy et al., 2023; Selvan, 2024). Poultry skin contains sulfur amino acids, collagen, elastin, minerals, and vitamins. However, in nutritional prevention and diet therapy, it is recommended to remove the skin from meat before consumption to regulate blood lipid profiles and/or reduce body weight (Soriano-Santos, 2010; Marangoni et al., 2015; Stangierski and Lesnierowski, 2015). The poultry industry is responding to consumer needs, and currently, a portion of production is sold as culinary elements, raw or after heat treatment (ready-to-eat products). Therefore, these products are gaining popularity due to, among other factors, their affordability, ease of preparation, and the absence of religious restrictions (Petracci, 2022). Catering services, which subject meat to various heat treatment techniques, are also becoming increasingly popular worldwide, as expected by consumers (Li et al., 2024).

This study investigated the impact of various thermal processing methods, representative of contemporary consumer practices, on the content and retention of selected B vitamins in goose breast muscles, both with and without skin. Additionally, it aimed to assess the potential contribution of a 100 g portion of goose meat, with or without skin, to meeting adult Nutrient Reference Values (NRV) for these vitamins.

Material and methods

Meat samples

The breast muscles (Pectoralis major) of 17-week-old White Koluda® geese were used in the study. This genotype was developed through decades of targeted breeding at the National Research Institute of Animal Production, Experimental Station in Kołuda Wielka (Gąsior et al., 2021). The birds were reared on the same commercial farm. They fed the same diet, including a premix containing vitamins B-group: 3 mg of B1, 5 IU of B2 (0.003 mg), 5 mg of B6, 13 mg of Ca pantothenate, and 35 mg of B12 per 1 kg of feed (Buzała et al., 2014; Wojciechowski, 2016; Goluch et al., 2021). Before slaughter, the geese were subjected to a 12-hour feed withdrawal. Then, the birds were slaughtered at a poultry plant, in accordance with the regulations in effect in the Polish poultry industry (Minister of Agriculture and Rural Development, 2004). The eviscerated carcasses were placed in a cold store at a temperature of 2°C to 4°C for 24 hours post-mortem, after which the breast muscles were cut out from the carcasses. The meat was transported under refrigerated conditions to the laboratory of Wrocław University of Economics. Immediately after delivery to the laboratory, the muscles intended for heat treatment were divided into groups of similar weight and subjected to a specific heat treatment.

The average weight of breast muscles with skin was 310.9 ± 9.6 g, whereas muscles without skin and subcutaneous fat weighed 260.6 ± 8.6 g (n = 36). The experimental material consisted of four raw samples (two with skin and two without skin) and 32 heat-treated samples, representing four cooking methods (n = 8 per method; four with skin and four without skin).

Heat treatments

The study employed heat treatment methods that reflect typical household poultry preparation practices, including boiling (water bath cooking – WBC), grilling (G), pan-frying (PF) without added fat, and oven convection roasting (OCR). Meat samples were not seasoned or supplemented with salt, spices, or additives. All heat treatments were conducted until the internal temperature of the thickest part of the muscle reached 75°C, in accordance with USDA guidelines for microbiological safety (USDA, 2025a). This temperature was verified using a handheld thermometer. Oven roasting and grilling procedures involved the use of Teflon-coated thermocouples (Type T, Omega Engineering Inc., USA) linked to a multichannel data logger (Doric, VAS Engineering Inc., USA). After cooking, the samples were cooled at ambient temperature for approximately two hours. Each raw and processed goose breast was then individually minced using an electric grinder (Zelmer MM/1000/887, Poland) fitted with a 3 mm mesh plate.

The method of performing individual heat treatments is described in detail in the publication by Wereńska et al. (2021). Briefly, during Water Bath Cooking (WBC), whole muscles were packed tightly in plastic bags, placed in a water bath (model SW 22, Julabo GmbH, Seelbach, Germany), and cooked at 90°C for 30 min. Grilling (G) was carried out using an electric grill (model PD 2020R, Red Fox, Warsaw, Poland) that was preheated to 200°C. Whole muscle samples were placed between two heated plates and cooked for approximately 25 min. In the Oven Convection Roasting (OCR) method, each goose breast was wrapped in aluminum foil to limit moisture loss. The oven (model EB7551B Fusion, Amica Ltd., Wronki, Poland) was preheated to 200°C, and the samples were then roasted for approximately 25 min. Pan Frying (PF) was performed using an electric frying pan preheated to 160°C (model 48155, Unold AG, Hockenheim, Germany). The whole breast was fried evenly on both sides until the temperature inside the muscle reached 75°C.

Cooking loss (CL)

Cooking loss (CL) was calculated from the differences in weight before (Wb) and after heat processing (Wt) of the samples, which were cooled to room temperature (Wołoszyn et al., 2020).

Chemical analysis

Vitamins B were quantified using liquid chromatography coupled with mass spectrometry (LC-MS). Certified standards of thiamine hydrochloride (B1), thiamine monophosphate (B1), riboflavin (B2), nicotinic acid (B3), nicotinamide (B3), calcium-d-pantothenate (B5), pyridoxine hydrochloride (B6), pyridoxamine (B6) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany), and pyridoxal (B6) from Toronto Research Chemicals (LGC Standards, Teddington, Great Britain). Stock solutions of the pure standards were prepared by dissolving them in 50 % ethanol (EtOH) at a concentration of 1 mg/mL. These solutions were subsequently used to prepare calibration standards in the range of 1–500 ng/mL. Isotopically labelled vitamins analogues (thiamine-13C3 hydrochloride, riboflavin-13C415N2, nicotinic acid-13C6, nicotinamide-13C6, pantothenic acid-13C315N hemicalcium, pyridoxine-methyl-d3 hydrochloride purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (Merck) and pyridoxal-methyl-d3, pyridoxamine-d3 dihydrochloride, purchased from Toronto Research Chemicals (LGC Standards, Teddington, Great Britain)) were used as internal standards.

Meat samples were weighed on an analytical balance and homogenized in a 50 % EtOH solution (5 µL solution to 1 mg sample) using Precellys Evolution Touch Homogenizer (Bertin Technologies, Montigny-le-Bretonneux, France), who performed homogenization by 30 seconds of shaking at 4 500 RPM, 10 seconds rest, 10 cycles at 4°C. Next, 50 µL of the sample was transferred to a new 1.5 mL polypropylene sample tube. 250 µL of internal standard solution in methanol with 0.1 % formic acid was added. The samples were vortexed for 10 min at 1,500 RPM and subsequently centrifuged for 10 min at 14,000 RPM. Supernatants were transferred to new sample tubes and evaporated to dryness under a gentle nitrogen stream at 40°C. Dry residues were reconstituted in 200 µL of 20 mM ammonium formate with 0.2 % NH4OH solution, transferred to autosampler vials, and subjected to LC-MS analysis.

Chromatographic separation was performed using an Acquity UPLC BEH C18 130 Å, 1.7 µm, 2.1 mm × 100 mm column (Waters, Milford, MA, USA), which was maintained in a column oven at 45°C. Mobile phase A (MPA) consisted of 20 mM ammonium formate with 0.1 % NH4OH in Milli-Q grade water, and mobile phase B (MPB) consisted of LC-MS grade methanol. The chromatographic gradient started at 0 % MPB, was maintained for 0.5 min, then increased to 95 % MPB from 1.8 to 2.5 min, and was kept at 2.5 min before changing to 0 % MPB at 2.51 min. The flow was maintained at a constant rate of 0.5 mL/min, and the total run time was 3.0 min. Mass spectrometer operated in multiple-reaction-monitoring (MRM) mode with positive ionization (ESI+). The tune method parameters consisted of a capillary voltage of 0.6 kV, cone voltage of 30 V, desolvation temperature of 600°C, desolvation gas flow of 800 L/hr, and cone gas flow of 150 L/hr.

Vitamin B12 was analyzed according to the method of Sami et al. (2014) with modifications. Two grams of the homogenized sample were soaked in 25 cm³ of 0.1 N H₂SO₄ solution and then exposed to 120°C for 30 min. The pH was then adjusted to 4.5 using a 2.5 M CH3COONa solution, and 0.05 g of diastase enzyme (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was added. The solution was incubated overnight at 37°C. The incubated solution was diluted to 50.0 cm3 with deionized water. The solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter, and a 67 μl aliquot of the filtrate was injected into the HPLC system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan). Chromatographic separation was performed using a Shim-pack Velox C18 reversed-phase high-performance liquid chromatography (RP-HPLC) column (2.7 μm, 4.6 × 100 mm). Separation was performed using a mobile phase in gradient mode, consisting of mobile phase A (phosphate buffer) and mobile phase B (acetonitrile). The time program for mobile phase B was (0.0–3.0 min), B (1 %) (3.1–10 min), B (50 %) (10.1–16.0 min), flow rate 0.93 cm3/min, temperature 25°C, UV detection 361 nm. A stock solution of pure cyanocobalamin standard (Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) was dissolved in 0.1 M HCl to obtain a 100 μg/mL solution, which was used to prepare solutions for the calibration curve in the concentration range of 0.05 to 2.00 μg/mL. Vitamin determinations were performed for each sample in duplicate.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using Statistica 13.1. The procedures included: (1) assessment of data distribution with the Shapiro–Wilk test and verification of variance homogeneity using Levene’s test; (2) calculation of arithmetic means and standard errors of the mean (SEM); (3) evaluation of differences in B-group vitamin contents between heat treatment methods (Raw, WBC, G, OCR, PF), meat types (with and without skin), as well as between treatments within each meat type, using Tukey’s multiple comparison test (P ≤ 0.05 and P ≤ 0.01); (4) one-way ANOVA, according to the model Yij = μ + Aj + eij, where Aj represents the effect of heat treatment within a meat type. Results are presented as mean values (±SEM) obtained from two independent chemical determinations. In addition, Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was applied to illustrate the relationships between B vitamin contents, meat type, and processing method, using Statistica 13.1 software.

Results and discussion

Cooking loss (CL)

In the conducted experiment, a significantly (P ≤ 0.01) higher CL was found in goose breast with skin than without skin (43.2 vs. 37.1 %) after all four heat treatments (Table 1). A similar relationship was found in other studies (Wołoszyn et al., 2020; Goluch et al., 2024), in which the CL for skinless breast meat ranged from 36.3 % to 38.53 %, and for breast muscles with skin from 45.4 % to 45.52 %. A higher content of CL in meat with skin may result from the presence of subcutaneous fat in geese and its release during heat treatments. Subcutaneous fat has been shown to melt at temperatures ranging from 40 to 105°C, and the amount of fat melted increases with increasing temperature (Sakunde, 2020).

In the conducted experiment, CL ranged from 36.9 to 42.2 %, and unlike other published studies (Wołoszyn et al., 2020; Goluch et al., 2024), no significant effect of the heat treatment technique used for goose breast meat on CL was found. Vitamins B composition in goose breast meat

Vitamins are highly labile, being affected to a greater or lesser extent by various factors, including temperature, light, oxygen, pH, reducing agents, oxidizing agents, and metal ions (Herrera-Ardila et al., 2022). Due to the high solubility and thermal instability of B vitamins, their losses may occur during heat treatment of food, depending on the matrix (Riccio et al., 2006). Unfortunately, there is a lack of research in the literature on the content of B vitamins in goose meat, particularly in relation to the culinary processes employed. The extent of vitamin losses during meat processing depends on the sensitivity of these components to various environmental factors, particularly their solubility in water.

Vitamin B1 (thiamine and thiamin monophosphate)

The thiamine content in wild goose carcasses is 0.28 mg/100 g (SFCDB, 2024). According to the food composition database of various countries, the thiamine content in raw meat of carcasses without skin ranges from 0.10 to 0.13 mg/100 g, while with skin it ranges from 0.07 to 0.125 mg/100 g (NFSA 2018; McCance, 2021; SFCDB, 2024; BDA, 2025; Frida Food Data, 2025).

In our study, the thiamine content in raw goose breast meat without skin was 0.015 mg/100 g, while the skin-on samples contained 0.012 mg/100 g. A higher thiamine level (0.284 mg/100 g) was reported for skinless breast meat of Canada Goose (USDA, 2025b). Conversely, a lower concentration (0.007 mg/100 g) in raw bone-in goose breast meat is listed in the Slovak Food Composition Database (SFCDB, 2024). In our experiment, no significant effect of meat type (with or without skin) on thiamine content was observed. However, all thermal processing methods applied resulted in a significant reduction (P ≤ 0.001) in thiamine content (Total for HT; Table 1) compared with raw meat (R > WBC, PF, OCR, G) (Madruga, 1997; Resconi et al., 2013; Bleicher et al., 2022). Other authors (Lombardi-Boccia et al., 2005) observed a complete absence of thiamine in chicken and turkey breast meat after cooking in an iron-free pan. The reduction in the content of this vitamin in heat-treated meat compared to raw meat may result from its transfer to heat leakage, degradation leading to the formation of hydrogen sulfide, and the formation of volatile, water-soluble compounds responsible for meaty aroma compounds (respectively 0.04 and 0.02 mg/100 g Fresh Weight) (Lombardi-Boccia et al., 2005). Similar observations related to the thiamine content in skinless chicken breast meat have been published by other authors (Çatak, 2022b; Çatak et al., 2024), who found a significant (P ≤ 0.05) reduction in its content after frying, baking, boiling, and grilling (respectively: 0.085; 0.059; 0.047 and 0.035 mg/100 g), compared to raw meat (0.115 mg/100 g). In general, there is little data available in the literature on the thiamine content of heat-treated goose breast. Food composition databases provide thiamine content in roasted goose carcasses without skin (0.092 - 0.13 mg/100 g) and with skin (0.077 - 0.55 mg/100 g), but without specifying the type of muscle (NFSA 2018; Kunachowicz et al., 2020; USDA, 2025b).

Thiamine occurs in the cells of living organisms and in food in free form and as phosphate esters (mono-, pyro-, and triphosphate), as well as adenosine triphosphate (ATP). Thiamine monophosphate (TMP) is present in various tissues of humans, animals, plants, fungi, and prokaryotes and is considered biologically inactive. TMP is derived from the breakdown of thiamine pyrophosphate (TPP), catalyzed by phosphatases of varying substrate specificity, and constitutes 6–7 % of the total thiamine and its phosphorylated derivatives. However, TMP plays a crucial role in maintaining the correct thiamine level in cells, as it is rapidly converted by phosphatases into free thiamine, thereby determining its immediate availability (Tylicki and Siemieniuk, 2011; Mrowicka et al., 2023). In our study, we found a significantly (P ≤ 0.05) higher TMP content in meat with skin compared to meat without skin (0.067 vs. 0.052 mg/100 g). However, we did not find any significant interactions between thiamine and TPM content in goose breast meat and the heat treatment techniques used. No similar studies on TMP content in goose meat were found in the available literature; therefore, a discussion on this form of vitamin is impossible.

According to population studies conducted in Western countries, meat is the second most significant source of riboflavin in the diet, after milk and dairy products (Turck et al., 2017). According to data from the food composition databases of various countries, the riboflavin content in raw goose carcasses ranges from 0.033 to 0.27 mg/100 g, and in wild geese, it is 0.31 mg/100 g (Kunachowicz et al., 2020; SFCDB, 2024).

In our study, the riboflavin content in goose breast meat did not differ significantly between samples with and without skin. The content of this vitamin in raw goose breast meat without or with skin (respectively: 0.028 and 0.021 mg/100 g) was lower than that reported in the Food Composition Database for raw meat, without specifying muscle type (0.35 - 0.38 without skin and 0.20 - 0.35 with skin mg/100 g) (NFSA 2018,; McCance, 2021; BDA, 2025; Frida Food Data, 2025; USDA, 2025b). A higher riboflavin content (1.52 mg/100 g) was reported in raw Canada goose breast meat without skin (USDA, 2025b) than that obtained in our study.

In our experiment, heat treatment significantly (P ≤ 0.001) increased the riboflavin content compared to raw meat (R< PF, WBC, G). However, this increase in B2 content in meat may be apparent, caused by a significant (P ≤ 0.01) loss of water during heat treatment, as found in other studies (Goluch et al., 2021).

The riboflavin content after oven convection roasting (OCR) was lower (0.040 mg/100 g in meat without skin and 0.052 mg/100 g in meat with skin) than values reported in the literature. In roasted goose meat without skin, riboflavin levels ranged from 0.39 to 0.55 mg/100 g, while in meat with skin they ranged from 0.323 to 7.0 mg/100 g (NFSA 2018; McCance, 2021; USDA, 2025b). In contrast, Lombardi-Boccia et al. (2005) observed a reduction in riboflavin content in chicken and turkey breast meat (to 0.01 and 0.03 mg/100 g, respectively) after cooking in an iron-free pan over medium heat, compared with raw meat (0.03 and 0.06 mg/100 g, respectively). The effects of thermal processing on vitamin B2 levels in skinless chicken breast were also reported by Çatak (2022) and Çatak et al. (2024). These authors noted a significant (P ≤ 0.05) decrease in riboflavin content after boiling, frying, roasting, and grilling (0.109, 0.089, 0.068, and 0.050 mg/100 g, respectively), compared with raw meat (0.180 mg/100 g). Furthermore, (Alugwu et al., 2023) demonstrated that both the cooking method (air frying, AF; baking, BK; deep-fat frying, DF; grilling, GR), as well as the duration (4, 8, and 12 min) and temperature (170, 180, and 190°C), significantly (P ≤ 0.05) affected the reduction of riboflavin in skinless chicken breast. The samples processed by AF, BK, DF, and GR contained, on average, 0.071, 0.068, 0.061, and 0.067 mg/100 g of vitamin B2, respectively. Air-fried samples exhibited significantly (P<0.05) higher vitamin B2 levels than those prepared by the other methods. These values were higher than those obtained in our study.

Most literature data on niacin content in food products refer primarily to its total concentration, while only a limited number of studies characterize the specific profile of this vitamin. Niacin occurs mainly in two forms: nicotinic acid and nicotinamide, both monocarboxylic derivatives of pyridine. This distinction is essential, as it has been demonstrated that nicotinic acid—not nicotinamide—contributes to reducing blood cholesterol levels in individuals with hyperlipidemia (Kamanna and Kashyap, 2008; Kim and Park, 2022).

According to the Food Composition Database, the total niacin content in 100 g of raw goose carcass is 6.40 mg, and in wild goose it is 6.8 mg (Kunachowicz et al., 2020; SFCDB, 2024). The total niacin content in raw goose meat without skin is 4.28–5.0 mg, while with skin it is 3.60–8.0 mg/100 g (NFSA 2018; SFCDB, 2024; BDA, 2025; Frida Food Data, 2025; USDA, 2025b). However, these data do not account for the niacin profile. A study conducted by Çatak (2019) examined both the total niacin content and its specific profile in raw chicken breast meat. The total niacin content in that study was 9.181 mg/100 g, of which nicotinic acid and nicotinamide contributed 3.739 and 5.442 mg/100 g, respectively. These forms accounted for 40.5 % and 59.3 % of the total niacin content.

In our research, the proportions observed in raw goose breast meat differed markedly. The contribution of nicotinic acid and nicotinamide to total niacin amounted to 2.7 % and 97.3 %, respectively. Thus, nicotinamide represented the predominant and biologically active form of this vitamin in goose breast meat, indicating that its consumption is unlikely to exert the hypocholesterolemic effect associated with nicotinic acid (Jin et al., 1999). However, in our study, no significant effect of heat treatment on total niacin content was observed; only a trend was noted (P = 0.064).

The mean niacin content in raw and thermally processed goose meat without skin (Total for meat type, Table 1) was significantly higher (P ≤ 0.05) than in meat with skin (5.23 vs. 4.26 mg/100 g, respectively). In comparison to our results, USDA FoodData Central reports a higher niacin content (6.56 mg/100 g) in raw breast meat of Canada goose without skin (USDA, 2025b). According to Kunachowicz et al. (2020), the total niacin content in 100 g of roasted goose carcass (without specifying the type of muscle) is 7.61 mg, which is higher than our finding (4.28 mg/100 g). However, the total niacin content observed by us in roasted meat with skin and without skin (respectively: 4.44 and 4.11 mg/100 g) was comparable to that published in USDA Food Data Central (respectively: 4.08 mg and 4.17 mg/100 g) (USDA, 2025b).

The effect of heat treatment on the total niacin content in chicken and turkey meat was found by Lombardi-Boccia et al. (2005), as in raw meat, it was 8.0 and 7.2 mg/100 g, respectively, while after cooking in an iron-free pan with medium heat, it decreased to 5.0 and 5.3 mg/100 g. Similarly, Çatak (2022) and Çatak et al. (2024) found a significant (P ≤ 0.05) decrease in total niacin content after cooking, baking, frying, and grilling chicken breasts (respectively: 9.341; 8.808; 7.744 and 2.009 mg/100 g), compared to raw meat (12.110 mg/100 g). Other authors (Alugwu et al., 2023) also found that the type of processing, duration, and temperature used significantly (P ≤ 0.05) reduced the total niacin content in skinless chicken breast meat.

Considering the profile of vitamin B3, heat treatment had no significant effect on nicotinamide content. In contrast, the presence of skin did (P ≤ 0.01) (respectively: 5.18 in meat without skin vs. 4.16 mg/100 g with skin-on meat). Due to the lack of results from other studies on the niacin profile in heat-treated goose meat, we can only compare our results to those of the profile in broiler chicken breast meat. Çatak (2022) and Çatak et al. (2024) found a significant (P ≤ 0.05) reduction in nicotinamide content after boiling, baking, frying, and grilling compared to raw meat without skin (respectively: 9.233; 8,589; 7,744; 1,979 vs. 11,517 mg/100 g). In our study, a trend toward reduced nicotinamide content after WBC and OCR was observed in goose breast meat without skin (P = 0.075); with a larger sample size, this effect might reach statistical significance. The discrepancy between our findings and those reported by Çatak (2022) and Çatak et al. (2024) may result from methodological differences, including both the poultry species studied (chicken breast in their work) and the conditions of the applied heat treatments (precise control of internal temperature at 75°C vs. sensory acceptability criteria). In our experiment, WBC was conducted at a lower temperature (90°C) than in the cited studies (99–101°C) and for a shorter time (30 vs. 35 min). Frying in our study was performed without sunflower oil (160°C for 15 min vs. 190°C for 10 min). Grilling was carried out using an electric contact grill (200°C for 25 min), whereas the cited authors used a gas grill (200°C for 35 min). The OCR procedure also differed (200°C for 25 min vs. 190°C for 45 min). These differences—including the use of goose breast meat rather than chicken, as well as the generally lower or comparable temperatures and shorter processing times used in our study—may have contributed to the milder reduction in nicotinamide observed.

In our study, both the type of meat and the applied heat-treatment method had a significant effect (P ≤ 0.001) on the nicotinic acid content. Regardless of the cooking method used, goose breast meat without skin contained significantly lower levels of nicotinic acid than meat with skin (0.058 vs. 0.105 mg/100 g, respectively). The impact of thermal treatments was reflected in a marked reduction of nicotinic acid content in WBC meat compared with both raw and PF samples (WBC < R, PF). Similarly, Çatak (2022) and Çatak et al. (2024) reported a significant (P ≤ 0.05) decrease in nicotinic acid levels in skinless chicken breast meat after baking, frying, boiling, and grilling relative to raw meat (0.219; 0.155; 0.108; and 0.030 vs. 0.593 mg/100 g, respectively).

Our findings showed no significant effect of either skin presence or heat treatment on the pantothenic acid content in goose breast meat. It confirms the opinion that this acid is the most stable vitamin during heat treatment at pH levels of–7 (Lešková et al., 2006). The vitamin B5 levels we measured in both raw and heat-treated goose breast meat were lower than those reported in other published sources. According to the food composition databases, the pantothenic acid content in raw goose meat without skin is 1.97 mg/100 g, and with skin, 1.29 mg/100 g (Frida Food Data, 2025; USDA, 2025b). In 100 g of roasted goose meat, the vitamin B5 content was 0.81 - 1.29 mg (Łoś-Kuczera and Piekarska, 1988; SFCDB, 2024), while in roasted meat without skin or with skin: 1.40 - 1.83 and 0.60 - 1.53 mg, respectively (NFSA 2018; McCance, 2021; USDA, 2025b).

Vitamin B6 is present in foods as free (pyridoxal PL, pyridoxine PN, pyridoxamine PM), phosphate (PLP and PMP), or glycoside (pyridoxine-glucoside PNG) forms, and these different forms are sometimes called vitamers in the literature (Dias et al., 2025). In the liver, after intestinal absorption, all forms are converted into the pyridoxal 5′-phosphate (PLP) form by pyridoxine 5′-phosphate oxidase and then metabolized to 4-pyridoxal acid, which is subsequently excreted. In foods of animal origin, the dominant and active form of vitamin B6 is PL-phosphate (PLP), which participates in the metabolism of amino acids and neurotransmitters and in the synthesis of nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) from tryptophan. The vitamin B6 content in food, determined by HPLC (high-performance liquid chromatography), is mainly reported as PN (pyridoxine), because during derivatization, the PL (pyridoxal) and PM (pyridoxamine) forms are converted to the PN form (Çatak and Çaman, 2020). The PL and PM forms are thermally stable in an acidic environment, but are not stable in an alkaline environment and are sensitive to light. The PN, PL, and PM forms are structurally similar. Still, PN is the easiest form to detect in food because it is chemically stable, does not convert to other forms, and undergoes little change during storage and processing. For this reason, PN is the primary determinant of vitamin B6 content in food, and the other forms (PL and PM) have been excluded from standard analysis (Je et al., 2025). Our study provides an essential contribution to the field by quantifying all three vitamers of vitamin B6 (PL, PN, and PM) in goose breast meat. This comprehensive approach overcomes the limitations of many food composition databases, which report only pyridoxine (PN). It therefore allows for a more accurate assessment of the actual vitamin B6 content and its dietary intake.

In the present experiment, both the type of meat (with or without skin) and the applied culinary treatments, as well as their interactions, significantly influenced the total vitamin B6 content in goose breast meat. Meat without skin contained considerably higher levels of vitamin B6 than meat with skin (0.20 vs. 0.16 mg/100 g; P ≤ 0.01). Considering the effect of thermal processing, raw goose breast meat exhibited the highest total vitamin B6 content (R > PF, OCR, WBC; P ≤ 0.01). In addition, grilled meat retained significantly more vitamin B6 than samples subjected to other treatments (G > PF, OCR, WBC).

According to literature data that do not take into account the presence of skin, the total vitamin B6 content in 100 g of raw goose carcass is 0.58 mg (Łoś-Kuczera and Piekarska, 1988; Kunachowicz et al., 2020; SFCDB, 2024). According to other composition tables, the vitamin B6 content in raw goose meat, both with and without skin, is 0.64 vs. 0.35 - 0.58 mg/100 g, respectively (NFSA 2018; McCance, 2021; SFCDB, 2024; BDA, 2025; Frida Food Data, 2025; USDA, 2025b).

Compared with our results for raw goose breast meat without skin (0.31 mg/100 g), FoodData Central reports a markedly higher vitamin B6 content in raw breast meat of Canada goose (1.08 mg/100 g) (USDA, 2025b). The vitamin B6 levels determined in our study after oven convection roasting (OCR) in goose breast meat without and with skin (0.16 and 0.14 mg/100 g, respectively) were also lower than those reported in the literature for roasted whole goose carcasses (0.56–0.86 mg/100 g) as well as for roasted goose meat (0.47–0.62 mg/100 g without skin and 0.37–0.42 mg/100 g with skin) (NFSA 2018,; Łoś-Kuczera and Piekarska, 1988; McCance, 2021; USDA, 2025b).

In our study, raw and G goose breast meat without skin contained significantly (P ≤ 0.05) more total vitamin B6 compared to other heat treatments (R, G > PF, OCR, WBC). However, raw meat with skin contained significantly (P ≤ 0.05) more of this vitamin only when compared to OCR and WBC (R> OCR, WBC). Alugwu et al. (2023) found that different heat treatments significantly (P ≤ 0.05) reduced the total vitamin B6 content in skinless chicken breast meat.

Unfortunately, no studies were identified in the available literature that examine the vitamin B6 profile in goose breast meat, which underscores the novelty of our research. Existing investigations have focused exclusively on chicken breast meat. Çatak and Çaman (2020) reported, consistent with our findings, a significantly (P ≤ 0.05) higher total vitamin B6 content in raw meat compared with heat-treated samples, following the pattern: raw > frying, boiling > grilling > baking (0.274 > 0.122; 0.115 > 0.072 > 0.050 mg/100 g, respectively).

The analyses revealed a significantly (P ≤ 0.01) higher pyridoxal (PL) concentration in goose breast meat with skin than in skinless samples (Table 1; total for meat type: 0.041 vs. 0.020 mg/100 g). Moreover, raw meat contained significantly (P ≤ 0.01) more PL than samples subjected to any form of heat treatment (R > PF, ORC, WBC, G). This pattern was evident in meat with skin, whereas in skinless breast meat, the PL level remained unaffected by thermal processing. Comparable trends were reported by Çatak and Çaman (2020), who observed a significantly (P ≤ 0.05) higher PL content in raw chicken breast meat relative to fried, boiled, grilled, or baked samples (0.237 > 0.119, 0.107 > 0.067 > 0.039 mg/100 g, respectively). A reduction in pyridoxal in beef subjected to stewing and baking, described by Bognar (1993), was attributed to extensive degradation of PL during heating, accompanied by its partial conversion to pyridoxamine (PM).

In our studies, we found a significantly (P ≤ 0.01) higher pyridoxamine (PM) content in goose breast meat without skin than with skin (0.175 vs. 0.113 mg/100 g, respectively). In addition, Raw and Grilled meat had a significantly higher PM content compared to meat after other heat treatments (R, G> PF, WBC, ORC). Such a significant (P ≤ 0.01) effect was observed in goose breast meat without skin. However, no differences in PM content were found in goose breast meat with skin. On the other hand, Çatak and Çaman (2020) found a significantly (P ≤ 0.05) higher PM content in raw chicken breast meat compared to heat-treated meat: Raw > baking > boiling > grilling, frying (respectively: 0.037 > 0.011 > 0.08 > 0 mg/100 g).

Neither the type of meat (without skin or with skin) nor the type of heat treatment techniques used significantly affected the pyridoxal content in goose breast meat. It may be because among the vitamers, pyridoxine is far more stable than pyridoxal and pyridoxamine (Dias et al., 2025). It was observed by Bognar (1993), who noted that almost 100 % of PN was retained in beef that had been roasted and stewed. In comparison to our results, Çatak and Çaman (2020) found a significantly (P ≤ 0.05) higher PN content in raw chicken breast meat compared to heat-treated meat: Raw > grilling > frying > baking > boiling (respectively: 1.0 > 0 mg/100 g).

The content of individual forms of vitamin B6 in the goose meat we examined was 0.079 mg/100 g (PM), 0.184 mg/100 g (PL), and 0.0029 mg/100 g (PN), which together constitute the total vitamin B6 content (0.27 mg/100 g). Based on the above values, individual vitamers account for 68 %, 29.3 %, and 1.2 % of the total B6 content. The profile of goose meat determined in this way is different from that of beef (51 % PM; 45 % PL; 5 % PN) and pork (24 % PM; 66 % PL; 10 % PN) (Bognar, 1993).

The vitamin B12 content in raw goose meat (Table 1) averaged 0.63 μg/100 g, including 0.84 μg/100 g with skin and 0.42 μg/100 g without skin. According to various databases, raw goose carcass contains approximately 0.3 µg/100 g (NFSA 2018; Kunachowicz et al., 2020; SFCDB, 2024), skinless goose meat 0.5 µg/100 g, and with skin 0.34-2.0 µg/100 g (NFSA 2018; McCance, 2021; BDA, 2025; Frida Food Data, 2025). The result obtained for raw breast without skin is comparable to the databases above, but significantly lower than the values provided in FoodData Central for Canadian goose (0.42 vs. 4.1 µg/100 g, respectively) (USDA, 2025b).

In our experiment, we found a significant (P ≤ 0.01) effect of both meat type (with or without skin) and heat treatment, as well as the interaction between them, on the content of this vitamin in goose breast meat (Table 1). The presence of skin significantly increased the vitamin B12 content (0.49 vs. 0.28 µg/100 g, respectively). It may be because the skin contains numerous blood vessels that transport plasma components, including vitamin B12.

We also found that the average content of this vitamin in goose meat significantly (P ≤ 0.01) decreased after heat treatment (R > OCR > PF > WBC, G). Different trends have been reported in the Norwegian Food Data Base (NFSA, 2018), raw goose meat without skin contains less vitamin B12 than roasted samples (0.5 vs. 3.3 µg/100 g). In contrast, the FoodData Central database (USDA, 2025b) lists identical values for both meats (0.49 µg/100 g). According to Kunachowicz et al. (2020), the difference in B12 content between raw and roasted goose carcasses is negligible too (0.30 vs. 0.32 µg/100 g). In the case of meat with skin, data from the McCance (2021) database also indicate that vitamin B12 levels remain unchanged after roasting (2 µg/100 g in both raw and roasted meat). In contrast, USDA (2025b) data suggest an increase following roasting (0.34 vs. 0.41 µg/100 g).

It is worth noting that the vitamin B12 content in older scientific studies was determined using microbiological methods. However, more recent data, such as that presented by us, were determined using a chromatographic method.

Vitamins B retention in goose meat after thermal processing

The retention values of individual vitamins are shown in Table 3. We found no significant differences in the retention of thiamine or thiamine monophosphate in goose breast meat, regardless of whether the skin was present or the type of heat treatment applied. On average, thiamine retention was 33.4 %.

Findings reported by other authors indicate that thiamine is highly susceptible to thermal degradation. Lombardi-Boccia et al. (2005) demonstrated that in chicken and turkey breast muscles cooked in an iron-free pan over medium heat, thiamine was degraded entirely and therefore undetectable in the analyzed samples. Additional studies on skinless chicken breast meat (Çatak, 2022; Çatak et al., 2024) showed different retention values depending on the treatment: after baking, boiling, grilling, and frying (30.5 %, 41.0 %, 51.7 %, and 74.2 %, respectively).

Alugwu et al. (2023) further demonstrated that cooking method, duration, and temperature significantly affected thiamine degradation in skinless chicken breast meat (P<0.05). The smallest losses occurred during grilling at 170°C for 8 min (retention 79.6 %; 0.039 mg/100 g), whereas the greatest reduction was noted after deep-fat frying at 190°C for 16 min (0.006 mg/100 g). The mean thiamine retention after 8 and 16 min of heat treatment was 44.9 % and 24.5 %, respectively. In our study, the average retention of thiamine monophosphate was 123.3 %; however, no comparable data were found in the available literature.

Riboflavin retention also did not differ significantly across the experimental factors used, averaging 133.3 % (Table 3). According to the literature (Lombardi-Boccia et al. 2005; Tang et al., 2006; Sheraz et al., 2014; Hrubša et al., 2022; Demir et al., 2025; Beltramo et al., 2025) increased retention and apparent increase in riboflavin content in meat after heat treatment may result from the synergistic effect of physicochemical and analytical factors. The primary mechanism is the riboflavin concentration effect, driven by significant cooking losses (thermal runoff, water evaporation, and fat rendering), which, given the high thermal stability of the isoalloxazine ring of this vitamin, results in a higher concentration of riboflavin per unit of product mass after heat treatment. Furthermore, the increased riboflavin content and its high retention in heat-treated goose breast meat also result from thermal denaturation of muscle proteins, which releases it from flavoprotein complexes (FMN and FAD), increasing its analytical availability during HPLC extraction. Furthermore, riboflavin is thermostable and naturally resistant to temperatures of 100–120°C. It is protected from photochemical degradation within the goose meat structure, particularly during methods that limit the vitamin's diffusion into the aqueous environment, such as roasting, frying, or grilling. In studies conducted on chicken and turkey breast muscles cooked in an iron-free pan at medium heat, Lombardi-Boccia et al. (2005) found that riboflavin was heat-stable, with retention rates of 66.7 % and 50 %, respectively. However, in skinless chicken breast meat, Çatak (2022) and Çatak et al. (2024) reported lower retention of riboflavin after baking, grilling, frying, and cooking (27.8 %, 37.2 %, 49.4 %, and 60.6 %, respectively). Alugwu et al. (2023) also found that the type of processing, its duration, and temperature significantly (P ≤ 0.05) influenced the reduction of riboflavin content in skinless chicken breast meat, with average retention after 8 and 16 min of heat treatment of 62.88 % and 42.3 %, respectively.

Considering the retention of total niacin (mean 48.1 %), significantly higher values (P ≤ 0.001) were observed in meat without skin than in meat with skin (75.4 % vs. 55.7 %). Still, there were no significant differences among the heat‐treatment techniques applied. In the study by Lombardi-Boccia et al. (2005), niacin retention in chicken and turkey breast muscles cooked in an iron-free pan at medium heat was 62.5 % and 92.6 %, respectively. Conversely, Çatak (2022) and Çatak et al. (2024) reported lower retention of total niacin after grilling, frying, baking, and cooking (16.6 %, 65.2 %, 72.7 %, and 77.1 %, respectively). Alugwu et al. (2023) also documented considerable reductions in niacin content in skinless chicken breast meat, with average retention values of 62.16 % and 48.65 % after 8 and 16 min of heat treatment.

In our study, nicotinic acid retention did not differ significantly across the various experimental factors applied, averaging 48.1 %. However, other authors (Çatak, 2022; Çatak et al., 2024) reported lower retention of nicotinic acid after boiling, frying, baking, grilling, and of skinless chicken meat (18.3 %, 26.1 %, 36.9 %, and 51.0 % and respectively).

A significantly higher nicotinamide retention (P ≤ 0.01) was observed in goose breast meat without skin than with skin (77.2 vs. 56.2 %), with an average retention of 66.7 %. By comparison, studies on skinless chicken breast meat reported markedly lower retention of nicotinamide following grilling, frying, baking, boiling with retention values of 17.2 %, 67.2 %, 74.6 %, and 80.2 %, respectively (Çatak, 2022; Çatak et al., 2024).

The relatively high retention of niacin, in both its nicotinic acid and nicotinamide forms, is consistent with the well-established stability of this vitamin. Niacin is considered the most heat-resistant water-soluble vitamin, generally exhibiting strong resistance to atmospheric oxygen, acidic conditions, heat, and light in both liquid and solid food matrices (Lešková et al., 2006).

In our study, pantothenic acid (a form of vitamin B5) retention averaged 53.2 %, and none of the experimental factors had a significant effect on this value. Similar results were reported by Łoś-Kuczera and Piekarska (1988), who found that losses of this vitamin in meat during stewing and cooking average about 40 % (ranging from 30 % to 50 %). According to Hrubša et al. (2022), the content of this vitamin in meat (pork, beef, and poultry) decreases by 15–50 % as a result of heat treatment (steaming, braising, boiling), mainly due to leaching into the cooking medium.

As reported by Łoś-Kuczera and Piekarska (1988), retention of vitamin B6 in meat during stewing and boiling average approximately 50 % (ranging from 40 % to 70 %), while during roasting and frying they amount to about 80 % (within a range of 0–60 %). In our study, significantly higher (P ≤ 0.01) retention of this vitamin was observed in goose breast meat without skin compared with meat containing skin (44.3 vs. 32.6 %). Moreover, total vitamin B6 retention was significantly greater (P ≤ 0.05) after grilling than under water-bath cooking (G > WBC). In addition, in skinless goose breast meat, grilling resulted in higher retention of this vitamin compared with oven convection roasting (G > OCR). Çatak and Çaman (2020) also demonstrated that heat treatment affects total vitamin B6 retention in chicken breast meat, with the following decreasing order relative to raw meat: frying (44.19 %), boiling (41.66 %), grilling (26.03 %), and baking (18.16 %).

No significant effect of the experimental factors on pyridoxal retention was observed, with an average value of 31.1 %. Çatak and Çaman (2020) reported PL retention in chicken breast meat of 50.0 % after frying, 44.9 % after boiling, 28.1 % after grilling, and 18.1 % after baking.

Significantly (P ≤ 0.01) higher PM retention was observed in goose breast meat without skin compared with meat with skin (66.9 vs. 40.9 %). However, the applied heat-processing methods did not significantly affect the retention of this vitamin form. In contrast, Çatak and Çaman (2020) reported PM retention in skinless chicken breast meat in the following order: baking (29.7 %), boiling (21.7 %), grilling (13.5 %) and frying (8.1 %).

The retention of pyridoxine (B6) and vitamin B12 did not differ significantly depending on the experimental factors.

In summary, when the mean retention values were considered, the following descending order was observed: riboflavin (133.3 %), thiamine monophosphate (123.3 %), pyridoxine (82.2 %), nicotinamide (67.7 %), total niacin (65.6 %), pyridoxamine (53.9 %), pantothenic acid (53.2 %), nicotinic acid (48.1 %), total vitamin B6 (38.5 %), vitamin B12 (36.5 %), thiamine (33.4 %), and pyridoxal (31.3 %). The pyridoxal showed the lowest retention, which is consistent with the findings of Bognar (1993). Likewise, in line with Bognar (1993), our results demonstrate that pyridoxal (PL) and pyridoxamine (PM) are more prone to degradation during thermal processing than pyridoxine (PN). Consequently, it may be concluded that the higher the PL content in the raw, unprocessed material, the greater the losses in total vitamin B6 (PL + PN + PM) induced by heat treatment.

As the available literature provides only limited data on the effects of heat treatment on B-vitamin content in goose breast muscle (with or without skin), a more extensive comparison with other studies is not feasible.

Coverage of the nutrient reference values-requirements by goose meat in adults

Nutrient Reference Values (NRV-Rs) are science-based guidelines that indicate the daily amounts of energy and nutrients needed to maintain health. The percentage of NRV-R coverage is shown on food packaging as part of the nutrition information (per 100 g or 100 ml, based on a 2000 kcal diet). NRVs help consumers make informed dietary choices and are also used by food manufacturers, dietitians, and health professionals for product formulation, labeling, dietary assessment, and planning. The Codex Committee on Nutrition and Foods for Special Dietary Uses (Lewis, 2019) specifies the Nutrient Reference Values – Requirements (NRV-R) for thiamine, riboflavin, niacin, pantothenic acid, and vitamin B6 as 1.2, 1.2, 15.0, 5.0, and 1.3 mg per day, respectively, and for vitamin B12 as 2.4 µg per day (Table 4).

A 100-g portion of raw goose breast meat (on average and with skin) provides the highest proportion of the NRV-R for thiamine compared with all heat-treated samples (R>WBC, PF, OCR, G) (Table 4). However, the consumption of raw goose meat (e.g., tartare or carpaccio) is uncommon due to potential microbiological risks (Gulmez et al., 2014; Mukhametov et al., 2023). For riboflavin, heat-treated goose breast meat with skin (WBC, PF, G, OCR) and without skin (PF, G) provided a higher percentage of the NRV-R than raw meat. Overall, meat without skin supplies a higher percentage of the riboflavin NRV-R than meat with skin (34.9 vs. 28.4 %). For niacin, thermal processing did not significantly affect NRV-R coverage. Similarly, no differences were observed for vitamin B5, either with respect to skin presence or the applied heat treatment. A 100-g portion of goose breast without skin covers a significantly higher proportion of the NRV-R for vitamin B6 than meat with skin (15.3 vs. 12.1 %), with raw and grilled samples contributing the most (R, G > PF, OCR, WBC). In contrast, goose breast with skin provides a significantly greater share of the NRV-R for vitamin B12 than meat without skin (20.4 vs. 11.8 %). Regardless of skin presence, the highest NRV-R contribution for vitamin B12 was observed in raw and oven-cooked roasted samples.

Under EU regulations, for a food to be labelled as a “source” of a nutrient, it must provide at least 15 % of the adult Nutrient Reference Value (NRV) per 100 g. The NRVs for thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, pantothenic acid, and vitamin B6 are 1.1, 1.4, 16.0, 5.0, and 1.3 mg/day, respectively, and for vitamin B12, 2.5 µg/day, according to the Annex to Regulation (EU) No 1169/2011 (European Parliament, 2011). Thus, a food must contain at least 0.165, 0.21, 2.4, 0.75, and 0.195 mg/100 g, and 0.375 µg/100 g of these vitamins, respectively, to qualify as a “source”. To be described as a “high source”, the food must provide at least 30 % of the NRV per 100 g, i.e., 0.33, 0.42, 4.8, 1.5, and 0.39 mg/100 g, and 0.75 µg/100 g, respectively. Therefore, in the adult diet, a 100 g portion of goose breast meat without skin may be classified as a “high source” (>30 % NRV) of niacin, and as a “source” (>15 % NRV) of vitamin B6 (without skin) and vitamin B12 (with skin). However, goose meat, whether raw or heat-treated, does not provide sufficient amounts to be considered a source (<15 % NRV) of vitamins B1, B2, or B5 in the adult diet.

It can also not be ruled out that, to preserve individual vitamins B in goose meat better, it would be advisable to apply other conventional heat-processing methods (e.g., shorter treatment times or different temperature ranges such as sous-vide), or modern technologies (ohmic heating, microwave, radio-frequency, or infrared heating) (Hassoun et al., 2021).

A limitation of our study is that it involved meat from a single goose breed (White Koluda®), slaughtered at a specific age (17 weeks) and obtained from only one anatomical part of the carcass (breast muscles), which limits the generalization of the results to other breeds or genetic lines, different slaughter ages, and other carcass parts. Another limitation concerns the number of samples per heat-treatment group, the number of measurements, and the natural variability of the biological material (i.e., meat). Because B vitamins are water-soluble, the naturally higher lipid fraction in samples with skin may have influenced extraction efficiency or the relative concentration of vitamins compared with lean tissue. In addition, the analysis of B vitamins content was performed in raw samples and after four heat-treatment methods commonly used by consumers (water bath cooking, grilling, oven convection roasting, and pan frying); therefore, the findings cannot be directly extrapolated to other processing methods such as steaming, stewing, sous vide, or microwave heating. In our opinion, to increase the reliability and scope of the conclusions, future studies should include a larger number of samples from geese of different genotypes and feeding conditions, various production systems, and other anatomical parts of the carcass (e.g., leg muscles), as well as a broader range of culinary processes.

Conclusion

Our research shows that both the presence of skin and the heat treatment techniques used have a significant impact on the profile and content of B vitamins in goose breast meat. Skinless meat contained significantly more niacin, nicotinamide, and vitamin B6, and less thiamine monophosphate, nicotinic acid, pyridoxal, and vitamin B12. The heat treatment techniques used considerably affected the content of thiamine, riboflavin, nicotinic acid, pyridoxal, pyridoxamine, total vitamin B6, and vitamin B12. However, significant differences in retention were observed only in niacin, nicotinamide, vitamin B6, and pyridoxine.

A 100-g portion of heat-treated goose breast meat, with or without skin, contributes most to the NRV-R for thiamin, vitamin B6, and vitamin B12, and least for riboflavin. The same serving of goose breast meat without skin qualifies as a “high source” of niacin (>30 % NRV-R) and as a “source” of vitamin B6, while meat with skin is a “source” of vitamin B12 (>15 % NRV-R).

It is not possible to indicate a single, optimal heat-treatment method, since different thermal processes are associated with varying degrees of vitamin loss. Given that the consumption of raw meat is unsafe, the choice of an appropriate technique for goose meat should be guided by any potential deficiencies in individual B vitamins. However, the actual bioavailability of these vitamins from this food matrix will also be important, as it depends on both the digestibility of the meat and the individual's physiological condition.

Information on the content and profile of B vitamins in goose meat may be helpful in the development of nutritional composition tables for this type of meat. In addition, the data obtained in our study can be implemented into dietetic software and used for assessing and planning nutrition in various population groups, in diet therapy, and in nutrition education. Providing information on vitamin content on product labels (including catering meals) not only increases the market competitiveness of manufacturers but also enables consumers to make informed food choices. Knowledge of the composition of the food consumed is essential for assessing the quality of a diet and developing and implementing dietary guidelines.
