Preliminary insights into gut microbiome shifts as screening proxy for MASLD disease progression
Authors: M Llirós Dupré, M Buxó, S Virolés, M Pujolassos, I Serra, J Martínez, A Lluansí, A Bahí, M Calle, X Aldeguer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD), a metabolic syndrome with chronic excessive non-alcohol related triglyceride accumulation in liver cells, is characterised by a gradient of hepatic inflammation and fibrosis which lead to hepatocellular carcinoma. Clinical diagnosis is commonly based on non-invasive imaging methods, but definitive and conclusive diagnostic is achieved throughout invasive liver biopsy. Recent research pointed to an association between unbalanced gut microbiome and MASLD pathogenesis. In this prospective pilot study we dissect the gradual disease phenotypes as per common clinical practices and gut microbiome profiling based on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing of stool samples from a set of 8 healthy and 46 MASLD-diagnosed individuals. Results evidenced gut microbiome shifts (both a reduction of microbial diversity and richness) as liver damage severity increases with respect to control subjects. Additionally, microbiome compositional data balancing revealed a slight discriminatory capacity between controls and patients’ groups or between patients groups, but with low power due to the reduced sample size. All in all, non-invasive proxies based on gut microbiome analyses might be useful as complementary tools for MASLD patients stratification and discrimination.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-42368-4.Keywords: MASLD, Microbiome, Diversity, Dysbiosis, Microbial balances, Compositional data.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Diseases, Gastroenterology, Microbiology

Introduction

Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD), previously known as Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD), is a liver manifestation of a metabolic syndrome characterised by chronic excessive triglyceride accumulation within the cytosol of liver cells in absence of excessive alcohol consumption (threshold 20–30 g/day;1,2. MASLD comprise a range of liver disorders encompassing from isolated excessive hepatocyte triglyceride accumulation and steatosis (steatotic liver disease (SLD)) to hepatic triglyceride accumulation together with inflammation and hepatocyte injury, different grades of inflammation and fibrosis (metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH)), and, finally, resulting in cirrhosis and even into hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC)1–8. Reported data evidenced that nearly one third of patients will develop hepatic cirrhosis after MASLD diagnosis9–11, however overall mortality on MASLD is related to the fibrosis stage and not to the degree of steatosis12.

Epidemiological data pointed towards MASLD and gastrointestinal malignancies such HCC as the most common cause of liver dysfunction worldwide in the near future (global prevalence of ca. 25% based on imaging data7,13. In this sense, MASLD has been recently considered the most common liver disease worldwide5,7,14–17 and the second leading aetiology for liver transplantation. MASLD is tightly linked to type 2 diabetes (T2D), obesity, and other cardiometabolic risk factors2. However, MASLD can also be observed in lean patients with diverse triggers (e.g., high-fructose/high-fat intake, congenital lipodystrophy, genetic causes, or endocrine disorders (e.g., normal-weight phenotype of metabolically obese people);7,18,19. Though, MASLD pathology is poorly understood, with assumptions of complex interactions between genetic susceptibility variants, environmental factors, insulin resistance, gut microbiome shifts, comorbidities and genetic risk factors16,20,21, resulting in altered lipid metabolism and excessive lipid accumulation in hepatocytes16,22. Recent reviews, still using NAFLD terminology, have highlighted the link between MASLD and gut microbiota22,23. In the clinical practice, current diagnostic methodologies for liver fibrosis consist of non-invasive serological tests and imaging, however the more invasive hepatic biopsy probe continues to be the gold standard despite its obvious disadvantages24–27. New strategies are being studied to improve steatohepatitis and fibrosis diagnosis through non-invasive methods28. However, MASLD and MASH still remains as diseases without effective therapies16 and neither are envisaged to be approved by corresponding regulatory agencies29.

The human microbiome is rich and diverse30–32 and host-specific33. In this sense, microbes provide protection to the host from pathogens through competition for ecological niches and fostering a stable environment for the development of host immunity34–36. Moreover, microbes benefit the human host by helping in digestion, vitamin synthesis, boosting the immune system, metabolism regulation, or prevention of specific pathogen invasion37–40. However, loss of microbiome equilibrium (i.e., dysbiosis;41 or presence of microbial secondary metabolites have also been determined in disease-related situations (e.g., inflammatory bowel diseases, neoplasia)42–51. In this sense, an equilibrium must be achieved and maintained to support the interactions of the human host and its microbiome under a healthy point of view. The adult healthy gut microbiome is commonly represented by Firmicutes, Bacteroides, Proteobacteria, Actinobacteria, Fusobacteria and Verrucomicrobia bacterial groups30–32,52. However and due to their dynamic behaviour, their composition and distribution will vary under different nutritional, immune and environmental conditions5,36,53. The human gut microbiome can also exhibit influence in liver diseases throughout the portal vein and the biliary tree36. Besides, multiple evidences show that MASLD pathogenesis is closely associated with an intestinal microbiome imbalance17,36,54–56 together with the influence of host ingested nutrients57, as is the case of other human diseases58,59. In fact, progressive gut microbiome shifts might be correlated with the different disease stages as recently evidenced by shifts in Proteobacteria (increase) and Firmicutes (decrease)56 but also with microbe-derived metabolites36.

The aim of the present prospective pilot study was to investigate shifts in gut microbial diversity in patients covering a set of liver stages with respect to triglyceride accumulation and fibrosis (i.e., from healthy liver to hepatocarcinoma) and correlate them with clinical data by combining either common clinical resources (i.e., fibroscan, nafld, and FIB-4 scores) and molecular tools (i.e., quantitative PCR and high-throughput sequencing).

Materials and methods

Patient recruitment and characteristics

The study population included 46 patients and 8 healthy individuals, all recruited from monographically hepatic steatosis clinical consultations at Hospital Universitari de Girona Dr Josep Trueta (Catalonia, Spain). Subjects under this pilot study include both genders in all groups. All subjects were followed up according to clinical criteria and all analysed parameters were in agreement with clinical routine and practice at the moment of enrolment. During a clinical visit, subjects provided a stool sample (frozen or immediately frozen), underwent blood sampling collection and clinical follow-up as per clinical routine. Clinically relevant characteristics of individuals grouped by disease severity are summarized in Suppl. Table 1. Patients were diagnosed according to standard clinical (serology), pathological (biopsy), and image criteria (abdominal ultrasound and/or nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR)) and categorised according to clinical routine and guidelines (e.g.4,60., ). It is worth mentioning that this study was performed prior to the publication of recommended guidelines to detect, manage and follow-up MASLD patients in our region61 or the EASL 2024 guidelines2. Hepatocarcinoma diagnosis was established according to clinical guidelines62, see belowe. Nonetheless, all patients fulfil the diagnostic criteria of the new MASLD nomenclature; therefore, this terminology will be used.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Briefly, we enrolled eligible individuals from December 2015 to December 2018 prospectively. The inclusion criteria were as follows: written consent provided, at least 18 years old, recruitment and inclusion after hepatic steatosis diagnosis using image techniques (abdominal ultrasound and/or NMR) and/or liver biopsy according to clinical practice. In contrast, patients that met any of the following criteria were excluded from the study: alcohol consumption (> 14 UBE/week in females and > 21 UBE/week in males), other ethology chronic liver disease (e.g., viral infection, hereditary hemochromatosis, Wilson disease, autoimmune disease or toxic consumption), systemic inflammatory diseases (e.g., arthropathies, connective tissue disease, inflammatory bowel disease), previous procedures of bariatric surgery, short bowel syndrome or digestive neoplasm over the last 3 years, pregnancy and/or current breast feeding, consumption over the last 3 months prior to study inclusion of: antibiotics, prebiotics, probiotics, cholestyramine or lactulose, corticosteroids, antiretroviral therapy, amiodarone, oestrogen or tamoxifen. Furthermore, subjects were excluded from the study if dose modification or starting clinical therapy with: metformin, thiazolidinediones, biguanides, statins, fibrates, insulin or anti-obesity therapy was conducted.

Clinical diagnostic of MASLD, fibrosis and cirrhosis

Clinical diagnosis of MASLD was based on altered hepatic blood profile (i.e., AST (aspartate aminotransferase or glutamic-oxalacetic transaminase), ALT (alanine aminotransferase or glutamate-pyruvate transaminase), and GGT (gamma-glutamyl transpeptidase) blood values) over the previous 6 months together with an image probe in order to confirm the presence of fat accumulation in the liver, diagnosis of portal hypertension and discard hepatic nodules or portal thrombosis; and at least one metabolic risk factor. Other causes for hepatic steatosis were discarded as per clinical practice, such as serological markers, or hepatic biopsy.

Hepatic fibrosis was determined using non-invasive tests named NAFLD fibrosis score, FIB-4 score or Transient Elastography (TE). The NAFLD fibrosis score is a clinical algorithm24 based on age, body mass index (BMI), impaired fasting glucose (IFG), diabetes, AST, ALT, platelet counts and albumin content data which allowed us to determine the presence (NAFLD score > 0.675 units) or absence (NAFLD score < -1.455 units) of fibrosis in analysed patients. The FIB-4 score (i.e., the combination of age, platelets, ALT and AST values) was also used to study, evaluate and classify studied patients63. FIB4 score values higher than 2.67 units allowed us to determine the presence of fibrosis, whereas values lower than 1.3 units ruled out fibrosis. Intermediate values for NAFLD scores (i.e., -1.455–0.675 units) and FIB4 scores (i.e., 1.3–2.67 units) did not allow for a proper stratification and image data or biopsy was required. TE was performed in all patients using a ECHOSENS Fibroscan 402 apparatus coupled to an M probe in order to measure liver stiffness. TE allowed us to stratify patients in absence of fibrosis (< 8 KPa) or presence of advanced fibrosis (> 18 KPa); whereas intermediate values (i.e., from 8 to 18 KPa) reflected either high liver inflammation or fibrosis and were further subjected to biopsy4,60. Liver biopsy was only performed in critical situations and as per clinical criteria and practice (see Suppl. Table 1).

According to the above mentioned criteria, subjects were grouped following clinical considerations as: patients with simple hepatic steatosis without fibrosis and inflammation (both Fibroscan® values lower than 8 KPa and normal blood hepatic profile; group sHS); patients with steatohepatitis (hepatic steatosis by image diagnosis without or with minimal fibrosis and hepatic inflammation (both Fibroscan® values lower than 8 KPa (i.e., F0, F1, F2) and altered blood hepatic-related parameters; group nfHS); patients with steatohepatitis (hepatic steatosis by image diagnosis) with hepatic fibrosis (Fibroscan® values higher than 18 KPa (i.e., F3, F4) and consideration of altered blood hepatic-related parameters or portal hypertension ultrasound signs; group fHS); and, finally, patients with hepatocarcinoma where cirrhosis by metabolic disease was observed (group HCC) (Suppl. Table 1). A fifth group of individuals was considered which included healthy non-MASLD patients (see below).

Non-MASLD control subjects

The present study included 8 non-MASLD control subjects (i.e., relatives of enrolled patients and anonymous volunteers). Non-MASLD control subjects were defined following clinical criteria according to Fibroscan® values (< 8.0 KPa), NALFD score algorithm (< -1.455), FIB-4 score (< 1.00), and blood hepatic profile (i.e., AST, ALT, and GGT), all indicating lack of fibrosis and hepatic inflammation. According to clinical practice, no liver biopsies were performed in this group.

Liver histology

Hepatic biopsies where only conducted according to clinical criteria. Liver histology was evaluated according to the NASH CRN histological scoring system. MASLD was defined as the presence of ≥ 5% macrovesicular steatosis based on histological examination. MASH was determined based on an overall pattern of histological hepatic injury consisting of steatosis, lobular inflammation, and ballooning64,65. Steatosis, lobular inflammation, ballooning and fibrosis severity were evaluated66. All hepatic biopsy results were reported as: F0, no fibrosis observed; F1, portal fibrosis; F2, periportal fibrosis without bridging; F3, bridging fibrosis; and F4, cirrhosis67,68.

Genomic DNA extraction and bacterial 16 S rRNA gene quantification

Total DNA was extracted from 150 to 200 mg of faecal samples using the Nucleospin® Soil kit (Machery-Nagel GmbH; Germany) as per manufacturer instructions. The quality and quantity of obtained nucleic acids were evaluated using a Qubit® BR kit on a Qubit®2.0 fluorimeter (ThermoFisher Scientific®) and on a Nanodrop ND-2000 UV-Vis spectrophotometer (Nanodrop, DE).

Genomic nucleic acid extracts from faecal samples were used to quantify the microbial load. Quantitative PCR using TaqMan chemistry was performed in order to determine abundance of total eubacteria, Escherichia coli, and Faecalibacterium prausnitzii bacterial species using primers and probes listed in Table 1 as previously reported69,70. All primer combinations targeted different regions of the 16 S rRNA gene sequence. Furthermore, no 16 S rRNA gene copy number normalization was carried out as total eubacterial counts might include more than one bacterial species. Besides, microbial quantifications reported here cannot be summed up due to above mentioned methodological issues.

All PCR conditions are described in Table 1. Briefly, all reactions were performed on 96-well plates QuantStudio 7 Flex Real-Time PCR apparatus (AppliedBiosystem®). PCR reactions (20 µL) consisted on: 1x TaqMan® Universal PCR Master Mix 2x (AppliedBiosystems®), 300 nM of each primer, 250 nM of each TaqMan probe and ca. 50 ng µL-1 of genomic nucleic acid as template in each reaction. An internal amplification control (IAC; 2 × 104 copies µL-1) was used in each F. prausnitzii PCR run using 300 nM of primer pair and 250 nM of IAC-probe. All primers and probes were HPLC purification degree and purchased from Macrogen® (Korea). All reactions were run on triplicate. Standard curves for titration of each targeted microorganism were obtained from 10-fold serial dilutions of linearized plasmids containing targeted 16 S rRNA gene fragments. Standard reactions ranged from102 to 107 copies/reaction (see Table 1) and consisted of at least five reactions of a known number of targeted genes in order to assess inter-run reproducibility. The standard curve built for F. prausnitzii quantification was also used for the total bacterial 16 S rRNA gene quantification as per recommended procedures71,72. Triplicates were considered valid if the standard deviation between quantification cycles (Cq) was < 0.33 (i.e., a difference of < 10% of the quantity was tolerated). Those runs not matching the above criteria were repeated. PCR inhibition was controlled on total F. prausnitzii quantifications by adding 103 copies of an IAC template to each reaction. No inhibition was considered if the obtained Cq was < 0.34 different from those obtained when quantifying the IAC alone for any of the replicates. In each run, a non-amplification control (NTC; i.e., lacking any template DNA (either bacterial or synthetic)) was also included. In all cases, NTC resulted in undetectable Cq values. Thermal PCR profiles are depicted in Table 1. Data was collected and analysed using a QuantStudio™ 7 Real-Time apparatus (Applied Biosystems®). All assays were performed under average PCR efficiencies of 97.13 ± 5.82 (mean ± sd; see Supp. Table 1).

Microbiome analyses based on 16 S rRNA gene sequencing

Total extracted genomic DNA was further subjected to Illumina® sequencing by targeting the V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene from each sample following common practices at external facilities (StarSEQ GmbH, www.starseq.com). Briefly, amplifications of the V4 region were performed using previously described primers73–76, equimolarly pooled to improve the unbalanced and biased base composition bias, and finally spiked with ca. 15% PhiX control prior to sequencing. Sequencing forward (5’-GGCTGACTGACT-3’) and reverse (5’-CCAATTACCATA-3’) primers were added to MiSeq reagent kit V3 (Illumina®) together with positive control (ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community DNA standard; Zymo Research corp, CA). Paired-End (2 × 300 bp) high-throughput DNA sequencing was carried out using the MiSeq platform (Illumina®).

Bioinformatic analyses

Obtained Illumina reads were initially processed, with default filtering and trimming settings, with DADA2 pipeline77 and further analysed using the phyloseq R package78. DADA2 built-in training models were used to learn error rates for the amplicon dataset. Identical sequencing reads were combined through DADA2’s dereplication functionality, and the DADA2 sequence-variant inference algorithm was applied to each dataset. Default parameters in DADA2 were applied with the exception of filtering and trimming steps where: truncLen = c(240,120), maxN = 0, maxEE = c(2,2), truncQ = 2 options were applied. Subsequently, paired-end reads were merged. After DADA2 denoising, chimera removal and filtering, the 54 samples included in the analyses generated a total of 9,754,189 reads. The DADA2 pipeline resulted in 2378 amplicon sequence variants (ASV’s). Finally, taxonomy at the genus level was assigned to ASV using the silva’s 138 DADA2-formatted database (www.arb-silva.de) version. Diversity indices (i.e., Chao1 and Shannon) together with beta-diversity metrics (e.g., weighted unifrac and aitchison) and ordination methods (principal coordinate analyses (PCoA)) were computed using phyloseq, vegan, ape, MicrobiotaProcess, UpsetR and mia R packages79–83. Rarefaction curves were constructed to estimate sequencing depth. Furthermore, colorbrewer2 (www.colorbrewer2.org) was used in order to select a colour scheme able to discriminate the five patients groups in a colour blind safe and print friendly manner.

The V4 16 S rRNA gene dataset obtained in the present study has been deposited under BioProject number PRJNA720274 at NCBI. All other metadata that support the findings of the present study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request. Source data is provided with this paper.

Composition analyses

In order to characterize bacterial signatures associated with MASLD stages, the coda4microbiome compositional algorithm84 was used. The algorithm aims to identify the model (i.e., combination of taxa abundances) that best predicts a given outcome (e.g., presence of disease). Accordingly, the final model is a log-contrast bacterial signature that fulfils compositional data rationale and can thus be interpreted as a weighted balance of two groups of taxa: one group of taxa (named G1) with positive coefficients and positively associated with the outcome; and another group of taxa (named G2) with negative coefficients and negatively associated with the outcome. The algorithm also provides an individual microbial score for every individual by combining (log) abundances of G1 and G2 taxa and their respective coefficients. Individuals with higher microbial scores will present higher abundances of G1 taxa compared to G2 taxa, while subjects with lower microbial scores will have lower abundances of G1 taxa compared to G2. Due to the high sparsity of microbiome data, we kept taxa represented in at least 5% of the samples, except for the case of control vs. fHS group, and nfHS vs. fHS group comparisons which were performed on taxa present in at least 10% of the samples. Furthermore and due to the reduced number of samples included within the HCC group (n = 2), this was discarded from coda4microbiome analyses to avoid spurious results.

Statistical analyses

Continuous demographics and clinical characteristics of subjects were summarized as means and standard deviations (SD), if normally distributed, or as median and 25th and 75th percentile if not normally distributed. Categorical variables were summarized by absolute and relative frequencies. The assumption of normality was evaluated with the Shapiro-Wilk test.

To analyse whether there were differences among groups of subjects (i.e., control, sHS. nfHS, fHS, and HCC) the non-parametric Kruskall-Wallis test using Dunn’s procedure for pairwise multiple comparisons and Bonferroni adjustments was used for the continuous variables and the Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact likelihood test for categorical variables.

The present pilot study included 46 participants with MASLD-cirrhosis and no formal calculation of sample size was conducted85. Authors are aware that sample size might be representative of the target population under study and should be large enough to provide useful information about the aspects being assessed for feasibility outcome measures, in this case MASLD management. However, the included number of participants is within the range of patients per group at follow-up for pilot trials as suggested by Julious86.

All tests were 2-sided and p < 0.05 was set as statistically significant. All analyses were performed using RStudio v4.3.0.

Results

Clinical characteristics

The analysed group of subjects included 46 individuals diagnosed within the MASLD range and 8 individuals without any clinical indication of liver disease (from now on controls). Detailed demographic, clinical and biochemical profiles of the entire group of patients are provided in Suppl. Table 1. The patients’ group included 24 patients (52.17% of total non-healthy subjects) with no inflammation (sHS group; 50.0% females), 10 subjects (21.74% of total non-healthy subjects) within first stages of liver fibrosis (F0, F1, F2; nfHS; 50.0% female), 10 subjects (21.74% of total non-healthy subjects) within the F3, F4 stages (fHS; 30.0% female), and 2 patients (4.35% of total non-healthy subjects) suffering from hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC; 50.0% female). MASLD diagnosis was performed as per clinical guidelines2,4,60 and the presence of cirrhosis was confirmed by liver biopsy or imaging. Even though the present study was conducted before the establishment of new nomenclature, diagnostic criteria remained basically the same, thus we adapted nomenclature to the current state. The median age of fHS and HCC patients (60.5 and 72.5 years, respectively) was higher than the other patients’ groups and controls. Included subjects reflected differences in their BMI (p = 0.016; see Suppl. Table 1), with lower values in control subjects with respect to patients (control vs. sHS, p = 0.03; control vs. nfHS, p = 0.02; and control vs. fHS, p = 0.039).

A higher degree of alcohol consumption (data not shown) was observed in healthy controls with respect to MASLD-diagnosed patients (p = 0.517).

In all analysed hepatic enzymes (i.e., AST, ALT, GGT), altered values with respect to controls subjects were observed (Suppl. Table 1). With the exception of the fHS group, an increasing pattern could be observed for all groups of subjects (Suppl. Table 1). Besides, prothrombin blood levels showed similar values among all analysed patients with the exception of HCC subjects (Suppl. Table 1). Furthermore, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) showed non-significant differences among all patients, despite 4 times higher values of the HCC group (Suppl. Table 1).

Comorbidities associated to the analysed subjects (i.e., diabetes mellitus (DM), arterial hypertension (AHT), dyslipidaemia, and hypertriglyceridemia) were also observed. In this sense, a higher incidence of DM was observed in analysed patients as MASLD severity increases as well as hypertriglyceridemia, and high LDL blood levels (Suppl. Table 1). However, an increase of arterial AHT linked to disease severity was not observed (Suppl. Table 1).

It is worth mentioning that critical confounders in basic biomedical research such as age, BMI, sex, metabolic comorbidities (e.g., DM, dyslipidemia), and medication use differ across the analysed groups of subjects, which limit our conclusions.

Diagnostic scores of MASLD

Patient stratification in MASLD is commonly performed according to NAFLD and FIB-4 scores. FIB-4 scores reported significant differences between control subjects and the fHS group (p-value 0.0085), and control subjects and the HCC patients (p-value 0.0243) (Suppl. Figure 1). However, FIB-4 scores did not show differences between controls and the two initial MASLD disease stages (i.e., sHS and nfHS). In the case of NAFLD score, control subjects and patients were statistically distinct in the case of fHS (p-value 0.0009) and HCC (p-value 0.0414) groups (Suppl. Figure 2), but also between sHS and fHS groups (p-value 0.0192; Suppl. Figure 2). Accordingly, an increase in NAFLD and FIB-4 scores as the disease worsens was observed with higher values for the worst clinical outcome (i.e., fHS and HCC groups; Suppl. Figures 1 and 2).

TE throughout Fibroscan® measurements were conducted in mostly (48 out of 54) all patients evidencing a higher fibrosis degree in those patients with an advanced state of MASLD disease (Suppl. Figure 3 and Suppl. Table 1). Three control patients, 2 HCC patients, and 1 fHS patient did not followed Fibroscan ® measurements due to logistic problems. Even so, statistical differences were observed based on Fibroscan® pressures between Control and fHS group (p-value ≤ 0.0001), sHs and fHS groups (p-value 0.00012), and nfHS and fHS groups (p-value 0.040506) (Suppl. Figure 3). Finally, up to 15 subjects were subjected to liver biopsy as per clinical routine and advice to MASLD disease stratification (Suppl. Table 1).

Bacterial quantification by means of qPCR

Bacterial 16 S rRNA gene copies of 3 bacterial targets (named total eubacteria, E. coli and F. prausnitzii) were quantified by means of qPCR using TaqMan chemistry (Fig. 1). The three primer pair combinations targeted distinct regions of the 16 S rRNA gene, consequently reporting different efficiencies and abundances. Accordingly, total eubacterial 16 S rRNA gene copies per gram of faeces were slightly lower than values specifically reported for E. coli and F. prausnitzii as detailed below due to reaction efficiency.

In analysed samples, 16 S rRNA gene copies per gram of faeces for total eubacteria ranged between 3.7 × 107 to 7.4 × 1011 (median value of 3.8 × 108; p-value: 0.563) with little non-significant differences in the analysed groups of patients. Control subjects and patients included within nfHS presented a higher dispersion of the obtained data than the other 3 analysed groups. In general and for total eubacteria, 16 S rRNA gene copy numbers were within the same range (never exceeding 3.5 × 1011 copies per gram of faeces) for all analysed groups and quite homogeneous (median values within 3.7–5.5 × 108 copies per gram of faeces), except for the HCC group (2.6 × 108 copies per gram of faeces; n = 2) (Fig. 1). Controls reflected median values of 4.02 × 108 of 16 S rRNA gene copies of total eubacteria per gram of faeces, whereas E. coli values were 7.93 × 104 and F. prausnitzii values were 6.47 × 1010 (Fig. 1). In the case of E. coli and F. prausnitizii 16 S rRNA gene copies, values ranged between 9.2 × 101 and 1.4 × 108 (median value of 2.1 × 105; p-value: 0.321), and 1.8 × 105 and 6,2 × 1011 (median value of 2.7 × 109; p-value: 0.053), respectively. E. coli and F. prausnitzii 16 S rRNA gene copies presented higher dispersion in all analysed group of samples. In the case of E. coli gene copy numbers, data evidenced slight increase in 16 S rRNA gene copies with increasing disease severity (from roughly 1.8 × 105 to 2.2 × 106, median values), except for the HCC group which exhibited a marked decrease of median values (Fig. 1). Finally, F. prausnitzii values evidenced a decreasing trend with respect to patient groups according to clinical severity (ranging from 1.4 × 109 to 3.6 × 1010, median values; Fig. 1). Even differences were observed in the three analysed targets, which were not statistically significant. It is worth mentioning that F. prausnitzii retrieved values were one order of magnitude higher than total eubacteria ones.

Microbial community composition

Sequencing analyses resulted on 9,754,189 total reads covering the v4 region of the 16 S rRNA gene from 54 samples with 180633.13 average reads. Quality criteria (filtering and denoising) and chimera removal resulted on 7,422,355 clean sequences (137451.02 average reads). Quality reads were grouped into 2378 amplicon sequence variants (ASV) in the final dataset (i.e., removal of Cyanobacteria related sequences or non-assigned ASV at Phylum level). Rarefaction curve analysis was performed to evaluate the level of ASV recovery for all analysed samples. Rarefaction curves evidenced that the majority of analysed samples attained the saturation plateau, indicating that the sequencing depth was sufficient (Suppl. Figure 4).

For all samples and groups analysed, values of richness (Chao1) and diversity (Shannon) estimators were higher in control subjects than in all other patient groups, with patient groups showing a decreasing pattern in line with disease severity (Fig. 2), with the exception of diversity values for fHS group. Significant differences in terms of total richness (Chao1 indice) and diversity (Shannon indice) were observed between controls and patients included in nfHS (p-value of 0.029 and 0.011, respectively for Chao1 and Shannon; Fig. 2) and fHS groups (p-value of 0.009 and 0.023, respectively for Chao1 and Shannon; Fig. 2). In turn, no differences were observed between controls and sHS or HCC subjects for both alpha diversity indices. Furthermore, sHS and nfHs groups reported the highest dispersion in both alpha diversity values unlike the HCC group (Fig. 2). Despite the lack of statistical power (n = 2), patients included within the HCC group reported the lowest diversity and richness values (Fig. 2).

A microbial signature for MASLD

In order to obtain a microbial signature based on balanced microbial compositions from individuals attending a hepatic clinical consultation unit for MASLD screening, we used an statistical algorithm that deals with the compositional nature of gut microbiome data (i.e., coda4microbiome). In this sense, the coda4microbiome cross-validation function, coda_glmnet, was applied to all samples and all possible pair comparisons in a dichotomic manner. Of all possible comparisons, only control vs. all MASLD patients, control vs. sHS groups and between hinge groups (i.e., sHS vs. nfHS and sHS vs. fHS) revealed bacterial taxa (i.e., ASV) able to discriminate between dichotomic diseases status (data not shown). Specifically, taxa involved in control vs. sHS and sHS vs. nfHS patient groups comparisons reported AUC values supporting their discrimination capacity between these pairs of groups, but with low discriminative power (Fig. 6A; cv-AUC: 0.636 ± 0.186; Fig. 6B; cv-AUC: 0.645 ± 0.128). Discussion

Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) is a global burden disease characterised by hepatic manifestations of metabolic disorders, ranging from simple steatosis (excessive accumulation of lipids in liver cells, mainly inside hepatocytes) to Metabolic Dysfunction-Associated Steatohepatitis (MASH), an aggressive histological form, ultimately ending in advanced fibrosis and cirrhosis16,87. Epidemiological data estimate global prevalence values around 24–30% with an increasing trend in parallel with obesity and metabolic syndrome88 making MASLD as the second cause of liver transplantation in westernised countries89. Although visceral fat, dietary composition, and genetic factors can be associated with MASLD18, additional studies considering other environmental factors are required to fully elucidate MASLD pathogenesis22. In this sense, interest in identifying and understanding the role of the gut microbiome and its metabolites in various metabolic diseases has increased (reviewed in90 particularly in light of the invasiveness and drawbacks (for both clinicians and patients) of gold standard diagnostic techniques, liver biopsy in the case for MASLD, in clear contrast with the use of non-invasive samples (e.g., faecal samples) and techniques (e.g., nucleic acid quantification or sequencing). Invasive techniques together with non-invasive clinical parameters traditionally allowed clinicians to develop algorithms for patient classification and better targeted therapies, but basically relying on tissue biopsies (e.g., liver) or imaging techniques (e.g., NMR) for definitive diagnostic.

Gut dysbiosis (imbalance of microbiota composition90, ) is associated with a decrease in beneficial short-chain fatty acid (SCFA)-producing bacteria91, changes in bile acids composition92, activation of immune responses against lipopolysaccharide93, increased alcohol production by overgrowth of alcohol-producing bacteria94,95, and conversion of phosphatidylcholine to choline and trimethylamine96. Accordingly, gut microbiome shifts affecting the gut–liver axis are associated with the progression of chronic liver disease and advanced fibrosis, such as MASLD and cirrhosis54,56,97–99.

In the present pilot study, a group of 46 patients evidencing different degrees in liver pathology related to MASLD was studied as per clinical criteria in the North Catalonia area (i.e., Girona) and further compared to 8 healthy un-related non-house-sharing subjects. The studied patients were stratified based on either direct (NAFLD, FIB-4, and Fibroscan® scores) or indirect (transaminase blood values) clinical evidences as for some patients (44 subjects) no histological diagnosis was available. Stratification of analysed patients according to clinical criteria (Suppl. Figure 1, and 2) evidenced gut microbiome shifts and a reduction of richness and diversity (Fig. 2, and 3) as liver injury progress, in line with recent data8,23,87,89 Click or tap here to enter text. Such reduction was also evidenced in a global NAFLD analysis23 and studies on cirrhotic patients of multiple aetiologies98,100. Interestingly, in our dataset there was not only a reduction of microbial diversity and richness, but also a reduction of the 16 S rRNA gene copies of F. prausnitzii, a positive commensal microbe for the gut microbiome101, and a slight increase in the 16 S rRNA gene copies of E. coli as the disease evolved (Fig. 1) in line with recent reports using gut microbiome data and machine learning tools to predict cirrhosis8. Even though, total eubacterial 16 S rRNA gene copies did not reflect differences among the analysed groups, total amounts were slightly lower than the addition of E. coli and F. prausnitzii counts due to methodological issues (i.e., distinct 16 S rRNA gene targeted region).

Among non-invasive methods, the most commonly used in clinics is TE, which estimates liver fibrosis by measuring liver stiffness102, and has become an important method for the assessment of liver fibrosis in patients. Even though TE data provided stratification capacity for MASLD patients, some limitations are well documented103–105, and other strategies must be envisaged. In this sense, faecal samples and high-throughput sequencing proxies have opened a new era in MASLD and other diseases analyses106.

In our study, roughly one third of retrieved ASV corresponded to the top 20 ASVs at genus level, representing 14 genus groups (Fig. 3), 10 out of 14 genera (i.e., Alistipes, Bacteroides, Bifidobacterium, Blautia, Escherichia-Shigella, Faecalibacterium, Parabacteroides, Prevotella, Ruminococcus, Streptococcus) were already identified as key groups in metabolic disorders (such as MASLD, cirrhosis, T2DM, and obesity) in a recent review23.

In this sense, genera retrieved in our pilot study revealed similar increasing (e.g., for Bacteroides, Blautia, Escherichia-Shigella, Prevotella) or decreasing (e.g., Faecalibacterium) trends in line with the literature reported for MASLD23. Besides that, Faecalibacterium relative abundance decreases as the disease progresses in concordance with reported data for MASLD23 and its role as commensal bacteria with known immunomodulatory potential107.

Sequencing data was grouped according to clinically relevant parameters splitting data into 4 different clinical categories (e.g., sHS, nfHS, fHS, and HCC). Furthermore, we applied a compositional algorithm (coda4microbiome84, ) to our data set in order to identify a collection of ASV (finally microbes) present with the highest discriminative value with respect to the clinical outcome (i.e., clinical group). We evidenced that microbial signatures were able to discriminate between two clinical outcomes based on the microbial community composition (Fig. 6), but their discriminatory capacity was hampered by the reduced sampling size resulting in a lower prediction power of our gut microbiome dataset to predict disease with accuracy108,109. In this sense, the use of the gut microbiome community composition as a non-invasive proxy to help clinicians in the stratification of MASLD suffering patients is a promising option and might require larger datasets and further exploration.

Previous studies reported gut microbiome shifts in MASLD-related patients, but some cofounding drawbacks were present in those datasets such as including DM suffering subjects87; stratifying a cohort as MASLD-related cirrhosis versus MASLD without advanced fibrosis and control non-NAFLD groups99; or obese vs. non-obese MASLD patients87. In parallel, our study presents several limitations that are worth taking into account. First, the limited size of our analysed cohort and thus the reduced number of included individuals for some groups, especially the HCC group. Second, we could not control for potential confounders such as age and BMI. The present descriptive pilot study was conducted during clinical visits in a prospective manner. In this sense, the longer the evolution of the disease, the older the patients must be and differences in BMI exist. However, when the objective is identifying signs for MASLD progression, rather than to stablish causal links between the microbiome and the disease, it can be valuable to explore also associations without adjusting for confounders (e.g., age and BMI). In this context, one might be interested in identifying a certain degree of dysbiosis related with significant disease advancement. Given the complex interplay between microbiome, MASLD and factors like age and BMI, the question of whether dysbiosis is driven by the disease itself or by these confounders becomes secondary. What matters most is identifying microbial alterations that reliably reflect worsening of metabolic liver disease. Therefore, further longitudinal studies including metabolome analyses together with metagenomic approaches might be of interest in order to provide the scientific community with foundational data to produce non-invasive tools based on metagenomic and metabolomic data leaving apart cofounding data106. Furthermore, analysing not only the prokaryotic fraction of the gut microbiome, but also the eukaryotic one might be of interest as a recent study evidenced that gut microbiome resident fungi, specially their secreted metabolites in in vitro mice models, might also exert some effect on metabolic disorders such as steatohepatitis110.

Our results pointed towards the capacity to develop new non-invasive tools to discriminate and stratify clinical patients for MASLD progression discrimination using molecular tools based on a non-invasive proxy such as gut microbiome composition, as recently suggested for other diseases (e.g.111., ), but larger datasets are required.

Conclusions

Our results further support the importance of gut microbiome in the health–disease axis, highlighting its utility as diagnostic tool and its potential therapeutic implications within a non-invasive microbiome–metabolite framework.

The present prospective pilot study was hampered by the limited number of samples included in some of the analysed groups of subjects (e.g., controls and HCC), and the lack of gender and age matching of the whole subject series. In addition, causality and spurious correlations cannot be ruled out by the data presented in this work. Future studies will need to focus on multi-centre involvement in order to increase subject inclusion and including ethnicity and socio-demographic differences and the use of other omic approaches.
