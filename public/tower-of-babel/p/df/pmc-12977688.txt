A young woman with membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis and persistent hypocomplementemia: a case report
Authors: Huimei Li, Li Jinpu, Lin Lan, Zhou Shiqiang, Li Shen, Rao Xiangrong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A young woman presented with a 17-year history of proteinuria, hematuria with persistent hypocomplementemia. She has been followed up for 2 years. The patient underwent comprehensive diagnostic evaluation, including two renal biopsies. The initial biopsy indicated membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN), while the subsequent biopsy revealed concurrent thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) kidney injury, suggesting a complement-mediated disorder. Genetic testing identified two heterozygous missense variants, c.848A > G (p.Asp283Gly) and c.1339C > T (p.Pro447Ser), in the Complement factor I (CFI) gene. Both are classified as variants of uncertain significance, suggestive of potential susceptibility to complement dysregulation. The clinical diagnosis was MPGN with overlapping TMA. Treatment with mycophenolate mofetil and losartan potassium resulted in reduced urinary protein, improved complement levels, and stabilized renal function; traditional Chinese herbal medicine was administered as supportive therapy. This case highlights the diagnostic challenges, when renal pathology indicates membranoproliferative-like lesions that cannot be explained by Ig-mediated or C3 glomerulopathy, especially accompanied by TMA lesions or persistent hypocomplementemia, complement system-related tests including complement regulatory gene mutation may helpful to establish the etiological diagnosis and guide management.

Introduction

Membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN) is a pattern of glomerular injury characterized by mesangial hypercellularity and capillary wall thickening, currently classified into immune-complex-mediated and complement-mediated forms based on underlying pathophysiology. [1] Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) manifests in diverse forms, characterized by thrombocytopenia, microangiopathic hemolytic anemia, and organ damage, including acute kidney injury [2]. Pathologically, TMA is defined by vascular damage, including arteriolar and capillary thrombosis, endothelial abnormalities, and vessel wall changes [3]. Although TMA and MPGN exhibit distinct pathological features, both are classified as complement-mediated disorders based on their etiology. Such diseases often have a protracted course and recurrent episodes, and repeated renal biopsy results in the same patient may undergo some changes.

We report a rare case of a young Chinese woman diagnosed with MPGN via initial renal biopsy, with a subsequent biopsy revealing concurrent TMA. Genetic testing identified two heterozygous missense variants, c.848A > G (p.Asp283Gly) and c.1339C > T (p.Pro447Ser), in the Complement factor I (CFI) gene, classified as variants of uncertain significance, suggestive of potential susceptibility to complement dysregulation. The final clinical diagnosis was MPGN with overlapping TMA.

Case report

A 30-year-old Chinese woman presented to our hospital in 2023 with a 17-year history of proteinuria and hematuria, accompanied by a 1-year history of elevated serum creatinine. The patient’s renal disease first manifested in 2006 as nephrotic syndrome with significant erythrocyturia; steroids treatment provided minimal relief. In 2009, a renal biopsy at a local hospital revealed membranoproliferative glomerulonephritis (MPGN), focal glomerulosclerosis, and mild tubulointerstitial disease. Immunohistochemical staining demonstrated focal, segmental, and mesangial positivity for IgG and IgM (+); IgA was focally mild to moderately positive (++); and C3d was diffusely positive (+), with strong positivity in capillary loops (+-++). Light microscopy of the 36 glomeruli revealed global sclerosis in three, segmental sclerosis in one, diffuse double contours of the basement membranes, and moderate mesangial proliferation. Clinical investigations ruled out autoimmune diseases such as systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), as well as secondary renal damage caused by infections such as viral hepatitis and infective endocarditis. The patient achieved complete remission of proteinuria following treatment with methylprednisolone (750 mg/d for 3 days), cyclophosphamide pulse therapy (cumulative dose 5.6 g), captopril, and prednisone (35 mg qod). The urinary total protein (UTP) level declined to a nadir of 0.28 g per day with slight microscopic hematuria. Prednisone was subsequently tapered and discontinued.

In 2016, she experienced relapse with edema, proteinuria (3.2 g/d), hematuria. Immunological evaluation revealed hypocomplementemia with reduced C3 (0.51 g/L), normal C4 (0.22 g/L) and normal total complement activity (CH50 49.3 U/mL). Autoimmune diseases and infection-related nephritis were excluded again. Treatment with mycophenolate mofetil (MMF) 0.5 g bid and prednisone (10–15 mg/d) reduced proteinuria to 0.16 g/d by 2020, with stable serum creatinine (SCr) (82 µmol/L) and mild anemia (HGB 90 g/L). She reported frequent colds and diarrhea but no other symptoms. After discontinuing MMF and prednisone, losartan potassium (50 mg qd) was continued. Thirty-one months after immunosuppressant withdrawal, SCr showed an upward trend (118 μmol/L) with UTP increasing to 0.3–0.45 g/24 h and reduced C3 (0.51 g/L).

Persistent hypocomplementemia and progressive decline in renal function prompted hospitalization. Additional immunological and hematological evaluations were performed to investigate the etiology. Systematic evaluation ruled out secondary etiologies, including ANCA-associated vasculitis, SLE, antiphospholipid syndrome, cryoglobulinemia, HBV, HCV, Shiga toxin-mediated hemolytic uremic syndrome, and monoclonal gammopathies. A second renal biopsy was performed in May 2023. Immunofluorescence for IgG on protease-digested paraffin sections was remained negative. Arteriolar wall thickening with hyalinization, intimal fibrotic hyperplasia and sclerosis, and luminal stenosis. Electron microscopy revealed electron-dense deposits in subendothelial, mesangial, and subepithelial locations, along with extensive foot process effacement, significant widening of the lamina densa interna in the GBM (Fig. 1h), and segmental double contours (Fig. 1i). Following the biopsy, detailed hematological evaluation was performed to assess for systemic involvement. Despite the presence of TMA lesions, there was no evidence of systemic microangiopathic hemolysis, indicated by normal platelet counts (194 × 109/L), normal lactate dehydrogenase (LDH 215 U/L, normal <247), and normal indirect bilirubin (4.0 μmol/L), with no schistocytes observed on the peripheral smear. Investigation into the mild anemia revealed an iron profile consistent with anemia of chronic disease rather than iron deficiency or hemolysis, characterized by low serum iron (4.06 μmol/L, normal 9–27) and low transferrin (164.58 mg/dL, normal 200–400), but elevated serum ferritin (136.98 μg/L, normal 10–120). To further differentiate the etiology, the activity of ADAMTS13 (a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with a thrombospondin type 1 motif, member 13) was measured at 90%, effectively ruling out thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura. Additionally, serum complement Factor H levels were normal (521.2 μg/ml), and tests for anti-Factor H antibodies were negative, combined with the extensive negative autoimmune screening mentioned above, making autoimmune-mediated atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS) unlikely. These findings, confirming localized histological injury without systemic hematologic abnormalities, supported a clinical diagnosis of renal-limited TMA. Prompted by this specific combination of biopsy-proven TMA, absence of systemic hemolysis, and long-standing hypocomplementemia, complement regulatory gene testing was performed. The patient was administered MMF at 0.5 g in the morning and 0.25 g in the evening. After treatment, the patient’s UTP decreased to 0.24 g/d, with C3 improving to 0.69 g/L. The dosage of MMF was reduced to 0.25 g bid and losartan potassium (50 mg qd) was resumed with Chinese herbal medicine and iron preparation added as supportive therapy; no relapses have been reported since then. Genetic testing, performed via next-generation sequencing for the proband and validated by Sanger sequencing for the family, identified two heterozygous CFI variants. Zygosity was confirmed by analyzing sequencing depth and allele frequency. The first variant, c.848A > G (p.Asp283Gly, dbSNP: rs756201106) was inherited from the mother, while the second, c.1339C > T (p.Pro447Ser), was inherited from the father (Fig. 3). According to the American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics (ACMG) guidelines [4], the c.848A > G (p.Asp283Gly) variant is classified as a variant of uncertain significance (VUS) but is suggestive of potential deleterious effects. This assessment is supported by criteria PM2_Supporting (extremely rare in population databases, allele frequency 0.00038) and PP3_Strong (deleterious predictions by multiple in silico tools including REVEL, SIFT, and PolyPhen-2). The c.1339C > T (p.Pro447Ser) variant is also classified as VUS. While it meets criteria PM2_Supporting (rare allele frequency 0.00011), some computational models suggest benignity (BP4). (Table 2). However, in this compound heterozygous context, it may act as a potential genetic modifier. The patient is an only child. Discussion

MPGN is classified by immunofluorescence into immune-complex-mediated forms (dominant Ig) or complement-mediated forms (C3-dominant with minimal Ig), the latter encompassing C3 glomerulopathy (C3G) and dense-deposit disease [1]. Differential diagnosis requires excluding immune-complex-mediated causes, such as infections, autoimmune diseases, and proliferative glomerulonephritis with monoclonal immunoglobulin deposits. Comprehensive diagnostic evaluation including serologic assays, autoantibody panels, and electrophoresis is essential. In both biopsies, immune complex-mediated MPGN was excluded through comprehensive serological evaluation, and pronase-digested paraffin immunofluorescence in the second biopsy confirming absence of masked IgG deposits [5]. The observed IgM positivity is interpreted as nonspecific trapping associated with glomerular sclerosis and chronic injury, rather than etiologic immune complex deposition [6]. Persistently negative autoantibodies, together with low C3, normal C4 and CH50 levels, point to alternative complement pathway activation. This pattern helps to differentiate the condition from classical pathway-mediated disorders such as active SLE, where reduced C4 is typically observed [7, 8]. However, rather than fitting neatly into the classical definition of C3G, this case represents a complement-mediated MPGN-like pattern. This distinct presentation highlights a gap in the current classification system regarding cases that do not strictly meet C3G criteria yet show clear complement dysregulation. Although MPGN and TMA may coexist, as observed in this case, their causal relationship remains unclear; emerging evidence suggests that TMA and MPGN may represent a pathological continuum in the context of complement dysregulation [9, 10]. The initial MPGN pattern with prominent C3d deposition likely reflects chronic complement dysregulation causing indolent glomerular injury, whereas the superimposed renal-limited TMA without systemic microangiopathic hemolysis indicates aggravated complement activation leading to endothelial damage and microthrombosis confined to the glomerular microvasculature (Fig. 4). The C3 convertase is regulated by complement proteins, including complement factor H (CFH), Membrane Cofactor Protein (MCP, CD46), and CFI, to prevent excessive C3b production. Mutations in these genes can disrupt this regulation, leading to uncontrolled complement activity and disease. [11, 12] CFI is a serine protease that downregulates complement activity in the fluid phase or on cell surface. Impaired CFI function is implicated in C3G and aHUS. In our patient, compound heterozygous CFI mutations exemplify this impairment. The CFI mutations c.848A > G (p.Asp283Gly) and c.1339C > T (p.Pro447Ser) are rarely reported in Chinese patients with concurrent MPGN and TMA. Recent studies have highlighted the clinical relevance of CFI variants in cohorts of patients with TMA and overlapping C3G phenotypes [13, 14]. Our findings align with these reports but present a unique long-term course. Specifically, the c.848A > G mutation (p.Asp283Gly) replaces an acidic aspartic acid with a neutral glycine in the LDL receptor class A domain. As supported by findings from Osawa et al. [15] regarding overlapping aHUS and C3G, Wen et al. [16] regarding post-transplant C3G and TMA, and Zhao et al.’s cohort study of C3G in Asia [17], this missense variant is predicted to disrupt Ca2+ binding sites, leading to protein misfolding, reduced C3b affinity, and diminished FI cofactor activity [18, 19], thereby promoting complement alternative pathway dysregulation. Regarding the c.1339C > T (p.Pro447Ser) variant, it substitutes a rigid proline with a polar serine in the trypsin-like serine protease domain of CFI. While unlikely to cause gross morphological changes to the protein, this substitution disrupts structural kinks and may induce subtle conformational alterations. These alterations potentially reduce proteolytic efficiency for C3b cleavage, acting as a genetic modifier [13, 20–22].

The distinct renal-limited TMA phenotype in our patient, in contrast to systemic aHUS, may be attributed to the specific functional consequences of these missense variants. Unlike loss-of-function nonsense mutations that often precipitate systemic thrombotic events, these missense variants such as p.Asp283Gly and p.Pro447Ser likely retain residual FI secretion and function sufficient to prevent widespread systemic thrombosis but inadequate to control complement activation under local renal microenvironmental stress [23]. This scenario is consistent with chronic MPGN-like glomerular injury with episodic exacerbation leading to localized endothelial damage and TMA, as supported by mouse models where analogous variants exacerbate C3 deposition only under inflammatory triggers without spontaneous systemic disease [24]. Notably, in kidney-limited TMA cohorts, complement defects predominate, often associated with better outcomes than systemic forms due to the absence of widespread thrombosis [25, 26]. Persistent hypocomplementemia reflects ongoing alternative pathway dysregulation driving proteinuria, endothelial injury, and progressive renal damage.

The patient has remained clinically stable on treatment with MMF and losartan potassium, supplemented with supportive Chinese herbal medicine. Although KDIGO guidelines recommend complement inhibition for complement-mediated glomerular diseases with active or progressive features, C5 inhibitors such as eculizumab or ravulizumab were considered but not initiated in this case due to the patient’s mild clinical manifestations and favorable response to current regimen [27]. This decision was guided primarily by the indolent, renal-limited clinical course and shared decision-making with the patient. However, should disease relapse occur with worsening proteinuria or declining renal function, emerging proximal complement inhibitors, such as the Factor B inhibitor iptacopan or C3 inhibitor pegcetacoplan, could be considered as potential therapeutic options based on recent evidence in C3 glomerulopathy and MPGN [28, 29].

Our study has certain limitations. First, limited accessibility of specialized complement assays in our region precluded the measurement of serum Factor I levels or cofactor activity, alternative pathway hemolytic activity (AH50), C3 nephritic factor (C3NeF) and tissue C5b-9 staining. Consequently, the functional relevance of the CFI variants was inferred based on clinical presentation, exclusion of other etiologies, and in silico predictions, rather than direct functional verification. Second, serial serum C3 levels were not consistently monitored during the clinical remission period (2009–2016) as the patient underwent maintenance follow-up at local facilities. Finally, as a single case report, the therapeutic efficacy is observational, and future functional studies are needed to definitively confirm the impact of these variants on complement regulation.

This case emphasizes the diagnostic challenges of overlapping MPGN and TMA associated with CFI gene mutations. Despite the absence of severe systemic TMA features, persistent proteinuria and hypocomplementemia necessitated sequential biopsies to monitor evolving renal pathology. Timely complement testing and genetic screening are critical for accurate diagnosis and tailored treatment, improving patient outcomes.
