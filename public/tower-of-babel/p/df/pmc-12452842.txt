Trends in depression related cardiovascular mortality among adults from 1999 to 2020: a retrospective study in the United States
Authors: Shahzaib Ahmed, Eeman Ahmad, Ayesha Ali, Hamza Ashraf, Hoor Ul Ain, Mahrosh Kasbati, Ibrahim Nagmeldin Hassan, Hana J. Abukhadijah, Shoaib Ahmad, Irfan Ullah, Chadi Alraies, Gregg C. Fonarow
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•CVD mortality in depression declined, then rose sharply post-2018.•Females had consistently higher CVD mortality than males.•Non-Hispanic Whites had the highest mortality across racial groups.•Rural areas faced higher mortality than urban counterparts.•Midwest and South showed the highest regional mortality rates.

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease (CVD) remains one of the leading causes of mortality in the United States (US), with a prevalence of approximately 48.6 %, and accounts for more deaths than chronic lower respiratory disease and cancer combined [1]. CVD and depression frequently co-exist, with an estimated 17 % to 27 % of CVD patients suffering from comorbid depression [2,3]. Both conditions exert influence over one another, with depression leading to the development of unhealthy behaviors, such as poor diet, sedentary lifestyle, and detrimental coping mechanisms like smoking or drinking, which, in turn, further exacerbate modifiable risk factors for CVD [[4], [5], [6]]. Consequently, individuals diagnosed with both CVD and depression have been observed to experience poorer outcomes and higher mortality than those with CVD alone [7]. Previous literature suggests that Black or African American individuals with CVD have higher mortality rates compared to their White counterparts [8]. Depression, particularly among older African Americans, often goes untreated, leading to unfavourable health outcomes [9]. These disparities can be linked to barriers in healthcare access and socioeconomic challenges [10,11].

The underlying pathophysiology of the association between depression and CVD appears to be complex and multifactorial. Chronic depression can contribute to a dysregulatory hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, resulting in increased cortisol levels and subsequent cardiovascular complications [12,13]. Inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP) and interleukin-6 (IL-6) are also elevated in both conditions, indicating a common inflammatory pathway [14].

Earlier studies have not fully addressed gaps in understanding mortality patterns in individuals coexisting with CVD and depression. For instance, Lichtman et al. reviewed studies on depression in patients with acute coronary syndrome and emphasized its role as a prognostic factor, but their focus was limited to post-ACS outcomes and did not include long-term mortality trends or subgroup analyses by age, sex, race, or geography [2]. Similarly, Li et al. and Krittanawong et al. provided narrative reviews of the bidirectional relationship between CVD and depression, but did not include original epidemiologic data or examine temporal patterns in mortality among comorbid populations [3,7]. Others have typically examined either cardiovascular disease or depression in isolation, focusing on prevalence, treatment patterns, or disparities, but have not assessed mortality trends in individuals with both conditions [8,9]

There is limited analysis of long-term trends in CVD mortality among adults with depression. Prior studies have often been limited by small sample sizes, short follow-up periods, a focus on CVD and depression as separate conditions rather than coexisting comorbidities, and a lack of stratification by key demographic variables. Our study aims to examine disparities in mortality trends within this population by using data from the CDC database, stratified by age, sex, race, and geographic location over the past two decades. An in-depth analysis of these trends will allow policymakers and public health specialists to draft targeted interventions that will address the rising mortality burden among these high-risk groups.

Methods

Study design

The CDC’s Wide-Ranging Online Data for Epidemiologic Research (WONDER) database was used to obtain death certificate data from 1999 to 2020 for adults aged 25 years and above [15]. Cardiovascular disease (CVD) deaths were extracted using ICD-10 codes I00–I99 as the underlying cause of death, with depression coded as a contributing cause (ICD-10: F32.0–F32.3, F32.8–F32.9, F33.0–F33.4, F33.8–F33.9) [16]. Since the dataset contains de-identified public use data, institutional review board approval was not applicable. The study was conducted in accordance with the STROBE (Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology) guidelines [17].

Data abstraction

Extracted variables included population size, year, sex, race/ethnicity, geographic region, urban-rural classification, and state. Race and ethnicity were grouped as Non-Hispanic (NH) White, NH Black or African American, Hispanic or Latino, NH American Indian or Alaska Native, and NH Asian or Pacific Islander. Census regions were classified as Northeast, Midwest, South, and West per U.S. Census Bureau standardised definitions [18]. Counties were categorised, utilising the National Center for Health Statistics urban/rural classification scheme, as urban (large metropolitan areas [population 1 million]), medium/small metropolitan areas [population 50,000–999,999]), and rural (population <50,000) [19]. Age-adjusted mortality rates (AAMRs) per 100,000 population were stratified by sex, race/ethnicity, region, urbanization, and state. Joinpoint regression was performed using AAMRs (per 100,000 population, standardized to the 2000 U.S. population) for all analyses, including subgroup analyses by sex, race/ethnicity, and region.

Statistical analysis

Trends in depression contributing- CVD mortality were evaluated using annual percentage change (APC) and 95 % confidence intervals (CIs). Data for NH American Indian or Alaska Native populations and for individuals aged 25–34 were excluded due to unreliability, defined as mortality estimates based on fewer than 20 deaths or a relative standard error (RSE) ≥23 % [20]. Non-Hispanic American Indian or Alaska Native individuals were excluded from trend analyses due to small sample sizes and unstable estimates but were included in descriptive summaries of age-adjusted mortality rates. APCs, AAPC and their corresponding CIs were estimated using the Joinpoint Regression Program [21]. The analysis detects significant variations in AAMR over time by modeling temporal trends using a log-linear regression method [22]. A two-tailed t-test was used to determine statistical significance of the variation in mortality from baseline, with a value of p < 0.05 being considered significant. For trend analysis, statistical significance of joinpoints and annual percent changes (APCs) was determined using Monte Carlo permutation tests with an overall significance level of 0.05, as per the default settings of the Joinpoint Regression Program (National Cancer Institute). Additionally, pairwise comparisons were conducted in Joinpoint to assess whether CVD mortality rates differed significantly between individuals with depression and the general population [23].

Results

From 1999 to 2020, a total of 79,813 CVD deaths were reported among adults with depression, with an average AAMR of 1.67 per 100,000 (95 % CI: 1.66 to 1.68) (Table 1). Mortality rates showed a stable trend between 1999 and 2002 (APC: 0.14; 95 % CI: −2.10 to 2.43), followed by a significant decline from 2002 to 2010 (APC: −6.87*; 95 % CI: −7.49 to −6.24). Between 2010 and 2018, there was a significant but modest decline (APC: −0.80*; 95 % CI: −1.50 to −0.09). However, from 2018 to 2020, there was a significant increase in mortality (APC: 8.52*; 95 % CI: 3.38 to 13.93) (Fig. 1, Table 2). Additionally, CVD mortality in adults with depression was significantly different from CVD mortality alone (p = 0.03), with an AAPC of −2.19* (95 % CI: −2.78 to −1.60) compared to −2.33* (95 % CI: −2.53 to −2.12) for CVD alone. Trends by sex

Females with depression had a higher CVD mortality rate compared to males (AAMR: 1.76; 95 % CI: 1.74 to 1.77 and AAMR: 1.50; 95 % CI: 1.48 to 1.52, respectively). For males, mortality rates showed a stable trend between 1999 and 2002 (APC: −0.23; 95 % CI: −2.62 to 2.21), followed by a significant decline from 2002 to 2011 (APC: −6.53*; 95 % CI: −7.04 to −6.00). Between 2011 and 2018, the trend remained stable (APC: 0.58; 95 % CI: −0.29 to 1.46). However, from 2018 to 2020, there was a significant increase in mortality (APC: 7.36*; 95 % CI: 2.57 to 12.36). Similarly, females’ mortality showed a stable trend between 1999 and 2001 (APC: 4.44; 95 % CI: −3.79 to 13.37), followed by a significant decline from 2001 to 2009 (APC: −6.81*; 95 % CI: −7.90 to −5.70). Between 2009 and 2017, there was another significant decline (APC: −2.03*; 95 % CI: −3.35 to −0.70). However, from 2017 to 2020, there was a significant increase in mortality (APC: 6.19*; 95 % CI: 1.33 to 11.27) (Fig. 1). The CVD mortality in adults with depression differed significantly from that of CVD alone in males (p = 0.039; AAPC: −2.03*; 95 % CI: −2.62 to −1.45 for CVD with Depression and AAPC: −2.22*; 95 % CI: −2.42 to 2.022 for CVD alone). Trends by race

When stratified by racial groups, NH White individuals had the highest rate (1.85, 95 % CI: 1.83 to 1.86), followed by NH American Indian or Alaska Native individuals (1.31, 95 % CI: 1.15 to 1.46) and NH Black or African American individuals (1.02, 95 % CI: 0.99 to 1.05). Hispanic or Latino individuals exhibited a rate of 0.87 (95 % CI: 0.84 to 0.90), while NH Asian or Pacific Islander individuals had the lowest rate of 0.53 (95 % CI: 0.50 to 0.57) (Fig. 2). From 1999 to 2002, NH White individuals exhibited a stable mortality trend (APC: 0.10; 95 % CI: −3.47 to 3.79). This was followed by a significant decline from 2002 to 2010 (APC: −6.68*; 95 % CI: −7.66 to −5.68). Between 2010 and 2016, mortality remained relatively stable (APC: −1.24; 95 % CI: −3.18 to 0.73). However, from 2016 to 2020, mortality rates increased significantly (APC: 4.32*; 95 % CI: 1.64 to 7.08). From 1999 to 2017, NH Black or African American individuals experienced a significant decline in mortality (APC: −2.54*; 95 % CI: −3.42 to −1.65). Trends by census region

From 2012 to 2020, mortality remained stable (APC: 0.40; 95 % CI: −0.92 to 1.72). In the West, mortality remained stable from 1999 to 2004 (APC: −0.26; 95 % CI: −2.83 to 2.38), followed by a significant decline from 2004 to 2009 (APC: −8.76*; 95 % CI: −12.21 to −5.18). Most deaths (80 %) occurred in urban areas, with an AAMR of 1.56 (95 % CI: 1.55 to 1.57), compared to rural areas, which had a higher AAMR of 2.17 (95 % CI: 2.14 to 2.2). Trends in mortality rates varied across urbanization. Mortality in urban areas remained stable from 1999 to 2002 (APC: −0.40; 95 % CI: −3.16 to 2.45), followed by a significant decline from 2002 to 2010 (APC: −7.47*; 95 % CI: −8.24 to −6.70). From 2010 to 2018, mortality remained stable (APC: −0.41; 95 % CI: −1.29 to 0.49), but from 2018 to 2020, there was a significant increase (APC: 9.26*; 95 % CI: 2.97 to 15.92). Trends by age group

When stratified by age groups, the highest mortality rate was observed among individuals aged 85 and above (CR: 30.39, 95 % CI: 30.08 to 30.70), followed by those aged 75–84 (CR: 7.46, 95 % CI: 7.36 to 7.56) and 65–74 (CR: 2.05, 95 % CI: 2.02 to 2.09). Mortality rates were lower in younger age groups, with CRs of 0.87 (95 % CI: 0.85 to 0.89) for individuals aged 55–64, 0.34 (95 % CI: 0.33 to 0.35) for those aged 45–54, 0.08 (95 % CI: 0.07 to 0.09) for individuals aged 35–44, and the lowest rate observed in the 25–34 age group (CR: 0.02, 95 % CI: 0.02 to 0.02). The CVD mortality in adults with depression differed significantly from that of CVD alone in 45–54 (p = 0.049), 55–54 (p = 0.0002), 65–74 (p = 0.01), 75–84 (p = 0.0008) and 85+ (p = 0.0002) age groups (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Trends by states

Differences in AAMRs were notable across states. The states with the highest AAMRs were North Dakota (AAMR: 4.25; 95 % CI: 3.89 to 4.61), Ohio (AAMR: 3.42; 95 % CI: 3.34 to 3.51), Vermont (AAMR: 3.35; 95 % CI: 3.00 to 3.69), and Minnesota (AAMR: 3.10; 95 % CI: 2.98 to 3.22). These states had mortality rates several times higher than those with the lowest AAMRs, such as Alabama (AAMR: 0.85; 95 % CI: 0.78 to 0.92), Florida (AAMR: 0.82; 95 % CI: 0.79 to 0.85), Arizona (AAMR: 0.74; 95 % CI: 0.69 to 0.80), and Nevada, which had the lowest mortality rate (AAMR: 0.48; 95 % CI: 0.41 to 0.56). Discussion

This retrospective study analyzed trends in cardiovascular disease mortality among adults with depression in the United States from 1999 to 2020. Our findings reveal a nuanced temporal pattern marked by a sustained decline in mortality from the early 2000s until the late 2010s, followed by a concerning resurgence in recent years. Overall, 79,813 CVD deaths occurred among adults with depression during the study period, with an age-adjusted mortality rate (AAMR) of 1.67 per 100,000. Importantly, CVD mortality in this population exhibited statistically significant differences from CVD mortality in the general population, underscoring the clinical relevance of depression as a modifying factor in cardiovascular outcomes [24].

The early decline in mortality between 2002 and 2010 may be attributed to improved clinical guidelines for depression screening within the primary care setting [25] that emerged during that period, as well as heightened public awareness efforts. However, the marked increase in CVD mortality from 2018 to 2020 warrants urgent attention. Although the early impact of the COVID-19 pandemic, which disproportionately affected individuals with pre-existing mental health conditions, likely contributed to the observed spike in 2020, the upward inflection began prior to the pandemic and points to additional contributing factors [26,27]. A national study of trends from 1999 to 2019 demonstrated a consistent and significant rise in substance-use related cardiovascular mortality in the U.S. population, particularly among young adults and racial minorities [39]. Between 2011 and 2019 alone, age-adjusted mortality rates from combined substance use and CVD nearly doubled, with opioids and alcohol being the predominant contributors. These findings suggest that growing substance use epidemics were already exacerbating cardiovascular risk well before the emergence of COVID-19. The pandemic likely acted as an accelerant to these pre-existing vulnerabilities, rather than their origin.

Sex-based disparities were evident, with females exhibiting higher CVD mortality rates compared to males. This aligns with prior research suggesting that depression may have a more profound physiological and behavioral impact on cardiovascular risk in women, potentially due to distinct neurobiological mechanisms. Recent evidence indicates that young women are particularly at risk, with stress not only playing a role in the development of cardiovascular disease but also in exacerbating its progression among those already affected. This heightened susceptibility to psychosocial stress may partly account for the increased prevalence of abnormal coronary vasomotion and microvascular dysfunction observed in women compared to men [28].

Racial and ethnic stratification revealed stark disparities. Non-Hispanic White individuals had the highest mortality rate, followed by Non-Hispanic American Indian or Alaska Native and Non-Hispanic Black individuals. Notably, Non-Hispanic Black individuals experienced a sharp increase in mortality from 2017 to 2020 after a prolonged period of decline, highlighting the persistent structural inequities that intersect with both mental and cardiovascular health [29]. This disparity is exacerbated in Non-Hispanic Black communities by structural and systemic factors that limit recognition and treatment of depression. These communities experience greater severity and chronicity of depressive illness despite paradoxically lower measured prevalence, in part due to delayed treatment-seeking, stigma, fragmented healthcare access, and lack of culturally responsive care [41]. While Hispanic or Latino and Non-Hispanic Asian or Pacific Islander populations exhibited comparatively lower mortality rates, the stability observed in recent years suggests a plateau in progress rather than continued improvement.

Regional variation further accentuates the heterogeneity in outcomes. The Midwest exhibited the highest mortality rate, and although there was a substantial decline between 2002 and 2012, recent trends have plateaued. A particularly sharp increase in mortality in the South between 2018 and 2020 reflects the high baseline CVD risk [30], and limited healthcare infrastructure in that region [31]. Differences between regions likely reflect a confluence of socioeconomic, healthcare access, and cultural factors influencing both mental health and cardiovascular risk profiles [32].

Urban–rural disparities were pronounced, with rural areas experiencing higher CVD mortality despite accounting for only 20 % of total deaths. This paradox suggests that while urban populations face a higher absolute burden, individuals in rural areas suffer from disproportionately worse outcomes, likely due to limited access to specialized mental [33] and cardiovascular care [34], and higher prevalence of comorbid conditions [35].

A key methodological consideration pertains to the identification of depression via ICD-10 codes on death certificates, where it is listed as a contributing cause of death. This approach is limited by known underreporting and inconsistent documentation of psychiatric comorbidities in mortality data. An analysis of U.S. mortality data using CDC WONDER found that mental and behavioral disorders are often underdiagnosed and underreported on death certificates, which may contribute to underestimation of their true burden in national mortality statistics [40].

Taken together, these findings underscore the urgency of comprehensive, equity-focused public health strategies. Evidence-based interventions such as collaborative care models, which integrate behavioral health into primary care, have demonstrated efficacy in improving health outcomes [42]. Rural telehealth expansion, community-based mental health initiatives, and culturally tailored care delivery can mitigate access barriers in high-burden populations [43].The observed reversal in declining trends in recent years suggests that previous gains may be eroding, emphasizing the urgency of renewed investment in mental health infrastructure [36] and culturally competent care delivery [37]. Future research should further investigate the mechanisms linking depression to adverse cardiovascular outcomes and evaluate the effectiveness of multidisciplinary interventions tailored to vulnerable subgroups.

Limitations

Several limitations must be acknowledged in interpreting the findings of this study. First, the use of death certificate data from national mortality databases is inherently subject to misclassification and underreporting [38], particularly concerning the presence of comorbid depression, which may not be consistently documented. Second, depression status was identified through mortality records and may not capture the full spectrum or severity of depressive disorders, nor distinguish between treated and untreated cases, as individual-level clinical histories, medication use, and other diagnostic data were not available. Third, this analysis does not account for potential confounders such as socioeconomic status, medication adherence, lifestyle factors, or comorbid psychiatric and medical conditions that may influence cardiovascular outcomes.

Conclusion

This study highlights concerning shifts in cardiovascular disease mortality among adults with depression in the United States from 1999 to 2020, with recent years marked by a reversal of earlier progress. Pronounced disparities by sex, race/ethnicity, region, and urban–rural residence underscore the complex interplay of biological, social and structural determinants shaping health outcomes in this population. These findings call for an urgent, multidimensional public health response - one that integrates mental and cardiovascular care, strengthens health systems in underserved regions, and centers equity in programmatic and policy design.These findings demand a multidimensional response: one that integrates mental and cardiovascular care, scales evidence-based interventions such as collaborative care and telehealth, and centers equity in both clinical and policy design. Addressing the syndemic burden of depression and cardiovascular disease is imperative to preventing further mortality and ensuring the health of vulnerable populations in an increasingly strained healthcare landscape.
