Analysis of burden of liver cancer by GBD 2021
Authors: Cong Hu, Rui Deng, Meng Wu, Shuxiong Nong, Yuan Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study used the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database to analyze and summarize the global disease burden and changing trends of liver cancer, which has strong public health reference value.Research Design and MethodsThe data for this study is drawn from the GBD 2021 study. In the analysis, we conducted a detailed stratification based on gender, 20 age groups, 21 GBD regions, 204 countries and territories, and 5 SDI quintiles. This study aims to explore the liver cancer burden of age-standardized prevalence rates (ASPR), incidence rates, death rates (ASMR), and disability-adjusted life years (ASDR).ResultsIn 2021, liver cancer continued to impose a substantial global burden. Although the global burden of liver cancer is declining overall, there are marked regional differences in the scale of reduction. Incidence of liver cancer varies markedly across regions and sexes within each age group, with older people bearing a disproportionately high burden, especially among males in high SDI regions and low-middle SDI regions. The declining global incidence of liver cancer among both genders from 2022 to 2040.ConclusionsThese patterns highlighted the necessity for focused public health measures and management plans to tackle the inequalities in liver cancer.Keywords: Liver cancer, GBD, DALY, incidence, prevalence

Introduction

Liver cancer ranks among the leading malignant neoplasms globally, presenting a formidable challenge to public health on a worldwide scale [1,2]. Histologically, the landscape is dominated by two entities: hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (iCCA). Even with advancements in the realms of prevention, diagnosis, and therapeutic strategies, liver cancer remains a considerable health burden for populations, with significant gaps among various regions and socio-economic groups [3,4].

Despite its rank as the sixth most diagnosed cancer and the fourth leading cause of cancer death worldwide, primary liver cancer has received disproportionately little policy attention compared with other malignancies. Most previous global analyses either focused on a single risk factor (e.g., hepatitis B or alcohol) or presented static snapshots of incidence and mortality [3–5]. The existing body of data on the global implications of liver cancer is insufficient, and there is a pressing need for an in-depth analysis utilizing the most recent GBD 2021 statistics to obtain a clearer picture of the global burden of liver cancer. GBD 2021 release to examine the joint, time-varying contributions of all major risk factors (HBV, HCV, alcohol, NAFLD, aflatoxin and metabolic risks) and to forecast their future impact is limited. Consequently, current estimates of preventable liver-cancer burden are fragmented, and global health agencies lack quantitative evidence to prioritize cost-effective interventions. Such comprehensive and current data are essential for crafting effective health policies and designing targeted intervention strategies. To fill this information gap, this study examined national, regional, and worldwide liver cancer statistics from 1990 to 2021. By utilizing the most recent GBD 2021 data to examine the global burden of liver cancer, this study expanded on earlier findings and offered a more thorough understanding of the burden of liver cancer. This study will be stratified by age, gender, location, and SDI to understand better how liver cancer varies across various groups. Here, the research significance or purpose was analyzing temporal trends in the worldwide burden of liver cancers from 1990 through 2021 and projects future trends. The findings underscore the urgent need for region-specific prevention initiatives and the strengthening of health-care infrastructures in the most heavily affected areas. These findings are essential for guiding global health policies and directing research resources to address the growing challenge of liver cancer.

Method

Across 204 nations and regions, the GBD 2021 research conducts a thorough evaluation of 369 illnesses, injuries, and health losses linked to 88 risk variables using the latest epidemiological data and established techniques. To handle missing data and adjust for confounding variables, the GBD database employs advanced techniques. There are many detailed explanations of the GBD study design and methodology in the body of extant GBD literature. GBD studies evaluate the burden of liver cancer using sophisticated modeling methodologies. A Bayesian geospatial program called dissmod-mr2.1 was used to calculate the incidence and prevalence rates. It combined geographical data, epidemiological correlations, and a variety of disease factors to generate reliable estimates. Using the Cause of Death Ensemble Model (CODEm) framework, mortality rates were calculated. Data was carefully adjusted before analysis to guarantee accuracy, and CODEm used a variety of models to predict mortality more precisely. To produce a thorough assessment of the liver cancer burden. To provide the evaluation of liver cancer incidence, prevalence, and mortality, this technique takes into account the variations in research design and methodologies across diverse data sources. Yearly Life Loss (YLL), which measures the effect of premature mortality, and Years Lived with Disability (YLD), which reflects the burden of life caused by liver cancer, are the two factors we take into account when calculating the DALYs attributable to liver cancer. The SDI, which takes into account factors including per capita income, education level, and birth rate, is used to gauge how developed a nation or area is. Greater socioeconomic development is indicated by a greater SDI, which ranges from 0 to 1. It is also known to be correlated with rates of illness incidence and death. This study examined the relationship between the prevalence of liver cancer and socioeconomic advancement by classifying nations and areas into five SDI categories, ranging from low to high. The theoretical minimum risk exposure levels (TMRELs) used for liver-cancer attributable burden follow the GBD comparative risk assessment protocol. The TMREL for high alcohol consumption is defined as the level of alcohol intake that minimizes all-cause mortality risk. Complete TMREL values for all risk factors are reported in the GBD 2019 Risk Factors Capstone appendix (available at http://ghdx.healthdata.org).

The study also looked at how different risk variables affected the burden of liver cancer in addition to the main indicators of mortality, morbidity, incidence rate, and DALYs. According to the GBD 2021 research, it contained information on fatalities and DALYs associated with liver cancer caused by the four main risk factors: smoking, excessive alcohol use, obesity, and inactivity. The study included additional regional stratification to highlight regional differences in the impact of these factors. To measure the impact of these risk factors, sophisticated techniques were used, such as spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression and dissed-mr2.1, which allow us to simulate the exposure distribution of each risk factor across various demographic groups and geographical areas. Based on epidemiological studies, a theoretical minimal risk exposure level (TMREL) for each component was calculated. These estimates are translated into meaningful health outcomes that reveal how changing these risk factors may affect liver cancer outcomes across different populations.

The estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) is used for statistical analysis to evaluate the trend of age-standardized rates (ASR) for the incidence, mortality, DALYs, and prevalence of liver cancer. With ai representing the age-specific rate for each group, wi representing the number of individuals in the standard population that belong to each age group, and A representing the number of age groups, the formula for calculating the ASR per 100,000 people is ASR = ∑(ai × wi)/∑wi × 100,000. The regression model used to construct EAPCs explains the trend of ASR over a certain period: Y is the natural logarithm of ASR, X is the year, α is the intercept, and Y = α + βX + e, e is the error term, while β is the slope or trend. EAPC, or annual percentage change, is computed as 100 × [exp(β) − 1. To get the 95% CI for EAPC, a linear regression model is employed. A rising trend in ASR is suggested if the EAPC and its 95% CI are both positive. On the other hand, a declining trend is indicated if both are negative. The ASR is regarded as steady if neither requirement is satisfied. The association between SDI and the age-standardized incidence of liver cancer is assessed using Spearman’s correlation.

To predict future changes in the burden of liver cancer, we use the Bayesian Age-Period-Cohort (BAPC) model in this work, which includes an integrated nested Laplace approximation. In comparison to alternative prediction techniques, this model has proven to have better coverage and accuracy. Across the candidate model configurations, we selected the optimal one by comparing root-mean-square errors (RMSEs). A lower RMSE shows a better model. Following known procedures from other studies, the computations are carried out using the R-package BAPC. The statistical program R (version 4.4.1) is used for all analyses and visualizations.

Result

According to the GBD study, there was a considerable rise in the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age-standardized mortality rate (ASMR), and age-standardized DALY rate (ASDR) of liver cancer from 1990 to 2010. However, the study’s findings supported the decline in age-standardized rates from 2010 to 2021. The results also emphasized the persistent differences between geographic regions and the quintiles of the SDI. The prevalence of liver cancer is still high worldwide in 2021, with a total of 739299.5 (673114.2–821948.2) cases and an ASPR of 8.7 (7.9–9.7) per 100000 cases. The ASIR is 6.2 (5.6–6.9) per 100000 cases, and the ASMR is 5.6 (5.1–6.3) per 100000 cases. Furthermore, the ASDR is 149.3 (135.2–167.5) per 100,000 cases. In terms of geographical comparison, low-medium SDI has the lowest ASDR, while high SDI has the highest ASPR, ASIR, and ASDR. Geographically, Southern Latin America has the lowest ASMR, ASPR, ASIR, and ASDR, whereas the high-incoming Asia Pacific area has the highest ASPR (Table 1, Figure 1).

Discussion

The results of the global liver cancer burden between 1990 and 2021 have shown a complex web of regional variations and patterns, especially when compared to socio-demographic indicators. ASIR, ASDR, ASPR, and ASMR for liver cancer showed an early rise before declining globally between 1990 and 2021. Regions with low-middle SDI had the lowest ASPR, ASIR, ASDR, and ASMR at the regional level, whereas those with high SDI had the greatest. In terms of geography, ASPR were lowest in Southern Latin America and highest in the high-income Asia-Pacific area. The persistent socioeconomic differences in the incidence of liver cancer worldwide are highlighted by these data. The absolute number of liver cancer diagnoses, events, fatalities, and DALY has significantly increased between 1990 and 2021, especially in industrialized North American nations. This rise is mostly caused by aging and population expansion, as well as epidemiological shifts in these areas, which raise the total burden of liver cancer by increasing the number of people afflicted. Additionally, risk factors like smoking, obesity, excessive alcohol use are becoming more prevalent, potentially contributing to the rising liver cancer burden [6,7]. Sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) bears the high liver cancer burden. Its high incidence is the result of a tangled web of chronic hepatitis B and C infection, constant exposure to aflatoxins, underlying genetic susceptibility and chronically under-resourced health systems. HBV and HCV provide the biological engine. Many West-African countries 8–14% of the population carry HBsAg, and the bulk of liver cancer cases can be traced to infections acquired at birth because birth-dose vaccine coverage is still below 50%. Aflatoxin B1 acts as an accelerator. The toxin contaminates dietary staples such as maize, groundnuts and sorghum under the region’s hot, humid storage conditions, and in HBV-positive individuals it multiplies the risk of liver cancer up to thirty-fold. In short, SSA’s liver cancer epidemic is a textbook example of an infectious disease colliding with an environmental poison, while weak health systems add insult to injury by leaving most patients diagnosed too late for any curative option.

These risk factors are more common in nations that are experiencing economic growth and adopting western foods, which raises the incidence of liver cancer and other non-communicable illnesses [8,9]. Better access to high-quality medical care leads to more liver cancer screenings, and as people age, the disease’s burden grows. Elderly people have the highest ASPR, ASIR, ASDR, and ASMR, in keeping with the known association between growing older and a higher risk of liver cancer. One possible explanation for the heightened load on older people is the long-term accumulation of risk factors. Males are also more likely than females to get liver cancer, especially in older adults. This could be attributed to the increased incidence of risk factors such as smoking and alcohol consumption among males. An analysis of risk factors indicates that the global burden of liver cancer is substantially affected by smoking, obesity, heavy alcohol consumption [10–12]. These risk variables’ effects varied by geography and SDI quintiles, underscoring the necessity of focused preventative tactics that take into account the unique risk factor profiles of various demographics [13–16].

Liver cancer arises from complex interactions between environmental exposures, viral pathogens, metabolic dysregulation, and genetic/epigenetic alterations. Chronic alcohol use alters gut microbiota, increasing endotoxin translocation and TLR4-mediated inflammation, which synergizes with viral hepatitis to accelerate fibrosis. Alcohol metabolites induce DNA hypermethylation, silencing tumor suppressor genes like p16 and RASSF1A. HBV X protein (HBx) disrupts lipid metabolism, exacerbating steatosis, while HCV core protein induces insulin resistance, accelerating NAFLD progression. Liver cancer is not merely the sum of individual risk factors but a dynamic disease state shaped by interactions between pathogens, metabolism, and the immune system. HBV, alcohol consumption, and HCV are the primary risk factors contributing to the global disease burden of liver cancer. To mitigate this burden, comprehensive intervention measures such as vaccination, alcohol control, HCV screening and treatment, multidisciplinary collaboration, and health education can be implemented. Globally, neonatal hepatitis B vaccination can reduce the risk of HBV infection by 90%, yet coverage remains as low as 17% in regions like Africa, necessitating legislative mandates for universal implementation. High-risk groups, including healthcare workers and sex workers, require regular vaccine booster shots to minimize horizontal transmission. Regional initiatives could involve universal hepatitis B virus screening for adults aged 18 and above, coupled with antiviral therapy to reduce the risks of cirrhosis and liver cancer. Antiviral treatment for HBV-positive pregnant women, combined with neonatal immunoglobulin administration, can significantly lower mother-to-child transmission rates. Raising the prices of alcoholic beverages can curb excessive drinking among low-income populations. Warning labels such as “Alcohol consumption increases liver cancer risk” should be mandated on alcohol packaging. Banning alcohol advertisements in media and public spaces can reduce exposure among adolescents. Strict alcohol abstinence orders should be enforced for patients with liver diseases to avoid the compounded risks of metabolic disorders and chronic alcohol use. Promoting healthy lifestyles, such as the Mediterranean diet, can slow the progression of alcoholic liver disease to cirrhosis. In HCV-endemic regions, free screening programs combined with direct-acting antiviral (DAA) therapies should be implemented. Nucleic acid testing for HCV in blood and organ donors is critical to prevent iatrogenic transmission. Strategies in high-income nations should include providing better dietary alternatives [17]. Campaigns to educate the public about the dangers of excessive alcohol use and its connection to liver cancer must be strengthened [18,19]. Air pollution may be addressed by boosting sustainable energy, improving public transit, and tightening emission regulations [20–22]. The growth in obesity may be halted by creating culturally relevant dietary standards and encouraging traditional, healthier eating habits [23–24]. In all situations, including liver cancer evaluation into primary care visits and emphasizing the early detection and treatment of modifiable risk factors are potential additional ways to lessen the burden of liver cancer. Future strategies must address these synergies through multi-modal interventions and context-specific policies to reduce global disparities in HCC burden.

It is important to take into account the limitations of this study when evaluating the findings. First, in different nations and areas, the quality and accessibility of data sources may have an impact on the estimation’s accuracy. An underestimation of the actual burden of liver cancer may result from the underreporting of cases and a lack of trustworthy epidemiological data in several low- and middle-income nations. In low-income countries, cause-of-death coding errors are not decimal-point noise. They are a systematic piston that shifts or compresses the entire mortality map. By funneling deaths into garbage codes, scrambling the causal sequence, or omitting the true underlying cause altogether, they force GBD’s redistribution algorithms to hard-wire the error into the final estimates. The resulting uncertainty intervals are far too narrow, distorting national rankings, time trends and resource priorities. By inserting a distinct “certification-and-coding uncertainty” layer into the Bayesian hierarchy of the GBD model can we give the measurement of health inequality a reliable ruler. Second, the estimation may be rather unclear due to the GBD methodology’s reliance on several assumptions and modeling approaches [25]. While sophisticated statistical techniques are used in GBD research to overcome these uncertainties, the findings should be regarded as the trustful estimates that are currently available.

In conclusion, there are still large differences in the absolute burden of liver cancer between nations, regions, and SDI areas, even if ASPR have decreased. To meet the unique demands of various populations, especially in low- and middle-income nations, the research findings highlight the necessity of focused preventive and treatment initiatives. Reducing socioeconomic gaps, encouraging healthy lifestyles, and fortifying healthcare systems are all crucial factors in lowering the incidence of liver cancer worldwide. Particularly in high-burden locations, future research should focus on determining and putting into practice the best liver cancer prevention and management strategies and regulations. Furthermore, research should concentrate on clarifying the intricate interactions that occur between lifestyle, environmental, and genetic variables in the development of liver cancer.
