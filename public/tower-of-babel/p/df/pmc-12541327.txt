The intestinal environment potentiates bacteriophage adaptation in response to phenotypic resistance in Salmonella Typhimurium
Authors: Nicolas Wenner, Anouk Bertola, Louise Larsson, Andrea Rocker, Nahimi Amare Bekele, Chris Sauerbeck, Leonardo F. Lemos Rocha, Valentin Druelle, Marco Molari, Alexander Harms, Médéric Diard
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SignificanceUnderstanding phage–bacteria coevolution is critical for the design of robust phage-based therapy. Heterogenous expression of surface glycan modifications known as phase variation allows populations of Salmonella Typhimurium to include both phenotypically phage-resistant and susceptible cells, enabling coexistence with certain virulent bacteriophages. We show that phase variation in Salmonella Typhimurium enables coexistence with phages in a liquid medium but only delays the emergence of genetically resistant mutants in the gut. The intestinal environment allows phages to evolve the ability to infect phenotypically resistant variants. This demonstrates how environmental factors in the gut influence phage evolution, emphasizing the importance to consider phage evolvability in natural settings when designing phage-based treatments.

Phenotypic heterogeneity, wherein subsets of cells exhibit distinct phenotypes from identical genomes, can have a decisive impact on the evolutionary dynamics of bacterial populations (1). Phenotypically tolerant cells can, for example, rescue populations exposed to lethal doses of antibiotics, thereby promoting the evolution of genetic resistance (2, 3). Here, we investigate the role of phenotypic heterogeneity in managing a widespread burden on bacterial fitness: the ubiquitous, diverse, and highly evolvable bacteriophages (phages).

Surface glycans including capsule, O-antigen, and cell-wall glycopolymers can hinder phage adsorption, thereby protecting the bacteria (4, 5). These glycans can be modified and the expression of glycan-modification systems is often regulated by epigenetic toggle switches, which fosters phenotypic heterogeneity (6). This process, known as phase variation, generates subsets of cells phenotypically resistant to phages affected by surface glycan modifications (7, 8). Every generation, cells can switch between phenotypically phage-susceptible and resistant states stochastically. This allows the coexistence of viruses with genetically susceptible host—a key aspect of virus–host interactions with significant implications for the design of effective phage therapy (9–13).

The coexistence with virulent phages is nevertheless costly to susceptible bacteria that lose a part of their population due to phage predation. Mathematical models predict that this cost should drive the selection of genetically resistant mutants (13). Moreover, the phages could also evolve the ability to overcome phenotypic resistance. Therefore, the evolutionary stability of the coexistence and the broader impact of phenotypic heterogeneity on phage–bacteria coevolution remains unclear.

To fill this knowledge gap, we studied the coevolution of a strain of Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (S.Tm) able to modify the composition of its O-antigen by phase variation and the virulent T5-like phage 37 (Ф37) (14). Given that the outcome of phage–bacteria coevolution depends on the ecological context (15), we followed the phage–bacteria coevolution in vitro and during colonization of the mouse intestinal tract. This is important because many ecological factors influence how phages and bacteria interact in the gut, rendering the evolutionary outcomes difficult to predict (16–19). S.Tm is also a model organism for entero-pathogens that are relevant targets for phage therapy in the intestinal tract, a promising but perfectible alternative to antibiotics requiring deeper mechanistic and evolutionary understanding (20).

We found that phase variation of the O-antigen can promote the coexistence of genetically susceptible S.Tm with Ф37. However, the selection regime in the intestinal tract of mice favors the accumulation of mutations in the phage that increase its host range to all O-antigen phase variants. This drives the rapid evolution of genetic resistance in S.Tm and destabilizes the coexistence of susceptible S.Tm with Ф37.

Results

O-Antigen Phase Variation in S.Tm Generates Phenotypic Resistance Against Ф37.

In nontyphoidal Salmonella, the outermost glycan layer is the O-antigen, a repetitive polymer of heteroglycans attached to the lipopolysaccharide (LPS) core that hides outer-membrane proteins (21). The reference strain S.Tm SL1344 (S.Tm WT) used in this study harbors two O-antigen modification systems controlled by epigenetic switches (Fig. 1A). First, the gtrABC operon encodes a glucosyltransferase that branches an extra glucose residue onto the galactose of the O-antigen backbone. This shifts the serotype of S.Tm from O:12 to O:12-2 (22). Second, the opvAB operon reduces the length of the O-antigen (23). Dam-dependent methylation of specific GATC sites in their respective promoters conditions the expression of these operons. The methylation patterns can change at every replication of the chromosome turning the expression ON or OFF. Importantly, silencing methylation patterns occur more frequently than patterns allowing expression, which means that most S.Tm WT cells do not express gtrABC or opvAB in the absence of selection (23, 24) (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A).

We first thoroughly characterized the impact of phase variation on the interaction between S.Tm and the T5-like virulent phage Ф37 in LB medium. The phage Ф37 can infect S.Tm cells that produce the canonical O-antigen serotype O:1,4[5],12 and, like most T5-like phages, uses the membrane protein BtuB as receptor (14). To assess the impact of O-antigen modifications on the replication of Ф37 we used constitutive expression of gtrABC [S.Tm gtrABCON (14)] and opvAB [S.Tm opvABON (7)]. We found that the expression of each of the two phase variation systems prevented the replication of Ф37 (Fig. 1B) by limiting phage adsorption (Fig. 1C). This was congruent with previous reports demonstrating that O-antigen glucosylation by GtrABC protects S.Tm against the T5-like phage SPC35 (8) and that O-antigen modification by OpvAB increases resistance to several phages (7).

Growth dynamics showed that S.Tm exposed to Ф37 quickly recovered after an initial decrease of the optical density (OD) (Fig. 1D). We hypothesized that the fast recovery was due to the growth of phenotypically resistant cells after killing of the population susceptible to the phage. To confirm the selection of phenotypically resistant ON cells by the phage, we monitored the expression of the transcriptional fusions gtrABC-lacZ and opvAB-gfp. As expected, the exposure to Ф37 shifted the serotype of S.Tm from O:12 to O:12-2 (SI Appendix, Fig. S1B) and increased the fraction of blue colonies on LB agar X-Gal (Fig. 1E and SI Appendix, Fig. S1C), meaning that gtrABC-lacZ was expressed. Pressure from the phage also increased the fraction of cells expressing gfp in S.Tm ΔgtrC opvAB-gfp (Fig. 1F). Accordingly, the constitutive expressions of gtrABC or opvAB fully protected S.Tm against the phage (SI Appendix, Fig. S1D). Moreover, when both O-antigen modification systems were deleted in the double mutant ΔgtrC ΔopvAB, the regrowth of the bacteria was either not observed at all or strongly delayed (Fig. 1D). The sequencing of isolated clones in these cases revealed the presence of Salmonella mutants that did not produce the wild-type BtuB (SI Appendix, Table S1). The selection of btuB mutants in the absence of gtrC and opvAB demonstrated that no other mechanism generating phenotypic resistance could protect S.Tm against Ф37 in these conditions. Moreover, the OFF to ON switch rate of gtrABC is several orders of magnitude higher than that of opvAB [SI Appendix, Fig. S1A (23, 24)]. This implies that there are more cells expressing gtrABC than cells expressing opvAB in the absence of selection (Fig. 1 E and F). Accordingly, we observed that gtrABC ON is the foremost phenotypic resistance state selected by Ф37. This is confirmed by the longer lag phase after killing in the ΔgtrC mutant compared to WT and the ΔopvAB mutant (Fig. 1D).

Phenotypic Resistance in S.Tm Promotes Coexistence With Ф37.

To further investigate the impact of phenotypic resistance on the evolutionary dynamics of S.Tm and Ф37, we performed serial passaging in LB medium generating a population bottleneck of 1/1,000 and enabling 10 bacterial generations per passage. Selective plating and plaque assays determined bacterial counts and phage titers respectively (Fig. 1 G and H). Phenotypic resistance in S.Tm WT, and in the single ΔgtrC and ΔopvAB mutants allowed phages and bacteria to coexist for at least 30 generations (i.e., three passages). In the double mutant ΔgtrC ΔopvAB, the impossibility to escape Ф37 by O-antigen phase variation led to the emergence of genetically resistant btuB mutants. In these cases, we observed a sharp decrease of the phage population unable to overcome the repeated bottlenecks (Fig. 1H). A similar trend was observed when phenotypic resistance was made constitutive, which drastically limits phage replication (SI Appendix, Fig. S1E). This is in accordance with the theoretical prediction that the ON to OFF switch rate must be high enough to generate a susceptible population of bacteria able to sustain phage replication as in S.Tm WT (13).

To confirm that phase variation prevents the selection of btuB mutants during phage exposure, we assessed the resistance to Ф37 in isolated clones of S.Tm after 3 passages by plaque assay (Fig. 1I and SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). We analyzed eight clones from two replicates without phage and six replicates exposed to phage. Ф37 produced turbid plaques when the lawns were generated from cells able to express gtrABC and pre-exposed to the phage during passaging. No visible plaques indicated genetic resistance via mutations in btuB.

DNA methylation profile analysis confirmed that lawns yielding turbid plaques contained mixtures of phenotypically resistant gtrABC ON cells and susceptible OFF cells (14) (SI Appendix, Fig. S2B). Moreover, no mutation was detected in the phages that could be responsible for the turbid plaque phenotype (SI Appendix, Table S2). The gtrABC ON cells were strongly enriched in turbid plaques and almost absent in lawns of bacteria yielding clear plaques when exposed to Ф37 (phenotypically susceptible clones).

Accordingly, Ф37 formed turbid plaques when blue clones expressing gtrABC-lacZ on LB agar X-Gal were used to generate lawns. Again, the DNA methylation profile revealed the enrichment of the gtrABC ON pattern in these lawns and in the turbid plaques (SI Appendix, Fig. S2C). No btuB mutants were detected in the turbid plaques (SI Appendix, Table S2).

No resistant clones were detected from the single mutant S.Tm ΔgtrC because OpvAB protects S.Tm from the phage (Fig. 1B). However, in this case, no turbid plaques were observed (Fig. 1I). While Ф37 could not infect S.Tm when it expresses opvAB in liquid LB medium (Fig. 1 B and C), the phage displayed clear plaques on the ΔgtrC opvABON strain (SI Appendix, Fig. S2A). This indicates that the opvAB-driven O-antigen modification protects S.Tm from phage infection in an environment-dependent manner and that the opvAB ON/OFF status cannot be deduced from the plaque morphology.

The fixation of resistant btuB mutants in S.Tm ΔgtrC ΔopvAB correlated with the sharp decrease of the phage titer (Fig. 1H), demonstrating that O-antigen phase variation is necessary to maintain high phage titers.

Longer experiments comprising 20 passages showed that the coexistence of genetically susceptible bacteria with phages mediated by phase variation could last for at least 200 bacterial generations in vitro (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B and Table S3). After 20 passages, the bacterial populations were dominated by phase variants yielding turbid plaques (SI Appendix, Fig. S3C) and harboring the methylation profile allowing expression of gtrABC (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 D and E). After 20 passages, S.Tm clones evolved in the presence or in the absence of phage displayed similar mutations (SI Appendix, Table S3). These mutations emerged independently of phage predation as an adaptation to growth in LB and no mutations in btuB were detected. Moreover, sequencing of turbid plaques using clones evolved in the presence of Ф37 for 20 passages revealed no btuB mutations (SI Appendix, Table S4), demonstrating again that O-antigen phase variation prevents the emergence of genetic resistance.

Taken together, these results indicate that S.Tm coexistence with phage Ф37 relies on the O-antigen phase variation. Here, we define coexistence as the ability of phage and susceptible bacteria to proliferate in the same environment without emergence of resistance mutations within the bacterial population.

Phenotypic Resistance Delays the Fixation of btuB Mutants When S.Tm Is Exposed to Ф37 During Intestinal Colonization.

Next, we investigated the impact of phase variation in the intestinal tract where habitat heterogeneity limits the physical interaction between bacteria and phages, which already favors the coexistence of phages with susceptible bacteria to some extent (19).

To study the phage–bacteria coevolution in the gut, we followed infections in mice during 10 d (Fig. 2A). For this, we used the attenuated strain S.Tm ΔssaV (S.Tm*) (25) and mice harboring a simplified intestinal microbiota that cannot exclude Salmonella (26). These conditions allow stable intestinal colonization by S.Tm* for 10 d (27). Importantly, following prolonged colonization, the adaptive immune response of the host interferes with the evolution of the bacteria. Immunoglobulin A is produced in mice in response to Salmonella infection after c.a. two weeks (28). The IgA neutralize Salmonella by binding to its O-antigen, which selects for phase variants and O-antigen mutants (14). The experiments were thus terminated at day 10 post infection to avoid this confounding evolutionary pressure.

Bacteria and phages were detected in homogenized fecal samples by selective plating (Fig. 2B) and plaque assay (Fig. 2C), respectively. We compared the outcomes of phage–bacteria coevolution using phase variable S.Tm* and the double mutant S.Tm* ΔgtrC ΔopvAB. Importantly, the deletion of ssaV in S.Tm* did not change the susceptibility to Ф37 compared to S.Tm WT (SI Appendix, Fig. S4) or the stability of the coexistence during repeated in vitro passages (SI Appendix, Fig. S3).

We first observed that the phage did not prevent the colonization of the gut by S.Tm*. The intestinal Salmonella loads were comparable with and without phase variation (Fig. 2B). On the other hand, the phage titers decreased significantly faster in the absence of phenotypic resistance when S.Tm* ΔgtrC ΔopvAB was the prey. We performed plaque assays to test the resistance of isolated Salmonella clones after 1, 4, 7, and 10 d of infection. Twelve clones from eight mice per condition were analyzed (Fig. 2D and SI Appendix, Fig. S5). The fixation of resistant btuB mutants in S.Tm* ΔgtrC ΔopvAB correlated with the decline of the phage loads (Fig. 2 D, Right panel), as observed in vitro (Fig. 1I). Intriguingly, although the phage–bacteria coexistence was more stable with S.Tm*, the phage titers eventually diminished in half of the mice after 8 d. This was also concomitant with the rise of resistant btuB mutants (Fig. 2 D, Left panel).

We verified that O-antigen phase variation in fully virulent S.Tm derivatives also delayed the fixation of resistant btuB mutants under phage pressure, including in ΔgtrC and ΔopvAB single mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). For this, we performed short-term infections in conventional C57BL/6 mice pretreated with streptomycin to allow robust intestinal colonization by S.Tm (29). Exhausted LB was used as mock treatment in control groups without phage (SI Appendix, Fig. S6A). The bacterial and phage loads were determined in fecal samples for three days (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 B and C). Twenty Salmonella clones per mouse were analyzed after 3 d post-infection. Turbid plaques formed on clones from the control group without phage showed that the expression of gtrABC might be slightly advantageous in the gut in the absence of Ф37 (SI Appendix, Fig. S6D). However, gtrABC was expressed at higher frequencies in clones exposed to the phage (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D and F). No resistant mutants (i.e., clones generating no plaque) were observed in control groups. In most phage-treated mice, phenotypic resistance via expression of gtrABC or opvAB prevented the fixation of resistant mutants (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 D–F) that were otherwise overrepresented in mice infected by the double mutant S.Tm ΔgtrC ΔopvAB (SI Appendix, Fig. S6G).

We concluded that phenotypic resistance mediated by O-antigen phase variation plays an important evolutionary role in the intestinal tract when S.Tm is exposed to phages: It protects Salmonella from quickly losing the conserved vitamin B12 transporter BtuB, receptor of Ф37. However, long infections also showed that the coexistence of the phage with the susceptible bacteria was short-lived, as genetically resistant btuB mutants eventually emerged in phase-variable populations. The next step was to understand this phenomenon by focusing on the evolution of the phage during infection.

Accumulation of Mutations in the Lateral Tail Fiber Protein Increases the Host-Range of Ф37.

The observation of different plaque morphologies made by phages isolated from mice infected with S.Tm* suggested the emergence of Ф37 variants (Fig. 3A). Sequencing indeed revealed nonsynonymous mutations in these phages compared to the ancestor. Mutations were particularly frequent within the ltf gene, encoding the lateral tail fiber protein (Ltf) (Fig. 3B). Except for a short deletion (mutation ΔA56-A97), all the nonsynonymous mutations in ltf were found between residues 468 and 556. To further characterize the accumulation of mutations in ltf, we sequenced amplicons of the ltf locus between residues 463 and 570. We randomly picked a set of 10 phages from each of the 16 mice presented in Fig. 2 on the last day phages were detected. Strikingly, all the phages in mice infected with S.Tm* had a mutated ltf allele (Dataset S1). By contrast, in mice infected with the ΔgtrC ΔopvAB mutant (no O-antigen phase variation), only three mutations, involving Ltf residues 469 and 556, were identified and 27.5% (22/80) of the phages harbored the ancestor locus. These mutations were also frequent after 20 passages in vitro (SI Appendix, Table S3).

The whole genome sequencing of a subset of evolved phages confirmed the accumulation of mutations in ltf (SI Appendix, Table S5) and revealed additional mutations in the rest of the phage genome (Dataset S2). Taken together, we have identified 10 altered ltf alleles in phages from mice infected with S.Tm* and only 3 variants in mice infected with S.Tm* ΔgtrABC ΔopvAB (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A). Some variants coexisted in the same mouse (e.g., evolved phages Ф37_AB288_1 and AB288_9). Phage populations were also sequenced at day 4 and 8 post-infection, revealing the frequency of mutated alleles (SI Appendix, Table S6). From day 4 to day 8 p.i., we detected an accumulation of mutations (present in >50% of the reads) in all phage populations. Mutations were present in various genes but especially in the ltf.

The lateral tail fiber is crucial in T5-like phages for specific host recognition via the reversible binding to O-antigen glycans (30). We therefore hypothesized that Ф37 accumulates mutations in ltf to overcome phenotypic resistance from phase variation of the O-antigen. Compared to the ancestor Ф37, phages evolved on S.Tm* in vivo were indeed better at infecting S.Tm gtrABCON (Fig. 3C) and/or S.Tm ΔgtrC opvABON (Fig. 3C and SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). None of the evolved phages were able to infect a btuB mutant (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C).

To demonstrate that the increased infectivity was caused by mutations in ltf only, we reconstructed a subset of ltf mutations in the ancestor Ф37 background (SI Appendix, Fig. S8 A and B) (31). Whole genome sequencing of the reconstructed phages confirmed the presence of the desired ltf mutations and the absence of other mutations (Dataset S2). The infectivity of all reconstructed ltf mutants was improved on gtrABCON bacteria compared to the ancestor phage (SI Appendix, Fig. S8C). Certain mutants were slightly less efficient at infecting S.Tm ΔgtrC opvABON than the ancestor, suggesting another trade-off that could explain the coexistence of different evolved phages in some mice.

Given that GtrABC generates a substantial modification of the O-antigen of S.Tm that leads to serotype shift, we hypothesized that the accumulation of mutations in ltf could alter the host range of Ф37 not only regarding S.Tm phase-variants but also other Salmonella serovars. To test this, we measured the infectivity of Ф37 and the ltf mutants on various Salmonella serovars including Enteritidis and Gallinarum (both O:9), Limete (a Typhimurium-like O:4), Senftenberg (O:1,3,19), Choleraesuis (O:7), Anatum (O:3,10), and Newport (O:8) (32). Interestingly, some evolved phages were better at infecting S. Newport (Fig. 3D and SI Appendix, Fig. S9). However, these alleles drastically reduced the ability to infect S. Gallinarum, although not Enteritidis, both O9 (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). All evolved phages also lost their ability to infect S. Senftenberg compared to ancestor Ф37. S. Anatum and S. Choleraesuis were fully resistant to all the tested phages.

In summary, these findings demonstrate that Ф37 has the capacity to adapt to O-antigen modifications by altering the lateral tail fiber protein through mutations. This alteration of the phage host range extends beyond the infection of different phase variants of S.Tm and implies trade-offs. For instance, it can enhance the ability to infect alternative hosts like S. Newport while simultaneously reducing infectivity on other hosts like S. Gallinarum and Senftenberg.

Evolved Phages With Broad Host-Range Drive Rapid Evolution of btuB Mutants During Infection.

We asked whether the ability of the evolved phages to kill the phase variants accelerates the fixation of resistant S.Tm btuB mutants (Fig. 4). We compared the outcome of S.Tm* evolution in mice with either the ancestor Ф37 or a mixture of evolved phages isolated from experiments described in Fig. 2 and characterized in Fig. 3. We mixed two evolved phages isolated from the same mouse infected by S.Tm* (Evolved phages Ф37_AB288_1 and AB288_9). We hypothesized that evolved phages could coexist due to their distinct abilities to infect the different S.Tm phase variants, together consequently promoting the fixation of btuB mutants. Like the ancestor, the evolved phages did not reduce the population size of S.Tm* in the gut (Fig. 4B). However, the intestinal loads of the evolved phages were decreasing faster than the ancestor (Fig. 4C). This correlated with the rapid appearance of resistant btuB mutants in the S.Tm* population exposed to the evolved phages (Fig. 4D and SI Appendix, Fig. S10), demonstrating that phages able to kill S.Tm despite phase variation favor genetically resistant mutants, which, in turn, impairs the replication of the phages in the infected mice.

The In Vitro Coexistence of Phages With Susceptible Bacteria Is Destabilized in Viscous Media.

The coexistence of Ф37 with susceptible S.Tm in LB medium (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) was contrasting with the selection for resistant btuB mutants in the gut (Figs. 2 and 3). To understand context dependency of coexistence mediated by phase variation, we first controlled for the impact of losing BtuB on the fitness of S.Tm in vitro and in the gut (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). We did not detect any significant effect on growth in LB (SI Appendix, Fig. S11A) or during infection in mice in the absence of Ф37 (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 B and C). This confirmed that the selection for btuB mutants in mice but not in LB was a consequence of the selective pressure from evolved phages in the gut. The next step was therefore to understand why evolved phages able to infect the phase-variants emerged in the gut and not in LB.

Intriguingly, we observed differences between the two methods used to measure phage infectivity in vitro, namely the plaque assays in LB agar and the growth curves in liquid LB. In comparison with the ancestor phage, the in vivo evolved phages Φ37_AB288_1 and Φ37_AB288_9 had an increased infectivity on S.Tm gtrABCON grown in soft agar (plaque assays, SI Appendix, Fig. S12A), while only the ancestor Φ37 and the Φ37_AB288_9 variant could infect effectively the opvABON strain. This contrasted strongly with phage infectivity observed in liquid LB cultures (growth curves, SI Appendix, Fig. S12B), where none of the Φ37 variants displayed detectable infectivity on S.Tm gtrABCON or opvABON. Φ37_AB288_1 displayed a weaker infectivity on S.Tm carrying unmodified O-antigen (ΔgtrC ΔopvAB), revealing a trade-off between infecting unmodified S.Tm and infecting the phase variants.

Differences in the infectivity of evolved phages in liquid and soft-agar media suggested that the presence of polymers that constrain the movement of bacteria and the diffusion of phages revealed the effect of mutations in Ltf. The gut contains high molecular weight polymers, for example mucin, that make the mucus particularly viscous (33). Therefore, we hypothesized that the viscosity in the gut might influence the phage–bacteria coevolution. To test this hypothesis, we aimed to recapitulate in vitro the evolution of the S.Tm and Φ37 observed in infected mice. For this, S.Tm WT was passaged in the presence of Φ37 in LB and in LB containing 4% polyvinylpyrrolidone (m = 360 kDa, hereafter medium LB + PVP), a polymer that has been used to investigate the effects of viscosity on bacterial motility (34–37) (Fig. 5 A–F). Here, we diluted the cultures 1/100 in order to also reduce the population bottleneck between passages compared to the experiments presented in Fig. 1 J and H and SI Appendix, Fig. S3 A and B (dilution 1/1,000). In this setup, the coexistence of Φ37 with susceptible S.Tm was stable in LB for at least 20 passages (Fig. 5 A–C). In contrast, after eight passages in LB + PVP, we observed a reduction of the Φ37 titers (Fig. 5E) concomitant with the apparition of resistant S.Tm mutants (Fig. 5F).

We hypothesized that, as observed in the gut, resistance evolution in LB + PVP was driven by the evolution of Φ37 host-range due to mutations in Ltf. We sequenced the populations of S.Tm and Φ37 at passage 8, focusing on mutations affecting the phage Ltf and the bacterial receptor BtuB (SI Appendix, Table S7). In the phage populations evolved in LB + PVP, we observed the fixation of the Ltf H468Y mutation (48-99% of the reads) and several rarer insertions and deletions. In addition to the fixed H468Y mutation, the sequencing of ltf from isolated phages (SI Appendix, Fig. S13A) confirmed several of these deletions and insertions, including a 28 amino acid insertion at position 164 (variant Φ37_95_2, Fig. 5G), that was found in isolated phage coming from different biological replicates. By contrast, in the population of Φ37 passaged in LB with S.Tm, only mutations on residue 469 (S469Y or S469F) were frequent (>40% of the reads) in all the replicates, while only one replicate displayed a G491A mutation in 20% of the reads. S.Tm population sequencing at passage 8 also confirmed the emergence of genetic resistance in LB + PVP, by mutations in btuB (SI Appendix, Table S7).

To demonstrate that the specific Ltf mutations found in vivo and in LB + PVP are causing the destabilization of Φ37-S.Tm coexistence, the ancestral Φ37, in vivo evolved phage variants (Fig. 5G) and LB + PVP evolved phage variants were passaged in the presence of S.Tm WT in LB + PVP. The coexistence was destabilized at passages 5 and 6 respectively with in vivo or LB + PVP-evolved phages (Fig. 5 H and I). By contrast, destabilization was only observed at passage 8 with the ancestral phage. Consistently, we observed earlier fixation of genetic resistance in S.Tm in the presence of in vivo evolved and LB + PVP evolved phages, indicating that the Ltf mutations are driving the destabilization of the coexistence in viscous environments (Fig. 5 J–L).

Further, we tested the infectivity of these Φ37 variants on S.Tm WT, ΔgtrC ΔopvAB, gtrABCON, or opvABON in LB and LB + PVP using growth monitoring. Because low phage-dependent killing can be difficult to detect by the sole measure of the optical density, we inserted gfp upstream of the pre-early gene A1 (38) of each Φ37 variant (SI Appendix, Fig. S13B) and we used the double read out OD600 and GFP fluorescence to monitor phage infectivity. In LB, none of the Φ37 variants had a detectable infectivity on the gtrABCON or opvABON strains (SI Appendix, Fig. S13 C–E). By contrast, in LB + PVP, we detected infectivity by all phages on the gtrABCON strain (SI Appendix, Fig. S14 A–C). Only the in vivo evolved Φ37_AB288_9 had a moderate increased infectivity on the opvABON strain in LB + PVP. For phage AB288_1 and 95_2, we observed a reduced infectivity on opvABON. Plaque assays with the same Ltf variants (SI Appendix, Fig. S14D) displayed similar results and showed increased infectivity of all PVP and in vivo evolved phages on gtrABCON strains and a reduced infectivity of variants AB288_1 and 95_2 on opvABON strains.

Taken together, our data indicate that Φ37 acquired Ltf variations in LB that were not sufficient to destabilize phage–bacteria coexistence. In contrast, in the gut and in the viscous LB + PVP medium, O-antigen modifications did not totally inhibit phage infection, permitting the phage adaptation through the selection of specific Ltf mutations. This allows Φ37 to overcome Salmonella O-antigen phase variation, destabilizing the coexistence of phages with susceptible bacteria.

Discussion

In the intestinal tract, the serotype shift due to phase variation of the O-antigen enables S.Tm to escape neutralization by the adaptive immune system and promotes chronic colonization of the host (14, 22). Nevertheless, when a polyvalent vaccine renders phase variation futile, S.Tm further adapts by producing a very short O-antigen at the cost of reducing its virulence and its resistance to environmental stress (14). This demonstrates that phase variation can prevent the evolution of detrimental mutations in Salmonella. However, the primary role of O-antigen modifications is to interfere with phage infection (5, 7, 8), therefore we reasoned that phase variation should affect the phage–bacteria interaction in the gut by safeguarding the bacteria from evolving potentially costly phage resistance, as it does against the adaptive immune system (14). The results presented in this study confirm this intuition: Phenotypic resistance mediated by phase variation of the O-antigen delays the emergence of resistance in the intestinal tract. Along those lines, virulent phages select for phase variation in glycans produced by the intestinal bacteria Bacteroides thetaiotaomicron (39) and Bacteroides intestinalis, which favors the persistence of virulent phages like crAss001 in the gut (40).

However, here, we found that genetic resistance eventually emerged in S.Tm exposed to Ф37 in the gut. This occurred more rapidly in the absence of protective phase variation in the double mutant ΔgtrC ΔopvAB, or after phages adapted to infect the phase variants than when S.Tm WT was exposed to the ancestor phage. In the absence of phase variation, resistant mutants became predominant in as little as three days of within-host growth (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Fig. S6), revealing the strong selective pressure that phages can exert on S.Tm in the intestinal environment. The mutations underlying resistance altered BtuB, with minimal redundancy observed between independent experiments (SI Appendix, Table S1). No mutational hot spot was necessary for this fast evolution, rather favored by the fact that multiple loss of function mutations can lead to resistance. While the phage did not affect the population size of S.Tm in the gut, whether phase variation was possible or not, these findings imply that phages could effectively counterselect traits that favor pathogenic bacteria, like virulence factors or antibiotic resistance, as long as this is not immediately costly to the bacteria, a concept called “phage steering” (41).

Preceding the evolution of resistance in S.Tm WT, in vivo evolved phages acquired mutations in the lateral tail fiber protein that interacts with the O-antigen and determines the host range (42) (SI Appendix, Table S6). Mutations that change residues 469 and 556, detected in evolved phages (SI Appendix, Tables S5 and S6 and Datasets S1 and S2), also emerged in mice infected with the double mutant S.Tm ΔgtrABC ΔopvAB. These mutations probably provided an advantage independently from phenotypic resistance, presumably by increasing infectivity on cells producing the unmodified O-antigen. However, when evolving in the presence of S.Tm WT, the phage acquired additional mutations in the lateral tail fiber protein that further improved the ability to kill one or the other of the phase variants, gtrABC ON or opvAB ON. The evolution of multiple phage ltf variants in the same population with different abilities to infect unmodified S.Tm cells, gtrABC ON, or opvAB ON phase-variants drives the evolution of genetic resistance (SI Appendix, Figs. S12–S14).

Restricting the host-range to the unmodified S.Tm cells could be a prudent exploitation of the bacteria as it ensures a stable high load of phages, as observed for 200 bacterial generations during serial passaging in LB medium (SI Appendix, Fig. S3) (43). This was different in the intestine where, by killing the phase variants, evolved phages selected for resistant btuB mutants, which destabilized the phage population (Figs. 2 and 4). Interestingly, the rise of btuB mutants did not systematically lead to the extinction of phages, even in mice where they apparently reached fixation. This may be because the intestinal tract is a complex and structured environment that forms a heterogeneous habitat, where susceptible S.Tm clones could persist at low frequency and fuel limited phage replication (19, 44–46). None of the evolved phages was able to infect a btuB mutant, suggesting that in this context the coevolution stops after one cycle with the fixation of resistant bacteria (SI Appendix, Fig. S7C). However, evolved Ф37 was also able to infect Salmonella serovar Newport more efficiently (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). Some evolved phages may therefore thrive in complex microbial communities in which multiple strains of Salmonella or related species like Escherichia coli could be alternative hosts.

The fixation of genetic resistance to the phage observed in the gut but not in liquid LB, cannot be attributed to a fitness cost of the btuB mutation in LB medium or to a benefit in the gut, as the btuB deletion was neutral in both conditions (SI Appendix, Fig. S11). Instead, we found that the evolution of Φ37 in response to S.Tm O-antigen phase variation is context-dependent and that viscosity seems to play an important role. Despite phenotypic resistance, Salmonella was slightly more susceptible to Φ37 infection in viscous medium (LB + PVP), which allowed rapid phage evolution that destabilized coexistence (Fig. 5 and SI Appendix, Figs. S13 and S14). Therefore, we propose that viscous LB + PVP better mimics the conditions in the gut, leading to similar phage–bacteria coevolution. This finding could be relevant in other environments, as phages often encounter their bacterial prey in structured environments, such as biofilms or microcolonies (47), where polymers are present (48).

The biophysical mechanism of the effect of increasing viscosity on Salmonella-Φ37 coevolution remains nevertheless to be investigated to also clarify how this could work in the gut. One hypothesis is that polymers, like the mucus in the gut, may slightly facilitate phage adsorption by reducing bacterial motility. A prolonged transient interaction between Ltf and the O-antigen could potentiate the effect of mutations in the Ltf that increase the host-range of the phage, thereby enabling phage evolution toward negating phase variation. On the other hand, it is also possible that the presence of polymers modulates bacterial physiology (e.g., the biosynthesis of LPS or of the phage receptor), leading to an increased susceptibility to the phage.

The swift adaptation of phages to phase variation observed in this study implies that phages can be trained via experimental evolution to bypass phenotypic resistance in enteropathogens (49). Our study highlights the importance of choosing relevant culture conditions for phage training in order to increase their in vivo infectivity. The selection regime in the gut increases the pace of phage evolution when compared to in vitro passages in LB. As we observed similar evolutionary trajectories in LB+PVP and in the murine gut, we propose that increasing the viscosity of the medium better recapitulates the conditions in vivo and could be used to evolve phages in the laboratory in order to overcome phenotypic resistance.
