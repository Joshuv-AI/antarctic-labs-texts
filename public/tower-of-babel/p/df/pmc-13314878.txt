Re-evaluation of the prognosis of patients with IgA nephropathy and mild proteinuria: a longitudinal study with unexpected results
Authors: Xiuhua Ma, Sen Zhang, Wenhui Chen, Xiaoling Zhou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIgA nephropathy (IgAN) is the most common primary glomerulonephritis worldwide and a major cause of chronic kidney disease. Mild proteinuria (< 0.5 g/24 h) is generally considered to indicate a favorable prognosis in IgAN. This study aimed to explore the clinicopathological features and prognostic factors of such patients.MethodWe performed a retrospective analysis of IgAN patients who had renal biopsies at our hospital from 2008 to 2023. We included patients with 24-hour proteinuria < 0.5 g, age ≥ 15 years, and follow-up ≥ 12 months. Risk factors were assessed using logistic regression and Cox proportional hazards models.ResultsOver a mean follow-up of 90.75months, 27 patients(10.19%) experienced endpoint events. The overall eGFR slope was − 2.07mL/min/1.73 m2 per year. Greater eGFR decline correlated with lower 10-year renal survival. Multivariate analysis showed severe tubulointerstitial damage, lower baseline eGFR and steeper eGFR decline were independent risk factors for renal endpoint events. Renal arteriolar lesions, lower baseline eGFR, steeper eGFR decline, and lower TyG-BMI were risk factors for poor renal prognosis.ConclusionsOur study challenges the assumption that IgAN patients with mild proteinuria have a favorable prognosis. Over 90.75 months, 10.19% of patients experienced endpoint events, with an eGFR decline of 2.07 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year. These findings indicate significant disease progression risk in IgAN patients with mild proteinuria. In addition to traditional risk factors, metabolic factors such as TyG-BMI are important prognostic predictors.

Introduction

IgA nephropathy (IgAN) is a leading cause of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and end-stage renal disease (ESRD) [1]. Proteinuria is a key predictor of IgAN prognosis and is used as a surrogate endpoint in IgAN management [2, 3]. The definition of optimal proteinuria control targets continues to be debated in nephrology.The KDIGO 2024 guidelines suggest reducing proteinuria to below 0.5 g/24h [4]. However, recent studies show that IgAN can still progress even with proteinuria below this level [5]. A UK study found that 20% of patients with time-averaged proteinuria < 0.44 g/g developed kidney failure within 10 years after diagnosis [6]. Similarly, a Chinese study showed that 28% of IgAN patients had proteinuria < 0.5 g/24 h. The 15-year kidney survival rate was 86% in the 0.3–0.5 g/24 h proteinuria subgroup, with a 1.08-fold higher ESRD progression risk compared to the < 0.3 g/24 h subgroup [7]. These findings suggest that IgAN patients with mild proteinuria (< 0.5 g/24 h) may still face significant clinical risks.

Current strategies for IgAN diagnosis and treatment focus on proteinuria levels and renal pathology. Patients with proteinuria ≥ 0.5 g/24 h are considered high-risk, while those with mild proteinuria (< 0.5 g/24 h) are often overlooked. This study aims to analyze the clinicopathological features and prognosis of IgAN patients with mild proteinuria, providing insights for clinical management.

Materials and methods

Study population

This retrospective study included 265 IgAN patients diagnosed by biopsy at Ningxia Medical University General Hospital from August 2008 to December 2023. To be included, patients needed to have 24-hour proteinuria < 0.5 g at biopsy, be at least 15 years old, and have at least 12 months of follow-up. We excluded patients with secondary IgAN, other kidney diseases, baseline steroid or immunosuppressive treatment, or eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2. The screening process is shown in Fig. 1.

Clinical and laboratory parameters

Clinical and laboratory parameters included age, gender, blood pressure, body mass index (BMI), triglycerides (TG), cholesterol (Chol), serum creatinine (Scr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid (UA), serum immunoglobulin A (IgA), serum complements C3 and C4, and 24-hour urinary protein quantification. eGFR was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration (CKD-EPI) Eq. [8], with CKD staging determined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) classification system [4]; CKM health stages were classified per American Heart Association (AHA) criteria [9]; and the TyG-BMI index was calculated using the formula by Er et al. [10].

Evaluation of histopathology

All renal biopsy tissues from enrolled IgAN patients were evaluated using a standardized protocol, including light microscopy, immunofluorescence, and electron microscopy. The biopsy specimens were independently scored by two renal pathologists according to the IgAN Oxford Classification (MEST-C) [11] and graded using to the Lee classification system [12]. Renal arteriolar lesions were evaluated as follows: 0 indicates no significant lesions; 1 indicates presence of significant lesions, including vascular wall thickening and hyalinosis.

Treatment and follow-up records

Medication profiles at baseline (renal biopsy date) and during follow-up were systematically recorded, including angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and angiotensin II receptor blockers (ACEI/ARB), glucocorticoids, and immunosuppressants.

The eGFR slope for each patient was calculated using least squares linear regression of eGFR values against time during follow-up [13]. Renal function progression was categorized into four groups based on eGFR slope [14, 15]: ① Stable renal function: eGFR slope ≥ 0 mL/min/1.73 m2/year; ② Mild renal decline: eGFR slope 0 to -1 mL/min/1.73 m2/year; ③ Moderate renal decline: eGFR slope − 1 to -5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year; ④ Rapid progression : eGFR slope < -5 mL/min/1.73 m2/year. Patients were also stratified into two groups based on the occurrence of composite renal endpoint events, with baseline clinicopathological characteristics were compared.

Endpoints

All patients were followed up until December 31, 2024. The primary endpoint was all-cause mortality. The secondary endpoint was a composite renal outcome defined as either: (a) a ≥ 30% sustained decline in eGFR from baseline, or (b) progression to ESRD(defined as eGFR < 15 mL/min/1.73 m2).

Statistical analyses

Data analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Chicago, IL, USA). Normally distributed continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using t-tests. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as median (interquartile range, IQR) and compared using the rank sum test. Categorical data were presented as numbers (percentages) and compared using the chi-squared test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. Risk factors for composite renal endpoint events were analyzed using binary logistic regression, and risk factors for composite renal outcomes were analyzed using Cox proportional hazards regression. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Prognostic analysis of IgAN patients with mild proteinuria

Overall prognostic characteristics

Over a mean follow-up of 90.75 ± 52.83 months, 27 patients experienced endpoint events. These included 2 deaths (1 due to esophageal cancer, 1 due to depression) and 25 cases meeting the composite renal endpoint. The composite renal endpoints consisted of 10 patients developing ESRD and 15 patients showing a > 30% decline in eGFR from baseline (details in Table 4).

At baseline, 170 patients (64.15%) received ACEI/ARB therapy, and 95 patients (35.85%) were treated with traditional Chinese medicine (details in Table 1). During follow-up, hormone therapy was initiated in 2 additional patients, and immunosuppressant therapy was added in another 2 patients.

The overall eGFR slope after kidney biopsy was − 2.07 ± 5.64 ml/min/1.73 m2/year. The stable renal function group comprised 94 patients (35.47%), the mild renal decline group included 52 patients (19.62%), the moderate renal decline group consisted of 81 patients (30.57%), and the rapid progression group included 38 patients (14.34%).

The forest plot showed that a more rapid eGFR decline over 6-195 months of follow-up was associated with lower 10-year kidney survival rates. Patients with an eGFR slope ≥ -1 ml/min/1.73 m2/year had a 10-year kidney survival rate of 1.00 (95% CI: 1.00, 1.00), while those with a slope < -5 ml/min/1.73 m2/year had a 10-year kidney survival rate of 0.31 (95% CI: 0.15, 0.68) (Fig. 2).

Univariate and multivariable cox regression analyses of renal outcomes in IgAN with mild proteinuria

Univariate Cox regression analysis of 263 IgAN patients with mild proteinuria revealed that age, hypertension history, diastolic blood pressure, CKM 2 and above, uric acid, eGFR, eGFR slope, S1 lesions, T1 + T2 lesions, and renal arteriolar lesions were all significant predictors of poor renal outcomes (all P < 0.05)(Table 7).

Based on clinical relevance, gender, age, hypertension, diastolic blood pressure, CKM 2 and above, serum uric acid, eGFR, eGFR slope, IgA / C3, S1, T1 + T2, renal arteriolar lesions, and TyG-BMI were included in the multivariate Cox regression analysis. The results showed that renal arteriolar lesions (HR = 3.531, 95% CI 1.186–10.507, P = 0.023), lower eGFR (HR = 0.960, 95% CI 0.928–0.992, P = 0.015), steeper eGFR decline (HR = 0.897, 95% CI 0.856–0.940, P = 0.000), and lower TyG-BMI (HR = 0.979, 95% CI 0.964–0.996, P = 0.013) were independent risk factors for poor renal prognosis in IgAN patients with mild proteinuria(Table 7).

Discussion

We retrospectively analyzed the clinical and histological findings at the time of renal biopsy in 265 IgAN patients with mild proteinuria. There were slightly more male patients than female ones, and the age of onset ranged from 15 to 68 years, which was consistent with previous studies [16]. 61.51% of the patients were in CKD stage 1, and 38.49% were in CKD stages 2–3, indicating that impaired renal function at the time of renal biopsy is not uncommon in IgA nephropathy patients with mild proteinuria. In our study, 79.62% of the patients had CKM syndrome, consistent with the high prevalence reported by AHA [9], with most in CKM stage 2 (68.30%). AHA recommends that management of stage 2 CKM should focus on lifestyle modifications and pharmacotherapy targeting modifiable risk factors to prevent cardiovascular diseases and ESRD. These recommendations may inform the clinical management of IgAN patients with mild proteinuria.

It was previously believed that IgAN patients with mild proteinuria exhibit mild renal pathological changes [17]. However, this study found that as many as 45.28% of patients had renal pathological lesions classified as Lee’s grade III-V. The Oxford classification showed that approximately one-third of the patients had segmental glomerulosclerosis/ adhesion, tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis, and cellular or fibrocellular crescents to varying degrees, among which the proportion of crescent formation was as high as 35.09%. However, previous studies have shown that these histological parameters are associated with a decrease in eGFR or progression to ESRD [18]. Di et al. [19] analyzed 183 IgAN patients in whom glomerular crescents accounted for < 50%, and found that renal survival was significantly shortened in those with a crescent proportion > 10%. A multicenter retrospective study in 2017 found that both C1 and C2 were risk factors for progression to ESRD or eGFR decline ≥ 50% [20]. Combining these classifications reveals that renal pathological injury in IgAN patients with mild proteinuria is not mild in this cohort; severe lesions may occur, with active and chronic lesions coexisting. This suggests that IgAN with mild proteinuria can progress and requires active treatment.

Currently, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) for IgAN use proteinuria as a surrogate endpoint and typically include patients with a baseline proteinuria level ≥ 0.5 g/24 h. Previous studies suggested that IgAN patients with mild proteinuria progress slowly and have a favorable long-term prognosis [7, 21]. However, our study found that during a mean follow-up of 90.75 ± 52.83 months, 64.15% of patients received ACEI or ARB treatment immediately after renal biopsy, yet 27 patients (10.19%) reached the composite endpoint. The overall eGFR slope after kidney biopsy was − 2.07 ± 5.64 ml/min/1.73 m2/year. The greater the eGFR decline, the lower the 10-year renal survival rate. These results indicate that IgAN cannot be regarded as a benign disease even when proteinuria is < 0.5 g/24 h. David et al. [6] showed that if IgAN patients fail to limit the eGFR decline rate to ≤ 1 mL/min/1.73 m2/year, they are all at risk of progressing to ESRD within their lifetime. However, the authors emphasized that current therapies fall far short of achieving this goal. Therefore, strategies to achieve this target must be explored (e.g., earlier diagnosis, interventions for patients with lower proteinuria levels, novel agents, and combination therapies).

In our study, patients who reached the renal endpoint had higher proportions of hypertension, T1 + T2 lesions, and renal arteriolar lesions at the start. They also had worse kidney function, faster eGFR drop, and higher uric acid levels. We found that T1 + T2 lesions, lower starting eGFR, and faster eGFR drop were key predictors of these events.

Our analysis showed that renal arteriolar lesions, lower starting eGFR, faster eGFR drop, and lower TyG-BMI were linked to a worse kidney outcome in IgAN patients with mild proteinuria. Interestingly, mild proteinuria levels themselves didn’t predict kidney outcomes.

Previous studies have shown that a lower starting eGFR is a key sign of kidney function decline and a poor prognosis in IgAN [13, 22, 23]. Meanwhile, the importance of eGFR slope in predicting the progression of renal function and prognosis in IgAN has attracted increasing attention [6, 13]. Our study supports this and shows that a lower eGFR and faster eGFR drop are still important predictors of disease progression and poor prognosis in IgAN patients with mild proteinuria.

TyG-BMI is a novel, simple, and efficient biomarker for assessing insulin resistance (IR) and predicting metabolic syndrome. As a common and early pathological alteration in chronic kidney disease (CKD), IR contributes to disease progression by exacerbating metabolic disorders, inflammatory responses, and renal fibrosis. Notably, IR can be present even when the glomerular filtration rate (GFR) remains within the normal range [25, 26]. A previous study by our team demonstrated that the prevalence of metabolic abnormalities in patients with IgA nephropathy is positively correlated with the severity of clinical manifestations and poor renal prognosis. Chen’s study [27] reported that both elevated and reduced levels of the TyG-BMI index were associated with an increased risk of adverse clinical outcomes in patients with chronic kidney disease. Our study confirms this finding and further specifies that a lower baseline TyG-BMI is independently associated with adverse renal outcomes in IgAN patients with mild proteinuria.

Tubular atrophy and interstitial fibrosis collectively represent a crucial pathological hallmark in the progression of chronic kidney disease to end-stage renal disease. The development of renal interstitial fibrosis can be driven by diverse etiologies, including vascular, metabolic, and immune system abnormalities, as well as genetic factors. Among these, metabolic dysregulation plays a significant role in both initiating and advancing fibrosis, primarily through mechanisms such as oxidative stress, activation of the transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signaling pathway, and dysregulated fatty acid metabolism. In the Oxford classification, the tubular atrophy/interstitial fibrosis score allows for earlier prediction of the risk of IgAN progression. Zhu et al. [28] conducted a multivariate logistic regression analysis on 742 IgAN patients, demonstrating that the T score in the MEST-C score at the time of biopsy was an independent predictor of decline in eGFR in IgAN patients. This finding was consistent with the results of our study.

Renal arteriolar lesions are frequently observed in renal biopsy specimens of IgAN patients, but the prognostic significance of these lesions remains unclear. A study by Hyeon et al. [18] showed that renal arteriolar lesions were present in 54.6% of IgAN patients, and that arteriolar wall thickening was associated with decreased eGFR and global glomerulosclerosis, but was not significantly associated with the development of ESRD. However, a study by Huang et al. [29] found that the presence of renal arteriolar lesions was significantly associated with progression to ESRD or death in IgAN patients. In our study, 49.06% of patients in the population with IgAN and mild proteinuria had concurrent arteriolar lesions, and arteriolar lesions were identified as an independent risk factor for poor renal prognosis in IgAN patients. This is consistent with the research by Wang et al. [30]; this consistency suggests that the importance of arteriolar lesions in the prognosis of IgAN cannot be ignored.

Although the progression mechanism identified in this study differs from the classical immune complex deposition mechanism of IgAN, it highlights the heterogeneity in the progression pathways of mild proteinuric IgAN. Specifically, in patients with low-grade proteinuria in early-stage disease, renal impairment may not stem solely from immune inflammation but may also involve chronic injury associated with nephrosclerosis, induced by hypertension and metabolic disorders. This finding also offers new insights for clinical management: for this high-risk subgroup, in addition to routine monitoring of immune parameters, intensified blood pressure control and metabolic management are necessary.

Our study challenges the assumption that IgAN patients with mild proteinuria have a favorable prognosis. Over 90.75 months, 10.19% of patients experienced endpoint events, with an eGFR decline of 2.07 mL/min/1.73 m2 per year. These findings indicate significant disease progression risk in IgAN patients with mild proteinuria. In addition to traditional risk factors, metabolic factors such as TyG-BMI are important prognostic predictors.
