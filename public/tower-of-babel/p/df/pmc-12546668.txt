Response to Letter to the Editor
Published: 2025

Dr. Parth Aphale and colleagues highlight essential future clinical research needs for individuals using a GLP-1 receptor agonist (GLP-1RA) for obesity treatment. We appreciate the contributions and agree with many of their points. While GLP-1RA medications have been approved by the FDA since 2005, nutritional research is still emerging. The current Joint Advisory guidelines encourage dietary patterns, such as increased fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and limiting refined carbohydrates and sugary beverages, which align with the Healthy Eating Index (HEI) [1,2]. Therefore, we aimed to understand if a sample of individuals using a GLP-1RA followed the HEI guidelines. The purpose of our secondary analysis was to help fill knowledge gaps within a population of individuals using a GLP-1RA. As previous nutrition related research with GLP-1RA is limited to single day feeding challenges and lacks dietary quality evaluation [3].

We appreciate the point regarding the applicability of HEI in populations with medically induced calorie reduction, as well the importance of concurrently evaluating micronutrient intake. In a primary analysis, we compared vitamin and mineral intake to the daily reference values to better understand micronutrient insufficiencies [4]. This secondary analysis aimed to evaluate diet quality. We acknowledge the limitation that the HEI may not be suitable to measure diet quality for specialized populations, such as medical weight reduction. However, as noted, the HEI aligns with the Joint Advisory dietary patterns that are recommended with GLP-1RA therapy. To our knowledge, no validated tool exists to assess diet quality for populations undergoing medical weight reduction with reduced caloric intake. Prior research within medical weight reduction interventions have applied the HEI following bariatric surgery, where caloric intake is also medically induced [5].

Secondly, we agree protein quality and sources (plant versus animal) are important considerations for long-term metabolic outcomes and muscle preservation. For example, whey supplementation, a high-quality protein, improved body composition in individuals with obesity with a significant reduction in body weight and total fat mass [6]. However, the question of protein sources represents a distinct line of inquiry beyond the scope of our secondary analysis. We view this as an important avenue for future research. There are currently large gaps in understanding optimal protein quality, dosing, and timing for specific GLP-1RA needs. Future research is needed, particularly in conjunction with resistance training, regarding optimal protein intake with GLP-1RA therapy.

Third, we recognize the inherent limitations of self-reported dietary assessment methods as noted in our primary and secondary analysis [1,7]. To enhance accuracy, our study utilized the ASA24 tool, which incorporates online tutorials and digital images for food identification and portion-size accuracy. While we have explored wearable or photo-based methods for nutritional intake assessments, we did not identify a tool that has been sufficiently validated against traditional dietary assessment techniques. Shims et al. [7], notes the technology is still in the experimental stage for use in research. Looking forward, future research should integrate objective validated methods, such as nutrition biomarkers for collecting dietary intake data.

Regarding generalizability of our findings, we noted limitations and the need for future research. As noted in our first publication, while 82.6 % of the sample identified as White/Caucasian, this is consistent with lower rates of GLP-1RA usage in Asian, African American, and Hispanic individuals [8]. We did not observe a statistically significant difference in education level within the population. Additional research is needed to understand factors that influence obesity management with GLP-1RA, such as the onset and duration of obesity, access to nutrition counseling, socioeconomic status, cultural influence, food security, and education. We agree that future research in more diverse cohort populations is essential to ensure equitable and culturally relevant dietary guidance.

Finally, we appreciate the suggestion regarding meal-timing intervention such as time-restricted eating. In our discussion we noted that a large meta-analysis found time-restricted eating, reduced meal frequency, or altering calorie distribution across the day found reduced weights compared to standard treatments [9]. We agree this is an important area for future research, and considerations for these dietary patterns are needed.

Our current and prior publications underscore the critical need for future nutrition research with GLP-1RA treatment to continue addressing knowledge gaps [1,[10], [11], [12], [13]]. We hope our work provides evidence-based data to raise new research questions to explore. Interventional trials are required to determine optimal dietary patterns and nutrient intake while using GLP-1RA. Future studies should compare different GLP-1RA treatment types and doses, examine the influence of gender, ethnicity, cultural difference, socioeconomic, and educational status. High-quality randomized clinical trials are needed to assess various nutrition interventions, dietary patterns, behavior change strategies, and the impact of comprehensive nutrition counseling delivered by registered dietitians. As the field continues to evolve, our team has already begun investigating how diet and body composition change over time, including whether certain dietary patterns are more optimal in protecting against GLP-1RA side effects, such as muscle loss. We hope our research contributes meaningful context to this emerging area of nutrition science and encourages the broader research community to engage in the many opportunities for scientific discovery.
