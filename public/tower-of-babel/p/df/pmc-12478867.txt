Computational Study to Assess the Influence of Elastic Strains on the Catalytic Activity of Au Surfaces for the HER and ORR Including the Effect of Coverage
Authors: Diego Schaefer-Dalmau, Carmen Martínez-Alonso, Javier LLorca, Valentin Vassilev-Galindo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
The influence of different surface coverages and the effect of biaxial elastic strains on the catalytic properties for the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR) were analyzed using the example of a Au fcc(111) surface. The adsorption energies of H, O, and OH for the corresponding intermediate reactions were obtained by density functional theory calculations. While for H and O, the adsorption energy increased with coverage, for OH, the lowest adsorption energy was observed at 0.5 ML. Then, after elastic strains were applied, the trends observed for the unstrained slab were maintained for the three adsorbates. Although neither tension nor compression modified the optimum coverage, they did change the differences in relative energies. This is considered to be a consequence of the deformations promoted on the surface. Finally, the catalytic activity of the HER and ORR was computed for the distinct coverages and elastic strains. Catalytic activity plots confirmed for both reactions that the more favorable adsorption energy promoted by tensile strains leads to an increment of the activity and that the most active coverage is 0.25 ML. Furthermore, an analysis of the free energies of each step of the dissociative mechanism of the ORR unveils that both coverage and elastic strains can modify the rate-limiting step and, therefore, the catalytic activity of a material. The results presented in this work provide ample characterization of the Au fcc(111) surface as a catalyst for the HER and ORR and offer, for the first time, an analysis of the influence of both coverage and elastic strain engineering on the catalytic activity.

Introduction

The global energy landscape is undergoing a transformative shift toward sustainability. Among various emerging solutions, the hydrogen economy has a major role. The production and use of green hydrogen are nonetheless constrained by the sluggish kinetics of the cathodic limiting reactions of electrolyzers and fuel cells. , These reactions, the hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) and the oxygen reduction reaction (ORR), currently require the use of noble metal catalysts. However, the scarcity and high cost of these materials have led to significant research efforts for new alternatives.

Different approaches have been explored to overcome these limitations. They include the use of carbon-supported nanoparticles and the introduction of carbon nanotubes and graphene to achieve maximum activity per unit mass of precious metal catalysts; − the addition of alloying elements to Pt, Pd, and Rh catalysts to reduce the noble metal content; − the exploration of different facets and defects; − and the introduction of elastic strains. − For instance, Escudero-Escribano et al. have recently shown that the activity of Pt catalysts can be increased by a factor of between 3 and 6 (with respect to pure Pt) by alloying them with lanthanides. The ORR activity in these alloys is related to the lattice parameter of the crystal structure, and the maximum values are reached when the lattice parameter is reduced by 3%, from 0.55 nm for Pt to 0.525–0.530 nm for Pt5Tb, Pt5Gd, or Pt5Sm.

Among all of these strategies to improve the catalytic activity, the introduction of large elastic strains in the lattice has been comparatively less explored. , However, their potential cannot be neglected. For instance, Li et al. recently demonstrated that the catalytic activity for the HER of Cu nanoparticles containing a large density of distorted nanotwins can overcome that of Pt. This performance is associated with the peculiar local structures that induce locally high tensile lattice strains (up to 10%) and decrease the Cu coordination number. The effect of elastic strains on the catalytic activity of metals and intermetallic compounds on the HER and ORR has been analyzed by means of first-principles calculations by Martínez-Alonso et al. , and the trends associated with either compressive or tensile strains have been verified experimentally for the HER in Au(111) surfaces. This behavior has been explained in terms of the so-called d-band model for transition metals. Elastic strains change the energy d-band center, and the variations in the adsorption energy are associated with upward or downward movement of the center of the d-band with respect to the Fermi level. However, more refined predictions of the influence of elastic strains on the catalytic activity must consider other factors. One of the areas that has received very limited attention is coverage.

Coverage indicates the fraction of adsorption sites on the surface of the catalysts that are occupied by an adsorbate. Previous studies have shown that this factor is a key feature in desorption and adsorption processes of H, O, and OH in the HER and the ORR and is hence critical for determining their adsorption energies. , Within this framework, the influence of biaxial strains on the catalytic properties of Au fcc(111) surfaces for the HER and the ORR was analyzed for different surface coverages by means of density functional theory (DFT) calculations. We selected Au fcc(111) as our test system because it has been shown in previous theoretical studies that its catalytic activity is quite sensitive to elastic strains and exhibits significant changes compared to other metal surfaces. , Moreover, these changes have been verified experimentally for the HER. Our findings indicate that the influence of elastic strains on the catalytic activity is dependent on the concentration of adsorbates on the surface and suggest that both elastic strains and coverage can be used to modify the rate-limiting step and, therefore, the catalytic activity of a material.

Methodology and Computational Details

The Au fcc(111) slab is constructed considering four atom layers separated by a 10 Å vacuum region in the direction perpendicular to the surface, as depicted in Figure . We have concluded in previous studies that, for this type of DFT calculations, four layers are enough to obtain converged values of energies for a Au fcc(111) surface. , From these four layers, the atoms in the top two layers were allowed to relax in order to simulate a free surface, while the bottom two layers remained fixed to mimic the bulk region. The starting (2 × 2) four-layered slab of Au fcc(111) was generated with the Atomic Simulation Environment (ASE) employing the relaxed lattice parameters of a Au bulk (Figure ).

(a) Representation of the (2 × 2) supercell slab and the four main possible adsorption sites of the Au fcc(111) surface. F corresponds to the FCC, H corresponds to the HCP, O corresponds to the ONTOP, and B corresponds to the BRIDGE position. (b) Surfaces in the supercells are separated by 10 Å of vacuum. L z had a constant value of 27.2 Å for all calculations, whereas L x and L y varied depending on the applied biaxial strain, starting from values of 5.88 and 5.09 Å when no strains were applied.

All DFT calculations performed in this work were carried out with the GPU-enabled 7.2 version of the Quantum Espresso package. The electron exchange–correlation was approached by the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) with the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) exchange–correlation functional. Grimme’s D3 correction was used to account for dispersion interactions. Brillouin zone calculations were performed using a Marzari–Vanderbit–DeVita–Payne cold smearing of 0.015 Ry. The plane-wave basis was expanded to a cutoff energy of 80 Ry, and the Monkhorst–Pack k-points were sized (12 × 12 × 1) for all slab supercells. The corresponding convergence analysis is shown in Figure S1 of the Supporting Information (SI). Au is a nonmagnetic material; hence, only spin-unpolarized calculations were considered. For the calculation of density of states (DOS) with Quantum Espresso, we employed a denser grid of k-points (36 × 36 × 3).

The electron density of the inner core electrons was approximated by ultrasoft pseudopotentials. Other approaches are possible, such as the projector augmented-wave method (PAW), which generally requires higher computational cost. , However, various studies have proven that the differences in adsorption energies of DFT calculations using ultrasoft pseudopotentials and the PAW method are negligible. ,

For computing atomic charges, we used the Bader program, developed by Yu and Trinkle, which uses the Bader definition of an atom to predict the electronic density. PAW pseudopotentials were used for obtaining the electron density used as an input for the Bader program. Charge density differences (CDD) were computed from the electron density obtained with the Bader program using the postprocessing tool for data analysis and plotting of the Quantum Espresso package (pp.x).

Adsorption Energy

The kinetics of the HER and the ORR are limited by the adsorption of the reaction intermediates onto the catalyst surface. The adsorption mechanism can be characterized by the adsorption energy. This energy is defined as the difference between the total energy of the surface with the chemical species adsorbed and the sum of the energy of the isolated surface and the isolated adsorbates. It can be written for the adsorption of H and O, considering n adsorbates in the supercell, as 1 EadsX=Eslab+Xn−(Eslab+12nEX2)n where E adsX represents the adsorption energy of the adsorbate X (H or O), E slab and E slab+X stand for the total energies of the slab without and with the adsorbate X, and E X 2 is the total energy of the X 2 molecule in the gas phase. For the adsorption of the OH molecule, the adsorption energy was computed as follows: 2 EadsOH=Eslab+OHn−(Eslab+EH2O−12nEH2)n In this case, the total energy of the OH molecule is accounted for as the difference between the total energy of the gaseous molecule of water and half of the energy of the hydrogen molecule in the gas phase. Other approaches may be selected, such as using the energy of a single H atom in the gas phase. Nevertheless, these calculations aim to estimate the most physically meaningful adsorption energies.

The adsorption energies depend on the position of the adsorbate on the catalyst surface. The four main adsorption positions available on an fcc(111) surface are FCC, HCP, ONTOP, and BRIDGE (Figure ). The HCP and FCC are threefold sites, where the adsorbate is positioned at the center of the space between three Au atoms. The difference between the FCC and HCP adsorption sites is that, in the latter, there is a metal atom in the layer below the adsorbate. Regarding the ONTOP position, the adsorption occurs above an Au atom, while in the BRIDGE position, the adsorbate is placed over the metallic bond of two catalyst atoms.

Application of Strain

Strain application was simulated by the modification of the cell parameters of the (2 × 2) slab supercell of the Au(111) surface. Among all of the different possible types of strains, biaxial deformations in the slab plane were considered. Specifically, we explored both tensile and compressive strains of 2 and 5%. We expect that the Au fcc(111) surface is stable within this range of elastic strains because it has been demonstrated before that Pt and Cu, other fcc(111) surfaces, remain stable up to tensile strains of 15 and 10%, respectively, and up to compressive strains of 10%. Mixed boundary conditions are imposed to solve the elastic problem in the DFT simulations. They include imposed strains in the slab plane and zero stresses on the free surface. The deformation gradient F applied to the supercells was 3 F=(1+ϵ001+ϵ) where ϵ stands for the normal strains along the x and y directions.

Coverage

The adsorption energies on a catalyst surface also depend on the surface coverage. The surface coverage Θ denotes the number of adsorbed molecules on a surface divided by the number of molecules in a filled monolayer on that surface. Therefore, in the case of modeling the surface with a supercell, the coverage can be expressed as 4 Θ=numberofadsorbatesnumberofsurfaceatoms where the surface atoms correspond to the atoms of the top atom layer of the supercell. The coverage (Θ) is measured in monolayers (MLs). In this work, we considered coverages of 0.25, 0.5, and 1 ML. This translates to having 1, 2, and 4 adsorbate atoms in the supercell, given that we have 4 Au atoms in the top layer. As an example, Figure shows the three different surface coverages for H adsorption. This is a simplified treatment of coverage, as we do not consider other possible arrangements with larger unit cells. Although this might lead to different results, the explored configurations serve as prototypes for assessing the influence of elastic strains, including the effect of coverage.

Catalytic Activity

The adsorption energy can be used to estimate the activity of a catalyst. The value of E ads obtained from DFT calculations can be employed to determine the Gibbs energy barrier according to 5 Gads=Eads+EZPE−TΔS where G ads, E ads, E ZPE, and ΔS stand for the variation in the free and adsorption energies, the zero-point energy (ZPE), and the entropy, respectively, during the adsorption of the intermediate species, and T is the absolute temperature. Formally, ZPE is part of the internal energy of a bound system. However, the contribution of the ZPE is calculated separately from the rest of the energy in typical quantum chemistry codes; therefore, we explicitly separate them in eq . This relationship should be evaluated for each intermediate species that appears in the catalytic process (only H in the HER and O and OH in the ORR), taking into account that the process with the highest free energy barrier will limit the rate of the reaction.

HER

This parameter describes the electron transfer between the electrode (the catalyst) and the solution. ORR

In this case, both O and OH must be considered. The reaction path considered here is the dissociative mechanism in acidic media. The modeling of the ORR differs considerably if one selects the associative mechanism over the dissociative one or if basic media is considered instead of acidic media. For instance, the reaction steps are different, and other adsorbed species, such as OOH, need to be taken into account. The dissociative mechanism comprises three different steps, 9 12O2+*→O*O*+H++e−→HO*HO*+H++e−→H2O+* where * corresponds to the surface slab. Hence, three different free energies (G 0, G 1, and G 2) are involved in the process. Note that in order to calculate this expression, E adsO′ must be considered instead of E adsO . E adsO′ considers that the adsorbed oxygen comes from water in the catalytic process from the H2O + * →O* + H2 reaction, and it is expressed as 11 EadsO′=EO*+EH2−EH2O−E*=EadsO+EH2−EH2O+12EO2 where E H2 , E O2 , and E H2O account for the total energy of the hydrogen, oxygen, and water molecules in the gaseous state, respectively.

Additionally, the effect of the potential in the cell must be taken into account because it is an electron transfer process. The free energy at pH 0, an atmospheric pressure of 1 bar, and a temperature of 300 K, with electrode potential corrections, is given by 12 G(U)=G+neU where U is the cell potential, n is the number of electrons that flow from the media to the electrode (if the electrons move in the opposite direction, n must be multiplied by −1), and e is the elementary charge carried by a single electron (−1). However, in the case of Au, where it is known that the O atom binds weakly compared to other metal surfaces, the barrier for O2 dissociation (E a diss) might be quite large. The value of such a barrier can be approximated as 16 Eadiss=1.8EadsO′−2.89 If E a diss is larger than the free energies presented in eq , it becomes the limiting step. Results and Discussion

Determination of Optimum Adsorption Sites

Adsorption energy varies among different adsorption sites. The determination of optimum adsorption sites is thus necessary for the characterization of the adsorption process of the main intermediate species present in the HER and ORR reactions. This means that one needs to account for all of the possible combinations of adsorption sites whenever more than one adsorbate is considered per unit cell. However, our goal here is to show the effects of elastic strains on coverage. Hence, we only considered a coverage of 0.25 ML (given the size of our supercell, this coverage corresponds to a single adsorbate in the unit cell) for finding the most stable adsorption site. This site is the one we later used to model other amounts of coverage.

For every adsorbate, the adsorption is expected to occur in the site where the adsorption energy is the lowest (FCC for both H and O and BRIDGE for OH). In the case of H and O, the adsorption is more favorable in the threefold positions (FCC and HCP), with the FCC being the optimal one for the adsorption of both H (0.04 eV) and O (−0.66 eV). The BRIDGE position is unstable (relaxations starting at this site end with adsorbates on an FCC position), and the ONTOP site presents a higher adsorption energy. This trend is supported by charge transfer. The larger the charge transfer from the surface to the adsorbate, the lower the adsorption energy. Large charge transfers from the surface, hence, indicate stronger stabilizing interactions. This is evident for the very unfavorable ONTOP position, where the charge transfer from the surface to the adsorbate is smaller (for H, even the transfer shifts to a transfer from the adsorbate to the surface) than that at the FCC and HCP positions.

For understanding the differences between FCC and HCP positions, we need to differentiate how H and O interact with the surface. The O atom, being an electronegative atom, requires sites where the charge transfer is high in order to be stably adsorbed. The charge transferred to the FCC is slightly larger than that transferred at the HCP position (0.01 |e|). This trend is more evident when looking at the charge transferred by the three Au atoms defining the threefold site (see the charge of atoms Au13, Au14, and Au15 for FCC and atoms Au14, Au15, and Au16 for HCP in Table S2 in the SI). In the FCC site, the transfer is 0.03 |e| larger than the one at the HCP position, thus leading to more stable O adsorption. Regarding H, it does not bind strongly to Au; hence, the available space in the adsorption site is important for stabilizing its adsorption. The Au–Au distances between the Au atoms defining the threefold sites are 3.028 and 3.015 Å in the FCC and HCP positions, respectively. This leads to a larger area in the FCC than in the HCP position, explaining why the adsorption in the FCC site is more favorable than in the HCP site.

The case of OH is different. Although the trends in the charge of O are the same as when O is adsorbed alone (FCC ≃ HCP < ONTOP), the adsorption energies are not. Specifically, we observe that the adsorption energies for the ONTOP and HCP positions are the same, even though the charge transferred to O is much larger in the HCP site. Also, the bridge position, albeit not having the largest charge transfer to the O atom, is the most stable adsorption site for OH. This can be understood when analyzing the charge of H. A larger transfer of charge from H seems to indicate a more favorable adsorption site for OH. In the FCC and HCP positions, the same amount of charge is transferred to O (1.07 |e|), but the more stable FCC position presents a larger charge transferred by H (0.59 |e|). The ONTOP and BRIDGE positions feature the largest transfer of charge from H (0.60 and 0.62 |e|, respectively) due to an inclination of around 65° with respect to the normal to the surface of the H atom toward the HCP position (see Figure ). This explains the fact that the ONTOP position is equally (energetically) favorable as the HCP position, despite being an unfavorable site for O, and that the BRIDGE position is the most favorable, even more than the FCC site (by 0.14 eV).

Influence of Coverage on the Adsorption Energies

Although there are many different structures that could be used to represent the different amounts of coverage, we expect that the first sites that will be occupied will be those that are energetically more favorable. We know that reality might be more complicated, but we consider this simplified model to be a valid one for our purposes. Such a scenario has been observed, for instance, in Mo surfaces, where adsorbates tend to locate on BRIDGE positions (the most favorable one). , Moreover, it can be anticipated that configurations with short adsorbate–adsorbate separation distances (e.g., with adsorbates occupying adjacent adsorption sites) will not be energetically favorable due to repulsion. Hence, we calculated the adsorption energies of the adsorbates onto the Au(111) surface for different surface coverages, considering only the FCC site for H and O and the BRIDGE site for OH.

In particular, three distinct coverage monolayers (MLs) were examined: 0.25, 0.5, and 1 ML. The adsorption energies for H, O, and OH considering the three distinct coverage MLs were computed considering the FCC and BRIDGE positions for H and OH, respectively. In the case of H and O, the adsorption energy increases with surface coverage. The adsorption energies of these adsorbates are 0.04 and −0.66 eV with 0.25 ML, 0.09 and −0.34 eV with 0.5 ML, and 0.20 and 0.64 eV with 1 ML for H and O, respectively. With increasing coverage, the total charge that the Au atoms can transfer does not change, but the number of adsorbates increases. This primarily affects the adsorption of O, as can be observed in the average charge transferred to them. The charge from the surface to the O atoms is reduced from 0.73 |e| with 0.25 ML to only 0.56 |e| with 1 ML. This supports the fact that a coverage of 1 ML is a very unfavorable one for O (0.64 eV). For H, the presence of additional H atoms does not affect the amount of charge transferred from the surface (it remains at a constant value of 0.04 |e|). However, the additional adsorbed atoms promote shrinkage of the FCC site, from a Au–Au distance of 3.028 Å with 0.25 ML to 2.941 Å with 1 ML. The smaller available space to fit the H atom leads to a more unfavorable adsorption with larger coverages (up to 0.20 eV with 1 ML).

Regarding the OH molecule, differently from H and O, the lowest adsorption energy is observed for 0.5 ML of coverage. The adsorption process is more favorable than in the case of 0.25 and 1 ML. The adsorption energy is lower by 0.20 eV than that with 0.25 ML and 0.30 eV lower than that with the 1 ML configuration. This can be understood by looking at the arrangement of adsorbed OH molecules with 0.5 ML (see Figure b). The H atoms are oriented in the in-plane direction toward the O atom of the closest OH molecule. The distance between the H and O atoms of distinct OH groups is 2.15 Å. This distance is in the range of strong hydrogen bonds. These interactions are absent in the configuration with 0.25 ML considered in this work. This explains the more favorable adsorption of OH with the 0.5 ML since hydrogen bonding is always a stabilizing interaction. For 1 ML, despite more hydrogen bonds being formed (see Figure c), the adsorption energy increases by 0.10 and 0.30 eV with respect to 0.25 and 0.5 ML, respectively. As observed in Table , the net charge transferred to the OH molecule is the lowest for 1 ML (0.36 |e|). This indication of weaker binding of the OH molecules makes 1 ML the most unfavorable configuration.

Influence of Elastic Strains

The impact of biaxial elastic strains on the adsorption of H, O, and OH was examined for all different coverages (0.25, 0.5, and 1 ML). Deformations were applied to the Au supercell, while the adsorbates were positioned at the most stable adsorption sites found at 0.25 ML (FCC in the case of H and O and BRIDGE for OH). It is important to mention that, for the adsorption of OH with 5% biaxial tensile strains, the BRIDGE position is unstable and the molecules stabilize to FCC positions for both 0.25 and 1 ML and to FCC+ONTOP positions for 0.5 ML. Although the relaxation into different expected adsorption sites after applying elastic strains is an interesting finding, any difference in adsorption energies (or catalytic activities) with respect to the other samples would be strongly related to such relaxation. Moreover, this change of the adsorption site only happens with very large tensile strains. Such strains, although interesting from a theoretical point of view, are hardly accessible in practice since relaxation (in the form of nucleation of dislocations or other defects) will occur in experimental systems (see, for instance, ref ). Nevertheless, for the sake of completeness, we include in our discussion the results derived from the adsorption of OH on Au under 5% tensile strains.

The adsorption energies of H, O, and OH as a function of the applied biaxial strain for the three surface coverage monolayers are depicted in Figure . The trends observed for the unstrained slab are maintained for the three adsorbates. In the case of H and O, the adsorption energy increases when the surface coverage is augmented. Under the application of any biaxial strain, the configuration with 0.25 ML of surface coverage presents the lowest adsorption energy. For OH, the most favorable coverage is 0.5 ML.

In the OH curve, the adsorption site changed from the BRIGDE sites when the slab was subjected to 5% tensile biaxial strain. Hence, the point is plotted disconnected from the others.

Taking a look at each value of coverage, the trends are the same for the three adsorbates. When compressive strains are applied, the adsorption energies become more positive, whereas the application of tensile strains leads to more favorable adsorptions. For instance, at 0.25 ML, for H, the adsorption energy is reduced to 0.06 eV after applying tensile strains and augmented to 0.11 eV when compressive strains are applied. This trend is also reflected in the charge transferred from/to the adsorbates (see Tables S4–S12 in the SI). Compression leads to a lower transfer of charge to the adsorbates, while tension increases this transfer. CDD, on the example of O adsorption at 0.25 ML, as shown in Figure , supports the trends found with the Bader charge analysis. As can be seen, when applying compressive strains, a very pronounced region of charge depletion originates below the O atom, whereas this region almost vanishes with tensile strains. This can be related to the charge transferred from the surface to the O atom, which is the greatest for 5% and the lowest for −5%. Also, the increment of charge density below the O atom (hence, between the O atom and the Au surface) further explains the fact that the adsorption becomes more favorable when applying tensile strains.

When the different coverages are compared with each other, two interesting facts are found. Whenever we go from high compression to high tension (left to right in Figure a–c), the difference in E ads between 0.25 and 0.5 ML increases. This means that compressive strains stabilize a coverage of 0.5 ML with respect to 0.25 ML (and the other way around for OH). The differences in E ads from zero strain to a compression of 5% fall from 0.05 to 0.02 eV for H, 0.32 to 0.18 eV for O, and 0.2 to 0.08 eV. On the other hand, when comparing 1 ML with the most stable coverage (0.25 ML for H and O and 0.5 ML for OH), the larger the compression, the bigger the difference in E ads between coverages. The differences increase from zero strain to a 5% compression from 0.16 to 0.22 eV for H, from 1.3 to 1.32 eV for O, and from 0.3 to 0.33 eV. These two facts can be understood by the distortions promoted by the application of elastic strains. One such distortion is the Poisson effect. Compression leads to the separation of the layers in the surface. At high compressive strains, these distortions might lead to a scenario where the adsorbates mostly interact solely with the uppermost layer. Hence, the difference in the adsorption processes between different coverages would be mostly defined only by how the adsorbates interact with that layer, which will be similar for 0.25 and 0.5 ML since there is still enough space between adsorbates.

The change of adsorption site of OH for the case of 5% tension has clear effects on the E ads of OH. The absence of the strong hydrogen bonds leads to more positive E ads for both 0.5 and 1 ML (as higher as 0.35 eV for 1 ML) and to a shift of the relative stability between 0.25 and 0.5 ML.

In Figure a, we observed that the adsorption energy of H onto Au becomes more negative with decreasing coverages and increasing tensile strains. This phenomenon is directly reflected in the catalytic activity plot for the HER (Figure a), as ΔG H is directly proportional to E adsH . Au is a noble metal that falls on the right side of a typical volcano plot; , therefore, the more negative the adsorption energy, the higher the catalytic activity. This translates to 1 ML being the coverage with the lowest activity (i 0 equal to −11.60) and to tensile strains, leading to higher activities compared to compressive strains (for instance, i 0 is equal to −5.09 with a strain of 5% and −7.91 with a strain of −5% with a coverage of 0.25 ML).

Regardless of the ML considered and the elastic strain applied, the limiting process of the dissociative mechanism in Au is the dissociation of O2, except at 5% tensile strain with 0.25 ML, where the desorption of OH (G 2(U 0)) is the limiting process. For all cases where the dissociation of O2 is the limiting process, the value of E a diss is larger than 1 eV and becomes as large as 4.57 eV when a compression of 5% is applied to the surface at 1 ML of coverage. In fact, E a diss is considerably larger than the maximum free energy among G 0(U 0), G 1(U 0), and G 2(U 0). The smallest difference is 0.56 eV for the case of 5% tension applied to Au at 0.5 ML of coverage. This can be understood from the fact that the O2 molecule is far more stable than the adsorbed O atom on Au (only after applying 5% tensile strain with 0.25 ML of coverage, the binding of OH to the surface gets so strong that its desorption becomes less energetically favorable than the dissociation of O2). The consequence of this is that the catalytic activity is entirely defined by E a diss for almost all of the considered cases, leading to rather low values. The most active coverage, 0.25 ML, has an activity of −0.61 without any applied strain, and for 1 ML, the catalytic activity on the unstrained surface is as low as −1.62 (Figure b).

The effect of elastic strains on the catalytic activity of Au is quite clear. Whenever a tensile strain is applied, the catalytic activity increases. It goes from −0.61 to −0.45 at 0.25 ML, from −0.85 to −0.72 at 0.5 ML, and from −1.62 to −1.47 at 1 ML when 2% of tension is applied to the unstrained surface. The reason for this behavior is that tensile strains reduce E adsO′ , thus making the adsorption of O slightly more favorable. On the contrary, the application of compressive strains leads to a reduction of the catalytic activity. The activity becomes as low as −0.95, −1.09, and −1.98 with 5% of compression for 0.25, 0.5, and 1 ML, respectively. The change in the adsorption site of OH when applying 5% tensile strains on the Au surface does not affect the trends of the catalytic activity, despite the shift of the limiting process from O2 dissociation to OH desorption.

Even though G 0(U 0), G 1(U 0), and G 2(U 0) do not play, in general, a role in the activity of the ORR on Au, we can observe in Table how coverage and elastic strains could be critical to modifying the rate-limiting step in surfaces in which E a diss is not large. First, for the cases of 0.25 and 0.5 ML, the highest free energy is that of the desorption of OH (G 2(U 0)) until high compressive strains are applied. After compression for 5% of the reaction, the highest free energy is that of the adsorption (G 0(U 0)). Indeed, compression progressively makes O adsorption even more unfavorable than on the unstrained surface, until it makes that process the most energy-intensive step of the dissociative mechanism. The second important finding is that, at 1 ML, the relative energetics among the steps of the mechanism are different from those at 0.25 and 0.5 ML. At this coverage, the adsorption of O (G 0(U 0)) is considerably more unfavorable than the other processes, regardless of the applied strain.

Finally, to better understand the observed effects of elastic strains on the catalytic activity of both the HER and ORR, we computed the DOS for the unstrained slab and the slab under 5% compression and 2% tension, considering a coverage of 0.25 ML (see Figure ). The effect of elastic strains on the Au surface is clear (Figure a). The application of compressive strains leads to a decrease of the states available for occupation at the d-orbitals of Au, while tension has the opposite effect. This can explain why the adsorbates have a stronger interaction (more negative E ads) with the surface under tensile strains, which is ultimately related to higher catalytic activities in the case of Au, as observed in Figure . In the same way, the DOS of Au surfaces with the adsorbed species (Figure b–d) supports the trends observed for the catalytic activities discussed in this work. First, at around −7 eV for OH and −8 eV for H, there is a shoulder that appears upon adsorption and becomes higher when going from 5% compression to 2% tension. In fact, the peak for adsorbed OH disappears with 5% compressive strains. Similarly, there is a peak that appears around −1 eV for the adsorbed OH and becomes higher when applying tensile strains. These small peaks are related to the s-orbital of H and the p-orbital of O. Their heights depict how strong the interaction is between the atom and the surface. Therefore, the DOS indicates that interactions between the adsorbate and the surface are the weakest for compressive strains and the strongest for tensile strains.

Conclusions

The influence of different surface coverages and the effect of biaxial strains on the catalytic properties of Au fcc(111) surfaces for the HER and the ORR were analyzed by means of DFT calculations. The results revealed that the optimum surface coverage depends on the adsorbate considered. In the case of H and O, the adsorption energy increases with the surface coverage, while the lowest adsorption energy of OH was observed for 0.5 ML of coverage. This result was explained by the formation of hydrogen H–O bonds between different adsorbate molecules.

The trends observed for the unstrained slab were maintained for the three adsorbates when elastic biaxial deformations were introduced. The optimum coverages are 0.25 and 0.5 ML, regardless of the applied strain for H, O, and OH. Nevertheless, there are differences in the relative energies between different amounts of coverage. Specifically, it was found that compressive strains tend to stabilize the 0.5 ML (0.25 ML for OH) with respect to 0.25 ML (0.5 ML for OH) and that they further destabilize the 1 ML system. The former was attributed to the layer separation induced by the Poisson effect, while the latter was hypothesized to be the consequence of the smaller available space for fitting the adsorbates due to the reduction of the cell size by compression.

Finally, the catalytic activity of the HER and ORR was computed for distinct surface coverages and elastic strains. The catalytic activity plots confirmed for both reactions that the more favorable E ads promoted by tensile strains lead to an increment of the activity and that the most active coverage is 0.25 ML. Although OH adsorption, whose optimum coverage is 0.5 ML, is involved in the dissociative mechanism of the ORR, the considerably weak binding of O on Au makes the dissociation of O2 the most energy-intensive process for almost all combinations of coverages and strains considered in this work. Furthermore, an analysis of the free energies of each step of the dissociative mechanism of the ORR (G 0(U 0), G 1(U 0), and G 2(U 0)) unveils that both coverage and elastic strain can modify the rate-limiting step and therefore the catalytic activity of a material. Specifically for Au, the most energy-intensive step at 0.25 and 0.5 ML is the desorption of OH until high compressive strains increase the free energy of adsorption of O over that of OH desorption, while at 1 ML, the adsorption of O is quite unfavorable, making its free energy the highest among the three steps of the mechanism regardless of the applied strain.

The results presented in this work provide ample characterization of the Au fcc(111) surface as a catalyst for the HER and ORR and give, for the first time, an analysis of the influence of both coverage and ESE on the catalytic activity. The findings described in this article indicate that the influence of the interplay of coverage and elastic strains is not trivial since it can enhance or diminish the activity by both modifying the E ads of an adsorbate or changing the rate-limiting step of the ORR. Hence, the consideration of the effects of both coverage and ESE must be taken into account whenever one studies the properties of new (or already known) potential catalysts.
