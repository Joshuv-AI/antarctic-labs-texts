Acute lung injury: pathogenesis and treatment
Authors: Huanqi Liu, Junli Dong, Cailin Xu, Yang Ni, Zhenbang Ye, Zhenjun Sun, Honggang Fan, Yongping Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute lung injury (ALI) is a serious clinical condition that often leads to respiratory failure and high mortality. This review describes the pathogenesis of ALI, including the involvement of inflammatory cytokines, the activation of NLRP3 inflammasome, the generation of oxidative stress, the occurrence of apoptosis, the dysfunction of mitochondrial function, and the breakdown of lung endothelial and epithelial cell barriers. These mechanisms interact to cause significant damage and dysfunction of lung tissue. In addition, the current situation of prevention and treatment of ALI was discussed, with emphasis on lung protective ventilation, fluid management, mesenchymal stem cell therapy and drug therapy. We also analyze the latest research advances in advanced nanomedicine for the treatment of ALI, and the application of this nanomedicine could provide new ideas for the development of effective ALI therapeutics in the future.Keywords: Acute lung injury, Pathogenesis, Prevention and treatment, Nanomedicine

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) is a globally recognized respiratory condition characterized by rapid onset and prolonged duration, posing a significant threat to life. It can be triggered by various factors, which are divided into direct pulmonary factors and indirect extrathoracic factors. Direct pulmonary factors include lung or chest trauma, bacterial or viral pneumonia, drowning, pulmonary hemorrhage, and inhalation of smoke or toxic gases. Indirect extrathoracic factors refer to factors outside the chest, such as sepsis (a life-threatening condition caused by the body’s extreme response to infection), transfusion overload, drug toxicity, severe non-thoracic trauma, disseminated intravascular coagulation (a disorder where blood clots form throughout the body’s small blood vessels), and pancreatitis. Both types of factors can damage alveolar epithelial cells (cells lining the air sacs in the lungs) and capillary endothelial cells (cells lining the tiny blood vessels in the lungs), thereby inducing ALI [1]. ALI can also occur secondarily due to complications like aspiration of gastric contents into the lungs after anesthesia, burn infections, fat embolism (blockage of blood vessels by fat droplets), heat stroke, and prolonged exposure to dust and ozone. Clinically, ALI primarily manifests as acute hypoxemic respiratory failure (a condition where the lungs cannot absorb enough oxygen, leading to low blood oxygen levels). It is accompanied by increased permeability of the alveolar-capillary membrane (the thin barrier between air sacs and blood vessels in the lungs), diffuse alveolar damage (widespread injury to the air sacs), non-cardiogenic pulmonary edema (fluid accumulation in the lungs not caused by heart problems), and pulmonary inflammation. If not treated promptly, ALI may progress to acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS, a more severe form of lung injury with worse respiratory failure), resulting in multiple organ failure (simultaneous failure of two or more organs) and ultimately patient death.

Since its formal introduction in 1967, ALI has garnered significant attention from researchers in the medical fields. With the increasing depth of research into ALI and ARDS, substantial progress has been made in understanding their pathophysiology (the biological mechanisms underlying the disease) and development. However, factors such as air pollution, changes in husbandry practices, and various viral influences—most notably the ongoing global pandemic of COVID-19—have led to a rise in the incidence and mortality rates of ALI/ARDS. In the United States, the incidence of ALI among individuals aged 15 and older is approximately 78.9 cases per 100,000 people, while the incidence of ARDS is about 58.7 cases per 100,000, with mortality rates of 38.5% and 41.1%, respectively [2]. Between 2007 and 2016, data indicated that among 146,058 intensive care unit (ICU) patients under the age of 18, the incidence and mortality rates of ALI were 1.8% and 20%, respectively [3]. A study reported alarmingly high figures in pediatric intensive care units (PICUs), where the incidence and mortality rates of ALI among 1,738 patients under 15 years old reached 51.2% and 7.4%, respectively [4]. Additionally, monitoring across 27 countries involving a total of 145 PICU patients revealed an ALI/ARDS incidence rate of 17% and a mortality rate of 3.2% [5]. A comprehensive study of 459 ICU patients from 50 countries found that 10.4% of ICU admissions were diagnosed with ARDS, with incidence rates of mild, moderate, and severe ARDS at 30%, 46.6%, and 23.4%, respectively, and corresponding mortality rates of 34.9%, 40.3%, and 46.1% [6]. Numerous studies have identified that the incidence and mortality of ALI/ARDS are influenced by seasonal variations, advanced age, smoking, and alcohol consumption. Notably, as age increases, the annual incidence and mortality rates of ALI rise from 0.016% to 24% in the 15–19 age group to 0.306% and 60% in individuals aged 75–84 and older [7]. These staggering incidence and mortality rates translate into profound public health burdens globally. The critical demand for ICU beds and mechanical ventilation acutely strains healthcare systems, frequently necessitating resource reallocation during outbreaks—compromising care for other populations. Beyond acute care, survivors often face chronic respiratory insufficiency and cognitive decline, driving sustained needs for medical rehabilitation and social support that escalate long-term costs. Crucially, premature mortality among working-age adults coupled with rising treatment expenditures depletes workforce productivity and burdens health economies, creating ripple effects across societies.

Therefore, a comprehensive investigation into the molecular pathogenic mechanisms of ALI and the development of effective preventive and therapeutic agents are urgent challenges that need to be addressed in the field of respiratory disease prevention and treatment. Given the profound and multifaceted public health burden of ALI/ARDS, advancing such research is imperative not only to improve clinical outcomes but also to mitigate systemic healthcare strains and broader societal costs.

Pathogenesis of ALI

The pathological mechanisms of ALI are complex and diverse, making it a focal point and challenge in medical research. Over more than 50 years of continuous exploration, significant roles have been identified for the involvement of inflammatory cytokines, the activation of the NOD-like receptor protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome (a protein complex in cells that triggers inflammation in response to harmful stimuli), oxidative stress, and the occurrence of apoptosis in the pathogenesis of ALI. Furthermore, recent studies have revealed that mitochondrial dysfunction and the disruption of the pulmonary epithelial barrier are critical mechanisms underlying the development of ALI. These mechanisms do not act in isolation but form an interconnected network: inflammatory cytokines can activate inflammasomes and induce oxidative stress; oxidative stress can promote cell apoptosis and mitochondrial damage; and mitochondrial dysfunction, in turn, amplifies inflammation and oxidative stress—ultimately collectively disrupting pulmonary barriers. The following sections will elaborate on their specific roles and interplay.

Involvement of inflammatory cytokines

Currently, it is widely accepted that the pathogenesis of ALI is associated with pulmonary or systemic inflammatory responses. When endogenous or exogenous harmful stimuli affect the body of humans or animals, pattern recognition receptors on pulmonary epithelial cells and macrophages recognize pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) or damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). This recognition triggers a cascade of events that lead to the release of inflammatory mediators and chemokines, promoting the recruitment of neutrophils and mononuclear macrophages to the sites of lung injury [8].

Activated neutrophils and macrophages further secrete and release a multitude of proteases, chemokines, and inflammatory factors, exacerbating the inflammatory response and damaging the function of pulmonary epithelial cells, ultimately inducing ALI [9]. Numerous studies indicate that inflammation caused by direct lung injury is more severe than that induced by indirect lung injury [10, 11]. For instance, research by Menezes et al. found that the number of inflammatory cells recruited in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) of mice with direct lung injury was four times greater than that in mice with indirect lung injury [12]. Furthermore, the majority of inflammatory cells in the BALF of direct lung injury mice were neutrophils, whereas mononuclear/macrophage cells predominated in the BALF of indirect lung injury mice. Additionally, the concentration of inflammatory cytokines in the BALF of direct lung injury mice was found to be 2–3 times higher than that in their indirect counterparts [13]. Flavia B Santos et al. revealed that the inflammation induced by direct lung injury could persist for up to three weeks, while the inflammation in mice with indirect lung injury typically resolved within a few days [14]. Thus, inhibiting the inflammatory response induced by direct lung injury is particularly crucial in the prevention and treatment of ALI.

Inflammatory cytokines detected in the plasma and BALF of ALI patients primarily include tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), interleukin-8 (IL-8), and interleukin-1 beta (IL-1β). TNF-α and IL-6 are responsible for initiating and regulating the inflammatory response in ALI [15]. Research by Florentin et al. have shown that IL-6 is essential for the accumulation of neutrophils in the lungs following lung injury [16]. Additionally, studies by Blot PL et al. indicate that plasma levels of IL-6 and IL-8 correlate with the incidence and mortality of ALI patients [17]. Furthermore, the concentration of IL-8 in BALF has been confirmed to have a negative correlation with changes in lung compliance (the ability of the lungs to expand and contract) [18]. In the early stages of ALI, IL-1β emerges as one of the most biologically active inflammatory cytokines, with increased plasma concentrations indicating poor clinical prognosis for patients with ALI/ARDS [19]. Moreover, IL-1β is a potent inducer of pulmonary fibrosis and can drive the release of various chemokines and inflammatory cytokines, such as myeloperoxidase, IL-6, IL-8, macrophage inflammatory protein 1α, and monocyte chemotactic protein 1. This cascade promotes the recruitment of inflammatory cells into the alveoli, increases the permeability of the pulmonary vascular endothelium and epithelial cell barriers, and leads to pulmonary edema [20].

Therefore, elucidating the specific sources of inflammatory cytokine secretion and inhibiting their release, as well as reducing their levels in the blood or BALF, may represent a crucial mechanism for the prevention and treatment of ALI.

Activation of NLRP3 inflammasome

As a key regulator of inflammatory cytokine maturation, the NLRP3 inflammasome acts as a bridge between early inflammatory signals and the amplification of ALI pathology. The NLRP3 inflammasome is a cytosolic protein complex that recognizes a variety of stress signals, exogenous microorganisms, and endogenous danger signals. It is widely expressed in epithelial and immune cells and primarily consists of NLRP3, pro-caspase-1, and apoptosis-associated speck-like protein (ASC) [21]. NLRP3 contains three structural domains: C-terminal leucine-rich repeats (LRRs), the central nucleotide-binding and oligomerization domain (NACHT), and the N-terminal pyrin domain. The LRRs are responsible for detecting endogenous danger signals, identifying microbial ligands, and regulating NLRP3 activity. The NACHT domain possesses ATPase activity, facilitating its own oligomerization. The N-terminal pyrin domain interacts with ASC, the adaptor protein of the NLRP3 inflammasome.

ASC comprises two signaling domains: one pyrin domain that can bind to upstream NLRP3 and another caspase recruitment domain that connects to downstream pro-caspase-1. In the absence of immune activators, the interaction between the LRRs and the NACHT domain inhibits the binding of NLRP3 to ASC, thereby preventing the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome [22]. However, in the presence of immune activators such as PAMPs, DAMPs, other exogenous invaders, or environmental stressors, Toll-like receptors are phosphorylated, activating the NF-κB signaling pathway. This leads to an increase in the transcription of NLRP3, pro-IL-1β, and pro-IL-18. Subsequently, the pyrin domain of ASC rapidly binds to the N-terminal domain of NLRP3, while the caspase recruitment domain quickly associates with the pro-caspase-1 domain [23]. This interaction drives the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome, initiating the autocleavage of pro-caspase-1 to generate cleaved caspase-1, which mediates the maturation and secretion of IL-1β and IL-18 (Fig. 1).

The assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome are influenced by a complex array of factors. Known endogenous triggers include adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and uric acid crystals, while exogenous factors encompass bacterial hemolysins and pneumolysin [24]. Environmental stimuli such as silica, asbestos, amyloid beta, and alum can also initiate the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Upon phagocytosis by immune cells, these stimuli form particulate or crystalline structures that disrupt lysosomes, releasing lysosomal contents like cathepsin B, which in turn triggers the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome. Moreover, reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated by PAMPs and DAMPs, mitochondrial damage, autophagy dysfunction, and redox protein interactions all promote the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome [25]. This highlights that NLRP3 inflammasome activation is not an independent event but is regulated by upstream factors such as oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, which will be elaborated later. Reports indicate that activated NLRP3 inflammasomes mediate endothelial cell injury in the lungs of mice subjected to hemorrhagic shock [26]. Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in alveolar macrophages contributes to inflammation and injury in the lungs of mice induced by mechanical stretching [27]. Heather D. Jones and colleagues found that the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome is a critical mechanism for hypoxemia in mice with ALI induced by both lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and mechanical ventilation [28].

Additionally, the products of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, namely IL-1β and IL-18, play pivotal roles in the progression of ALI. IL-1β is capable of inducing the expression of genes that regulate fever, pain thresholds, vasodilation, and hypotension, facilitating the infiltration of immune cells into infected or damaged tissues. Neutralization of IL-1β with antibodies or the use of IL-1 receptor antagonists has been shown to alleviate the severity of ALI in various rodent models [29]. Elevated serum levels of IL-18 in the early stages are indicative of poor prognosis in patients with ALI/ARDS [30]. Neutralizing IL-18 antibodies can mitigate lung injury in experimental models of ALI. Increasing evidence suggests that inhibiting the activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome may improve ALI induced by LPS and hyperoxia [31]. These cytokines, in turn, can further activate immune cells to produce more ROS and inflammatory mediators, forming a positive feedback loop that exacerbates oxidative stress and barrier damage.

Generation of oxidative stress

Oxidative stress is a critical mechanism in the pathogenesis of ALI and ARDS. When the body is exposed to risk factors associated with ALI/ARDS, there is an excessive generation of ROS, which include both free radicals and non-free radicals. Free radicals comprise hydroxyl radicals and superoxide anions, while non-free radicals include singlet oxygen and hydrogen peroxide [32]. Under normal circumstances, cells produce various substances to eliminate ROS, such as glutathione, heme oxygenase-1, the catalytic subunit of glutamate-cysteine ligase, NADPH, NQO1, catalase, and superoxide dismutase. However, when ROS levels exceed the capacity of these antioxidants, it can lead to the peroxidation of fatty acids in cell membranes, resulting in decreased membrane fluidity and increased permeability, ultimately causing pulmonary edema and impaired lung expansion [33]. Moreover, ROS can upregulate the expression of inflammatory cytokines and adhesion molecules, damaging both alveolar epithelial cells and pulmonary endothelial cells. This disruption of the pulmonary vascular and epithelial barrier exacerbates lung tissue injury and worsens pulmonary edema [34]. Research by Liu et al. has demonstrated that excessive ROS production drives the assembly of the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to the generation of cleaved caspase-1 and promoting the maturation of IL-1β, which triggers inflammatory responses and further aggravates ALI [35].

Additionally, during the pathogenesis of ALI, inflammatory cytokines can also induce oxidative stress. Inflammation and oxidative stress are interdependent and mutually reinforcing pathological processes. It has been reported that the substantial release of inflammatory cytokines activates peripheral polymorphonuclear neutrophils, which are recruited to the alveoli, releasing additional inflammatory factors that further intensify the inflammatory response and induce oxidative stress [36]. Furthermore, ROS-induced lipid peroxidation and protein damage can directly trigger mitochondrial dysfunction and cell apoptosis, as discussed in subsequent sections, creating a multi-layered pathogenic network. Therefore, inhibiting ROS production and clearing oxidative stress products in the lungs is a primary objective in the prevention and treatment of ALI.

Occurrence of apoptosis

Apoptosis is both a consequence of upstream pathogenic mechanisms (e.g., oxidative stress, inflammatory cytokine overload) and a driver of further lung damage, linking cellular death to barrier disruption and inflammation amplification. Apoptosis is a regulated process of cell death that is initiated by specific signaling pathways, leading to the elimination of cells. These death pathways can be activated in response to receptor-ligand interactions, environmental factors such as ultraviolet light, and genomic encoded internal signals. Ultimately, apoptosis results in DNA fragmentation, a reduction in cell volume, and the phagocytosis of the dying cells by neighboring phagocytes. Abnormal activation of apoptosis can lead to disease in both humans and animals, either through the premature death of healthy cells or the prolonged survival of unhealthy ones [37].

Increasing evidence suggests that apoptosis plays a significant role in the occurrence and development of ALI and ARDS induced by various stimuli [38]. Apoptotic cells can accumulate abnormally in the lungs, promoting sustained inflammatory responses through the release of inflammatory mediators, proteases, ROS or lysozymes [39]. Reports indicate that the apoptosis of neutrophils, alveolar epithelial cells, and pulmonary endothelial cells are critical factors in the pathological progression of ALI [40]. Neutrophil apoptosis is a key determinant in the induction of lung injury. Activated neutrophils release oxidants, proteases, leukotrienes, and platelet-activating factors, which induce damage to pulmonary epithelial and endothelial cells, leading to the inactivation of pulmonary surfactant and severely compromising the integrity of both the epithelial and capillary endothelial barriers. This results in increased permeability of the epithelial and endothelial layers and the massive release of inflammatory cytokines.

The apoptosis of alveolar epithelial cells reduces the secretion of surfactant, further disrupting the structural integrity of the epithelial and endothelial barriers, exacerbating lung injury [41]. During the resolution phase of ALI, the apoptosis of type II alveolar epithelial cells is considered a major contributor to excessive epithelial cell loss. Damage and apoptosis of pulmonary endothelial cells are critical mechanisms in indirect lung injury. Research by Roxlau et al. has shown that the apoptosis of pulmonary vascular endothelial cells disrupts endothelial structure and barrier function, increasing pulmonary endothelial permeability and facilitating the accumulation of inflammatory cells and protein-rich exudate in the alveoli and lung interstitium, ultimately leading to pulmonary edema and the onset of ALI [42]. Notably, inhibiting caspase activity has been shown to extend the survival of mice with LPS-induced ALI, while suppression of pro-apoptotic signaling pathways reduces apoptosis of type II alveolar cells and the excessive generation of inflammatory cytokines in LPS-induced models [43]. Furthermore, preventing apoptosis in lung tissue can protect against damage, suppress immune responses, and accelerate tissue resolution and healing. Therefore, targeting the inhibition of apoptosis is crucial for developing new therapeutic strategies for ALI.

Dysfunction of mitochondrial function

Mitochondria are often referred to as the “powerhouses” of cells, but their dysfunction in ALI acts as a upstream trigger, fueling oxidative stress, inflammasome activation, and apoptosis through the release of mitochondrial DAMPs. Mitochondrial involvement in the pathophysiological mechanisms of ALI is a prominent area of research in both medical sciences. Mitochondria, as double-membraned organelles derived from prokaryotic ancestors, have been integrated into the cytoplasm of eukaryotic cells throughout evolution. Consequently, mitochondria retain unique structures, including CpG-rich DNA, membrane lipids (particularly cardiolipin), N-formylated peptides, and bioenergetic pathways, while functioning as an integral part of the intracellular system primarily regulated by genomic DNA. Structurally, mitochondria are divided into the outer membrane, intermembrane space, inner membrane, and matrix. The outer membrane is permeable to molecules smaller than 5,000 Da through channel proteins, while the inner membrane is highly impermeable to ions and molecules, serving as an anchor for components of the electron transport chain [44]. The matrix contains components of the citric acid cycle and mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), which encodes 37 proteins, most of which are redox-active proteins located in the inner membrane that regulate mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation [45]. This oxidative phosphorylation process is the primary pathway for ATP production in mitochondria. The citric acid cycle in the mitochondrial matrix oxidizes acetyl-CoA, generating nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) and guanosine triphosphate. NADH donates electrons to the mitochondrial electron transport chain through complex I, which are subsequently transferred through complexes II, III, and IV to oxygen. The movement of electrons along the electron transport chain generates a proton gradient across the mitochondrial membranes, and the energy stored in this gradient is utilized by ATP synthase to convert adenosine diphosphate to ATP [46]. The ATP produced by mitochondria meets the energy demands of various lung cells.

Interestingly, different subtypes of airway epithelial cells, including ciliated cells, goblet cells, club cells, basal progenitor cells, and type I and II alveolar cells, contain varying numbers of mitochondria, with distinct mitochondrial localization within each cell type. For instance, in ciliated airway epithelial cells, mitochondria are predominantly located at the apical region, exhibiting highly folded cristae and dense matrices [47]. In contrast, goblet cells and type II alveolar cells have mitochondria situated around the nucleus, while in basal and club cells, mitochondria are dispersed throughout the cytoplasm [48]. This cell-specific distribution of mitochondria influences the levels of ATP and metabolites in these cells, allowing them to perform distinct functions. For example, differentiated airway epithelial cells primarily defend against harmful stimuli through mucus secretion or ciliary movement, while type II alveolar cells protect the lungs by secreting surfactant.

When lung-associated or systemic factors stimulate the body, mitochondrial damage occurs in lung cells, leading to the release of mitochondrial ROS (mtROS), Ca2+, mtDNA, ATP, cardiolipin, and cytochrome c (Cyt-c) into the cytoplasm, acting as DAMPs that subsequently activate the innate immune system [49]. The mechanisms by which mitochondrial dysfunction induces pulmonary inflammation are illustrated in Fig. 2. mtROS can act as signaling molecules, inducing the transcription of inflammatory cytokines, activating signaling pathways, and regulating important transcription factors involved in redox homeostasis, proliferation/survival, inflammatory responses, extracellular matrix production, and the regulation of Ca2+ in pulmonary epithelium and airway smooth muscle. Elevated levels of mtROS in the cytoplasm can induce the translocation of NF-κB p65 to the nucleus, directly driving inflammatory responses [50]. mtDNA serves as a ligand for Toll-like receptor 9, activating the NLRP3 inflammasome [51]. Additionally, N-formylated peptides encoded by mtDNA are released into the cytoplasm during cellular damage, activating formyl peptide receptor 1 and serving as DAMPs that recruit neutrophils to sites of lung injury [52]. Extracellular ATP also activates NLRP3 through purinergic receptors P2 × 7R, promoting its translocation to mitochondria and enhancing mtROS production, thereby exacerbating lung injury [53]. Cardiolipin can directly activate the NLRP3 inflammasome [54]. Cyt-c in the cytoplasm can participate in the formation of apoptosomes, promoting the generation of cleaved caspase-9 and cleaved caspase-3, inducing apoptosis [55]. Moreover, mitochondrial DAMPs can trigger a robust CXCL8 response in airway epithelial cells, recruiting neutrophils to the site of injury and initiating inflammatory responses [56]. Another pattern recognition receptor, NLRX1, is localized to mitochondria and interacts with mitochondrial antiviral signaling pathways. Studies have shown that NLRX1 exerts anti-inflammatory effects by preventing the activation of NLRP3, thereby reducing the activation of NF-κB. A decrease in NLRX1 levels can induce mtROS-mediated oxidative stress [57]. The evidence indicates that mitochondrial dysfunction drives the production of pulmonary inflammation and oxidative stress responses, playing a significant role in the pathogenesis and progression of pulmonary diseases.

Disruption of pulmonary endothelial and epithelial barriers

Pulmonary endothelial cells and alveolar epithelial cells are critical components of the endothelial and epithelial barriers that constitute the lung’s capillary network. The integrity of these barriers is vital in the pathophysiological processes of ALI. When various pulmonary or systemic factors stimulate the body, DAMPs can induce diffuse damage to the alveoli, compromising the integrity of both the epithelial and capillary endothelial barriers. This disruption facilitates the accumulation of protein-rich exudate containing inflammatory cells in the alveolar space and/or lung interstitium, inhibiting the production of pulmonary surfactant, and ultimately reducing lung compliance and impairing gas exchange [58].

Disruption of pulmonary endothelial

Pulmonary endothelial cells account for approximately 50% of all lung cells. They are interconnected through tight junctions (protein complexes that seal gaps between adjacent cells) and adherens junctions (cell-to-cell connections that maintain tissue structure), forming the endothelial barrier. The homeostasis and structural integrity of the pulmonary endothelium are regulated by a specific adhesion protein known as vascular endothelial cadherin (VE-cadherin) [59]. When exposed to exogenous stimuli, levels of vascular endothelial growth factor, TNF-α, thrombin, and leukocytes increase, disrupting the VE-cadherin junctions and compromising the endothelial barrier. This results in increased pulmonary endothelial permeability, allowing water, solutes, leukocytes, platelets, and other inflammatory molecules to infiltrate the alveolar space and interstitium, leading to pulmonary edema. Moreover, stabilizing VE-cadherin interactions through the modulation of its association with catenins or preventing its phosphatase-mediated degradation can reduce protein concentrations and leukocyte counts in BALF in LPS-induced mouse models [60]. This indicates that alterations in VE-cadherin not only affect the function of the pulmonary endothelial barrier but also influence leukocyte migration, making it a significant inducer of clinical ALI/ARDS.

Given that pulmonary endothelial cells receive the entire cardiac output, they are continuously exposed to circulating pathogens and bacterial endotoxins (such as LPS), leading to the disruption of the endothelial barrier and serving as a vital pathological mechanism for indirectly induced ALI. A complete pulmonary endothelial barrier tightly regulates endothelial permeability, preventing the recruitment of leukocytes to the lung interstitium and alveoli. However, when this barrier is compromised, pro-coagulation pathways are activated, resulting in increased exudate in lung tissues and the recruitment of neutrophils, which in turn induces the production of inflammatory cytokines. A moderate increase in endothelial permeability during inflammation is an adaptive feature of the host defense response, facilitating leukocyte migration to help resolve infections and prevent ALI. In contrast, severe infections can trigger excessive inflammatory responses and endothelial barrier disruption, ultimately leading to high mortality rates associated with purulent ALI. Dysfunction and damage to the pulmonary endothelium may be consequences of excessive production of inflammatory cytokines; for instance, mediators like TNF-α can increase endothelial permeability by activating inflammatory responses and contracting the microvascular cytoskeleton [61]. Additionally, activated neutrophils can further compromise pulmonary endothelial permeability through the release of pro-coagulation molecules, neutrophil extracellular traps, proteolytic enzymes, ROS, and inflammatory cytokines [62]. Research by Cheng et al. has shown that caspase-11-mediated endothelial pyroptosis is also involved in the process of LPS-induced pulmonary endothelial barrier disruption [63]. Furthermore, inhibiting NF-κB activation can block cytokine signaling, thereby reducing pulmonary inflammation, decreasing pulmonary endothelial permeability, and improving lung function. Therefore, enhancing the integrity of the pulmonary endothelial barrier and inhibiting pulmonary edema may be key therapeutic targets for indirectly induced ALI/ARDS.

Disruption of the alveolar epithelial barrier

The alveolar epithelium consists of a continuous monolayer of type I and type II alveolar cells, serving as a physical barrier that protects the underlying tissues from bacteria, viruses, allergens, and other harmful substances. It also functions as an immune barrier, crucial for maintaining normal lung homeostasis, fluid balance, and gas exchange. Type I alveolar cells are flat, membrane-like cells that cover the majority of the alveolar surface area (approximately 96%), but they have limited proliferation capabilities and are highly susceptible to damage. Type II alveolar cells, on the other hand, possess proliferative capabilities and are involved in ion transport and surfactant production [64].

Damage to type I and/or type II alveolar cells can lead to structural defects and functional impairment of the alveolar epithelial barrier, resulting in ALI/ARDS, with increased risk in patients with pulmonary infections or chronic inflammatory airway diseases. Additionally, impaired expression of structural factors that maintain the epithelial barrier can also contribute to dysfunction. The epithelial barrier is formed by airway epithelial cells connected through junctional complexes, primarily tight junctions and adherens junctions. Tight junctions, located at the apical surface of epithelial cells, consist of claudins, occludins, adhesion molecules, and zonula occludens (ZO), tightly linking the plasma membranes of adjacent cells to regulate paracellular transport of ions and molecules. Adherens junctions, located below tight junctions, are composed of E-cadherin, α-catenin, β-catenin, and p120-catenin, mediating cell-to-cell adhesion [65]. However, a reduction or loss of occludin and ZO-1 expression can compromise alveolar barrier function and increase alveolar permeability. Research by Pao et al. has demonstrated that reduced expression of occludin and ZO-1 disrupts tight junction structures in a rat model of ischemia-reperfusion-induced lung injury [66]. Additionally, the loss of E-cadherin can impair cell adhesion and increase permeability and motility of the lung epithelium, leading to the accumulation of protein-rich exudate in the alveolar space and resulting in pulmonary edema, which may further progress to ARDS. It has been reported that TNF-α can upregulate the expression of chemokines in alveolar epithelial cells while downregulating occludin and E-cadherin, thereby increasing alveolar barrier permeability [67]. This aligns with the earlier observation that inflammatory cytokines are key drivers of barrier damage. Moreover, silencing E-cadherin in bronchial epithelial cells leads to downregulation of ZO-1, disrupting tight junction integrity and compromising epithelial barrier function.

The loss of adhesion and the increased paracellular permeability of alveolar epithelial cells are central mechanisms in the pathogenesis of ALI. Compared to the pulmonary capillary endothelial barrier, the epithelial barrier exhibits greater resistance to protein and fluid influx, with alveolar epithelium actively clearing edema fluid. Notably, while damage to the pulmonary endothelium is a prerequisite for the development of pulmonary edema in ALI/ARDS, endothelial injury alone is insufficient to induce pulmonary edema; damage to alveolar epithelial cells is also necessary for severe lung injury [68].

Inflammatory mediators are key factors inducing pulmonary edema, as they initiate inflammatory responses that increase epithelial permeability. Furthermore, the disruption of alveolar epithelial barrier integrity is associated with the activation of chemokines and adhesion molecules in alveolar epithelial cells, leading to the recruitment of inflammatory cells into the alveolar space. The migration of neutrophils can damage intercellular junction structures, induce apoptosis, and ultimately result in alveolar epithelial cell injury, increasing the permeability of the epithelial barrier [69]. Here, neutrophil infiltration—driven by inflammatory cytokines—and apoptosis—triggered by oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction—collectively contribute to epithelial barrier breakdown. Loss of the integrity of the alveolar epithelial barrier and damage to type II alveolar cells not only heighten epithelial permeability, resulting in alveolar flooding, but also inhibit the expression of pulmonary surfactant, decreasing surface tension at the air-liquid interface, and impairing the clearance and reabsorption of fluid in the alveoli. Additionally, the loss of alveolar epithelial barrier integrity can induce septic shock symptoms in patients with bacterial pneumonia. If the damage to alveolar epithelial cells is extensive, leading to structural and functional disruptions in lung tissue and poor epithelial repair, this may facilitate further progression to fibrosis. Therefore, reducing damage to alveolar epithelial cells and restoring the structure and function of the alveolar epithelial barrier may represent a novel approach for the prevention and treatment of ALI/ARDS in both medical and veterinary fields.

Prevention and treatment of ALI

As research into ALI continues to advance, significant breakthroughs have been made in understanding its pathophysiological mechanisms. However, effective preventive and therapeutic strategies for this syndrome remain limited. The accumulation of protein-rich exudate containing inflammatory cells in the alveolar space and lung interstitium triggers inflammatory responses, damaging the epithelial and/or endothelial cells, which leads to increased capillary permeability and respiratory failure. Therefore, strategies aimed at delivering oxygen to the lungs, inhibiting or clearing pulmonary edema, reducing inflammatory responses, and improving epithelial and endothelial cell integrity are crucial for the prevention and treatment of ALI. Currently, known interventions for ALI include lung protective ventilation, fluid management, mesenchymal stem cell therapy, and pharmacological treatments.

Lung protective ventilation

Lung protective ventilation is a supportive therapy that can enhance survival rates, although the optimal approach is still unclear. Clinical trials have shown that low tidal volume and airway pressure ventilation can reduce the risk of ARDS [70]. Patients receiving a tidal volume of 6 mL/kg exhibited a threefold faster resolution of pulmonary edema compared to those receiving 12 mL/kg, along with decreased plasma levels of inflammatory cytokines IL-6 and IL-8 [71]. However, current evidence suggests that there may be no safe tidal volume or airway pressure levels for patients with ALI, as even normal tidal volumes can lead to localized overdistension of the lungs, denaturing surfactant, damaging epithelial and endothelial cells, and inducing systemic inflammatory responses.

Guidelines from the American Thoracic Society, European Society of Intensive Care Medicine, and Society of Critical Care Medicine strongly recommend reducing tidal volume and airway pressure before initiating invasive mechanical ventilation for all ALI/ARDS patients. When plateau airway pressure exceeds 30 cm H2O, tidal volume should be reduced from 6 mL/kg to 4 mL/kg to decrease the risk of mortality associated with ALI/ARDS. This approach has been shown to reduce mortality by 9% points compared to strategies that increase tidal volume. Additionally, the use of positive end-expiratory pressure (PEEP) of at least 5 cm H2O is recommended. A meta-analysis of three randomized trials found that maintaining PEEP at relatively high levels in patients with moderate to severe ALI/ARDS reduces mortality risk [72]. However, the best method for adjusting PEEP remains unknown. During ALI/ARDS, the end-expiratory pleural pressure is typically positive, which may lead to higher-than-conventional PEEP levels, resulting in negative transpulmonary pressure and lung collapse. Measuring esophageal pressure to estimate pleural pressure can help adjust PEEP to induce a positive gradient of transpulmonary pressure. Additionally, minimizing driving pressure by adjusting PEEP or tidal volume is reasonable, as this method allows tidal volume to be tailored to the patient’s respiratory system compliance, aligning PEEP with optimal compliance and preventing lung overdistension [70]. However, while applying continuous positive airway pressure and intermittently releasing the applied pressure can reduce mechanical ventilation tolerance and improve oxygenation, there is currently no evidence that this approach decreases mortality in ALI/ARDS and it may adversely affect hemodynamics.

Fluid management

The primary principle of fluid management is to clear pulmonary edema, a strategy that has been shown to be effective since the late 1970 s [73]. Conservative fluid management can improve lung function, reduce vascular pressure, and shorten the duration of mechanical ventilation and intensive care. Additionally, conservative fluid strategies can lower plasma levels of angiopoietin-2, thereby providing protection to the endothelium [74]. Common resuscitation fluids include colloids, crystalloids, and blood products. For patients who have experienced severe trauma leading to significant blood loss and subsequent lung injury, colloids are the preferred resuscitation fluid. In cases of septic shock accompanied by sepsis, crystalloids should be the first choice. For patients with massive blood loss, blood products are indicated.

In clinical practice, the infusion rate of resuscitation fluids should be strictly controlled; administering fluids too rapidly can exceed the body’s capacity, leading to tissue edema and exacerbating injury, while a slow rate may hinder the maintenance and correction of homeostasis. It is important to note that crystalloids can partially enter the interstitial space, failing to ensure adequate volume effects. Additionally, the high cost of colloids can lead to coagulopathy or renal dysfunction and may trigger allergic reactions. Blood products also carry the risk of immune rejection. Furthermore, studies suggest that diuretics and albumin-based fluid management strategies can shorten the duration of mechanical ventilation, reduce inflammatory responses, eliminate pulmonary edema, and improve lung injury [75]. However, it is crucial to recognize that the use of diuretics to clear pulmonary edema may reduce cardiac output, resulting in inadequate lung perfusion. Although albumin-based strategies are beneficial, they do not significantly reduce mortality in ALI patients.

Mesenchymal stem cell therapy

Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) possess properties such as immune modulation, multipotent differentiation, and self-renewal, allowing them to evade immune clearance through various mechanisms. In recent years, clinical and preclinical research on MSC therapy has advanced significantly. Both clinical and animal studies have demonstrated the therapeutic potential of MSCs for ALI and other pneumonia-related diseases. Research by Chen et al. confirmed that MSCs protect against phosgene-induced ALI in rats by participating in the repair of the pulmonary gas-blood barrier and modulating inflammatory responses [76]. Zhang et al. found that administering exogenous MSCs to the site of injury can activate the Wnt3/β-catenin signaling pathway, reducing alveolar epithelial permeability. MSCs can also influence the proliferation and differentiation of various lung cells, including alveolar epithelial cells and endogenous lung stem cells [77]. Ye et al. reported that MSCs improve ALI by activating the Notch signaling pathway, which affects the proliferation of endogenous lung stem cells [78]. Despite the promising therapeutic effects of MSCs on ALI, there is a potential risk of inducing iatrogenic tumors.

Pharmacological therapy

Given that ALI is an inflammatory lung disease, various anti-inflammatory agents, including Ulinastatin, nitric oxide synthase inhibitors, angiopoietin-1, and glucocorticoids, have been extensively studied. Ulinastatin is a broad-spectrum serine protease inhibitor used to treat acute inflammation in various organs. Intraperitoneal administration of Ulinastatin has been shown to alleviate inflammatory lung injury in rats by downregulating the synthesis and release of pro-inflammatory factors such as IL-6, cell adhesion molecule-1, and IL-1α [79]. Nitric oxide synthase inhibitor 1400 W can suppress nitric oxide production, enhance the expression of surfactant protein B and ZO-1, and reduce the destructive effects of nitric oxide synthase on the alveolar epithelial barrier. Angiopoietin-1 is a glycoprotein that maintains endothelial barrier function and stabilizes vascular homeostasis. Research by He et al. demonstrated that angiopoietin-1 reduces lung tissue inflammation and decreases vascular permeability by inhibiting the NF-κB pathway [80]. Glucocorticoids possess non-specific, rapid, and potent anti-inflammatory effects, primarily exerting their action through various mechanisms, including inhibiting the production of inflammatory cytokines, restraining the activation of neutrophils, downregulating the permeability of alveolar blood vessels, and promoting the differentiation of alveolar macrophages to the M2 phenotype. In the early stages of ALI, the injection of dexamethasone significantly reduces the number of neutrophils and protein levels in the BALF of patients, alleviating pulmonary injury caused by phosgene inhalation. Zhou et al. found that low-dose methylprednisolone protects against lung injury by blocking the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in lung tissue, thereby reducing the expression of inflammatory factors like IL-1β [81]. However, not all glucocorticoids confer protective effects in ALI; for instance, mometasone does not significantly improve pulmonary edema or survival rates in rats with phosgene-induced ALI [82].

While glucocorticoids can reduce airway pressure and improve oxygenation in pneumonia patients, they are not suitable for long-term use, and high doses can exacerbate lung injury and lead to osteonecrosis [83]. Although these medications have shown benefits for ALI in animal studies, clinical recommendations for their use as first-line treatments for ALI are limited due to uncertainties regarding dosing, timing, and administration routes, as well as their potential side effects. Given the high incidence and mortality rates of ALI, which severely impact the quality of life for both humans and animals, there is an urgent need to identify safe and effective novel drugs for the prevention and treatment of ALI.

Nanomedicine

Nanomedicine has emerged as a promising therapeutic strategy for treating ALI and ARDS, due to its unique properties that enhance drug delivery and therapeutic efficacy [84]. A significant challenge in managing ALI is the poor bioavailability of conventional medications, which limits their effectiveness. Nanoparticles address this issue by improving the solubility and stability of drugs. For instance, self-assembled nanoparticles, such as disulfide bond-bridged prodrug nanoparticles, can encapsulate drugs like dexamethasone, achieving a drug loading capacity that significantly exceeds traditional formulations [85]. This ensures that higher concentrations of therapeutic agents reach the target site in the lungs, enhancing pharmacokinetics and reducing the frequency of administration, ultimately improving patient compliance and treatment outcomes.

Moreover, nanomedicine facilitates targeted drug delivery, allowing for specific targeting of inflamed lung tissues while minimizing systemic side effects [86]. This targeted approach improves the therapeutic index and reduces the risk of adverse effects associated with systemic drug distribution. Recent studies have highlighted the potential of macrophage-targeted nanomedicines to modulate inflammatory responses in ALI, effectively altering the disease course by reprogramming the inflammatory response [87]. Additionally, nanomedicine plays a vital role in regulating immune responses by interacting with immune cells to enhance or suppress inflammation as needed. Certain nanomedicines promote M2 macrophage polarization, which is associated with anti-inflammatory effects, while nanocarriers delivering anti-inflammatory agents directly to the injury site can significantly reduce pro-inflammatory cytokines and improve lung function [88].

Recent preclinical advancements have strengthened the foundational evidence for nanomedicine’s translational potential in ALI/ARDS, supporting ongoing and future clinical investigations. For example, studies on lipid-based nanocarriers—including lipid micelle-encapsulated inhibitors [89]—have demonstrated enhanced drug accumulation in injured lung tissues and reduced systemic exposure, addressing a key limitation of conventional therapies. Consistent with these findings, artificial exosomes derived from mesenchymal stem cells showed 5-fold higher accumulation in inflamed lung regions compared to controls, with no severe adverse events observed, confirming favorable nanoparticle biocompatibility [90]. Extending these observations, investigations of medicinal plant-derived nanovesicles have further demonstrated attenuated lung injury, reduced mortality, and improved pulmonary function in ALI models, with their therapeutic mechanisms specifically linked to the modulation of alveolar macrophage activity and mitochondrial fitness [91]. Specifically, nanoparticle-encapsulated therapeutics targeting alveolar epithelium effectively reduced pro-inflammatory cytokines and necroptosis-mediated tissue damage [89], while engineered extracellular vesicles exerted modulatory effects on macrophage polarization and promoted tissue repair [92]. These preclinical data provide critical support for ongoing phase I/II trials evaluating nanomedicine formulations in ALI/ARDS patients, with preliminary clinical observations aligning with the therapeutic trends observed in preclinical models.

Overall, the integration of nanomedicine in the management of ALI represents a novel and effective approach that not only addresses the symptoms of this condition but also targets the underlying pathological processes, paving the way for future advancements in respiratory medicine and fostering better patient outcomes through optimized drug delivery and increased adherence to treatment regimens.

Potential innovative mechanisms and therapeutic directions

Building on the pathological mechanisms of ALI and the translational potential of nanomedicine, several innovative hypotheses and therapeutic directions warrant in-depth exploration, which may break through the limitations of current treatments.

First, nanoparticle-mediated “mitochondrial-targeted metabolic reprogramming” could be a breakthrough. Mitochondrial dysfunction (e.g., mtROS overproduction, ATP depletion) is a core driver of ALI pathogenesis, and existing studies have shown that mesenchymal stem cell-derived exosomes can transfer functional mitochondria to damaged alveolar epithelial cells [93]. We hypothesize that engineering nanocarriers with dual properties, coated with mitochondrial membrane proteins (to enhance targeting) and loaded with SIRT3 activators (to restore mitochondrial function), could “hitchhike” on endogenous mitochondrial transport pathways. This would enable precise delivery to damaged lung cell mitochondria, reducing mtROS-induced oxidative stress while restoring ATP production, thereby synergistically repairing the alveolar epithelial barrier. Preclinical data on mitochondrial-targeted nanoparticles [94] support the feasibility of this strategy, which may simultaneously address energy metabolism disorder and inflammatory amplification in ALI.

Second, inflammatory microenvironment-responsive “smart nanosystems” may solve the problem of spatiotemporal heterogeneity in ALI inflammation. ALI exhibits distinct inflammatory characteristics at different stages: early stages are dominated by neutrophil infiltration, while mid-late stages involve macrophage polarization [95]. We propose designing nanocarriers with stage-specific response modules. For example, nanoparticles sensitive to neutrophil elastase (abundant in early ALI) could release IL-10 to limit excessive inflammation; when the disease progresses to the mid-late stage, the same nanoparticles could switch to releasing TGF-β1 (a pro-repair factor) in response to macrophage-derived IL-1β, achieving dynamic matching of therapy to disease progression. This “one-system, multi-stage” design has been preliminarily validated in tumor therapy [96] and may be adapted to ALI’s dynamic pathological features.

Third, nanomedicine-mediated “cross-regulation of pyroptosis and epithelial regeneration” deserves attention. Pyroptosis (NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated pro-inflammatory cell death) of alveolar epithelial cells exacerbates barrier disruption, while impaired epithelial regeneration hinders recovery [97]. Studies have shown that medicinal plant-derived nanovesicles can inhibit NLRP3 inflammasome activation, and nanoparticle-encapsulated Wnt agonists can promote alveolar epithelial stem cell differentiation [98, 99]. We hypothesize that dual-functional nanocarriers co-loaded with pyroptosis inhibitors (e.g., MCC950) and epithelial regeneration promoters (e.g., Wnt agonists) could simultaneously block pyroptotic cell loss and accelerate alveolar epithelial repair, forming a “damage inhibition-repair promotion” synergistic effect. This approach targets both injury and recovery processes, which may significantly improve the prognosis of severe ALI.

Conclusion and outlook

ALI represents a clinically challenging pathological condition characterized by its complex and diverse pathogenesis, which involves the release of inflammatory cytokines, activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, generation of oxidative stress, occurrence of apoptosis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and disruption of lung endothelial and epithelial barriers. These pathological processes intertwine, collectively leading to significant lung tissue damage and dysfunction, ultimately resulting in respiratory failure and high mortality rates. This article not only systematically reviews the mechanisms underlying ALI but also discusses current prevention and treatment strategies, including lung protective ventilation, fluid management, mesenchymal stem cell therapy, and pharmacological interventions.

As research into the pathophysiological mechanisms of ALI deepens, particularly with the rapid advancements in nanomedicine, new therapeutic options are expected to emerge. The application of nanomedicine offers unprecedented opportunities for the treatment of ALI, enhancing drug delivery and bioavailability to improve therapeutic outcomes. Future research should focus on integrating multiple treatment modalities to achieve a comprehensive management approach for ALI. Notably, the development of personalized treatment strategies warrants prioritization, as ALI exhibits marked heterogeneity in pathogenesis, clinical presentation, and response to therapy among individual patients. Such strategies could leverage advancements in precision medicine—including genetic profiling, biomarkers of inflammation or organ damage, and patient-specific risk stratification—to tailor interventions such as drug selection, dosage adjustment, and the timing of supportive care for optimal efficacy. To operationalize this, a pivotal focus should be placed on advancing personalized regimens through biomarker-driven patient stratification, integration of multi-omics data, and deployment of real-time diagnostic technologies, all of which would enable precise matching of therapies to individual pathophysiological profiles. Additionally, the design of clinical trials should emphasize the exploration of personalized treatment strategies to achieve better efficacy in clinical practice.

The innovative directions proposed above, from mitochondrial metabolic reprogramming to smart responsive nanosystems and cross-regulation of injury and repair, bridge the gap between basic research and clinical translation. By combining nanomaterial engineering with ALI’s dynamic pathological features, these hypotheses provide actionable targets for future studies, which may accelerate the development of next-generation ALI therapies. Through continuous innovation and interdisciplinary collaboration, we are hopeful for breakthroughs in the treatment of ALI, significantly improving patient prognosis and reducing mortality rates.
