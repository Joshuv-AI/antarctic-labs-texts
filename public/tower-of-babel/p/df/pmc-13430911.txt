Too burdened to benefit: job demands and the fading perceived usefulness of health IT
Authors: Olivier Arsene, Claudio Vitari
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHealthcare professionals in France are exposed to increasing job demands, including clinical workload, administrative burden, and work–life intrusion, all of which contribute to elevated strain and burnout. Health Information Technologies are expected to act as valuable job resources by improving efficiency and supporting task coordination. Yet little is known about whether HITs are associated with a weaker relationship between job demands and strain, and whether their perceived usefulness declines when workload becomes excessive. This study applies the Job Demands–Resources model to outpatient healthcare professionals to examine how job demands are associated with strain, vocational attachment, and the perceived usefulness of HIT.MethodsA nationwide cross-sectional survey was conducted among users of a major French scheduling HIT. A total of 1,298 valid responses were analyzed. Job demands and strain were specified as formative higher-order constructs. Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modeling was used to test the model, assess moderating effects, and evaluate potential curvilinear relationships be-tween job demands and perceived usefulness.ResultsJob demands were strongly and positively associated with strain (β = 0.67), which was in turn associated with lower vocational attachment (β = –0.48). Recognition was positively associated with perceived fairness of remuneration (β = 0.40), and perceived fairness of remuneration was positively associated with vocational attachment (β = 0.18). Perceived usefulness (PU) was asso-ciated with a small but significant weakening of the job demands–strain relationship (β = –0.05). At the same time, increasing job demands were associated with a decline in PU (β = -0.22).ConclusionThese findings indicate that while perceived usefulness of health information technologies is as-sociated with a small weakening of the relationship between job demands and strain, their per-ceived usefulness declines as work demands intensify. This self-limiting pattern highlights the risk of treating digital tools as stable resources without addressing the workload conditions that shape professionals’ capacity to benefit from them.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14841-3.

Introduction

Healthcare professionals in France work under growing structural pressure. They face increasingly complex patient needs and pathologies, workforce shortages, and territorial disparities in care provision [1]. These tensions are periodically intensified by epidemic crises, such as seasonal influenza or COVID-19, which further affect professionals’ psychological and physical well-being [1]. At the same time, Health Information Technologies (HIT), digital tools intended to support healthcare delivery, have become increasingly embedded in healthcare practice since the early 2000s. Prior research highlights their expected benefits, including more efficient consultation management, improved scheduling, better handling of patient information, gains in care quality, and the development of new patient-facing services [2].

Beyond these expected operational benefits, regulations also reinforce the growing role of HIT in daily practice. Requirements related to electronic documentation, remote billing, and the secure transmission of sensitive health data have made digital tools increasingly difficult to avoid. While French regulation does not mandate the use of specific software solutions, the absence of compatible HIT substantially complicates regulatory compliance by forcing healthcare professionals to rely on manual procedures through dedicated public platforms. These constraints reinforce the centrality of HIT in outpatient care, positioning them as important instruments for administrative compliance, coordination, and secure professional communication.

At the same time, the use of HIT among healthcare professionals remains uneven. A substantial body of literature has documented barriers that may hinder their adoption and use. These include varying levels of familiarity with digital technologies [3], concerns regarding possible degradation of clinical practice or ethical tensions associated with digitalization [4], and more classic obstacles such as limited perceived usefulness or ease of use [5]. In healthcare settings, perceived usefulness is closely tied to the extent to which technologies effectively address concrete professional needs [2]. Several contributions further stress that such evaluations cannot be dissociated from the broader work context, especially in environments characterized by strong regulatory constraints, organizational pressures, and high workloads [6]. Empirical investigations of healthcare digitalization also show that, beyond adoption challenges, HIT may reshape everyday work by introducing additional tasks, duplicating documentation requirements, and accelerating work rhythms, thereby transforming professionals’ cognitive and organizational load [7, 8].

However, an important issue remains insufficiently understood. If digital tools are expected to support work under pressure, their perceived usefulness may vary with the intensity of that pressure. Existing research has shown that perceived usefulness depends on technological characteristics, user dispositions, and conditions of use. Yet less attention has been paid to whether rising job demands themselves are associated with lower perceived usefulness of HIT. This issue matters because it suggests that digital tools may not remain stable resources across work conditions. When job demands intensify, professionals have less time, attention, and cognitive capacity to perceive and mobilize the benefits of HIT. As a result, the resource value of these tools may be perceived as weaker precisely when it is most needed. The present study therefore shifts attention from whether healthcare professionals accept HIT to whether they can still perceive HIT as useful under high job demands.

This question is especially salient in outpatient healthcare. In France, self-employed healthcare professionals operate in a fragmented care environment while facing growing clinical, administrative, and coordination pressures. HIT are increasingly embedded in scheduling, billing, documentation, and secure communication, yet these professionals remain largely responsible for integrating such tools into their own everyday routines. The French outpatient context therefore offers a revealing setting for examining a broader issue that extends beyond a single national case: in decentralized healthcare systems, digital tools are increasingly mobilized as part of everyday work organization, while the intensity of job demands may be associated with a lower perceived capacity to benefit from them. This setting is analytically relevant beyond France because many healthcare systems are shifting coordination, documentation, and patient-facing services toward digital infrastructures while simultaneously relying on semi-autonomous outpatient professionals to absorb these changes in already overloaded work routines.

Against this background, the present study relies on the JD–R model to examine how job demands and job resources are associated with strain and vocational outcomes among self-employed healthcare professionals, while giving particular attention to the role of HIT within this broader pattern. More specifically, the study investigates whether rising job demands are associated with lower perceived usefulness of HIT, and whether HIT perceived as useful are associated with a weaker relationship between job demands and strain.

Accordingly, the research question guiding this study is as follows: How are job demands and job resources associated with strain and vocational attachment among self-employed healthcare professionals, and to what extent are higher job demands associated with lower perceived usefulness of Health Information Technologies as resources in this process?

By addressing this question, this study contributes to the literature in two ways. First, it applies the JD–R framework to self-employed outpatient healthcare professionals and shows how job demands, job resources, strain, and vocational attachment are associated in this underexamined setting. Second, it identifies a specific digital mechanism within this broader JD–R pattern: perceived usefulness of HIT is not only related to technological characteristics or user attitudes, but is also negatively associated with the intensity of job demands. The study further shows that HIT perceived as useful are associated with only a small weakening of the job demands–strain relationship, suggesting that their role as job resources is conditional and limited.

The remainder of the paper is structured as follows. We first present the theoretical framework and develop the study hypotheses based on prior research. We then describe the research design, survey methodology, and analytical approach. Finally, we report the empirical results and discuss their implications for the everyday use of HIT by self-employed healthcare professionals.

Theoretical background and hypothesis development

The job demands–resources model in healthcare

The Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) model is one of the leading frameworks in the professional stress literature [9]. It distinguishes between two broad categories of workplace characteristics: job demands and job resources. Job demands refer to aspects of work that require sustained effort and are associated with physical and psychological costs. In stressful work situations, these demands may lead to strain. Stress has been defined as “a relationship between a person and the environment that is assessed by that person as demanding or exceeding his or her resources, thereby endangering his or her well-being” [10]. In this perspective, a stressor is the source of stress arising from this interaction, whereas strain refers to the individual’s negative response to it [10].

Healthcare work is repeatedly characterized by high job demands. Clinical workload is shaped by workforce shortages, complex pathologies, and the need to manage continuous patient flows [11, 12]. Administrative demands, including documentation, billing procedures, and regulatory compliance, constitute another salient stressor in modern healthcare and are increasingly intensified by digital systems that generate additional coordination, correction, and follow-up work [7, 13]. In addition, weak boundaries between work and personal life, particularly in outpatient professions with unpredictable schedules, challenge work–life balance and reinforce emotional exhaustion [14]. These demands are consistently associated with elevated strain and burnout.

In the case of self-employed healthcare professionals, the consequences of demanding work conditions do not concern well-being alone. They may also affect the perceived sustainability and meaning of one’s professional activity. In such settings, higher strain may weaken professionals’ attachment to their vocation and reduce the extent to which they continue to view their work as desirable and sustainable.

Job resources, by contrast, are physical, psychological, social, or organizational aspects of work that support goal attainment, learning, and personal growth [15]. Examples include autonomy, positive feedback, supportive interactions, and self-efficacy. In healthcare, resources may also arise from the relational dimension of care. Positive interactions and recognition can support motivation and professional fulfilment. When such resources are available and salient, they may foster job satisfaction and strengthen vocational attachment.

Hypothesis 3

Job resources are positively associated with job satisfaction.

Hypothesis 4

Job satisfaction is positively associated with job attachment.

Taken together, the JD–R model provides a relevant basis for examining how job demands, job resources, strain, and vocational outcomes are associated in healthcare work. Prior healthcare research further suggests that increasing demands may rapidly weaken the protective effects of available resources, particularly among physicians [16]. In increasingly digitalized care environments, some of these resources are also mediated by technologies, raising the question of whether digital tools are perceived as supportive resources under demanding work conditions.

Health IT as a conditional job resource in the JD–R model

Digital technologies occupy an ambivalent position in healthcare work. Recent research shows that they may create additional burden by increasing workload, disrupting routines, or blurring boundaries between work and personal life [17–24]. At the same time, studies suggest that HIT can act as a resource by helping healthcare professionals better manage demanding work environments. Studies have identified IT as beneficial in helping professionals address job demands and reduce the strain associated with them [25]. Scholze and Hecker identified two key dimensions through which IT may operate as a resource: autonomy, where IT facilitates flexible work, and efficiency, where IT enhances job performance. These dimensions have also been observed in other work examining how HIT supports everyday tasks for healthcare practitioners [3]. Within the JD–R model, this role is consistent with the buffer hypothesis, according to which job resources moderate, or buffer, the positive association between job demands and strain. Bakker et al. [15] emphasize that “job resources become salient and are most important for burnout and work engagement when job demands are high”. In demanding healthcare settings, HIT may therefore function as valuable job resources, but only to the extent that professionals perceive these systems as useful and capable of helping them cope with their workload rather than adding new stress [23]. Accordingly, the present study treats the perceived usefulness of HIT as the key mechanism through which digital tools may function as job resources in the broader JD–R process.

Hypothesis 5

When HIT are perceived as useful, they are associated with a weaker positive relationship between job demands and strain.

Perceived usefulness of HIT under cognitive load

If HIT may function as resources, an important question becomes whether professionals are equally able to perceive them as useful under all work conditions. Perceived usefulness, defined as “the degree to which a person believes that using a particular system would enhance his or her job performance” [26] is a central concept in the technology adoption literature. Across models such as TAM and UTAUT [5], perceived usefulness depends not only on system characteristics, but also on the contextual and individual conditions under which technology is used [5, 26].

Such contextual sensitivity is particularly relevant in healthcare. Prior work shows that characteristics such as specialty, geographical location, professional experience, or personal situation may shape the digital features that healthcare professionals need and the way they evaluate HIT [12]. Beyond contextual factors, user-specific dispositions also influence usefulness perceptions. Salo et al. [27] demonstrate that individuals may voluntarily engage in IT use patterns that produce strain because of personal habits, self-imposed norms, or social pressure. During periods of elevated workload, such as the COVID-19 crisis, healthcare professionals relied intensively on HIT despite potential drawbacks, illustrating how pressured environments alter the meaning and evaluation of technology use [28]. Similarly, Singh & Verma [29] show that heightened anxiety and workload during constraint digital use during crisis reduce users’ perceived value of technology. Other studies of digitalized healthcare work show that when time pressure and task fragmentation are high, professionals struggle to integrate digital systems into routines, leading potentially useful tools to be experienced as burdensome rather than supportive [8]. These findings suggest that the ability to actualize HIT as a job resource depends not only on the technology itself but also on the cognitive, temporal, and emotional conditions in which professionals operate.

Several theoretical perspectives help explain this mechanism. Affordance theory suggests that even genuinely helpful technological affordances may remain unperceived or unactualized when the “actualization cost” is high [27, 30, 31]. Similarly, conservation of resources theory posits that under resources loss, such as time, energy, or staffing, individuals tend to avoid activities perceived as costly or risky, even when potentially beneficial [32]. Under such constraints, professionals have fewer opportunities to explore system features, integrate them into their routines, or experience the potential benefits of HIT.

These perspectives indicate that when job demands intensify, the ability of HIT to function as a usable job resource diminishes, as IT-related stressors might result in coping behaviors and workarounds that undermine effective system use [33]. Technostress research supports this cognitive-resource mechanism, showing that high stress impairs users’ appraisal of technology benefits and reduces their capacity to enact coping strategies [34]. As cognitive and temporal resources become depleted, health professionals’ capacity to perceive HIT as useful is reduced, even if the technology is objectively valuable [8, 22].

Hypothesis 6

Higher job demands are associated with lower perceived usefulness of HIT.

Research method and data analysis

We conducted a nationwide cross-sectional self-report survey among self-employed healthcare professionals in France. The survey was distributed electronically through Doctolib’s online scheduling system, which redirected respondents to a secure third-party platform after informed consent was obtained. A total of 1,298 valid responses were collected from approximately 60.000 invitations sent, representing a diverse set of medical and allied health professions. This distribution protocol ensured that respondents were already using digital tools in their everyday practice, which was appropriate for examining perceived usefulness of HIT among current users. At the same time, because recruitment occurred through a single major digital platform, the sample primarily reflects healthcare professionals already engaged with such tools in routine practice.

Job demands were measured through three stressors. A recurrent stressor in healthcare is work overload that may originate from workforce shortages leading to clinical workload [11] or increasing administrative tasks [13]. Another stressor arises from work-life imbalance when professional responsibilities spill over into personal life [14]. Strain was measured through burnout, “a syndrome of emotional exhaustion, depersonalisation, and reduced personal accomplishment” [36]. The JD–R literature traditionally conceptualizes job attachment through indicators such as turnover intention or commitment to an employer [25]. However, these indicators are ill-suited to practitioners who do not belong to a formal employing structure. We therefore adopted a profession-centered operationalization of job attachment based on prior research showing that regret about career choice [37] and the willingness to recommend one’s profession to younger generations [38] constitute valid markers of vocational attachment in autonomous professional groups. This operationalization was intended to capture the sustainability and perceived desirability of professional activity in the absence of a formal employing structure. Regarding job resources, existing JD–R studies often emphasize feedback, autonomy, and self-efficacy [25], less applicable in self-employment, where autonomy is inherent to the profession and less peer interaction. Instead, we used perceived recognition from patients and society, strongly contributing to motivation and job satisfaction among healthcare workers [16, 39, 40]. Finally, job satisfaction was measured using perceived fairness of remuneration, a consensual indicator across heterogeneous healthcare professions and a recognized motivational factor [41].

The questionnaire was developed following established methodological guidelines for scale design [42] and the validation principles outlined by Straub, Boudreau, and Gefen [43]. This process included defining scale objectives, selecting and adapting items from prior research, specifying response formats, and conducting expert validation with practicing physicians (Appendix A). Validated scales were adapted for perceived usefulness [26], burnout with the validated six-item short form of the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI), distinguishing personal, work-related, and patient-related exhaustion [44], work–life balance [45], recognition [46], while shortened versions of established measures were used for clinical and administrative workload [47]. All items were reviewed for clarity and contextual suitability, and the full survey was designed to be completed in under ten minutes. All study variables were collected through the same questionnaire at a single point in time, consistent with the cross-sectional design of the study. Ethical approval was obtained from an institutional research ethics committee, and participation remained voluntary and anonymous throughout the study.

Because all study variables were collected through a single self-report questionnaire at one point in time, common method bias was considered both procedurally and statistically. Following recommendations that common method bias should be addressed primarily through the research design and interpreted cautiously when assessed through post hoc statistical diagnostics [48], several procedural precautions were used. The questionnaire relied on established scales adapted from prior research, measured conceptually distinct constructs, was reviewed with practicing physicians, and was kept concise to reduce respondent fatigue. Participation was voluntary and anonymous. Statistically, we assessed common method bias using full collinearity VIFs. Each latent construct was regressed on all other latent constructs, and the resulting VIF values ranged from 1.64 to 2.32, below the commonly used threshold of 3.3. As a supplementary diagnostic check, Harman’s single-factor test indicated that the first unrotated component accounted for 35.03% of the total variance. Taken together, these results do not indicate a major common method bias problem. However, because the study relies on cross-sectional self-report data, common method variance cannot be entirely excluded and is acknowledged as a limitation.

Following Gefen and Straub’s [49] guidelines, constructs were modeled as reflective or formative based on their conceptual foundations and on the assumed directional relationship between indicators and latent variables. All lower-order constructs (LOCs) except job resources were modeled reflectively, consistent with psychometric theory that treats psychological perceptions, such as burnout (CBI), perceived usefulness (PU), work–life balance (WLB), job satisfaction (REMU), and job attachment (VOCA), as latent traits that manifest through correlated item responses. Accordingly, they were specified as reflective constructs. In contrast, job resources (RECO) was treated formatively because its two indicators capture distinct sources of recognition that contribute additively and non-interchangeably to the overall construct.

Two higher-order constructs (HOCs) were modeled formatively from their reflective lower-order components, using a hierarchical composite specification consistent with recommended PLS-SEM practices [50, 51]. Job demands (JD) were conceptualized as a formative composite comprising work overload, administrative overload, and work–life balance. This specification reflects job demands theory, in which these dimensions represent analytically distinct and non-substitutable stressors. Similarly, Strain (CBI) was modeled as a formative second-order construct formed by work-related strain (CBI_W), patient-related strain (CBI_PA), and personal strain (CBI_PE). The three subdimensions, based on the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory, capture different facets of strain that do not necessarily co-vary and are therefore theoretically suited for a formative aggregation.

This hierarchical modeling approach is aligned with the JD–R framework, which conceptualizes job demands and strain as composites emerging from multiple underlying stressors, and with prior IS research recommending formative HOC specifications when dimensions jointly form, rather than reflect, a broader theoretical construct [51].

In addition to the theoretical constructs, three standard demographic and professional variables were included as controls: age, sex, and professional specialty. Their inclusion follows prior evidence that such background characteristics may shape burnout, work attitudes, or technology perceptions and could otherwise confound the structural relationships of interest. Age was retained because several studies report age-related differences in burnout and well-being among healthcare workers [52, 53], and because older professionals tend to report lower perceived usefulness of digital technologies [54]. Sex was included given documented, although heterogeneous, gender differences in burnout and job satisfaction across healthcare settings [55, 56]. Finally, professional specialty was controlled for, using medical doctors as the reference category, since psychosocial demands and resources vary substantially across healthcare professions [12] and may influence both strain and the perceived utility of HIT. These controls allow for a more conservative estimation of the hypothesized JD–R and technology-related effects while acknowledging that the empirical literature is not fully consensual regarding the strength or direction of demographic differences.

Regarding the redaction of this article, AI-based tools were used solely to refine English phrasing from my initial formulations; all conceptual content, analyses, and references were entirely produced and verified by the authors.

Measurement model analysis

We used R (version 4.5.0) and the seminr package to estimate a PLS-SEM model, assess the measurement properties of our constructs, and test the hypothesized relationships. PLS-SEM was selected because it can accommodate complex hierarchical composite structures and formative higher-order constructs such as Job demands and strain, which are central to our theoretical model. To complement the evaluation of the structural model, we also assessed global model fit using the standardized root mean square residual (SRMR). As the seminr package used for the main analysis does not directly provide this index, SRMR was estimated using an equivalent composite model specified in the cSEM package. In addition, common method bias was further examined through full collinearity variance inflation factors (VIF) and Harman’s single-factor test.

We first evaluated whether the sample size was adequate for the complexity of our model. A post-hoc power analysis indicated that, with 1298 observations and up to six predictors for an endogenous construct, the model has sufficient power (0.80; α = 0.05) to detect effect sizes as small as f² ≈ 0.0105. Given that even small effects are theoretically meaningful in psychosocial models of job strain and resources perception, the available sample size is more than adequate for testing our hypotheses. We next examined the distributional properties of the measurement items. PLS-SEM is robust to moderate deviations from normality, particularly for skewness up to approximately 1.1 and kurtosis up to 1.6 [57]. However, studies suggest that skewness and kurtosis over 1 may reduce statistical power [58] and that extreme departures from normality (e.g., skewness ≈ 1.8 or kurtosis ≈ 3.8) can impair estimation stability [57]. In our dataset, 27 items were evaluated. Most exhibited skewness and kurtosis values within the recommended ± 1 range, indicating only mild non-normality. Only one item had a skewness greater than 1, with a maximum absolute skewness of 1.02. Four items exceeded a kurtosis = 1, with a maximum kurtosis of 1.19. These values remain well below the thresholds associated with estimation issues in PLS-SEM. Given these distributional characteristics and the large sample size, we concluded that using PLS-SEM was appropriate for our data and that deviations from normality are unlikely to compromise model estimation or statistical power.

The reflective lower-order constructs (LOCs) were first evaluated based on standardized outer loadings, internal consistency reliability, and convergent and discriminant validity (Table 1). The initial estimation revealed that most indicators exceeded the recommended loading threshold of 0.70. However, three indicators, PU_1, JD_WLB_3, and JD_satu_1, displayed substantially lower loadings (0.25, 0.53, and 0.47 respectively). Although all three reflected conceptually relevant facets of their constructs, their weak empirical performance reduced the Average Variance Extracted (AVE) of the corresponding LOCs and generated heterogeneity in the loading structure.

Overall, the refined measurement model demonstrates satisfactory reliability and convergent validity (Table 1), strong indicator specificity based on outer and cross-loadings (Table 2), and acceptable discriminant validity via both the Fornell–Larcker criterion (Table 3) and HTMT (Table 4). Removing the three underperforming indicators increased the parsimony, stability, and interpretability of the reflective constructs without compromising theoretical coverage, confirming the appropriateness of the refinement.

Beyond the constructs affected by item removal, overall internal consistency reliability was satisfactory across the full set of reflective LOCs. Cronbach’s alpha coefficients ranged from 0.63 (RECO) to 0.95 (REMU), composite reliability (ρC) ranged from 0.68 (CBI_PA) to 0.95 (REMU), and Dijkstra–Henseler reliability (ρA) ranged from 0.68 (CBI_PA) to 1.00 (RECO). All values met or exceeded the recommended 0.70 threshold, with the exception of CBI_PA (α = 0.68; ρC = 0.68; ρA = 0.68), which remains acceptable in exploratory research given the conceptual specificity of patient-related burnout. Convergent validity was likewise supported at the construct level: Average Variance Extracted (AVE) exceeded 0.50 for all LOCs except JD_satu (AVE = 0.46), which is marginally below the guideline but theoretically justified and empirically stable following refinement.

Job resources was modeled formatively, composed of two non-interchangeable indicators capturing different sources of recognition. Multicollinearity diagnostics revealed that both indicators showed low VIF values (1.26), well below the recommended thresholds of 3.3 proposed by Diamantopoulos and Winklhofer [60]. Bootstrapped outer weights (5,000 resamples) indicated that both RECO_1 and RECO_2 significantly contributed to the composite (RECO_1: β = 0.44, t = 3.64, 95% CI [0.20; 0.66]; RECO_2: β = 0.72, t = 7.20. 95% CI [0.50; 0.90]). This confirms the relevance of both formative indicators in defining the recognition construct (Table 5).

The model accounts for a substantial proportion of variance in key outcomes. Strain (CBI) shows an R² of 0.49, indicating that almost half of the variance in burnout is associated with job demands, perceived usefulness, their interaction, and the control variables. Vocational meaning (VOCA) is also well explained (R² = 0.41), while remuneration fairness (REMU) reaches a moderate R² of 0.16. Perceived usefulness (PU) has a smaller, but non-marginal R² of 0.09, reflecting that it is shaped both by job demands and by demographic characteristics such as age of health professionals.

Hypothesis 1

proposed that job demands are positively associated with strain. This hypothesis is strongly supported by our results. Job demands were strongly and positively associated with strain (β = 0.67, SE = 0.02, t = 36.72, 95% CI [0.63; 0.70]), indicating that higher levels of combined workload saturation, administrative overload, and work–life imbalance are associated with markedly higher strain symptoms. Hypothesis 2 posited a negative association between burnout and vocational attachment. The results support this expectation. Strain was negatively associated with job attachment (β = − 0.48, SE = 0.02, t = − 21.72, 95% CI [–0.53; − 0.44]), indicating that higher strain is associated with lower vocational meaning and identification with one’s profession. Hypothesis 3 posited that job resources would be positively associated with job satisfaction. This hypothesis is clearly supported (β = 0.40 SE = 0.03, t = 15.77, 95% CI [0.35; 0.45]) and indicate that professionals who feel recognized by peers and by institutional/societal actors are also more likely to perceive their remuneration as fair. Hypothesis 4 proposed that perceived Job satisfaction would positively influence vocational attachment which appeared supported by our data (β = 0.18, SE = 0.02, t = 8.45, 95% CI [0.14; 0.23]). Together, these results are consistent with a resource-based view in which recognition appears as a relational resource associated with stronger distributive justice perceptions, which are themselves associated with higher vocational attachment.

Hypothesis 5

stated HIT perceived as useful would negatively moderate the relationship between job demands and strain, with the positive relationship between job demands and strain weaker when HIT used by physicians are perceived as more useful. The interaction term JDxPU has a small but statistically significant negative interaction coefficient on strain (β = − 0.05, SE = 0.02, t = − 2.33, 95% CI [–0.09; − 0.01]). This indicates that, although job demands remain the strongest correlate of strain in the model, HIT perceived as useful by healthcare professionals are associated with a slightly weaker positive relationship between job demands and burnout symptoms. Hypothesis 6 proposed that higher job demands would be associated with a lower perceived usefulness of digital resources. The results support this linear, negative association. In the structural model including a single linear path from Job demands to PU (controlling for age, sex, and professional specialty), job demands are negatively associated with perceived usefulness (β = − 0.22, SE = 0.03, t = − 6.78, 95% CI [–0.28; − 0.16]). This suggests that, as health professionals face more intense combined clinical, administrative, and work–life boundary demands, they tend to perceive their HIT as less useful in supporting their work. The model explains a modest but meaningful proportion of variance in perceived usefulness (R² = 0.10; adj. R² = 0.09), indicating that job demands and controls jointly account for about 10% of the variability in how useful digital resources are perceived.

Age, sex, and professional specialty (with medical doctors as the reference category, and dummy variables for psychologists and paramedical professionals) were included as control variables on all endogenous constructs (Strain, Job satisfaction, Job attachment, and perceived usefulness of HIT). The main purpose of these controls is to distinguish demographic and professional differences so that the core JD–R and PU effects can be interpreted more cleanly. Younger professionals tended to report higher levels of strain, as reflected in the negative coefficient for age (β = − 0.14). By contrast, sex and specialty coefficients were small in magnitude (β ≤ 0.03) and showed only limited additional contribution to explained variance beyond job demands.

Younger professionals, women, and especially paramedical professionals tended to perceive their remuneration as slightly less fair than older male medical doctors, consistent with the negative coefficients observed for age, sex, and specialty (β between − 0.03 and − 0.11). However, these coefficients remained modest compared with the association between recognition and job satisfaction.

Psychologists showed a relatively strong positive coefficient for job attachment (β ≈ 0.24), and paramedical professionals a smaller positive coefficient (β ≈ 0.09), indicating that, net of strain and remuneration, these groups tended to report higher vocational attachment than physicians. Sex also showed a small positive association with job attachment (β ≈ 0.07), suggesting slightly higher vocational centrality among women, whereas age was essentially unrelated to vocational meaning (β ≈ − 0.01).

Older professionals tended to report somewhat lower perceived usefulness of digital tools, consistent with the negative coefficient observed for age (β ≈ − 0.20). Coefficients for sex and specialty were again small (β < 0.05) and indicated only minor demographic differences in perceived usefulness.

Discussion

JD–R relationships among self-employed healthcare professionals

This study supports the relevance of the Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) model [15, 62, 63] in the context of self-employed healthcare professionals in France. The positive association between job demands and strain is consistent with prior JD–R applications in healthcare [11–13], as well as with stress appraisal theory, which conceptualizes strain as a negative response to excessive or threatening demands [10]. The magnitude of this association (β = 0.67; R² = 0.49) suggests that even in decentralized outpatient practice, characterized by autonomy and flexible self-organization, the accumulation of clinical workload, administrative tasks, and work–life intrusion remains closely associated with burnout symptoms. Strain was also negatively associated with vocational attachment. This result is consistent with prior research showing that burnout is linked to lower engagement and commitment in healthcare professions [15, 25]. In self-employed practice, vocational meaning is not expressed through turnover intentions in the traditional organizational sense, but through broader judgments about the value, desirability, and transmissibility of the profession to younger generations. In this respect, the negative association between strain and vocational attachment (β = − 0.48; R² = 0.41) suggests that burnout may also be associated with lower perceived sustainability of liberal care provision.

The findings also support the motivational side of the JD–R model. Recognition, conceptualized here as a composite of patient and societal/institutional recognition, was positively associated with perceived fairness of remuneration, which in turn was positively associated with vocational attachment. This is consistent with research emphasizing the role of justice and symbolic appreciation in supporting job satisfaction and professional motivation [15, 41]. As self-employed professionals operate in a regulatory and public environment where remuneration and recognition remain central to professional identity, these resources appear closely linked to engagement and meaning at work.

Overall, these results suggest that JD–R relationships previously observed in hospital-based or salaried contexts are also relevant in French outpatient care. From a health services perspective, this indicates that workforce sustainability in self-employed practice is likely to require not only reducing clinical and administrative pressures, but also on maintaining relational and symbolic resources such as recognition and perceived fairness of treatment.

A self-limiting mechanism of HIT’s perceived usefulness in high job demands context

An important contribution of this study lies in the finding that higher job demands were associated with lower perceived usefulness of HIT. This result suggests that usefulness is associated not only by the intrinsic qualities of digital tools, but also by the cognitive, attentional, and temporal resources required to take advantage of them. When job demands intensify, these resources may become more limited, which could reduce professionals’ ability to notice, engage with, or benefit from the functionalities offered by HIT, even when these tools may be objectively helpful.

This interpretation is consistent with prior information-systems research showing that technology outcomes depend not only on system design, but also on users’ situational capacity to identify and integrate digital resources into their work. From this perspective, HIT functionalities may be useful in principle but remain difficult to mobilize when professionals face high workload, administrative pressure, and work–life intrusion. Similar difficulties have been observed in healthcare settings where professionals may have limited time to acknowledge, learn, or appropriate new systems [64] or simply lack the time to use them properly [65]. Affordance theory [66] offers a useful lens here. Affordance is “a goal-oriented potential of action which originates from an actor and artefact relationship” [67], namely here what HIT can do to assist healthcare professionals. Yet, prior work shows that these affordances may or may not be actualized [30]. Non-actualization may arise because users do not perceive the affordance, lack the resources to use the artefact, or pursue goals that diverge from those supported by the system [30, 31]. In other words, regardless of how useful HIT functionalities may be in principle in helping health professionals to address their job demands, if they lack the opportunity to perceive them or the time to use them efficiently, their perception of HIT usefulness may accordingly decrease.

These results point to an important self-limiting pattern in the functioning of HIT as a job resource. Perceived usefulness was associated with a small but significant buffering effect on the relationship between job demands and strain. However, perceived usefulness itself was lower when job demands were higher. This dual pattern is consistent with a paradox documented in IS and organizational research, where a tool may help alleviate strain while being constrained, or even undermined, by the very pressures that make it valuable [17, 68].

This dynamic suggests that HIT may operate effectively as a resource only up to a certain level of demand. Beyond this level, professionals may become less able to actualize the support that digital systems can provide. This interpretation resonates with the “too-much-of-a-good-thing” logic described by Vitari et al. [69], whereby the perceived value of digital resources may be lower once contextual demands exceed users’ cognitive and temporal capacity. Although the composite model of job demands showed only a linear association with perceived usefulness, a post-hoc exploratory test of quadratic patterns revealed no curvature at the aggregated level but did uncover a meaningful quadratic component for administrative overload, a well-known source of burden in healthcare [13, 70]. The quadratic term for administrative overload was significant (β² = − 0.069, SE = 0.031, t = − 2.22, 95% CI [–0.132; − 0.0077]). This pattern suggests that perceived usefulness of HIT may initially increase slightly as administrative burden rises, but only up to a moderate level. Beyond that point, the relationship appears to reverse direction, and perceived usefulness is lower, consistent with the negative linear trend posited in H6.

Overall, these findings highlight a reciprocal constraint. HIT may be associated with a weaker relationship between job demands and strain, yet escalating demands are also associated with lower perceived usefulness of HIT. This tension suggests that interventions should operate not only at the technical level, by aligning HIT functionalities with the most burdensome aspects of practice, but also at the structural level, by reducing administrative complexity and acknowledging the cognitive costs of digital work. Without such measures, the potential of HIT to function as a resource may remain fragile, particularly when professionals face the highest demands.

HIT as a conditional and limited job resource

A complementary finding concerns the role of perceived usefulness of HIT as a digital job resource associated with a weaker relationship between job demands and strain. The interaction coefficient was small but statistically significant, indicating that perceived usefulness of HIT was associated with a slightly weaker positive relationship between job demands and strain. This finding is consistent with the JD–R buffer hypothesis [63], which posits that resources become particularly important when job demands are high. It also aligns with information systems research conceptualizing IT as a digital job resource that supports autonomy and efficiency [3, 25]. In demanding healthcare settings, HIT perceived as useful may be experienced as helping professionals cope with high levels of demand, although this supportive role remains limited in magnitude.

The absence of a direct association between perceived usefulness of HIT on strain is also informative. It is consistent with the Technology Acceptance Model [26], which frames perceived usefulness as an instrumental belief related to performance rather than emotional well-being. Recent HIT studies similarly show that usefulness supports task effectiveness but is not directly associated with lower psychological strain [2, 17]. In this sense, the role of perceived usefulness in our model is not that of a direct predictor of lower strain, but rather that of a conditional resource associated with a modest attenuation of the relationship between job demands and strain.

These results also complete the technostress literature, which mostly focuses on the negative effects of HIT, such as techno-overload or techno-invasion, on strain. We here identify a pattern consistent with a modest buffering role. The present findings point to a more nuanced role of digital tools. HIT may be associated with strain when they are experienced as adding burden, but they may also operate as conditional resources when they are perceived as useful. This contributes to understanding the complex interplay of digital tools as both potential stressors and conditional resources, a duality well documented in recent studies of digital work [17, 71–74]. It also reinforces the idea that usefulness may operate as a protective cognitive appraisal: when HIT help solve concrete work problems and are perceived as useful, they are associated with a modest reduction in the strength of the relationship between excessive demands and strain.

Taken together, this buffering effect should be interpreted cautiously. HIT perceived as useful may be associated with lower reported psychological cost associated with demanding work conditions, but they do not compensate for high job demands. More importantly, their perceived usefulness is itself lower when job demands are higher. This helps explain why the protective role of HIT remains conditional and limited. Digital tools are more likely to support healthcare professionals when work conditions allow them to perceive, appropriate, and use these tools as resources.

Practical implications for decision makers and healthcare managers

For healthcare decision makers, regional health agencies, professional bodies, and managers of outpatient organizations, the findings suggest that digital transformation should be accompanied by explicit demand-reduction strategies. HIT deployment should not be evaluated only through adoption rates or availability, but also through whether it removes tasks, reduces duplication, and preserves professionals’ cognitive and temporal resources. In practical terms, this implies simplifying administrative workflows before digitizing them, protecting time for training and appropriation, involving healthcare professionals in the configuration of digital tools, and monitoring whether new digital requirements create additional hidden work.

For healthcare managers and decision makers, the results also suggest that HIT should not be treated as a substitute for addressing structural workload problems. Digital tools may help professionals cope with some demands, but they cannot compensate for persistent clinical overload, administrative accumulation, or work–life boundary intrusion. Policies that rely on HIT to absorb increasing demands may therefore be counterproductive if they add reporting, documentation, or coordination tasks without removing existing burdens.

For health software providers, the priority is not only to add functionalities, but to ensure that core features remain easy to identify, learn, and mobilize under high workload conditions. This requires attention to interface simplicity, workflow fit, interoperability, and the reduction of unnecessary clicks or duplicate entries. More broadly, software providers, public authorities, and professional bodies should collaborate to ensure that digital transformation supports care work rather than transferring additional administrative responsibility to already overloaded professionals.

Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations that lead to avenues for future research. First, the questionnaire was intentionally compact to limit additional burden to self-employed healthcare professionals. Therefore, some constructs were measured with a reduced number of indicators. Although this choice aligns with established recommendations for parsimonious scale design in applied health research [42], future studies would benefit from more HIT-specific measures. Prior information systems work shows that clinicians’ evaluations and use of digital tools depend on the specific functionalities they use and on the context in which these functions are engaged [30, 31]. This indicates that more granular measurement of specific HIT mechanisms would provide a better understanding of the studied phenomenon.

Second, the survey was distributed through a single HIT ecosystem. While this facilitated access to a large national sample, relying on one vendor’s platform may introduce selection bias and limit variability in digital exposure. Respondents were necessarily already engaged, at least to some extent, with digital tools in routine practice and may therefore differ from less digitally equipped or more digitally reluctant professionals. Evidence from comparative EHR studies further suggests that different systems and vendors generate distinct patterns of workload, usability problems, and documentation burden [75]. The findings should therefore be generalized with caution beyond comparable digitally equipped outpatient settings. Future research could strengthen external validity by comparing professionals operating within different digital ecosystems or by using stratified sampling across varying levels of digitization.

Third, the cross-sectional design limits the extent to which temporal or causal ordering can be inferred. The observed relationships should therefore be interpreted as associations rather than as evidence of directional effects. Because all variables were collected through the same self-report questionnaire at a single point in time, common method variance cannot be entirely excluded, despite the procedural precautions and statistical checks reported in the Methods section. The observed relationships should therefore be interpreted as associations rather than as evidence of directional or causal effects. In addition, technostress and usefulness perceptions may evolve over time in response to changing work conditions, such as workload surges, staffing shortages, or system updates. Longitudinal studies would provide a more robust understanding of how job demands, perceived usefulness, and strain change as work environments evolve.

Finally, this study focused on perceived usefulness as the primary digital job resource, but digital work is multifaceted. Research has documented how HIT design and documentation processes can create substantial hidden workloads for clinicians, and how digital systems can consume a disproportionate share of clinicians’ time relative to patient care [76]. Future models should therefore incorporate dimensions such as usability, interoperability, documentation load, digital literacy, and trust in digital systems. Moreover, the exploratory quadratic effect observed for administrative overload suggests that under high administrative pressure, professionals may reevaluate the benefits of HIT in non-linear ways. This pattern warrants further investigation using more fine-grained HIT usage measures and task-specific digital activity indicators.

Conclusion

Self-employed healthcare professionals in France operate under structurally high job demands, combining clinical workload, administrative overload, and work–life boundary intrusion. Our results show that these cumulative pressures are strongly associated with elevated strain and with lower vocational attachment, raising concerns for the long-term sustainability of liberal care provision. Within this context, perceived usefulness of HIT emerges as a meaningful but fragile digital resource. On average, respondents reported a positive perception of HIT usefulness, and when perceived as useful, HIT perceived as useful are associated with a slightly weaker relationship between job demands and strain. However, our analyses also showed that perceived usefulness is lower at higher levels of job demands. Taken together, these findings point to a self-limiting mechanism: “HIT perceived as useful are associated with a weaker job demands–strain relationship, but higher demands are simultaneously associated with lower perceived usefulness.

These results carry important implications for public health policy and for health software providers. Policies that aim to expand or mandate HIT use cannot treat digital tools as a costless buffer to ever-increasing demands. They must explicitly address the underlying job demands of healthcare providers, avoid adding layers of administrative operations through digitalization, and temper the tendency to use HIT as a vehicle for new reporting or compliance tasks, which may produce counterproductive outcomes. For health software providers, the findings underline that the perceived usefulness of their solutions is likely to depend not only on intrinsic design qualities, but also on external features of the work context—staffing levels, regulatory workload, and the organization of care. It is therefore in their own strategic and corporate social responsibility interest to collaborate closely with public authorities, payers, and professional bodies to reduce non-essential demands and streamline administrative processes. Such conditions are likely to be necessary for HIT to realise their potential as sustainable resources that support, rather than exhaust, self-employed healthcare professionals.
