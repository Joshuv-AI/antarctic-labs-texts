First aid for sports-related dental trauma: a systematic review of knowledge, attitudes, and educational gaps among physical education students
Authors: Tamara Pawlaczyk-Kamieńska, Weronika Kamieńska, Bartosz Jastrzębski, Patrycja Kamieńska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTraumatic dental injuries (TDIs) significantly impact children and adolescents, often occurring during contact sports. This study aims to synthesize literature on the knowledge, attitudes, and skills related to the prevention and first aid management of dental injuries among physical education students.Methods A systematic review was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. Six electronic databases (Web of Science, PubMed, Wiley Online Library, Dentistry & Oral Science Source, Medline, and SCOPUS) were searched for cross-sectional questionnaire-based studies evaluating knowledge, attitudes, and skills related to the prevention and first aid management of traumatic dental injuries among physical education students.Results The included studies consistently demonstrated insufficient knowledge of dental trauma management among physical education students, particularly regarding tooth avulsion, replantation procedures, appropriate storage media, and the practical use of mouthguards. Most respondents reported limited formal education in dental trauma first aid despite frequent exposure to sports-related injuries.Conclusion Physical education students demonstrate insufficient knowledge of emergency management of traumatic dental injuries and the use of mouthguards. Incorporating dental trauma education into physical education curricula may improve the preparedness of future trainers and teachers.Keywords: Tooth avulsion, Mouthguard, Sports-related injuries, Clinical guidelines

Introduction

Traumatic dental injuries (TDIs) are injuries affecting the teeth, periodontal tissues, and soft tissues of the oral cavity [1, 2]. These injuries can occur as isolated incidents, affecting only the teeth, periodontal tissues, and soft tissues of the head and their supporting structures, or they may be associated with other dentofacial injuries [3]. According to data from 2020 published by the International Association of Dental Traumatology (IADT), approximately 33% of individuals have experienced trauma to their permanent teeth, with a significant portion occurring before the age of 19 [3, 4].

Dental trauma is the second leading cause of early tooth loss, surpassed only by dental caries, highlighting its significance as a public health issue worldwide [1, 3–8]. Research suggests that sports activities result in dental injuries more frequently than is typically recognized [1, 3]. A significant proportion of dental trauma results from sports accidents, ranging from 13% to 39% [1, 3, 5, 7]. Athletes involved in contact sports have about a 10% chance per season of an orofacial injury, or 33% to 56% chance during their athletic career. Additionally, data indicate that 25% of children aged 11 to 13 sustain injuries during sports activities, with more than half occurring during competitions and the remainder during training [3, 5].

Sports such as American football, rugby, hockey, basketball, football (soccer), handball, water polo, and boxing are associated with a particularly high risk of traumatic dental injuries [5, 9]. Injuries to the teeth and surrounding tissues can lead to a range of issues, including aesthetic, functional, economic, and psychological challenges, especially for young patients. While these injuries predominantly affect children and adolescents, the aesthetic and psychological repercussions can extend into adulthood. Complications arising from dental trauma may include inhibited tooth root development, tooth resorption, necrosis, crown discoloration, and even tooth loss.

The symptoms associated with post-traumatic damage to dental tissues can vary widely, ranging from minor issues that require only periodic check-ups to more complex complications that may result in tooth loss [1, 3, 7]. The prognosis largely depends on the time elapsed since the injury and the effectiveness of the first aid provided, which is defined as immediate measures applied at the site of injury before professional dental care is available [1, 2]. Given the generally favourable outlook and the possibility of maintaining a healthy, complete set of teeth, it is crucial for caregivers of children and adolescents to be well-versed in basic first aid procedures during physical activities. Timely emergency care can significantly enhance not only the prognosis but also the likelihood of utilizing specific therapeutic interventions later on. Delays in treatment can lead to complications, including the potential loss of one or more teeth [2, 3].

It is well established that the use of mouthguards during sports activities can prevent traumatic dental injuries or significantly reduce their severity, however, although some studies suggest a potential association between mouthguard use and a reduced risk of concussion, the current evidence remains limited and inconclusive, precluding definitive conclusions [10–13]. Mouthguards are generally classified into three main types: stock (ready-made), boil-and-bite, and custom-made, which differ substantially in terms of fit, comfort, retention, and protective effectiveness [10]. When properly designed and fitted, mouthguards can absorb and disperse impact forces, thereby reducing their transmission to the teeth and alveolar structures and lowering the risk of orofacial injuries. Their use has also been associated with a reduced risk of temporomandibular joint injuries and jaw fractures; however, evidence suggesting a protective effect against concussion remains limited and inconclusive [9, 10]. Custom-made mouthguards fabricated by dental professionals take into account individual factors such as the athlete’s age and the specific sport practiced and have been shown not to impair athletic performance, respiratory function, or speech [4, 10–14].

First aid training provided to physical education students typically focuses on general medical emergencies, while dental trauma management is often insufficiently addressed or omitted, despite the elevated risk of dental injuries associated with sports activities and the crucial role of physical education teachers, instructors, and coaches in preventing and managing such injuries among children and adolescents. This includes understanding the effective use of mouthguards and being equipped to provide immediate first aid for TDI. Unfortunately, research indicates that many physical educators often lack adequate knowledge about dental injuries [15–21].

This systematic review aims to address a critical gap by evaluating and synthesizing the existing literature on the knowledge, attitudes, and skills related to the prevention and first aid management of dental injuries among physical education students, and all results and conclusions should be interpreted in the context of this specific population. Unlike narrative summaries, this study adheres to a systematic review methodology following PRISMA guidelines [22], guaranteeing a transparent and reproducible synthesis of existing evidence regarding knowledge gaps among physical education students.

Methods

Information sources and search strategy

The publication search followed a pre-established protocol defining the search strategy, study selection procedures, and methods for data synthesis. The search strategy was devised in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines (Fig. 1) [22]. A comprehensive search was conducted across six electronic databases: Web of Science, PubMed, Wiley Online Library, Dentistry & Oral Science Source, Medline, and SCOPUS. The search strategy combined MeSH terms and Boolean operators to identify studies addressing knowledge, attitudes, or competencies related to dental trauma management among physical education students. The search terms employed in the databases were: (“knowledge” OR “attitudes” OR “competency”) AND (“dental trauma” OR “dental injury” OR “tooth avulsion” OR “tooth fracture” OR “sports mouthguards” OR “mouthguards”) AND (“first aid” OR “dental trauma prophylaxis” ) AND (“physical education students” OR “students of physical education”). Additionally, to identify publications not included in the databases, the electronic search was complemented with a manual search of the reference lists of all relevant articles. The final search was conducted on May 20, 2025.

PRISMA flowchart of the study selection process

Eligibility criteria

The initial selection of articles was performed through title and abstract screening using the PICO framework (Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome) [22]. The research question was defined as follows: Population (P) – physical education students; Intervention (I) – questionnaire-based assessment of knowledge related to first-aid management of dental injuries and the use of sports mouthguards; Comparison (C) – not applicable; Outcome (O) – knowledge-related outcomes. Studies involving mixed populations (e.g., physical education students combined with students from other disciplines, teachers, or coaches) were excluded because population-specific data could not be extracted. Only original cross-sectional questionnaire-based studies were included, whereas randomized controlled trials and cohort studies were excluded, as they did not align with the predefined PICO framework.

Study eligibility was determined based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Inclusion criteria were: (1) original cross-sectional questionnaire-based studies, (2) physical education students as the study population, (3) assessment of knowledge, attitudes, or skills related to dental trauma prevention, first aid management, and/or mouthguard use, (4) full-text articles published in English or Polish. Exclusion criteria: (1) studies involving mixed populations (e.g. teachers, coaches, students of other disciplines), (2) interventional studies, randomized controlled trials, and cohort studies, (3) reviews, case reports, conference abstracts, and editorials, (4) studies with insufficient or non-extractable data.

Study selection

The selection of studies was carried out independently by three researchers (KW, KP, JB), who were previously calibrated using 10% of the publications to ensure consistent application of the inclusion criteria for further analysis. The reviewers demonstrated strong agreement, as indicated by a kappa value of 0.82. Initially, the three researchers screened titles and abstracts to eliminate duplicates and determine which articles would be included in the review using the established screening tool. Subsequently, the full texts of the identified articles were obtained and assessed to determine their eligibility for inclusion in the comprehensive analysis. Any discrepancies between the three primary screeners were resolved by the fourth reviewer (P-KT).

Data collection process

Data was collected from the analyzed studies, which included demographic information, study purposes, methods used, results, and conclusions. Survey questions with similar structures and assessment criteria were grouped into question groups (QGs). These were then analyzed and presented as a " Knowledge-Skills-Attitudes Heat Map " (KSA – Heat Map) [23, 24], where each color represented the level of knowledge among the surveyed physical education students (levels: < 25%, 25–50%, 51–75%, and > 75%). Two reviewers (KP and JB) were responsible for extracting the data from the included studies. To minimize errors and bias, a third reviewer (KW) was tasked with verifying the accuracy of the extracted data. Duplicate publications of the same studies were evaluated only once.

Assessment of methodological quality

Methodological quality and risk of bias were independently assessed by two reviewers (KW and KP) using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) Critical Appraisal Checklist for analytical cross-sectional studies. This standardized tool evaluates key methodological aspects, including sample representativeness, recruitment bias, objectivity, and reliability. Studies were categorized as having low, moderate, or high risk of bias based on the proportion of criteria fulfilled. Any discrepancies between reviewers were resolved through discussion with a third reviewer (JB) [25].

Synthesis

The analytical approach was predefined in the study protocol. Due to substantial methodological and statistical heterogeneity among the included studies—particularly regarding questionnaire design, outcome measures, and reporting formats—a quantitative synthesis and meta-analysis were not feasible. Therefore, a qualitative narrative synthesis was conducted. The findings were summarized using detailed textual descriptions and structured tables, and further illustrated with a KSA - heat map to facilitate comparison across the included studies. The systematic review framework facilitated the organized identification, selection, and synthesis of evidence, as depicted in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1), promoting methodological transparency and reproducibility of the review process.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

A systematic search of medical information databases was conducted, and duplicates were removed. This process identified 41 publications that met our established search criteria. These scientific reports were then screened based on their titles and abstracts. After reviewing the full texts, 21 publications were deemed suitable for further analysis. Ultimately, 10 publications were selected for qualitative analysis (Fig. 1). Studies are referred to consistently by reference number as shown in Tables 1 and 2. The PRISMA flowchart (Fig. 1) demonstrates the comprehensive multi-step selection process used in our study. It illustrates the significant decrease in the number of records identified initially compared to the final set of included studies. This reduction underscores the strict inclusion criteria applied and the limited availability of high-quality research specifically targeting physical education students.

Characteristics of the studies included in the systematic review

Qualitative analysis

The studies included in this review were conducted between 2005 and 2024 across various regions, including Asia [25], South America [16, 27–31], and Europe [32–34] (Table 1). The publications collectively included 1,908 physical education students, with participant numbers in individual studies ranging from 39 [31] to 392 [28].

In the research conducted by Oleszkiewicz and Emerich [32], only final-year students were included. In contrast, Soares et al. [31] focused on fourth-year students, while Terra et al. [27] examined both third and fourth-year students. The remaining studies [16, 26, 28, 30] involved participants from all years of study. One publication [34] did not specify the academic years of the surveyed students; however, it included individuals who had engaged in related sports modalities for at least 10 years and trained for at least 8 h daily (Table 1). However, the duration and structure of physical education programs may vary across countries, and detailed information regarding the academic curriculum or program length was not consistently available in the included studies.

The aim of the studies included in the systematic review [16, 26–34] was to assess the knowledge of physical education students regarding TDI. Furthermore, four of these publications [16, 29, 33, 34] specifically targeted the evaluation of knowledge and skills related to the use of mouthguards (Table 2). The studies analyzed incorporated survey questions that examined the sociodemographic profiles of the participants, as well as queries regarding their training in dental trauma, their experiences and attitudes towards this type of injury, their self-assessed knowledge of dental trauma, and scenario-based questions addressing the emergency management of dental trauma, among other relevant topics.

The KSA - heat map detailing the studies included in the systematic review is presented in Tables 3 and 4, and 5. Five publications included inquiries about the training received regarding first aid [26, 27, 29, 30, 32], while seven publications focused on the provision of TDI first aid [16, 26–31]. Attendance rates for first aid classes among students varied significantly, ranging from 46% [30] to 84.5% [27]. In contrast, among the seven publications discussing pre-dental TDI aid, fewer than 11% of students reported attending the classes; only the study by Mekka et al. [26] reported a higher attendance rate of 46.74%. More than half of the surveyed students recognized that engaging in sports activities poses a risk for TDI, with awareness levels documented at 56.3% [29] and 87.7% [27]. Furthermore, a notable percentage of students (7–58%) [28–30, 32–34] reported having experienced a dental injury while participating in sports, while 14–61% [26, 27, 31, 32] had witnessed dental injuries. In one publication [27], students were asked about their interest in pursuing a professional career working with children and adolescents, to which over 47% of respondents expressed a positive interest (Table 3).

In the studies conducted by Panzarini et al. [28], only 5% of respondents correctly defined “tooth avulsion,” while 45% accurately described “tooth replantation.” However, approximately 75% reported understanding the meaning of “tooth replantation” [16, 28]. Another publication by Soares et al. [31], which assessed students’ first-aid knowledge through a survey, found that the average number of correct responses to 10 questions was 4 ± 2. Notably, over 70% of students [28, 30] expressed doubts regarding their understanding of TDI first aid. A significant portion of respondents emphasised the importance of dental education in managing dental trauma. In five publications [16, 26, 28–30], more than 90% indicated the necessity for such education, while one study reported that 67.3% [32] shared this sentiment (Table 3). In six studies [16, 26–29, 33], students were asked how they would respond upon witnessing a traumatic dental injury. The percentage of respondents who would immediately refer the individual to a dentist ranged from 16% [16] to 85% [27], whereas those who would guide the person to a basic health unit varied from 2% [16] to 50% [28] (Table 4).

Questions concerning the appropriate management of tooth fractures were explored in two studies [26, 27]. The percentage of respondents demonstrating knowledge of how to handle a tooth crown fracture varied considerably, ranging from 15% [27] to 80% [26]. One publication [29] examined the management of primary tooth avulsion and found that only 12% of respondents were aware of the correct procedure. When it came to recognizing the importance of replanting an avulsed permanent tooth, fewer than 25% of participants acknowledged this in three studies [16, 26, 32]. In contrast, two additional studies reported knowledge levels of 25%-50% [28, 34], while one study reported that 57% [29] understood the significance of replantation. The ability to manage an avulsed tooth by replanting it into the socket showed variability, with knowledge levels ranging from 8.26% to 43.20% across multiple studies [28–30, 33]. Only 20% of respondents recognized the necessity of preserving an avulsed tooth [16]. Additionally, fewer than 41% of respondents were aware of the critical extra-alveolar time [16, 28, 32, 34]. Knowledge of the correct cleaning techniques for an avulsed tooth prior to replantation was reported in 4 publications, with awareness levels ranging from 25% to 50% [16, 28, 29, 32], while one study [26] reported a slightly higher figure of 51%. Furthermore, less than 50% of respondents were informed about the appropriate storage medium for an avulsed tooth [16, 26, 28, 29, 32, 34] (Table 4).

Four publications [16, 29, 33, 34] investigated the knowledge and competence of physical education students regarding the use of mouthguards (see Table 5). In the study conducted by Soğukpınar Önsüren et al. [33], 69.3% of respondents agreed with the statement, “Do you think mouthguards protect athletes from traumatic injuries?” This percentage increased to 83.4% in the study by de Oliveira et al. [29]. However, fewer than 25% of physical education students reported using mouthguards during sports [16, 29, 33, 34]. Self-assessments of knowledge concerning mouthguards, their effects, types, and indications for use yielded positive responses ranging from 27.5% [33] to 84% [29] among the participants. Furthermore, the need for dental education on mouthguards was acknowledged by 67.3% [33] to 91% [29] of students.

Discussion

Timely assessment and appropriate management are crucial for many types of traumatic dental injuries, but the urgency of intervention varies based on the type and severity of the injury. For instance, uncomplicated enamel fractures may only require a dental evaluation and follow-up, whereas tooth avulsion is a true dental emergency that requires immediate action [2, 3]. Emergency care plays a critical role in improving prognosis and increasing the likelihood of tooth preservation, particularly in cases of tooth avulsion, which may involve damage to the neurovascular bundle and surrounding periodontal tissues. Tooth avulsion should always be treated as a dental emergency. According to the IADT guidelines, the optimal management of avulsed permanent teeth is immediate replantation, preferably at the site of injury and ideally within 1 h [3, 35]. After assessing the condition of the socket, the avulsed tooth should be reinserted by grasping it by the crown and aligning it with the adjacent teeth [35]. The patient should then be referred immediately to a dentist, where the replanted tooth can be stabilized with a flexible splint for an appropriate period according to current IADT guidelines. The success of replantation largely depends on the duration the tooth remains outside the socket. However, the developmental status of the avulsed tooth is also a critical prognostic factor. Immature teeth with open apices demonstrate greater healing potential and a higher likelihood of pulpal revascularization following timely replantation. In contrast, mature teeth with closed apices carry a higher risk of pulp necrosis and may lead to long-term complications. Replantation performed more than one hour after injury is classified as delayed and is associated with a less favorable prognosis and a higher risk of complications, including periodontal ligament necrosis [3, 31]. In such cases, the tooth should be stored and transported in physiological solutions, such as Hanks’ Solution, saline, milk, or saliva [1, 31].

Although tooth avulsion is often emphasized because of its urgency, crown fractures are significantly more prevalent in sports-related dental trauma. Therefore, first aid education for physical education students should also address the immediate management of fractured teeth and the basic principles of splinting following replantation, in accordance with the IADT guidelines. This broader educational focus better reflects the spectrum of dental injuries encountered in real-life sports settings.

As reported in the Results section, numerous studies have highlighted the high incidence of dental trauma among athletes, with prevalence varying depending on the type of sport. Dental injuries occur particularly frequently in contact sports such as soccer, basketball, volleyball, and boxing [11, 14, 36–38]. The prognosis of an injured tooth depends largely on appropriate emergency management, often provided by individuals without formal medical training, such as parents, teachers, coaches, and physical education instructors, who are commonly present at the site of injury. Consequently, it is essential that those responsible for supervising children and adolescents during sports activities possess adequate knowledge and skills in first-aid management of traumatic dental injuries. However, as demonstrated in the Results section, the available literature consistently indicates insufficient knowledge of dental trauma management among physical education students and educators [15–17, 37]. In this context, the management of traumatic dental injuries requires close collaboration between medical and dental professionals. While physicians, physical education teachers, and first responders are often the first to assess and manage injuries at the trauma site, dentists play a crucial role in providing definitive diagnosis, treatment, and long-term follow-up care. Effective communication between disciplines is essential to ensure appropriate referral pathways and timely interventions. Incorporating basic knowledge of dental trauma into medical and physical education training can enhance this collaboration and improve patient outcomes. This underscores the importance of an integrated healthcare approach in managing sports-related injuries to the orofacial region.

As reported in the Results section, findings from Chan et al. [17] indicate that although the vast majority of teachers (98.8%) have received general first aid training, only a small proportion have received specific education on dental trauma management. The present review demonstrates that a similar educational gap exists among physical education students. While many participants reported attendance at first aid courses, dental trauma prevention and management were frequently absent or insufficiently covered within these programs. This gap is particularly concerning given the high level of exposure of physical education students to sports-related injuries.

Several included studies reported that a substantial proportion of physical education students were active athletes themselves and had either experienced traumatic dental injuries or witnessed such events during sports activities [27–29, 33]. The percentage of students reporting personal experience with dental injuries ranged from 7% to 35% in most studies [28–30, 32, 33], while one study reported a notably higher incidence of 57.9% [34]. This discrepancy may stem from their inclusion of not only dental trauma but also various other forms of oral injuries, collectively termed orofacial trauma. These injuries were categorized into three distinct types: (i) soft tissue injuries (affecting the cheeks, lips, and tongue), (ii) bone tissue injuries (involving the maxilla and mandible), and (iii) dental injuries (encompassing hard and periodontal tissues of the teeth). Interestingly, there was no statistically significant difference in the frequency of dental trauma among students engaged in different sports modalities [16, 29, 34]. Given that this demographic is statistically more prone to TDIs, it is surprising to note the prevailing lack of awareness, particularly among those who have either experienced or witnessed dental injuries, regarding the proper response procedures, first aid measures, and the correct way to preserve a knocked-out tooth.

Research conducted by Chan et al. [17] and Jorge et al. [16] among educators indicates a consensus among educators on the need for training in the management of dental trauma. A review of literature concerning the knowledge of physical education students indicates that this cohort similarly recognizes the need for education in this area [16, 26, 28–30, 33]. Additionally, studies by Panzarini et al. [28] and Jorge et al. [16] indicate that only 5% of participants have a clear understanding of the term “tooth avulsion,” whereas 45% can accurately define “replantation.” However, participants tend to overestimate their own knowledge of these concepts.

In the study conducted by Chan et al. [17], approximately 70% of teachers expressed confidence in their ability to respond appropriately in the event of a tooth fracture. However, only about 20% correctly identified the procedure for tooth avulsion [16, 17]. While more than 35–60% of teachers deemed it “very urgent” to seek specialized assistance, many lacked knowledge regarding the proper procedures for the replantation or storage of avulsed teeth [16, 17]. The analysis in this study corroborates these findings, revealing that fewer than 40% of students know the critical time for replantation. Furthermore, there is a notable lack of knowledge concerning the correct cleaning technique for an avulsed tooth prior to replantation, as well as the appropriate storage medium. Students proposed various cleaning methods, including using a toothbrush on the root and crown, rinsing with tap water, or not cleaning it at all [26, 28]. For storage, students suggested a variety of options, including tap water, mineral water, ice, and dry methods such as napkins, paper tissues, and cotton balls [28]. Delayed and improper treatment is associated with a high risk of treatment failure, which can jeopardise the vitality of periodontal cells crucial for the healing process. Although tooth avulsion presents significant risks of complications, replantation should always be considered. Delayed replantation often results in a poor long-term prognosis, as the periodontal ligaments may become nonviable, frequently culminating in alveolar bone resorption [1, 3]. Ultimately, the aim of replantation is to preserve the tooth for aesthetic, functional, and psychological reasons, as well as to maintain the integrity of the alveolar bone.

To reduce the impact force during sports activities, athletes are advised to wear mouthguards. Properly fitted mouthguards are designed to absorb high-impact energy, preventing the transfer of force to oral tissues, including teeth and alveolar bone [10, 13]. Mouthguards can be categorized into three types based on their method of fabrication: ready-made, boil-and-bite, and custom-fitted. Research indicates that custom-fitted mouthguards provide superior comfort, adaptation, retention, and protection for oral tissues. They are also the only suitable option for individuals with mixed dentition or fixed orthodontic appliances [10]. In contrast, poorly fitted mouthguards, such as standard or boil-and-bite versions, can interfere with speech and breathing, potentially hindering athletic performance [12]. Conversely, custom mouthguards do not obstruct sports activities, body efficiency, or respiratory functions [4, 9, 10]. The present review further demonstrates that, although awareness of mouthguards among physical education students is relatively high, their actual use remains limited. As reported in the Results section, while a majority of students acknowledged the protective role of mouthguards, fewer than 25% reported using them during sports activities [16, 29, 33, 34]. Commonly reported barriers to mouthguard use included lack of opportunity, perceived lack of necessity, discomfort, interference with communication, and breathing difficulties [29].

Analysis of the included studies indicates that many physical education students demonstrate limited knowledge of injury prevention, particularly regarding mouthguard use and dental trauma management. As reported in the Results section, although a considerable proportion of students had attended first aid courses, only a minority of these programs included content specifically related to traumatic dental injuries. Given the learning outcomes expected of physical education graduates, including knowledge, practical skills, and social competencies, this educational gap warrants attention.

The present review demonstrates a clear discrepancy between the high risk of sports-related dental trauma and the limited preparedness of physical education students to manage such injuries effectively. To address this gap, structured educational interventions focused on dental trauma prevention and first-aid management should be incorporated into physical education curricula. These interventions should include both theoretical instruction and practical components, such as simulations and scenario-based training conducted in collaboration with dental professionals. Strengthening education in this area may improve preparedness among future physical education teachers and trainers and help reduce sports-related dental injuries among children and adolescents.

When proposing the inclusion of new educational content in undergraduate curricula, one important consideration is the need to balance the overall curricular load. To incorporate dental trauma management training, it may be necessary to modify or integrate it within existing first aid or emergency medicine modules instead of creating entirely new standalone courses. Given the already dense structure of physical education programs, curriculum designers should consider embedding dental trauma education into current first aid training, sports safety modules, or injury prevention courses. This integrative approach can enhance educational efficiency without overwhelming students.

This work aims to analyse, synthesise, and comprehensively evaluate the findings of published scientific studies on the knowledge of physical education students regarding the prevention and first-aid management of traumatic dental injuries, as well as their awareness and use of mouthguards. However, this systematic review has several limitations. First, only studies published in English and Polish were included, potentially excluding relevant research published in other languages. Second, the search strategy was limited to six databases, potentially missing studies indexed in other databases. Furthermore, the studies included in this review demonstrated substantial methodological heterogeneity, including differences in questionnaire design, validation status, item number, and outcome measures, which limits the direct comparability of the results. In addition, most studies were conducted in a limited number of countries, primarily in South America and Europe, which may restrict the generalizability of the findings to regions with different educational systems and sports cultures. Another factor that may affect reported knowledge levels is the academic stage of the students in the studies analyzed. Some studies focused exclusively on final-year students, while others included participants from all years of study. Since clinical exposure and curriculum content typically increase as students progress through their training, differences in academic level may help explain the observed variability in knowledge across these studies.

Due to heterogeneity in study design, outcome definitions, and assessment tools, a meta-analysis was not feasible; therefore, the findings were synthesized using a qualitative narrative approach. This represents a valid and appropriate method for integrating evidence when a meta-analysis is not feasible. The observed variability among the included studies may be explained by several factors, including differences in questionnaire design and validation, variations in the number and types of survey items, discrepancies in educational curricula across countries, and differences in participants’ academic stage. In addition, the studies assessed diverse aspects of dental trauma knowledge, ranging from theoretical definitions to scenario-based clinical decision-making, further limiting the direct comparability of the findings.

Conclusions

This systematic review reveals that physical education students often lack sufficient knowledge about managing traumatic dental injuries, particularly in handling avulsed teeth, replantation procedures, and proper post-injury care. While students generally have a positive attitude toward injury prevention, their understanding of mouthguard use remains inadequate. To address these gaps, the review recommends integrating structured educational modules on dental traumatology into physical education curricula, providing practical simulations for managing dental emergencies, and promoting the use of custom-made mouthguards during contact sports. Implementing these strategies could improve the preparedness of future educators and reduce sports-related dental injuries among children and adolescents.
