Understanding the metabolome of four stress-relieving and cognitive-enhancing medicinal plants: a comprehensive HR-LC-ESI-MS/MS analysis
Authors: Muhammad Noman Khan, Syed Muhammad Zaki Shah, Saeedur Rahman, Syed Ghulam Musharraf
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeHigh-resolution liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (HR-LC-MS/MS) enables comprehensive metabolite profiling of medicinal plants. This study aimed to characterize the metabolomes of four stress-relieving and cognitive-enhancing plants, Centella asiatica, Lavandula angustifolia, Herpestis monnieri, and Canscora decussata, and explore whether their similar therapeutic effects arise from shared or distinct chemical architectures.MethodsA dual acquisition approach combining Data-Dependent Acquisition (DDA) and Data-Independent Acquisition (DIA) was applied in both positive and negative ionization modes. Metabolites were annotated using targeted and untargeted workflows. Selected prominent metabolites were further analyzed using in-silico network pharmacology, linking metabolites to protein targets, pathways, and disease associations via SuperPred, UniProt, and DisGeNET databases.ResultsThe integrated DDA–DIA strategy identified 100 metabolites, including species-specific bioactive compounds. Minimal overlap was observed among plants, with only four metabolites shared between pairs. Heatmaps, PCA, and Venn diagrams confirmed distinct metabolomic fingerprints. Network pharmacology analysis revealed that prominent metabolites modulate multiple targets involved in neuronal signaling, DNA repair, immune regulation, and metabolic pathways, highlighting multi-target pharmacological convergence. Notably, neuroactive genes such as MAOA, ACHE, GRIA2, SLC6A5, and NTRK3 were commonly targeted, supporting the traditional use of these plants in cognitive enhancement, stress relief, and neuroprotection.ConclusionsDespite distinct metabolomic profiles, the four plants converge functionally at the pathway level, demonstrating that similar therapeutic effects can emerge via multi-target, chemically diverse mechanisms. This study provides a robust framework for integrated metabolomic and network pharmacology analyses, offering mechanistic insight, quality control guidance, and a foundation for future pharmacological validation of medicinal plants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11306-026-02411-x.

Introduction

Medicinal plants have long been used for their therapeutic effects and as a rich source of bioactive compounds. These plants and their formulations are considered natural remedies for various ailments. Among these, Centella asciatica (CA), Lavandula angustifolia (LA), Herpestis monnieria (HM), and Canscora decussata (CD) are some plants long used for medicinal values in different cultures, especially for their stress-relieving and cognitive-enhancing (All the plant names have been checked with http://www.worldfloraonline.org, accessed on: Mar 04, 2025). These plants are also chief constituents of Intellan, a polyherbal formulation long used for enhancing brain functions (Ahmad et al., 1994). Despite the shared therapeutic properties of the selected plants, little is understood about their underlying molecular mechanisms. This study investigates whether common or distinct metabolic profiles are responsible for the similar therapeutic effects of these plants.

The key contributors responsible for the observed pharmacological activities of such medicinal plants are their secondary metabolites. These bioactive compounds have very high structural diversity, which is why they exhibit a range of biological activities that underlie the traditional uses of these plants in folk medicines. Comprehending the intricate relationship between the secondary metabolites and the therapeutic effects of medicinal plants is crucial for understanding the full potential of these plants. The selected plants belong to different families, and demonstrate similar therapeutic properties, highlighting the diversity of molecular mechanisms that can lead to similar therapeutic outcomes. Investigating the unique metabolomic profiles and linking the diversity of metabolites to the therapeutic effects of these plants is critical for understanding the different molecular pathways that lead to similar therapeutic effects in these medicinal plants. This insight will not only help in the better use of these plants in medicines but it will also demonstrate the versatility of herbal medicines and help in developing more effective approaches for their standardization.

In the present work, advanced mass spectrometry techniques have been employed to identify the metabolites in aerial parts of Centella asciatica, Lavandula angustifolia, Herpestis monnieria, and Canscora decussata, comprehensively exploring the chemical constituents responsible for their reputed medicinal properties. Mass spectrometry is a reliable, sensitive, and high-throughput analytical technique that, when coupled with chromatographic techniques like Ultra-performance liquid chromatography (UPLC) can be used for the identification of compounds in complex mixtures. This study takes advantage of the complementary potentials of Data-Dependent Acquisition (DDA) and Data-Independent Acquisition (DIA) modes to broaden the metabolite coverage. The DDA mode is beneficial for detecting high-abundance metabolites, while DIA increases the metabolite coverage by the detection of both high- and low-abundance metabolites, as well as the co-eluting compounds. By utilizing these complementary modes, comprehensive metabolomic profiles of the selected plants were developed.

The medicinal plants selected for this study belong to a diverse range of families. These plants have been historically valued for their health advantages. Centella Asciatica is commonly called “Gotu Kola”. The aerial parts of this plant have been reported to possess anticancer (Pittella et al., 2009), antibacterial, antifungal (Dash et al., 2011), antinociceptive, anti-inflammatory (Somchit et al., 2004), neuroprotective (Sabaragamuwa et al., 2018), antioxidant (Orhan et al., 2013), wound healing (Yao et al., 2017), anti-depression (Kalshetty et al., 2012), antidiabetic (Abas et al., 2016), cognitive function improving (Gray et al., 2017), and hepatoprotective (Choi et al., 2016) potential. Some studies also suggested that this plant reduces migraine (Bobade et al., 2015) and has the potential to prevent atherosclerosis (Belcaro et al., 2015).

Lavandula angustifolia, which is commonly known as lavender, is well-known for its anti-inflammatory and calming properties. Many studies reported its neuromodulatory activities and suggested and evidenced its use for the treatment of epilepsy, depression, and anxiety (Patil et al., 2022), Other reported potentials include antioxidant, anti-microbial (Andrys et al., 2017), anti-inflammatory and analgesic(Hajhashemi et al., 2003).

Herpestis monnieria, which is also termed “Brahmi”, is known for its cognitive-enhancing properties as well. Studies frequently reported neuroprotection against amnesia (Kishore & Singh, 2005), memory dysfunction (Dwivedi et al., 2013), dementia, Alzheimer’s disease (Uabundit et al., 2010), Parkinson’s disease (Srivastav et al., 2017), schizophrenia (Giramkar et al., 2013), and epileptic convulsions (Giramkar et al., 2013) as the most evident therapeutic effects of this plant. Herpestis monnieria has also been shown to have anti-inflammatory (Channa et al., 2006), antimicrobial (Chaudhuri et al., 2004), anticonvulsant (Mathew et al., 2011), antihypercholesterolemic effect (Kamesh & Sumathi, 2012), antinociceptive (Rauf et al., 2012), antidepressant (Rauf et al., 2014), anti-aging (Rastogi et al., 2012), antiulcer (Choudhary et al., 2013), anticancer (Mallick et al., 2015), broncho-vasodilatory (Channa & Dar, 2012), anti-allergic (Taur & Patil, 2011), and hepatoprotective (Sumathi & Nongbri, 2008) properties.

Canscora decussata has been traditionally used for its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant attributes. Different studies reported it’s anti-inflammatory (Chaure et al., 2014), anti-diabetic (Irshad et al., 2015), antioxidant (Kousalya et al., 2023), cognition boosting (Sethiya et al., 2012), hepatoprotective (Akhtar et al., 2015), and anxiolytic (Sethiya et al. 2010a) activities.

The four medicinal plants selected in this study were chosen based on three key considerations: (i) their long-standing and independent use in traditional medicine systems for stress relief and cognitive enhancement, (ii) their inclusion as major constituents in polyherbal formulations such as Intellan, and (iii) their taxonomic diversity, spanning different plant families and phytochemical lineages. This deliberate selection enables a comparative metabolomic investigation aimed at determining whether shared therapeutic effects arise from overlapping bioactive compounds or from distinct chemical repertoires acting on convergent biological pathways.

In this study, we utilized both DDA and DIA modes of LC-MS to compare the metabolomic profiles of four medicinal plants. These chemical profiles offer a basis for understanding the therapeutic significance of these plants and open doors for future pharmacological studies. It also signifies the value of integrating complementary DDA and DIA strategies for metabolomic studies. Although Data-Dependent Acquisition (DDA) and Data-Independent Acquisition (DIA) strategies are individually well established in metabolomics, their integrated application across dual ionization modes remains underutilized for addressing biologically driven questions in botanical research. In the present study, this combined analytical framework is employed not merely to expand metabolite coverage, but to interrogate whether medicinal plants with comparable ethnopharmacological indications share common chemical determinants or instead rely on distinct metabolomic architectures to achieve similar therapeutic outcomes. By focusing on botanically unrelated species with convergent traditional uses, this work positions metabolomics as a tool for exploring pharmacological convergence rather than chemical redundancy.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

All solvents used for HPLC and sample preparation were commercially acquired. Analytical grade methanol was obtained from Merck K GaA, 64,271 (Darmstadt, Germany), while formic acid (> 95% pure) was procured from Daejung (Daejung Chemicals and Metals Co. Ltd, Korea). Type I water (resistivity 18.1 MΩ cm at 25 ℃) was generated in-house using a water purification system from Thermo Scientific, USA. Raw plant materials were acquired from verified commercial suppliers.

Sample preparation

Shade-dried aerial parts of the plants were individually pulverized using a blender. Subsequently, 1 g of each plant powder was precisely weighed and subjected to extraction with a 10 mL mixture of methanol and water (8:2) using sonication for 30 min. After extraction, each sample was centrifuged for 15 min at 6000 rpm to settle down the large particles, and the resulting supernatant was then filtered through a 0.22 μm PVDF syringe-driven filter. For LC-MS and LC-MS/MS analysis, 50 µL of the filtered extract was diluted to 1000 µL with methanol.

Equipment and experimental conditions

All the LC-MS and MS/MS analyses were performed using the Bruker maXis II HR-QTOF mass spectrometer (Bremen, Germany), coupled to the Dionex Ultimate 3000 series UPLC system from Thermo Fischer Scientific (Waltham, MA, USA). The UPLC system included an auto-sampler, a thermostatted column compartment, and a binary RS pump. The Macherey-Nagel™ Gravity column (3.0 × 100 mm, 1.8 μm) was used for the chromatography. The mobile phase consisted of 0.1% formic acid in water (A) and methanol (B). UPLC employed a linear gradient solvent composition, initiating with 10% B and increasing to 90% B in 6.5 min, maintaining 90% B for 1.5 min, then returning to 10% B in 1.0 min, with 1 min equilibration time before and after the gradient. A constant solvent flow rate was set at 0.5 mL/min. For each LCMS run, 5 µL of the samples were injected in both positive and negative ion modes.

Data acquisition

High-resolution LC-MS and MS/MS data were acquired in both positive and negative modes of ionization using Data-Dependent Acquisition (DDA) and Data-Independent Acquisition (DIA) strategies. In DDA mode, precursor ions were selected based on their intensities. This prioritizes the detection of high-abundance metabolites for MS/MS fragmentation, allowing the targeted fragmentation of the most prominent ions in each scan cycle. In the DIA approach, overlapping isolation windows were utilized which enables the simultaneous acquisition of MS/MS data for all precursor ions within each window.

Data processing and metabolite identification

Data processing was performed using different software including Compass DataAnalysis, TargetAnalysis, MS-Dial, and Skyline. Two complementary approaches were used to identify metabolites: targeted and untargeted identification. For targeted identification, a .csv file containing a comprehensive database of known metabolites and their molecular formulas was pre-compiled. These metabolites were screened against the LC-ESI-HR-MS spectra of the selected medicinal plants using Compass TargetAnalysis software was utilized. The key identification parameters included a mass error tolerance of ± 5 ppm and mSigma tolerance of ± 50. This approach allowed the precise matching of expected metabolites. For untargeted identification, DDA data was processed in MS-Dial, while Skyline was used for the deconvolution DIA peaks.

Integration of DDA and DIA datasets was performed in a complementary and hierarchical manner. Initially, high-confidence MS/MS spectra acquired in DDA mode were used to establish reference fragmentation patterns for structurally characterized metabolites through spectral library matching. These DDA-derived annotations were subsequently employed to guide DIA data interpretation, where Skyline-assisted deconvolution enabled the extraction and validation of fragment ions from complex, multiplexed MS/MS spectra. Concordance among accurate mass, retention time alignment, and MS/MS fragmentation across both acquisition modes was required for final metabolite annotation, thereby enhancing confidence while minimizing false positives.

Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) analysis

The spectra data were exported to Analysis Base File (ABF) format and then analyzed using MS-DIAL software. Several available open-source LCMS/MS databases, such as MassBank of Europe and MassBank of North America, were incorporated to increase the compound identification capabilities. The untargeted approach allowed for the identification of potentially novel or previously unreported metabolites by comparing the experimental spectra with a vast number of metabolomic databases.

Data-independent acquisition (DIA) analysis

Skyline (version 21.1.0.146) was used for the analysis of DIA data for small molecules to streamline the identification workflows. A transition list generated in MS-DIAL was imported into Skyline to enhance the MS2 deconvolution and the chromatogram visualization. The robust features of the skyline, such as retention time alignment and the visualization of peak area, were very helpful in the identification of metabolites across samples. The DIA-specific parameters setup in Skyline included Time-of-Flight (TOF) as the precursor mass analyzer, with a resolving power of 30,000 and a mass range of 70–1200 Da. The isolation windows were set to 25 and 50 Da with a margin of 0.5 m/z (e.g., 99.5–150.5, 149.5–200.5), to ensure that there is a good MS2 spectrum coverage for metabolite identification.

Statistical analysis

For the thorough examination of the metabolomic data, different statistical and visualization tools were utilized to understand the distribution and correlation of identified metabolites across different ionization and acquisition modes. Principal Component Analysis (PCA) was performed using Perseus (Version 2.1.3.0), utilizing normalized and log-transformed peak areas of all identified metabolites. Venn diagrams were used to depict the distribution of metabolites, highlighting distinct and overlapping metabolites between positive and negative modes of ionization as well as between Data-Dependent Acquisition (DDA) and Data-Independent Acquisition (DIA) modes. Heatmaps were created using hierarchical clustering of normalized peak areas, with Euclidean distance as the metric for clustering. These heat maps provided a thorough overview of the relative distribution of metabolites across the four plant samples, exhibiting various clustering patterns based on ionization modes and acquisition methods. In addition, 3D bar graphs were generated to show the quantitative differences in peak areas of metabolites identified in positive and negative modes of ionization. Through the combination of these complementary strategies, the visualization tools enabled a comprehensive understanding of metabolite distributions, providing both qualitative patterns and quantitative insights.

Network pharmacology analysis

To explore the potential biological relevance of the identified metabolites, an in-silico network pharmacology analysis was performed on a subset of prominent or marker metabolites representative of each plant species. These metabolites were selected based on their annotation confidence, relative abundance, and documented relevance in the literature. Canonical SMILES of selected metabolites were retrieved from PubChem, and putative protein targets were predicted using the SuperPred 3.0 platform. Targets with the highest prediction probability and reported biological relevance were retained. Corresponding NCBI gene identifiers were obtained via the UniProt database. Disease and pathway associations were retrieved using the DisGeNET database. The resulting metabolite–target–pathway–disease relationships were visualized as an integrated interaction network.

Results and discussion

Method optimization

The optimization of chromatographic and mass spectrometric parameters was crucial for comprehensive metabolite identification. Among chromatographic parameters, solvent flow rate, linear gradient, and column gradient were optimized for better separation. Varying compositions of methanol and water (0.1% formic acid in both) were used to get better separation of visible peaks. The optimum separation was achieved by starting the linear gradient at 10% B and going to 90% in 6.5 min. The column temperature and the solvent flow rate were also varied and the optimum temperature was found to be 40 °C while the best flow rate was found to be 0.5 mL/min.

For mass spectrometric analysis, key parameters were optimized for improved ionization and fragmentation efficiency. These include a capillary voltage of 4500 V, end plate offset of 500 V, drying gas (nitrogen) at a flow rate of 10 mL/min, drying gas temperature set at 280 °C, and nebulizer gas pressure of 45.0 psi. Data-dependent acquisitions (DDA) mode was performed utilizing auto MS/MS mode with a mass range of 70–1200 m/z, precursor ion exclusion time was set as 5 s, and a scan speed of 20 Hz was used. Data-independent acquisitions (DIA) were carried out with Q1 windows of 25 and 50 Da within the mass range of 99-1200 m/z, using a collision energy of 30 V. The Base Peak Chromatograms (BPCs) of the four plant species acquired in negative and positive ionization modes are shown in Fig. S1.

Metabolite identification and statistical analysis

The comprehensive profiling of Centella asiatica, Lavandula angustifolia, Herpestis monnieria, and Canscora decussata revealed a total of 100 metabolites. Although untargeted metabolomics workflows can yield thousands of spectral features, the 100 metabolites reported in this study represent high-confidence annotations obtained under stringent identification criteria, including accurate mass matching (± 5 ppm), MS/MS spectral agreement, and retention time consistency across acquisition modes. This conservative strategy was intentionally adopted to prioritize annotation reliability over numerical abundance, particularly given the complexity of plant matrices and the risk of false assignments in large-scale untargeted datasets. The metabolomic profiles of each plant formed distinct clusters with minimal overlap. The metabolites of Lavandula angustifolia and Centella asiatica, represented by red squares and green circles, clustered separately in the upper-right and upper-left quadrants, respectively. Herpestis monnieria, shown in blue triangles, formed a distinct group in the middle-right region, while Canscora decussata, represented by orange crosses, clustered in the lower region of the plot. The distinct clustering of their metabolomic profile validates our conclusion that the shared therapeutic effects of these plants are achieved through different molecular mechanisms.

The positive mode (Fig. S2) revealed a distinct profile for each plant, with significant contributions from terpenoids in Centella asiatica and Herpestis monnieria. Analysis of metabolomic profiles

The comprehensive metabolomic profiles of the selected medicinal plants showed distinct metabolites despite their similar therapeutic properties. Only four out of a hundred identified compounds showed any overlap, and these were limited to specific pairs of plants. Kaempferol-3-O-glucuronoside was shared among Lavandula angustifolia and Herpestis monnieria; hydroquinidine was found common in Centella asiatica and Herpestis monnieria; vicenin 2 was present in both Centella asiatica and Canscora decussata; while dihydroxybenzoic acid was detected in Centella asiatica and Lavandula angustifolia. Distinct classes of compounds were present in each of the selected plants. Centella asiatica predominantly contained triterpenoid saponins; Lavandula angustifolia was rich in phenolic compounds and monoterpenes; Herpestis monnieria contained bacosides and their derivatives; while Canscora decussata showed xanthones and unique alkaloids. These diverse metabolomic profiles highlight the complexity of these plants. Despite minimal overlap in individual metabolites among the four plant species, their distinct chemical profiles may converge functionally by modulating shared biological pathways related to oxidative stress regulation, neuroprotection, inflammation, and neurotransmission. This phenomenon, often referred to as pharmacological convergence, suggests that chemically diverse metabolites can exert similar therapeutic effects by targeting overlapping molecular networks. The findings of this study, therefore, support the concept that therapeutic similarity does not necessitate chemical uniformity, particularly in complex botanical systems.

Data-dependent acquisition (DDA) analysis

In DDA analysis, high-abundance precursor ions were targeted for MS/MS fragmentation, which enables high-confidence identification of known metabolites. Asiaticoside in Centella asiatica and rosmarinic acid in Lavandula angustifolia were identified with precise fragmentation patterns through MS/MS library matching (Fig. S4). Some unique metabolites such as bacoside A from Herpestis monnieria and rare xanthones from Canscora decussata were effectively resolved in DDA mode.

While DDA offers comprehensive structural information for high-abundance metabolites, low-abundance compounds are ignored due to their reliance on ion intensity. This limitation was also recognized in previous studies, where less abundant compounds were less efficiently detected using DDA strategies (Ramabulana et al., 2023). Because of the targeted nature of DDA, well-characterized bioactive compounds are precisely profiled, highlighting its strength in targeted metabolomics.

Data-independent acquisition (DIA) analysis

The DIA mode uses overlapping isolation windows for MS/MS acquisition to capture all ions within a given range simultaneously. This strategy increases the metabolite coverage by detecting low-abundance and co-eluting metabolites. For example, in the present study, DIA distinctively identified rare xanthones in Canscora decussata and diterpenoids in Herpestis monnieria. Skyline helped in the untargeted identification of a wide range of metabolites due to its advanced features, such as peak deconvolution and retention time alignment. This wide coverage of metabolites offered by the DIA mode is consistent with the findings of other metabolomic studies that incorporated the same approach for extensive metabolite profiling (Guo & Huan, 2020). Furthermore, recent literature also highlighted the ability of DIA mode to detect co-eluting compounds and emphasized its usefulness in comprehensive metabolite profiling (Guo et al., 2021; Shah et al., 2023).

Comparative analysis of DDA and DIA

The integration of DDA and DIA modes demonstrated their complementary strengths. DDA excelled at producing high-resolution spectra for high-abundance metabolites, while DIA provided broader coverage by identifying low-abundance and co-eluting metabolites. Venn diagrams (Fig. 3a and b) illustrate the distinct and overlapping metabolites detected by DDA and DIA in positive and negative ionization modes.

Hierarchical cluster analyses (Fig. S5a and b) illustrate the clustering patterns of metabolites identified by DDA and DIA. DDA-derived clusters focused on high-abundance compounds, while DIA-derived clusters revealed a more diverse metabolomic profile. The combination of DDA and DIA provided a comprehensive view of the metabolome, emphasizing the complementary nature of these acquisition techniques.

Ionization modes and their impact

The use of dual modes of ionization expanded the metabolome coverage complementing each other (Fig. 3c). The positive mode of ionization predominantly identified terpenoids and alkaloids, such as centellin and xanthosine. Meanwhile, the negative mode of ionization was more effective for the identification of acidic compounds like rosmarinic acid and jasmonic acid. This aligns with previous studies which demonstrated the efficacy of positive ionization mode for the analysis of less polar and basic metabolites, and negative ionization mode for the analysis of more polar and acidic metabolites in medicinal plants (Kim et al., 2016). Using both ionization modes was proven essential for comprehensive compound detection ensuring a complete understanding of the metabolite profiles of these plants, as supported by literature (Lei et al., 2011).

Biological relevance of identified metabolites

Many of the identified metabolites validate the traditional therapeutic uses of these medicinal plants. Asiaticoside and centellin from Centella asiatica, rosmarinic acid from Lavandula angustifolia, bacoside A from Herpestis monnieria, and xanthones from Canscora decussata are the key identified metabolites. These metabolites are well-documented for their pharmacological activities, which support the traditional uses of these plants in cognitive enhancement, stress relief, and wound healing.

Asiaticoside and centellin, known for their neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory properties, were found abundant in Centella asiatica, substantiating its role in cognitive improvement and wound healing(Gray et al., 2018). Similarly, Lavandula angustifolia showed a high abundance of phenolic compounds, particularly rosmarinic acid, which contributes to its antioxidant and stress-alleviating effects (CAKIR & YILMAZ, 2024). Bacoside A and bacogenins dominated the metabolomic profile of Herpestis monnieria, these metabolites are associated with memory enhancement and neuroprotection (Wal et al., 2024). Finally, the xanthones and diterpenoids identified in Canscora decussata align with their traditional use for treating oxidative stress and anxiety (Sethiya et al. 2010b).

Network pharmacology analysis of selected marker metabolites

To strengthen the functional interpretation of the metabolomic findings, an in-silico network pharmacology analysis was conducted on a selected subset of prominent or marker metabolites representative of each medicinal plant. The resulting integrated metabolite–target–pathway–disease network (Fig. 4) reveals a highly interconnected architecture, supporting the multi-target pharmacological nature of the investigated botanicals.

In Centella asiatica, centellin and asiaticoside E were predicted to interact with key regulatory proteins including NFKB1, CTSD, BLM, CNR2, and APEX1, which collectively participate in immune regulation, extracellular matrix organization, signal transduction, DNA repair, and transcriptional control. These interactions provide a molecular basis for the well-documented neuroprotective and stress-modulating properties of C. asiatica.

For Lavandula angustifolia, metabolites such as linalool oxide, lavandunat, and rosmarinic acid exhibited interactions with targets including MAOA, CNR2, APEX1, TDP1, HSD17B10, and POLB. These targets are centrally involved in neurotransmitter metabolism, neuronal signaling, and genome maintenance, indicating that L. angustifolia may exert therapeutic effects through coordinated modulation of neuronal and DNA repair pathways.

In Herpestis monnieri, bacoside A and bacosterol demonstrated multi-target interactions with NTRK3, ACHE, NFKB1, CNR2, AKR1C3, APEX1, and KDM1A, encompassing neuronal survival, synaptic function, immune regulation, metabolic control, and epigenetic modulation. This broad target spectrum supports the long-standing use of H. monnieri in cognitive enhancement and neuroregenerative applications.

Similarly, metabolites from Canscora decussata, including canscoradione and xanthosine, were predicted to interact with PTGS1, GRIA2, NR1I2, NFKB1, and multiple DNA repair-associated proteins. These interactions implicate inflammatory signaling, neuronal transmission, transcriptional regulation, and metabolic pathways, reinforcing the neuropharmacological relevance of this species.

Notably, despite limited overlap of individual metabolites across species, the network reveals convergence at the target and pathway levels, particularly involving neuronal system regulation, immune–inflammatory signaling, and DNA repair mechanisms. The frequent involvement of neuroactive genes such as MAOA, ACHE, GRIA2, SLC6A5, and NTRK3, alongside the repeated targeting of CNR2, highlights a shared neuromodulatory framework among chemically distinct plants.

The convergence of DNA repair–associated proteins (APEX1, BLM, TDP1) with neuronal signaling pathways suggests a mechanistic link between genomic maintenance and neuronal health, a relationship increasingly recognized in cognitive decline and neurodegenerative disorders. Furthermore, the intersection of immune and neuronal pathways supports a neuroimmune interface, indicating that these medicinal plants may exert therapeutic effects through coordinated, multi-pathway modulation rather than reliance on single active constituents.

Conclusion

The present study provides a comprehensive metabolomic characterization of Centella asiatica, Lavandula angustifolia, Herpestis monnieria, and Canscora decussata, medicinal plants widely recognized for their cognitive-enhancing, stress-relieving, and neuroprotective effects. By integrating data-dependent and data-independent acquisition strategies across dual ionization modes, a robust HR-ESI-LC-MS workflow was established, enabling the confident identification of 100 metabolites. Despite their shared therapeutic indications, the four species exhibited largely distinct metabolomic profiles, with minimal overlap at the individual metabolite level, challenging the assumption that similar therapeutic effects necessarily arise from shared chemical constituents.

The metabolite profiles generated in this study captured characteristic marker compounds of each species, including asiaticoside, rosmarinic acid, bacoside A, and xanthones, supporting their traditional medicinal use while highlighting the chemical diversity underlying similar pharmacological outcomes. This limited overlap underscores the complexity of plant-based medicines and suggests that different molecular mechanisms may contribute to convergent therapeutic effects. A limitation of the present study is the inclusion of a single representative sample per species, which does not account for variability arising from environmental, geographical, or processing-related factors; therefore, the reported profiles should be interpreted as representative rather than exhaustive. Future studies incorporating biological replicates and multi-location sampling will be essential to strengthen metabolomic-based standardization efforts.

Importantly, network pharmacology analysis of selected marker metabolites revealed convergence at the level of biological targets and pathways, particularly those associated with neuronal signaling, immune–inflammatory regulation, and DNA repair mechanisms. These findings support a model of pharmacological convergence, in which chemically diverse metabolites act on overlapping molecular networks to produce similar therapeutic effects. Collectively, this study advances our understanding of how distinct medicinal plants can achieve comparable biological outcomes through multi-pathway modulation and provides a methodological and conceptual framework to guide future experimental validation, quality control, and rational development of herbal medicines.
