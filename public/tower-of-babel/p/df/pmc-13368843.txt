The clinicopathologic characteristics and outcomes of uncommon KRAS mutations in patients with non-small cell lung cancer
Authors: Yongjing Zhang, Jing Zhao, Qiqi Gao, Mei Kong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) mutations occur in 25–30% of non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) patients. Pooled analyses in previous research have largely focused on the prognostic and predictive value of overall or common KRAS mutation status. Uncommon KRAS variants regarding their clinicopathologic features and associations with clinical outcomes following treatment are largely unexplored. This study characterizes the clinicopathologic features and outcomes associated with uncommon KRAS mutations in NSCLC. Of 4661 NSCLC patients subjected to oncogenic mutation profiling, 378 (8.1%) harbored KRAS mutations. Among the 209 cases with confirmed specific KRAS subtypes, 14 (6.7%, 14/209) presented with uncommon KRAS mutations. G12F was the most prevalent subtype, identified in 4 cases (28.6%, 4/14), followed by G12S and G12R, each detected in 2 cases (14.3%, respectively). Concomitant mutations were observed in 4 additional cases (28.6%), including G12D&G12V, G12A&G13R, G12V&G12C, and G12R&G12C. G12Y and G13R were each identified in 1 case (7.1% each). Compared with a matched cohort of patients with common KRAS mutations, no significant differences were observed in age, sex, smoking history, clinical stage, TTF-1 expression, or pathological subtypes. The uncommon KRAS mutation group had numerically inferior progression-free survival (PFS) and overall survival (OS); however, this difference did not reach statistical significance in our study (mean PFS: 15.14 months vs. 30.39 months, p = 0.074; mean OS: 24.50 months vs. 38.79 months, p = 0.157). These findings underscore the value of broad molecular profiling in establishing critical links between specific morphologic features, genetic alterations, and clinical behavior for tumors harboring these uncommon KRAS variants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00428-026-04475-5.

Introduction

Asians are the only ethnic group worldwide for whom cancer is the leading cause of death [1]. Among all cancer-related deaths, lung cancer is the most common cause, with non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) accounting for approximately 85% of all lung cancer diagnoses [1, 2]. Over the past few years, a growing understanding of NSCLC molecular biology has uncovered critical genomic drivers of tumorigenesis and resistance mechanisms, paving the way for precision-targeted therapeutic approaches.

Among common molecular alterations, Kirsten rat sarcoma viral oncogene homolog (KRAS) mutations have emerged as pivotal biomarkers, occurring in 25–30% of lung adenocarcinomas [3]. The most prevalent variants involve missense mutations at KRAS exon2 codon 12 glycine (p.G12), notably G12C (c.34G>T; 28.5–40%), G12D (c.35G>A; 20.6–21.1%) and G12V (c.35G>T; 20.2–20.7%) in both Western and Asian populations [4–6]. Currently, therapeutic targeting of KRAS mutations has emerged as a primary focus in oncology research, with the majority of clinical efforts concentrated on the KRAS G12C variant. Covalent inhibitors such as sotorasib, adagrasib, and fulzeretasib have demonstrated encouraging clinical efficacy and manageable safety profiles in this molecular subset [7–9]. Concurrently, considerable efforts have been directed toward targeting non-G12C KRAS mutations, with preclinical and early-phase clinical studies suggesting potential antitumor activity in these populations [10–12]. In a recent study involving 33 advanced non-G12C KRAS mutant NSCLC patients, the combination of trametinib and anlotinib demonstrated promising efficacy, significantly outperforming standard therapy [13]. This regimen establishes a new therapeutic paradigm for KRAS mutant lung cancer and underscores the importance of focusing on uncommon KRAS subtypes. However, the significance of uncommon variants remains poorly characterized. Due to their low prevalence, there is a notable scarcity of detailed reports on the uncommon KRAS mutation profiles and clinicopathological features. Furthermore, comprehensive clinical analysis supporting the efficacy of different treatment is also limited, highlighting a significant gap in the current research.

In this study, uncommon KRAS mutations were defined as subtypes constituting less than 2% of all KRAS mutations. We collected a cohort of 14 cases which harbor uncommon KRAS gene mutations among 209 cases known KRAS-mutant subtypes from 4661 NSCLC receiving oncogenic mutation testing (Fig. 1). KRAS mutation analysis was performed using the AmoyDx KRAS Mutation Detection Kit, Sanger sequencing and/or next-generation sequencing (NGS) for cross-validation. We assessed the distribution of uncommon KRAS mutations and the clinicopathologic characteristics of the corresponding patients. Furthermore, we compared clinical characteristics and outcomes between uncommon KRAS mutations and a matched cohort of patients with common KRAS mutations.Fig. 1Tumour KRAS genotype analysis in patients screened for enrolment into the trial. Materials and methods

Patients and samples

Patients diagnosed with NSCLC who underwent oncogenic mutations testing at our institution between January 2022 and January 2024 were considered for this study. Among 4661 tested NSCLC patients, 378 harbored KRAS mutations, with 209 cases having confirmed KRAS subtypes. In the existing literature characterizing KRAS mutation subtypes, G12A, G12C, G12D, G12V, and G13C are consistently recognized as the major and common KRAS mutation subtypes [14–16]. In our study, all subtypes with a mutation frequency >2% fell exactly within this cohort of well-established common mutations. Therefore, KRAS mutant subtypes with an allelic frequency of less than 2% among all identified KRAS mutations were defined as uncommon. Previous chemotherapy or radiotherapy and other severe systemic diseases were excluded. Based on this criterion, 14 consecutive cases harboring uncommon KRAS mutations were included in the final analysis. Additionally, we have comprised a cohort of common KRAS mutated patients, age, sex, and clinical stage matched to uncommon KRAS mutation group (ratio of 3:1; n = 42), to evaluate uncommon KRAS mutation impact on outcome and clinicopathology characteristics. Patient demographics and clinical characteristics, including histopathology, age, gender, smoking history, and treatment records, were retrospectively collected through medical chart review.

Tumor samples were obtained via multiple methods: endobronchial ultrasound-guided transbronchial needle aspiration (EBUS-TBNA), computed tomography (CT)-guided biopsy of lung lesions, cell block preparation from pleural effusion, or surgical resection of lung tumors. All excised tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, processed routinely, embedded in paraffin (FFPE), and sectioned. Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E)-stained sections from each FFPE block were independently evaluated by two experienced pathologists to confirm the histopathological diagnosis and to estimate the tumor cell content. Only samples with a tumor cell content exceeding 10% were subjected to molecular testing. For specimens exhibiting significant tumor heterogeneity, microdissection was performed prior to DNA extraction to enrich tumor cells and ensure a final tumor content of >20%.

Immunohistochemical studies

For immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis, 3-μm-thick sections were cut from FFPE tumor blocks. Staining was performed using a standardized EnVision method on either Leica or Roche automated immunohistochemical staining platforms. This method utilizes a secondary antibody conjugated to a dextran polymer-based enzyme complex (EnVision complex). External control tissues (in-house prepared) were included on each slide to monitor staining performance throughout the assay. Following primary antibody incubation, the EnVision complex was applied. A chromogenic reaction was then developed using 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB), enabling visualization of antigen localization. Details regarding the primary antibodies used, including dilutions and vendors, are provided in Supplementary Table 1.

KRAS gene mutation studies

DNA extraction

Previously, DNA was extracted from ten 5-μm FFPE tumor tissue samples using the Genomic DNA FFPE one-step extraction Kit (Concert Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China) according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. DNA concentration and purity (A260/A280 ratio) were measured using a NanoDrop 1000 Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Wilmington, USA). All extracted DNA samples were stored at −20 °C prior to analysis.

ARMS-PCR analysis

Common KRAS mutations were detected using an AmoyDx KRAS Mutation Detection Kit (Amoy Diagnostics Co., Ltd., Xiamen, China). The KRAS mutation loci covered by the detection kit are summarized in Supplementary Table 2. PCR amplification was performed in a SLAN-96S PCR System (Hongshi Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The PCR conditions were set as follows: 1 st stage: 42 °C for 5 min, 95 °C for 5 min for one cycle; 2nd stage: 95 °C for 25 s, 64 °C for 20 s, 72 °C for 20 s for 15 cycles; 3rd stage: 93 °C for 25 s, 60 °C for 35 s, 72 °C for 20 s for 31 cycles. Fluorescence signals (FAM and HEX channels) were acquired at the 60 °C annealing step. The cycle threshold (Ct) was determined by setting the fluorescence threshold at 5% of the maximum fluorescence value of the positive control in the 31 st cycle. Results were interpreted in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines.

Sanger sequencing analysis

For direct sequencing, KRAS exon 2 was amplified using the following primers: forward, 5′-AAAAGGTACTGGTGGAGTATTTGA-3′; reverse, 5′-CTGTATCAAAGAATGGTCCTGC-3′. PCR products were purified and sequenced bidirectionally on an ABI 3500 Genetic Analyzer (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA). Sequence traces were analyzed using Chromas software (Technelysium Pty Ltd.), and all reported variants were confirmed by independent replicate experiments.

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) analysis

This study employed targeted sequencing with the Gastrointestinal Tract Tumor 69-Gene Detection Kit (Mygene, Guangzhou, China), which covers the entire exon regions of genes including KRAS, NRAS, and BRAF, among others. NGS libraries were prepared from 20 ng of input DNA using the standard protocol for the MGI2000 platform (BGI, Shenzhen, China). Libraries met the following quality control criteria prior to sequencing: DNA concentration ≥ 20 ng/µL. Sequencing was performed to achieve a mean effective depth of coverage of ≥400× after duplicate removal. Raw sequencing reads were processed through an established bioinformatics pipeline on the Mygene Automated Analysis Platform (v0.4.0). Briefly, reads were quality-trimmed and filtered using Fastp (v0.21.0), aligned to the human reference genome with BWA (0.7.17-r1188), and variants were called using VarDict (v1.8.3). The following quality metrics were required: Q30 score ≥ 80%, mapping rate ≥ 95%, and coverage uniformity ≥ 80%. For accurate variant calling, normal control DNA was extracted from histologically confirmed tumor-free liver tissue obtained from the surgical margin of routine hepatectomy specimens, which were stored in our pathology archive. Additionally, DNA from human lung cancer tissue with a known KRAS G12C mutation was included as a positive control to verify the sensitivity and specificity of the detection system for target variants.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 25.0. Categorical data were summarized as frequencies and proportions. For continuous variables, data were described using medians. Chi-squared test was used for categorical and ordinal variables and t-test for continuous variables. Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from diagnosis until death or last contact. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from treatment initiation to time of disease progression, death, or last follow-up for patients alive without progression. OS and PFS were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. The log-rank test was used to assess differences between groups. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Demographic and clinicopathologic findings

Clinicopathologic data for the 14 NSCLC patients harboring uncommon KRAS mutations were summarized in Table 1. The study cohort had a median age of 66.5 years (range: 38–75), with a male predominance (male:female ratio = 11:3) and a smoking history in three patients (21.4%). The tumors arose in the left lung (6 cases) and right lung (8 cases). Five patients presented with metastases as the initial manifestation of disease; one patient had metastases during the follow-up period. Distant metastatic sites included the lung (2 cases), bone (pelvis and ribs, 3 cases), pleura (1 case), and mediastinal (1 case). Case 5 presented with local recurrence. Regarding clinical stage, 7 patients were classified as stage I, and the remaining 7 had advanced (stage III–IV) disease. Distribution of uncommon KRAS mutations

Of 4661 NSCLC patients subjected to oncogenic mutation profiling, 378 (8.1%) harbored KRAS mutations. Among the 209 cases with confirmed KRAS subtypes, 14 (6.7%, 14/209) presented with uncommon KRAS mutations (Fig. 2A). To evaluate the reliability of the AmoyDx kit in detecting multiple KRAS mutation sites, we compared its results with those from Sanger sequencing and targeted next-generation sequencing (NGS) in a subset of 14 NSCLC cases (Supplementary Table 3). As anticipated, the most frequently detected KRAS mutation was G12C (c.34G>T), being found in 82 samples (39.2%) as a single KRAS mutation (Fig. 2C). A complete list of oncogenic KRAS mutations and their frequencies was provided in Supplementary Table 4. Within the uncommon mutation group, KRAS exon 2 p.G12F was the most prevalent subtype, identified in 4 cases (28.6%), followed by KRAS exon 2 p.G12S and p.G12R, each detected in 2 cases (14.3%, respectively). Concomitant mutations were observed in 4 additional cases (28.6%), including KRAS exon 2 p.G12D and p.G12V, p.G12A and p.G13R, p.G12V and p.G12C, p.G12R and p.G12C. Furthermore, two missense uncommon mutations were each found in a single case (7.1% each): exon 2 p.G13R and p.G12Y. Notably, uncommon mutations not previously described, particularly KRAS exon 2 p.G13R, p.G12Y, and concomitant mutations were confirmed using direct sequencing and/or NGS, as shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Histopathologic and immunohistochemical findings

Histopathological evaluation was performed according to the World Health Organization Classification of Thoracic Tumors (5th edition) [17]. Among the 14 patients, 11 (78.6%) were diagnosed with invasive non-mucinous adenocarcinoma and 3 (21.4%) with invasive mucinous adenocarcinoma. Five surgically resected cases displayed an infiltrative growth pattern. The predominant histological patterns observed included solid, acinar, and papillary growth (Case 4); a composite of 80% large cell carcinoma and 20% acinar pattern (Case 5); lepidic growth (Case 8); mixed acinar and lepidic patterns (Case 9); and mixed acinar and papillary patterns (Case 12). The remaining six cases were diagnosed via biopsy, EBUS-TBNA, or pleural effusion cell block as poorly differentiated adenocarcinoma, favoring non-mucinous type. Two invasive mucinous adenocarcinomas, confirmed by surgical specimens and biopsy, harbored concomitant KRAS mutations (Supplementary Fig. 2). Additionally, a previously unreported KRAS exon 2 p.G12Y mutation was identified in Case 3, which exhibited both adenocarcinoma and mucinous adenocarcinoma components, as confirmed by direct sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 1). Representative H&E-stained pathological features are illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 3.

Immunohistochemically, all cases were positive for CK7, negative for p40, ALK (Ventana), and CDX2. Negative TTF-1 expression was identified in 5 cases (5/14). Positive MUC-5AC expression was identified in 2 cases (2/8). Based on integrated histological phenotypes and immunoprofiles, including TTF-1 and MUC5AC status, 3 cases were classified as non-TRU (terminal respiratory unit) type, 5 cases as TRU type, and 6 cases remained unclassifiable due to limited tissue availability, equivocal morphological features, or inconsistent immunophenotypic results (Supplementary Table 5).

Comparing patients with uncommon KRAS mutations with common KRAS mutations

We aimed to examine if uncommon KRAS mutated patients have distinctive clinical characteristics compared to a cohort of patients with common KRAS mutations. So, we have comprised a cohort from 195 common KRAS mutated patients, age, sex, and clinical stage matched to the uncommon KRAS mutations group (ratio of 3:1, n = 42). In this comparison, age, sex, smoking history, clinical stage, the expression of TTF-1, and pathological subtypes were not significantly different (Supplementary Table 6).

Evaluation of uncommon KRAS mutations impact on survival

We also aimed to evaluate the impact of uncommon KRAS mutations on patients’ clinical outcomes. Notably, patients with uncommon KRAS mutations exhibited worse PFS compared to those with common KRAS mutations (mean PFS: 15.14 months vs. 30.39 months; HR = 2.91, 95% CI: 9.44–20.85; p = 0.074). Although this difference did not reach statistical significance, it suggested a trend toward inferior PFS in the uncommon KRAS mutation group. Similarly, numerically shorter mean OS was observed in patients with uncommon KRAS mutations compared with their common KRAS mutation counterparts (24.50 months vs. 38.79 months; HR = 3.77, 95% CI: 17.11–31.89; p = 0.157) (Fig. 3), and this OS difference was also not statistically significant.Fig. 3A, B Kaplan–Meier curve for PFS and overall survival in uncommon KRAS mutations (blue, N = 14) compared with common KRAS mutations (red, N = 42)

Discussion

In our cohort of 4661 NSCLC patients who underwent molecular testing, 378 (8.1%) harbored KRAS mutation. Among the 209 cases with confirmed specific KRAS subtypes, 14 (6.7%, 14/209) were uncommon KRAS mutations. These frequencies are consistent with, though slightly lower than, previously reported rates from Asian NSCLC cohorts, where total KRAS mutation rates generally fell below 12% [18, 19]. Patients with uncommon KRAS mutations in our study were more likely to be male and elderly. Notably, only 3 cases (21.4%) had a smoking history, a prevalence considerably lower than the 62.1%–81.6% typically reported for KRAS mutations in smokers across most studies [5, 6]. This observation may be attributed to the uncommon KRAS mutations profile in our cohort and the limited sample size. Since comparable population data for these uncommon mutations are lacking, prior statistics provide only a tentative reference. Therefore, large-scale future studies are warranted to enable robust statistical evaluation.

Histologically, lung adenocarcinoma can be classified into TRU and non-TRU subtypes, both of which may harbor KRAS mutations [20–22]. We therefore performed exploratory subclassification in our cohort by assessing TTF-1 and MUC5-AC expression, two key markers for non-TRU phenotype [23, 24]. Among 8 cases with evaluable MUC5-AC staining, only 2 showed positive expression. Due to occasional inconsistencies between morphological and immunophenotypic features, a definitive TRU/non-TRU classification was achieved in 8 cases. Of these, 3 were designated non-TRU. Our limited data tentatively suggest TRU-type may be more prevalent in uncommon KRAS mutations (5/8). However, given the small number of evaluable cases and incomplete pathological data, this hypothesis remains preliminary and requires validation in larger, well-characterized cohorts.

Different KRAS mutations have been reported to have distinct prognostic impact [25, 26]. Retrospective data from China indicated that KRAS-mutant NSCLC patients can achieve a median OS of 22.9 months, with ICI-based regimens demonstrating superior outcomes [26, 27]. In recent years, significant breakthroughs have been made in the development of targeted therapies for KRAS G12C-mutant NSCLC, leading to the approval of multiple innovative domestic agents. These drugs are now recommended by authoritative Chinese guidelines for later-line treatment, presenting a promising therapeutic avenue for patients [28]. While G12C remains the most clinically actionable mutation, other KRAS variants (e.g., G12D, G13R, and G12F) are emerging as potential therapeutic targets in ongoing trials [29]. In addition, systematic analyses of different KRAS mutation types have shown that the oncogenicity and signaling output of KRAS alterations are modulated by allele-specific and tissue-specific contexts, highlighting the complex genetic interactions that influence clinical behavior [30].

There are six reported subtypes of uncommon KRAS mutations (G12F, G12R, G12S, G12C&G12V, G12D&G12V, G12C&G12R). Our study identified nine uncommon KRAS subtypes, expanding the spectrum of rare KRAS variants observed in NSCLC. The p.G12F subtype accounted for 1.9% (4/209) of all KRAS mutations in our cohort, a frequency closely aligned with the 1.8% (19/1084) reported in previous different studies [31]. Interestingly, 3/4 G12F mutation occurred in male patients with stage I lung adenocarcinoma, all of whom achieved complete remission during follow-up. Although Case 5, which harbored a G12F mutation and exhibited a histologic composition of 80% large cell carcinoma and 20% acinar pattern, experienced disease progression at 5 months, subsequent surgical intervention achieved a favorable outcome. However, given the small subgroup size (n = 4), these observations are preliminary and should be interpreted with extreme caution, requiring further validation in larger, independent cohorts. Both histologic subtype and KRAS mutation status appear to be critical determinants of disease progression and clinical outcome, though this hypothesis remains to be confirmed in adequately powered studies.

Notably, two cases of mucinous adenocarcinoma in our cohort (Cases 6 and 7) harbored concomitant KRAS mutations (G12C&G12V and G12D&G12V, respectively). A large-scale analysis of NSCLC tumors harboring KRAS alterations reported that 1.6% of cases (38/2306) with KRAS G12C mutations also carry additional KRAS variants, of which KRAS G12V (c.35G>T) was most frequently detected and translating to either G12F or G12C and G12V double mutant, when occurring in cis or trans, respectively [31, 32]. These observations, alongside our findings, suggest that heightened awareness of KRAS co-mutations in routine clinical and pathologic practice may be warranted, as accurate detection of such complex genotypes could potentially inform more individualized therapeutic strategies for affected patients.

We observed two patients with the G12S mutation in our cohort, with notable clinical heterogeneity in their outcomes. The one, presenting at an early stage, is disease-free after surgical treatment. Conversely, the other patient was diagnosed with SMARCA4 deficient poorly differentiated advanced adenocarcinoma and died from disease progression. This is consistent with previous reports linking SMARCA4 deficiency to poor outcomes in lung cancer, especially within KRAS-mutant cohorts [33, 34]. Furthermore, the previous studies indicate that metformin or pemetrexed could potentiate sotorasib efficacy in cells with KRAS p.G12S mutation [35, 36]. Given the small number of G12S-mutant cases in our study (n = 2), these preclinical findings highlight the need for meticulous genetic characterization in larger patient cohorts to identify potential prognostic markers and actionable therapeutic vulnerabilities associated with this rare variant.

Additionally, the two advanced-stage adenocarcinoma patients harboring the KRAS p.G12R mutation achieved durable disease control, with survival outcomes that appeared superior to those seen in other advanced uncommon KRAS-mutant cases in this study. These preliminary observations raise the hypothesis that the G12R mutation may potentially act as a favorable prognostic biomarker in advanced NSCLC, though this finding is based on only two cases and requires rigorous validation. Even so, the tailored chemoimmunotherapy regimens administered to these patients likely played a pivotal role in driving these improved clinical outcomes, which highlights the potential interplay between oncogenic molecular subtype and therapeutic strategy efficacy in advanced KRAS-mutant NSCLC. Confirmation of the putative prognostic value of G12R, and clarification of its utility in guiding personalized treatment decisions, will necessitate validation in larger, prospective patient cohorts with standardized treatment and follow-up.

The clinical significance of several gene mutations identified in this study, particularly the novel KRAS variants G12Y and G13R, as well as the concomitant G12A and G13R mutation, has not been previously investigated in NSCLC. KRAS Codon 13 variants, although less common than G12 alterations, have been increasingly recognized for their distinct biological behavior and clinical implications. For instance, G13D and G13R have been implicated in altered sensitivity to EGFR inhibitors in colorectal cancer and may exhibit different signaling output compared to G12 mutations in lung cancer [37, 38]. From a structural perspective, most oncogenic KRAS mutations affect glycine residues at codons 12 and 13, and glutamine at codon 61, with codon 12 being a predominant hotspot [39, 40]. Mutations at these residues impair GTPase activity, leading to accumulation of the active KRAS-GTP form and consequent constitutive downstream signaling that drives tumorigenesis and progression [41, 42]. All patients within these three rare mutation subtypes were found to be dead of disease, with a mean overall survival of 5.3 months. While advanced disease stage also contributed to mortality, these single-case observations tentatively suggest that these rare variants may potentially exert a pronounced deleterious effect on the pathogenetic mechanism and clinical outcome in NSCLC.

Finally, we proceeded to assess the differences in the clinical characteristics between uncommon mutants and a matched common KRAS mutation bearing NSCLC patients. None of the clinical factors typical were found to be distinctive of the uncommon KRAS mutations. Interestingly, the uncommon KRAS mutation group had numerically shorter survival, but this difference was not statistically significant in our study. Examination of a larger cohort of such patients will be required in order to shed light on the prognostic value of an uncommon KRAS mutation.

While our study characterizes the clinicopathological features and outcomes of patients with uncommon KRAS mutations, we must acknowledge the limitations of our study. Most notably, the small cohort size and low prevalence of these mutations limit statistical power for subgroup analyses and robust clinical conclusions. Additionally, real-world survival data may be biased by irregular, abbreviated follow-up, compromising the reliability of some findings. Patients with uncommon KRAS mutations are often underrepresented in prospective trials due to their rarity. Future prospective studies with larger cohorts and comprehensive pathological evaluation are needed to clarify these features and identify optimal therapeutic strategies for this understudied patient population.

In conclusion, in our limited cohort, uncommon KRAS mutations in NSCLC appear to exhibit similar clinicopathologic features to those of patients with common KRAS mutations, but with heterogeneous prognostic outcomes. However, subtype-specific differences in therapeutic responsiveness and survival reiterate the complexity of this subgroup and the necessity of further investigation. The clinicopathologic features of uncommon KRAS mutation subgroup require confirmation in larger studies to determine their clinical relevance.
