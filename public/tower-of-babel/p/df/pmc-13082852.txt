A 67-year-old man with diabetes presenting with a foot ulcer
Authors: Krishan Yadav, Caroline Nott, Hans Rosenberg
Published: 2026

A 67-year-old man with a history of type 2 diabetes, diabetic peripheral neuropathy, and hypertension presents to the emergency department with a skin ulceration on the bottom of his great toe (Figure 1). Three weeks before, he had noted breakdown of the skin, and within the last 4 days, he developed spreading erythema from the ulcer margin. He has no previous history of foot ulcers. Current medications include metformin, empagliflozin, and ramipril, and his glycated hemoglobin level 1 month earlier was 8.5% (normal < 5.7%; prediabetes 5.7% to 6.4%; diabetes ≥ 6.5%). His vital signs at presentation are within normal limits.

Plantar ulcer on the left great toe of a 67-year-old man with diabetes.

How are the severity of the foot ulcer and the likelihood of infection assessed?

The diagnosis of a diabetic foot ulcer infection is made clinically (disruption to the skin integrity in the presence of erythema, edema, induration, warmth, tenderness, or purulence).1 If present, severity of infection is graded using the International Working Group on the Diabetic Foot (IWGDF) and Infectious Diseases Society of America (IDSA) classification (Table 1).1 When osteomyelitis is suspected, a probe-to-bone test should be performed by inserting a blunt sterile metal probe into the ulcer. Feeling a hard, gritty structure is a positive test (sensitivity 0.87, specificity 0.83).2

Should imaging be obtained for a diabetic foot ulcer that is infected?

A negative probe-to-bone test is insufficiently sensitive in isolation to rule out osteomyelitis. Sensitivities and specificities for several diagnostic tests are outlined in Table 2. For patients with foot ulcers of clinical severity grade 2 and higher, a plain radiograph of the foot is the initial test of choice.5 When sensitivity and specificity of a test are low (as in the case of plain radiograph; Table 2),3 and radiograph is negative for infection, a repeat radiograph should be done in 2 to 3 weeks if clinical suspicion of infection remains high. In the case of a clinically worsening ulcer (e.g., increasing ulcer depth or spreading erythema), despite a normal initial radiograph, magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) of the affected foot is recommended. If MRI is not available, computed tomography with contrast or a bone scan should be considered.

What is the role of blood tests and wound swabs?

If the diabetic foot ulcer is grade 3 or 4 severity, the diagnosis of diabetic foot infection remains unclear, or osteomyelitis is suspected despite a normal radiograph, then a leukocyte count and erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), C-reactive protein (CRP), or procalcitonin should be obtained. For grade 4 infections, blood cultures should be obtained. Because they are insufficiently sensitive and specific in isolation, 4 inflammatory marker tests (ESR, CRP, or procalcitonin), probe-to-bone test, and plain radiography should be used together to exclude osteomyelitis from a diabetic foot ulcer (Table 2).1

Clinicians should obtain a specimen from the wound using an aseptic technique.1 After wound débridement, a sterile scalpel or curette is used to scrape a tissue sample from the ulcer base. Superficial swabs should not be sent, as culture results from such samples have poor sensitivity and specificity.

This patient’s blood tests show a leukocyte count of 13 (reference range 4.5 to 11) × 109/L and a normal ESR and CRP. Given that this patient has a normal radiograph, inflammatory markers, and probe-to-bone test, osteomyelitis is excluded. A tissue sample obtained by curettage of the ulcer base is sent for culture, which will be useful to identify organisms such as methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and to tailor antibiotic therapy if required.

What are the recommended antibiotic regimens for infected diabetic foot ulcers?

Antibiotic choice, dose, and route are summarized in Table 1. For patients without signs of inflammation (grade 0 severity), antibiotics should not be prescribed. Oral antibiotics are appropriate for infections of mild (grade 2) and moderate (grade 3) severity. A randomized controlled trial of 1054 adults being treated for a bone or joint infection found that treatment in the first 6 weeks with oral antibiotics was noninferior to intravenous (IV) antibiotics for treatment failure within 1 year after randomization.6 Therefore, patients with grade 3 and 3O (moderate severity with osteomyelitis) infections can receive oral instead of IV antibiotics. Patients with severe (grade 4) infection should be treated with IV antibiotics.

What additional management helps with ulcer healing and reduces recurrence?

All patients with diabetic foot ulcers should be educated on how to examine their feet daily and identify new or worsening foot problems. If a patient self-identifies new or worsening foot ulcers, they should see their physician as soon as possible. People living with diabetes with either loss of protective sensation or peripheral arterial disease and a history of a foot ulcer are at high risk of developing an ulcer and should have a foot examination by a health care provider every 1 to 3 months.7 Patients with diabetic foot ulcers should be referred to a foot specialist (foot or wound care nurse, podiatrist, or chiropodist).

Case revisited

This patient has a grade 3 diabetic foot infection. He is prescribed amoxicillin–clavulanate 875 mg orally twice daily for 2 weeks. He is advised to follow up with his family physician in 2 weeks to have a repeat foot radiograph if the wound has not healed. He is referred to a wound care nurse specialist and advised to offload to reduce mechanical pressure on the ulcer to optimize chance of wound healing. To achieve offloading, he is prescribed a nonremovable knee-high offloading device (e.g., total contact cast or nonremovable knee-high walker)8 and referred to a podiatrist for fitting. He is reminded to check both feet daily and that he requires a foot examination by a health care provider every 1 to 3 months. He is advised to return to the emergency department if he experiences increased swelling, redness, fever, or pain.
