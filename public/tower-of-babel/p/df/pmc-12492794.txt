Association between volatile organic compounds in urine and dyslipidemia: findings based on analysis of NHANES data
Authors: Lili Wu, Zhihang Ma, Yan Ma, Jing Gao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEvidence linking urinary metabolites of volatile organic compounds (UMVOCs) to dyslipidemia remains limited and scarce. This research sought to thoroughly clarify the UMVOCs-dyslipidemia associations and evaluate the inflammation’s mediating effect in this association.MethodsNationally representative data (from 6,962 enrolled participants) obtained from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) formed the basis of this analysis. We applied weighted logistic regression models for the assessment of relationship between individual UMVOC exposure and dyslipidemia, and weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression for the evaluation of the impact of co-exposure to multiple mixed UMVOCs on dyslipidemia. We performed mediation analysis for investigation into inflammation’s role as a mediator, with white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil count (NC), as well as lymphocyte count (LC) incorporated for the evaluation of their respective contributions to the overall mediation effect.ResultsUrinary concentrations of 34MHA and CEMA were positively linked to dyslipidemia, with respective odds ratios (OR) of 1.10 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.00–1.21) and 1.21 (95% CI: 1.08–1.35). Furthermore, through WQS regression analysis, we found that exposure to a mixture of 16 UMVOCs was significantly and positively linked to dyslipidemia (OR = 1.14, 95% CI: 1.04–1.24, P = 0.002), with CEMA making the greatest relative contribution (29.75%). Mediation analysis demonstrated that WBC, NC, and LC partially mediated the association between CEMA and dyslipidemia, accounting for 10.35%, 6.54%, and 6.52% of the total mediation effects, respectively (all P < 0.001).ConclusionSimultaneous exposure to multiple UMVOCs is positively and significantly linked to the prevalence of dyslipidemia, with inflammation partially mediating this relationship. Our findings suggest that prolonged or high-level exposure to UMVOCs may contribute to dyslipidemia through inflammatory pathways, underscoring the potential metabolic health risks posed by environmental pollutants.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-025-02731-2.Keywords: Volatile organic compound, Dyslipidemia, Inflammation, Cross-sectional study

Introduction

Dyslipidemia, as a prevalent chronic disease, requires long-term treatment and monitoring [1]. Its main features include abnormal levels of triglycerides (TG), cholesterol, and associated lipoproteins in the serum [2]. Such abnormalities severely disrupt the body’s metabolic homeostasis and have been in close relation to a rise in the prevalence of type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease (CVD), obesity, and other metabolic syndromes [3, 4]. Currently, dyslipidemia remains an escalating global health challenge. Elevated total cholesterol (TC) accounts for approximately 2.6 million deaths as well as 29.7 million disability adjusted life years (DALYs) each year. In 2019 alone, high low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) contributed to 4.4 million deaths and 98.6 million DALYs [5]. Recently, environmental pollutants have come to light as enmerging risk factors influencing lipid levels [6], among which volatile organic compounds (VOCs), widely detected in air pollution, industrial emissions, cigarette smoke, and household products, are known to pose notable health risks [7]. Although some VOCs are naturally produced (e.g., via forest fires or biological transformations), anthropogenic activities now represent the dominant source, contributing approximately 25% of global VOC emissions [8]. Humans are mainly exposed to VOCs through inhaling, touching, or consuming contaminated substances. Once absorbed, VOCs are subsequently metabolized into urinary metabolites of volatile organic compounds (UMVOCs), which are then excreted through the urine [9]. Compared to blood-based markers, UMVOCs typically have longer biological half-lives and provide a more stable reflection of cumulative exposure. Their stability during storage and processing also reduces detection errors [10]. Therefore, the presence of UMVOCs may reflect the level of VOC exposure and help estimate related health consequences. Moreover, inflammation can be triggered by environmental pollutants and is also considered a crucial mediating factor in the initiation and advancement of dyslipidemia [11].

While the link between air pollution and dyslipidemia has been explored in earlier studies, there is a relative scarcity of evidence on how individual VOC exposure influences lipid metabolism. This research represents the first effort to elicidate the UMVOCs-dyslipidemia association and to examine inflammation’s mediating effect in this association. Through analysis of data collected from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), we palnned to apply weighted logistic regression as well as weighted quantile sum (WQS) regression models to investigate how exposure to 16 UMVOCs relates to dyslipidemia. We also aimed to carry out mediation analysis to assess whether inflammation played a mediating role in this relationship. This research was designed to uncover novel scientific insights into the effects of environmental pollutants on lipid metabolism, thereby laying the groundwork for developing effective and practical solutions for addressing public health challenges.

Methods

Study population

The NHANES stands as a nationally representative survey that collects data through interviews in combination with physical assessments. The National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS)’ Research Ethics Review Board granted approval for the study, and all individuals involved gave their written informed consent. Between 2011 and 2020, NHANES data were collected over four cycles (2011–2012, 2013–2014, 2015–2016, and March 2017–2020), encompassing a total of 45,462 participants. NHANES field operations were halted in March 2020 amid the COVID-19 outbreak, which led to data collection for the 2019–2020 cycle being incomplete. To address the lack of national representativeness caused by this, NHANES constructed a representative dataset of the pre-COVID-19 period by combining data from the 2017–2018 cycle with the available data collected between 2019 and March 2020. In this study, 13,475 participants with UMVOCs measurements were initially included. We excluded 5,166 participants aged ≤ 20 and 1,347 participants lacking UMVOC data. After these exclusions, 6,962 eligible subjects were retained for the primary analysis. For carrying out mediation analysis, participants lacking data necessary for calculating mediator variables were further excluded. Figure 1 shows the detailed steps of participant selection.

Exposure and outcome assessment

Participants’ levels of exposure to VOCs was quantified based on the detection of UMVOCs’ concentrations. Urine specimens collected from a subset of participants were preserved at −20 °C and analyzed at the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences. UMVOCs’ concentrations were detected via ultra-high-performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS). Values falling below the lower limit of detection (LLOD) were reported as half of the LLOD. A total of 16 UMVOCs were revealed to have detection rates exceeding 75%. The 16 UMVOCs were 2-aminothiazoline-4-carboxylic acid (ATCA), N-acetyl-S-(2-carbamoylethyl)-L-cysteine (AAMA), N-acetyl-S-(benzyl)-L-cysteine (BMA), N-acetyl-S-(n-propyl)-L-cysteine (BPMA), 2-carboxyethyl mercapturic acid/N-acetyl-S-(2-carboxyethyl)-L-cysteine (CEMA), N-acetyl-S-(2-cyanoethyl)-L-cysteine (CYMA), N-acetyl-S-(3,4-dihydroxybutyl)-L-cysteine (DHBMA), N-acetyl-S-(2-hydroxypropyl)-L-cysteine (2HPMA), N-acetyl-S-(3-hydroxypropyl)-L-cysteine (3HPMA), N-acetyl-S-(3-hydroxypropyl-1-methyl)-L-cysteine (HPMMA), mandelic acid (MA), N-acetyl-S-(4-hydroxy-2-butenyl)-L-cysteine (MHBMA3), methylhippuric acid (2MHA), 3- and 4-methylhippuric acid (34MHA), N-acetyl-S-(N-methylcarbamoyl)-L-cysteine (AMCC), and phenylglyoxylic acid (PGA). The outcome variable, dyslipidemia, was defined based on the measured levels of TG, LDL-C, TC, as well as high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). The diagnostic criteria for dyslipidemia were as follows: TG ≥ 150 mg/dL, LDL-C > 100 mg/dL, TC > 200 mg/dL, or HDL-C below sex-specific thresholds (< 40 mg/dL in males, < 50 mg/dL in females). Dyslipidemia was determined if any of these established thresholds were exceeded.

Covariates

To mitigate confounding effects, the analysis adjusted for the following listed covariates: age (years), gender (male or female), marriage (categorized as “married” for those married/living together with a partner; “unmarried” for those without a marital history; and “other” for those widowed/divorced/separated), race (White, Black, Mexican American, or other), education (≤ high school: below 11th grade or high school graduate/equivalent; >high school: college or higher), smoking (current smoker:≥100 lifetime cigarettes and still smoking; former smoker:≥100 lifetime cigarettes but not currently smoking; non-smoker: <100 lifetime cigarettes), drinking (non-drinker; low-to-moderate drinker: <1 drink per day for the females or < 2 drinks per day for the males; heavy drinker: ≥1 drink per day for the females or ≥ 2 drinks per day for the males), physical activity (calculated by converting activity intensity: a single minute of vigorous activity was treated as the same as two minutes of moderate activity; individuals with no reported activity were assigned a value of 0), diabetes (including self-reported diabetes, glycated hemoglobin > 6.5%, fasting glucose ≥ 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL), or administration of glucose-lowering medications/insulin), hypertension (including self-reported hypertension, on antihypertensive medications, systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg, or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg), CVD (including coronary heart disease, myocardial infarction, angina, or congestive heart failure), chronic kidney disease (CKD) (including an estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 mL/min/1.73 m² and/or urine albumin/creatinine ratio ≥ 30 mg/g), lipid-lowering drugs, dietary intake and aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and urinary creatinine (UCR). Additionally, to account for urine dilution effects on urinary volatile organic compound (UMVOC) concentrations, we implemented a covariate-adjusted creatinine normalization approach [12]. This method utilized age, sex, race, body mass index (BMI), and estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) to predict creatinine concentrations. UMVOC concentrations were then standardized using the ratio of predicted-to-measured creatinine values. Consequently, urinary creatinine was not included as a separate covariate in the final analytical models.By incorporating these variables, the study aimed to minimize confounding effects in order to produce more precise and reliable results.

Statistical analysis

To address missing data across key variables, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was implemented. Twenty imputed datasets were generated, incorporating all exposure variables (UMVOCs concentrations), outcome (dyslipidemia), and covariates.Baseline characteristics were derived from the original unimputed dataset.Subsequent analyses (including regression, WQS, and mediation) were performed on pooled imputation results.This study reported continuous covariates as weighted means ± standard deviations (SD), with group differences evaluated via weighted t-tests, whereas categorical covariates were presented as weighted percentages, with group comparisons analyzed via chi-square tests. Given the non-normal distribution of UMVOCs’ concentrations, values were log-transformed and categorized into quartiles. Pearson correlation coefficients were applied to examine associations between UMVOCs’ concentrations. Associations between UMVOCs’ concentrations and dyslipidemia were analyzed by applying multivariable weighted logistic regression models, and the outcomes were described using odds ratios (ORs) along with their 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Three models were respectively established, with Model 1 established with adjustments for age, gender, and race; Model 2 with Further adjustments for marriage, education, and physical activity on the basis of Model 1; and Model 3 with additional adjustments for smoking, drinking, diabetes, hypertension, CVD, CKD, Lipid-lowering drugs, AST, and dietary intake on the basis of Model 2. Moreover, UMVOCs’ concentrations were categorized into quartiles, and their association with dyslipidemia was assessed using Model 3. We applied restricted cubic spline regression models, adjusting for all covariates, to investigate potential nonlinear relationships between UMVOCs’ concentrations and dyslipidemia. Besides, we performed subgroup analyses for clarifying whether the interactions between UMVOCs’ concentrations and dyslipidemia differed among diverse population subgroups. Interaction terms were included to test for heterogeneity between subgroups.

To evaluate how simultaneous exposure to multiple UMVOCs influences the prevalence of dyslipidemia, WQS regression was employed. WQS regression, as a statistical technique designed to address complex correlation patterns in high-dimensional data, is particularly suitable for evaluating environmental exposures. It summarizs the combined influence of multiple components into a single WQS index. UMVOCs’ concentrations were categorized into quartiles, and a WQS index was constructed as a proxy variable to represent mixed exposure. In this study, the “gWQS” package in R software was employed for conducting WQS regression analysis, with 10,000 bootstrap iterations to generate a WQS index representing positive exposure-outcome associations. This constructed WQS index (scaled between 0 and 1) represents the degree of mixed exposure to UMVOCs. Additionally, mediation analysis was performed to investigate whether inflammatory markers (including white blood cell count (WBC) (1000 cells/uL), lymphocyte count (LC) (1000 cells/uL), and neutrophil count (NC) (1000 cells/uL)) mediated the association between UMVOCs’ concentrations and dyslipidemia. With the facilitation of the “mediation” package in R software, nonparametric bootstrap methods (n = 1,000) were applied for estimating the indirect, direct, and total mediation effects, with their 95% CIs calculated accordingly. The mediation models included adjustments for the full set of the aforementioned covariates..To assess the robustness of the primary findings, we performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding participants with chronic kidney disease (CKD). Multivariable logistic regression models (Model 3) were re-run to re-evaluate the associations between UMVOCs concentrations and dyslipidemia.R software (version 4.4.1) was applied for conducting statistical analyses, and results with a two-sided P value under 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Association beteeen co-exposure to multiple mixed UMVOCs and dyslipidemia

The impact of 16 UMVOCs on the prevalence of dyslipidemia was assessed using the WQS regression model (Fig. 2). Through the WQS index, a marked positive association was demonstrated between co-exposure to multiple mixed UMVOCs and dyslipidemia. In Model 3, which accounted for all available covariates, the OR was 1.14 (95% CI: 1.04–1.24, P = 0.004). Among all UMVOCs, CEMA contributed the most to the prevalence of dyslipidemia, accounting for 34.62% of the overall weight. To Further assess the impact of co-exposure to multiple UMVOCs on dyslipidemia, we included 2MHA, 34MHA, and CEMA in a multivariate-adjusted logistic regression model, as they demonstrated significant trend (P trend < 0.05) in the univariate models (Table 4, Table S6). The results indicated a notable link between increased concentrations of CEMA and elevated risks of dyslipidemia (OR = 1.19, 95% CI: 1.07–1.33, P < 0.001). A marked rise in dyslipidemia prevalence was demonstrated among participants in the higher quartiles of CEMA relative to Q1 (OR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.18–2.01, P < 0.001). In the trend test, consistent findings as above were observed (P trend < 0.005). These results were found robust in sensitivity analysis (Tables S7-S8). Additionally, subgroup analyses were further conducted to evaluate whether the CEMA-dyslipidemia link persisted across various population subgroups. It was revealed that the positive associations between CEMA exposure and dyslipidemia remained stable across most subgroups (Fig. 3). Nonetheless, significant gender-based (P interaction < 0.001) and education-based (P interaction = 0.002) interactions were identified in the analysis.

Inflammation as a mediator linking CEMA exposure to dyslipidemia

To examine whether inflammatory biomarkers mediate the link between CEMA exposure and dyslipidemia, we performed mediation analyses after adjusting for all relevant covariates (Table 5). The results indicated that WBC, NC, and LC partially mediated this relationship, with respective estimated indirect effects of 0.0055 (0.0025–0.0082), 0.0034 (0.0005–0.0077), and 0.0035 (0.0017–0.0052). These mediators accounted for 10.35%, 6.54%, and 6.52% of the total mediating effects, respectively (all P < 0.001).

Discussion

The potential health risks posed by UMVOCs as environmental pollutants have attracted increasing attention. Nevertheless, direct evidence linking exposure to UMVOCs and dyslipidemia remains scarce. This research offers initial solid proof of a positive link between exposure to UMVOCs and dyslipidemia, demonstrating that co-exposure to multiple UMVOCs significantly elevates the prevalence of dyslipidemia, with CEMA determined to be the leading contributing compound involved. Apart from the direct correlation between CEMA and dyslipidemia, our findings also highlight the important mediating role of inflammation in this relationship.

VOCs are prevalent air pollutants originating from various sources, including cigarette smoke, paints, industrial emissions, vehicle exhaust, and other environmental and occupational exposures [13]. The health hazards associated with VOCs have long been a concern, with prior research linking VOC exposure with an elevated prevalence of numerous chronic conditions, including asthma, diminished lung function, and cardiovascular diseases [14, 15]. Moreover, growing evidence also points to a link between VOC exposure and adverse metabolic health outcomes, particularly in relation to abnormal lipid profiles [16]. Notably, varying degrees of VOC exposure are closely related to changes in various hematological indices, with smoking revognized as a key source of increased VOC exposure [17]. Furthermore, non-occupational exposure to VOCs has been implicated in heightened risks of certain diseases in the general population, such as kidney stones and CVDs [18, 19]. However, limited research has specifically addressed the VOC exposure-dyslipidemia link. In the current research, our findings support as well as extend previous observations by providing evidence of a strong relationship between UMVOCs and dyslipidemia. The detection of UMVOCs serves as a biomarker primarily reflecting the body’s metabolism and elimination of VOCs following exposure [20]. The mechanisms by which UMVOCs may influence dyslipidemia likely involve multiple lipid metabolism pathways. These metabolites may disrupt lipid synthesis by altering the biosynthetic pathways of fatty acids and cholesterol in hepatocytes, thereby affecting the production and secretion of lipoproteins [21]. Moreover, UMVOCs can disrupt lipid metabolism by inducing oxidative stress as well as triggering inflammatory responses. Inflammatory mediators released through stimulation in these processes impair adipose tissue function and alter lipid storage, mobilization, and transport [22].It is noteworthy that the biological matrix used for exposure assessment (e.g., blood vs. urine) may significantly influence the observed health effects of VOCs. Blood measurements primarily reflect circulating parent compounds and recent exposure, with half-lives typically ranging from hours to days [23]. In contrast, urinary metabolites like UMVOCs represent cumulative exposure and metabolic processing, capturing longer-term exposure windows [24]. This distinction may explain why urinary metabolites demonstrate stronger associations with chronic conditions such as dyslipidemia in our study, whereas blood VOCs are more frequently linked to acute health events (e.g., cardiac arrhythmias) [25]. Mechanistically, blood VOCs can directly induce endothelial dysfunction and oxidative stress, while urinary metabolites reflect hepatic detoxification processes that may chronically disrupt lipid metabolism through inflammatory pathways, as evidenced by our mediation analysis showing significant involvement of WBC, NC, and LC.

Our study proves that co-exposure to multiple environmental UMVOCs, particularly elevated levels of CEMA, may be crucial in the development of dyslipidemia by triggering inflammatory responses. CEMA, a key UMVOCs, has been strongly associated with metabolic syndrome (MetS), obesity, and visceral fat accumulation in multiple studies [26, 27]. Chronic low-grade inflammation is a hallmark of numerous metabolic disorders. Inflammatory mediators, including interleukins and tumor necrosis factor-α, can impair adipocyte function and dysregulate fatty acid synthesis, degradation, and transport, ultimately leading to abnormal elevations in lipid levels [28]. CEMA serves as a biomarker for acrylonitrile metabolism and is primarily used to assess VOC exposure in occupational settings. Acrylonitrile, a common environmental pollutant, is widely utilized in producing synthetic rubber, plastics, as well as textiles [29]. One of its metabolites, acrolein, can not only induce inflammation but may also function as an autoantigen and extracellular signaling molecule, contributing to the vicious cycle of autoimmune diseases [30]. Animal studies have shown that oral administration of acrolein causes a rise in very LDL-C and TG levels [31]. Moreover, acrolein down-regulates key genes in the liver involved in cholesterol synthesis and uptake. It also engages in the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, causing upregulated expression of multiple pro-inflammatory cytokines and initiating a systemic inflammatory response [32]. Therefore, CEMA exposure may stimulate the immune system by increasing WBC, NC, and LC, reflecting the activation of systemic inflammatory responses. This inflammatory state not only aggravates lipid metabolism abnormalities, but may also be a major factor in the onset and advancement of MetS and other chronic conditions.

In the subgroup analysis of the present study, a potential interaction effect on the association between CEMA exposure and dyslipidemia was observed with respect to gender and education. The association between CEMA exposure and dyslipidemia may be more pronounced among females, which may result from their higher degree of VOC exposure from cosmetics and related scouces including household cleaning agents as well as household cooking activities [33, 34]. Although individuals with lower educational attainment are typically at greater risk of VOC exposure due to suboptimal living environments or occupational exposures, no significant interaction was detected in this subgroup. This may be attributed to a saturation effect, which results in a non-significant dose-effect relationship. On the contrary, a more pronounced interaction effect was observed in individuals with higher educational attainment. This population is often concentrated in urban areas, where exposure to complex sources of pollution (e.g., traffic exhaust or industrial emissions) is more prevalent. Additionally, higher health literacy and greater access to healthcare among this group may lead to increased detection and diagnosis of dyslipidemia [8]. These investigations imply that the observed CEMA exposure-dyslipidemia association is not solely dependent on the level of individual VOC exposure, but may also be influenced by gender-specific behaviors, lifestyle patterns, and socioeconomic factors. These factors deserve further investigations in future studies.

From a public health perspective, implementing effective strategies to reduce population-level exposure to harmful VOCs is of great importance. Strategies such as improving residential and occupational environments, enhancing indoor air quality management, and reducing emissions from traffic and industrial sources can effectively mitigate VOC exposure at its origin [35–38]. These environmental interventions not only contribute to the reduction of VOC-related respiratory diseases and CVDs, but may also play a beneficial role in preventing dyslipidemia and other chronic metabolic conditions. To further validate the observed association between UMVOCs and dyslipidemia, and to address cross-sectional studies’ restriction in drawing causal inferences, we plan to carry out animal experiments in which the levels of exposure to VOCs can be precisely controlled. These experiments will enable the assessment of how VOC exposure influences blood lipid levels as well as related metabolic pathways, thereby elucidating the underlying biological mechanisms. Additionally, we aim to design prospective cohort studies to monitor the levels of exposure to VOCs and lipid changes in individuals, enabling a more robust assessment of the cause-effect link between VOC exposure and dyslipidemia.

Strengths and limitations

The current research possesses certain noteworthy strengths that should be highlighted. This research pioneers the evaluation of the correlation between exposure to UMVOCs and dyslipidaemia, utilizing data from a large, rigorously controlled population-based database, thereby enhancing the reliability of the findings. We carried out multiple statistical analyses, with adjustments made for potential confounders in our analyses. UMVOCs’ concentration was used as a proxy indicator of individual exposure levels. Compared with other biological samples such as blood or exhaled gas, urine sample collection is non-invasive, convenient, easy to store, and suitable for large-scale population studies. However, several limitations still warrant acknowledgment. Firstly, one-time detection might not reliably represent long-term exposure to UMVOCs. Additionally, the concentrations of UMVOCs can be influenced by various physiological factors, potentially leading to variability and affecting the accuracy of exposure assessment. Secondly, permanent U.S. residents constituted the largest portion of the study participants. Differences in race, lifestyle, dietary structure, environmental pollution, and metabolic characteristics between populations in different regions may affect the metabolic process of UMVOCs and its pathway to lipid levels. Finally, due to the constraints inherent in a cross-sectional study design, establishing a causal link between exposure to UMVOCs and dyslipidemia was not feasible. Although the WQS model was applied for assessing the effects of co-exposure to multiple UMVOCs, it is imperative to recognize that real-world exposures to UMVOCs frequently involve more complex mixtures. This complexity introduces uncertainty in the model’s capacity to predict dyslipidaemia. Future studies should consider evaluating exposure to UMVOCs through additional biological specimens, such as blood, to further clarify their metabolic impacts.

Conclusion

Evidence from the current research supports a close link between exposure to UMVOCs and the prevalence of dyslipidemia, with CEMA identified as the predominant contributing metabolite. Additionally, it was found that inflammation partially mediates the link between CEMA exposure and the incidence of dyslipidemia, indicating that inflammation may be a potential mechanism in this process. Future longitudinal investigations are necessary to further confirming causal links and developing effective intervention strategies aimed at mitigating the adverse effects caused by exposure to UMVOCs on lipid metabolism, thereby lowering the prevalence of associated metabolic diseases.
