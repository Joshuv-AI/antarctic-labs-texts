Sclerotherapy for Aneurysmal Bone Cyst in Canine

Introduction

Aneurysmal bone cysts (ABCs) are benign but locally aggressive, expansile osteolytic lesions characterized by blood‐filled cavities separated by fibrous septa containing multinucleated giant cells, fibroblasts and reactive bone (Dowdle et al. 2003; Deventer et al. 2021; Olimpo et al. 2022). Although well described in human medicine in young adults and children, ABCs are uncommon in dogs, where they can mimic primary bone tumours such as osteosarcoma or giant cell tumour, both clinically and radiographically. Accurate differentiation from malignant bone neoplasia is critical and management of ABCs includes modalities like curettage, bone grafting or minimally invasive sclerotherapy (Deventer et al. 2021; Cottalorda and Bourelle 2006; Tsagozis and Brosjö 2015).

The pathogenesis of ABCs remains speculative and may be multifactorial in both humans and dogs. Most commonly, ABCs most commonly arise in the metaphyseal region of long bones but have also been documented in the vertebrae, pelvis, ribs and scapula (Barnhart 2002). Although ABCs are benign, local recurrence following treatment has been reported (Mei et al. 2009), and rare cases of malignant transformation have been described in humans as well as in a Labrador Retriever (Barnhart 2002). Numerous treatment modalities have been explored in both human and veterinary medicine; however, a definitive gold‐standard therapy has yet to be established (Olimpo et al. 2022; Anson et al. 2020; Muratori et al. 2019).

In human medicine, percutaneous sclerotherapy with polidocanol has emerged as a safe and effective treatment for ABCs, demonstrating high rates of cyst resolution with reduced morbidity compared to surgical excision (Deventer et al. 2021; Rastogi et al. 2006; Puri et al. 2020; Weber et al. 2023). Sclerosants damage the endothelium and start a coagulation cascade, which ends in thrombosis (Puri et al. 2020; Weber et al. 2023). Reports of such minimally invasive management strategies in veterinary patients remain limited but promising. This case report describes the diagnostic challenges and therapeutic management of a distal radial ABC in a geriatric German Shepherd dog using polidocanol sclerotherapy.

Case Description

A 10‐year‐old neutered female German Shepherd dog (31 kg) was presented with a 3–4 week history of progressive right forelimb lameness and swelling localized to the distal radius (Figure 1). The dog had no known history of trauma. Clinical examination revealed intermittently non‐weight‐bearing lameness (Grade 4), distal limb pain on palpation (Pain Score 3) and firm and painful swelling over the radial metaphysis (Mich et al. 2010; Garcia et al. 2017). Systemic parameters were within normal limits.

Photograph of the dog at the first clinical evaluation showing bulging at distal radius. The lesion was painful, firm and bony to touch.

Initial radiographs and CT imaging performed at a local facility identified an expansile lytic lesion affecting the distal radius, involving the articular surface with peripheral sclerosis and cortical erosion (Figure 2). Based on imaging, differentials included ABC and giant cell tumour (Figures 3 and 4). Owner declined the recommended MRI.

Survey radiograph of the right forelimb of the dog at presentation. Note the marked osteolytic changes seen at the distal diaphysis of the radius.

Snapshot of computed tomography showing aneurysmal bone cyst at the distal extremity of right radius with egg shell/blown‐out appearance.

Snapshot of 3D segment generated by computed tomography showing aneurysmal bone cyst at the distal extremity of right radius.

Ultrasound‐guided FNAC performed externally (by referring veterinarian) suggested a tentative diagnosis of osteosarcoma. The patient was presented with microcytic anaemia (+) and neutrophilia. The patient was prescribed carprofen at 2 mg/kg as palliative therapy.

Diagnostics

The FNA slides performed by the referring veterinarian were not available for review. Hence, repeat FNA was suggested for definitive diagnosis. FNAC of the lesion, repeated at the facility (ACTREC), revealed occasional osteoblasts and stromal fragments with no neoplastic cells. These findings were compatible with the features of ABC. Repeat radiographs at ACTREC demonstrated an expansile lytic lesion with cortical thinning, peripheral sclerosis and dimensions of approximately 4 × 2.5 × 1.5 cm, without evidence of aggressive periosteal reaction such as metastatic spread on thoracic imaging (Figures 3 and 4). Radiographic evaluation revealed sharp lesion margins, smooth zone of transition and no periosteal reaction was seen. These findings were suggestive of ABC. To confirm the diagnosis, a bone biopsy was obtained from the wall of the sclerotic lesion, following which haematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed. The biopsy findings showed presence of fibrous tissue and osteolytic lesions absence of giant cells and neoplastic cells, confirming the diagnosis of ABC (Figures 5 and 6).

Photomicrograph showing sclerotic bone with intervening fibrous stroma, consistent with an aneurysmal bone cyst. H&E stain (10×).

Photomicrograph of the lesion showing sclerotic bone trabeculae within a fibrous stromal background, consistent with aneurysmal bone cyst. H&E stain (10×).

Treatment

Following histopathological confirmation of an ABC, ultrasound‐guided percutaneous sclerotherapy with polidocanol was recommended. After obtaining informed consent from the owner, treatment was initiated. The patient was premedicated with atropine sulphate (Tropine, Neon Laboratories Ltd., India) at 0.04 mg/kg body weight subcutaneously. Dexamethasone sodium (Dexona, Zydus Cadilla, India) was administered at 0.1 mg/kg subcutaneously along with buprenorphine (Buprigesic, Neon Laboratories Ltd., India) at 0.01 mg/kg intravenously as an analgesic during the procedure. Additionally, diazepam (Lori, Neon Laboratories Ltd., India) was used as a sedative, administered intravenously at a dose of 0.2 mg/kg body weight. Induction was achieved with propofol (Neorof, Neon Laboratories Ltd., India) at 2 mg/kg IV and maintenance with isoflurane aiming for a 1% of minimal alveolar concentration after endotracheal intubation, in a mixture of oxygen and air. The site for injection was shaved and prepared aseptically using Betadine scrub, to minimize the risk of postoperative infections. Sclerosant—3% polidocanol solution (30 mg/mL; Samarth Life Sciences Pvt. Ltd., Solan, India) was administered into the cystic cavity percutaneously under general anaesthesia. The required treatment volume was calculated based on cyst size measured from biplanar radiographs, using the product of its length (4 cm), width (2.5 cm) and depth (1.5 cm), yielding an estimated volume of 15 mL. Approximately 1 mL of 3% polidocanol per 1 cm3 volume of cyst was injected, with a maximum of 6 mL per injection site, calculated based on reports by Rastogi et al. (2006) and Nair and Jacob (2016). Backflow of the sclerosant was minimized by maintaining needle occlusion for 1 min, followed by a flush with 1 mL of normal saline (Figure 7). Post‐treatment, the affected part of the limb was immobilized by epoxy bandage for 8 weeks (Figure 8). The pet parents were advised course of oral antibiotics, cefixime at 100 mg (Cefix, Cipla Ltd., India), dosed at 15 mg/kg for 7 days, along with carprofen 100 mg (Cardoyl, SavaVet Ltd., India) at a dose of 3 mg/kg orally, for 3 days post‐procedure pain management.

Photograph of the dog after treatment showing the injection site of sclerosant.

Photograph of the 8th‐week follow‐up post‐treatment.

Survey radiographs at 8th‐week post‐treatment showed healing of the affected area yet multiple cystic areas can be seen (Figure 9). Repeat sclerotherapy session was planned but due to hyper‐creatinemia and elevated ALT and ALP, general anaesthesia was not possible.

Eighth‐week post‐treatment radiograph of the dog showing healing of the lesion and yet multiple cystic areas can be seen.

Results

At the 8th‐week follow‐up visit, the patient displayed lameness at walk that worsened while trotting (Grade 2) and pain score of 1 was observed. Survey radiographs at the 8th‐week post‐treatment showed healing of the affected area yet multiple cystic areas can be seen. The dog regained a normal symmetrical forelimb muscle tone bilaterally and no evidence of lameness was observed during walking or trotting (Grade 0); pain score of 0 was recorded as the dog displayed no distress when right forelimb was handled at the 7‐months follow‐up examination. The pet parent declined request for the 7th‐month follow‐up radiograph. Pet parent on follow‐up stated that the dog survived for 13 months post‐treatment; cause of death was reported to be renal insufficiency and haemorrhagic enteritis. No lameness was observed up to the death of the patient.

Discussion

ABCs are rare in dogs and often mimic malignant bone tumours on imaging (Barnhart 2002). Differentiation from osteosarcoma is essential yet challenging because both can present with cortical destruction, lytic changes and giant‐cell–rich cytology (refer Table 1) (Dowdle et al. 2003; Deventer et al. 2021; Olimpo et al. 2022; Barnhart 2002; Anson et al. 2020). This case underscores the importance of multimodal diagnostics, including repeat cytology and definitive histopathological evaluation.

Reported therapies include grafting of the cystic cavity, sclerotherapy (Deventer et al. 2021; Rastogi et al. 2006; Brosjö et al. 2013; Puri et al. 2020; Anson et al. 2020; Adamsbaum et al. 2003; Lambot‐Juhan et al. 2012), cementoplasty and radiation therapy (Vignoli et al. 2015), ostectomy of the affected bone segment (Barnhart 2002) and curettage (Olimpo et al. 2022; Lee et al. 2025).

The use of polidocanol sclerotherapy, though well established in human medicine, is still emerging in veterinary practice. Polidocanol like other sclerosants induces endothelial damage, thrombosis and subsequent fibrosis within the cystic cavity (Guex 1993; Frullini and Cavezzi 2002). Human studies report success rates exceeding 80%–90% without compromising on safety and efficacy (Rastogi et al. 2006; Brosjö et al. 2013; Puri et al. 2020; Weber et al. 2023). In this case, sclerotherapy was well tolerated and avoided the morbidity associated with curettage, bone grafting or limb amputation.
