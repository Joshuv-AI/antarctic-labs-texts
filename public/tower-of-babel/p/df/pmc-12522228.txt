Bridging “isolated islands”: heterogeneous social networks facilitate the social integration of Chinese migrant workers
Authors: Hanrui Wang, Noshaba Aziz, Qingjun Zhao
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChina’s migrant workers have long resided in the city’s “isolated islands”, facing challenges in fully integrating into urban life. This study investigates the impact of social networks on the social integration of migrant workers, focusing on the role of diverse social networks in enhancing integration.MethodsUsing 2018 CLDS data, the study empirically analyzes the relationship between social networks and migrant workers’ social integration, employing endogeneity treatment, robustness testing, and Heterogeneity analysis with a 2SLS model.ResultsThe results show that social networks significantly influence migrant workers’ social integration, particularly through increased participation in social organizations and activities. Moreover, diverse social networks improve social integration by enhancing social trust levels, with effects varying across age, education, living environment, and migration type.ConclusionsThese findings deepen our understanding of social integration processes and offer insights for policy interventions to promote inclusive urbanization. By emphasizing the importance of diverse social networks in fostering social participation, this study underscores the need to enhance interpersonal relationships to facilitate migrant workers’ integration into urban communities. To enhance migrant workers’ social integration, it is essential to enhance the institutional environment, promote communication between migrant workers and urban residents, and foster a culture of trust in contemporary society.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03438-w.Keywords: Migrant workers, Heterogeneity social network, Social integration, Social trust

Introduction

Migrant workers play a crucial role in propelling China’s economic advancement [21]. Since the initiation of reforms and the adoption of an open-door policy in 1978, China has transitioned into a market-oriented economy, leading to a significant influx of rural farmers into urban labor markets and fueling the nation’s rapid economic growth. This shift from rural to urban settings and from agricultural to non-agricultural sectors has brought about substantial changes in the workplace dynamics for migrant workers. Nevertheless, migrant workers primarily view their urban employment as a means of livelihood and have not fully integrated into the fabric of urban life [55]. They have long been confined to urban enclaves, utilizing urban employment solely for sustenance [8]. The lack of access to housing, education, healthcare, and other social welfare amenities contributes to their sense of alienation and disconnection from urban communities [18, 62], thereby undermining social cohesion in China and impeding sustainable economic development. The 2023 Survey Report on Migrant Workers Monitoring by the National Bureau of Statistics of China revealed that merely 38.2% of migrant workers in urban areas engaged in community activities, with only 29.1% participating sporadically.

Recent years have seen advancements in laws, implementation of supportive policies, and a gradual increase in the income of migrant workers. This has led to a relaxation of institutional constraints, a thawing and loosening of the household registration system, and an enhancement in the willingness and ability of migrant workers to integrate into society [68]. The primary challenge facing migrant workers’ social integration lies in their adaptation to urban life. Many migrant workers have not fully embraced urban culture, leading to distinct behavior patterns compared to urban residents. The persistence of rural thought processes, life concepts, and behavioral norms among migrant workers acts as a barrier to their ongoing socialization [22]. Failure to integrate socially, including in daily behaviors and lifestyles, may impede their economic integration, further marginalizing them within urban settings [38, 70].

Individual decision-making among migrant workers is influenced not only by institutional constraints and economic factors but also by their social networks [16, 17]. Migrant workers navigate two distinct types of social networks simultaneously: one characterized by familial and village ties based on blood, kinship, and geography, and the other by relationships with locals and other non-native individuals in urban settings, grounded in business and mutual interests. These networks differ fundamentally in their cultural underpinnings, with the former reflecting traditional rural and acquaintance-based societies, and the latter embodying modern urban settings dominated by unfamiliar interactions. Consequently, the roots of these networks shape their impact on migrant workers’ social integration behaviors. While heterogeneous social networks theoretically align more closely with urban social norms, their practical role in facilitating migrant workers’ integration into cities and the underlying mechanisms remain underexplored in current literature. Addressing these gaps is crucial for a comprehensive understanding of migrant workers’ experiences and societal integration dynamics. Contact with local residents is a prerequisite for migrant workers’ integration, enabling them to grasp urban culture, understand residents’ lifestyles, and gradually become bridges between rural and urban communities [53]. Concurrently, migrant workers undergo changes in their own behaviors, exhibiting increased resemblance to urban residents, shifts in social identity, and the reconfiguration of their cultural values.

Social integration is a critical marker of migrant workers’ transition from urban "isolated islands" to genuine inclusion, representing the external expression of their sense of belonging and identity [55]. Previous research has identified three distinct levels of migrant labor integration within urban areas: economic, social, and psychological inclusion [19, 29, 56–58]. The primary impetus for migrant workers relocating to cities is the higher income potential, with economic gain being their primary objective at the outset. This phase represents a pragmatic choice aimed at securing livelihoods rather than true integration. Social integration, the subsequent stage, serves as a bridge between the initial economic drive and deeper integration. It is characterized by migrant workers gradually assimilating into the lifestyle and daily practices of urban dwellers. Over time, influenced by factors such as residency status and sense of belonging, they assimilate into community groups, embrace urban customs, and ultimately engage in urban life. The final phase, psychological integration, involves a reshaping of migrant workers’ values through urban work and life experiences, leading to a psychological acceptance of urban culture and values, and a sense of belonging for migrant workers within the urban environment [73]. The three dimensions unfold progressively: economic integration underpins social integration, which in turn lays the foundation for psychological integration. This sequential trajectory is essential to the gradual formation of a "civil society."

However, currently, academic research on the social integration of migrant workers lacks specificity, often treating it as an indicator of urban integration rather than a primary research focus. Social integration is believed to facilitate migrant workers’ understanding, recognition, and acceptance of urban culture, thereby enhancing their integration into cities. Social exclusion theory provides the primary framework for explaining the difficulties migrants face in integration. It emphasizes that exclusion is not a single phenomenon but spans economic, political, and sociocultural domains, and that individuals may enter or escape exclusion at different stages of the life cycle [1, 12, 33, 51]. Building on this theory, some studies explain the social integration of Chinese migrant workers through the lens of the hukou system, housing rights, excessive labor, and subjective class. They argue that an agricultural household registration system diminishes migrants’ public consciousness, constraining their integration [9]. Conversely, access to housing ownership and quality public services has been shown to positively impact migrants’ social integration [49]. Zhu [74] suggests that excessive labor leads to psychological stress among migrant workers, diminishing their subjective social status and impeding their social integration. The perception of subjective hierarchy plays a crucial role in the social integration of migrant populations, with the belief in upward mobility opportunities motivating their engagement in social activities [52].

Our analyses suggest that heterogeneous social networks align more closely with the operational logics of urban social life. Yet an empirical question remains: do such networks truly enable Chinese migrant workers to escape their "isolated islands" and achieve substantive urban integration, and through what mechanisms? Existing Literature still exhibits shortcomings. To address this gap, we leverage the 2018 China Labor-force Dynamics Survey and frame the inquiry within social exclusion theory. We disaggregate social integration into five measurable domains-culture, sports, learning, skills, and public welfare-and test whether heterogeneous social networks facilitate integration via peer effects and social trust. Distinguishing itself from prior studies, this research emphasizes social integration as a catalyst for migrant workers’ comprehensive urban assimilation. By examining the underlying mechanisms of social networks, we aim to deepen the understanding of migrant workers’ social integration processes. Specifically, we delve into how heterogeneous social networks affect social integration through peer effects and social trust, elucidating the pathways through which social networks exert their influence.

The paper’s subsequent structure is outlined as follows: Chapter 2 comprises a Literature review and theoretical analysis, presenting theoretical hypotheses. Chapter 3 details data sources, main variables, and identification strategies. Chapter 4 presents the primary results of empirical analysis. Chapter 5 discusses the findings of the study. Ultimately, research conclusions and policy recommendations are derived based on the preceding analysis.

Literature review and research hypothesis

Literature Review

Social exclusion, segmented assimilation, cultural integration, and structural-economic disparities constitute the dominant theoretical lenses through which immigrant incorporation is analysed [1, 45, 47, 51, 59, 71]. Segmented-assimilation theory posits that incorporation outcomes are heterogeneous: ethnic identity, socio-economic origins, household composition, and the institutional context of the receiving society jointly carve divergent assimilation trajectories [2, 40, 43]. Equally consequential are the social networks forged during migration. Mitra & Murayama [35] demonstrate that network externalities are decisive in channelling migrants toward specific destinations and occupations, thereby interlinking geographically and institutionally distinct markets and fostering cross-regional cultural convergence [39]. These networks simultaneously lower migration costs and raise expected returns, while also exerting pressure for legal-institutional reforms that shape migrants’ subsequent integration [34]. Lin [25, 27] argues that individuals leverage social ties to access resources and enhance social status-a dynamic corroborated among disadvantaged Hispanic immigrants [28, 48].

The influence of social networks on immigrant integration transcends pecuniary gains, extending to the enhancement of public quality of life [42]. As repositories of reciprocity norms, networks possess not only private value but also positive externalities: sustained interaction between immigrants and natives lubricates collective integration and elevates the quality of civic life [7, 41]. Consequently, firms and civic associations-qua communities-have incentives to augment their members’ social capital [26]. Granovetter’s [16] seminal notion of "weak ties" captures this mechanism with precision. Relative to strong ties, weak ties exert a disproportionately stronger effect on participation in public affairs [67, 75]. Extending the argument, Granovetter [17] advanced the concept of embeddedness, portraying markets as enmeshed in relational networks and foregrounding how social structures shape individual action-an analytical scaffold now indispensable to migration scholarship.

Current research on the social integration of Chinese migrant workers largely draws on social exclusion theory, institutional economics, and cultural integration, interpreting migrants’ integration choices as products of institutional constraints or economic behaviours such as the household registration system, property rights, identity, labor conditions, and institutional barriers. Chen et al. [9] discovered that having an agricultural hukou diminishes the public awareness of migrant workers, consequently hindering their social integration. This effect remains significant regardless of increases in income or education level. Moreover, institutional barriers linked to the household registration system negatively impact the public participation of migrant workers. Apart from the household registration system, access to housing property rights and public services significantly influences public participation. Complete housing property rights and high-quality public services notably enhance the social integration of migrant populations [49]. Furthermore, the economic foundation remains a key factor in the social integration of migrant workers, with greater job stability correlating with increased participation in social activities [72], yet longer working hours lead to heightened psychological pressure and a decreased subjective social status among migrant workers, consequently lowering their social integration level [74]. Beyond economic and institutional determinants, scholars have examined the factors influencing the social integration of the floating population from three dimensions: identity, field, and identity [32, 55]. Some researchers have observed that the social integration rate is higher among young immigrant groups when viewed through an intergenerational lens, as they utilize the Internet as a platform [61]. The perception of subjective stratum is a critical factor influencing the social integration of the floating population, where upward mobility opportunities can stimulate their integration [52]. Additionally, differences among ethnic groups significantly impact the social integration of the floating population, with ethnic minorities exhibiting lower levels of participation in system involvement and social activities compared to the Han nationality [69]. Moreover, the use of dialects is believed to impede the social integration of migrant workers and their inclination to permanently settle [31].

Framed as a key explanatory paradigm for migration, researchers have recently drawn on social network and social capital theories to construct qualitative accounts of migrant workers’ social integration through relational lenses such as "New-Year’s nets" and "discussion nets" [50]. Social networks play a crucial role in their job search process. Compared to urban residents, migrant workers’ social networks exhibit high homogeneity. These networks impact migrant workers’ city integration in two ways: firstly, by reducing job search and transaction costs in the job market due to asymmetric information [29],and secondly, by fostering a sense of local identity among migrant workers [30, 54]. Bian and Lu [5] have studied the development of social capital, noting that cross-border intergenerational and population mobility significantly expand individuals’ social networks, thereby enhancing social capital. Moreover, their analysis of job-hunting survey data spanning from 1978 to 2014 reveals a consistent rise in the importance of "guanxi" in Chinese society [6].

In recent years, there has been a significant advancement in the study of social networks, particularly in understanding the influence of various network structures on economic interactions within society. Research has revealed that traditional clan relationships notably impact the employment of migrant workers. Contrary to a simplistic view of kinship as a social network, investigations have shown that kinship operates through the mechanism of trust. However, the efficacy of traditional kinship ties is constrained by their reliance on local cultural norms, thereby limiting their utility in aiding migrant workers in accessing valuable job information [10].

This research extends the understanding of social network dynamics to the realm of trust, aligning with existing studies on the significance of trust in academic discourse. At a broader societal level, trust plays a crucial role in facilitating the integration of migrant workers [63] and can bolster economic development by reducing overall transaction costs within society [20]. In rural settings, social trust has been shown to enhance labor mobility [15]. Furthermore, empirical investigations have examined the impact of various forms of trust on diverse aspects such as job search strategies [10], financial decisions related to family risk assets [66], and engagement in social organizations [36].

Research hypotheses

The current literature has addressed the social integration and social networks of migrant workers, offering valuable insights and policy recommendations. However, certain shortcomings persist. Firstly, research on migrant workers’ social integration lacks specificity, often treating it as a minor aspect of urban integration. This perspective attributes the decision-making process of migrant workers’ social integration primarily to institutional constraints or economic factors, overlooking the significant influence of their social networks. Secondly, existing studies focusing on social networks fail to provide a thorough analysis of migrant workers’ social connections and offer unclear explanations of the mechanisms through which social networks affect them.

Social networks play a crucial role in the social integration of migrant workers, particularly in urban settings characterized by significant information asymmetry within social activities markets. Non-profit social organizations and activities face high costs when disseminating information through public media, leading to a concentration of information within the publishers’ social networks. Given the dispersed demand for social integration among urban residents, the formation and growth of social organizations and activities heavily rely on interpersonal information exchange. Consequently, social networks enable migrant workers to access social integration information more efficiently, reducing information acquisition costs. Notably, a more diverse social network enhances the likelihood of migrant workers accessing a variety of social integration information, aligning with their specific needs and enhancing their prospects for social integration. Based on these premises, the following research hypotheses are posited:

Hypothesis 1: Greater heterogeneity within social networks correlates positively with the level of social integration among migrant workers.

The influence of social networks on the social integration mechanisms of migrant workers is not singular. Social networks can affect the social integration of migrant workers through peer effects. Peer effects represent a distinct externality stemming from social networks, characterized by the cascading impact of behaviors and attitudes among peers. In situations where information is limited, individuals within the same social group frequently look to their peers’ choices as a reference point when making their own decisions, giving rise to mutual peer effects.

Social trust, in addition to peer influence, likely serves as the primary mechanism through which social networks impact the social integration of migrant workers. According to Fukuyama F [14], trust represents a key metric for assessing "transaction costs" within the framework of new institutional economics. Trust essentially embodies a cultural hierarchy that facilitates transactions and engagement. It is both a product of rational decision-making and the cultivation of individual social connections. Greater diversity within social networks is associated with heightened levels of social trust among migrant workers, consequently fostering increased levels of social integration. Furthermore, the rise in trust levels propelled by heterogeneous social networks may not be confined to interpersonal trust, prolonged local interactions can engender migrant workers’ institutional trust in the broader urban social order, thereby further elevating their social integration [4, 13]. Based on these premises, the following research hypotheses are posited:

Hypothesis 2: Heterogeneous social networks facilitate the social integration of migrant workers by fostering peer effects and social trust.

In addition, the impact of social networks on migrant workers’ social integration is likely heterogeneous. One source of heterogeneity is generational divergence: cohorts differ systematically in social interaction patterns and identity formation [11, 44]. In the Chinese context, earlier migrants-shaped by a historical attachment to land-viewed urban employment primarily as a temporary income strategy rather than a pathway to permanent settlement, whereas younger migrants express stronger aspirations for long-term urban residence. Education constitutes another potential moderator; higher-educated migrants may invest more heavily in cultivating heterogeneous social ties. Moreover, because large numbers of migrant workers reside on the urban–rural fringe, spatial segregation from native urban residents can restrict network expansion, implying that residential environment shapes the extent to which networks influence integration. Finally, the type of destination matters: townships and suburban zones intermediate spaces that are economically semi-urban yet socially and behaviourally rural-offer fewer social activities, thereby attenuating network effects. Based on these premises, the following research hypotheses are posited:

Hypothesis 3: The positive effect of heterogeneous social networks on migrant workers’ social integration is heterogeneous; the impact is stronger among younger migrants, those with higher education, those residing in well-serviced communities, and those who have moved into urban districts.

Materials and method

Data source

The primary data source for this study is the China Labor-force Dynamic Survey (CLDS) conducted nationwide in 2018 by the Social Science Survey Center of Sun Yat-sen University in China. This survey utilized a multi-stage, multi-level sampling approach to gather information on individual demographic and employment details, as well as family and community characteristics, encompassing three levels of analysis: individual, family, and community. The sample, representative of China as a whole, includes data from 28 provinces, municipalities, and autonomous regions, aligning with the study’s objectives.

To specifically investigate the social integration of migrant workers, the study focused on 1302 valid samples selected from the male and female floating population aged 15 and above. These individuals were identified based on their agricultural household registration status outside their current district (county or city), migration history related to labor/work or business, and the absence of missing key variables.

Variables

The independent variable under consideration is the social integration of migrant workers, as defined by behaviors facilitating their assimilation into urban environments and engagement in urban pursuits.

Following Li [24] and Zhu [74], and conditioned by data availability, we operationalize social integration as behavior that enable migrant workers to assimilate into urban environments and engage in urban pursuits. Grounded in social exclusion theory, we treat social integration as a multidimensional construct [1, 51] and measure it along five dimensions: participation in cultural activities, engagement in sports activities, involvement in educational pursuits, participation in skill-building endeavors, and engagement in public welfare initiatives. Specifically, participation in cultural activities is assessed by involvement in events organized by entertainment and art groups in the past year, while engagement in sports activities is determined by participation in sports organization activities during the same period. Participation in learning activities is evaluated based on involvement in knowledge-sharing or learning groups, such as reading clubs, within the past year. Participation in skill-building activities is gauged by participation in skill development group activities over the same timeframe. Lastly, involvement in public welfare activities is measured by participation in initiatives organized by volunteer organizations, social workers, or volunteers within the past year. The overall level of social integration among migrant workers is quantified by the cumulative number of dimensions in which they engage in social activities. Within the sample population, the highest participation rate in sports activities is 21.1%, while the lowest participation rate in skill-building activities stands at 7.5%. Participation rates in cultural, learning, and public welfare activities are 16.8%, 10.4%, and 10.1%, respectively. On average, migrant workers exhibit a social activity participation score of 0.660 across all dimensions.

The independent variable under consideration is the social network of migrant workers, operationalized as the number of individuals capable of providing practical support and assistance in both work and personal matters. Social network Heterogeneity is assessed by the ratio of local individuals and other non-local individuals among neighbors and coworkers who have migrated to the city. Within the study cohort, the mean social network size of farmers is 4.366, with a corresponding social network Heterogeneity rate of 23.1%.

The study utilized instrumental variables, specifically neighborhood mutual aid behavior and language ability within migrant workers’ residential communities, to address potential endogeneity issues between the social network and social participation of migrant workers. Social networks may be influenced by self-selection among migrant workers, leading to non-random sampling and potential bias in estimation results if directly regressed upon. Additionally, the level of social participation among migrant workers could impact the development and restructuring of their social networks, creating a situation of reverse causality.

To mitigate these issues, we employed "neighborhood mutual aid behavior" and "language ability" at the community level as instrumental variables for the heterogeneity of migrant workers’ social networks. "Neighborhood mutual aid behavior" pertains to the presence of mutual aid organizations within migrant workers’ communities, while language ability refers to the proficiency of migrant workers in the local dialect or Mandarin.

Neighbourhood mutual assistance within the community increases interactions between migrant workers and local residents, and language proficiency is likewise a key constraint on such interactions, so the two instrumental variables satisfy the relevance condition. At the same time, neighbourhood mutual assistance is unlikely to directly influence migrant workers’ participation in the five social activities examined-cultural, sports, learning, skills, and public-welfare activities-because these activities rarely take place within a single community. They are typically organised across multiple communities or by governments and firms, with only a negligible share occurring inside the community (e.g., occasional blood-donation drives) that would scarcely affect our estimates. Similarly, while language proficiency influences migrant workers’ interactions with locals, it does not directly determine whether they participate in these five activities. Even migrants who cannot speak the local dialect or Mandarin can still take part, because listening and non-verbal communication usually account for a far larger share of interaction in these social activities than verbal expression.

Control variables in this study encompass established research methodologies and oversight of demographic factors such as gender, age, marital status, education, Internet usage, and migrant workers’ health status. Additionally, to capture heterogeneity in dialect command and destination-specific social-network endowments between migrants who move across provinces and those who move within a province, we include a dummy indicating whether the migrant worker move across provinces.

For the mechanism variables, we examine peer effects and social trust. Peer effects are captured by the average participation level of other migrant workers excluding the focal individual. Social trust is proxied by the survey item "trust in society as a whole." To dissect the trust mechanism further, we differentiate between interpersonal trust-comprising particularized trust (relatives, friends, fellow villagers) and generalized trust (strangers)-and institutional trust. Because no direct measure of institutional trust is available, we use two indirect indicators: perceptions of the overall national situation and willingness for one’s children to join the military, both of which reflect respondents’ attitudes toward existing institutions. Endogeneity

Despite controlling for potential factors influencing migrant workers’ social integration in the baseline regression analysis, the issue of reverse causality may persist in the empirical analysis. To address this concern, we employed a two-stage least squares (2SLS) model for further examination. Table 3 presents the estimates derived from the 2SLS analysis. The first-stage estimation results in column (1) indicate a significant positive impact of both neighborhood mutual aid behavior and language proficiency on the extent of diverse social networks among migrant workers, thereby satisfying the instrumental variable correlation requirement. Subsequently, the results from the second stage, as shown in column (2), reveal a persistently positive and statistically significant effect of diverse social networks on the social integration of migrant workers, with a substantial marginal effect of 0.881. The weak instrumental variable test confirms the greater reliability of the 2SLS model estimates compared to those of the baseline regression. Consequently, the positive association between heterogeneous social networks and migrant workers’ social integration is further substantiated, thereby providing additional confirmation of Hypothesis 1.

Robustness

To validate Hypothesis 1, we assessed the impact of diverse social networks of migrant workers on their engagement across five social activity dimensions. The outcomes, detailed in Table 4, reveal that diverse social networks significantly enhance migrant workers’ involvement in cultural, sports, skill, and public welfare activities, with respective marginal effects of 0.098, 0.061, 0.036, and 0.87. Nonetheless, the diversity of social networks does not influence migrant workers’ participation in educational activities, potentially attributed to limited avenues for direct engagement in learning activities, which are often facilitated by governmental or corporate entities. Overall, the affirmative influence of diverse social networks on the social assimilation of migrant workers has been further substantiated.

Heterogeneity analysis

It has been determined that the diverse social networks of migrant workers can enhance their social integration. However, it is essential to recognize that this effect is only an aggregate observation across the entire sample, without considering its variability. To obtain more nuanced insights, it is imperative to examine the impact of diverse social networks among migrant workers in various contexts. Consequently, we categorize migrant workers based on factors such as age, educational attainment, residential setting, and migration destination. The outcomes are depicted in Fig. 2.

Heterogeneous analysis of the impact of heterogeneous social networks on social integration

Panel A presents the age-grouped outcomes. The integration of migrant workers into urban areas has long been a focal point in labor economics research, particularly concerning generational disparities. Previous studies commonly use the Birth year of 1980 as a demarcation, categorizing migrant workers into two cohorts: the older and younger generations, to examine urban integration discrepancies [19]. In contrast to existing literature, this study further stratifies migrant workers born post-1980 into two subgroups: "pre-90 s" and "post-90 s" investigating the impact of social networks on the social integration levels across three generations of migrant workers. The findings reveal that social network diversity significantly enhances social integration solely among the most recent cohort of post-1990 migrant workers. This phenomenon may stem from the primary objective of pre-1990 migrant workers migrating to urban areas to augment their family’s income without a long-term urban residency plan, while post-90 s migrant workers exhibit a stronger inclination towards urban integration, with their expanded social network playing a more pronounced role in fostering social integration.

Panel B displays the outcomes of education-based grouping. We hypothesize that individuals with higher levels of education may exhibit greater emphasis on fostering social connections, with increased diversity in social networks correlating with heightened engagement in social endeavors. To investigate the impact of education on social network dynamics, this study categorizes migrant workers into three groups based on their educational attainment. These groups include individuals with junior high school education or below, those with high school education, and those with college education or higher. The findings indicate that among migrant workers with college education or higher, greater social network diversity is associated with increased social integration. Thus, for this subgroup of workers, network heterogeneity emerges as a pivotal factor in facilitating social engagement decision-making processes.

The presence of ample social organizations and diverse social activities is essential for migrant workers to engage in social interactions and networks effectively. However, many migrant workers reside in peripheral urban areas, such as urban–rural fringes, where they are often isolated from urban residents, limiting their opportunities to expand social networks and participate in social activities crucial for enhancing social integration. Consequently, the level of social integration among migrant workers in different residential environments may be influenced by their social networks. To investigate this hypothesis, this study categorizes migrant workers’ residential communities based on whether all roads within them are equipped with street lamps. The analysis, presented in Panel C, reveals that social network diversity does not significantly impact the social integration of migrant workers residing in communities with inadequate infrastructure. Conversely, for migrant workers living in communities with better infrastructure, greater social network diversity is associated with higher levels of social integration.

Based on the varying social network impacts stemming from different residential environments as discussed above, it is more plausible to posit that distinct types of inflow areas may yield more pronounced disparities. Accordingly, this study categorizes the sample into three groups based on the interviewees’ inflow locations: migrant workers entering townships, those entering urban suburbs, and those entering urban areas. Each group of migrant workers is analyzed separately, with the outcomes presented in Panel D. The findings indicate that the size of the social network does not significantly influence the level of social integration among migrant workers relocating to townships. Conversely, for migrant workers moving to suburbs and cities, a positive correlation exists between network heterogeneity and the extent of social integration. Thus, the efficacy of social networks in enhancing the social integration of migrant workers hinges on the presence of ample social organizations and activities in their inflow regions, as well as their residence in communities with superior infrastructure and heightened interactions with urban dwellers. Detailed heterogeneity results are reported in Appendix Table A.1-Table A.4.

Mechanism analysis

As previously discussed, social networks can generate a unique externality that influences the social engagement of migrant workers in decision-making processes. Moreover, the expansion of social network size and increased heterogeneity can lower the "transaction cost" associated with migrant workers’ involvement in social activities, encouraging their participation and integration into urban life. To investigate this phenomenon, we examine the impact of peer effects and social trust as explanatory variables on the social integration of migrant workers. The empirical findings are presented in Table 6. Column (1) displays the regression results of peer effects within social networks among migrant workers. In this context, peer effects in the social engagement of migrant workers are gauged by the average participation level of other migrant workers excluding the focal individual. If the peer effects in the decision-making processes of migrant workers are sufficiently strong, they may resemble the social integration patterns of their peers in the community. The results indicate that the heterogeneity of social networks does not significantly influence the peer effects among migrant workers. This may stem from the complexity of peer effects mechanisms: information sharing and behavioural norms are both potential channels, and from the ambiguity of the "peer" concept. Migrants’ neighborhoods are often determined by occupational clustering, and the generally low level of social integration across the migrant worker population may further attenuate these effects.

Further analysis

The study aims to explore the impact of social integration on migrant workers’ psychological well-being in urban areas. Specifically, the research examines how diverse social networks influence the social integration of migrant workers, which in turn plays a crucial role in enhancing their happiness and satisfaction with urban life. To investigate this relationship, the study considers migrant workers’ happiness, life satisfaction, and economic well-being as dependent variables, while their degree of social integration serves as the independent variable. The regression analysis results, presented in Table 7, demonstrate significant positive effects of social integration on migrant workers’ happiness, Life satisfaction, and economic well-being. The estimated coefficients are 0.039, 0.047, and 0.068, respectively, and are statistically significant at the 10%, 5%, and 1% levels. These findings indicate that higher levels of social integration among migrant workers are associated with increased happiness, life satisfaction, and economic well-being. Therefore, fostering diverse social networks and enhancing social integration are crucial for promoting the psychological well-being and integration of migrant workers in urban environments.

Discussion

Accelerating the integration of migrant workers into urban areas and promoting new urbanization are essential for achieving agricultural modernization. As institutional constraints gradually loosen, facilitating the transition of migrant workers from urban enclaves to full integration into city life has emerged as a pressing historical imperative. Prior research has explored this issue through the lenses of the household registration system, property rights, identity, labor markets, and social integration, attributing the level of integration primarily to institutional barriers and economic behaviors. Notably, economic factors such as household registration status and housing conditions in urban areas have been identified as critical determinants of migrant workers’ social engagement [9, 49]. Nevertheless, despite improvements in income and housing, migrant workers continue to face challenges in social integration, underscoring the inadequacy of solely economic considerations in addressing this issue. Consequently, scholars have underscored the significance of identity distinctions between migrant workers and native urban residents [32, 55]. Furthermore, the subjective perception of their social class by migrant workers, influenced by urban class disparities, also significantly impacts their social integration [52].

Prior studies have predominantly focused on economic and social factors rather than delving into the genesis of individual traits among migrant workers. However, as integral components of the societal framework, migrant workers’ social networks play a pivotal role in shaping their engagement in social endeavors and assimilation into urban settings. This research, therefore, scrutinizes the influence of diverse social networks on the social integration of migrant workers. Our findings reveal a substantial positive correlation between the heterogeneity of social networks and the social integration of migrant workers. Specifically, heightened diversity within migrant workers’ social circles correlates with increased participation in social activities and enhanced integration into urban communities. Employing a 2SLS model with neighborhood mutual aid behavior and language proficiency as instrumental variables, the impact of diverse social networks remains robust. Furthermore, we extend our analysis to explore the effects of cultural, sporting, educational, vocational, and philanthropic engagement on the influence of diverse social networks. The outcomes underscore the significant positive impact of diverse social networks on migrant workers’ involvement in cultural, sporting, educational, vocational, and philanthropic pursuits. Subsequent heterogeneous analysis reveals a more pronounced effect of diverse social networks on the social integration of migrant workers "post-90 s", individuals with higher educational attainment, improved living conditions, and those migrating to urban centers. Moreover, our findings reveal that social trust constitutes a critical mechanism through which heterogeneous social networks enhance migrant workers’ social integration. In particular, we disaggregate trust into particularized and generalized components, underscoring that network heterogeneity not only elevates interpersonal trust but also fosters institutional trust. Although institutional trust cannot fully substitute for institutional inclusion, it operates as a "catalyst" that amplifies the efficacy of inclusive institutions [3, 65].

Our study underscores more robust policy implications compared to prior research. While our findings do not contradict existing literature on economic and social factors, they reveal that enhancing communication between migrant workers and local residents can facilitate migrant workers’ transition from social isolation to active participation in urban social life. This integration into the urban fabric signifies the importance of governmental focus on promoting social integration among migrant workers. Leveraging migrant workers’ communities and workplaces [23, 46], expanding their social networks, encouraging interactions with local residents [37], diversifying their social connections, and mitigating alienation between migrant workers and locals are crucial steps for fostering social cohesion in urban settings.

Indeed, the Chinese government has already undertaken extensive initiatives in this domain. In 2014, the State Council issued the Opinion on Further Improving Services for Migrant Workers, mandating the construction or renovation of cultural activity centres, libraries and e-reading rooms in communities and industrial zones where migrant workers are concentrated, so as to fully incorporate this group into the urban public cultural-service system. Local governments in Beijing, Guangdong and other regions have launched neighbourhood-mutual-aid programmes for migrant workers through social-enterprise competitions, establishing community public spaces and volunteer service teams to create platforms for communication and assistance. At the same time, the central government attaches great importance to vocational-skills training: the Ministry of Human Resources and Social Security and other departments have introduced the New-Generation Migrant-Worker Vocational-Skills Enhancement Programme and similar schemes, providing direct subsidies and social-insurance allowances to migrant workers [60]. Finally, the government has built digital platforms that serve migrant workers by leveraging big-data technologies to track employment, mobility and innovation in real time [64]. These measures offer a clear roadmap for tackling rural–urban exclusion in developing countries.

Conclusion and limitations of the study

Integrating migrant workers into urban environments and advancing new urbanization are essential for China’s modernization objectives. As institutional barriers ease, a new challenge arises: integrating migrant workers into urban life beyond the economic sphere, encompassing behavior, lifestyle, and social identity. This study examines this issue through the lens of social networks, exploring ways to enhance the social participation of migrant workers.

This paper utilizes the China Labor Force Dynamics Survey 2018 (CLDS2018) to examine how diverse social networks influence migrant workers’ social participation and the underlying mechanisms. The key findings are as follows: First, diverse social networks significantly enhance migrant workers’ engagement in various social activities, including cultural, skill-based, sports, and philanthropic endeavors. Second, this influence is not merely a result of peer effects; rather, these networks boost social trust, thereby facilitating greater participation. Third, the impact of diverse social networks varies among different demographic and contextual groups, with stronger effects observed among younger migrants (particularly those born post-1990), those with higher education levels, those in well-serviced housing, and those moving to urban areas.

These findings offer valuable insights into social integration processes and inform policy design for fostering inclusive urbanization. By identifying diverse social networks as crucial to social participation, the study underscores the importance of fostering varied interpersonal connections to enhance migrant workers’ urban integration.

This study has three main limitations. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data precludes observation of long-term changes in migrant workers’ social integration and is vulnerable to self-selection into cities. Access to longitudinal migrant surveys combined with panel-data techniques to address self-selection and control for unobservable factors would strengthen future findings. Second, measuring peer effects and social trust is inherently difficult; the former complicates mechanism identification, while self-reported trust may introduce measurement error and bias. Third, the results are confined to Chinese migrant workers; their validity for other countries and institutional contexts remains uncertain. Extending the analysis to migrant populations across diverse cultural and institutional settings would enhance the study’s external validity.
