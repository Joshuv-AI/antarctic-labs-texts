The greasy finger region of DTMUV NS1 plays an essential role in NS1 secretion and viral proliferation
Authors: Hantai Tan, Senzhao Zhang, Zhen Wu, Yu He, Tao Wang, Wangyang Tan, Xuedan Tang, Wei Li, Mingshu Wang, Renyong Jia, Dekang Zhu, Mafeng Liu, Xinxin Zhao, Qiao Yang, Ying Wu, Shaqiu Zhang, Juan Huang, Xumin Ou, Di Sun, Bin Tian, Anchun Cheng, Shun Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractDuck Tembusu virus (DTMUV) of the Orthoflavivirus genus poses a significant threat to waterfowl aquaculture. Nonstructural protein 1 (NS1), a multifunctional glycoprotein, exists in various oligomeric forms and performs diverse functions. The greasy finger (GF) region within NS1 of other flaviviruses has been shown to be a crucial component of the hydrophobic protrusion aiding in anchoring NS1 to the endoplasmic reticulum (ER). However, detailed studies on the role of the GF region in viral proliferation in vitro and the biological properties of NS1 remain scarce. A series of recombinant DTMUV (rDTMUV) with mutations in the GF region, including NS1-F158A, G159A, F160A, G161A, V162A, L163A, F160R, multipoint mutations (GF-4M), or regional deletions (ΔGF), were rescued using a DNA-based reverse genetics system. Only 5 rDTMUV variants (G159A, F160A, G161A, V162A, and L163A) could be rescued successfully, and these mutations were found to impair replication, reduce virulence, and decrease plaque size, as shown by growth kinetics, duck embryo virulence, and plaque assays, respectively. Upon examining NS1 expression by western blot, we discovered that secreted NS1 (sNS1) presented in large quantities in the supernatant of cells infected with rDTMUV-NS1-G159A, whereas intracellular NS1 was less abundant. These mutations also impacted the primary forms and secretion rates of NS1 in cases of overexpression by western blot and indirect ELISA. Exception for F160A and G161A, which showed decreased secretion rates, all other mutations increased sNS1 expression, with the most pronounced increase observed in F158A and ΔGF, and rDTMUV with these mutations can't be rescued. Co-localization studies of NS1 with the ER demonstrated that the ΔGF mutation attenuated NS1 anchoring to the ER, thereby inhibiting its intracellular residence and promoting secretion. Although these effects vary between flaviviruses, our data reveal that the GF region of NS1 is crucial for viral proliferation and NS1 secretion.Key words: Duck Tembusu virus, nonstructural protein 1, greasy finger, viral proliferation, NS1 secretion

INTRODUCTION

Tembusu virus (TMUV) was first identified in 1955 in Culex tritaeniorhynchus mosquitoes in Malaysia (Platt et al., 1975). Over the past decade, the egg drop syndrome caused by Duck Tembusu virus (DTMUV) has emerged as a significant avian health problem in China. DTMUV infections result in weight loss and decreased egg production in laying ducks, along with paralysis, depression, and other neurological symptoms in the later stages of the disease, thereby posing a serious threat to waterfowl aquaculture (Zhang et al., 2017). Although human infection with DTMUV has not been reported, DTMUV particles and neutralizing antibodies have been detected in the blood of duck farm workers, indicating a potential public health risk (Mao et al., 2022).

DTMUV is part of the Orthoflavivirus genus within the Flaviviridae family, which includes viruses that cause severe diseases in humans, such as dengue virus (DENV), Japanese encephalitis virus (JEV), West Nile virus (WNV), yellow fever virus (YFV), and Zika virus (ZIKV) (Postler et al., 2023). DTMUV is a single-stranded positive-sense RNA virus with an open reading frame encoding a polyprotein. This polyprotein is cleaved by host signal peptidase and viral serine protease, producing 3 structural proteins and 7 nonstructural proteins. Nonstructural protein 1 (NS1) is a highly conserved secreted glycoprotein among orthoflaviviruses, with a molecular weight of approximately 46 kDa, depending on its glycosylation status (Zhang et al., 2021). The flavivirus NS1 monomer structure comprises 3 domains: the β-roll, wing, and β-ladder, each containing a connector subdomain (Biering et al., 2021). NS1 exists in various conformational forms during the viral life cycle, including dimers, tetramers, and hexamers (Alvin Chew et al., 2024; Pan et al., 2024). The NS1 dimer participates in viral replication by interacting with other viral proteins to form replication complexes (Lindenbach and Rice, 1999; Muller and Young, 2013). Soluble NS1 hexamers, also known as secreted NS1 (sNS1), form from oligomers through further glycosylation in the Golgi apparatus and are secreted into the extracellular compartment. sNS1 has been shown to play roles in viral particle formation and immune system evasion (Gutsche et al., 2011; Zhang et al., 2023).

During translation, the newly synthesized NS1 is translocated into the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) lumen via a signal peptide encoded by the last 24 amino acids of the E protein (Parrish et al., 1991; Xu et al., 2016). The NS1 dimer is crucial for reorganizing ER structure and facilitates viral replication by inserting its hydrophobic region into the ER membrane, forming a replication-like structure (Biering et al., 2021; Ci et al., 2020; den Boon and Ahlquist, 2010). Coarse-grained models and molecular dynamics simulations have confirmed that differences in virulence between the 2 ZIKV strains arise from distinct protein-membrane interactions of the NS1 dimer, attributed to variations in amino acid sequences (Poveda Cuevas et al., 2023; Song et al., 2016). Three hydrophobic regions on the membrane-associated face—β-roll, Greasy Finger (GF), and wing flexible loop—have been shown to insert into lipid membranes (Ci et al., 2020). Notably, the first 8 amino acids in the β-roll significantly influence NS1’s interaction with liposomes. Previous studies have shown that mutations at amino acids 159 to 162 in the GF region of DENV2 NS1 are detrimental to viral replication (Biering et al., 2021). Specifically, substituting phenylalanine at position 160 (F160D) and valine at position 162 (F162D) with the charged amino acid aspartic acid, or double substitutions of G159A and F160A, resulted in viral lethality undetectable by plaque assay. In contrast, a single substitution of F160A impaired viral viability and RNA synthesis. It has also been shown that point mutations (F160A/F163A) in the hydrophobic regions of ZIKV NS1 abolish the luciferase activity of ZIKV replicons (Ci et al., 2020). Given the limited studies on the GF region and its conserved nature across various orthoflaviviruses, exploring the effects of mutations in the GF region of DTMUV NS1 on viral and NS1 functions will help elucidate their roles and mechanisms during infections, providing a theoretical basis for future orthoflavivirus research.

Considering the amino acid properties affecting NS1 dimer-membrane interactions, we expanded our study of the GF region by introducing point mutations (F158A, G159A, F160A, G161A, V162A, L163A, and F160R), multipoint mutations (GF-4M), or regional deletions (ΔGF) at positions 158-163 of DTMUV NS1. These mutant viruses were rescued using a reverse genetics system. Except for some lethal mutations that could not be recovered, we conducted growth kinetic studies, duck embryo virulence assays, and plaque assays on the successfully rescued viruses. We also examined the secretion properties, dimerization properties, thermal stability, and co-localization with the ER of NS1 under infection or overexpression, using western blot, indirect ELISA, and indirect immunofluorescence assay (IFA).

In summary, our study reveals the critical role of the GF region in viral proliferation and NS1 secretion, providing new insights into the functions and pathogenic mechanisms of various orthoflavivirus NS1 and offering new strategies for the prevention of flavivirus-associated diseases.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Cells and Virus Templates

The baby hamster kidney fibroblast (BHK-21) cell line was cultured in Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM) (Gibco, Shanghai, China), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco, NY). Duck embryonic fibroblast (DEF) cells were isolated from 8-day-old duck embryos and cultured in DMEM with 10% FBS. All cells were incubated at 37°C in 5% CO2.

Plasmid Construction and Recombinant Virus Rescue

The recombinant plasmid was constructed using the backbone of the infectious clone pACNR-CQW1-Intron and the subgenomic replicon pACYC-CQW1, with several modifications (Guo et al., 2020; He et al., 2019). Briefly, 9 recombinant infectious clones and subgenomic replicons were constructed by introducing sequential point mutations in amino acids 158-163, multiple point deletions, or complete deletions in the GF region of DTMUV NS1. In addition, pCAGGS vectors were used to construct a series of recombinant DTMUV NS1 plasmids containing the Flag-tag. During the above construction, plasmid linearization was performed using the restriction enzymes XhoI and PmlI (New England Biolabs, Beijing, China), and the recombinant plasmids were then transformed into TOP10 or SURE chemically competent Escherichia coli. The primer sequences for overlapping PCR.

To rescue recombinant DTMUV (rDTMUV), BHK-21 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and transfected with recombinant infectious clones using Hieff Trans Liposomal Transfection Reagent (Yeasen Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), according to the manufacturer's instructions. When a clear cytopathic effect (CPE) appeared, the supernatant of the F0 virus was harvested and used to re-infect fresh BHK-21 cells, allowing the collection of the supernatant of the F1 virus for experiments. Recombinant viruses or subgenomic replicons are named by the item followed by the modification, e.g., rDTMUV-NS1-ΔGF, replicon-NS1-ΔGF.

Western Blot

BHK-21 cells, infected or transfected with recombinant plasmids, were harvested at specified time points according to experimental requirements. The supernatant was centrifuged at 40,000 rpm for 10 min, and the cells were lysed using Pierce IP Lysis Buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Shanghai, China). Protein samples from the supernatant or cell lysates were separated by 10% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred onto polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes1620177, Bio-Rad) using semi-dry transfer. The monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) against DTMUV NS1, mouse anti-Flag, and mouse anti-GAPDH (Abcam, Shanghai, China) were used as primary antibodies. Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Abcam, Shanghai, China) was used as the secondary antibody. Protein bands were detected using enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) solution (Bio-Rad).

Indirect ELISA

Indirect ELISA was used to quantify the amount of NS1 secreted in the supernatant. Ninety-six-well microtiter plates were coated with supernatants from cells infected with different rDTMUV strains in 0.1 M carbonate buffer (pH 9.6) at 4°C overnight, followed by blocking with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS at 37°C for 2 h. The mAb8E6 from hybridoma supernatant was added to each well and incubated at 37°C for 1.5 h. The secondary antibody, HRP-conjugated goat anti-mouse IgG (Abcam, Shanghai, China), was then applied, and the samples were incubated for 1 hour at 37°C. The microtiter plates were washed 3 times with PBS supplemented with 0.01% Tween-20 (PBST) after each incubation step. 100 μl of the substrate 3,3′,5,5′-tetramethylbenzidine (TMB) (TIANGEN, Beijing, China) was added to each well and incubated at room temperature for 15 min in the dark. The reaction was stopped with 2 M sulfuric acid, and the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Bio-Rad). An OD450 value greater than 2.1 times that of the negative control was considered positive.

Indirect Immunofluorescence Assay

BHK-21 cells were seeded in 12-well plates and infected with either parental or rDTMUV at 300 TCID50. At 60 h postinfection (hpi), the cells were washed with PBS, fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 1 hour at 4°C, permeabilized with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBS at 4°C for 1 h, and blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in PBS for 1 hour at 37°C. Thereafter, the cells were treated with mAb 8E6 as the primary antibody at 4°C overnight. The cells were then incubated with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled goat anti-mouse IgG (Beyotime Biotech, Beijing, China) at a 1:1000 dilution for 1 hour at 37°C and counterstained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Solarbio, China) for 15 min at room temperature. Finally, the cells were observed and photographed using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Tokyo, Japan).

Growth Curve

To evaluate the proliferation properties of rDTMUV in vitro, BHK-21 and DEF cells were cultured in 24-well plates. When the cell density reached 70-90%, the culture supernatant was removed, and the cells were infected with different rDTMUV strains at 300 TCID50, followed by incubation at 37°C for 2 h. After incubation, the viral supernatant was removed, and DMEM containing 2% FBS was added to maintain cell growth. The supernatant was harvested at specified time points, and the viral titer was determined using the median tissue culture infectious dose (TCID50) method, calculated by the Karber method as previously reported (Zhang et al., 2021).

Duck Embryo Virulence

Pekin duck embryos were obtained from the Waterfowl Breeding Center at Sichuan Agricultural University. Nine-day-old embryos were divided into 6 experimental groups and 1 control group (n = 8 per group). The experimental groups were inoculated with 500 TCID50 of either parental or rDTMUV via the allantoic cavity using 100 µL, while the control group received DMEM. All embryos were incubated at 37°C in a Chengdu Beili HX-176 avian incubator for 7 d, with daily monitoring and recording of embryonic mortality.

Plaque Assay

Recombinant viral supernatants containing 10 TCID50 were added to BHK-21 cells cultured in 12-well plates. After a 2-hour incubation period at 37°C and 5% CO2, the viral inoculum was removed, and a 1% methylcellulose overlay was applied. Subsequently, the cells were maintained at 37°C for 6 d. Upon the formation of visible plaques, the residual medium was aspirated, and the cells were washed with PBS. Following this, the cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde at room temperature for 15 min and stained with 1% crystal violet. The area of multiple plaques was then quantified using ImageJ software and subjected to statistical analysis.

Subcellular Localization of NS1 to ER

To determine the subcellular localization of NS1 mutants in the ER, we used a red fluorescent marker from the pDS Red2-ER plasmid and transfected various NS1 mutant plasmids into BHK-21 cells. Twenty-four hours post-transfection, the supernatant was removed, and the same experimental procedures as described above in the IFA were followed. Finally, we used laser fluorescence scanning (Olympus VS200, Beijing, China) to visualize the signals and assessed co-localization by examining 5 randomly selected fields for each group. Co-localization was quantified using the Colocalization Finder in ImageJ and represented as a Pearson correlation coefficient.

Temperature Shift Experiments

To verify the effect of temperature shifts on NS1 stability, we placed whole cell lysates and supernatants, following overexpression of various NS1, in incubators set at 4°C or 50°C. Samples were collected at 24-hour intervals over 3 time points. The effect of temperature on NS1 stability was determined by assessing protein degradation via western blot, using samples preserved at -80°C as a control.

Statistical Analysis

All data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism 9.0 (La Jolla, CA). All experiments were performed in triplicate across 3 independent trials.

RESULTS

Screening of Monoclonal Antibody

The GF region, located between the wing domain and the β-ladder domain of NS1, is more conserved in the Orthoflavivirus genus (Figures 1A and 1B). We constructed a series of eukaryotic expression plasmids with Flag-tagged mutations in the NS1 GF region. To test the effects of different mutations on the virus and NS1, we examined the ability of various mAbs to recognize mutant NS1 through western blot analysis (Figure 1C). We found that the 3 previously screened mAbs (1H11, 3D5, and 5C11), which recognize the amino acids 156EDFGFGVL163 in the GF region, exhibited varying degrees of inability to recognize mutant NS1 (Tan et al., 2023). The 3 mAbs did not recognize the same key amino acids in this region: amino acids 158-161 are critical for mAb 1H11 recognition, while mAb 3D5 requires amino acids 160-161, and mAb 5C11 requires amino acids 158, 161, and 163. In addition, mAb 8E6, which recognizes amino acids 243IPKTLGGP250, and mAb 10F12, which recognizes amino acids 267PWDEK271, exhibited the same recognition of both NS1 mutant strains as the anti-Flag antibody. Therefore, mAbs 8E6 and 10F12 were chosen for subsequent experiments.

The Greasy Finger Region of NS1 is Necessary for Viral Replication

To verify the effect of the GF region on rDTMUV rescue, we constructed 6 infectious clones with a single point mutation to alanine (F158A, G159A, F160A, G161A, V162A, and L163A) sequentially from amino acids 158-163 of NS1 using the reverse genetics-based system of infectious clones, pACNR-CQW1-Intron. (Guo et al., 2020). Additionally, we constructed infectious clones with a 4-point mutation to alanine (GF-4M), a complete GF region deletion (ΔGF), and an arginine substitution at amino acid 160 (F160R) (Figure 2A). We evaluated virus rescue through observation of NS1 fluorescence of F0 generation recombinant viruses and western blot detection of viral proteins E and NS1 (Figure 2, Figure 2). The results showed that the F158A, GF-4M, ΔGF, and F160R mutations all rendered the virus lethal (Figure 2E).

(C) Expression of E and NS1 proteins during recombinant F0 virus rescue. (D) Subgenomic replicons containing identical mutation types were used to assess viral replication. Additionally, we constructed subgenomic replicons based on the pACYC-CQW1 replicon with the same mutation types to assess viral replication (He et al., 2019). The results indicated that all nonrescuable viruses had mutations that impaired replication, demonstrating that mutations in the GF region impair viral replication and consequently prevent the rescue of rDTMUV (Figure 2, Figure 2).

Mutations in the Greasy Finger Region Negatively Affect the Proliferation and Virulence Characteristics of DTMUV In Vitro

We investigated the in vitro growth kinetics of rDTMUV by analyzing its growth curves in various cell lines. The experimental results demonstrated that the viral titer of rDTMUV with mutations in the GF region decreased during proliferation in BHK-21 cells (Figure 3A). At each time point of infection, the viral titer of the wild-type virus was consistently higher than that of the mutated rDTMUV. The mutated rDTMUV showed a 3-fold to 316-fold decrease in viral titers at 48 hpi. Although the proliferation kinetics of all rDTMUV strains in DEF cells were also reduced compared to parental viruses, the differences were not as pronounced as those observed in BHK-21 cells (Figure 3B).

(C) Mortality rates of 9-day-old duck embryos inoculated with 500 TCID50 doses of different mutant rDTMUV. (D) Area measurements of plaques in BHK-21 cells at 10 TCID50 doses of different mutant rDTMUV.

We assessed the virulence of rDTMUV in 9-day-old duck embryos. At a virus dose of 500 TCID50 per embryo, the parental virus caused embryo death starting at 3 days postinfection (dpi), with complete mortality by 4 dpi. In contrast, rDTMUV with mutations in the GF region exhibited a slower mortality trend, with all embryos succumbing by 7 dpi. The survival curves of duck embryos inoculated with rDTMUV-NS1-G159A and rDTMUV-NS1-L163A were statistically significantly different from those inoculated with the parental virus (Figure 3C). Finally, we evaluated the plaque morphology of rDTMUV. The results showed that all 5 recombinant viruses containing point mutations in the GF region produced smaller plaques in BHK-21 cells compared to the parental virus (Figure 3D).

In summary, mutations in the GF region exhibited varying degrees of negative effects on the growth kinetics, virulence in duck embryos, and plaque morphology of rDTMUV.

Mutations in the Greasy Finger Region Affect the Forms of NS1 During Infection

Having established that these mutations impact the virus properties in vitro, we next aimed to investigate their effects on NS1 expression. The expression of intracellular NS1 was observed via an IFA assay at 48 hpi in BHK-21 cells. Five fields of view were randomly selected under a light microscope for quantitative analysis of the fluorescence area. The results showed that cells infected with the wild-type virus exhibited nearly complete green fluorescence, whereas cells infected with the mutated rDTMUV displayed a reduced fluorescence area. Among the 5 recombinant viruses, V162A had the least impact on reducing the fluorescence area, followed by G159A and L163A mutations. Mutations at positions F160A and G161A had the greatest effect on intracellular NS1 expression (Figure 4A).

We examined the expression of NS1 in infected cells using western blot. Consistent with the IFA results, intracellular NS1 expression in mutant rDTMUV-infected cells was lower at 24 and 48 hpi compared to wild-type virus, reaching wild-type levels only at 60 or 72 hpi (Figure 4B). Secretory NS1 expression was also detected in the culture supernatant. Unlike intracellular NS1, detectable as early as 24 hpi, sNS1 production requires 24-48 h postinfection. Interestingly, in BHK-21 cells infected with rDTMUV-NS1-G159A, which had low intracellular NS1 expression at 48 hpi, more sNS1 was present in the supernatants compared to wild-type virus (Figure 4C). This higher level of secreted NS1 from G159A was consistently observed in subsequent infection periods. Conversely, rDTMUV-NS1-F160A, G161A, and L163A all showed reduced expression of intracellular and secreted NS1 (Figure 4, Figure 4). Among these, the F161A mutation showed the most pronounced reduction, with less sNS1 than the F160A mutation, despite both having similar levels of intracellular expression at 72 hpi. In contrast, the V162A mutation had a weaker impact on NS1 expression, detectable at early time pointes, and its NS1 expression ranked second only to the G159A mutation in subsequent infections (Figure 4, Figure 4). These results suggest that mutations in the GF region may affect NS1 forms during infection.

Mutations in the Greasy Finger Region Affect NS1 Secretion Rate by Influencing the Capacity of Membrane-Binding

To verify whether the mutation affects NS1 dimerization, and consequently its conformation and secretion rate, we examined NS1 dimerization in overexpressed samples without heat treatment using western blot. Since NS1 dimerization is heat-sensitive and prone to depolymerization, this approach allowed us to observe potential changes in dimerization under these conditions (Hall et al., 1999). We found that mutations in the GF region did not affect NS1 dimerization formation but did influence both intracellular and extracellular NS1 expression (Figure 5A). Deletion of the GF region resulted in reduced intracellular NS1 expression and increased NS1 secretion into the supernatant compared to the wild type. This phenomenon was confirmed using both anti-Flag and mAb of NS1, yielding consistent results (Figure 5A).

(A) Expression of intracellular NS1 and sNS1 was detected via western blot under overexpression conditions, and the associated expression of NS1 dimers was also observed. The NS1 secretion rate was calculated as the ratio of sNS1 to total NS1. All other mutant NS1 secretion rates were expressed relative to the wild-type. (B) Quantification of sNS1 in cell culture supernatants 48 h post-transfection was performed via indirect ELISA. An OD450 value exceeding 2.1 times that of the negative controls was considered a positive result. (C) The degradation of NS1 in supernatants and whole cell lysates post-transfection under treatment at 4°C or 50°C was examined over time by western blot to assess changes in the thermal stability of NS1 due to mutation. (D) The intracellular co-localization of NS1 with the ER was detected by IFA, and the degree of co-localization was quantified using the Pearson correlation coefficient. The ER was visualized using a pDS Red2-ER plasmid-derived red fluorescent label.

We quantified the impact of various mutations on NS1 secretion rate by calculating the percentage of secreted NS1 relative to the total NS1. The results indicated that, under overexpression conditions, both ΔGF and F158A mutations significantly increased NS1 secretion compared to the wild type. Notably, these 2 mutations were the only ones that could not be rescued from the recombinant virus. In other mutation groups, G159A, V162A, and L163A slightly increased NS1 secretion, whereas F160A and G161A mutations decreased NS1 secretion; however, none of these changes were significantly different from the wild-type (Figure 5A). Secreted NS1 in overexpressed supernatants was also detected using indirect ELISA, showing results consistent with western blot. Mutants F158A, G159A, V162A, L163A, and ΔGF all increased NS1 secretion, with ΔGF being 2.8-fold higher than the wild type. Mutants F160A and G161A showed lower levels of secreted NS1, with G161A not even exceeding the positivity threshold (Figure 5B).

To exclude quantification errors due to altered thermal stability of NS1 caused by mutations, we examined the protein expression of intracellular and secreted NS1 after treatment at 4°C or 50°C. The results showed that both intracellular and secreted NS1, whether at 4°C or 50°C, exhibited no significant degradation within 24, 48, or 72 h of treatment. This demonstrates that the mutations did not affect the thermal stability of NS1, thereby proving that the mutation in the GF region truly affects the secretion rate of NS1 (Figure 5C).

Finally, we examined the intracellular co-localization of NS1 with the ER using IFA. Both wild-type and mutant NS1 groups exhibited co-localization with the ER under fluorescence microscopy. Quantitative analysis of co-localization, based on 5 randomly selected fields of view, revealed that the Pearson correlation coefficient was lower in the F158A and ΔGF groups compared to the wild-type (Figure 5D). These groups also exhibited fewer regions of NS1 co-localization with the ER. In contrast, no significant difference in co-localization was observed in the other mutation groups compared to the wild type. In summary, mutations in the GF region of NS1 may attenuate its membrane-binding capacity, thereby altering its secretion rate.

DISCUSSION

DTMUV can cause severe egg drop syndrome in laying ducks and poses a risk of crossing species barriers, necessitating vigilance regarding its potential threat to humans (Wu et al., 2022). NS1 is the first nonstructural protein of DTMUV, playing a crucial role in early viral replication, immune evasion, and pathogenesis. According to other antiviral studies on flaviviruses, the secretory nature of NS1 distinguishes it from other nonstructural proteins, allowing sNS1 to be detected in the blood of patients at an early stage of infection, before clinical symptoms appear (Watterson et al., 2016; Wessel et al., 2021a). Furthermore, compared to mAbs against structural proteins, which may enhance antibody-dependent effects, anti-NS1 mAbs provide protection without this drawback in various animal viral challenge assays (Tien et al., 2022; Wessel et al., 2021b; Yu et al., 2021). Consequently, NS1 has emerged as a significant target protein for the diagnosis of flaviviruses and the development of antiviral drugs (Modhiran et al., 2021).

In our previous study on DTMUV NS1, we identified 3 mAbs that recognize amino acids 156-163 in NS1 (Tan et al., 2023). These mAbs demonstrated good cross-reactivity with NS1 from other orthoflaviviruses, including DENV, JEV, WNV, and ZIKV. Notably, the recognition epitopes of these mAbs unexpectedly include the GF region of NS1, which has been previously associated with membrane-bound functions. Previous studies on the GF region of DENV2 NS1 have demonstrated that mutations at amino acids 159-162 impair viral replication, with the F160A mutation being the most significant (Biering et al., 2021). Subsequently, using the F160D mutation as a replication-deficient NS1 model, researchers verified that NS1 plays a dual role in viral replication and viral particle formation by performing a trans-complementation experiment with the I273H mutation as a secretion-deficient NS1 (Tamura et al., 2022). Another study on ZIKV showed that the GF region, an important hydrophobic region in NS1, is involved in the remodeling of the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) (Ci et al., 2023; Ci et al, 2020). However, the attenuation of ER remodeling due to the deletion of the GF region differs from the previous conclusion that the 159-162 mutation in DENV2 does not affect NS1-lipid interactions. The former focused on the function of the entire GF region or a few residues but lacked specifically detailed studies related to this region. In contrast, the latter study targeted only a single residue or a double mutant residue without examining the entire GF region, which may account for the observed differences.

Our study conducted detailed experiments on the GF region in DTMUV NS1, involving point mutations and overall deletions, to investigate their effects on the virus and NS1. First, we screened 2 mAbs, 8E6 and 10F12, for subsequent experiments using western blot and found that they had equal recognition abilities for NS1 mutations in the GF region (Figure 1C). Additionally, 3 previously identified mAbs (1H11, 3D5, and 5C11) that recognize the GF region exhibited a certain loss of recognition and varied requirements for key amino acids, further confirming the differences among these antibodies (Figure 1C). We obtained rDTMUV by constructing a series of mutant infectious clones (Figure 2A). By observing cytopathic effects (CPE) and detecting viral proteins, we found that 4 mutations (F158A, F160R, GF-4M, and ΔGF) were unable to rescue the recombinant virus. These mutations were subsequently confirmed to impair viral replication in subgenomic replicon assays (Figures 2B, 2C, and 2E). In the GF-4M mutation, all 4 amino acids (F158, F160, V162, and L163) are hydrophobic. The mutation of these amino acids directly disrupts the hydrophobic property of the GF region, thus impairing the function of NS1, which may explain why the virus cannot be rescued. Additionally, we replaced the phenylalanine at position 160 with either alanine (F160A) or positively charged arginine (F160R), but only the former was able to rescue the virus. A previous study on DENV2 NS1 confirmed that mutating the phenylalanine to negatively charged aspartic acid (F160D) also failed to rescue the virus, whereas F160A did (Biering et al., 2021). Another study showed that using F160D as a replication-defective NS1 in JEV viruses also impaired viral replication (Tamura et al., 2022). These results, combined with our experimental findings, confirm that the change in amino acid charge at position 160 affects the function of the GF region. These amino acid sequence differences lead to alterations in electrostatic properties and hydrophobicity, both of which affect the membrane-binding function of NS1.

By examining the growth kinetics of mutant rDTMUV, we found that the growth curves of all point mutant viruses were somewhat reduced compared to the wild-type DTMUV (Figure 3, Figure 3). Duck embryo virulence and plaque assays demonstrated that these mutations weakened virus virulence and reduced the plaque area (Figure 3, Figure 3). The G159A mutation had the greatest effect on duck embryo virulence, delaying embryo death. However, these weakening effects were not as strong as we expected. Specifically, the F160A mutation did not seriously affect the replication of DTMUV, indicating that the effects of the mutated GF region may vary among different orthoflaviviruses. By examining the expression of intracellular NS1 and sNS1 during infection, we discovered that G159A was detected in large quantities in the extracellular form of sNS1, despite very low intracellular expression (Figure 4, Figure 4). We also examined the expression of more mutant NS1 under conditions of overexpression and found that although deletion or mutation of the GF region did not affect NS1 dimerization and protein thermal stability, it greatly affected the major form and secretion rate of NS1 (Figure 5, Figure 5). Notably, the ΔGF and F158A mutations, which were unable to rescue the virus, had significantly more sNS1 than intracellular NS1, with a greatly enhanced secretion rate (Figure 5, Figure 5). Co-localization experiments confirmed that these mutations also reduced the co-localization area of NS1 with the ER (Figure 5D). These results suggest that the deletion of the GF region promotes the secretion of NS1 by disrupting its function to anchor to the ER. Mutations at other amino acid positions, such as G159A, V162A, and L163A, also slightly increased the secretion rate, with the increase due to GA being more pronounced during infection (Figure 4C). We also observed that the F160A and G161A mutations decreased the secretion function of NS1. However, the exact reasons and mechanisms behind this remain to be explored, and whether the differences in secretion rates are consistent across flaviviruses has yet to be verified.

We have compiled the effects of these mutations on DTMUV and NS1 in the form of tables (Figure 6). Overall, mutations in the GF region affect DTMUV replication, virulence attenuation, and the secretion of NS1. The infection mechanisms of different flaviviruses vary, thus the effects may differ depending on the specific flavivirus pathogen. However, this study of the GF region provides new insights into the differences in NS1 function and pathogenesis among flaviviruses, potentially contributing to future research and the development of therapeutics.
