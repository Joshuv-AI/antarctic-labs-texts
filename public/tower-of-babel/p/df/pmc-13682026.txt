Drug-drug interaction between ticagrelor and fazamorexant: pharmacokinetic alterations in healthy subjects from a phase I, open-label, fixed-sequence study
Authors: Xingxing Huang, Yuzhu Chen, Lu Jin, Yanyan Kong, Fengxia Bao, Ruwei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeFazamorexant, a dual orexin receptor antagonist (DORA) targeting insomnia, demonstrated in vitro metabolism via CYP3A4. Thus, the effects of CYP3A4 inhibitors on the pharmacokinetics, safety, and tolerability of fazamorexant were investigated.MethodsIn this single-center, open-label, non-randomized, fixed-sequence study, 24 healthy subjects received a single oral dose of fazamorexant (20 mg) alone on D1, followed by a 6-day regimen of the CYP3A4 weak inhibitor ticagrelor (90 mg twice daily, Days 2 to 7) to reach steady state, with a second dose of fazamorexant co-administered on Day 6. Area under the plasma concentration–time curve from time zero to infinity (AUC0–inf), maximum plasma concentration (Cmax), time to Cmax (Tmax), and other relevant pharmacokinetic parameters were derived from plasma concentrations of fazamorexant collected at prespecified time points. Adverse events (AEs) were recorded.ResultsCYP3A4 weak inhibition by ticagrelor (90 mg, twice daily) increased the Cmax of fazamorexant by approximately 14% (90% confidence interval (CI): 100.92-129.07), whereas AUC0−t (90% CI: 86.44-125.58), AUC0− inf (90% CI: 85.60-124.53) and Tmax (P = 0.665) remained essentially unchanged. Fazamorexant, whether given alone or in combination with ticagrelor, was generally safe and well tolerated.ConclusionOverall, co-administration of CYP3A4 weak inhibitors with fazamorexant is not expected to result in clinically significant drug interactions, and no dosage adjustment is required when these agents are used concomitantly.Trial registrationThis trial was registered on the Drug Clinical Trial Registration and Information Disclosure Platform (registration number CTR20242889, accessible at https://www.chinadrugtrials.org.cn/clinicaltrials.searchlist.dhtml?keywords=CTR20242889) on 5 August 2024, and on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06671470, accessible at https://clinicaltrials.gov/ct2/show/NCT06671470) on 31 October 2024. Introduction

Insomnia, characterized by persistent difficulty in falling asleep, maintaining sleep, or non-restorative sleep accompanied by daytime dysfunction (e.g., fatigue, impaired cognition, mood disturbances) [1], has emerged as a critical global public health challenge. A recent review synthesizing over 50 epidemiological studies reported that the prevalence of insomnia symptoms in the general population ranges from 30% to 48% [2–4].

Traditional pharmacotherapies, including benzodiazepines (e.g., lorazepam) and non-benzodiazepines (e.g., zolpidem), remain first-line treatments despite well-documented limitations. Meta-analyses demonstrate that zolpidem is associated with a 1.82-fold increased odds of drowsiness (95% confidence interval: 1.25–2.65), necessitating cautious prescribing due to daytime impairment risks [5]. Chronic benzodiazepine use poses additional concerns, with a 70% probability of developing dependence [6] and dose-dependent reductions in hippocampal, amygdala, and thalamic volumes [7], raising concerns regarding long-term cognitive safety. These neurostructural changes further underscore the imperative to develop safer and more effective alternatives for chronic insomnia management.

In recent years, dual orexin receptor antagonists (DORAs) have emerged as a breakthrough class in insomnia management. By selectively blocking orexin OX1R and OX2R, which regulate the wake-sleep cycle, DORAs promote sleep while preserving natural sleep architecture, minimizing the adverse effects associated with traditional sedatives [8]. Globally, four orexin receptor antagonists have been approved: suvorexant (Belsomra®, Merck, 2014), lemborexant (Dayvigo®, Eisai, 2019), daridorexant (Quviviq®, Idorsia, 2022), and vornorexant (Vorzzz®, Taisho, 2025), demonstrating superior efficacy in clinical trials with favorable pharmacokinetic profiles. In 2025, the updated Chinese Insomnia Diagnosis and Treatment Guidelines elevated DORAs to first-line therapy for insomnia, citing their favorable benefit-risk profile [9]. Among these, fazamorexant, a novel DORA developed by Shanghai Haiyan Pharmaceutical, has shown promising preclinical and clinical results. Phase I studies have characterized its pharmacokinetics (PK) as linear, with rapid absorption (median Tmax of 0.625–1.25 h) and a short elimination half-life (arithmetic mean t1/2 of 1.9–3.7 h) [10]. This pharmacokinetic profile supports once-daily dosing while minimizing the risk of next-day residual effects, aligning with the guidelines emphasizing the minimizing daytime impairment.

However, the clinical utility of fazamorexant may be constrained by drug-drug interactions (DDIs), particularly with cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4) inhibitors. CYP3A4 is the primary metabolic enzyme for fazamorexant, as confirmed by in vitro metabolic profiling (unpublished data). Inhibition of CYP3A4 by concomitant medications can increase fazamorexant’s exposure, potentially elevating the risk of adverse effects.

Ticagrelor, a reversible P2Y12 antagonist, acts as a widely used antiplatelet agent for acute coronary syndrome (ACS). Laboratory investigations utilizing human hepatic microsomal preparations demonstrate that ticagrelor undergoes CYP3A-mediated metabolism while exhibiting weak inhibitory activity against this enzyme system [11]. Notably, up to 60% of ACS patients experience comorbid insomnia [12], with difficulty falling asleep and nocturnal awakenings being the most common symptoms. This high comorbidity rate underscores the likelihood of co-prescription of fazamorexant and ticagrelor in clinical practice.

To address this gap, we designed a DDI study to evaluate the effects of ticagrelor (a CYP3A4 weak inhibitor) on the PK and safety of fazamorexant. The results of this study will provide evidence-based guidance for clinicians to optimize dosing regimens, minimize DDI risks, and ensure the safe and effective use of fazamorexant in patients with comorbid insomnia and ACS.

Methods

Study designs

The research was performed at Shulan (Hangzhou) Hospital in strict adherence to the principles of Good Clinical Practice and the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethical approval was secured from the institutional ethics committee prior to the initiation of the study. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants before they participated in any study-related procedures.

This was a single-center, open-label, non-randomized, fixed-sequence study, enrolled 24 healthy Chinese volunteers and comprised three phases: screening, treatment, and safety follow-up. After screening, eligible subjects were admitted to the Phase I clinical trial ward on the day before dosing, where they received standardized daily management with no food or beverages permitted other than the provided standardized meals. Each subject received a single oral dose of 20 mg fazamorexant under fasting conditions alone on Day 1 (period 1) and on Day 6 of a 6-day multiple-dose regimen of 90 mg ticagrelor twice daily from Day 2 to Day 7 (period 2), i.e., at steady-state exposure of ticagrelor (Fig. 1). No drinking was allowed 1 h prior to or 1 h post-dosing, and no food intake was permitted within 4 h after-dosing.

Study design

The proposed clinical doses of fazamorexant are 20 mg and 40 mg, with 20 mg being the primary therapeutic starting dose. Although fazamorexant exhibits a favorable safety profile across a wide dose range (2–80 mg) [10], the 20 mg dose was selected for this DDI study to balance the sensitivity of detecting a metabolic interaction with subject safety. Specifically, we aimed to minimize the potential for exaggerated pharmacodynamic effects (e.g., excessive somnolence) that could result from increased exposure due to CYP3A4 inhibition. Furthermore, the pharmacokinetics of fazamorexant are linear within the 5–80 mg range [10], ensuring that the interaction magnitude observed at 20 mg is directly applicable to the 40 mg dose, which represents the maximum clinical dose. This approach is consistent with regulatory guidance for assessing DDI potential at therapeutic doses.

Ticagrelor was administered at its clinical maximum maintenance dose (90 mg twice daily for ACS patients) [13] for 4 days (Days 2–5) to achieve steady-state concentrations, followed by fazamorexant co-administration on Day 6 (D6) during PK sampling. This design aligns with ticagrelor’s half-life (7–9 h) [14] and prior DDI studies of statins [15], ensuring robust evaluation of CYP3A4-mediated interactions while adhering to clinical exposure patterns.

Subjects

Eligible subjects were male and female aged between 18 and 45 years, weighing ≥ 45.0 kg (female) or ≥ 50.0 kg (male), with a body mass index (BMI) between 19.0 and 26.0 kg/m2. They were evaluated as healthy based on a comprehensive physical examination, laboratory tests, 12-lead electrocardiogram, and had no history of severe or chronic diseases (respiratory, circulatory, digestive, or other systems).

Exclusion criteria included any history of allergy to foods or medications, alcohol abuse, or a positive alcohol test. Additionally, subjects were excluded if they had smoked more than 5 cigarettes per day within the 3 months prior to the trial, reported soft drug use (e.g., cannabis) within 3 months, hard drug use (e.g., cocaine/amphetamines) within 1 year, or had a positive pre-screening drug test. Futhermore, subjects were excluded if they had took any prescription drugs or traditional Chinese medicine within 14 days, or CYP3A enzyme inhibitors (e.g., itraconazole, erythromycin, boceprevir), CYP3A enzyme inducers (e.g., rifampicin, carbamazepine) and/or SSRI antidepressant inhibitors (e.g., benzodiazepines, opioids, tricyclic antidepressants) within 28 days. Pregnant or lactating women were excluded.

Pharmacokinetic (PK) assessments

According to the results of previous trials, after single oral administration of fazamorexant (2–80 mg) under fasting state, the median time to peak concentration was about 1 h (0.625–1.25 h) and the mean elimination half-life was about 1.91–3.68 h in each dose group [10]. The whole sampling should have 3 to 5 elimination half-lives, and AUC0−t/AUC0−inf was greater than 80%. Considering that combined CYP3A4 inhibitors may accumulate the exposure of fazamorexant and prolong the metabolic time of the drug, the blood samples were set to collect up to 24 h post-dose for fazamorexant monotherapy and up to 48 h post-dose for co-administration with ticagrelor.

Venous blood samples (3 mL) were collected into tubes containing heparin sodium anticoagulant at the following time points: pre-dose (0 h), 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0, 4.0, 6.0, 8.0, 12.0, 24.0, 36.0 and 48.0 h, notably, the 36.0 h and 48.0 h post-dose samples were exclusively collected in the co-administration period. The plasma was separated by centrifugation at 1500 g for 10 min at 2 ~ 4 °C and then stored at -60 °C until bioassay. Plasma concentrations of fazamorexant were determined using liquid chromatography and tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method.

The separation was performed on a Shimadzu system equipped with a Shim-pack Scepter C18-120 column (1.9 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm). The mobile phase consisted of (A) a mixture of formic acid: 1.0 M ammonium acetate aqueous solution : water = 1:5:1000 (v/v/v) and (B) acetonitrile. A gradient elution was applied at a flow rate of 0.3 mL/min, with the column temperature maintained at 50 °C. The mass spectrometer, SCIEX API-4000, was operated in positive electrospray ionization mode with the multiple reaction monitoring for fazamorexant (m/z 433.2 → 197.2) and internal standard (m/z 436.2 → 200.1). The method was fully validated in accordance with the Chinese Pharmacopoeia (ChP) Guideline for the Validation of Quantitative Analytical Methods for Biological Samples (2020 Edition, Vol. IV, General Chap. 9012). The calibration curve was linear over the range of 8.00 to 9000 ng/mL (r2 > 0.99).

Main PK parameters included area under the concentration–time curve (AUC), AUC from time zero (pre-dose) to the time of the last measurable concentration (AUC0−t), AUC from time zero (pre-dose) to infinity (AUC0−inf), maximum observed plasma concentration (Cmax), time to maximum plasma concentration (Tmax), terminal elimination half-life (t1/2), apparent distribution volume (Vz/F), clearance rate (CL/F), and mean retention time (MRT).

Safety assessments

Safety assessments were performed throughout the study, including collection of adverse events (AEs), laboratory tests, vital signs, electrocardiogram (ECG) records, and physical examinations. The all-treated analysis set, which included all subjects who received at least one dose of treatment, was used for the safety analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was conducted using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute, Inc, Cary, NY). Baseline and demographic data were summarized and tabulated by dosing sequence group. The PK parameters of fazamorexant were summarized using a non-compartmental model in Phoenix WinNonlin (Verison 8.2).

To evaluate the PK differences between monotherapy and co-administration, analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed with the natural log-transformed primary PK parameters (Cmax, AUC0−t, and AUC0−inf) as dependent variables and with the dosing regimen (monotherapy vs. co-administration) as a fixed effect. Geometric mean differences were calculated from the model, followed by back-transformation by exponentiating the results to derive the geometric mean ratio (GMR) and its 90% confidence interval (CI). Nonparametric tests were used to assess differences in Tmax between treatments.

Safety and tolerability data were analyzed using descriptive statistics.

Results

Study population

A total of 27 subjects were enrolled in the trial. Three subjects withdrew prior to dosing due to elevated blood pressure, resulting in their exclusion from all datasets. Of the 24 subjects who received treatment, 1 discontinued prematurely due to an adverse event that precluded further participation, while the remaining 23 completed the trial as planned.

The treated subjects consisted of 21 males and 3 females with a mean age (SD) of 29.33 (6.397) years and a mean BMI (SD) of 22.41 (2.060) kg/m2.

Safety and tolerability

All 24 treated subjects (100%) reported adverse events (AEs). A total of 64 treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs) occurred, of which 55 were drug-related. The most frequently reported TEAEs were drowsiness (95.8%), somnolence (8.3%), headache (8.3%), and dizziness (4.2%), and their incidences were similar between the two dosing periods.

One subject (4.2%) experienced a TEAE leading to study withdrawal (papular urticaria, grade 2), which was assessed as not related to fazamorexant but possibly related to ticagrelor by the investigator. The subject recovered completely after medication treatment. Another subject had elevated blood triglycerides (grade 2) detected during the discharge examination, while all other reported TEAEs were grade 1 and resolved spontaneously without intervention. No serious adverse events or TEAEs of grade 3 or higher occurred during the trial. Table 2 summarized the number and percentage of healthy subjects experiencing TEAEs.

Discussion

Consistent with the metabolic profile of other orexin receptor antagonists [16], in vitro results suggest that CYP3A4 is also the major metabolizing enzyme of fazamorexant (unpublished data). Given that ticagrelor, a weak CYP3A4 inhibitor IC50 = 68.74µM [17]), exhibits an unclear pharmacokinetic interaction profile with the novel dual orexin receptor antagonist fazamorexant, yet their co-administration is clinically necessary in patients with comorbid ACS and insomnia. To our knowledge, this is the first published analysis of the CYP3A drug-drug interaction potential of fazamorexant. Utilizing a single-center, open-label, sequential dosing design, ticagrelor (90 mg BID) was administered as the perpetrator drug, while fazamorexant (20 mg QD) served as the victim drug. This investigation systematically evaluated the impact of weak CYP3A4 inhibitor on fazamorexant exposure metrics. By comparing pharmacokinetic parameter variability (90% CI) between monotherapy and combination groups, alongside safety assessments (TEAE incidence and severity), this study ultimately established a dosing strategy and provided evidence-based guidance for personalized therapy in ACS-insomnia comorbid populations.

In this study, the co-administration of fazamorexant with ticagrelor resulted in a modest 14.13% increase in Cmax (90% CI: 100.92-129.07%) of fazamorexant, with AUC parameters remaining within the bioequivalence range (80–125%). This finding contrasts sharply with the marked pharmacokinetic interaction observed when fazamorexant is combined with the potent CYP3A4 inhibitor itraconazole, where Cmax and AUC of fazamorexant were increased by 68.96% and 782.80%, respectively (unpublished data). The discrepancy underscores the critical role of inhibitor potency in modulating DORA exposure. Ticagrelor’s weak inhibition profile likely results in partial enzyme blockade, insufficient to cause clinically significant exposure elevations. Notably, AUC metrics remained within the 80–125% bioequivalence range, aligning with regulatory guidelines for no-effect boundaries [18–20]. Importantly, the findings from this 20 mg study are applicable to the 40 mg therapeutic dose. The First-in-Human study have confirmed that fazamorexant exhibits linear pharmacokinetics with respect to AUC across the 5–80 mg dose range [10], meaning exposure increases proportionally with dose. Consequently, the magnitude of interaction observed with ticagrelor is not expected to change significantly at the higher 40 mg dose. Furthermore, the absolute exposure levels in this study remain well within the safety margins established in the completed Phase 3 study (data unpublished). In previous studies, lemborexant exhibited only approximately 30% increase when co-administered with CYP3A4 weak inhibitors (fluoxetine, ranitidine, chlorzoxazone, cilostazol, fosaprepitant, istradefylline, ivacaftor, lomitapide, ranolazine, tacrolimus, or ticagrelor; n = 15) [21]. Similarly, physiologically based pharmacokinetic (PBPK) modeling of daridorexant predicted that weak CYP3A4 inhibitors (e.g., cimetidine, ranitidine) would induce no significant changes in AUC0-t and Cmax [22]. Collectively, these findings support the conclusion that CYP3A4 weak inhibitors exert no clinically significant pharmacodynamic effects on fazamorexant, warranting no routine dosage adjustment.

The safety analysis revealed no serious adverse events or grade 3 or higher treatment-emergent adverse events. The most common TEAE was drowsiness (95.8%), followed by somnolence (8.3%) and dizziness (4.2%), all of which occurred during the administration of fazamorexant. It is important to acknowledge that while drowsiness and somnolence are categorized as adverse events in this study, they also represent the intended pharmacological effect of fazamorexant, reflecting target engagement at orexin receptors. In the therapeutic context of insomnia, such effects are indicative of efficacy rather than toxicity. This dual interpretation aligns with results from other aligns with results from other clinical studies of fazamorexant [10, 23]. Notably, ticagrelor’s co-administration did not exacerbate somnolence severity, suggesting additive pharmacodynamic effects are manageable and do not pose an increased safety risk. Headache occurred during ticagrelor administration and was a commonly reported adverse reaction in the ticagrelor tablet instruction manual [13]. The isolated case of grade 2 urticaria papular, which occurred during the ticagrelor treatment period, was assessed as idiosyncratic and unlikely related to the drug interaction with fazamorexant.

While this sequential pharmacotherapy study provides foundational pharmacokinetic parameters for fazamorexant-ticagrelor interactions, several limitations warrant consideration. The single-center design and limited sample size (n = 24) may restrict the generalizability of findings. Additionally, the short-term observation period precludes evaluation of chronic pharmacodynamic effects or cumulative safety risks, which is critical considering the prolonged use of both agents in real-world clinical practice. In light of the current increase in patients with ACS and insomnia comorbidity, future studies should assess long-term efficacy and safety in patients with chronic insomnia and cardiovascular disease. These steps are essential to establish robust clinical guidelines for managing ACS-insomnia comorbidities with optimized pharmacotherapeutic strategies.

Conclusion

In summary, this study demonstrated that co-administration of ticagrelor (90 mg twice daily) with fazamorexant (20 mg) was safe and well-tolerated in healthy subjects. The combination resulted in approximately 14% increase in fazamorexant’s Cmax without significant alterations in systemic exposure (AUC), suggesting that clinically meaningful pharmacokinetic interactions between fazamorexant and CYP3A4 weak inhibitors are unlikely.
