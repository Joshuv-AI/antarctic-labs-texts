Associations of cardiometabolic index with diabetic statuses and insulin resistance: the mediating role of inflammation-related indicators
Authors: Bin Xu, Qian Wu, Guoqing Yin, Lingchen Lu, Rui La, Yaxin Zhang, Jiasuer Alifu, Wen Zhang, Fushan Guo, Beina Ji, Fuad A. Abdu, Wenliang Che
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to analyze the associations of cardiometabolic index (CMI) with diabetic statuses and insulin resistance (IR) using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and examined the potential mediating role of inflammation in these correlations.MethodsThis study enrolled 9477 participants across four NHANES cycles from 2011 to 2018. The primary outcomes of the study included the risk of having prediabetes, diabetes and the level of the homeostasis model assessment of IR (HOMA-IR). Other outcomes including the levels of fasting blood glucose (FBG), hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) results, fasting insulin, the risk of oral hypoglycemic medicine use, insulin use, and retinopathy were also collected and analyzed. Logistic regression model, subgroup analysis, restricted cubic spine (RCS), and Pearson correlation coefficients were conducted to assess the associations of CMI with diabetic statuses and IR. The mediating role of inflammation was evaluated to investigate the potential mechanism of the associations between CMI and diabetic statuses.ResultsAmong included participants, the CMI levels in normal participants, prediabetes and diabetes in this study were 0.48, 0.73 and 1.07. After multivariable adjustment, CMI was positively associated with the risk of prediabetes (OR = 1.49, 95% CI = 1.24–1.79), diabetes (OR = 2.14, 95% CI = 1.82–2.50) and the level of HOMA-IR (β = 2.57, 95% CI = 2.14–3.01). Besides, an increased CMI was correlated with higher levels of FBG, HBA1c, OGTT results and fasting insulin as well as the greater risk of oral hypoglycemic medicine use and insulin use. The RCS showed an inverted L-shaped association of CMI with prediabetes and diabetes (P for non-linearity < 0.001). According to Pearson correlation coefficients, higher CMI was linked to higher rises in HOMA-IR (r = 0.224, P < 0.001). Inflammation-related indicators including leukocyte and neutrophil demonstrated significant mediating effects in the associations of CMI with prediabetes (15.5%, 9.8%), diabetes (5.1%, 6.0%) and HOMA-IR (3.3%, 2.6%).ConclusionCMI was positively associated with the risk of worse diabetic statuses and higher level of IR while the associations may be partially mediated by inflammation-related indicators, suggesting that CMI could be a promising indicator for the prediction of severe diabetes and IR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20048-0.

Background

Diabetes, a serious and chronic disease, is marked by elevated glycemic levels due to abnormal β-cell function impacting insulin action [1]. In 2021, around 537 million individuals globally were affected by diabetes with a prevalence rate of 6.1% [2]. Ranking as the eighth most common cause of disability and death worldwide, diabetes led to health expenditures of about US$966 billion globally, projected to exceed US$1054 billion by 2045 [1, 3, 4]. Evidence from clinical trials suggested that intensive glycemic management can reduce the development of microvascular complications such as neuropathy, retinopathy, and kidney disease by 50–76% [5, 6]. Furthermore, insulin resistance (IR) is almost universal among diabetes patients and is strongly linked to vascular diseases [7, 8]. Notably, over 50% of non-diabetic vascular disease patients also exhibit IR [9]. Thus, identifying modifiable risk factors for various diabetic statuses and IR is crucial for global public health and prevention strategies.

The cardiometabolic index (CMI) is a novel anthropometric index, calculated as the ratio of triglycerides (TG) to high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) multiplied by the waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), which has been validated as a predictor of diabetes [10]. Rising CMI level is associated with increasing risks of cardiovascular disease (CVD), chronic kidney disease (CKD) and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [11–13]. Compared to other anthropometric indices, CMI has been proven to be one of the most effective in predicting metabolic syndrome (MetS) [14, 15]. Numerous anthropometric indices including WHtR, waist circumference (WC), and waist-to-hip ratio (WHpR) have been validated correlated with IR [16]. However, the relationships of CMI different diabetic statuses with IR have not been explored. As an accessible and effective tool, the potential value of CMI in predicting different diabetic statuses and IR merits further investigation.

Diabetes is marked by chronic low-grade inflammation leading to hyperglycemia and IR, which are key factors in the development of its chronic complications [17, 18]. Excess nutrients stress pancreatic islets as well as insulin-sensitive tissues, triggering the production and release of inflammatory chemokines and cytokines. This results in the recruitment of immune cells, exacerbating tissue inflammation and further promoting inflammation in other areas, including the pancreatic islets [19]. On the other hand, research by Jovanovic et al. revealed that individuals on an anti-inflammatory diet exhibited significantly lower CMI and inflammatory indexes including IL-6, tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α as well as high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP) [20]. Individuals with high CMI may experience elevated systemic inflammation, potentially aggravating IR and escalating the risk of diabetes. However, the specific mechanisms underlying the relationship between CMI and diabetes have not been thoroughly studied.

Thus, the present study aims to evaluate the associations of CMI with different diabetic statuses and the level of IR, utilizing data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) and further illuminate whether inflammatory indicators have a potential role in mediating these associations.

Methods

Study design and population

NHANES, as a comprehensive cross-sectional survey, was executed and designed to collect population information including the health and nutritional status in the United States. For a representative sample, NHANES conducts a stratified multistage random sampling method [21, 22]. NHANES was granted approval from The National Center for Health Statistics study ethical review board, with every participant providing informed consent through signed agreement forms [23]. The NHANES datasets, complete with detailed documentation and protocols, are publicly accessible on their official website.

For our study, we analyzed data spanning four two-year cycles, covering the period from 2011 to 2018. Measurements and tests were all performed in mobile test facilities that were set up on-site, providing consistency in procedures and data collection protocols. In the present study, to maintain the integrity and reliability of the results, the specific exclusion criteria were adopted including: (1) individuals under 18 years of age (N = 15331); (2) individuals without CMI value (N = 14167); (3) individuals with incomplete data on diabetes or prediabetes (N = 6), and the homeostasis model assessment of IR (HOMA-IR) calculation (N = 175). A total of 9477 participants were enrolled from the years 2011 to 2018 in our study (Fig. 1).

Assessment of CMI

The related calculating formulas about CMI are as follows:

WHtR = WC (cm)/ height (cm),

CMI = TG (mmol/L) / HDL-C (mmol/L) × WHtR [10].

In the present study, we treated CMI as a continuous variable and based on CMI value, we subsequently categorized participants into three tertiles. Additionally, CMI was considered an exposure variable in our study design.

Assessment of diabetic statuses and insulin resistance

The primary outcomes of the study included the risk of diabetes, prediabetes, and the level of HOMA-IR. Diagnosis criteria for diabetes and prediabetes were based on those from the American Diabetes Association for 2023 [24]. Diabetes was diagnosed as one of the following criteria: (1) participants self-reported diagnosis of diabetes; (2) hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥ 6.5%; (3) fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥ 7.0 mmol/L; and (4) 2 h oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) results ≥ 11.1 mmol/L. Prediabetes was diagnosed as any one of the following criteria: (1) participants self-reported diagnosis of prediabetes; (2) HbA1c levels between 5.7 and 6.5%; (3) FBG levels between 5.6 mmol/L to 7.0 mmol/L; and (4) OGTT results between 7.8 mmol/L to 11.1 mmol/L. Additional diabetes-related outcomes including the levels of FBG, HbA1c, OGTT results, fasting insulin, the risk of oral hypoglycemic medicine use, insulin use, and retinopathy were also collected and analyzed.

In the present study, IR was evaluated by HOMA-IR (HOMA-IR = fasting insulin (µU/ml)*FBG (mmol/L)/22.5) [25].

Covariates

In the present study, various covariates were used to explain potential confounding factors, including age, gender, race, body mass index (BMI), leukocytes, neutrophils, lymphocyte, systemic immune-inflammation index (SII), neutrophil to lymphocyte ratio (NLR), hemoglobin, platelet, total Cholesterol (TC), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), creatinine, urinary albumin/creatinine ratio (UACR), hypertension and smoking. Smoking was through a questionnaire (SMQ040), including the following question: ‘Do you now smoke cigarettes?’ In the subgroup analysis, participants were divided into two groups according to the cutoff value of 65 years, providing a comparison of age-related effect.

The formulas for calculating the relevant indexes are as follows:

NLR = neutrophil (/L)/lymphocyte (/L),

SII = platelet (/L) *neutrophil (/L)/lymphocyte (/L).

Statistical analysis

R software (version 4.1.3) and EmpowerStats (version 2.0) were utilized to perform all statistical analysis. Study participants were classified into three groups according to tertiles (T1-T3) of CMI. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages whereas continuous variables were presented as median ± interquartile range according. Different CMI tertile groups were analyzed using the Chi-squared test or Kruskal-Wallis H test. A statistically significant result was determined as a two-sided p-value < 0.05.

Multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluated the associations of CMI with diabetic statuses. We treated Model 1 as no adjustment and we adjusted Model 2 for gender, age, and race. Model 3, as the major model in our study, was adjusted for all variables in Model 2 as well as TC, LDL-C, creatinine, UACR, hypertension, smoke, leukocyte, and weight.

The identical statistical techniques mentioned above were also utilized in the subgroups analysis to examine potential variations within specific populations including gender, age, race and hypertension subgroups. The restricted cubic spine (RCS) curve was conducted to flexibly model and visualize the nonlinear relationships of CMI with the risk of prediabetes and diabetes. Pearson correlation coefficient was performed to assess the association between CMI and the level of HOMA-IR. These statistical methods provided a relatively comprehensive exploration of the potential linearity and nonlinearity associations of CMI and the risk of different diabetic statuses and the level of IR.

Mediation analysis was performed using R software (version 4.2.2, R Foundation for statistical computing, Vienna, Austria), along with the use of MSTATA software (https:/www.mstata.com/), aligning with the laboratory technologists and anthropometry procedures of our previous studies [26, 27]. The mediating effects of inflammatory indicators (leukocyte, neutrophil, lymphocyte, NLR, and SII) on the associations of CMI with diabetes, prediabetes and HOMA-IR were evaluated, adjusted by gender, age, race, TC, LDL-C, creatinine, UACR, hypertension and smoking. Mediating effect existence was defined as meeting all of the following criteria: (1) significant indirect effect; (2) significant total effect; and (3) the proportion of mediator effect was positive.

Results

Enrollment of the study population

A total of 39,156 participants were enrolled from the years 2011 to 2018 was collected. This study excluded people aged < 18 years (N = 15331), WHtR, CMI with missing values (N = 14167) and prediabetes, diabetes (N = 6), HOMA-IR with missing values (N = 175). Finally, a total of 9477 participants were eligible in our study, with 3390 normal participants, 4161 participants with prediabetes and 1926 with diabetes (Fig. 1).

Participants characteristics

The baseline characteristics of enrolled participants in the present study with different diabetic statuses are presented in Table 1. Compared with the normal individuals, the participants with prediabetes and diabetes had higher age, BMI and WC, and more likely to be men. CMI was higher among participants with prediabetes (0.73 ± 0.89) and diabetes (1.07 ± 1.50) than normal participants (0.48 ± 0.52), of which the diabetes group is the highest (P < 0.001).

Associations of CMI with diabetic statuses and insulin resistance

Tables 3 and 4 display the associations of CMI with the risk of different diabetic statuses and the level of IR after multivariate logistic regression. In model 1, CMI was significantly associated with higher risk of prediabetes (OR = 2.16, 95% CI = 1.95–2.39), diabetes (OR = 1.70, 95% CI = 1.59–1.81) and level of HOMA-IR (β = 1.85, 95% CI = 1.69–2.01). In model 2, these significant associations sustained exist in all groups (OR = 2.00, 95% CI = 1.80–2.22; OR = 1.83, 95% CI = 1.70–1.96; β = 1.84, 95% CI = 1.68–2.01). Even after adjusting all interfering factors in model 3, the associations of CMI with higher risk of prediabetes, diabetes and level of HOMA-IR remained significant and positive (OR = 1.49, 95% CI = 1.24–1.79; OR = 2.14, 95% CI = 1.82–2.50; β = 2.57, 95% CI = 2.14–3.01). In Additional, the ORs with corresponding CIs showed a positively significant correlation in T2 as well as T3 compared to T1 after fully adjusting for all covariates. When compared to T1, the ORs and βs of T3 were 1.59 (95% CI = 1.26–2.01, P for trend < 0.001), 4.08 (95% CI = 3.11–5.37, P for trend < 0.001) and 2.68 (95% CI = 1.97–3.38, P for trend < 0.001) and 2.68 (95% CI = 1.97–3.38, P for trend < 0.001). Besides, after adjusting for all relevant covariates in the fully adjusted model, an increasing CMI was associated with higher levels of FBG (β = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.51–0.72), HBA1c (β = 0.30, 95% CI = 0.25–0.36), OGTT (β = 0.98, 95% CI = 0.77–1.19) results and fasting insulin (β = 6.37, 95% CI = 5.27–7.47) as well as the greater risk of oral hypoglycemic medicine use (OR = 1.39, 95% CI = 1.09–1.77) and insulin use (OR = 1.32, 95% CI = 1.08–1.62).

Mediating role of inflammationrelated indicators

Figure 6 shows that leukocyte mediated 15.5%, 5.1% and 3.3% of the associations of CMI with prediabetes, diabetes and HOMA-IR (P < 0.001). The results were observed similar in the analysis of neutrophils (9.8%, 6.0% and 2.6%, P < 0.001). Additionally, we also assessed the mediating roles of other inflammatory indicators including lymphocytes, NLR, and SII (Appendix Figs. 1, 2 and 3). However, not all the inflammation-related indicators had significant mediation effects. Only NLR was found a significant mediation effect for the association of CMI with prediabetes and lymphocytes for the association of CMI with diabetes.

Discussion

In our study, we demonstrated that CMI was positively associated with various diabetic statuses and IR while the associations may be partially mediated by inflammation-related indicators. A higher CMI was related to an increased risk of prediabetes, diabetes and IR, along with more frequent use of oral hypoglycemics medicine and insulin use. These associations also extended to elevated levels of FBG, HbA1c, OGTT results and fasting insulin, and all findings remained significant in the fully adjusted model. The RCS curve revealed an inverted L-shaped relationship of CMI with both prediabetes and diabetes. Pearson correlation analysis further confirmed that higher CMI related to greater level of HOMA-IR. Our mediation analysis highlighted the significant roles of leukocytes and neutrophils in linking CMI with the risk of worse diabetic statuses and the higher level of IR, suggesting inflammation as a potential underlying mechanism in these associations. Therefore, monitoring CMI in patients offers a straightforward yet potent approach for epidemiological studies on diabetic conditions and IR.

In 2015, Wakabayashi et al. introduced CMI, a novel anthropometric index for identifying diabetes, demonstrating its significant correlation with hyperglycemia and diabetes [10]. Subsequent research further explored the association between CMI and diabetes risk among Japanese adults (HR = 1.65) and identified a non-linear relationship with an inflection point at a CMI of 1.01 [28]. Similarly, Qiu et al. reported that individuals with high baseline CMI had a considerably increased risk of new-onset diabetes (HR = 1.78), and those with initially low CMI who shifted to high CMI during follow-up saw their risk of developing diabetes elevated by 75% [29]. Beyond diabetes, CMI has been demonstrated linked to MetS, which is characterized by abdominal obesity, hypertension, hyperglycemia, and atherogenic dyslipidemia. Lazzer et al. indicated that CMI had higher sensitivity and specificity in detecting MetS compared with other indices like WHpR, WtHR, body mass fat index (BMFI) and visceral adiposity index (VAI) [14]. Radetti et al. revealed the validity of CMI in identifying MetS in severely obese children and adolescents (AUC = 0.8476) [30]. Liu et al. found that CMI was the most effective index in discerning the metabolically obese normal weight phenotype (AUC = 0.876), surpassing BMI, WC, WHtR, and TG/HDL-C [31]. Additionally, the predictive value of CMI for systemic diseases has been increasingly recognized. Zou et al. identified a positive independent association between CMI and elevated risk of NAFLD in the general population (AUC = 0.8359) [11]. Liu et al. observed that CMI was a more potent predictor of NAFLD in Chinese women (AUC = 0.833) [32]. Miao et al. suggested CMI as an independent risk factor for albuminuria, highlighting its potential in predicting renal dysfunction (OR = 1.160) [12]. Cai et al. founded a positive correlation between CMI levels and the new-onset risk of CVD in hypertension and obstructive sleep apnea patients (HR = 1.31) [13]. Higashiyama et al. discovered a higher risk for ischemic CVD in participants without MetS who had higher CMI [33]. Thus, as a novel anthropometric index relatively easy to obtain, CMI may hold potential as a predictive tool for systemic diseases. Comparatively, other anthropometric indices like BMI, WC, WHpR, and WHtR have been validated associated with diabetes and IR [16, 34, 35]. Zhu et al. reported a positive correlation of WHtR with FBG and 2-hour postprandial blood glucose [34]. Kruger et al. noted that a WHtR of 0.41 yielded 64% sensitivity and 58.5% specificity for a HOMA-IR above 3.4 and the value of AUC was 0.66 [35]. Can et al. confirmed the statistical significance of the correlation between WHtR and log HOMA-IR (β = 0.43) and indicated WHtR as the best index for distinguishing diabetes (AUC = 0.70) and MetS (AUC = 0.74) [16]. Several studies have revealed IR-related index was associated with systematic diseases and worse prognosis [36, 37]. Besides, Other metabolic indexes have been validated the ability to identify prediabetes and diabetes. The study conducted by Shi et al. showed that a higher AIP was significantly associated with an increased prevalence of prediabetes and diabetes (OR = 2.49) [38]. Park et al. revealed that revealed the validity of Triglyceride glucose (TyG) index in predicting type 2 diabetes (T2DM) [39]. Wen et al. indicated that TyG could be a potential predictor (OR = 1.38) to identify the higher risk individuals of prediabetes [40]. Despite these findings, the specific associations of CMI with the risk of different diabetic statuses and the level of IR, and whether inflammation mediate these associations remain unexplored. Our study hypothesizes that CMI, closely related to WHtR, would have significant predictive value for worse diabetic statuses and IR. Our results confirm significant associations of CMI with the higher risk of prediabetes, diabetes, higher level of IR, and elevated glycemic levels. Moreover, we observed inverted L-shaped associations of CMI with prediabetes and diabetes according to the RCS curves. The risk of both prediabetes and diabetes increased rapidly until around 1.2 and 1.5 of CMI and then started to be relatively flat afterward, which indicated that interventions for CMI are more valuable at less than 1.2 and 1.5, and CMI was positively associated with the risk of prediabetes and diabetes especially in the range of 0-1.2 and 0-1.5.

Although CMI is highly related to diabetes as demonstrated by both prior and current studies, the exact biological mechanisms underlying the correlation between CMI and diabetes are not fully understood. Previous research underscored the significant role of inflammation in diabetes development [41]. Nutrient excess, including free fatty acids and glucose, stresses pancreatic islets as well as insulin-sensitive tissues, resulting in the production and release of inflammatory mediators like interleukin-1β, TNF, CC-chemokine ligand 2, and CXC-chemokine ligand 8, while concurrently reducing the production of IL-1 receptor antagonist by β-cells. This results in immune cell recruitment and subsequent tissue inflammation [19]. Increased levels of inflammatory indicators, including CRP, serum amyloid A, sialic acid, and cytokines like IL-1β and IL-6, have been validated to be associated with diabetes and are predictive of its onset [42–44]. On the other hand, Jovanovic et al. found that an anti-inflammatory diet was significantly associated with both lower CMI and inflammatory indicators such as IL-6, hs-CRP, and TNF-α [20]. Meta-analysis conducted by Masson et al. showed the use of anti-inflammatory therapy in patients with T2DM and atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease was associated with reduced risk of major cardiovascular events (MACE) [45]. The use of anti-inflammatory therapy was associated with a lower risk of MACE (HR = 0.80, 95% CI = 0.69–0.93). Higher CMI is linked to more serious systemic inflammation, which may further exacerbate IR and diabetes, leading to the worse prognosis. Previous studies demonstrated that chronic inflammation is thought to be a significant factor in the progression of diabetes, which may explain the positive association between CMI and various diabetic statuses. Chronic low-grade inflammation established aggravate insulin resistance and hyperglycemia, leading to development of diabetes [17, 41]. Different pathways, which includes NADPH Oxidase Pathway, TCR Pathway, BCR Pathway and NF-κB Signaling Pathway, are corresponded to the various inflammation indicators, which are critical in the regulation of various leukocyte functions [46–48]. This study, therefore, also explored the mediating effect of inflammation in the associations of CMI with the risk of different diabetic statuses and the level of IR. Our findings confirm the significant mediating role of inflammation, providing robust evidence for its involvement in these associations.

Furthermore, as the calculation of CMI includes HDL-C and TG, it suggests a potential relationship with lipid metabolism disorders, which may further explain its associations with diabetes and IR. Previous studies have shown that lipotoxicity contributes to diabetes and IR progression, with elevated plasma levels of free fatty acids resulting in impaired β cell function and apoptosis, as well as IR [49–51]. The positive correlations of CMI with NAFLD and MetS, especially considering the lipid-related calculation of CMI, implied that higher CMI levels may be indicative of lipid metabolism disorders [11, 32]. However, no study has directly investigated this relationship, nor has our study included lipid-related mediators in the analysis, suggesting a potential avenue for future research. In the present study, we validated the mediating role of inflammation in the associations of CMI with diabetes and prediabetes. Moreover, since CMI was calculated as the ratio of TG to HDL-C multiplied by WHtR, TG is an effective target for early intervention of CMI in clinical work. Therefore, medications with TC control and anti-inflammatory effects like statins may be beneficial to improve the diabetic statuses and IR of patients.

A major strength of our study is the utilization of a large and representative NHANES database, strengthening the generalizability of our findings. We provide additional evidence supporting the positive correlation between CMI and various diabetic statuses. Our study also highlights the potential of CMI as an easily obtainable anthropometric index for identifying individuals at risk of diabetes and IR and underscores the mediating role of inflammation in these associations.

Study limitations

The cross-sectional of the NHANES dataset lacks longitudinal data, and despite adjusting for several confounders, unmeasured or residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. Treatment variables that might influence CMI, such as fibrates, statins, and specific oral antidiabetic medications, were not considered. Besides, controlled or uncontrolled diabetes patients, were analyzed in the same method in the present study, regardless of whether they were taking oral hypoglycemic medications or were taking insulin, which may affect the results of analysis. Additionally, the specific data of diabetes diagnosis in this study was limited, preventing the classification of participants into specific diabetes types, which affects the extrapolation of these findings to broader populations.

Conclusion

The findings of this study demonstrated positive associations between CMI and various diabetic statuses, as well as the IR, while also highlighting the significant mediating role of inflammation in these relationships. These insights add to the growing evidence supporting the clinical utility of CMI in predicting severe diabetes and IR, contributing valuable perspectives for early risk stratification and the development of intervention strategies in individuals with different diabetic statuses.
