Effectiveness of eHealth lifestyle interventions on management of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Yangguo Zhao, Xinrui Yang, Yang Bai, Dorothy Ngo Sheung Chan, Caixia Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstracteHealth lifestyle interventions have emerged as promising management strategies for metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). Our review aimed to synthesize the most recent evidence to evaluate the effectiveness of eHealth lifestyle interventions on clinical and behavioral outcomes in adults with MASLD. Twelve databases (Medline, Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, ProQuest Health & Medical Collection, CINAHL Complete, Academic Search Complete, Scopus, Emerald, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data, and VIP Database) were searched from inception to April 2, 2025. Thirteen randomized controlled trials and fifteen quasi-experimental trials comprising 2579 adult patients with MASLD were included. Seven intervention components were identified: goal setting, health education, consultation, self- and device-monitoring, feedback, patient reminders, and social support. Meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials demonstrated that eHealth lifestyle interventions were significantly superior to standard care in reducing weight, body mass index, waist circumference, alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and γ-glutamyl transferase levels, and improving MASLD status. Improvements in self-management, dietary adherence, physical activity, exercise, and quality of life were also observed. Our findings suggest that ehealth intervention is a better choice for the patients with MASLD to achieve precise and scalable management. Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) has gradually emerged as a significant public health concern that is closely linked to excess adiposity, insulin resistance, and the metabolic syndrome [1]. It affects approximately 38% of adults worldwide [2], with prevalence exceeding 30% in Asia and the Middle East. China alone has an estimated 150–200 million cases [3]. Beyond its high prevalence, MASLD poses a significant threat because of its potential to progress along a deleterious spectrum, from simple steatosis to nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), advanced fibrosis, cirrhosis, and hepatocellular carcinoma [4]. Critically, it independently increases the risk of cardiovascular mortality and mutually exacerbates metabolic dysregulation [4, 5]. This dual burden on hepatic and extrahepatic outcomes translates into substantial healthcare costs, productivity losses, and diminished quality of life, making MASLD a critical public health challenge in the 21st century [6].

Lifestyle interventions, comprising calorie restriction, dietary adjustment, and regular exercise, are the cornerstone of MASLD management in international consensus guidelines [7]. These interventions can effectively reverse hepatic steatosis and delay MASLD progression [8–10]. However, owing to multiple barriers (e.g., lack of intensive supervision), real-world adherence to lifestyle interventions in patients with MASLD is often suboptimal in the short- and long-term [11, 12]. One study showed that only 30% of patients with MASLD lose more than 5% of their body weight within a 13-month lifestyle intervention [13].

eHealth lifestyle interventions based on digital technologies have provided a new solution for removing obstacles to adherence to lifestyle prescriptions. The initial eHealth lifestyle interventions for patients with MASLD were relatively simple and non-personalized, manifesting as providing MASLD treatment knowledge and conducting follow-up visits through regular text messages [14] and phone calls [15]. In the past 5 years, comprehensive eHealth lifestyle intervention programs have emerged that integrate functions such as goal setting, self-monitoring, MASLD disease cognition education, diet planning, exercise supervision, remote consultation, patient reminders, and emotional relief [16, 17]. Smooth implementation of these programs relies on the assistance of a health-related electronic platform and mobile application that integrates multiple technologies such as multimedia, wearable devices, the Internet of Things (IoT), image recognition, electronic communication, and chatbots [18]. Educational slides on multimedia technology that can be used for MASLD treatment based on guidelines and diet tips are highly interactive and adaptable [19], enhancing patients’ perceptions of MASLD and their knowledge of health. Wearable devices, the IoT, and specific algorithms enable convenient and continuous self-monitoring of health behaviors and data, such as physical activity, sleep patterns, and heart rate [16]. By taking photos of their meals, patients could track calorie intake and nutrient composition in real-time, thereby evaluating whether the food complied with the MASLD dietary guidelines [19]. Electronic communication and chatbots can provide effective personalized support, education, and remote consultation to patients with MASLD, enabling them to obtain answers promptly and reduce their concerns about physical symptoms [20, 21]. Together, these technologies make it easier and more desirable to adhere to lifestyle prescriptions and contribute to improved long-term management of MASLD [16].

More importantly, compared to traditional lifestyle interventions, eHealth lifestyle interventions are more effective in improving patients’ anthropometric indicators, liver function indicators, and other metabolic indicators, and enhancing patients’ MASLD self-management abilities. A study demonstrated that the MASLD disease improvement rate of the eHealth intervention group was 20–60% higher than that of the control group [22, 23]. Among the MASLD patients in the group that followed the guidance from eHealth app for 1 year, nearly 41% of them had their fatty liver index return to normal levels [16]. In addition, eHealth lifestyle interventions can reduce alanine aminotransferase (ALT) by 5–80 IU/L [17, 24, 25], aspartate aminotransferase (AST) by 4–545 IU/L [19, 24, 25], and γ-glutamyl transferase (GGT) by 12–27 IU/L [19, 26] in MASLD patients with abnormally elevated liver enzymes. After adhering to an eHealth lifestyle intervention for 1 year, the proportion of MASLD patients with normal MASLD-NFS increased 15% in the eHealth group compared with 5% in the control group [16]. In a study that used magnetic resonance imaging proton density fat fraction (MRI-PDFF), more patients in the e-Health group achieved a liver fat content <6% than in the control group (129 [83.2%] vs 17 [34%], respectively) [16]. It was also observed that the total cholesterol (TC), triglyceride (TG), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) levels of the patients decreased in several studies [18, 21, 26, 27]. Patients’ MASLD self-management ability, self-management knowledge level, and self-efficacy were significantly enhanced [19].

While previous meta-analyses have established the efficacy of e-health interventions for MASLD on core parameters like weight, body mass index (BMI), ALT, and AST [28, 29], the recent emergence of trials utilizing more comprehensive digital platforms, combined with a need to synthesize a broader range of clinically relevant outcomes, necessitates an updated evidence synthesis. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to comprehensively synthesize contemporary evidence on eHealth lifestyle interventions for MASLD.

The specific objectives of this review were to [1] qualitatively summarize and characterize the components of eHealth lifestyle interventions for MASLD, and [2] meta-analytically compare the effectiveness of eHealth interventions versus standard care on anthropometric, clinical, and behavioral outcomes in patients with MASLD.

Methods

Study design and registration

This systematic review and meta-analysis were conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-analysis (PRISMA) (Supplementary File) [30].

The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO CRD420251001470 on 4 March, 2025.

Search strategy and data sources

To gather all randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and quasi-experimental studies investigating the effect of eHealth lifestyle interventions on patients with MASLD, a systematic literature search was performed by an information specialist together with the authors. The search was conducted in Medline, Web of Science Core Collection, Embase, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, ProQuest Health & Medical Collection, CINAHL Complete, Academic Search Complete, Scopus, Emerald, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, Wanfang Data and VIP Database until April 2025 using the keywords “non-alcoholic fatty liver,” “MASLD,” “NASH,” “steatohepatitis,” “Telemedicine,” “digital intervention,” “web-based intervention,” and “digital therapy.” A medical librarian refined the search strategy by reviewing the objectives of the review, search terms, and detailed search records in MEDLINE. The search terms and strategies are detailed in Table S1–S2 (Supplementary information 1).

Eligibility criteria: inclusion and exclusion

Types of participants

Studies involving adult (aged ≥18 years) patients with MASLD, including non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFL) and non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), who were diagnosed by imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI) and/or liver biopsy were included in the analysis. Studies that included healthy individuals without MASLD, patients with simple obesity, and those with hepatic steatosis caused by other factors (e.g., excessive alcohol consumption, medication, and genetic diseases) were excluded.

Types of interventions

eHealth lifestyle intervention was defined as the healthy lifestyle intervention content in the research that was delivered and implemented based on digital technologies, such as mobile applications, online consultation, wearable devices, or AI algorithms. All of the patients were coached on how to use the apps or platforms and to ensure a reliable electronic connection. Studies that intervened through text messaging or phone calls were excluded.

Types of control

The control groups were considered as those who received standard care, traditional face-to-face interventions, or routine MASLD lifestyle interventions, without any eHealth methods or tools.

Types of outcome measures

From each study, the changes in the clinical and behavioral indicators of the intervention and control groups were extracted, including the following:

Primary outcomes: changes in body weight, BMI, waist circumference, anthropometric parameters, liver markers (ALT, AST, and GGT), improvement rate of hepatic steatosis degree, and other metabolic parameters.

Secondary outcomes were changes in self-management level, quality of life, and adherence to diet, physical activity, and exercise.

Types of studies

We considered randomized controlled trials and quasi-experimental trial studies for this systematic review and meta-analysis, irrespective of language, publication status, or date of publication. Non-English articles were translated using professional services where needed. Case reports, protocols, and articles that did not meet these criteria were excluded.

Data extraction

Articles that met our selection criteria were selected and independently screened by two authors (LC and ZY). Study characteristics (author, year, region, study design, and sample size), intervention characteristics (delivery mode and intervention components), and study outcomes were extracted. The lifestyle intervention program was divided into four parts for data extraction: (a) intervention components, (b) intervention sessions, (c) intervention duration, and (d) key interventionists.

When the resulting data had inconsistent units, they were converted to uniform units and then subjected to meta-analysis to calculate the pooled effect size. When the resulting data derived from an article are not in the form of means and standard deviations, their means and standard deviations were estimated using verified mathematical methods described by Wan et al. and Luo et al. [31, 32].

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias assessment was independently conducted by two researchers (LC and ZY). Quality assessment of the included RCTs was conducted using the Cochrane tool for Risk of Bias (RoB2) [33]. The ROBINS-I tool was used to assess the risk of bias for quasi-experimental trials [34].

The ROB2 tool has five domains: randomization process, deviations from the intended intervention, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selection of reported results. For each domain, studies were graded as yes, partly yes, partly no, no, or no information. Following this assessment, each domain was assigned a risk of bias of low, some concern, or high.

The ROBINS-1 tool has seven domains: confounding, selection of participants for the study, classification of intervention, deviations from intended interventions, missing data, measurement of outcomes, and selection of reported results. Following this assessment, each domain was assigned an overall risk of bias: low, moderate, serious, or critical concerns. Each reviewer was trained in both the ROB2 and ROBINS-I tools. In addition, the overall strength of evidence on outcomes was evaluated using the GRADE approach (McMaster University, 2020) [35, 36]. Evidence was rated as high, moderate, low, or very low.

Data analysis

The results of the RCTs were included in the meta-analysis, with the pooled effect serving as a measure of the differences between the intervention and standard care control groups. The intervention components of the included quasi-experimental studies were descriptively summarized together with the RCT studies to comprehensively reflect the diversity of the implemented eHealth intervention programs.

The significance level was set at P < 0.05. Since most of the outcomes measured were continuous, data were presented as standardized mean difference (SMD) and their 95% confidence interval (CI). We used the interpretation thresholds proposed by Cohen et al. [37]. According to Cohen’s thresholds, SMDs <0.2, ≥0.2 and <0.5, ≥0.5 and <0.8, and ≥0.8 correspond to insignificant, small, moderate, and large effect sizes, respectively.

Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q test, with P < .1 indicating statistically significant heterogeneity. Low, moderate, and high heterogeneity were determined using I2 statistics of 25%, 50%, and 75%, respectively [38].

Subgroup analyses were conducted to examine the sources of heterogeneity for different study characteristics, including study region, sample size, intervention duration, and intervention components. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using the leave-one-out approach to explore the robustness of the results. Egger’s statistical test was used to evaluate the evidence of publication bias [39]. For the interpretation of Egger’s test, statistical significance was defined as P < 0.1.

Descriptive summarization and integration were adopted for ineligible outcomes to be pooled in the meta-analysis.

Results

Search results

As shown in Fig. 1, the database search identified 10 023 articles. In total, 924 duplicates were removed, and 8999 articles were excluded after screening the titles and abstracts. Among the 100 full-text articles retrieved, 69 were excluded mainly because of the lack of published data. Studies that conducted follow-up using only communication methods (such as text messaging or phone calls) were excluded. A total of 2579 patients with MASLD were enrolled in 28 studies. The 13 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) included 1410 patients. Individual RCTs were categorized as small (n < 50; 1 study) [42], medium (n = 50–100; 5 studies) [23, 26, 44, 46, 47], or large (n > 100; 7 studies) [16, 17, 19, 22, 43–45]. The intervention-to-control group allocation was 1:1 in 11 RCTs [17, 19, 22, 23, 26, 40, 42, 43, 45–47], 2:1 in one [16], and unclear in another [44]. Among the 12 RCTs with defined groups, the intervention and control groups comprised 704 and 600 patients, respectively.

Fifteen quasi-experimental trials enrolled 1169 patients. Most were small-sample studies (n < 50; 12 studies) [21, 27, 49–55, 58–60], with two medium sample size studies (n = 50–100) [25, 48] and one large-sample study (n > 100) [57].

The mean age of participants across the studies ranged from 38.3 to 59.5 years. The inclusion criteria of the studies frequently aligned with the guidelines for MASLD diagnosis. The key exclusion criteria included other chronic liver diseases (e.g., viral hepatitis) and a history of significant alcohol consumption. One study included patients with both MASLD and diabetes [16], and another study included patients with both MASLD and HIV [47].

Components of the eHealth lifestyle interventions.

Goal setting

Goal-setting refers to the formulation or personalized customization of final intervention goals by the interventionist, which was adopted in 23 studies [16, 17, 19, 21–23, 26, 27, 40, 42, 44–47, 50, 52, 53, 55, 58–61]. The interventionists directly established intervention goals based on the patients’ baseline conditions [16, 17, 22–24, 42, 44, 45, 47], such as age, sex, initial weight, dietary preference, and presence of comorbidities. In 1 study, patients were allowed to set their own lifestyle modification goals and plans on the app, which were specific, measurable, achievable, relevant, and time-limited [26]. In 7 studies, goals were set jointly by the patients and interventionists, or with the assistance of algorithms.

Weight loss goals were set in the 3 studies [19, 21, 50, 51], and included goals such as achieving 7% weight loss [21]. Exercise goals were set in 19 studies that involved resistance training [25, 42, 61] and aerobic exercise [25, 42, 60]. Generally, the exercise frequency was set at 3–5 times a week, or 3 times a week at the beginning of the intervention and increasing to 5 times a week in the later stage of the intervention [16, 42]. Patients were also encouraged to meet specific and measurable exercise goals, such as daily step targets, for example, achieving 5000 steps per day during the first 3 months, and increasing to 10,000 steps per day thereafter [16]. Dietary goals were set in 11 studies, typically involving calorie restriction [17, 42, 47], increased protein, plant protein [42], or dietary fiber intake [17], low-saturated fat dietary intake, and vitamin supplementation [17]. Policarpo et al. developed a tailored nutritional plan based on a Mediterranean diet designed specifically for patients with MASLD [47]. The plan incorporated a caloric deficit of 500 kcal and accommodated additional dietary restrictions while aligning with individual nutritional requirements [47].

Health education

Health education could include disease cognitive health education and lifestyle education, which were adopted in 25 studies [16–19, 21, 24–26, 40, 42, 43, 45–49, 51, 53, 55, 57–61]. One study only mentioned that health education was conducted and did not describe the contents of health education in detail [22].

In 12 studies that involved disease cognitive health education, the interventionists taught the patients about MASLD, including the definition of MASLD, its causes, the degree of progression to liver cirrhosis and liver cancer, its association with other metabolic diseases, and the methods of prevention and treatment [19, 21, 43, 47, 51, 52, 55, 57, 59].

Twenty-five studies reported on lifestyle education, including dietary and exercise suggestions [16, 17, 19, 21, 23–26, 40, 42–49, 51, 55, 57–60]. In the dietary health education modules, core knowledge encompasses the following key areas: principles of balancing energy intake and weight management, the association between dietary patterns and MASLD, the physiological roles of carbohydrates and fats, practical strategies for caloric restriction, and an overview of evidence-based alternative dietary approaches. In the 25 studies involving health education on physical activity and exercise, the interventionist helped the patient distinguish different types of exercise (e.g., aerobic and resistance exercise), along with instructions on how to warm up, cool down, increase the amount of physical activity, and determine exercise intensity and duration [16, 17, 19, 23, 24, 26, 40, 42–46].

In addition, lifestyle education in the intervention programs comprised smoking cessation [17, 22, 40], limiting alcohol intake [17, 47, 51, 55], keeping healthy sleep patterns [16, 26, 59], and stress management [26, 48, 52, 59]. Furthermore, the interventionists provided a referenceable time schedule in the health education section of 1 study [17].

Self-monitoring and device monitoring

Self-monitoring refers to the process of allowing patients to observe, measure, and record their health situations and behavioral changes by themselves, and was adopted in 16 studies [17, 19, 21, 23, 25, 26, 40, 45–48, 50, 51, 55, 58, 59, 61]. Patients were taught to record and upload their weight loss progress, dietary diaries, physical activities, and exercise, and to record their emotional and sleep conditions.

Leveraging built-in functions, wearable devices enable the comprehensive monitoring of health and exercise-related data. These devices can automatically record metrics, such as step count, sedentary time, active minutes, calories burned, and heart rate. In addition, they allow users to manually log specific types of exercise. Fitbit sensor watches [16, 27, 44, 60] and the Huawei bracelet 4 [53] were utilized as wearable devices for health monitoring.

Consultation

Consultation refers to a situation in which patients have opportunities to proactively ask professionals about knowledge they are not familiar with or problems they encounter; this component was adopted by 23 studies [16, 18, 19, 21, 23–25, 27, 40, 42, 48–53, 55, 59–61]. Remote consultation channels (including contacting health coaches within the app, sending emails, making phone calls, and holding group meetings with free questions and answers) were provided to enable patients to initiate consultations when they encountered problems. Among the studies using remote consultation, four simultaneously provided the opportunity for face-to-face consultation [21, 45, 47, 49]. The only consultation channel for one study was face-to-face consultation [47].

Patient reminders

Patient reminders, or regular prompts or supervision from the interventionist to ensure that patients complete their plan and attend follow-up visits, were adopted in 13 studies [17, 19, 22, 27, 43–46, 50, 58, 60, 61]. These reminders were delivered through various means such as automated notifications via apps, text messaging, phone calls, or e-mail.

Feedback

Following a review of patient-uploaded self-monitoring data (e.g., weight, diet, physical activity, and other health behaviors), the interventionist provided a tailored evaluation and constructive feedback. The feedback loop was designed to reinforce positive behaviors, address areas for improvement, and offer personalized recommendations to enhance patients’ self-management strategies. Among the reviewed studies, this feedback process was implemented in 13 [19, 26, 27, 40, 44–46, 49–51, 59–61].

Social support

Social support refers to the emotional support provided in the project, such as family members participating in the project to provide companionship, patients forming a group to communicate with each other, complimenting or encouraging information provided during the project consultation, and complimenting or encouraging information in the project email. In seven studies involving social support, intervention programs regularly provided patients to inspiring and praising words [21, 25–27, 48, 50, 61]. Two studies included the family members of patients in the intervention programs [40].

Intervention delivery

Twenty studies were mobile applications dominant, among which 4 relied on mainstream social applications [22, 40, 43], and 16 developed dedicated health management platforms or applications. Two studies were conducted through websites [50, 61] with disease management and health education functions, whereas other research interventions were conducted through Zoom, phone calls, and emails.

Intervention duration and frequency

The shortest intervention duration was 4 weeks, with a follow-up of 4 weeks [26]. The longest intervention duration lasted for 2 years and had a follow-up period of 5 years [18, 56, 57]. The intervention duration of 24 studies was preset; 11 studies conducted a short-term intervention (≤3 months) [17, 42], 9 conducted a medium-term intervention (4–6 months), and 4 conducted a long-term intervention (≥12 months) [16, 18, 22, 56, 57]. The patients in 3 studies could self-determine the application usage duration based on their individual needs [18, 25, 49]. The intervention duration of only 1 study was determined based on whether the entire series of intervention courses had been completed [48].

The frequency of contact varied from daily to weekly, every other week, and every 3 months.

Interventionists and the availability of coaches

Among the included studies, the majority of interventions were led by nurses and physicians, with 4 [22, 23, 40, 45] and 10 studies [17, 23, 25, 40, 48, 50, 51, 53, 57, 61], respectively. Applications and electronic platforms serve as the primary means of intervention, with healthcare providers providing secondary support to facilitate remote consultations for patients. Notably, 10.7% (3/28) of studies utilized apps without requiring additional participation from health-care providers [17, 58].

Nutritionists contributed to the intervention design and implementation in 13 of the 28 studies, providing nutritional education, performing dietary assessments, and analyzing diet diaries with feedback. Additionally, exercise coaches were included in 4 studies [19, 42, 52, 59] to support the intervention process.

Effectiveness of eHealth lifestyle interventions

For the meta-analysis, 1 RCT [44] that lacked complete pre- and post-intervention data was excluded, and data from the remaining 12 RCTs [16, 17, 19, 22, 23, 26, 40, 42, 43, 45–47] were included. The forest plots for subgroup analysis of the primary outcomes were shown in Fig. S1–S40 (Supplementary 2). Some anthropometric and metabolic indicators were measured and reported in only 2 or 3 studies, and their forest plots were displayed in Fig. S45–S48 (Supplementary 2). Descriptive summarization and integration were adopted for the quasi-experimental studies.

Other health indicators

Given the pathophysiological association between type 2 diabetes and MASLD, glucose metabolism was included in the measurement outcomes of six studies. Four RCTS [26, 40, 46] and three quasi-experimental studies [17, 18, 21, 25] measured fasting blood glucose levels before and after the intervention. Based on the four RCTs, fasting blood glucose levels improved more in the intervention group than in the control group, although this difference was not statistically significant (Figure S45). Cho et al. reported a reduction in fasting blood glucose in patients [24]. One RCT and two quasi-experimental studies reported changes in glycosylated hemoglobin (HbA1c, [%]) before and after the intervention. Two RCT trials and three quasi-experimental studies reported on the homeostasis model assessment for insulin resistance (HOMA2-IR) index.

Three RCTs [17, 26, 40] and four quasi-experimental studies [18, 21, 25, 27] reported lipid indicators, including TC, TG, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL), and LDL-C [17]. Although not statistically significant, the pooled lipid indicators of the intervention group improved more than those of the control group based on RCTs (Fig. S46).

Three RCTs reported on changes in systolic and diastolic blood pressure in the intervention group and the control group [17, 26, 45]. Cho et al. reported the average systolic and diastolic blood pressure values of the intervention group increased 0.64 ± 17.21 mmHg and decreased 0.08 ± 9.69 mmHg, respectively [26]. Lim et al. reported the average systolic and diastolic blood pressure values of the intervention group decreased 12.40 ± 14.80 mmHg and 6.80 ± 8.90 mmHg, respectively [45]. Wang et al. reported the average systolic and diastolic blood pressure values of the intervention group decreased by –8.36 ± 1.61 mmHg and –6.6 ± 0.81 mmHg, respectively [17]. However, pooled effects were not clinically significant (Figure S47).

Quality of Life

The quality of life mainly reflects the subjective experiences and satisfaction of patients with MASLD for their physical, psychological, and social adaptation states. This was measured using the HRQOL-4 or SF-12. In one study, participants completed HRQOL-4 once at baseline and once at the end of the study, there was an average improvement of 2.2( ± 11.4) healthy days per month [58].

In another study, participants completed the SF-12 (physical health) and SF-12 (mental health) at baseline and 3-months. There was an improvement in the physical and mental health scores of 3.0( ± 3.0) and 0.5( ± 2.7)points, respectively.

Diet adherence

Policarpo et al. [47] reported that 93.3% of patients in the standard care group stated that “diet got worse” vs 6.7% in the intervention group (P < 0.01), and 35.3% vs 15.7% (P = 0.014) reported increases in appetite, respectively. The mean Mediterranean diet score during COVID-19 lockdown was 7.4 ± 2.8 points, with patients on the intervention group presenting higher scores (9.2 ± 1.9 vs 5.6 ± 2.4 points) in the STD group.

Physical activity and exercise

Freer et al. [41] reported changes in moderate-to-vigorous physical activity (MVPA) and 30 s sit-to-stand (STS) performance among patients with MASLD. Weekly MVPA increased on average by 86 min (95% CI: –117, 290) in the Pro-Ex group and 40 min (95% CI: −38, 118) in the control group at week 12. For physical function, the Pro-Ex group demonstrated a mean 4.0 (95% CI: 2.4, 5.6) increase in 30 s STS number at 12 weeks compared with a mean change of 1.3 (95% CI: 0.4, 2.3) in the control group, which represented a large (d = 1.29) effect. Kaewdech et al. [43]. indicated that a significant improvement in HGS (median: 0.7; interquartile range (IQR): –0.7 to 3, P = 0.014) was observed only in the intervention group, not in the control group. Motz et al. [60] reported that all patients with MASLD completed over 80% (mean: 84%; Standard Deviation: 3%) of exercise sessions.

GRADE certainty of evidence

Among the primary outcomes, “waist circumference”, “ALT”, and “AST” were rated as moderate-certainty evidence; “body weight loss”, “BMI”, “GGT”, “improvement and cure rate” and “self-management” were rated as low-certainty evidence; and “body weight loss percentage” was rated as very low-certainty evidence. The downgrading of the majority of outcomes was primarily due to risk of bias (e.g., deviations from intended interventions) and inconsistency (substantial statistical heterogeneity across studies), while “body weight loss percentage” was further downgraded to very low-certainty evidence due to imprecision (the 95% CI of the pooled RR crossed the null). Detailed GRADE assessment is provided in Supplementary file 3.

Publication bias

The funnel plot for the primary outcome (change in body weight) was presented in Fig. S49 (Supplementary 2). Visual inspection of the funnel plot showed slight asymmetry, with a paucity of small studies on the right side (favoring the eHealth lifestyle intervention group).

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was conducted for each meta-analysis using the leave-one-out method, and forest plots were generated. As shown in Fig. 10, for both AST and ALT, excluding the study by Wang et al. substantially reduced heterogeneity, with I² decreasing from 96% to 50% for AST and from 89% to 60% for ALT. The pooled standardized mean difference for AST changed from –0.90 (95% CI: –1.67, –0.12) to –0.37 (95% CI: –0.60, –0.15), and for ALT from –0.64 (95% CI: –1.05, –0.22) to –0.44 (95% CI: –0.67, –0.20). In contrast, removal of any other study maintained consistently high heterogeneity (I² ≥ 96% for AST; I² ≥ 90% for ALT) and yielded similar effect estimates (AST range: –1.02 to –0.86; ALT range: –0.70 to –0.56), indicating that no single study unduly influenced the overall estimates, confirming the robustness of the meta-analysis findings. Discussion

This systematic review provides a comprehensive synthesis of the most recent evidence regarding the effectiveness and implementation of eHealth lifestyle interventions for the management of MASLD. Accumulating evidence over the past 5 years has demonstrated the efficacy of eHealth lifestyle interventions in MASLD management, driving increased clinical adoption.

This review aimed to qualitatively summarize and characterize the composition of eHealth lifestyle interventions for MASLD. We systematically cataloged components across existing eHealth lifestyle intervention protocols. The following seven components existed simultaneously or partially in all protocols: goal setting, health education, self-monitoring and device monitoring, consultation, patient reminders, feedback, and social support. Some components, such as goal setting, self-monitoring, device monitoring, health education, appeared in >80% of protocols. Although these components are integral to traditional lifestyle interventions, eHealth-based delivery offers critical advancements through technological enablement. First, self-monitoring is transformed into a dynamic and visually intuitive process via mobile health application interfaces, which provide real-time feedback to patients, markedly enhancing the continuity and tangibility of behavioral change [19, 45]. Health education is delivered through multimodal formats (e.g., text messages [42, 43], infographics [43], video clips [19, 43, 45, 59, 61], and gamified elements [18]) beyond traditional pamphlets, significantly improving comprehensibility and accessibility across diverse health literacy levels. Importantly, in studies utilizing AI algorithms, participants received immediate dietary evaluations and personalized meal-by-meal guidance, which mitigated cognitive barriers and facilitated adherence to dietary protocols, thereby promoting long-term compliance [16]. In this study, 25 out of 28 studies included dietary and exercise knowledge and guidance in the health education intervention module, indicating that the view that MASLD management requires comprehensive guidance is no longer controversial in clinical practice [10].

The second objective was to quantitatively evaluate, through a meta-analysis, the effectiveness of eHealth interventions compared with standard care on primary clinical outcomes and secondary behavioral changes in patients with MASLD. Our findings (SMD = –0.93) demonstrate a significant reduction in body weight, which aligns with the results reported by Albhaisi et al. [62] (MD = –3.4 kg) and Kwon et al. [28] (SMD = –0.45). The greater effect size in our study may be attributed to the inclusion of more recent trials [16, 17, 26, 42] with improved digital intervention designs, such as AI-driven personalized nutrition and integrated wearable technologies, which enhance patients’ engagement and adherence. In terms of BMI reduction, our results (SMD = –0.71) are consistent with an earlier meta-analysis on lifestyle intervention effects by Katsagoni et al. [10], who reported an SMD of –0.66 for combined diet and exercise interventions. However, a previous meta-analysis by Kwon et al. [28] on eHealth interventions reported a more modest reduction in BMI (SMD= –0.56) compared to the effect observed in our study, which may be attributed to differences in intervention components such as remote consultation, patient reminders, interaction, and social support [26, 43, 45]. Waist circumference is a simple and more accessible indicator with performance similar to that of the fatty liver index (FLI); our analysis showed a significant reduction in weight circumference (SMD = –0.70), which is notably larger than the effect reported by Katsagoni et al. [10] (SMD = –0.60). This suggests that eHealth lifestyle interventions may be particularly effective in reducing central adiposity, possibly owing to features such as continuous monitoring, regular reminders, and goal setting that promote consistent physical activity and dietary adherence. Concerning liver enzymes, our study revealed substantial improvements in ALT (SMD= –0.64) and AST (SMD = –0.90). These effects were markedly stronger than those reported by Katsagoni et al. [10] for diet and exercise interventions (ALT: SMD = –0.58; AST: SMD = –0.35), suggesting that eHealth lifestyle interventions may more effectively mitigate hepatocellular injury than lifestyle intervention. Moreover, these effects were stronger than those reported by Kwon et al. [28] (ALT: SMD = –0.67; AST: SMD = –0.72). GGT improvement was not summarized in previous reviews on the effectiveness of e-Health lifestyle interventions in patients with MASLD [28, 29, 62]. Nonetheless, the significant reduction in GGT (SMD = –0.48) in our study is a notable finding, as this marker was not significantly improved in the meta-analysis performed by Katsagoni et al. [10]. As GGT is not only a marker of hepatobiliary function but is also associated with oxidative stress and metabolic syndrome, its improvement suggests that eHealth lifestyle interventions may confer systemic metabolic benefits among patients with MASLD. The RR value (RR = 3.45) of the disease improvement rate of the eHealth lifestyle intervention group compared to that of the control group more intuitively demonstrates the superiority of the eHealth lifestyle intervention. This outcome underscores the clinical relevance of eHealth lifestyle interventions in facilitating disease remission, likely through improved patient engagement, self-management, and sustained behavioral changes.

Despite these positive findings, the subgroup analyses based on country, intervention duration, and specific intervention components did not reveal any significant differences. This absence of subgroup effects suggests that the core efficacy of eHealth interventions may be generalizable across diverse geographic and socioeconomic contexts and that the benefits are primarily driven by fundamental components such as health education and self-monitoring rather than specific additive features. This implies flexibility for real-world adaptation; a simple app with automated reminders could suffice in resource-limited settings, whereas integrated coaching may better serve complex cases.

In addition to anthropometric parameters and liver enzymes, we summarized metabolic outcomes in the limited number of studies available—an aspect not previously addressed in earlier reviews of eHealth interventions. Few RCTs (3/13, 23.1%) and quasi-experimental trials (4/15, 26.7%) measured metabolic indicators and lipid indicators. Based on RCTs, the decrease in total cholesterol in eHealth lifestyle intervention group was better than that in the control group. However, contrary to expectations, there was no significant difference in the degree of blood glucose reduction, triglyceride reduction, LDL-C reduction, or HDL-C increase between the eHealth lifestyle intervention and standard care groups. Moreover, there were no statistically significant between-group differences in the pooled effect size of the 3 studies that reported on changes in diastolic and systolic blood pressure before and after the intervention. We consider that there is a time lag between behavioral change and improvement in these indicators.

Patient compliance with guidance on healthy living is directly related to improvements in clinical outcomes [11, 63, 64]. Patients in the eHealth lifestyle intervention group had higher levels of self-management and dietary compliance (e.g., adherence to Mediterranean diet patterns) and increased weekly physical activity time than those in the control group. However, the lack of a unified, comprehensive scoring system for healthy lifestyle behaviors has hampered consistent quantitative synthesis of the relationship between behavioral change intensity and clinical improvement. Future research should consider quantifying behavioral changes to better predict the clinical improvement of MASLD and related metabolic disorders.

A key strength of this review is the detailed categorization of intervention components into core and supplementary elements, providing insights into the active ingredients of eHealth interventions. Through subgroup analysis, we initially inferred that the core components were the main reason for the greater improvement in the patients’ anthropometric and clinical indicators. A distinctive strength of our review lies in the synthesis of evidence regarding waist circumference, GGT improvement, and MASLD disease improvement rate. In addition, we attempted to aggregate the combined effect sizes of other metabolic indicators and behavioral changes.

Although we characterized the intervention components and outcomes across quasi-experimental studies, substantial heterogeneity in the outcome measurements precluded quantitative meta-analysis of this subset. A key limitation of this meta-analysis is the substantial statistical heterogeneity observed in primary outcomes. Firstly, regarding interventions, although all were eHealth-based, there was considerable variation in their active components. Secondly, pertaining to participants, the included populations differed in age (e.g., participants in Wang et al. were notably younger), baseline disease severity (from simple steatosis to NASH), comorbidities (e.g., prevalence of type 2 diabetes), and ethnicity or geographic setting; however, subgroup analyses by ethnicity were constrained by data availability, limiting assessment of cross-cultural generalizability. Finally, methodological differences, particularly in the intervention duration (ranging from 8 weeks to 12 months) and the definition of standard care protocols (from passive leaflet distribution to regular clinic visits), are also potential sources of heterogeneity. Meta-regression was not performed due to the insufficient number of studies (fewer than ten) to yield reliable estimates. Although we employed a random-effects model and conducted subgroup analyses to explore these factors, residual heterogeneity persisted, suggesting the influence of other unmeasured confounders. Consequently, the pooled effect estimates should be interpreted as a range of possible effects rather than a single precise value, and while the findings are likely most robust in Asian cohorts, validation in non-Asian populations is warranted in future studies.

This review provides a detailed characterization of the seven eHealth lifestyle intervention components. Based on RCTs, the meta-analysis provided strong evidence that eHealth lifestyle interventions significantly improved key clinical outcomes, including reductions in body weight, BMI, waist circumference, and liver enzyme levels (ALT, AST, and GGT). These improvements underscore the potential of eHealth interventions in mitigating MASLD progression. Furthermore, it has been proposed that the effectiveness of eHealth lifestyle interventions over standard care does not vary because of differences in countries and regions, study sample sizes, and some intervention components.

The core principle of eHealth lifestyle interventions is to make a healthy lifestyle the easiest choice for patients. Achieving natural behavioral transformation by reducing operational resistance, strengthening medical system support, and eliminating adherence barriers are crucial. Future research should focus on evaluating long-term sustainability, cost-effectiveness, and developing dynamic artificial intelligence algorithms to power the next generation of scalable, precise, and potent eHealth tools for MASLD management.
