Evidencing the capability of Debaryomyces hansenii for using VFAs from organic digestates as feedstock
Authors: Mònica Estrada, Elia Tomás-Pejó, José L. Martínez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundVolatile fatty acids (VFAs) have emerged as promising alternative substrates to replace sugars (such as glucose) for microbial production, which can help reduce production costs. Moreover, VFAs can be sustainably produced through anaerobic fermentation (AF) of organic waste streams, revalorizing these by-products and thus contributing to the transition toward a circular bio-based economy. However, finding microorganisms able to tolerate and consume VFAs is a crucial need to achieve this goal. This study assesses, for the first time, the ability of the halotolerant and oleaginous non-conventional yeast Debaryomyces hansenii (D. hansenii) to metabolize VFAs as the sole carbon and energy source, using different total VFAs concentrations and acid profiles in synthetic media as well as microalgae VFAs-rich digestates.ResultsD. hansenii was able to grow with up to 15 g/L of total VFAs and metabolize all of them when acetic acid was the main acid present in the medium. An improved growth of the yeast was observed when using an organic VFAs-rich digestate as a feedstock derived from the AF of microalgae biomass, compared to growth in synthetic media. In this case, a biomass yield of 0.43 g/g was achieved. Finally, the capability of D. hansenii to produce and accumulate lipids from VFAs was assessed, showing that when the C:N ratio of the medium was increased to 200, the yeast accumulated up to 20.87% w/w of lipids, being oleic, palmitic, and linoleic acids the primary fatty acids produced. Interestingly, the odd-chain fatty acid C17 was also detected.ConclusionsOur study shows the potential of D. hansenii for bioconversion of VFAs, particularly from organic digestates, into industrially high-value compounds such as lipids. This particular yeast exhibits a preference for acetic acid and the ability to metabolize a wide range of VFAs when this acid is supplied in higher concentrations than the others. Our research moreover highlights the importance of C:N ratio and VFA composition for lipid accumulation in Debaryomyces and furthermore provides a foundation for further exploration of D. hansenii as a suitable candidate for sustainable bioproduction of microbial oils.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13068-026-02761-7.

Introduction

Most of the research on the microbial production of valuable bioproducts relies on conventional carbon sources like glucose, which are expensive and compete with essential food resources [1–3]. The use of glucose as feedstock can account for up to 60–80% of the overall production cost, highlighting the need for more sustainable and cost-effective alternatives [4, 5]. One promising option is to utilize volatile fatty acids (VFAs) as an alternative carbon and energy source for microorganisms’ growth. VFAs, also known as short-chain fatty acids, include a group of aliphatic monocarboxylic acids that contain between two and six carbon atoms: acetic (C2), propionic (C3), iso-butyric (C4), butyric (C4), iso-valeric (C5), valeric (C5), iso-hexanoic (C6), and hexanoic (C6) acids [6]. They are valuable compounds for the chemical industry, which can be used for food preservation, cosmetics, textiles, and pharmaceuticals [4] and have been traditionally synthesized from fossil-based resources through petrochemical pathways. However, to replace petroleum-based compounds and foster a bio-based economy, VFAs can also be sustainably produced from organic waste streams through a shortened anaerobic digestion (AD) process called anaerobic fermentation (AF) [4, 7, 8].

AD is a process where complex organic matter is degraded into methane and CO2 through four steps: hydrolysis, acidogenesis, acetogenesis, and methanogenesis, with VFAs as intermediate compounds produced during the acidogenesis phase. However, by tuning operational parameters of the AD, such as pH, temperature, organic loading rate, or hydraulic retention time, the VFAs-consuming microorganisms involved in the last two stages of the AD (archaea, which can only metabolize acetate, and bacteria, responsible for breaking down the VFAs into acetate) can be inhibited, stopping the AD process after the acidogenesis. This strategy is defined as AF, and its resulting effluent is a digestate rich in VFAs, whose composition depends on the feedstock used [4, 9].

Apart from being used in the chemical industry, VFAs can also serve as an alternative low-cost carbon source for some microorganisms to grow and produce valuable bioproducts [8]. However, the main challenge in this process is to find species able to metabolize them and which are not hindered by their toxic effects [10, 11]. VFAs-rich digestates typically contain high concentrations of short-chain VFAs, which are generally less toxic and easier to metabolize for some microorganisms, and lower concentrations of long-chain VFAs, which are more toxic and can inhibit microbial growth [7, 8, 12]. As a result, VFAs-rich organic digestates have significant potential as a sustainable alternative feedstock. Indeed, such digestates have already been used as an economical substrate to produce microbial oils with some oleaginous yeast species. Morales-Palomo et al., (2022) used VFAs-rich digestates derived from food-waste streams to produce up to 43.4% w/w lipids with Yarrowia lipolytica. In another study, Llamas et al., (2020a) screened the ability of five different oleaginous yeasts to accumulate lipids using a VFAs-rich digestate derived from the AF of microalgae biomass. Promising results were obtained using Cutaneotrichosporon curvatum and Cyberlindnera saturnus, which reached lipid accumulation values up to 36.9% and 33.9% w/w lipids, respectively [12]. However, finding other microorganisms capable of efficiently metabolizing VFAs has been a recent research focus.

Debaryomyces hansenii is a promising candidate for this purpose. It is a halotolerant, osmotolerant and xerotolerant non-conventional yeast isolated from environments with low water activities (seawater, cheese, or cured meat, among others) [13, 14]. Salt seems to exert a protective effect, making D. hansenii resilient to adverse conditions like extreme temperatures, pH fluctuations, and oxidative stress [15–17], which distinguishes it from the above-mentioned microorganisms. This yeast can metabolize a broad range of carbon sources apart from glucose, such as lactose, xylose, or glycerol [18], but its intrinsic capacity to use VFA as a carbon source has never been explored. It moreover tolerates a wide range of temperatures and pH and it is able to grow in the presence of fermentation inhibitors, like furfural or vanillin [19]. All these intrinsic capabilities suggest that D. hansenii is well suited to grow using complex waste streams as feedstock, including AF digestates, particularly those derived from salt-rich organic biomass such as algae.

In addition to recombinant protein production [20, 21], D. hansenii could potentially be used for microbial oil production, as it has been described as an oleaginous yeast [18]. However, there is limited information about D. hansenii’s lipid profile and the metabolic pathways implied.

Thus, the present study aimed to assess, for the first time, D. hansenii’s capability to metabolize VFAs and to grow on VFAs-rich organic digestates, with the potential to reuse these waste streams as substrates for the production of valuable lipids. The consumption of individual VFAs, as well as mixtures, was analyzed at different concentrations and ratios in synthetic media. Parameters such as yeast growth, potential VFAs toxicity, the influence of the VFAs profile, and different total VFAs concentrations were evaluated. Additionally, the feasibility of using a VFAs-rich organic digestate derived from the AF of microalgae biomass as feedstock for D. hansenii’s growth was analyzed. Our study additionally explored the potential of D. hansenii to produce lipids from VFAs. Increasing the C:N ratio of the media was analyzed as a possible strategy to achieve higher lipid yields. Moreover, the lipid profile and fatty acids composition of D. hansenii were also revealed [8, 22]. Our study demonstrates the high potential of D. hansenii to produce valuable bioproducts, such as microbial oils, from VFAs-rich organic digestates produced through AF of organic wastes, which represents a promising strategy for transitioning from a linear to a circular economy.

Materials and methods

Yeast strain and standard pre-culture conditions

D. hansenii strain IBT22 from the Institut for Bioteknologi (IBT) at the Technical University of Denmark was used in this study. Cells were plated from glycerol stocks (20% final glycerol concentration) stored at -80 °C into yeast extract–peptone–dextrose (YPD) plates containing 1% yeast extract, 2% peptone, 2% agar, and 2.2% of D-( +)-glucose monohydrate (Sigma-Aldrich, Germany). From YPD agar plates, liquid pre-cultures were performed in 250-mL baffled Erlenmeyer shake flasks (100 mL working volume) by inoculating a colony in YPD medium (same composition as mentioned above but without agar). Yeast cells were incubated overnight in a rotary shaker at 28 °C and 170 rpm until the cultures reached the late exponential growth phase.

The pH of all media was adjusted to 6.0 with 2 M NaOH, and all solutions were autoclaved at 121 °C for 20 min. Glucose was autoclaved separately and added to the media afterward.

D. hansenii’s cultivations performed in this study

Throughout this study, D. hansenii was cultivated in different media and at multiple laboratory scales. Microcultivations (1.5 mL) were performed using the Biolector II to enable high-throughput screening of D. hansenii growth in synthetic media containing individual VFAs. This experiment allowed a rapid assessment of its ability to tolerate and potentially metabolize each VFA. However, at this small scale, it was not possible to take samples during cultivation to monitor VFAs’ consumption.

Shake-flask experiments at higher working volumes (50 and 100 mL) were performed to monitor both yeast growth and VFAs consumption over time. At this scale, both synthetic media (SM) with different VFAs profiles (Table 1 and 2) and a real VFAs-rich digestate derived from the AF of microalgae biomass (Table 3) were evaluated. In addition, a Design-of-Experiments (DoE) approach was conducted to deeply understand the influence of each individual VFA on D. hansenii’s growth. D. hansenii’s microcultivations in BioLector II

BioLector II (Beckman Coulter, Germany) was used to perform microcultivations (1.5 mL working volume) of D. hansenii IBT22 in biological triplicates. A 48-well FlowerPlate (MTP-48-B, m2p Labs, Germany) was used to cultivate the cells at 28 °C and 1000 rpm. The filter: biomass (gain 3) was selected, the humidity was maintained at > 85% using distilled H2O, and the oxygen supply was the atmospheric concentration.

Yeast pre-cultures were incubated overnight at 28 °C and 170 rpm. Then, cells were washed twice with distilled H2O, and inoculated in the corresponding well with an initial OD600 of 0.1.

Different culture media were used for cultivations. All of them contained 6.7 g/L of complete Yeast Nitrogen Base (YNB) medium (Thermo Fisher) and a specific VFA as a carbon source: acetic, propionic, butyric, valeric or hexanoic acids (Sigma-Aldrich). Acetic acid was tested at concentrations of 1, 5, 10, and 20 g/L, while the longer-chain VFAs were tested at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 g/L. Media containing valeric or hexanoic acids were supplemented with 5 g/L glucose to assess the potential toxicity of these acids on D. hansenii.

Potassium phthalate monobasic 99.5% (20.4 g/L, Sigma-Aldrich, Germany) was added to all media and used as a pH buffer. The pH was adjusted to 6.0 with 2 M NaOH. All solutions were filter-sterilized using 0.22 µm filters.

D. hansenii’s shake-flask cultivation in VFAs-rich synthetic media

Biological triplicates of D. hansenii IBT22 were cultivated in 250-mL baffled shake flasks with a working volume of 100 mL. Different SM (named from SM 1 to SM 11) were used for cultivations, as summarized in Table 1. All of them contained YNB medium (1.7 g/L) (CONDA Pronadisa), ammonium sulfate (7.5 g/L) (Sigma-Aldrich), and different combinations of VFAs as a carbon source. All SM were prepared to mimic VFAs profiles and concentrations commonly obtained in real VFAs-rich organic digestates derived from the AF of organic residues [8, 23]. The pH of all media was adjusted to 6.0 with 2 M NaOH and the solutions were filter-sterilized using 0.22 µm filters.

A colony of D. hansenii IBT22 was pre-cultured overnight in YPD. Then, cells were washed with distilled H2O and inoculated in the corresponding shake flask (containing a specific SM) with an initial OD600 of 1 (equivalent to 0.44 g DW cells/L). Yeast cultures were incubated in a rotary shaker at 170 rpm and 28 °C during the fermentation, and the shake flasks were covered with a gas-permeable lid to avoid oxygen limitation. The pH was measured every 12 h with pH strips. As cells grew and consumed VFAs, the pH of the media increased and it was readjusted manually to 6.0 every 12 h of cultivation by adding 2 M H2SO4. Samples were taken throughout the cultivations to measure the OD600, and the consumption of VFAs.

The capability of D. hansenii IBT22 to produce lipids from VFAs was studied at different C:N ratios (80, 120, 160, and 200). Design of experiment methodology (DoE)

To delve into how each particular VFA influences D. hansenii’s behavior when growing in a mix of VFAs, a Design-of-Experiments (DoE) was performed. The program JMP Pro 16 (JMP Statistical Discovery LCC, Cary, North Carolina) was used, and a two-level fractional factorial design that included five continuous factors: acetic, propionic, butyric, valeric, and hexanoic acid concentrations was selected [24]. This model analyzes the impact of each individual acid but also how combinations of two acids, known as 2-factor interactions, either positively or negatively affect D. hansenii’s growth.

Sixteen runs with different SM compositions (summarized in Table 2) were performed as determined by the JMP software. Maximum and minimum concentration values were chosen for each specific acid (SM Table 1). The maximum values were selected based on previous experiments to ensure that the concentration of each individual acid, as well as the total VFAs, was not high enough to be toxic for the yeast. The minimum concentration values were, in any case, zero, as the VFAs-rich organic digestates derived from AF always contain traces of the five VFAs. Therefore, all media mimicked potential VFAs profiles of real digestates, which typically have high concentrations of short-chain VFAs, and low concentrations of long-chain VFAs.

The 16 media contained YNB (17 g/L), ammonium sulfate (7.5 g/L), and the specific combination of VFAs in each case. The pH was adjusted to 6.0 and all solutions were filter-sterilized using 0.22 µm filters.

Pre-cultures of D. hansenii were incubated overnight. Then, cells were washed with distilled H2O and inoculated in 125-mL baffled shake flasks (50 mL working volume) with the corresponding culture media to have an initial OD600 of 1. Yeast cultures were incubated in a shaker at 170 rpm and 28 °C for 310 h, and all the shake flasks were covered with a gas-permeable lid to avoid oxygen limitation. As the cells grew and consumed VFAs, the pH of the media increased (measured with pH strips) but was readjusted manually to 6.0 every 12 h of cultivation by adding H2SO4. Samples were taken throughout the cultivations to measure OD600 and the consumption of VFAs.

The JMP software was also used to analyze the obtained results. The percentage of total VFAs consumed in each medium at specific time points during the cultivation (24, 72, 144, and 310 h) was calculated and used as a response variable.

D. hansenii’s cultivation in a microalgae VFAs-rich digestate

D. hansenii IBT22 was also cultivated in the liquid fraction of a VFAs-rich digestate derived from the AF of microalgae biomass. Chlorella vulgaris biomass was purchased from Allmicroalgae (Portugal), and the AF was carried out in 1-L continuous stirred tank reactors under semi-continuous feeding mode as previously described in the literature [12, 25]. The VFAs composition of the microalgae digestate (the liquid fraction) is compiled in Table 3. However, the media was supplemented with acetic acid (marked with an asterisk in the table) to enhance D. hansenii’s metabolism. Before being used, the digestate was centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min, the pH was adjusted to 6.0, and it was filter-sterilized using a 0.22-µm filter.

Biological triplicates of D. hansenii IBT22 were cultivated in 250-mL baffled shake flasks (100 mL working volume) using the VFAs-rich microalgae digestate as feedstock (Table 3).

Yeast pre-cultures were carried out overnight. Then, cells were washed with distilled H2O and inoculated into the corresponding shake flask with an initial OD600 of 1. All shake flasks were covered with a gas-permeable lid to avoid oxygen limitation. Yeast cultures were incubated at 28 °C and 170 rpm for 210 h. The pH was measured every 12 h with pH strips and manually readjusted to 6.0 by adding H2SO4. Samples were taken throughout the cultivations to measure the OD600, and the consumption of VFAs.

The growth and acid consumption of D. hansenii in the VFAs-rich microalgae digestate (Table 3) were also assessed in a controlled 1-L bioreactor (Biostat A, Sartorius), with a 500-mL working volume. The bioreactor was inoculated with an overnight yeast pre-culture at an initial OD600 of 1, and a batch fermentation was performed.

The temperature in the bioreactor was kept at 28 °C, the stirring was set at 600 rpm, the air flow rate was set at 1 vvm during the entire cultivation to avoid oxygen limitation, and the pH was controlled by pH sensors (EasyFerm Bio HB MS 160, Hamilton) and maintained at 6.0 by the automatic addition of 2 M NaOH/H2SO4.

Samples were taken throughout the cultivations to measure the OD600 and the consumption of VFAs.

Analytical VFAs determination

The concentration of VFAs was determined by liquid chromatography (Agilent 1260 HPLC-RID, Santa Clara, CA, USA) using an Aminex HPX-87H ion exclusion column (300 × 7–8 mm I.D.) (Biorad), and a Cation H Refill Cartridge Microguard column (Biorad, Hercules, CA, USA). The injection volume was 20 µL, the mobile phase was 5 mM H2SO4, the oven temperature was set at 25 °C, the detector was set at 35 °C, and the elution was performed in isocratic mode at a 0.6 mL/min flow rate.

Cell growth measurement

To monitor the growth of the yeast during the cultivations, OD600 was measured using SPECTROstar OMEGA (BMG Labtech).

Lipid extraction and quantification

D. hansenii’s total lipids production (% of lipids per dry weight) and fatty acids composition were analyzed by gas chromatography (GC-FID). Yeast cultures were centrifuged at 5000 rpm for 5 min. Then, the supernatant was discarded and the pellet was washed twice with distilled H2O. Yeast cells were frozen at -80 °C and freeze-dried. The freeze-dried biomass was ground to a fine powder. Then, 10 mg of powder was mixed with 50 µL of carbon-13 (C13) (used as a standard), 400 µL of a mix of chloroform and methanol (2:1 v/v), and 600 µL of a mix of 0.6 M HCl and methanol. After mixing, the samples were heated at 90 °C for 1 h. Once the samples were at room temperature, 2 mL of hexane was added to each one and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. At this point, two liquid phases were formed (methanol at the top and chloroform at the bottom, where the lipids were retained). Finally, enough volume of the bottom phase (chloroform with the lipids) was transferred to a vial for GC measurement.

C13 was used to perform a calibration line, which was mixed with hexane at different known increasing concentrations (0.02, 0.04, 0,08, 0.2, 0.4 g/L) in 1 mL vials.

Samples were analyzed in an Agilent 7890A GC System (GC-FID) using an HP-88 column (60 m × 0.32 mm) and an FID detector. Helium was used as the carrier gas at a 4.19 mL/min flow rate. The injector temperature was 250 °C and the detector was 280 °C. The oven’s initial temperature was 50 °C with a hold time of 1 min. Then, the temperature was increased to 175 °C at a rate of 25 °C /min, and to 230 °C at a rate of 4 °C /min. Each sample was run for 24.75 min.

Statistical analysis

OriginPro (OriginLab Corporation, version 2022, USA) was used to compare the biomass yields, lipid concentrations, and lipid yields obtained in this study. One-way ANOVA Tukey test (p < 0.05 confidence) was performed to find whether the values exhibited statistically significant differences among them.

D. hansenii was able to metabolize up to 20 g/L of acetic acid and used it to grow, as higher biomass levels were achieved with increased acid concentrations. Nevertheless, increasing the acetic acid concentration resulted in extended lag phases showing the longest lag phase in the presence of 20 g/L acetic acid, as illustrated in Fig. 2A.

Similar results were observed when the yeast grew at higher concentrations of propionic and butyric acids (Fig. 2B and 2C). In both cases, the yeast could grow at the highest tested concentration (5 g/L). However, in the case of propionic acid, lower biomass levels were achieved at the end of the cultivation when the acid concentration increased to 2, 3, 4, and 5 g/L compared to 1 g/L. Besides, longer lag phases were observed with 4 g/L and 5 g/L of acid (Fig. 2B). In the case of butyric acid, this effect was intensified. Although the yeast could grow in the presence of 4 g/L and 5 g/L of butyric acid, the lag phases observed were threefold longer than when growing with 2 g/L (15 h vs 45 h). Additionally, the biomass levels reached at the end of the cultivation were up to fivefold lower than with 1 g/L (Fig. 2C).

In the case of valeric acid, the yeast was able to grow in the presence of 1, 2, and 3 g/L of the acid when combined with 5 g/L of glucose (Fig. 2D). This indicated that, although the yeast could not metabolize valeric acid as a sole carbon source during 72-h cultivation (Fig. 1), concentrations up to 3 g/L were not toxic for the yeast when glucose was present in the media, as the yeast’s growth was not inhibited. However, concentrations above this threshold might inhibit the yeast, as no growth was observed even in the presence of glucose. Again, higher valeric acid concentrations resulted in lower biomass levels achieved at the end of the cultivation.

Similar results were observed for hexanoic acid. In this case, the yeast could grow when glucose and 1 g/L of the acid were present in the media, contrary to what was observed in Fig. 1, where hexanoic acid was the only carbon source present. Nevertheless, concentrations higher than 1 g/L were detrimental for the yeast, as no growth was observed during the cultivation time (72 h) (Fig. 2E).

D. hansenii’s cultivation using VFAs mixtures as carbon source

Since one goal of this study is to evaluate D. hansenii as a potential cell factory for revalorizing a VFAs-rich microalgae digestate (composition detailed in Table 3), an SM mimicking the same VFAs profile was designed (Table 1, SM 1), with a total VFAs concentration of 5 g/L. Discussion

This study constitutes the first evidence of D. hansenii’s ability to use VFAs as a sole carbon source to grow. However, significant differences were observed when using an individual VFA or a mixture of VFAs for yeast cultivation.

When individual VFAs were used as a substrate, the yeast was able to grow in the presence of up to 20 g/L of acetic acid, and 5 g/L of propionic and butyric acids that were completely metabolized for biomass production. Nevertheless, when increasing the concentration of the latter two acids, longer lag phases and lower biomass levels were observed (Fig. 2A, 2B and 2C). On the contrary, D. hansenii could only grow in the presence of up to 3 g/L of valeric and 1 g/L of hexanoic acids when an additional carbon source (glucose) was available in the medium (Fig. 2D and 2E). The yeast could not consume 1 g/L of these acids as a sole carbon source within 72 h of cultivation (Fig. 1). This suggests that D. hansenii may not be able to metabolize these acids, but they do not hamper the yeast’s growth at low concentrations. However, later experiments performed in shake flasks showed that the yeast can actually metabolize 1 g/L of hexanoic acid; it just needs more cultivation time (240 h) (Fig. 6). Nevertheless, concentrations of this acid higher than 1 g/L inhibit the yeast’s growth.

These results are consistent with previous reports in the literature on other yeast species about VFA toxicity, which relates to the lag phase’s duration and the biomass yields attained at the end of the cultivations. For example, Gao et al., (2017) concluded that the inhibitory effect of VFAs on Y. lipolytica becomes more pronounced with longer VFA chain lengths [10]. Llamas et al., (2020) also detected shorter lag phases when the yeast grew with acetic and propionic acids (4.5 h) compared to longer acids (7–9 h), being even more prolonged with hexanoic acid (120 h of lag phase) [7]. This pattern has been substantiated by other researchers [27, 28], and for other yeast species [29, 30]. Kolouchová et al., (2015) screened the growth of seven yeast strains (such as Candida or Trichosporon cutaneum) using either acetic or propionic acid as a carbon source. All yeast reached lower biomass yields with propionic acid than with acetic acid, providing further evidence for the reduced toxicity of shorter VFAs [29].

D. hansenii’s behavior was different when it grew with a mix of VFAs in SM. Regardless of the total VFAs concentration, 5 g/L (SM 1) or 10 g/L (SM 2), the yeast rapidly co-consumed the acetic and hexanoic acids (the shortest and longest VFAs), but it could almost not metabolize the rest of the acids (Fig. 3). Moreover, approximately twofold lower biomass yields were observed at a higher concentration of total VFAs (10 g/L) compared to 5 g/L. Although VFA mixtures at concentrations above 5 g/L have been reported to exert stress and inhibit yeast growth [1], the SM 2 medium containing 10 g/L VFAs did not impose inhibitory conditions on D. hansenii.

After increasing the concentration of acetic acid in the medium (SM 3), D. hansenii could also metabolize valeric, butyric, and propionic acids (with this order of preference), once acetic and hexanoic acids were depleted (Fig. 4). These results suggest a potential beneficial relation between the metabolism of acetic acid and the other VFAs by D. hansenii. Interestingly, the yeast only used acetic and hexanoic acids to produce biomass, resulting in a lower biomass yield than in SM 1 (0.31 ± 0.02 vs 0.55 ± 0.04). The consumption of the remaining acids was probably used by the cell for purposes other than biomass generation, such as maintenance or the production of macromolecules like lipids.

Finally, further increasing the ratio between acetic acid and the rest of the VFAs present in the medium (SM 4) did not boost the consumption of additional VFAs by D. hansenii (Fig. 5A). However, the yeast was not inhibited despite the high concentration of total VFAs present in SM 4 (15 g/L), unlike some other yeast species, such as Rhodotorula toruloides or Cyberlindnera saturnus [12].

Overall, these results indicate that the ability of D. hansenii to grow in VFAs-rich media is influenced by several factors: (1) the total concentration of VFAs present in the medium, which should be maintained below the inhibitory levels for the yeast; (2) the concentration of each particular VFA, which also has to remain lower than the inhibitory level for the yeast; and (3) the VFAs profile (the ratio between all VFAs available in the medium). This behavior has also been described for other yeast species. Fei et al., (2011) studied the growth of Cryptococcus albidus in four different acetic:propionic:butyric ratios (4:3:3, 8:1:1, 6:1:3, and 7:2:1). They observed a faster growth of the yeast in a ratio of 8:1:1, which contained more acetic acid [1].

Llamas et al., (2020) studied the growth of Y. lipolytica in two media containing 10.6 g/L of total VFAs, but one had a higher concentration of long-chain VFAs and the other of short-chain VFAs. The yeast showed a preference for short-chain VFAs. When acetic acid was the main VFA in the medium, Y. lipolytica was able to metabolize a greater quantity of valeric and hexanoic acids and at a higher consumption rate [7], as also observed with D. hansenii (Fig. 4).

These observations were even more evident when a fractional factorial DoE revealed which acids have a statistically significant impact on the total consumption of VFAs present in the medium by D. hansenii (Fig. 8). The analysis concluded that adding acetic acid into the medium is crucial for maximizing the total consumption of VFAs by the yeast since it significantly boosts its metabolism. On the other hand, longer-chain acids initially hinder its growth. However, none of the acids has a significantly negative effect on the yeast’s growth at the end of the cultivation, when its metabolism is already adapted to use VFAs as a carbon source.

Currently, to the best of our knowledge, no literature is available about the metabolic pathways employed by D. hansenii to metabolize VFAs. Moreover, information on how other yeast species do it, particularly with hexanoic acid, is limited and yet unclear. However, it is generally accepted that long-chain VFAs are assimilated via the ex novo pathway (as hydrophobic carbon sources) and undergo multiple biochemical transformations until they are cleaved into acetyl-CoA. This slows down the overall uptake rate [9, 11, 31]. Instead, several authors have hypothesized that acetic acid could be metabolized via the de novo pathway, similar to sugars, and cleaved directly into acetyl-CoA. This could explain the preference and faster metabolism of acetic acid by many yeast species, including D. hansenii [8, 10]. However, the ability of D. hansenii to rapidly consume other VFAs (mainly valeric and hexanoic) when acetic acid is predominant in the medium requires further investigation. It could be related to an interconnected membrane transport of these acids into the cell [9].

Thus, acetate is a critical intermediate that can be valorized either through lipid production by yeasts or through biomethane generation in anaerobic digestion processes. From a circular economy perspective, directing acetate toward biomethane prioritizes energy recovery and process robustness, supported by a stable and well-established market. In contrast, converting acetate into yeast lipids enables the production of higher-value bio-based materials, promotes longer carbon retention, and offers greater economic potential across multiple end-markets, albeit at the cost of higher operational complexity and more demanding downstream processing. On the other hand, this work also evaluated the potential use of VFAs-rich organic digestates as a feedstock for D. hansenii’s growth, avoiding the use of commercial medium, current water, and glucose. In this case, the digestate used was derived from the AF of microalgae biomass, and it mainly contained short-chain VFAs (acetic and propionic) and low concentrations of butyric, valeric, and hexanoic acids. This real digestate also contained the isoforms of butyric, valeric, and hexanoic acids, which are known to be toxic and poorly metabolizable by most microorganisms [11]. Remarkably, D. hansenii could grow in the microalgae digestate and metabolize all VFAs available, including the isoforms of the long-chain VFAs (Fig. 9). Indeed, the yeast grew faster in the organic digestate compared to synthetic media containing the same VFAs profile (SM 3). This could be because real digestates may contain additional nutrients like nitrogen, phosphorous, salts, and minerals that can improve the yeast’s growth.

As expected, D. hansenii showed a faster metabolism when grown in bioreactors compared to shake flasks. After 140 h of cultivation, the yeast had already consumed most of the VFAs and achieved higher biomass levels and yields (0.43 g/g in bioreactors vs 0.27 g/g in shake flasks). This might be attributed to the controlled conditions maintained during the cultivation process, which prevented the medium from reaching extreme alkaline pH levels when VFAs were consumed and ensured proper aeration throughout the process. Indeed, the biomass yield achieved by D. hansenii in the bioreactor (0.43 g/g) was higher than those obtained by Llamas et al. (2020) when growing five different yeast strains in a microalgae biomass digestate with a very similar VFAs profile [12].

Finally, this study evaluated the potential production of microbial oils by D. hansenii using VFAs as a sustainable carbon source. Oleaginous microorganisms are able to accumulate high concentrations of lipids per dry weight when cultivated under appropriate conditions. Although D. hansenii is considered an oleaginous yeast, very little research has been conducted to exploit its potential for microbial oil production. Moreover, the available studies have used glucose as a substrate. Usually, the accumulation of lipids in oleaginous yeasts increases when a key nutrient in the medium, such as nitrogen, is limited. Under nitrogen limitation, the excess carbon source is redirected toward lipid accumulation instead of cell growth [8, 32–34]. However, this comes at the expense of achieving lower biomass yields. For that reason, in this work, adjusting the C:N ratio has been tested as a possible strategy to boost lipid accumulation in D. hansenii.

The yeast demonstrated its ability to accumulate lipids when grown in a mix of VFAs (SM 3). However, the lipid percentages per dry weight obtained were generally low compared to other studies conducted with Y. lipolytica in similar growth conditions [8, 12] (Table 4). Raising the C:N ratio of the medium up to 160 or 200 significantly increased lipid production, which reached 20.87% w/w in the best scenario (when the C:N ratio was 200). This corresponded to a lipid yield on VFAs of 0.07 g/g. It has been extensively reported that, under nitrogen limitations, some species redirect the excess carbon source toward lipid production instead of cell growth [4, 9]. This behavior can now be extended to D. hansenii, although the optimal C:N ratio still needs to be determined. Achieving a high C:N ratio in real VFAs-rich digestates is often challenging because organic wastes typically contain substantial nitrogen levels. In this sense, struvite precipitation has been reported as an effective method to tune the C:N ratio in such digestates prior to yeast lipid production [35]

Regardless of the C:N ratio (80, 120, 160, 200), similar fatty acid profiles were observed (Fig. 10). D. hansenii mainly produced palmitic acid (C16:0), oleic acid (C18:1), and linoleic acid (C18:2). This aligns well with the fatty acid profiles observed by Llamas et al. (2020) for other yeast species growing in VFAs-rich media, such as Y. lipolytica, Cyberlindnera saturnus, or Rhodotorula toruloides. In all cases, up to 87% of the total lipids produced were C16 and C18 fatty acids. These are also the most abundant fatty acids in vegetable oils traditionally used by the industry [12].

Interestingly, heptadecanoic acid (C17:0 and C17:1) was also produced by D. hansenii, which is a high-value-added odd-chain fatty acid, which is unusually produced by oleaginous microorganisms from conventional substrates (like glucose) but has valuable applications in the therapeutic, nutritional and chemical industries [12, 31]. It has been recently hypothesized that the consumption of propionic acid can lead to the synthesis of odd-chain fatty acids (de Vicente et al., 2025).

Apart from increasing the C:N ratio of the medium, other strategies could be tested to improve lipid accumulation in D. hansenii. Morales-Palomo et al. (2023) recently proved that phosphate limitations also increase lipid production in Y. lipolytica [36]. However, adjusting nutrient concentrations can be challenging when using an organic digestate derived from AF as a substrate. In this case, process conditions should be optimized. For example, pH, temperature, aeration, agitation, nitrogen source and cultivation length are parameters that influence lipid production. Another alternative could be engineering D. hansenii to upregulate the expression of some genes involved in the lipid synthesis or to repress some involved in the lipid degradation pathways [1, 4].

All in all, this study provides a preliminary characterization of D. hansenii’s metabolic capability to utilize VFAs as the sole carbon source for growth. Moreover, it highlights the potential of this yeast as a candidate for the revalorization of VFAs-rich digestates, which are generated from the AF of organic waste streams. This approach supports the sustainable production of valuable bioproducts, such as microbial oils, and contributes to the development of a circular economy.
