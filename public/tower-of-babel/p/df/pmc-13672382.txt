Prophylaxis and management of thromboembolism in pnh patients: an italian survey
Authors: Anna Paola Iori, Rosario Notaro, Martina Salvatori, Camilla Frieri, Eros Di Bona, Antonio De Vivo, Simona Sica, Andrea Patriarca, Francesco Lanza, Alessandra Ricco, Fabio Giglio, Antonella Sau, Eloise Beggiato, Simona Raso, Alessandro Costa, Amalia Figuera, Maria Bruna Greve, Filippo Achille Brioschi, Marica Laurino, Giulio Giordano, Elisabetta Metafuni, Francesca D’adamo, Teresa Maria Santeramo, Angela Amendola, Giorgia Mancini, Bruno Fattizzo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractParoxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) is a rare clonal hematopoietic stem cell disorder characterized by uncontrolled complement-mediated intravascular hemolysis and a disproportionately high thromboembolic risk. Although terminal complement inhibition (CIT) has substantially modified the disease course, reducing thromboembolic events (TE), the residual risk remains elevated and evidence-based anticoagulation guidelines are lacking. To characterize real- world practice, we conducted a nationwide, cross-sectional survey involving 24 Italian Centers caring for 291 patients, evaluating strategies for primary antithrombotic prophylaxis (PAP), secondary prophylaxis (SAP), and management of breakthrough hemolysis (BTH). Marked heterogeneity emerged. PAP prior to CIT was routinely employed in only one-third of Centers, whereas an equal proportion did not use it; the remainder adopted risk-based approaches integrating thrombophilia traits or clone size. SAP was universally administered after TE, with 75% of Centers maintaining indefinite anticoagulation regardless of CIT response. Anticoagulation during BTH was likewise variable, with LMWH as the preferred agent and duration largely dictated by biochemical resolution. These findings demonstrate substantial inter-center variability, underscoring the lack of harmonized national frameworks. The results highlight the need for structured expert consensus and standardized clinical algorithms to optimize thrombosis prevention and management in PNH in the era of complement inhibition.Keywords: Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria, Venous thromboembolism, Complement inhibition, Antithrombotic prophylaxis

Introduction

Paroxysmal nocturnal hemoglobinuria (PNH) is a rare, acquired, clonal, non-neoplastic hematopoietic stem cell disorder characterized by complement-mediated intravascular hemolysis, bone marrow failure, and increased risk of thromboembolic events (TE) [1–3]. Before the use of Complement inhibition therapy (CIT), thrombosis was the leading cause of morbidity and mortality in affected patients [4], prompting PNH to be defined as “the most vicious acquired thrombophilic state known in medicine” [5]. The pathogenesis of thrombosis in PNH is multifactorial, involving several not mutually exclusive mechanisms, including complement-mediated platelets and coagulation activation, release of prothrombotic factors secondary to hemolysis, impaired fibrinolytic activity, and endothelial dysfunction [6].

Indeed, in the pre-complement inhibition therapy (CIT) era, the lifetime risk of thrombosis in individuals with PNH was estimated to range between 40% and 50% [7–10].

Thrombotic events accounted for up to 67% of PNH-related deaths [4, 11, 12] and patients presenting with thrombosis at diagnosis had a 4-year overall survival rate of only 40% [13].

Furthermore, thrombotic episodes in PNH were often unpredictable, sometimes presenting as catastrophic events (e.g., those involving abdominal and cerebral veins at onset) [6, 14, 15], and could even arise in patients receiving adequate anticoagulation therapy [6].

The advent of CIT has significantly transformed the natural history of PNH by effectively controlling hemolysis, and reducing the risk of TE from 7.37 events/100 patient-years prior to eculizumab treatment to 1.07 events/100 patient-years with CIT (P < .001) [7–10, 16]. However, considering that the risk of thromboembolic events in the general population is estimated at only 1 to 2 per 1,000 person-years in Western countries, the residual risk in PNH patients remains significantly elevated even during CIT. Thus, managing thrombosis remains a major clinical challenge, largely due to the lack of standardized guidelines [16–21]. Although direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) have emerged as a potential alternative to vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) in several thromboembolic conditions [19, 21], data on their safety and efficacy in PNH remain limited, leaving a significant gap in evidence-based clinical decision-making [22–25].

Therefore, despite the evolving therapeutic landscapes—marked by the integration of complement inhibitors and novel anticoagulant agents—significant unmet clinical needs persist regarding the optimal management of thrombotic risk in PNH.

This study aimed to evaluate current practices and institutional policies concerning the prophylaxis and management of thrombotic complications in patients with PNH across Italian Centers. By mapping the national approach to thrombosis in PNH, the study seeks to identify shared practices and existing disparities, thereby providing a foundation for the development of standardized and harmonized clinical strategies.

Methods

A cross-sectional survey was conducted between September 2024 and January 2025 to assess thrombosis management practices among Italian Centers that follow and treat PNH patients, provided that at least one patient per center was treated with a complement inhibitor.

Operational definitions

Primary anti-thrombotic prophylaxis (PAP) is defined as the preventive use of medications to reduce the risk of blood clot formation in patients who have not yet experienced a thrombotic event, but who are considered at increased risk due to hematologic features of PNH or coexisting thrombotic risks.

Secondary anti-thrombotic prophylaxis (SAP) refers to the ongoing use of medications or interventions to prevent the recurrence of thrombotic events in PNH patients who have already experienced a thrombotic episode.

Breakthrough hemolysis (BTH) is defined as the occurrence of any level of intravascular hemolysis, despite ongoing treatment with CIT. Clinically significant BTH is the occurrence of intravascular hemolysis associated with significantly increased lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) levels (≥ 2 ×basal value) and falling hemoglobin levels (> 2 gr/dl) [23].

Discussion

Despite the evolving therapeutic armamentarium for PNH, data remain scarce regarding the optimal selection, duration, and clinical indications for anticoagulation in patients receiving CIT. Moreover, the use of the newer class of DOACs has not been formally investigated.

This nationwide survey provides the first comprehensive overview of thrombosis management strategies in PNH across Italian Centers, encompassing more than half of the known national PNH population.

Recently, a large real-world study of 267 patients from 4 US Centers explored the features, predictors of TE, and anticoagulation strategies covering a total of 2,043 patient-years [23] Overall, 21% of patients developed TEs, 43% of which occurred at disease onset. The rate of TEs was halved in patients receiving CIT compared to untreated patients (20 vs. 40 TEs per 1,000 patient-years, respectively). This study described in detail the management of TEs, and the anticoagulation strategy consisting of warfarin (45%), DOACs (41%), and LMWH (14%), with a median treatment duration of 46 months. However, no information was specifically reported on the PAP and SAP strategies employed in the 4 US Centers, particularly in patients awaiting or on CIT.

The results of our survey reveal substantial variability in clinical practices, reflecting the lack of unified guidelines and confirming that PAP remains a controversial area. In fact, we observed that before CIT, only one-third of centers systematically prescribe prophylaxis in untreated patients or those awaiting complement inhibitors. Another quarter implement PAP selectively, based on thrombophilia screening, while a small minority base decision on the percentage of PNH neutrophils (9%). This diversity in clinical practice reflects both the absence of definitive evidence-based guidance and the historical perception of thromboembolism as a key driver of morbidity and mortality in untreated PNH [4–6, 11, 12]. Such clinical uncertainty in the field, has been emphasized by Fattizzo et al. [26], who highlighted the paucity of robust evidence supporting universal primary prophylaxis in PNH and underscored the importance of individualized risk assessment strategies, including the size of the PNH granulocyte population and the concomitant thrombophilia traits. Also Gurnari et al. have proposed that an extended coagulation workup at diagnosis may help identify a subset of patients who could benefit from early intervention [23]. However, uncertainty regarding this approach emerged in our survey, as only 11 out of 24 Centers perform thrombophilia screening at diagnosis as a potential tool to guide decisions on PAP treatment.

Among centers prescribing PAP, traditional anticoagulants were favored; vitamin K antagonists (VKAs) were utilized slightly more frequently than low molecular weight heparin (LMWH). The limited adoption of DOACs for primary prophylaxis may be attributed to concerns regarding their safety and efficacy in the setting of intravascular hemolysis and complement-mediated coagulopathy, reinforced by the lack of clinical trials investigating DOACs within this specific patient population.

The discontinuation of PAP represents another controversial issue, even though the initiation of CIT, good disease control (using LDH value < 1.5 the upper limit of normal as a surrogate marker for suppressed intravascular hemolysis), and the absence of signs of thrombosis may be considered reasonable criteria for the safe suspension of antithrombotic therapy. In our survey fifteen of sixteen centers (93%) that employed PAP reported discontinuing anticoagulation therapy following CIT initiation. Only one center continued PAP indefinitely in patients with underlying thrombophilia. This high degree of consensus suggests that clinicians perceive CIT as sufficiently protective against thrombosis to permit de-escalation of prophylactic anticoagulation in PNH patients without additional risk factors. This clinical behavior aligns with the established evidence that CIT substantially reduces thrombotic risk in PNH [8, 10].

Regarding SAP, our findings indicate that most centers continue SAP indefinitely irrespective of CIT. This practice reflects the scarcity of published cases on SAP discontinuation in patients receiving CIT [23, 27, 28]. Moreover, the reluctance to stop SAP is consistent with the conservative approach proposed by Brodsky who recommends a cautious approach to stopping anticoagulation due to the unpredictable nature of thrombosis in PNH, even in the setting of CIT [28]. He suggests evaluating SAP discontinuation when thrombotic symptoms have resolved and hemolytic activity is well controlled; such decisions must involve a careful discussion with the patient, emphasizing the paucity of evidence regarding the risks and benefits and taking into account patient preferences. However, in the last years, emerging data from long-term follow-up (up to 8 years) suggest that SAP discontinuation may be safe, without an increased risk of recurrent thromboembolic events, in a selected subset of patients with well-controlled hemolysis and no radiologic evidence of residual thrombosis [23].

Consistent with previous reports, the small number of centers in our survey, that discontinue SAP, take into consideration prolonged remission of TE, resolution of imaging findings (e.g., CT scan, MR angiography, or Doppler ultrasound), and sustained control of hemolysis.

However, in the absence of established guidelines, the reluctance of clinicians to discontinue SAP in patients with a prior history of TE is understandable, even when adequate disease control is achieved through CIT.

Furthermore, even in the SAP setting, DOACs were used by only a minority of centers, suggesting a persistent caution in adopting these newer anticoagulants in PNH, despite their expanding use in other high-risk thrombophilic states [19, 21]. While, preliminary case series and pharmacologic analyses suggest that DOACs may be safe and effective in selected PNH patients [23–25], robust prospective controlled trials trial remain lacking.

The management of breakthrough hemolysis (BTH) also shows significant variation in centers’ policy. LMWH initiation during BTH was influenced by the presence of precipitating factors (e.g., infections or immobilization) and the severity of hemolysis, highlighting a reactive management strategy rather than the application of standardized protocols. Fattizzo et al. have emphasized that not all BTH events carry the same thrombotic risk, and that clinical context—including markers such as LDH surge and D-dimer elevation— should guide the choice and introduction of anticoagulant therapy [29].

This study has several limitations including its reliance on self-reported institutional policies and incomplete data regarding individual patient characteristics. Nevertheless, the high response rate and broad national coverage strengthen the representativeness of our findings, which clearly suggest the urgent need to produce shared guidelines with the aim of harmonizing patient management in clinical practice. Moreover, a potential limitation of this study is the disparity in patient numbers across participating sites. However, our primary objective was to capture a comprehensive overview of real-world clinical practices in a setting where established guidelines are currently lacking. While we acknowledge that institutional volume and experience may influence clinical decision-making, this survey was not specifically powered to stratify results by center volume. Future large-scale international studies are warranted to investigate whether practice patterns significantly differ between high- and low-volume centers.”

In conclusion, the therapeutic landscape of PNH has been dramatically transformed by complement inhibitors, but clinical practice is not yet standardized. The findings of our study underscore the need for collaborative prospective studies, registry-based analyses and evidence-based recommendations to harmonize anticoagulation strategies for both primary (PAP) and secondary long-term (SAP) prophylaxis. However, given the rarity of the disease and the great variability of risks and manifestations of TEs, the conduct of traditional clinical trials remains inherently challenging. Therefore, guidelines derived from formal and structured expert consensus studies may represent a valuable tool to harmonize antithrombotic strategies and, ultimately, to improve the outcomes for patients with PNH.
