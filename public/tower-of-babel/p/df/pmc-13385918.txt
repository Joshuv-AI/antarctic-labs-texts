Oncolytic vaccinia virus JX-594 shows subtype-specific activity and candidate biomarkers in gastric cancer cell lines
Authors: Jee Hung Kim, YeLin Kim, Youkeun Shin, Inho Park, Woo Sun Kwon, Tae Soo Kim, Namhee Lee, Keunhee Oh, Chan Kim, Hei-Cheul Jeung, Sun Young Rha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOncolytic vaccinia virus JX-594 (pexastimogene devacirepvec) selectively replicates in tumour cells and stimulates antitumour immunity; however, its therapeutic potential in gastric cancer (GC) has not been systematically evaluated. In this study, we investigated the antitumour activity of JX-594 across a panel of 49 molecularly characterized GC cell lines and a xenograft mouse model and explored molecular features associated with viral sensitivity. JX-594 induced cytopathic effects (median effective dose, ED50, 0.37 MOI (multiplicity of infection)) in a subset of gastric cancer cell lines and significantly suppressed tumour growth in vivo. Sensitivity to JX-594 varied across molecular subtypes, with microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) cell lines showing greater susceptibility, whereas Epstein–Barr virus (EBV)–associated cell lines were relatively resistant. Transcriptomic analysis identified candidate biomarkers associated with viral sensitivity, including upregulated genes involved in G-protein signalling (GPR87, GRPR, PRDX5, MX1), interleukin production (IL1A, IL4I1), and vascular activation/dysfunction (OLR1, MMRN2), and downregulation of metastasis-related GAL3ST2. Notably, JX-594 activity was not correlated with TK1 or EGFR expression, suggesting additional determinants of viral susceptibility in GC. These findings provide preclinical evidence that molecular heterogeneity influences the response of GC to oncolytic virotherapy and support further investigation of JX-594 as a potential therapeutic strategy for gastric cancer.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-51477-z.Subject terms: Biomarkers, Cancer, Computational biology and bioinformatics, Immunology, Oncology

Introduction

The prognosis of advanced gastric cancer (GC) is extremely poor; according to the GLOBOCAN 2022 estimates, approximately, 968,784 new stomach cancer cases and 660,175 deaths occurred worldwide1. Although the incidence of stomach cancer among Koreans has decreased over the past two decades, GC is the second most common carcinoma and remains the third leading cause of cancer-related death in Korea2. The prognosis of patients with advanced GC remains poor, as most chemotherapies provide partial responses of short durations, conferring a median survival advantage of fewer than 12 months3. Furthermore, because of the complexity of the mutation landscape and the simultaneous activation of multiple signaling pathways in GC, targeted agents with or without standard chemotherapeutic regimens might be insufficient to provide a significant benefit to patient survival4. Paclitaxel-based chemotherapy is commonly used as a second-line treatment for advanced GC and remains an important therapeutic option in clinical practice; however, responses are often transient, highlighting the need for novel treatment strategies. Hence, more effective and tolerable treatment strategies will be needed to improve clinical outcomes for inoperable GC.

Oncolytic viruses (OVs) mediate tumour regression through selective replication and lysis in tumour cells and the induction of systemic anti-tumour immunity without damaging normal cells 5. At present, various natural or genetically engineered viruses are being investigated to treat solid tumours. Accumulating clinical evidence supports their safety and efficacy as a monotherapy and in combination with other treatment modalities6. The use of OVs that target GC has been gaining interest, as several OVs are undergoing development for the treatment of GC7–9.

JX-594 (pexastimogene devacirepvec, SillaJen Inc.) is an oncolytic vaccinia virus engineered to express an immune-activating transgene, granulocyte-macrophage colony stiumlating facotr (GM-CSF) gene, and contains a disrupted viral thymidine kinase (TK) gene10,11. JX-594 exhibits tumor selectivity through several mechanisms, including dependence on elevated cellular TK levels12, activation of the epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) pathway13, and tumor resistance to interferon responses14. Deletion of the viral TK gene restricts viral replication primarily to rapidly proliferating tumor cells with high cellular TK expression, thereby providing a mechanism for tumor-selective viral replication.

JX-594 exerts antitumor effects through multiple mechanisms, including direct tumor cell infection and lysis, induction of antitumor immune responses, and disruption of tumor-associated vasculature15. Preclinical studies have demonstrated antitumor activity of JX-594 in several cancer models, including hepatocellular carcinoma, colorectal cancer, and melanoma10,13,16.

Clinical studies have primarily evaluated JX-594 in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma10 and other advanced solid tumors17,18. In phase I/II trials, intratumoral administration of JX-594 demonstrated tumor-selective replication and objective responses in patients with hepatocellular carcinoma. In addition, intravenous administration has been shown to enable viral replication and transgene expression within tumor tissues in patients with advanced solid tumors18–21.

However, the therapeutic potential of JX-594 in gastric cancer has not yet been systematically evaluated. Gastric cancer remains a major global health burden with limited therapeutic options for advanced disease, and the clinical outcomes of patients with advanced GC remain poor despite the use of systemic chemotherapy. We hypothesized that molecular features of GC cells, including cellular TK levels and EGFR pathway activity, may influence the sensitivity of GC cells to JX-594. Furthermore, gastric cancer exhibits substantial molecular heterogeneity, with several biologically distinct subtypes identified through genomic studies, and these differences may influence the susceptibility of tumor cells to oncolytic viral therapy. Therefore, this study aimed to evaluate the antitumor activity of JX-594 in GC models both in vitro and in vivo, and to explore molecular features associated with viral sensitivity as well as potential differences in response across GC molecular subtypes.

Materials/subjects and methods

Characteristics of the oncolytic virus JX-594

JX-594 is a Wyeth strain vaccinia virus engineered for viral TK gene inactivation and the expression of the hGM-CSF and β-gal (β-galactosidase) transgenes under the control of the synthetic early-late and p7.5 promoters, respectively. The disrupted TK gene confers cancer cell-selective ability to JX-594. GM-CSF activates and stimulates the anti-cancer immune response and lacZ is a marker to monitor viral activity.

Cell culture

Forty-nine human GC cell lines were used in this study and maintained according to our institutional protocol22,23. Three cell lines were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), ten from the Korean Cell Line Bank (KCLB, Seoul, Republic of Korea), and ten from the Japanese Cancer Research Resources Bank (JCRB, Osaka, Japan). In addition, twenty-six cell lines were established by the Cancer Metastasis Research Center (CMRC) and the Songdang Institute for Cancer Research (SICR), Yonsei University College of Medicine (Seoul, Republic of Korea), from metastatic GC patients using ascites or pleural fluid. Cells were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM, Gibco™ , USA, NY, 12360038), Roswell Park Memorial Institute 1640 (RPMI-1640, Gibco™ , USA, NY, 11875093), or Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Gibco™ , USA, NY, 11995073), supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco™ , USA, NY, 26140079), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 µg/mL streptomycin (Gibco™ , USA, NY, 15140122). The origin and source of all gastric cancer cell lines used in this study are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Short tandem repeat (STR) profiling was used to authenticate the cell lines, and the authentication information is provided in the Supplementary Table 1. All cell lines were maintained at 37 ℃ in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. These cell lines were authenticated via short tandem repeat profiling using multiplex PCR and were categorized according to the TCGA (The Cancer Genome Atlas) molecular classification of gastric adenocarcinoma24. The cell lines were classified as Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)-related, microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H), and chromosomal instability (CIN)- or genomically stable (GS)-like25. Human umbilical vein endothelial cells (HUVECs, Lonza, C2519A) were cultured in an endothelial growth medium (EGM-2, Lonza, CC-3162). Control cell lines, such as 293, HeLa, and FHs173We, were cultured in minimal essential medium (MEM, Gibco™, USA, NY 12360038). The control cell lines were included as reference comparator models for in vitro experiments and were not intended to serve as matched normal gastric epithelial controls.

Cell viability assay

Cell survival was assessed using the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay. All cell lines (8 × 103 cells) were resuspended in 100 μL culture medium supplemented with FBS, counted, and seeded per well in 96-well microplates. After 24 h, the cells were washed and incubated with serially diluted JX-594 at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 0.1, 0.2, 0.5, 1, 2, and 5. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm, and the results were analyzed using CalcuSyn software (version 2.0; https://www.biosoft.com/w/calcusyn.htm). The ED50 values, representing the initial virus MOI to achieve 50% cell death, were reported, compared to the untreated control cells at 72 h post-inoculation. Cell viability was assessed at 72 h post infection to allow sufficient time for virus-induced cytopathic effects and cell death to occur following viral replication.

Quantification of virus replication by plaque assay

Virus titers were quantified as plaque-forming units (PFU) using a standard plaque assay. Serial dilutions of viral samples were transferred to gastric cancer (GC) cell monolayers and incubated for 1 h to allow viral adsorption. The cells were then overlaid with 0.5% agarose in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS. After incubation, the cells were stained with 0.5% crystal violet solution and plaques were counted to determine viral titers.

Plaque assays were performed in selected representative gastric cancer cell lines exhibiting different levels of sensitivity to JX-594 to evaluate viral replication across models with distinct biological characteristics. Virus replication was quantified at 24 h post infection to assess early viral replication before extensive virus-induced cytopathic effects develop.

Apoptosis analysis using Annexin V staining

Apoptosis analysis via flow cytometry was conducted using a FITC Annexin V Apoptosis Detection Kit I (BD Biosciences, 556547) to identify apoptotic cells after treatment with JX-594. Briefly, 2.0 × 105 GC cells were seeded per well in a 6-well plate and incubated for 24 h at 37 ℃ with 5% CO2. After 24 h, the cells were treated with JX-594 (0.000625–0.0025 MOI per well). After 72 h, the GC cells and the corresponding supernatants were harvested using a StemPro Accutase Cell Dissociation Reagent (Gibco, A1110501). The cells were then stained with FITC and propidium iodide (PI), following the manufacturer’s protocol. Cells were analyzed by flow cytometry and classified as live (Annexin V−/PI−), early apoptotic (Annexin V+/PI−), and late apoptotic (Annexin V+/PI+) populations.

RNA sequencing and data analysis

For differential gene expression analysis via RNA sequencing, total RNA was isolated from the GC cell lines using a RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA quantity and integrity were assessed using both Nanodrop and Bioanalyzer systems according to their respective manufacturer’s instructions. RNA libraries were prepared using standard Illumina library preparation protocols. Paired-end RNA sequencing was performed on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 platform with 2 × 100 bp reads. On average, approximately 119 million sequencing reads were generated per sample (range: 21.6–150.1 million reads), corresponding to approximately 10–75 million paired-end read pairs per sample. Raw sequencing reads were processed using standard quality control procedures, and high-quality reads were used for downstream analysis. Gene expression levels were quantified as fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads (FPKM). Differential gene expression analysis was performed to compare JX-594–sensitive and –resistant GC cell lines. P-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) method. Hierarchical clustering analysis was performed using z-score–normalized gene expression values, and heatmaps were generated to visualize differentially expressed genes.

Western blot

The total protein was extracted with a RIPA Lysis and Extraction Buffer (ThermoFisher, 89900) with 1X of Halt™ Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail, EDTA-free (ThermoFisher, 78441). The extracted protein concentrations were determined using Micro BCA™ Protein Assay Kit (ThermoFisher, 23235) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The lysates were subsequently adjusted to 25 µg per 10 µL to ensure equal protein loading. The protein was separated via sodium dodecyl sulphate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis using 10% polyacrylamide gels. Proteins were then transferred onto polyvinyl difluoride membranes (GE Healthcare, WI, USA). The membranes were blocked with 5% BSA, followed by incubation with the following primary antibodies: anti-Thymidine Kinase 1 (1:1000; Rabbit polyclonal; Cell Signaling Technology, 8960S), anti-EGFReceptor (D38B1) (1:1000; Rabbit monoclonal; Cell Signaling Technology, 4267S), anti-GAPDH (1:10,000; Rabbit Monoclonal; Cell Signaling Technology, 2118S), anti-alpha tubulin (DM1A) (1:1000; Mouse Monoclonal; Cell Signaling Technology, 3873S), and anti-alpha actinin (H-2) (1:1000; Mouse Monoclonal; Santa Cruz, sc-17829). Afterward, the membranes were incubated with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary anti-rabbit IgG, HRP antibodies (1:5000; Cell Signaling Technology, 7074S), and anti-mouse igG, HRP antibodies (1:5000; Cell Signaling Technology, 7076S). Finally, the membranes were developed using an enhanced chemiluminescence system (SuperSignal West Pico PLUS Chemiluminescent Substrate, ThermoFisher Scientific, USA, 34580). Densitometric analysis of western blot bands was performed using Fiji (ImageJ, Java 1.8.0_452 (64-bit); https://imagej.net/software/fiji/). Band intensities were normalized to the corresponding GAPDH signal.

Xenograft mouse model

Female BALB/c nude mice (6–8 weeks of age; 16–20 g) were obtained from Orient Bio Inc. (Gyeonggi-do, Korea). Tumours were induced via subcutaneous injection of 1 × 107 NUGC3 cells into the right flank of the mice. Tumour cell injection was performed under isoflurane anesthesia (3–5% in oxygen) using an inhalation vaporizer (HANA Pharm Co. Ltd., Seoul, Republic of Korea). When the tumour volume reached 50–100 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to three treatment groups: PBS (control), JX-594, or paclitaxel. JX-594 (1 MOI (1 × 107 plaque-forming units)) was administered via intratumoral (IT) injection, and paclitaxel (Genexol®, 30 mg/5 mL; Samyang Biopharm, South Korea) was administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 10 mg/kg. Treatments were given every 7 days (days 0, 7, 14, and 21).

The tumour length and width were measured every 3 days using a digital caliper, and the tumour volumes were calculated using the modified ellipsoid formula [1/2 × (length × width2)]. The mouse body weights were also measured.

Mice were humanely euthanized at the experimental endpoint or earlier if predefined humane endpoints were met, including tumor ulceration, significant (> 20%) body weight loss, reduced mobility, or inability to obtain food or water. Euthanasia was performed when tumor volume exceeded 1500mm3. Euthanasia was conducted using a gradual-fill CO2 inhalation method, with CO2 supplied from a compressed gas cylinder equipped with a calibrated flow regulator, and the flow rate set at 30–70% of the chamber volume per minute, in accordance with AVMA guidelines. After cessation of respiration, cervical dislocation was performed as a secondary physical method to ensure death.

All animal experimental procedures in this study were conducted in accordance with institutional and national guidelines and were approved by the Yonsei University Health System Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC approval no. 2021-0120). The study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines. All animals were monitored regularly, and humane endpoints were applied according to institutional animal care guidelines.

Immunohistochemical analysis

Frozen sections (50 µm thick) were fixed in 10% formalin and processed for paraffin embedding. The tissue blocks were serially sectioned and stained with H&E or the specific target antigens. The sections were incubated with polyclonal antibodies against vaccinia virus (Abcam, ab35219), CD31 (Abcam, ab182981), or Ki67 (Abcam, ab16667) overnight at 4 ℃.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (version 5.01; https://www.graphpad.com) and IBM SPSS Statistics (version 18.0; https://www.ibm.com/products/spss-statistics). Values are represented as the mean ± standard error of the mean unless otherwise indicated. Statistical differences between groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparison test when appropriate. Survival curves were generated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and statistical differences between curves were analyzed using the log-rank test. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05. For immunohistochemical quantification, statistical significance was assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparisons test.

All experiments were performed with at least three independent biological replicates unless otherwise stated. Technical replicates were included within each experiment to ensure measurement reliability.

Results

Anti-tumour effects of JX-594 in gastric cancer

To determine the cytotoxic effect of JX-594, we assessed the sensitivity of 49 human GC and 4 control cell lines to JX-594. The results showed that GC cells were sensitive to JX-594 at various ranges (0.001–10.0 MOI) (Fig. 1, Supplementary Fig. 4). The median ED50 of JX-594 in GC cell lines was 0.37 MOI (range, 0.02–6.18). The threshold (ED50 < 0.1) was chosen based on prior JX-594 solid tumors study13. Overall, the 49 GC cell lines showed modest sensitivity to JX-594.

The viral infection and replication of JX-594 in GC cell lines were confirmed via RT-PCR and a plaque assay (Supplementary Fig. 1). Furthermore, apoptosis analysis was performed with GC cells infected with JX-594. The results showed that cell apoptosis was significantly increased in a dose-dependent manner upon JX-594 treatment (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Association of JX-594 replication and TK1 and EGFR expression in gastric cancer cell lines

To assess the mechanism of JX-594 replication on cellular TK and EGFR/RAS/MAPK pathway signaling in GC cell lines, we analyzed TK1 and EGFR expression in the 49 GC cell lines via western blot (Fig. 2A, B). Although we observed a correlation between virus concentration and plaque numbers (Fig. 2C), the expression level of TK1 was not significantly correlated to JX-594 replication and infection in GC cell lines (Fig. 2D). Similarly, EGFR expressions did not correlate with JX-594 sensitivity in GC cell lines (Fig. 2E). To further address potential variability in loading controls, we performed additional densitometric normalization using α-tubulin and α-actinin in addition to GAPDH. Although absolute normalized values varied depending on the reference protein used, correlation analyses demonstrated that the relative expression patterns across cell lines were largely preserved across all normalization methods, supporting the robustness of these findings (Supplementary Figure 3–8).

Potential candidate biomarkers associated with sensitivity of JX-594 in gastric cancer

We previously reported that the 49 GC cell lines used in this study were classified into four TCGA subtypes, the MSI-H, EBV, CIN- and GS-like subtypes22. Based on this classification, we investigated which genes had potential candidate biomarkers capacity for JX-594 sensitivity in GC. Cell lines of the EBV-associated subtype (SNU-719, YCC-10) showed relative resistance to JX-594 than the others (Fig. 3A). When classifying the sensitivity of the cell lines to JX-594 based on an ED50 of 0.1 (Supplementary Fig. 4), 100% of the EBV subtype, 86% of the CIN subtype, and 71% of the GS subtype showed resistance. In contrast, 75% of the MSI-H subtype showed sensitivity to JX-594. However, resistance was not restricted to the EBV subtype, as some cell lines from other subtypes, such as SNU-668, also demonstrated reduced sensitivity. In addition, Lauren’s classification of 49 GC cell lines was not associated with JX-594 sensitivity (Fig. 3B). Lauren’s classification was classified into intestinal type and diffuse type based on histopathological characteristics25.

To explore the potential candidate biomarkers of JX-594 sensitivity, gene expression levels were analyzed in all 49 GC cell lines using RNA sequencing. We compared the gene expression profile of the GC cell lines and found that 19 genes were significantly upregulated (log2 fold change > 1 and adjusted p < 0.00001) and 5 genes were significantly downregulated (log2 fold change < 1 and adjusted p < 0.00001) in the JX-594-sensitive GC cell lines (ED50 < 0.1, n = 12). The upregulated genes were associated with G protein signaling (GPR87, GRPR, PRDX5, MX1), interleukin production (IL1A, IL4I1), and vascular endothelial cell activation and dysfunction (OLR1, MMRN2), while the downregulated genes were associated with tumor metastasis (GAL3ST2) (Table 1, Fig. 4A, B). Gene set analysis of the gene ontology terms of the differentially expressed genes revealed that the significantly expressed genes in the JX-594 sensitive group were associated with antibacterial humoral response, interleukin-12, -17 production, T cell-mediated immunity, cytokine receptor binding and cytokine activity, and oxidoreductive activity (Fig. 4C).

Oncolytic efficacy of JX-594 treatment in a tumor xenograft mouse model

A xenograft mouse model of human NUGC-3 tumors was used to study the anti-tumor effects of JX-594 and paclitaxel monotherapy in vivo. Paclitaxel is one of the standard chemotherapeutic regimens in patients with advanced GC; therefore, we used this regimen as the control. In the NUGC-3 xenograft model, the tumor volumes significantly decreased after the IT injection of JX-594 and IP injection of paclitaxel compared with the control group (Fig. 5A). No significant differences in body weight were observed between the groups at the beginning and end of the study (Fig. 5B). No morbidity attributable to either tumor progression or therapy was identified, and no mortality was observed. Quantitative immunohistochemical analysis showed that JX-594 significantly reduced both CD31-positive vessel density and Ki-67-positive proliferating cells compared with controls (CD31, mean ± SD: 3.00 ± 1.97% vs. 20.33 ± 7.20%, p < 0.01; Ki-67, mean ± SD: 4.47 ± 5.16% vs. 60.89 ± 2.43%, p < 0.05). Paclitaxel showed a trend toward reduced CD31 and Ki-67 expression; however, these differences were not statistically significant. (Fig. 5C and Supplementary Figure 5).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study provides one of the preclinical evaluations of the anti-tumor activity of JX-594 in gastric cancer models. We investigated the direct anti-tumor effects of JX-594 on GC cells, showing that it can replicate and induce cancer cell death in 49 human GC cell lines in vitro and in a xenograft model.

We first confirmed the cytotoxicity of JX-594 in human GC cell lines and whether its efficacy differed across cell lines. In GC cells highly sensitive to JX-594 (YCC-33, YCC-42, and YCC-20), JX-594 monotherapy may be sufficient. On the other hand, other therapeutic modalities are needed for the more resistant GC cells, such as SNU-668, YCC-10, and MKN-7. In a previous report13, cells from several solid tumors, such as melanoma, ovarian cancer, kidney cancer, and breast cancer, needed ED50 values of < 0.1 MOI to reduce their cell viability effectively. Compared to this finding, JX-594 demonstrated relatively modest anti-tumor activity in GC cell lines. However, direct comparisons of ED50 values across different tumor types should be interpreted cautiously due to differences in experimental systems and biological contexts.

Although Annexin V/PI staining suggested increased apoptotic cell populations following JX-594 treatment, additional molecular assays such as caspase activation analysis would be required to further clarify the specific mechanisms of cell death. Therefore, the present findings should be interpreted as evidence of virus-induced cytotoxicity rather than definitive confirmation of a specific apoptotic pathway.

TK is a cell cycle-dependent enzyme of the pyrimidine salvage pathway that catalyzes the phosphorylation of thymidine to thymidine monophosphate. As the engineered inactivation of TK in JX-594 resulted in a dependence on high cellular TK levels for successful viral replication, we hypothesized that JX-594 viral replication levels might correspond to cellular TK levels. Cancer cells have higher constitutive TK levels than normal cells26. There was a report that the JX-594 burst size was attenuated in cells with reduced TK levels compared to control cells; however, no effect on wild-type Wyeth viruses was observed in a TK-knockdown clone13. In this study, we did not observe a correlation between TK expression and JX-594-induced cytotoxicity in GC cell lines. Some reasons for this observation might be the method of detecting TK expression may not be adequate to accurately evaluate TK expression in GC cells, the cut-off value for JX-594 sensitivity was not determined, or other mechanisms related to the efficacy of JX-594. Therefore, further studies are needed to clarify the significance of TK expression to predict the clinical outcomes of patients treated with JX-594.

Vaccinia growth factor (VGF) is an early viral protein that enhances viral pathogenesis and induces the continuous activation of EGFR signaling throughout the virus infection cycle due to its lower affinity for the receptor27. Prolonged EGFR activation and signaling ensure that infected cells receive a sustained survival signal that antagonizes infection-induced cell death. The secreted VGF binds to and sends signals through the EGFR. EGFR tyrosine phosphorylation then facilitates the uptake of the vaccinia virus via micropinocytosis and actin rearrangement, demonstrating that EGFR is required for the initial entry of the virus into uninfected cells28; treatment with EGFR inhibitors blocked vaccinia replication29. The incubation of human colorectal cancer cell lines with an inhibitor specific for extracellular signal-regulated kinase inhibited JX-594-GFP+/β-gal-replication. This result suggests that JX-594-GFP+/β-gal-replication depends on EGFR/RAS/MAPK pathway signaling13. In this study, however, we did not find a relationship between EGFR expression and JX-594 activity. Notably, variability was observed in commonly used loading controls, including GAPDH, α-tubulin, and α-actinin, across cell lines. However, consistent expression patterns were preserved across different normalization methods, supporting the robustness of the findings, although these results should be interpreted with caution. Therefore, the downstream mediator in the EGFR/Ras pathway that most potently drives JX-594 replication, spread, and cytolysis remains to be determined.

We also investigated the potential candidate biomarkers for JX-594 sensitivity in GC cells. We showed that the EBV-associated subtype was relatively more resistant to JX-594 than the other subtypes. EBV-associated GC comprises 10% of all gastric malignancies. Consistent with this observation, MSI-H subtype cell lines appeared relatively more sensitive to JX-594, followed by GS subtype cell lines, whereas CIN subtype cell lines showed comparatively lower sensitivity. These findings suggest that molecular heterogeneity in gastric cancer may influence susceptibility to oncolytic viral therapy. According to TCGA, gastric adenocarcinomas can be classified into four molecular subtypes: EBV-associated, MSI, CIN, and GS tumors30. EBV-associated gastric cancer accounts for approximately 10% of gastric malignancies31 and is characterized by several distinct genomic alterations32, including frequent PIK3CA mutations, ARID1A mutations, and amplification of chromosome 9p24.1 including the PDCL1L1,2 and JAK233. These molecular characteristics contribute to the unique biological behavior of EBV-associated gastric cancer. In the present study, EBV-associated GC cell lines showed relatively reduced susceptibility to JX-594 compared with other molecular subtypes. However, the mechanism underlying this subtype-specific resistance remains unclear. Our transcriptomic analysis identified genes associated with JX-594 sensitivity; however, functional perturbation experiments were not performed in the present study. Therefore, these genes should be considered candidate biomarkers requiring further mechanistic validation. Future studies integrating subtype-specific transcriptomic analyses and functional experiments may help clarify the molecular determinants of JX-594 resistance in EBV-associated gastric cancer.

Furthermore, analysis of the RNA sequencing data from the 49 GC cell lines in this study revealed potential candidate biomarkers associated with sensitivity to JX-594. The upregulated genes included those involved in vascular endothelial cell activation and dysfunction, such as OLR1 and MMRN2, which were associated with JX-594 sensitivity. OLR1 encodes the oxidized low-density lipoprotein receptor 1 (LOX-1), which has previously been reported to be highly expressed in gastric cancer tissues compared with non-cancerous tissues. Analyses of two independent mRNA microarrays from the GSE27342 database34,35 and the TCGA cohort have shown increased OLR1 expression in gastric cancer33. Previous studies have also reported that increased LOX-1 expression is associated with tumor invasion, lymph node metastasis, and poor clinical outcomes in gastric cancer36. These findings suggest that OLR1 expression may be associated with tumor progression and may potentially influence the sensitivity of GC cells to JX-594. However, because our findings are based on transcriptomic correlations rather than direct protein measurements, further functional studies, such as OLR1 knockdown or pharmacological inhibition, will be required to determine whether OLR1 directly contributes to JX-594 sensitivity.

We also confirmed the efficacy of JX-594 in a NUGC-3 GC xenograft mouse model. JX-594 monotherapy significantly reduced tumor growth, which was associated with a decrease in the expression level of CD31, a tumor vessel marker, and Ki-67, a cell proliferation marker. These results suggest that decreased tumor vasculature may be a mechanism of action of JX-594 in GC. It has been reported that JX-594 administration could potentially induce direct oncolysis and significantly decrease tumor vessel density in a Renca mouse model37. This virus could be safely administered via multiple IT injections and not cause toxicity. Therefore, JX-594 may be a novel therapeutic agent against GC. Although paclitaxel demonstrated antitumor activity in this model, the present study was designed to evaluate the preclinical activity of JX-594 rather than to establish superiority over standard chemotherapy. Future studies evaluating survival outcomes and potential combination strategies with cytotoxic agents such as paclitaxel may further clarify the therapeutic potential of JX-594 in gastric cancer.

Despite these findings, this study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size in the in vivo experiments was unavoidable due to the use of immunodeficient mouse models. Second, the in vivo studies were conducted using immunodeficient xenograft models because human gastric cancer cell lines were used. Although this approach allows evaluation of the direct oncolytic activity in human tumor cells, it does not fully recapitulate the complex interactions between tumors and the host immune system. This limitation is particularly relevant for oncolytic virotherapy, as immune-mediated antitumor responses are known to contribute substantially to therapeutic efficacy. Therefore, the potential contribution of immune-mediated mechanisms could not be fully evaluated in the present study. Future studies using immunocompetent syngeneic models or humanized mouse models will be necessary to better elucidate the role of the immune system in mediating treatment responses. Third, JX-594 demonstrated only modest antitumor efficacy in gastric cancer cells in vitro. Therefore, further studies are warranted to explore optimal combination strategies and to better understand the mechanisms underlying the antitumor activity of JX-594 in gastric cancer and other tumor types. Fourth, normal gastric epithelial cells were not included in this study. Although the present work focused on evaluating the heterogeneity of JX-594 sensitivity across a large panel of gastric cancer cell lines, comparison with normal gastric epithelial cells would provide additional insight into tumor selectivity. Future studies incorporating normal gastric epithelial models will be important to further evaluate the therapeutic window of JX-594. Fifth, although we investigated the response of gastric cancer cells to JX-594 treatment, subtype-associated differences were explored, but the number of cell lines within each subtype was limited. Given the molecular heterogeneity of gastric cancer, future studies examining differential responses across distinct molecular subtypes will be important. Lastly, clinical trials involving patients with gastric cancer are warranted to validate whether the preclinical findings observed in this study translate into meaningful clinical outcomes.

In conclusion, JX-594 demonstrated moderate but subtype-specific anti-tumor efficacy in GC cell lines and xenografts. The identification of novel candidate biomarkers and mechanistic pathways provides a rationale for further clinical investigation of JX-594, either as monotherapy or in rational combination strategies. These findings establish a preclinical foundation for incorporating JX-594 into future trials to expand therapeutic options for patients with gastric cancer.
