Effectiveness of existential group psychotherapy in reducing perceived rejection and anxiety among individuals with psychiatric disorders and comorbid substance use disorder: an experimental study
Authors: Maryam Latifian, Sahar Esmaeili, Ronak Mihan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn recent years, humanistic approaches have gained considerable popularity in treating individuals with psychiatric disorders and comorbid substance use disorders. Among these, existential therapy is one of the most prominent. This study aimed to evaluate the effectiveness of existential group psychotherapy in reducing perceived rejection and anxiety in this population.MethodsThis experimental study utilized a pre-test, post-test, and follow-up design with a control group. The study population comprised individuals with psychiatric disorders and comorbid substance use disorder admitted to Razi Psychiatric Hospital during the first half of 2024. Participants were recruited through convenience sampling and randomly assigned to either the experimental group (n = 30) or the control group (n = 30) using a random number table. Measures included the Perceived Rejection Scale, the DASS-21 Anxiety Subscale, and the Existential Group Psychotherapy Protocol. Participants were reassessed two months after completion of the intervention. Data analysis was conducted using SPSS version 22.ResultsFindings showed a significant reduction in perceived rejection and anxiety in the experimental group receiving existential group psychotherapy compared to the control group (P < 0.01). These results support the efficacy of existential group psychotherapy in alleviating these symptoms among individuals with psychiatric disorders and comorbid substance use disorder.ConclusionThis study confirms that existential group psychotherapy effectively reduces perceived rejection and anxiety in this population. To promote the widespread adoption of this intervention, policymakers and stakeholders in mental health and substance use treatment should consider implementing relevant policies and mechanisms to facilitate its integration into specialized services.Trial registrationThis study protocol was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT20230411057882N1) on March 1, 2024.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40359-026-05126-9.

Background

Substance use disorder is a widespread global issue, with its psychological and social consequences posing significant challenges to modern societies. Despite extensive efforts and considerable financial investments aimed at addressing substance use disorder, an effective and universally accepted solution has yet to be established [1–3]. Today, substance use disorder has become a major personal, social, and public health crisis, threatening human resources and national assets in many countries [4, 5]. The complexity of problems related to substance use disorder calls for a comprehensive treatment framework that addresses multiple aspects of life and diverse behavioral patterns.

Moreover, a substantial proportion of patients seeking or referred for substance use disorder treatment present with dual diagnoses, meaning they meet criteria not only for substance use disorder but also for at least one comorbid psychiatric disorder [6–8]. This comorbidity poses significant challenges in the treatment of substance-dependent individuals. Research suggests that over 70% of individuals with substance dependence suffer from additional disorders such as sexual dysfunction, anxiety disorders, depression, feelings of loneliness and social isolation, as well as physical illnesses [9–11].

Among these, anxiety disorders are among the most common mental health conditions, affecting a large segment of the population and arising from various genetic, environmental, and biological factors. These disorders manifest with both psychological and physical symptoms and can be effectively treated through interventions such as cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) and pharmacotherapy [12].

Individuals with anxiety disorders face numerous challenges that significantly affect their daily lives [13]. These challenges can also diminish their ability to actively participate in social settings, especially when exposed to anxiety-provoking stimuli [14]. For instance, evidence indicates a high comorbidity between cannabis use and anxiety disorders [15].

Another important consequence of the comorbidity between substance use disorders and psychiatric disorders is the experience of social exclusion, where individuals feel abandoned, undesired, and insecure. Notably, feelings of abandonment or exclusion do not necessarily depend on the physical presence or absence of others around the individual [16, 17]. Social exclusion and disconnection from society represent persistent threats, contributing to a range of psychological and social disorders across diverse populations [18, 19].

In recent years, there has been increasing emphasis on psychological treatments for psychiatric disorders co-occurring with substance use disorders, with clinical and specialist-driven approaches commonly employed to address these complex cases [9, 20].

Humanistic approaches, which emphasize freedom, choice, and the unique meanings individuals assign to their life experiences, appear to be effective in treating these disorders [21]. One such approach is existential therapy, which centers on confronting four existential givens—meaning, freedom, death, and isolation—as key factors in improving personal relationships and enhancing self-awareness across all aspects of existence. The primary goal of this therapy is to help individuals achieve an authentic presence in life [22, 23].

In existential group psychotherapy, participants reassess their values and compare them with those of other group members. Recognizing that they are not alone in their struggles helps restore self-esteem, and through contributing to and benefiting others, individuals experience a sense of empowerment [24, 25]. This approach emphasizes raising awareness of oneself and others within the group and assists members in clarifying the changes they wish to make in their lives [21, 26].

The existential approach in counseling addresses profound issues such as loneliness, meaninglessness, death, and freedom, transcending cultural, religious, ethnic, and racial differences. It posits that connecting with others as part of the world enables individuals to discover both themselves and reality [21, 27].

Several studies have examined the effectiveness of this approach. For example, Alami et al. found that existential group therapy positively influenced life satisfaction among individuals undergoing methadone maintenance treatment, helping them cope with fundamental life challenges and achieve well-being [20]. However, to date, no study in Iran has explored the impact of existential group therapy on individuals with comorbid substance use disorder. Therefore, the present study aims to investigate the effectiveness of existential group psychotherapy in improving perceived social exclusion and anxiety among individuals with comorbid psychiatric and substance use disorders.

Methods

Study design and participants

This experimental study employed a pre-test, post-test, and follow-up design with a control group. The target population included all patients hospitalized at Razi Psychiatric Hospital in Tehran, Iran, during the first half of 2024.

The required sample size was calculated using the “comparison between means” formula, with a 95% confidence interval, a type I error rate of 0.05 (Z₁-α/₂ = 1.96), a study power of 80%, and a relative risk of 1.8. Based on these parameters, the sample size was determined to be 24 participants per group. To accommodate potential dropouts, 30 participants were recruited for each group. Participants were selected through convenience sampling from individuals diagnosed with psychiatric disorders and comorbid substance use disorder. These participants were then randomly assigned to either the experimental group (n = 30) or the control group (n = 30) using a random number table. Blinding of participants and therapists was not feasible due to the nature of the psychotherapeutic intervention. The control group received treatment as usual (TAU) at the same psychiatric hospital. During the study, one participant in the control group withdrew due to unwillingness to continue, and four participants in the experimental group were excluded due to missing more than two sessions. Consequently, 55 participants completed the study.

Razi Psychiatric Hospital, established in 1918, was chosen as the study setting. It is one of the oldest psychiatric hospitals in Iran and the largest psychiatric treatment and educational center in the Middle East, with approximately 1,400 beds. The hospital serves a substantial number of patients with both acute and chronic psychiatric disorders.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study were as follows: participants aged 18 years or older with a psychiatric disorder diagnosis confirmed by a psychiatrist; a documented history of hospitalization in a psychiatric hospital; and verified substance use documented in their clinical records, as confirmed by hospital psychiatrists. Additionally, participants were required to demonstrate sufficient clinical stability and communication capacity to participate in group sessions, as determined by the treating psychiatrist. This included the capacity to establish appropriate interpersonal relationships, exhibit empathy toward others, engage in healthy reality testing, practice self-disclosure during group sessions, and show potential for self-awareness.

Exclusion criteria encompassed individuals who missed more than two sessions, entered the acute phase of a psychotic disorder, experienced acute intoxication from substances or alcohol, developed cognitive impairments during the intervention, had insufficient intellectual functioning, showed inadequate response to psychiatric medications, exhibited active suicidal ideation, were at risk of symptom exacerbation due to group participation, or expressed unwillingness to continue in the study.

Instruments

Three instruments were used in this study:

Social exclusion perception scale: This scale comprises four items designed to measure the perception of social exclusion [19]. The items, based on definitions by Larry and McDonald, include statements such as “I feel I have been left out,” “I feel socially deprived,” “I feel I am disliked,” and “I feel excluded.” This scale serves as a global assessment tool that effectively captures individuals’ subjective experiences. Participants rate how frequently they experience these feelings on a 7-point scale, ranging from “never” [1] to “often” [7]. The reliability coefficient of this scale was reported as 0.85 in the study by Penhaligon and colleagues [19]. Research implementation procedure

To evaluate the effectiveness of existential group psychotherapy, this study utilized both an experimental group and a control group. Participants were selected from patients hospitalized in the acute wards of a psychiatric hospital, all of whom were deemed capable of participating in group therapy sessions based on a psychiatrist’s diagnosis.

The study began with a pre-test phase for both groups. After obtaining the necessary permissions, eligible participants were invited to attend an informational group session on a predetermined date. During this session, the study’s objectives, procedures, and ethical considerations were thoroughly explained. Participants then provided informed consent—where legally incapacitated patients were represented by their legal guardians—and completed demographic information forms as well as the Social Exclusion Perception and Anxiety questionnaires. The researcher provided detailed instructions on how to complete the questionnaires.

Following this, existential group psychotherapy sessions were conducted exclusively for the experimental group at Razi Psychiatric Hospital. These sessions were facilitated by a social worker and a psychiatrist, following the established intervention protocol. Upon completion of the intervention, a post-test was administered. Contact information for all participants was collected to facilitate follow-up. Two months after the intervention, participants were contacted and invited to voluntarily visit either Razi Psychiatric Hospital or the Kamrani Shush Clinic (an outpatient center affiliated with the hospital) to complete the questionnaires once more.

The control group completed the same questionnaires at three time points: pre-intervention, immediately post-intervention, and at the two-month follow-up. However, they did not receive any therapeutic intervention and continued to receive routine hospital care. To uphold ethical standards and respect participants’ rights, the control group was offered four therapy sessions identical to those provided to the experimental group after the conclusion of the study. Statistical analysis

Data were analyzed using descriptive statistics, including mean, median, variance, and standard deviation. Inferential statistical analyses were conducted using independent t-tests, paired t-tests, and repeated measures ANOVA. All analyses were performed using SPSS version 22.

Ethical considerations

All procedures involving human participants complied with the ethical standards of institutional and national research committees and adhered to the principles outlined in the 1964 Helsinki Declaration and its subsequent amendments or comparable ethical standards. This study did not involve any animal subjects.

The research protocol was approved by the University of Social Welfare and Rehabilitation Sciences Ethics Committee under the code IR.USWR.REC.1402.216. Additionally, the study was registered in the Iranian Registry of Clinical Trials (IRCT) under the registration number IRCT20230411057882N1 on March 1, 2024.

Informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. For individuals deemed legally incapacitated based on forensic medical evaluations, consent was provided by their legal guardians. Key ethical considerations included the right to withdraw from the study at any time, respect for participant privacy and confidentiality, prevention of harm, avoidance of discrimination, and protection from exploitation. Furthermore, upon study completion, members of the control group were invited via telephone to participate in educational sessions similar to those provided to the experimental group, with the majority accepting and attending.

Result

The study sample comprised 55 individuals diagnosed with psychiatric disorders and comorbid substance use disorder, with ages ranging from 21 to 75 years. The mean age of participants was 42.62 ± 12.98 years in the control group and 41.73 ± 11.50 years in the experimental group. The duration of substance use ranged from 2 to 35 years among all participants. The mean duration of substance use was 14.03 ± 8.9 years in the control group and 13.30 ± 7.62 years in the experimental group. In terms of gender distribution, 76.92% of the experimental group were male and 23.07% female, whereas the control group consisted of 72.42% males and 27.58% females. Similarly, there was no significant difference in the mean anxiety scores between the two groups at the pre-test stage. Yet, the post-test results revealed a significant reduction in anxiety scores in the experimental group relative to the control group. This reduction was maintained in the follow-up phase, with the experimental group continuing to report lower anxiety scores than the control group.

To determine the statistical significance of these changes over time, a repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted. Prior to performing this test, the necessary assumptions were assessed. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test results confirmed that the assumption of normality was met for both variables in the experimental and control groups across all three stages (pre-test, post-test, and follow-up) (p > 0.05). Furthermore, the assumptions of sphericity and homogeneity of variances were verified through Mauchly’s test and Levene’s test, respectively, and both were satisfied (p > 0.05).

Table 3 indicates that the within-subject effects across the three assessment time points, as well as the interaction effects between time and group, were statistically significant for both perceived rejection and anxiety variables (p < 0.001). Additionally, the between-group effects were significant, reflecting meaningful differences between the experimental and control groups in both perceived rejection and anxiety scores (p < 0.001).

Similarly, for the anxiety variable, significant differences were observed between the pre-test and post-test scores (p < 0.001), the pre-test and follow-up scores (p < 0.001), and the post-test and follow-up scores (p = 0.01). Discussion

This study suggests that existential group psychotherapy may contribute to reductions in anxiety and perceived rejection among individuals with psychiatric disorders and comorbid substance use disorder.

The findings of this research support the idea that all individuals have the capacity for self-awareness, which they can use to enhance their quality of life. Each person possesses a unique identity that is recognized only through relationships with others. Individuals need to continuously reconstruct themselves, as the meaning of life is constantly evolving, and anxiety is an inherent part of the human condition. These findings may be interpreted within an existential framework, which views psychological difficulties as being related to challenges in personal responsibility and life direction. Substance use disorder is one such issue that, when combined with psychiatric disorders, exacerbates individuals’ difficulties.

The present findings may reflect therapeutic processes emphasized in existential psychotherapy, including meaning-making, personal responsibility, self-awareness, and authentic interpersonal engagement.The results showed that existential therapists aim to identify and address various symptoms such as excessive anxiety, apathy, alienation, nihilism, social withdrawal, shame, substance use disorder, hopelessness, depression, guilt, anger, rage, hatred, irritability, purposelessness, psychosis, and violence. They also focus on fostering life-enhancing experiences such as relationships, love, caring, commitment, courage, creativity, power, will, presence, spirituality, individuality, self-actualization, authenticity, acceptance, excellence, and respect.

The findings are consistent with those of previous studies conducted by Alami et al. [20], Sadri et al. [3], Bayanfar et al. [23], Karavalaki et al. [12], Wesselmann et al. [18], as well as the theoretical framework proposed by Irvin Yalom [7]. Alami and colleagues compared the effectiveness of existential group therapy and cognitive-behavioral group therapy in increasing life satisfaction and reducing social rejection among patients receiving methadone maintenance treatment. Their results indicated that existential group therapy significantly enhanced life satisfaction and reduced feelings of social rejection in these patients. Furthermore, meaning therapy was shown to alleviate symptoms, enable individuals to cope with fundamental life challenges, promote well-being, and improve mental health, ultimately contributing to greater life satisfaction among individuals with substance use disorders. Participation in existential group therapy sessions was found to strengthen life goals and values. Additionally, interaction with peers promoted personal responsibility. The process of selecting goals and discovering meaning in life may assist individuals who have experienced failure or emotional distress in overcoming depression and assuming greater responsibility for their lives [20]. Unlike CBT, which primarily focuses on cognitive restructuring and symptom reduction, existential psychotherapy emphasizes meaning-making, responsibility, authenticity, and existential concerns such as isolation and freedom. These features may be particularly relevant for individuals with psychiatric disorders and co-occurring substance use disorders.

In Sadri et al.‘s study, which examined the effectiveness of existential therapy on loneliness and anxiety, it was found that existential therapy significantly reduced feelings of loneliness and death anxiety in the experimental group compared to the control group [3]. Bayanfar et al. evaluated the impact of group-based existential therapy on social rejection, social adaptation, social competence, and cognitive avoidance in elderly individuals with empty nest syndrome. Their findings indicated that existential mindfulness-based therapy effectively reduced social rejection, enhanced social competence, and decreased cognitive avoidance in this population [23]. Karavalaki et al.’s research on integrated existential therapy for adolescents with substance abuse demonstrated that group-based existential therapy improved mental health and reduced anxiety among these adolescents [12]. Wesselmann et al. highlighted that all humans have a fundamental need for belonging, which existential therapy can strengthen. Their study emphasized that daily efforts to build and maintain stable social connections are central to fulfilling this need. This need for belonging is particularly pronounced among individuals with substance use disorders due to their frequent experiences of loneliness. However, the inherently social nature of humans also has challenges, as interpersonal interactions are not always positive. Social rejection—being physically or emotionally separated from others—often elicits immediate painful emotions such as anger, sadness, and shame, and threatens essential psychological needs beyond belonging, including positive self-esteem, control, and a sense of meaningful existence. Individuals who experience chronic rejection are prone to loneliness, social anxiety, fear, and anticipation of future rejection. They also tend to exhibit elevated levels of depressive symptoms, helplessness, alienation, and perceived existential meaninglessness [18].

The findings may also be interpreted within the framework of Yalom’s existential psychotherapy theory [7]. Yalom distinguishes between being alone and isolation. Isolation arises from social, geographical, or cultural factors that disrupt intimacy. Individuals may lack social skills or possess personality traits that hinder intimate connections. However, existential loneliness is a deeper experience. It occurs when individuals confront the realization that there are moments when no one in the world is thinking about them, triggering a profound fear of existential aloneness.

Many people choose to remain in unsatisfying interpersonal relationships simply to use these connections as a shield against existential loneliness. Within this context, psychotherapy emphasizes the professional boundaries of the therapist-client relationship, incorporating discussions about confrontation, authenticity, honesty, accurate empathy, and unconditional positive regard. Yalom asserts that a crucial aspect of therapy is helping clients understand that there comes a point when the therapist can offer nothing further. Both in therapy and in life, individuals face the inescapable reality of acting and existing alone. To foster personal responsibility, individuals must confront their existential loneliness [7, 21, 25].Given the relatively small sample size and the outcomes assessed, the present findings should be considered preliminary and interpreted with caution until replicated in larger studies.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, participants were recruited through convenience sampling from a single psychiatric hospital, which may limit the generalizability of the findings. Second, although the sample size was determined through a priori power analysis, studies with larger samples are needed to increase statistical precision and external validity. Third, follow-up assessments were limited to two months, preventing conclusions regarding the long-term sustainability of treatment effects. Fourth, the sample included heterogeneous psychiatric diagnoses and different types of substance use disorders, which may have influenced treatment outcomes. Finally, the study focused on perceived rejection and anxiety; future studies should include additional outcomes such as PTSD symptoms, quality of life, psychosocial functioning, and relapse-related indicators.

Conclusion

The findings of this study suggest that existential group psychotherapy may help reduce perceived rejection and anxiety among individuals with psychiatric disorders and comorbid substance use disorder. Participants in the intervention group showed greater improvement in these outcomes than those in the control group. By emphasizing personal choice, responsibility, autonomy, and the search for meaning, existential psychotherapy may help individuals better cope with psychological and environmental challenges. Given the study’s sample size and the outcomes assessed, the findings should be considered preliminary and interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, existential group psychotherapy may be considered a complementary intervention in mental health and addiction treatment settings and may support recovery and overall well-being in individuals with comorbid disorders.
