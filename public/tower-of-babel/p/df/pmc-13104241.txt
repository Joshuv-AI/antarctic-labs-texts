Trends, magnitude and determinants of viral load suppression among HIV patients on highly active anti-retroviral therapy in Tigray region, Ethiopia: a retrospective study (2016–2024)
Authors: Kibriti Mehari Kidane, Araya Gebreyesus Wasihun, Dawit Wolday, Haftu Gebrehiwot, Hayelom Kahsay, Gebrecherkos Teame Gebrehiwot, Tewolde Wubayehu Woldearegay, Tsegay Berihu, Tsehaye Asmelash Dejene
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundViral load suppression (VLS) is a critical marker of treatment success among people living with HIV (PLWH), yet prolonged conflict and health-system disruptions can undermine ART continuity and monitoring. The Tigray region of Ethiopia experienced severe service interruptions between 2020 and 2022, making an assessment of suppression patterns essential.ObjectivesTo examine the magnitude, temporal trends, and determinants of viral load suppression among people living with HIV receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART) in the Tigray region. from 2016 to 2024.MethodWe analyzed routinely collected viral load data from ART sites across Tigray. Annual aggregated viral load results were used to describe temporal trends before, during, and after the regional conflict. For the analytic component, a cross-sectional dataset was constructed by retaining each client’s most recent viral load result. Included participants were HIV-positive individuals on ART with documented viral load, demographic, and clinical information. Trend patterns were summarized descriptively, and determinants of viral suppression were assessed using multivariable logistic regression. Model diagnostics, including assessment of multicollinearity and overall model fit, were conducted. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultBetween 2016 and 2024, 145,769 people living with HIV underwent viral load testing, of whom 127,644 (87.6%) achieved viral suppression. Among 23,222 children living with HIV, 18,797 (80.9%) were suppressed. Suppression increased steadily from 2016 to 2018, declined sharply during the conflict years (2021–2022), and showed partial recovery thereafter, though not to pre-conflict levels. Multivariable analysis identified advanced WHO stage, poor adherence, low baseline CD4 + count, and certain ART regimens as significant predictors of non-suppression.ConclusionViral suppression in Tigray remains below the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV and AIDS 95% target and below pre-conflict levels, reflecting the substantial impact of the 2020–2022 conflict on ART service continuity, monitoring, and adherence. Geographic disparities and conflict-related disruptions highlight the need for targeted recovery strategies, strengthened supply chains, and differentiated care models to restore and sustain optimal treatment outcomes.Keywords: Viral load suppression, HIV, HAART, Trends, Determinants, Magnitude, Tigray region, Ethiopia

Background

Globally, an estimated 39 million people were living with HIV in 2022 [1]. The introduction and scale-up of ART have dramatically improved survival rates and quality of life for PLWH, transforming HIV from a fatal condition to a manageable chronic illness [1, 2]. To further accelerate progress, the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV/AIDS has set ambitious targets to end the AIDS epidemic as a public health threat by 2030, including the “95-95-95” goals: 95% of all people living with HIV knowing their status, 95% of those diagnosed receiving sustained ART, and 95% of those achieving viral suppression [2, 3]. As of 2020, 66% of PLWH on ART achieved viral load suppression, increasing to 68% in 2021 [1].

A study report in 2021 indicated that sub-Saharan Africa (SSA) remains the global epicenter of the HIV epidemic, accounting for approximately 67% of the global total of PLWH [4]. In 2022, joint united nations programme on HIV and AIDS estimated that 25.6 million people in SSA were living with HIV out of the 39 million globally infected [5].The region was responsible for nearly 670,000 of the 1.5 million new infections and 280,000 of the 650,000 AIDS-related deaths reported globally in 2021 [6]. ART coverage has significantly improved across SSA, with approximately 20.8 million people receiving treatment as of 2022 [7]. However, gaps remain in achieving Joint United Nations Programme on HIV and AIDS 95-95-95 targets, as only 84% of PLWH in SSA know their status, 77% were on ART, and just 68% achieved viral suppression. Factors contributing to the high prevalence in SSA include limited access to healthcare, poverty, stigma, and inadequate resources for prevention and treatment programs [8]. Studies in SSA have shown varying rates of viral load suppression, influenced by factors such as ART adherence, duration on ART, and access to viral load monitoring [9].

In Ethiopia, the HIV epidemic has seen a steady decline but remains a significant public health concern [9]. According to estimates from the Ethiopian Public Health Institute (EPHI) for 2022–2023, approximately 610,350 people were living with HIV, with 8,257 new infections and 11,322 AIDS-related deaths annually. The national adult HIV prevalence was estimated at 0.9% [10]. Women account for more than half of these cases, and urban areas show a much higher prevalence (2.9%) compared to rural regions (0.4%) [11]. The regions most affected include Addis Ababa and Gambella, where prevalence rates are as high as 3.42% and 4.5%, respectively [10]. ART coverage in Ethiopia stands at about 78% [11], with viral suppression achieved in roughly 71% of those on treatment [12]. In Ethiopia, the Ethiopian National Consolidated Guidelines for comprehensive HIV prevention, care, and treatment [11] has set viral load suppression cut point of less than 1000 copies/ml.

The Tigray region in northern Ethiopia has faced significant challenges in its fight against HIV due to the conflict that began in November 2020 [6, 12]. Prior to the conflict, Tigray had made notable progress in expanding HIV services. As of 2021, there was a report of 50,703 people living with HIV in Tigray, approximately 43,000 were accessing ART drugs in the 141 ART clinic facilities [13]. The HIV/AIDS epidemic in Tigray showed sharp increase following the war crisis, the HIV prevalence more than doubling from a pre-war rate of 1.4% (2015–2020) to 3% by 2024–2025 [14]. This surge marks one of the fastest regional increases in sub-Saharan Africa and signals a devastating reversal of decades of progress that had once positioned Tigray as a national model for HIV prevention and control [15].

Viral load suppression is vital to preventing disease progression, reducing transmission, and improving health outcomes [6, 16]. It is also the most accurate method for monitoring ART effectiveness, surpassing clinical or CD4 + count monitoring [17]. Factors such as adherence, ART regimens, and socio-demographic characteristics influence suppression rates, and failure to suppress viral load may indicate poor adherence or drug resistance [18]. Research on viral load suppression among HIV patients on ART in Ethiopia’s Tigray region is limited [19]. One study found that about 74% of adolescents and adults on ART had achieved viral suppression, though this remains below WHO targets (95-95-95) [20].

Given the limited data on viral load suppression in the Tigray region, it is important to investigate the trends, magnitude and determinants of viral load suppression among HIV patients on ART in this specific context. The aim of this study is, therefore, to assess the trends, magnitude and determinants of viral load suppression among HIV patients on antiretroviral therapy in the Tigray Regional State, Northern Ethiopia, from 2016 to 2024. Temporal trends were examined using aggregated annual data, while determinants of viral suppression were analyzed using a cross-sectional dataset constructed by retaining each participant’s most recent viral load result.

Materials and methods

Study setting

Tigray, the northernmost regional state of Ethiopia, has been at the center of a devastating conflict escalated in November 2020.The region is administratively divided into seven zones (Mekelle-regional capital, Southern, Southeastern, Central, Eastern, Northwestern and Western Zones). These zones are further divided into 93 Woredas (districts) and towns, and 814 tabias (lower government’s administrative structure below the Woreda), also referred to as kebeles [16]. This war-torn region, bordered to the north by Eritrea, to the South by Amhara Regional State, to the east by Afar Regional State, and to the west by the Sudan [16], covers a surface area of 53,638 km2covers and the population of the region’s 7.1 million people [19–21]. Viral load data for this study were obtained from the Tigray Health Research Institute (THRI), Mekelle and Axum branches, which serve as the regional referral laboratories for HIV viral load testing.

Study design, period and population

A retrospective facility-based cross-sectional study was adopted to analyze HIV Viral Load electronic data of PLWH enrolled for viral load examination or testing in Tigray Health Research Institute Mekelle (THRI) and Axum branch, between January 2016 to December 2024, aiming to describe temporal trends in viral load suppression and identify factors associated with viral load non-suppression at the time of testing. The study population included all HIV-positive clients PLWH initiated ART at various health facilities in Tigray, had undergone Viral load test in THRI - Mekelle and Axum branch within the study period and as a result, had at least one documented viral load test result. Both male and female patients from all zones of Tigray were included.

Data structure and analytic unit

The viral load dataset obtained from the Tigray Health Research Institute consisted of test-level records, with each entry representing a single viral load measurement performed during the study period. Prior to data transfer, the dataset was fully de-identified by the data custodians, and all true patient identifiers were removed. As a result, repeated viral load tests belonging to the same individual could not be reliably linked across years or facilities. Because unique individuals could not be reconstructed, it was not possible to assemble a longitudinal cohort or determine how many tests belonged to each person. Given this structure, the analytic unit for this study was defined as a viral load test, not an individual. Logistic regression was therefore conducted at the test level to identify predictors associated with viral load suppression for each measurement event. Although some individuals may have contributed more than one test, these repeated observations could not be distinguished, and the analysis necessarily treated each test as an independent record. This limitation precluded the use of repeated-measures methods such as generalized estimating equations or mixed-effects models, which require identifiable within-person linkages. The potential impact of this constraint particularly the inability to account for within-subject correlation is acknowledged in the study’s Limitations section.

Eligibility criteria

The study included HIV-positive individuals who initiated ART between January 1, 2016, to December 31, 2024, and had at least one documented viral load test result during the study period. Patients with complete and valid records on key demographic, clinical, and treatment-related variables such as age, sex, and viral load results were eligible. Exclusion criteria were individuals those whose place of residence or treatment facility was outside the Tigray region (e.g., Afar Zone 2). Duplicate records or entries with conflicting or inconsistent data identified during data quality checks were also excluded from the analysis.

Data source

The viral load data used in this study were secondary and obtained from the Tigray Health Research Institute (THRI) in Mekelle and its branch office in Axum [22], the sole facility in the region responsible for viral load testing. THRI compiles HIV patient information through its Laboratory Information System (LIS), serving as the central repository for viral load monitoring data across the Tigray region. The dataset includes detailed records on demographics, ART initiation, clinical stage, CD4 + count, viral load measurements, treatment regimens, adherence status, and follow-up outcomes. Data were aggregated from health centers and hospitals across the region and de-identified prior to analysis to ensure confidentiality. Prior to analysis, data were checked to validate completeness and consistency.

Sampling technique

A total of 148,653 PLWH were enrolled for viral load (VL) testing at THRI -Mekelle and Axum Branch laboratories from 2016 to 2024. Of these, 325 individuals from other regions (specifically Afar/Zone-2) were excluded, resulting in 148,328 PLWH from Tigray Zones only. From this subset, 2,460 PLWH without any recorded viral load data were excluded, yielding a dataset of 145,868 PLWH with available VL results. An additional individuals whose not recorded properly their age were excluded to ensure data validity. Furthermore, 35 PLWHwho underwent VL testing before 2016 were excluded to align with the study period. Thus, a total of 145,769 PLWH with complete data from 2016 to 2024 were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Study variables

Outcome variable

The primary outcome variable in this study was viral load suppression status, a binary variable, categorized as “suppressed” or “unsuppressed. Viral suppression (virological success) was defined as having a plasma HIV-1 RNA level of less than 1,000 copies per milliliter of blood, in accordance with the Ethiopian National Consolidated Guidelines for comprehensive HIV prevention, care, and treatment. Individuals with viral loads equal to or above 1,000 copies/mL were classified as unsuppressed (virological failure) [23, 24]. This variable served as the key indicator of treatment success and was used to assess both the prevalence of viral suppression and its associated factors across the study population. We adopted the World Health Organization (WHO) defined virological failure with a threshold of 1000 copies/mL [25], that is, viral load with < 1000 copies/mL considered as virological success and ≥ 1000 copies /mL considered as virological failure.

Predictor variables

A wide range of independent variables were included to identify potential predictors of viral load suppression. These were broadly grouped into socio-demographic, clinical factors, and treatment-related categories. Socio-demographic variables included age categorized into groups: sex (male or female), Type of health facility where patients received treatment (Clinic, Health Center, Primary Hospital, General Hospital, Referral Hospital) and Zone (Southern, South Eastern, Mekelle, Eastern, Central, North Western and Western). These variables were used to explore disparities in viral suppression outcomes across different population segments. Clinical variables included baseline CD4 + cell count at ART initiation (classified as < 200, 200–349, 350–499, and ≥ 500 cells/mm³), WHO clinical stage at ART initiation (Stage I–IV), and the duration on ART (Below 1 year or otherwise), which captured the length of time an individual had been receiving antiretroviral therapy. The clinical stage and CD4 + count provided important information on disease progression at the start of treatment, while duration on ART was used to assess how sustained therapy affects viral suppression. Additionally, Treatment-related variables included the initial ART regimen type, adherence level, and participation in Enhanced Adherence Counseling (EAC), a targeted intervention aimed at supporting patients with previously high viral load results. These variables were selected based on clinical relevance, WHO standard guidelines for HIV patients [17, 26, 27], and availability within the Tigray Health Research Institute database.

Variable selection

Variables for the multivariable logistic regression model were selected a priori based on clinical relevance, evidence from prior literature, and availability in the Tigray Health Research Institute database. All selected variables were entered simultaneously into the model; no automated variable selection procedures (such as forward, backward, or stepwise methods) were used. This approach was considered appropriate given the large sample size and the study objective of estimating adjusted associations rather than developing a prognostic prediction model. Multicollinearity was assessed to ensure model stability.

Statistical method and analysis

Descriptive statistics were computed to summarize socio-demographic, clinical, and treatment-related characteristics of the study population. Frequencies and percentages were used for categorical variables, while means and standard deviations (SD) were used for continuous variables. Temporal trends in viral load suppression between 2016 and 2024 were described using annual aggregates of test-level data. Bivariate analysis was conducted to explore associations between viral load suppression status (suppressed vs. unsuppressed) and each independent variable. Chi-square tests (or Fisher’s exact tests, where appropriate) were used for categorical variables, and independent-sample t-tests were used for continuous variables, depending on normality. Model performance (fit) was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test [28], and model discrimination was evaluated using the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve [29]. Due to limitations in reliably linking all repeated tests to unique individuals across years and facilities, we were unable to apply generalized estimating equations (GEE) or mixed-effects models to account for within-subject correlation. As a result, the standard errors from the logistic regression model may be underestimated, and this is explicitly acknowledged as a limitation. Missing data were assessed for all study variables prior to analysis. Observations with missing values in the outcome variable (viral load suppression status) or in key predictor variables were excluded from the respective analyses using a complete-case approach. No statistical imputation was performed, as the proportion of missing data for most variables was low and the study relied on routinely collected secondary data. The crosstabulation results for both adults and children reveal significant shifts in viral suppression patterns across the pre-war, during-war, and post-war periods, reflecting the profound impact of the Tigray conflict on HIV service delivery. Among adults, viral suppression increased from 84.8% pre-war to 89.8% during the conflict and reached 93.3% post-war, while children showed a similar pattern, rising from 77.6% pre-war to 89.5% during the conflict and 89.9% afterward. Although these increases might suggest improved outcomes, they more likely reflect substantial reductions in viral load testing, where only clients who remained in care and were clinically stable were tested during the conflict period. Patients experiencing treatment interruption, displacement, or loss to follow-up, who are more likely to be unsuppressed, were underrepresented in testing data. This selective testing is supported by the significantly different distributions of viral suppression across periods for both groups (adults: χ² = 1701.66, p < 0.001; children: χ² = 444.02, p < 0.001) and modest but meaningful contingency coefficients (0.107 and 0.137, respectively). Together, these findings demonstrate that while apparent suppression improved during and after the conflict, these patterns likely mask underlying vulnerabilities and service disruptions, especially among populations who faced the greatest barriers to continued HIV care during the crisis.

Epidemiological trends of viral load status (2016–2024)

The trends illustrate the temporal dynamics of viral load suppression and non-suppression in Tigray from 2016 to 2024. Viral suppression rose sharply from 2016, reaching its highest level in 2018, a period when HIV services, supply chains, and laboratory systems were fully functional across the region. A gradual decline followed through 2020, coinciding with the period of political instability leading up to the outbreak of the Tigray conflict in November 2020. A marked downturn occurred during the peak conflict years (2021–2022), when the health system experienced severe disruption, including facility closures, ART stockouts, communication blackouts, and the suspension of viral load testing. Suppression dropped to its lowest point in 2022, reflecting the collapse of routine HIV service delivery. Post-war, a partial recovery emerged in 2023 following the Pretoria peace agreement and the re-establishment of ART supply chains and laboratory services; however, suppression levels in 2024 remained below pre-war peaks, indicating persistent gaps in service restoration. In contrast, unsuppressed cases showed smaller fluctuations, increasing modestly up to 2019, dipping during the war, as testing capacity sharply declined, and stabilizing at relatively low levels throughout 2023–2024 (Fig. 4).

Similarly, Fig. 5 presents the temporal trends of viral load suppression among children living with HIV. Suppression increased rapidly from 2016 and peaked around 2018, followed by a gradual decline through 2020. As with adults, a sharp downturn occurred during the conflict years (2021–2022), when widespread displacement, health facility damage, and interruptions in pediatric ART services severely limited viral load monitoring. Suppression reached its lowest level in 2022, consistent with the collapse of service delivery during this period. Post-war, a partial rebound is visible in 2023 as health facilities gradually resumed functionality, though suppression levels in 2024 still lag behind the pre-war peak. Unsuppressed cases among children rose moderately until 2018, decreased during the conflict, largely reflecting reduced testing rather than true clinical improvement and remained relatively stable at low levels in the post-war recovery phase (Fig. 5).

Using the zonal time-series data (2016–2024), we fitted a binomial logistic regression model with calendar year (centered at 2018), zone, and a to formally test whether trends in viral non-suppression differed by geography. In the reference zone (Central), the odds of viral non-suppression decreased by about 15% per year (OR per year = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.83–0.87, p < 0.001). In contrast, the Western zone showed virtually no improvement over time (OR per year = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.93–1.05), and the interaction was statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating a markedly flatter trend compared with Central. Mekelle and North Western zones exhibited steeper declines in non-suppression (Mekelle: OR per year = 0.87, 95% CI: 0.86–0.89; North Western: OR per year = 0.79, 95% CI: 0.77–0.81; both p < 0.001). At the pre-war reference year (2018), the odds of viral non-suppression in Western were 44% higher than in Mekelle (OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.34–1.55). Together, these findings statistically confirm that temporal improvements in viral suppression were uneven across zones, with Western Tigray experiencing both higher baseline non-suppression and significantly weaker gains over time compared with better-performing zones such as Mekelle and North Western.

Model performance and parameter estimates

Model performance and sensitivity analysis

This section focused on identifying the key determinants of viral load suppression among people living with HIV using a multivariable binary logistic regression model. The model demonstrated excellent explanatory power, with a Nagelkerke R² of 0.792, indicating that nearly 79% of the variability in viral load status (suppressed vs. unsuppressed) was accounted for by the included predictors. Furthermore, the Hosmer and Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was non-significant (χ² = 16.088, p = 0.41), suggesting an adequate fit between the observed and predicted outcomes. To address potential overfitting due to highly predictive variables, we conducted a sensitivity analysis excluding the variable “ART Clients Requiring Targeted Viral Load Testing” (and, in a second model, also excluding “Suspected virological failure Failure”). Removing these variables reduced the Nagelkerke R² from 0.792 to 0.61, as expected. However, the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of key determinants (such as adherence level, baseline CD4 count, WHO stage, age, and sex) remained materially unchanged. These findings confirm that the main conclusions of our primary model are robust.

As seen in Table 4 several individual- and facility-level factors were significantly associated with viral load suppression status. Patients who had been on ART for at least one year had markedly lower odds of being unsuppressed (OR = 0.28, 95% CI: 0.23–0.33, p < 0.002), highlighting the critical role of sustained treatment in achieving and maintaining viral control. In contrast, male patients exhibited higher odds of viral non-suppression compared to females (OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.80–0.91, p < 0.001).

Health facility type was also associated with viral suppression outcomes. Patients receiving care at primary and referral hospitals exhibited higher odds of being unsuppressed compared to those attending health centers (OR = 1.23 for both; p < 0.01), Conversely, general hospitals were associated with slightly reduced odds of non-suppression (OR = 0.91, p = 0.017) (Table 4).

Geographic variation was notable. Compared to the Southern zone, individuals from the Mekelle zone (OR = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.62–0.76, p < 0.001) and Central zone (OR = 0.81, 95% CI: 0.73–0.91, p < 0.001) were significantly less likely to be unsuppressed. In contrast, no significant differences were observed for the South Eastern, Eastern, North Western, or Western zones.

Clinical status and treatment behavior were among the strongest predictors of viral suppression. As expected, individuals who underwent targeted viral load testing had substantially higher proportions of non-suppression in descriptive analysis, reflecting the programmatic use of targeted testing for cases with suspected virological failure. Because this variable lies on the causal pathway and can introduce collider bias, it was not included in the multivariable regression model. In contrast, advancing WHO clinical stages (II–IV) remained strongly associated with higher odds of unsuppressed viral load in the adjusted model, highlighting the impact of disease severity on treatment outcomes.

Compared to those with poor, patients with fair adherence (85–94%) were 53% less likely to be unsuppressed (OR = 0.47), while those with good adherence (> 95%) had an even greater reduction in risk (OR = 0.21, p < 0.001).

Several ART regimens were associated with varying suppression outcomes. Notably, regimen 1 g (OR = 9.17) and 1f (OR = 2.94) were associated with substantially higher odds of unsuppressed viral load, potentially reflecting their use in more complex or treatment-resistant cases. The “Other” category also showed a significant association (OR = 2.71), while some regimens (e.g., 2f, 2 h, 2i) were not statistically significant.

Higher baseline CD4 + counts were associated with better viral suppression (OR = 0.86 per unit increase in log-transformed CD4 + count, p < 0.001). Moreover, age demonstrated a protective gradient, with progressively lower odds of unsuppressed status in older age groups. Individuals aged ≥ 61 years had the lowest odds (OR = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.21–0.39, p < 0.001) (Table 4).

Discussion

This study found a viral load suppression rate of 87.6% among people living with HIV in the Tigray region and in the same period, 23,222 children living with HIV were tested, with 18,797 (80.9%) achieving suppression and 4,425(19.1%) remaining unsuppressed. According to the Joint United Nations Programmed on HIV/AIDS 95-95-95 strategy, the final “95” aims for 95% of individuals on antiretroviral therapy to achieve viral suppression, with the overarching goal of ending the AIDS epidemic by 2030 [6]. our results were found to be lower comparing this target and the lower suppression rate observed among children highlights an important treatment gap. underscoring both the resilience of HIV programs and the heightened vulnerability of pediatric populations to service disruptions.

Our finding showed similarity with a study conducted in Addis Ababa which reported a Viral load suppuration rate of 88% [9], However, in comparison to an earlier report from Tigray, the current finding showed a declining trend. A pre-conflict study in Tigray (2019) reported viral suppression levels exceeding 91% [13, 25, 31], suggesting that Tigray was on course to meet global targets before the war crisis. When compared to the trend analysis of HIV infection and antiretroviral treatment outcomes in the Amhara region, Northeast Ethiopia, which reported a suppression rate of 94.2% in 2021, the lower suppression rate observed in our study may be due to differences in study period, population characteristics, sample size, regional healthcare service delivery, and the impact of war and conflict in the Tigray region [32].

The decline to 87.6% among PLWH and 80.9% among CLWH in this study is likely due to the armed conflict between 2020 and 2022, which significantly disrupted healthcare services, supply chains, and follow-up care for both PLWH and CLWH.

The conflict in Tigray resulted in widespread destruction of health infrastructure, large-scale displacement, and prolonged interruptions in access to essential medications, including ART [11, 13, 25]. These disruptions likely contributed to the observed decline in viral suppression and the relatively high proportion (12.4%) of individuals with unsuppressed viral loads. These individuals could face increased risks of disease progression, HIV transmission, and the development of drug resistance. Similar patterns have been observed in other conflict-affected regions such as South Sudan and the Central African Republic, where prolonged instability significantly undermined ART coverage and viral suppression efforts [33].

The 87.6% suppression rate reported by this study is also higher than those observed in several comparable, resource-limited settings. For instance, a study research conducted in the Amhara region of northern Ethiopia showed a suppression rate of approximately 79% [16, 23] and in Nigeria reported a VLS rate of 75.6% [34] indicating regional disparities even within Ethiopia. In contrast, studies in more challenging contexts such as areas marked by ongoing conflict, limited healthcare infrastructure, and extreme poverty have reported even lower suppression rates [35–37].

Another study from Amhara region of Ethiopia reported the incidence of viral load suppression among HIV-positive adults undergoing enhanced adherence counseling.

The findings in West Gojjam Zone reports that 51.73% of participants achieved viral load suppression after EAC [38], which is considerably lower compared to rates observed in the current results 84.7%.

Likewise, a study in Kenya showed 75% of ART recipients had achieved viral suppression, despite consistent care engagement [9]. In Nigeria and Malawi, suppression rates between 70% and 80% have often been linked to systemic issues such as drug stockouts, inadequate laboratory capacity, and high rates of loss to follow-up [22, 39].

In this study a view of the temporal trend reveals a steady improvement in viral suppression from 2016, peaking in 2018, followed by a gradual decline through 2021 and a significant drop in 2022. This downturn coincides with the outbreak and intensification of armed conflict in Tigray, which disrupted health services, supply chains, and patient monitoring. Notably, a partial recovery in suppression rates was observed in 2023. Significant and sustained efforts are needed to return to pre-conflict levels of viral suppression. Rebuilding health infrastructure, re-engaging lost-to-follow-up patients, and addressing both psychosocial and logistical barriers to treatment adherence will be essential to achieving Joint United Nations Programme on HIV and AIDS 95-95-95 goals in the region [25, 40–42].

The initial rise in suppression up to 2018 reported by this study aligns with national advancements in ART coverage and viral load monitoring, as documented by the Ethiopian Public Health Institute and Joint United Nations Programme on HIV and AIDS [9]. Conversely, the subsequent decline reflects trends seen in other conflict-affected settings, where violence, displacement, and systemic disruption have undermined HIV care continuity. For instance, studies in South Sudan and the Central African Republic have linked conflict-related healthcare interruptions with lower ART retention and diminished viral suppression [5, 43].

In the current result Females had a higher suppression rate (89.1%) compared to males (84.3%), male patients were more likely to be unsuppressed (OR = 0.85, 95% CI: 0.80–0.91, p < 0.001), consistent with prior studies indicating sex-based disparities in ART adherence and care engagement. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in KwaZulu-Natal and Ehlanzeni, South Africa [44, 45] which may be attributed to better adherence to treatment and greater engagement in care among women.

These disruptions might have impeded patients’ ability to maintain adherence to ART and attend follow-up visits, contributing to treatment failure and a potential rise in drug resistance [24, 41]. Nevertheless, the relatively stable proportion of individuals with unsuppressed viral loads across the study years showing only moderate variation suggests that a core subset of patients may have retained access to ART through informal or humanitarian channels.

Geographic disparities in viral suppression were evident in this study. Individuals from Mekelle and Central zones had significantly lower odds of viral non-suppression compared to those in the Southern zone. This may reflect stronger ART program implementation or better access to healthcare services in more urbanized or well-resourced areas. These findings align with previous studies in other parts of Ethiopia and Kenya, where urban centers and referral facilities have demonstrated better viral suppression outcomes due to superior infrastructure, consistent drug availability, and access to specialized healthcare personnel [10, 23, 46, 47].

Conversely, the lack of significant differences in suppression rates across other zones (Eastern, South Eastern, North Western may indicate common challenges in rural healthcare delivery o [12, 48]. However, the situation in the Western zone may differ, as available data is limited to the pre-war period in Tigray. Since 2022, there was no data from the Western zone due to its exclusion from the Interim Government of Tigray, making data access difficult.

Clinical and treatment-related factors observed in this study are consistent with both national and international literature. Patients who underwent targeted viral load testing typically initiated due to suspected treatment failure had significantly higher odds of non-suppression (OR = 3.15), reaffirming the association between clinical suspicion and confirmed treatment challenges.

Similar findings have been reported in Ethiopian studies, highlighting the utility of targeted testing in identifying individuals requiring regimen changes or adherence [25]. Another study in Ethiopia, has demonstrated the utility of targeted viral load testing in detecting individuals requiring regimen modifications or adherence support [49]. Highlights the importance of targeted testing in such context.

In our study an advanced WHO clinical stage was also strongly associated with increased risk of viral non-suppression. This relationship between disease progression and poor treatment outcomes has been widely documented. For instance, a study in Uganda found that patients classified under WHO stages III and IV were more likely to have unsuppressed viral loads [50], likely due to prolonged disease duration, opportunistic infections, or immune system exhaustion.

This current study showed adherence to be one of the most critical predictors of viral suppression. Patients with good adherence (≥ 95%) had a 79% lower likelihood of viral non-suppression compared to those with poor adherence, which is consistent with both Ethiopian and global studies [51, 52]. For example, a meta-analysis by Ortego et al. (2011) demonstrated that individuals with ≥ 95% adherence were up to five times more likely to achieve viral suppression than those with suboptimal adherence [30]. This underscores adherence as a fundamental determinant of treatment success.

Certain ART regimens, such as 1 g and 1f, were associated with higher odds of viral non-suppression. While this could suggest reduced efficacy, these regimens are often prescribed in more complex cases or following first-line treatment failure. Thus, they may indicate a more treatment-resistant population rather than reflect the inherent ineffectiveness of the drugs themselves. Similar trends have been reported in South Africa, where patients on ART treatment or salvage regimens experienced lower suppression rates [53] which could be due to prior resistance or advanced disease stages.

In this study Immunologic status, particularly baseline CD4 + count, was also predictive of suppression outcomes. Higher CD4 + counts were associated with improved viral control, emphasizing the importance of early diagnosis and prompt initiation of ART. This finding supports WHO guidelines, which advocate for early treatment initiation regardless of CD4 + count to preserve immune function and enhance outcomes [29].

And in this current study age appeared to be a protective factor, with older individuals especially those aged 61 years and above demonstrating lower odds of viral non-suppression. This could reflect greater adherence consistency, prolonged engagement with healthcare services, or enhanced treatment literacy among older adults [53–55]. Studies in Ethiopia and Tanzania have similarly found that younger populations, particularly those aged 15–24, experience higher rates of non-suppression due to behavioral, psychosocial, challenges [28, 56]. The current result emphasizes both the resilience of ART programs and the critical importance of targeted interventions in underserved and conflict-affected areas, with special attention to children and adolescents.

In the current study Pregnant and breastfeeding women showed higher suppression rates (91.2% and 92.5%, respectively) compared to the general population. Similarly in a study of pregnant women in Amhara region, viral suppression was ~ 90.9% [57]. A national-level study conducted in Ethiopia reported even higher suppression rates of 96.8% and 96.5%, which exceed our findings [41]. This difference may be attributed to variations in timing of viral load measurement, sample size, areas could be conflict-affected areas, which may face greater challenges in treatment adherence, service access. Generally This is the largest study ever made in the region which provides evidence on which could support policy makers at sub nationals and national levels.

Limitation

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the analysis relied on routinely collected viral load data, which may contain missing, incomplete, or inaccurately recorded clinical or demographic variables despite rigorous data cleaning. Second, the dataset for the Western zone only covers the period from 2016 to 2020, meaning that all results reported for this zone reflect the pre-war situation. As a result, the Western zone could not be included in trend analyses for the conflict and post-conflict periods, which may limit comparability across zones. Third, because some individuals contributed multiple viral load tests over the study period and we could not fully account for within-patient correlation (e.g., using generalized estimating equations or mixed-effects models), our logistic regression analysis treated test-level observations as independent. This may have led to underestimation of standard errors and a potential inflation of Type I error. Fourth, clinical predictors such as adherence measurements, regimen changes, and opportunistic infections may have been inconsistently captured across facilities, potentially influencing the precision of model estimates. Fifth, the conflict significantly disrupted health services, supply chains, and laboratory operations in Tigray, which may have introduced selection bias, individuals able to access ART and viral load testing during the conflict years may not be fully representative of the broader PLWH population. Additionally, the retrospective design limits the ability to infer causality between predictors and viral load outcomes. Finally, although the study included a large regional dataset, individuals those with unrecorded viral load tests were not captured.

Conclusion

This study found a viral load suppression rate of 87.6% among PLWH In the same period, 23,222 CLWH were tested, with 18,797 (80.9%) achieving suppression and 4,425 (19.1%) remaining unsuppressed, lower than pre-conflict levels (> 91%) and below the Joint United Nations Programme on HIV and AIDS 95% target. The 2020–2022 conflict disrupted ART services, supply chains, and monitoring, contributing to reduced adherence and care continuity. Suppression varied by geography, with urban areas faring better than rural, conflict-affected zones. Predictors of non-suppression included poor adherence, advanced disease stage, low baseline CD4 + count, targeted testing, and certain ART regimens. Despite setbacks, even though the prewar was high suppuration status partial recovery in 2023 suggests progress toward united nations programme on HIV/AIDS goals are possible with strengthened ART services in underserved areas, restored supply chains, and enhanced adherence support.

Recommendation

strengthen viral suppression and advance progress toward the UNAIDS 95-95-95 targets, health facilities should prioritize Enhanced Adherence Counseling for patients with high viral loads or poor adherence, and establish structured follow-up systems supported by timely appointment scheduling. Consistent viral load testing must be carried out in line with national guidelines to ensure early detection of treatment failure. Targeted support should be provided to patients at higher risk of virological failure, including those with low baseline CD4 counts or those receiving complex ART regimens. Improving healthcare worker capacity through training on WHO staging, ART management, and standardized viral load monitoring is essential for enhancing the quality and uniformity of HIV care across facilities. Pediatric patients and other high-risk groups also require continuous, close follow-up to achieve optimal treatment outcomes. Future research should explore the effects of conflict on viral load monitoring and continuity of HIV care by collecting prospective conflict-related data and linking it with facility-level damage assessments and patient-level ART outcomes. Implementing these targeted and system-level strategies can improve patient retention, increase viral suppression, and support progress toward epidemic control.
