The Relationship Between Pulmonary Spiculation and Central Fibrosis, Along With Other Clinicopathological Features, in Invasive Lung Adenocarcinoma and Its Prognostic Significance
Authors: Hanbo Le, Yongkui Zhang, Heping Wang, Yuanyuan Wen, Liyong Qian, Hui He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundSpiculation is a critical radiological feature in invasive adenocarcinoma (IAC). This study explores its pathological association with central fibrosis subtypes to refine prognostic evaluation.Materials and MethodsA total of 327 cases of IAC were divided into spiculated (n = 158) and non‐spiculated (n = 169) groups. Associations between spiculation, central fibrosis, intratumoral fibrosis, and other factors were analyzed. Multivariate logistic regression was used to identify contributors to spiculation formation. Survival was assessed using Kaplan–Meier curves and Cox regression. Propensity score matching (PSM) was utilized to minimize confounding variables.ResultsThe spiculated group showed higher rates of pleural indentation, central fibrosis > 5 mm, and increased intratumoral fibrosis, collagen content in both the original and matched cohorts (all p < 0.05), while spiculation was associated with elastin content in the original cohort (p = 0.020), this correlation disappeared after PSM. Multivariate logistic regression identified central fibrosis, collagen content, pleural indentation, tumor size, and lymph node metastasis as independent predictors of spiculation. Spiculation was associated with poorer survival in the original cohort (p < 0.001), but this significance disappeared after PSM (p = 0.860). In Cox analysis, spiculation was not an independent prognostic factor. Alveolar collapse > 5 mm was an independent protective factor, while intratumoral fibrosis and lymph node metastasis were adverse prognostic factors.ConclusionSpiculation is closely associated with central fibrosis. It shows a stronger correlation with collagen content than with elastin content. While reflecting underlying aggressiveness, spiculation lacks independent prognostic value. Integrating spiculation with central fibrosis is essential for precise risk stratification.Keywords: alveolar collapse, central collagenous fibrosis, central fibrosis, elastin fiber, pulmonary spiculation

Introduction

As the utilization of CT imaging becomes more widespread and public awareness of health screenings increases, the detection rate of lung nodules has consistently risen. Invasive lung adenocarcinoma is the most prevalent type of lung cancer [1]. Effective identification of malignant invasive adenocarcinoma (IAC) through CT not only enhances patient survival rates, but also substantially alleviates the societal burden associated with late‐stage cancer treatment [2, 3, 4].

In the radiological evaluation of pulmonary nodules, pulmonary spiculation has long been acknowledged as a hallmark of malignancy. Pulmonary spiculation is characterized by fine, short, radial, unbranched lines that emanate from the tumor periphery and extend into the surrounding lung parenchyma, visible on the lung window, yet unattached to the pleura [5, 6]. Prior pathological investigations have attributed spiculation to fibrous bands resulting from the tumor's fibroblastic response or infiltration of neoplastic cells into adjacent bronchovascular or lymphatic pathways [5, 6]. The underlying mechanism is principally linked to collagen fiber proliferation within the nodule, with no reported association with elastic fibers [7]. However, our preliminary clinical observations have revealed a correlation between pulmonary spiculation in the IAC and the presence of central fibrosis (CF), potentially involving the proliferation of elastic fibers.

CF, also known as a central scar, frequently occurs in the core or subpleural region of lung adenocarcinoma. It is categorized into two distinct forms: one is characterized by fibrous bands primarily composed of collagen fibers and fibroblast proliferation, often undergoing hyalinization, and the other is primarily composed of elastic fibers, commonly referred to as alveolar collapse (AC) [8, 9, 10] Alveolar collapse denotes a significant reduction in alveolar air volume, leading to tumor cell degeneration and an accumulation of elastic fibers at the tumor center, manifesting as diffuse fibrosis [9]. While early studies linked central fibrosis to poor prognosis [8, 10, 11], recent perspectives have associated it with more favorable outcomes [12, 13, 14]. This contradiction likely arises from the imprecise understanding and classification of CF, with previous research primarily focusing on collagen‐dominant fibrosis and often overlooking the role of elastic fiber‐composed fibrosis [13]. As previously highlighted by Takise et al. [15] and Shimosato et al. [8], the extent of collagenization in central fibrosis serves as a vital prognostic marker for lung adenocarcinoma, and its clinical importance even outweighs tumor size.

However, these early studies on CF in lung adenocarcinoma were conducted prior to the establishment of the current standardized lung adenocarcinoma classification system [8, 9, 10, 11, 15]. Consequently, the study cohorts may have included early‐stage lesions such as minimally invasive adenocarcinoma (MIA). Given the significant differences in stromal remodeling and biological behavior between MIA and IAC [16, 17], such sample heterogeneity might have masked the true fibrotic characteristics inherent to IAC. More importantly, these investigations primarily focused on the relationship between fibrosis and clinical outcomes (e.g., lymph node metastasis and survival rates) [10, 11], failing to explore the radiopathologic mechanistic links between internal fibrotic patterns and external radiological manifestations, such as pulmonary spiculation. Furthermore, from a pathological assessment perspective, earlier studies predominantly defined CF based solely on its presence or absence [8, 15]. However, small foci of CF measuring approximately 1–3 mm are frequently observed in lung adenocarcinoma and are generally considered to lack clinical significance [11, 13]. This limitation makes it difficult to accurately reflect the complex stromal heterogeneity within IAC. Meanwhile, CF has been largely disregarded by imaging specialists in prior research, and existing imaging studies on spiculation frequently include a variety of heterogeneous tumors, such as squamous cell carcinoma, in which CF is rarely present [8]. The combination of coarse pathological evaluation and mixed imaging samples has collectively limited the accuracy of research findings regarding pulmonary spiculation. Therefore, the specific relationship between radiological spiculation and central fibrosis subtypes remains to be further elucidated.

In light of these considerations, our study focused on a relatively homogeneous cohort of invasive non‐mucinous adenocarcinoma to mitigate the confounding effects observed in prior research. Diverging from previous investigations that primarily examined the relationship between spiculation and nonspecific intratumoral fibrosis [5, 7], this study directs its attention toward the structural subtypes of CF. By employing a semiquantitative histomorphological approach, we aimed to provide a more granular analysis of the pathological substrates underlying pulmonary spiculation. We hypothesize that the formation of pulmonary spiculation in IAC is not driven exclusively by isolated collagen proliferation. Instead, it is also modulated by specific subtypes of central fibrosis, appearing either as collagenous fiber hyperplasia or as elastin‐rich alveolar collapse. Furthermore, we posit that the clinical significance of spiculation, particularly regarding its capacity to indicate tumor invasiveness or prognostic risk, may be contingent upon these distinct underlying morphological characteristics of CF. Nevertheless, systematic radiopathologic evidence characterizing these specific associations remains insufficient in the existing literature.

This study aimed to meticulously investigate the relationship between pulmonary spiculation and CF. By incorporating propensity score matching (PSM) and multivariable logistic regression, we sought to identify independent pathological drivers of spiculation while evaluating their latent impact on patient prognosis. From a translational medicine perspective, investigating the radiopathologic nexus between spiculation and specific fibrotic patterns holds promise for enhancing the differential diagnosis of pulmonary nodules. This approach enables noninvasive preoperative inference of the tumor's stromal remodeling state, thereby providing a robust rationale for individualized surgical strategies, such as the selection between sublobar resection and standard lobectomy. Furthermore, our findings may provide theoretical support for the development of artificial intelligence (AI)‐assisted diagnostic models, ultimately enhancing the clinical utility of future intelligent systems in interpreting complex lesions.

Material and Methods

Study Participants

This retrospective study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Zhoushan Hospital (No. 2022446) with a waiver for informed consent, analyzed the clinical, pathological, and CT data of IAC patients who underwent surgical resection between January 2015 and December 2017. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) diagnosis of invasive non‐mucinous adenocarcinoma, (2) solitary primary tumor, (3) availability of all relevant microscopic sections for review, and (4) absence of prior adjuvant or neoadjuvant therapy. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) history of other cancers; (2) tumors with a diameter exceeding 3 cm based on gross pathological examination; (3) incomplete or conservative resections; (4) lack of lymph node dissection; (5) tumors diagnosed as mucinous adenocarcinoma or other adenocarcinoma subtypes (e.g., fetal, enteric, or colloid adenocarcinoma); and (6) incomplete imaging data. The flow diagram of the selection process is shown in Figure 1. A total of 327 patients met the inclusion criteria. The minimum follow‐up duration for the cohort was 79 months, with the final follow‐up conducted on July 31, 2024.

Schedule of patient enrolment.

Minimization of Selection Bias via PSM

To reduce potential confounding factors and selection bias between patients with and without pulmonary spiculation, we performed 1:1 PSM. Propensity scores were estimated using a multivariable logistic regression model that incorporated five well‐established clinicopathological covariates: tumor size, histological differentiation, lymph node metastasis, pleural invasion, and spread through air spaces (STAS).

CT Scan

Preoperative chest CT was conducted using a Toshiba Aquilion 64‐slice spiral CT machine. Each subject was positioned supine on the scanning table to ensure that the scanning baseline was perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the body. Scanning was performed during breath‐holding at full inspiration, encompassing the area from the lung apex to the lung base. The scanning parameters were set to a voltage of 120 kV, current of 110 mA, and collimation of 0.625 × 64 mm, with both slice thickness and spacing maintained at 3 mm. For target reconstruction, a minimum slice thickness of 1 mm and an interval of 0.5 mm were used, employing high‐resolution algorithms (HRCT) and standard reconstruction methods. All CT images were assessed using lung (window width, 1500 HU; window level, −500 HU) and mediastinal windows (window width, 400 HU; window level, 40 HU). Two senior radiologists conducted a blinded review documenting the presence of spiculation and pleural indentation, achieving consensus through discussion when the initial opinions differed.

Spiculation was defined as fine, short, radial, and unbranched lines extending from the nodule edge into the surrounding parenchyma and unattached to the pleura [5]. Pleural indentation was categorized into three types: Type I: A single linear shadow between the tumor and pleura on lung windows, potentially with an associated triangular/funnel‐shaped shadow; type II: Two or more linear shadows between the tumor and pleura, resembling a rabbit ear or V‐sign, with a small triangular shadow near the visceral pleural surface; and type III: Pleural displacement toward the nodule, resulting in indentation or notching, observed on mediastinal windows (Figure 2) [18, 19].

Histologic Examination

The gross tumor size was measured on unfixed specimens. All the specimens were fixed in 3.7% neutral formalin after surgical resection. To achieve an approximate spatial correlation between radiologic and pathologic findings, pathologists referenced the cross‐sectional CT images to guide the optimal sectioning plane during gross sampling. Tumors with a diameter of less than 3 cm were completely excised. The tumor and surrounding tissue were then cut into tissue samples approximately 2 × 2 cm in size and 0.2–0.3 cm in thickness. If the tumor was located within 2 cm of the visceral pleura, the tumor and adjacent pleura were included in the tissue block (one end representing the tumor and the other end representing the pleura). The samples were then placed in embedding cassettes. The samples were then paraffin‐embedded, dehydrated, sectioned, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Two pathologists (Yuanyuan Wen and Hui He) who were blinded to the radiological images and patient characteristics conducted all histological analyses. Their decisions were reached through a consensus. The analysis was performed on the archived tissue sections.

IAC of the lung was diagnosed and classified according to the 2021 WHO criteria, defined as a malignant epithelial tumor with glandular differentiation and/or mucin production, exhibiting an invasive component measuring > 5 mm in greatest dimension. The invasive component is characterized by any histological pattern other than a lepidic growth pattern (e.g., acinar, papillary, micropapillary, or solid patterns) or the presence of tumor cells infiltrating the myofibroblastic stroma. Histological grading is based on the predominant histological pattern combined with the presence of high‐grade patterns, which include solid, micropapillary, complex glandular, and cribriform. The system classifies tumors into three grades: grade 1 (well‐differentiated; predominantly lepidic with or without < 20% high‐grade pattern), grade 2 (moderately differentiated; predominantly papillary or acinar with or without < 20% high‐grade pattern), and grade 3 (poorly differentiated; tumors with ≥ 20% high‐grade pattern) [20].

The Victoria Blue Elastin Fiber Staining and Evaluation

The extracellular matrix of normal lung tissue is primarily composed of elastic and collagen fibers, which are also present in the interstitium of lung tumors. In the Victoria blue staining method, elastic fibers were stained deep blue, whereas collagen fibers appeared pink. Pathological sections of the lung tumors were processed according to the instructions provided with the Victoria Blue Elastic Fiber Kit (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co. Ltd.). The assessment followed the methodology described by Yamashita et al., and was adjusted based on practical circumstances [21]. Intratumoral fibrosis, as well as individual elastic and collagen fiber contents, was graded into three levels based on their respective proportions within the tumor area: Grade 1 (< 20%), grade 2 (20%–40%), and grade 3 (> 40%). Intratumoral fibrosis was specifically evaluated as the combined presence of both elastic and collagen fibers. The proportions of elastic and collagen fibers were assessed in five different, nonoverlapping random fields of view at 200× magnification for each tumor. For each specimen, the fibrous components were semiquantitatively graded (grades 1–3) across these five distinct fields, and the median grade was selected to represent the overall fibrotic landscape of the tumor.

Assessment of Central Fibrosis

Central fibrosis refers to the presence of extensive fibrotic areas in the central region or beneath the pleura in lung adenocarcinomas. It is currently classified into two types: one is characterized by the proliferation of collagen fibers and fibroblasts, which may even display translucency [11], and the other is predominantly composed of elastic fibers [9] (Figure 3). In this study, a 5 mm cutoff was used to categorize the cases into the following groups [11, 13]: central fibrosis > 5 and ≤ 5 mm, central collagenous fibrosis > 5 and ≤ 5 mm, and alveolar collapse > 5 and ≤ 5 mm.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical data were analyzed using the chi‐square test or Fisher's exact test, andthe results were expressed as frequencies and percentages. Continuous variables were compared using Student's t‐test. PSM was implemented to balance clinicopathological characteristics between patients with and without pulmonary spiculation, as previously described. A nearest‐neighbor matching algorithm was employed with a stringent caliper width of 0.1; furthermore, exact matching was mandated for the presence of STAS. The balance of covariates was evaluated using the standardized mean difference (SMD), where an SMD < 0.1 indicated an adequate balance. All PSM‐related analyses were conducted using the “MatchIt” package in R software. The chi‐square test or Student's t‐test was utilized to analyze correlations between pulmonary spiculation and other pathological features (e.g., central fibrosis) in both the original and PSM‐matched cohorts. In the original cohort, variables showing statistical significance in the univariate analysis were candidates for the multivariable logistic regression model. To ensure model stability and address potential multicollinearity among pathological features, collinearity diagnostics were performed using variance inflation factors (VIFs). A backward stepwise procedure based on the likelihood ratio test (backward: LR) was then employed to identify independent predictors of pulmonary spiculation. The entry and removal criteria were set at p < 0.05 and p > 0.10, respectively. The model's robustness was evaluated by ensuring that all retained variables maintained a VIF < 5.

Survival curves were estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method, and differences were compared via the log‐rank test across both cohorts. Additionally, univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses were conducted in the original cohort to evaluate survival‐related variables. For the multivariate analysis, a backward stepwise elimination method (backward: LR) was employed to identify independent prognostic indicators. The results were presented as hazard ratios (HRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 22.0 and R version 4.5.3. Two‐tailed p‐values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

The Relationship Between Pulmonary Spiculation and Central Fibrosis Along With Other Clinical Pathological Features in the Original Cohort

In the original cohort, a total of 327 cases of IAC were included, consisting of 125 male and 202 female patients, with ages ranging from 34 to 84 years (mean ± SD: 61.63 ± 9.13 years). Tumor diameters varied between 0.6 and 3 cm (mean ± SD: 1.57 ± 0.58 cm). Among these cases, 158 showed spiculation, whereas 169 did not. Table 1 provides an overview of the associations between spiculation, central fibrosis, and other clinicopathological features. Compared with the non‐spiculated group, the spiculated group had larger tumor diameters, higher probabilities of pleural indentation signs II and III, and a higher probability of central fibrosis > 5 mm, alveolar collapse > 5 mm, and central collagenous fibrosis > 5 mm (p < 0.05). The spiculated group also exhibited higher degrees of intratumoral fibrosis, elastic fiber formation, and collagen fiber accumulation than the non‐spiculated group. Furthermore, the spiculated group had higher rates of lymph node metastasis, pleural invasion, necrosis, and occurrence of STAS than the non‐spiculated group. No statistically significant differences in sex, age, or tumor location were observed between the spiculated and non‐spiculated groups.

After matching for clinical and prognostic factors, the pulmonary spiculation sign remained closely related to several distinct pathological features. Specifically, the spiculation group exhibited a markedly higher prevalence of central fibrosis (> 5 mm: 36.8% vs. 16.8%, p = 0.003) and alveolar collapse (> 5 mm: 31.6% vs. 14.7%, p = 0.010). The spiculation group demonstrated a significantly higher intratumoral fibrosis grade (p = 0.005) and increased collagen fiber grading (p = 0.022). Additionally, this group was more likely to exhibit the pleural indentation sign (p < 0.001). In contrast, there was no statistically significant difference in elastic fiber grade (p = 0.250) between the two groups, suggesting that it may play a secondary role in the formation of the spiculation sign. While necrosis (p = 0.179) and central collagenous fibrosis (p = 0.444) did not reach statistical significance, this may be attributable to limited event rates in the sample, resulting in insufficient statistical power.

Multivariable Logistic Regression Analysis of Independent Predictors for Spiculation

Initially, multivariable logistic regression was performed using the Enter method; however, significant multicollinearity was observed among several pathological indicators, with VIFs for intratumoral fibrosis, elastic fiber, and collagenous fiber reaching as high as 29.896. Notably, Central collagenous fibrosis was excluded from the full equation due to severe statistical redundancy (Table S1). To eliminate these redundant interferences, a backward stepwise procedure based on the likelihood ratio test (backward: LR) was employed to optimize the model. After systematically removing nonsignificant and collinear variables, the final optimized model demonstrated high stability. The model's robustness was confirmed as all retained independent predictors maintained a VIF < 5 (ranging from 1.05 to 2.42), indicating no significant multicollinearity interference. The multivariable analysis identified five independent predictors of pulmonary spiculation (Table 3). Among the pathological features, central fibrosis (p = 0.010), collagen fiber grading (p = 0.001), and pleural indentation (p < 0.001) remained significantly associated with spiculation. Furthermore, clinical indicators including tumor size > 2 cm (p = 0.017) and lymph node metastasis (p = 0.038) were also confirmed as independent predictors.

Prognostic Impact of Clinicopathological and Morphological Features in the Original Cohort

In the original cohort, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis revealed that invasive lung adenocarcinoma with spiculation was associated with a significantly poorer overall survival (p < 0.001; Figure 4A). No significant difference in survival was found between the group with central fibrosis ≤ 5 mm and the group with central fibrosis > 5 mm (p = 0.910; Figure 4B). In contrast, the group with alveolar collapse > 5 mm showed significantly better overall survival than the group with alveolar collapse ≤ 5 mm (p = 0.015; Figure 4C). On the other hand, the group with central collagenous fibrosis > 5 mm demonstrated notably worse overall survival than the ≤ 5 mm group (p < 0.001; Figure 4D). When intratumoral fibrosis in IAC was classified into three grades, grade 3 indicated the poorest prognosis, grade 1 was the best, and grade 2 was intermediate (p < 0.001; Figure 4E). Additionally, when IAC was graded based on elastin content, the prognostic differences among the three grades were not statistically significant (p = 0.710; Figure 4F). In contrast, when categorized by collagen content, grade 3 again presented the worst prognosis; grade 1, the best; and grade 2, intermediate (p < 0.001; Figure 4G). Furthermore, when pleural indentation was classified into four types, patients with invasive pulmonary adenocarcinoma had a more favorable prognosis if there was no pleural indentation or if it was classified as type I; however, those with type III pleural indentation experienced the most adverse prognosis (p = 0.036; Figure 4H). Moreover, when IAC was classified into three groups based on tumor size (≤ 1, > 1 cm and ≤ 2, > 2 cm, and ≤ 3 cm), the results indicated that larger tumors were correlated with worse prognosis (p < 0.001; Figure 4I). When histological types of tumors were categorized based on differentiation, lower levels of differentiation corresponded to poorer prognoses (p < 0.001; Figure 4J). Additionally, patients with invasive pulmonary adenocarcinoma accompanied by pleural invasion (p < 0.001; Figure 4K) or lymph node metastasis (p < 0.001; Figure 4L) had a significantly poorer prognosis.

Identification of Independent Prognostic Factors via PSM and Multivariable Cox Analysis

Univariate Cox regression analysis initially indicated that spiculation was associated with an increased mortality risk (HR = 3.276, p = 0.001; Table 5). However, spiculation failed to maintain its prognostic independence in the multivariable Cox model, which was strictly optimized using the backward stepwise elimination method (backward: LR) and further cross‐verified by the full model incorporating all covariates (Table S2). This was further validated by KM analysis in a PSM cohort; after balancing baseline clinicopathological characteristics, the prognostic impact of spiculation was no longer observed (p = 0.860, Figure 5). According to the multivariable analysis (Table 5), alveolar collapse greater than 5 mm emerged as an independent protective factor for overall survival (HR = 0.081, p = 0.001). Furthermore, intratumoral fibrosis and lymph node metastasis have been identified as adverse prognostic factors.

Discussion

Pulmonary spiculation, an imaging manifestation observed on CT scans, is characterized by irregular, sharp, and linear protrusions at the periphery of the tumor [5]. Predominantly observed in IAC, this hallmark correlates strongly with adverse clinicopathologic features. Reflecting the tumor's underlying invasive growth pattern, spiculation serves as a critical noninvasive indicator of malignant potential [5, 22]. It frequently co‐occurs with pleural indentation [23], a finding that is corroborated by our study. The pathological mechanisms underlying these phenomena are similar. Current evidence suggests that the formation of both pulmonary spiculation and pleural indentation is primarily attributed to intratumoral fibrosis [5, 6]. However, the potential relationship between pulmonary spiculation and central fibrosis remains unclear.

Central fibrosis is a prevalent characteristic of lung adenocarcinoma and is commonly categorized as alveolar collapse and central collagenous fibrosis [10]. In this study, we stratified alveolar collapse and central collagenous fibrosis into two groups based on a 5 mm threshold. The results demonstrated that in invasive lung adenocarcinoma with spiculation, the probability of central fibrosis > 5 mm, alveolar collapse > 5 mm, and central collagenous fibrosis > 5 mm was significantly elevated (p < 0.05). This robust association largely persisted even after PSM was employed to minimize potential confounders. Although the association between central collagenous fibrosis (> 5 mm) and spiculation did not reach statistical significance following PSM (p = 0.444), we contend that this does not necessarily imply a lack of biological correlation. This outcome is likely attributable to the relatively low prevalence of this specific subtype within the IAC spectrum (only 6.12%), where the limited sample size may have constrained the statistical power of our quantitative analysis, thereby potentially masking the contribution of central collagenous fibrosis in driving marginal invasive alterations such as spiculation. Such findings underscore that spiculation is far from a stochastic imaging finding; instead, it serves as a faithful radiographic reflection of the intricate fibrotic remodeling occurring within the tumor stroma.

While pulmonary spiculation correlates with both central collagenous fibrosis and alveolar collapse, these two fibrotic subtypes exhibit markedly divergent prognostic implications. Univariate Cox analysis identified spiculation and central collagenous fibrosis as predictors of unfavorable clinical outcomes, whereas alveolar collapse emerged as a potential protective factor. This prognostic divergence likely reflects the inherent pathological heterogeneity underlying the spiculation phenotype rather than an intrinsic contradiction in our findings. To gain a more nuanced understanding of this intricate relationship, we further explored the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms of central collagenous fibrosis and alveolar collapse and their differential influences on tumor behavior.

Alveolar collapse denotes the aggregation and condensation of extensive regions of elastic fibers within the central area. In contrast to other tissues, normal pulmonary parenchyma contains a substantial quantity of elastic fibers. When hypoxia or necrosis occurs within a neoplasm, tumor cell proliferation is hindered, leading to intratumoral collapse [24, 25, 26]. Based on univariate analysis, spiculation demonstrated a significant association with alveolar collapse across both the primary and post‐PSM cohorts. However, in multivariate logistic regression, alveolar collapse and intratumoral elastic fiber grading did not emerge as independent risk factors for spiculation formation.

Alveolar collapse is regarded as a favorable prognostic marker, possibly due to its underlying molecular mechanisms and the tumor microenvironment with relatively lower invasiveness [13]. While larger tumor diameters are traditionally correlated with poorer outcomes [27, 28]. Previous studies, along with our data, indicate that extensive alveolar collapse can lead to an “overestimated” gross tumor size. This increase in size does not necessarily reflect a corresponding enhancement in malignancy potential [13, 14]. In our subgroup analysis of tumors between 2.0 and 3.0 cm (Table 4), we found that the group with alveolar collapse > 5 mm had a significantly better prognosis than the ≤ 5 mm group (100.00% vs. 75.00%, p = 0.011). Multivariate Cox regression analysis further reinforced this observation, confirming that alveolar collapse > 5 mm is a robust independent protective factor (HR = 0.081, p = 0.001; Table 5). Furthermore, although the detection rate of spiculated signs, a radiological feature of invasiveness, tends to increase with the gross tumor size (Table 1), significant alveolar collapse seems to modulate the associated risk. Therefore, we propose the existence of a “prognostic balance” between these morphological features: true malignant expansion indicates increased biological invasiveness, while extensive alveolar collapse, which leads to spiculation, acts as a key modifying factor, potentially “counteracting” the prognostic risk typically associated with an increase in tumor diameter.

Central collagenous fibrosis denotes the host's proliferative response of connective tissue to tumor invasion, resulting in the characteristic stellate morphology of the tumor. In contrast to noncancerous scars, such as those observed in tuberculosis, this proliferative response is characterized by an abundance of host myofibroblasts and a distinctive collagen structure with a notably high concentration of type V collagen [29]. Myofibroblasts are known to possess contractile properties; consequently, the extensive presence of these cells within the central collagenous fibrosis accounts for the observed pleural retraction and spiculated features of the lesion [30, 31]. Although central collagenous fibrosis may superficially augment tumor size, it is consistently associated with poorly differentiated histological subtypes and lymph node metastasis, which reflects a higher degree of malignancy [10, 11]. Additionally, compared to the relatively common finding of alveolar collapse, central collagenous fibrosis is infrequent within the overall spectrum of IAC, typically observed only in cases with prominent stromal fibrosis and high‐grade invasive patterns. Hence, central collagenous fibrosis serves as an indicator of poor prognosis.

While univariate analysis suggested a potential association between spiculation and elastin‐rich alveolar collapse, our multivariate logistic regression analysis demonstrated that collagen fiber grading was a robust independent predictor of spiculation formation. This indicates that the development of spiculation is more significantly correlated with collagen content than with elastin accumulation.

In addition to being associated with central fibrosis, the occurrence of pulmonary spiculation is also linked to intratumoral fibrosis, elastin fiber grading, collagen fiber grading, tumor differentiation, lymph node metastasis, pleural invasion, tumor necrosis, STAS, and other factors. These findings are consistent with previous studies [22, 32], suggesting that pulmonary spiculation serves as a radiological surrogate for increased biological invasiveness and aggressive pathological features in patients. The independent prognostic significance of spiculation disappeared in both PSM analyses and multivariate Cox regression, indicating that spiculation merely reflects the underlying invasive pathological process rather than serving as an independent predictor of survival. This finding strongly supports our hypothesis that the clinical relevance of spiculation is not isolated but closely linked to the morphological characteristics of central fibrosis. When spiculation arises from elastin‐rich alveolar collapse, its associated risk appears attenuated; however, when underpinned by collagenous remodeling, it signals a shift toward aggressive disease and poorer outcomes. Consequently, risk stratification for invasive lung adenocarcinoma should evolve beyond the binary assessment of spiculation to incorporate internal fibrotic phenotyping.

Through the implementation of PSM, our study sought to mitigate potential selection bias, thereby strengthening the internal validity of our radiologic–pathologic correlation analysis. Our findings regarding the biological substrates of pulmonary spiculation align with existing literature while offering substantive supplementary evidence. Specifically, although spiculation appears to correlate with elastin‐rich central fibrosis, its association with collagen fiber content was observed to be more pronounced. The analysis of the relationship between spiculation and central fibrosis may offer a more precise pathological interpretation for evaluating its prognostic value, potentially contributing to a more refined risk stratification for IAC.

Although this study offers valuable clinical insights, several limitations must be acknowledged. First, the retrospective, single‐center design may introduce inherent selection bias. In addition, the lack of external validation across diverse ethnic populations means that the generalizability of our findings on the pathological basis of spiculation remains uncertain. Second, the relatively small sample size may reduce the statistical power needed to detect subtle relationships between spicule morphology and specific stromal remodeling patterns, such as central collagenous fibrosis. Equally important are concerns regarding subjective evaluation and potential spatial mismatches. Despite aligning pathological sampling with the CT scanning plane, differences in slice thickness between CT images and microscopic sections, along with interobserver variability, may still affect the accuracy of our radiopathologic correlations. Finally, the cross‐sectional nature of this study prevented us from observing the dynamic progression of spiculation over time. Moving forward, prospective multicenter studies that incorporate longitudinal imaging and more detailed morphological analysis are needed to establish a more robust theoretical foundation for AI‐assisted radiopathologic diagnostic models.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study provides substantive supplementary evidence for the biological basis of pulmonary spiculation in IAC. Our findings indicate that the formation of spiculation is closely associated with central fibrosis. Although spiculation correlates with increased biological invasiveness, its prognostic significance appears to be modulated by specific subtypes of central fibrosis. Notably, the “prognostic balance” created by extensive alveolar collapse may attenuate the malignancy risk typically associated with spiculated lesions. Consequently, we propose that clinical risk stratification for IAC should transition from a binary assessment of spiculation to a more detailed evaluation of internal fibrotic patterns. This proposed radiopathologic framework may offer helpful insights for personalized prognostic assessment and inform the future development of AI‐assisted diagnostic models.
