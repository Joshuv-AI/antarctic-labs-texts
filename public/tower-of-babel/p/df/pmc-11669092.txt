Installing a Trigger to Upcycle High-Density Polyethylene
Authors: Tianhao Nan, Quan Chen, Zhangfan Zheng, Yuxin Liang, Yufei Qin, Yanhui Wang, Bo Liu, Dongmei Cui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Creating C=C bonds as “weak” sites in the stable C–C chains of polyethylene (PE) is an appealing strategy to promote sustainable development of the polyolefin industry. Compared to methods, such as dehydrogenation and postpolymerization modification, the copolymerization of ethylene (E) and butadiene (BD) should be a convenient and direct approach to introduce C=C bonds in PE, whereas it encounters problems in controlling the composition and regularity of the copolymer due to the mismatched activities and mechanisms between the two monomers. Herein, we report by employing the amidinate gadolinium complex, controllable E/BD copolymerization was achieved, where BD was incorporated in the uniformly discrete 1,4 mode. The obtained copolymer possesses the same physical, mechanical, processing, and antioxygen (aging at 100 °C for 28 days) properties as commercial high-density-PE, which, strikingly, were degraded by C=C bonds into α,ω-telechelic oligomers with narrow distribution. These degraded functional products were transferred to compatibilizers via atom-transfer radical polymerization or immortal ring-opening polymerization, achieving upcycling.

Introduction

Polyethylene (PE), created by Imperial Chemical Industries a century ago, has developed into the mostly high-produced synthetic polymer to reach 1.04 × 108 t in 2020 and is widely used in our daily life due to its versatile properties fitted to various application fields.1 Meanwhile, the mechanical durability and chemical inertness allow PE to exist hundreds of years after disposal and thus becoming a source of “white” pollution that threatens our environment and even the lives of the ocean. Therefore, how to recover these waste PE has been a research focus; however, it only reaches ca. 10% weight-amount through down-valued physical grinding and remolding or thermolysis into liquids via energy-consuming and less-controlled procedures. Fabricating and using bio- or chemically recyclable substitutes, such as polylactide, polyethylene terephthalate, poly(amino acid)s, polyesters, and CO2-based polycarbonates, fit the sustainable concept all over the world, whereas properties and application fields of these materials are rather different from PE products. Modification of polyolefins by incorporating ester,2−7 ether,8 or carbonyl9−16 functional groups into their main chains generates the so-called “polyolefin like” (Scheme 1a), which allows chemical recycling, however, it is unable to cover all properties and application fields of PE products and is considerably more expensive. Creating unsaturated C=C bonds into PE main chains to break the homogeneous stable C–C bonds, which are “weak” sites to initiate controllable cleavage via oxidation and metathesis reactions to realize upcycling PE into liquid fuels17,18 or value-added precursors19−23 and even propylene monomer24−26 provides a sustainable development route for PE (Scheme 1b). Dehydrogenation is the most appealing strategy to create C=C bonds in PE main chains,17−19,24,25 which is usually carried out under harsh conditions by means of sacrifice agents, such as olefins or norbornene, and noble metal catalysts. Bromination of low-molecular-weight PE (Mn < 6.6 kDa) in benzene irradiated by 400–410 nm light, followed by dehydrobromination under critical conditions to avoid substitution reactions, is another choice.20 Very recently, an ingenious manner was reported to incorporate C=C double bonds into PE through the cascade copolymerization of ethylene and dimethyl 7-oxabicyclo[2.2.1]hepta-2,5-diene-3,5-dicarboxylate and retro-Diels–Alder postpolymerization modification.27 Obviously, exploring more promising and practicable strategies to fabricate PE bearing in-chain C=C bonds in situ is an attractive but challenging target.

Strategies for the Degradation of Polyolefins

The copolymerization of industrially available ethylene (E) and butadiene (BD) should be a direct method to synthesize PE containing in-chain C=C bonds, which dates back 60 years to the discovery of Ziegler–Natta catalysts.27 However, BD and E have extremely different intrinsic activities and perform in different mechanisms, leading to difficulty in controlling composition and regularity. Thus, E/BD copolymerization with Ziegler–Natta catalyst afforded crystalline copolymers containing alternating E/trans-1,4 BD sequences in major28−30 or containing trans-1,4 PBD (or cis-1,4 PBD) and PE multiblocks.31−33 Employing homogeneous zirconocenes34−41 and FI catalysts,42 crystalline copolymers were obtained but showed lower melting points (Tm) than PE. For instance, incorporating 1% content of BD units reduces the melting point by 15 °C34 due to the presence of cyclopentane and cyclopropane rings in the main chain arising from the intramolecular cyclization of 1,2 BD units.43 Using constrained geometry configuration (CGC) titanium catalysts and bis(phenoxy) titanium catalysts yields elastomers mainly composed of trans-1,4 BD.44,45 The sterically less bulky neodymocenes46−50 and the CGC rare-earth metal catalysts51 allow the BD 1,4-coordination to give syn-π-allyl species. The resultant copolymers contain alternating E/trans-1,4 BD sequences contaminated by a trace of cycloalkane motifs, which are new materials differing from PE. In addition, this alternating E/trans-1,4 BD sequence is detrimental to the palladium catalyst for the isomerization-ethenolysis, the crucial step to upcycle PE into propylene monomers.24 An opening thiophene-fused half-sandwich scandium catalyst was reported to adopt BD η4-coordination to form anti-π-allyl intermediate, leading to E-BD rubber composed of cis-1,4 PBD and short PE multiblocks.52 To date, PE containing cleavable C=C bonds, without scarifying its bulk properties via E/BD copolymerization has still not been accessed. The challenge is to construct a copolymerization system, where E insertion is highly encouraged while BD must propagate in uniformly discrete 1,4-mode, particularly in a limited amount to maintain the PE original properties.

Herein, we employed amidinate lanthanide complexes to catalyze E/BD copolymerization at the kilogram scale under mild conditions to achieve the targeted PE containing in-chain C=C bonds distributed in uniformly discrete 1,4-mode. The physical, mechanical, and processing properties as well as the oxidation resistance of the obtained copolymer were determined, which are similar to those of commercial HDPE. The degradation performance and upcycling potential of the copolymer aged at 100 °C for 28 days were evaluated through a metathesis reaction using Hoveyda-Grubbs II. The resultant degraded products are α,ω-telechelic oligomers, which were used as macroinitiators or chain transfer agents to prepare triblock compatibilizers via ring-opening polymerization or atom-transfer radical polymerization.

Results and Discussion

Copolymerization of E and BD and Copolymer Identification

Compared with the widely employed cyclopentadienyl fragments, the amidinate ligand bears a much stronger negative charge to generate a more Lewis acidic metal center,53 which benefits E polymerization. Indeed, the entire lanthanide metal complexes supported by the amidinate ligand show high activity toward E polymerization.54 In this work, four representative complexes with gradually increased metal ion radii, alternatively, different sterics and electronic metal centers, were selected (Scheme 1c), and their catalytic behaviors toward BD polymerization were assayed (Table S1). The smallest scandium (0.89 Å) complex 1 showed 95.5% 1,2 selectivity. Lutetium (1.00 Å) complex 2 exhibited moderate 1,4 selectivity (53.4%). With increasing metal size, yttrium (1.04 Å, 3) and gadolinium (1.07 Å, 4) complexes gave PBD containing more 1,4 units (3: 73.8%, 4: 83.1%). All complexes 1-4 showed much higher catalytic activities toward E polymerization than BD (Table S2), among which 3 and 4 provided activities of 20 times higher, in agreement with the previous report.54

This intrigued us to examine E/BD copolymerization under mild conditions of [BD]0 = 0.46 M, E pressure = 4 bar, [Al]0:[Gd]0 = 10:1, and polymerization temperature of 40 °C. The system based on 1 gave P1 containing 89.2% E units and 1.2% 1,4-BD units and 9.6% 1,2-BD units (Table 1, entry 1). A lower activity was found for 2 by affording P2 with a slightly dropped E incorporation (78.8%) but increased 1,4-BD content (13.7%) (Table 1, entry 2). A further increase in 1,4 selectivity was achieved by using systems 3 and 4 bearing larger central metal sizes, albeit scarifying both the catalytic activity and E incorporation as shown in P3 and P4 (74.2% E and 19.6% 1,4-BD vs 62.5% E and 31.0% 1,4-BD) (Table 1, entries 3 and 4). The sequence distributions of P1-P4 were identified according to their13C NMR spectra (Figure 1). For P1, the resonances appearing at 32.66 and 29.35 ppm are assigned to Tαδ and Tβγ carbons of the alternating trans-1,4 BD/E (TET) unit,47 while the multiplets around 41.32 and 39.02 ppm are attributed to the continuous 1,2-BD units (V). Both the TET and V sequences are undesired structures for recyclable PE.24P2 also shows TET sequences and contains discrete 1,2-BD units connected to E in the forms of EVEβ and EVEγ (35.19 and 27.25 ppm) and neighboring trans-1,4 BD unit in the forms of VTα and VTγ (38.24 and 34.38 ppm). The desired discrete trans-1,4 BD structure appears in P3 by giving the resonance Tα at 32.77 ppm, albeit it is still contaminated by the unwanted TET and VT sequences. To our delight, the TET and VT resonances became subtle in P4. All P1-P4 show 13.94 and 22.60 ppm resonances assigned to the methyl carbon from the ethyl and isobutyl end groups arising from β-H elimination and chain transfer to AliBu3, respectively. The subsequent polymerizations were performed under low [Al]0:[Gd]0 molar ratios (5:1 and 3:1) to give P4’ and P4” with doubled and ten times higher molecular weights than P4 (Table 1, entries 5 and 6), indicating that the copolymerization was rather controllable. When the E pressure was increased to 6 bar, the E incorporation ratio reached 85.5% (P5). Lowering the initial concentration of BD to 0.08 M, resulted in the continuous increase of E content (92.4% P6) (Table 1, entries 7 and 8). A shown in P5 and P6, sequences of CV (cis-1,4/1,2-vinyl connection) and EVE are absent, but the consecutive cis-1,4 PBD sequences (CC) are present. For copolymer P7, with E content increasing up to 97.6%, BD inserts in the discrete 1,4-regularity in major (1,4:1,2 = 1.9:0.5%), and CC units disappear completely (similar to P8, Table 1, entries 7 and 8), which is consistent with the most advantageous structure for a recyclable PE.24

Comparison Between Copolymers and HDPE

Thus, the degradation ability of P7 was examined in the presence of E and stoichiometric HG II following previously reported procedure.24 Telechelic macromonomers capped with vinyl ends were harvested (Table S3, entry 1; Figure S1).18 On the contrary, under the same conditions, P5 and P6 generated fewer telechelic macromonomers, since they had consecutive cis-1,4 PBD sequences (CC) formed at the initial stage of the copolymerization, and the CC degraded to small molecules (Table S3, entries 2 and 3). To investigate whether P7 bearing a minor amount of BD exhibits similar physical and mechanical properties as well as processing properties akin to HDPE, comparison among P7, Pristine-P7 (a mixture of P7 and 1.2 wt % IRGANOX GX2921 blending at 150 °C) and commercial HDPE was carried out. Both P7 and Pristine-P7 show the same crystalline structure, showing the sharp and strong peaks at 2θ = 21.65° [110] and 2θ = 24.01° [200] for HDPE (Figure 2A) and Tm values (Figure 2B). Meanwhile, they gave slightly higher tensile strength (40.8 and 41.1 MPa vs 32.0 MPa) and elongation at break (1702% and 1701% vs 1686%) (Figure 2C) owing to the higher molecular weight (Mw = 240.8 kDa for P7 and Pristine-P7 vs Mw = 119.4 kDa for HDPE), since all traces deriving from P8 (Table 1, entry 10) bearing the same molecular weight as HDPE completely overlapped with HDPE. So are the cases for linear viscoelasticity in which the master curves for moduli G′ and G′’ under various logω for P8 and HDPE coincide, and so do those for P7 and Pristine-P7 (Figure 2D). These results indicate that the presence of minor C=C bonds does not affect P7 and P8 bulk and compatibility properties when they are used as HDPE alternatives.

Aging Experiments and Upcycling of Copolymers

The main concern regarding the E/BD copolymer as a recyclable HDPE is its antioxidation property because the C=C double bonds are the anchored “weak” sites in the stable C–C bonded HDPE. Thus, P5-P7 and HDPE were subjected to an aging experiment at 100 °C in air for 4 weeks. For both Pristine-P7 and Aging-P7, the 1,4-PBD content, the molecular weight, and the molecular weight distribution, physical and mechanical properties as well as degradation behavior, remain at almost the same levels (Figure S2 and Table S4). In addition, the traces of modulus G′ and G″ versus log ω are completely coincided within the entire logω range, indicating that Aging-P7 also maintains the same chain structure as its precursor P7 without cross-linking aroused by C=C bonds after the oxidation process (Figure 3). However, for P5 and P6, the higher the 1,4-PBD content (11.9% and 6.5%), the higher the G′ value of the aged product (Aging-P5 and Aging-P6) is, especially at low logω values, which is ascribed to the presence of cross-linking. As depicted in Figure 3, samples P5-P7 and Aging-P7 can be completely dissolved in chlorobenzene at 100 °C within 30 min (Movie S1). Under the same conditions, both Aging-P5 and Aging-P6 were partly dissolved in 72 and 49 min (Movies S2 and3), respectively, to remove the cross-linking shell, indicating that the cross-linking occurred gradually from the surface to the inner place through air oxidization of the double bonds. Therefore, except for the discrete distribution, a lower C=C bond content is another key factor that endows the E/BD copolymer with the necessary antiaging properties, which increases its crystallinity to block oxygen diffusion.

To prove that these E/BD copolymers can serve as an alternative to HDPE can be upcycled to high-value-added materials after disposal, detailed degradation of Aging-P7 was investigated. First, isomerizing ethenolysis (I/E) was conducted to give propylene in a yield of 70.3% (Figures 4A and S16), comparable to the result obtained using the dehydrogenated PE.24 Then, Aging-P7 was subjected to cross-metathesis with functional α-olefins in the presence of Hoveyda-Grubbs II catalyst, and α,ω-telechelic oligomers with various end groups were harvested in quantitative yields (Figure 4A). Despite the α-olefin types, the obtained telechelic oligomers have similar molecular weights and relatively narrow molecular weight distributions, confirming again the uniform distribution of C=C bonds within the E/BD copolymers. These telechelic oligomers are versatile precursors for preparing triblock copolymers, especially those composed of polar and nonpolar blocks, through distinct polymerization mechanisms depending on the end groups. For instance, when the bromo-end oligomer was used as the initiator, the atom-transfer radical polymerization (ATRP) with methyl methacrylate (MMA) afforded PMMA–PE-PMMA triblock copolymer (Figure 4A). Employing the hydroxyl-end oligomer as the chain transfer agent of an immortal ring-opening polymerization of lactide (LA) produced PLA–PE-PLA triblock copolymers (Figure 4A). The length of the polar blocks can be easily tuned by adjusting the ratio of the monomer to the corresponding α,ω-telechelic oligomer. In the presence of 2% PLA–PE-PLA, the mechanical properties of the HDPE/PLA blends were substantially improved. In addition, it can be seen from the SEM images of HDPE/PLA (20/80) blends that the addition of triblock copolymer can reduce the droplet size of the HDPE phase during the melt mixing process. These conclusions indicate that the triblock copolymer is an efficient compatibilizer for the mechanical recycling of mixed waste streams (Figure 4B).

Conclusion

An unresolved issue of E/BD copolymerization was addressed by balancing the Lewis acidity and steric bulkiness of the catalytic metal center, where the activity priority of BD as compared to E was successfully suppressed, allowing it to be inserted into PE chains in the discrete 1,4-mode with a controllable ratio. The obtained copolymer with 3 mol % C=C bond content possesses the same physical, mechanical, and processing properties as well as excellent oxidation resistance as commercial high-density PE. Even after heating at 100 °C in air for 28 days, the polymer can still be swiftly degraded into telechelic oligomers with a molecular weight distribution of less than 3 via a metathesis reaction catalyzed by Hoveyda Grubbs II, suggesting a uniform distribution of C=C bonds within the main chain. The afforded telechelic oligomers can be converted to useful compatibilizers through various approaches according to the type of end groups, realizing upcycling. Compared to the currently adopted postpolymerization approaches involving multisteps, precious catalysts, and harsh conditions, this polymerization technique is compatible with the industrialized slurry polymerization process and has high industrialized potential. Hence, this work provides an applicable avenue to access upcycling polyolefins, such as polypropylene and ethylene-propylene rubber as well as olefin-based elastomers, contributing significantly to the sustainable development of the polyolefin industry.
