Intraoperative cardiac arrest in patients undergoing congenital cardiac surgery
Authors: Morgan L. Brown, Steven J. Staffa, Phillip S. Adams, Lisa A. Caplan, Stephen J. Gleich, Jennifer L. Hernandez, Martina Richtsfeld, Lori Q. Riegger, David F. Vener
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo describe intraoperative cardiac arrest in patients undergoing congenital heart surgery.MethodsThe Society of Thoracic Surgeons Congenital Heart Surgery Database was queried. Predictors of intraoperative cardiac arrest were assessed using univariate and multivariable analyses. The univariate relationship between intraoperative cardiac arrest was also compared with available outcomes in the database.ResultsA total of 92,764 cases had anesthesia adverse event data, and 357 patients (0.38%) had an intraoperative cardiac arrest. Multivariable predictors of an intraoperative cardiac arrest included age (odds ratio [OR], 0.98 per year; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.97-0.99; P = .036), preoperative cardiac arrest (<48 hours) (OR, 9.6; 95% CI 6.3-14.6, P < .001), preoperative neurologic deficit (OR, 2.0; 95% CI, 1.3-3.1, P = .002), noninsulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (OR, 6.4; 95% CI, 1.9-21.9, P = .003), increasing Society of Thoracic Surgeons-European Association for Cardio-Thoracic Surgery (STAT) category (OR, 2.3 for STAT 5 vs STAT 1; 95% CI, 1.3-3.9, P = .003), urgent (OR, 2.0; 95% CI, 1.6-2.6, P < .001) or emergent surgery (OR, 3.1; 95% CI, 1.9-5.0, P < .001), and increasing length of total operating room time (OR, 1.2 per hour; 95% CI, 1.2-1.3, P < .001). Intraoperative cardiac arrest was associated with a greater 30-day mortality (14.6% vs 1.8%, P < .001). There were more morbidities in the intraoperative cardiac arrest group including postoperative neurologic deficits (12% vs 1.0%, P < .001), multisystem organ failure (5.9% vs 0.7%, P < .001), and greater rates of unplanned reoperation (19.3% vs 5.0%, P < .001) or interventional cardiac catheterization (7% vs 3.2%, P < .001).ConclusionsThe incidence of intraoperative cardiac arrest is low; however, it is an important indicator of significant patient perioperative morbidity and mortality.

Intraoperative cardiac arrest in patients undergoing congenital cardiac surgery.

Central Message

The incidence of intraoperative cardiac arrest is low in congenital cardiac surgery; however, it is an indicator of significant perioperative morbidity and mortality.

Perspective

There are few published data on intraoperative cardiac arrest in patients undergoing congenital cardiac surgery. Through querying the STS-CHSD, we found that incidence of intraoperative cardiac arrest is low but that it is an important indicator of perioperative morbidity and mortality. Institutions should be closely monitoring their own rates, given the association with poor clinical outcomes.

Pediatric perioperative (intraoperative and postoperative) cardiac arrest is thought to be rare in children undergoing anesthesia.1 Despite a greater rate in patients with congenital heart disease, there are few published data on intraoperative cardiac arrest in patients undergoing congenital cardiac surgery. Single-center studies in congenital cardiac surgery and anesthesia have suggested intraoperative cardiac arrest rates between 0.79% and 2.6%.2, 3, 4 However, there are no large multicenter studies.

The Society of Thoracic Surgeons (STS) Congenital Heart Surgery Database (CHSD) contains data on patients undergoing congenital heart surgery and collects demographic and clinical information on more than 95% of congenital heart surgical procedures performed in the United States. As part of this database, there is an optional Congenital Cardiac Anesthesia Society (CCAS) module, where anesthesia-related data, including adverse events, are recorded. Approximately 60% of participating surgical sites also contribute data to the CCAS module. Our objective is to determine the incidence of intraoperative cardiac arrest while under the care of a congenital cardiac anesthesiologist in patients undergoing cardiac surgery using the STS-CHSD and the CCAS module. These data could then be used to benchmark the rate of intraoperative cardiac arrest. The risk factors for an intraoperative cardiac arrest will be identified and the relationship of intraoperative cardiac arrest to clinical outcomes will be assessed.

Methods

A Participant User File was obtained using the STS Participant User File Research Program and the Access and Publications Task Force. Because of the deidentified nature of this data, our institutional review board waived the need for research consent (IRB-P00022312, January 12, 2022). The STS-CHSD contains data on children and adults undergoing cardiac surgical procedures for congenital heart disease. Patients who underwent cardiac surgery (with or without cardiopulmonary bypass) or a ventricular assist device placement from 2014 to 2021 were included. Patients who had thoracic surgery or noncardiac surgical procedures were excluded. There is an optional anesthesia module in the database: the STS-CCAS. Cases with a completed STS-CCAS module were included; cases were excluded in whom the anesthesia adverse event field was missing.

Intraoperative cardiac arrest is defined as cardiac arrest requiring cardiopulmonary resuscitation (external chest compressions or internal cardiac massage) while under the care of a cardiac anesthesiologist. The database divides intraoperative cardiac arrest into 2 fields: cardiac arrest related to anesthesia care and cardiac arrest unrelated to anesthesia care. The definition for cardiac arrest related to anesthesia care is based on the timing of the intraoperative cardiac arrest, with those related to anesthesia occurring before skin incision (surgery start) or after skin closure (surgery end). Cardiac arrest unrelated to anesthesia care is defined as occurring between incision and closure or during the operating time period.

Variables included demographics such as age, weight, gender, race, and prematurity. The presence of any syndrome, including the 3 most frequent in the database (Down, DiGeorge, and “other”). Williams syndrome patients were also included because they are felt to be at high risk of anesthesia. “Other” genetic syndromes are those not covered by the other available named syndromes. Preoperative risk factors were included. Preoperative cardiac arrest is defined as cardiac arrest within 48 hours of the surgery. Preoperative neurologic deficits include any central or systemic neurologic deficit present before surgery. Gender is defined as the patient's gender at birth as male, female or ambiguous per the STS congenital database. All other definitions can be located on the STS website (www.sts.org/data-collection-forms#CHSD-DCF). Variables were assessed for missingness. All percentages are reported as appropriate based on the available data. Normality of continuous data was evaluated using the Shapiro-Wilk test, and continuous variables are expressed as median (interquartile range).

Using univariate logistic regression, patients who had an intraoperative cardiac arrest were compared with those who did not. A multivariable analysis of intraoperative cardiac arrest was performed using multivariable logistic regression with backwards elimination, where all clinically relevant variables were included, and those with P > .1 were iteratively removed. This identified variables that were clinically and statistically significantly associated with intraoperative cardiac arrest. Variables were checked for collinearity using the variance inflation factor, and covariates were excluded as appropriate. In addition, low-frequency variables (<5) were also excluded. Results of regression analyses are presented as adjusted odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and P values. Model discrimination was evaluated using the c-statistic with 95% confidence interval, where values greater than 0.700 are considered as indicating good discrimination, and model calibration was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

As mentioned, the STS-CCAS database divides intraoperative cardiac arrest into 2 fields: cardiac arrest related to anesthesia care and cardiac arrest unrelated to anesthesia care. There were 38 reported intraoperative cardiac arrests that were before incision or after surgical completion (ie, anesthesia-related per STS-CCAS definitions). Patients who had an anesthetic-related intraoperative cardiac arrest were compared with those who had an intraoperative cardiac arrest unrelated to anesthesia using univariate logistic regression analysis. Patients (n = 10) who had both types of intraoperative cardiac arrest listed were excluded from this analysis.

The univariate relationship between intraoperative cardiac arrest was also compared with available outcomes in the database. Intraoperative death is defined as death during the procedure. The 30-day mortality is the patient status at day 30 postoperatively. Postoperative neurologic complications included seizure or a postoperative neurologic deficit (transient or persisting at discharge). Cardiac failure is defined as severe dysfunction. Unplanned cardiac operation or unplanned interventional cardiac catheterization are procedures which were not anticipated during the postoperative period.

Results

The rate of intraoperative cardiac arrest was not statistically different in any year of our study (P = .186). Comment

Our multi-institutional study using STS data is the largest study of intraoperative cardiac arrest with a total of 357 cases with intraoperative cardiac arrest or 0.38%. Single centers have reported intraoperative cardiac arrest rates during cardiac surgery of 0.69%,2 1.27%,3 and 2.6%.4 Our rate is lower than previously reported single-center data. This may be attributable to underreporting of intraoperative cardiac arrest, although it is also possible that single centers included more high-risk patients. Intraoperative cardiac arrest is a rare event, and our estimate using STS data can be used to provide a benchmark for individual institutions and quality improvement projects.

Of all the multivariable risk factors identified, a history of preoperative cardiac arrest within the prior 48 hours had the greatest odds ratio (OR 9.6; 95% CI, 6.3-14.6). Clearly, these patients are at greater risk, but there may be opportunities for improvement in preoperative care, optimization, or transportation of these patients to the operating room. In addition, patients with a preoperative neurologic deficit, noninsulin dependent diabetes mellitus, increasing STAT category, urgent or emergent surgery, and a longer total operating room time were found in the multivariable model to be associated with intraoperative cardiac arrest. However, these may be nonmodifiable.

The most well-known multicenter registry examining cardiac arrest and anesthesia is the Pediatric Perioperative registry, which collected data from 1994 to 2005 on cardiac arrests from 79 institutions.5 Of these, 127 (34%) of cardiac arrests reported were in patients with congenital or acquired heart disease. When compared with cardiac arrest in patients without congenital heart disease, patients with congenital heart disease had a higher American Society of Anesthesia physical status class and were less likely to survive. Just more than one quarter (26%) of arrests in this registry occurred during cardiac surgery, despite cardiac surgery only comprising <5% of a typical children's hospital surgical volume. Importantly, this dataset is limited in that it included only anesthesia-related cardiac arrests and does not include the denominator of cases under anesthesia care. The Wake Up Safe Database has reported an incidence of intraoperative cardiac arrest of 0.053% in pediatric patients undergoing more than a million anesthetic procedures.1 Of these patients, 54% had congenital heart disease. However, the number of cardiac arrests occurring specifically during cardiac surgery were not reported and there may be some underreporting of cardiac arrests in cardiac patients.

Previous studies have tried to differentiate intraoperative cardiac arrest related to anesthesia or surgical factors. In our dataset, intraoperative cardiac arrests are divided by timing, with those before incision or after incision closure considered to be anesthesia-related. We found few differences between these 2 groups of patients and have unfortunately no more information regarding the cause of arrest.

In previous large studies looking at perioperative cardiac arrest, intraoperative cardiac arrest has been associated with better prognosis than postoperative.6, 7, 8, 9 Our data suggest that an intraoperative cardiac arrest in patients undergoing congenital cardiac surgery is associated with considerable morbidity, including 30-day mortality (14.6% intraoperative cardiac arrest vs 1.8% no intraoperative cardiac arrest, P < .001). Importantly, intraoperative cardiac arrest was associated with an increased need for an unplanned reoperation (19.3% intraoperative cardiac arrest vs 5.0% no intraoperative cardiac arrest, P < .001) and/or unplanned cardiac catheterization procedure (7% intraoperative cardiac arrest vs 3.2% no intraoperative cardiac arrest, P < .001). It is important to consider the potential for residual anatomic defects in patients who experience an intraoperative cardiac arrest.

We acknowledge many limitations. Reports of cardiac arrest are limited, as they are self-reported adverse events, which are well known to be underreported.10 However, unlike other adverse events, it is difficult to not report a case in which cardiopulmonary resuscitation was performed. Our definitions of the timing of intraoperative cardiac arrest may lead to misclassification in reporting in the STS-CHSD database. We do not have any data on the differences between sites that report anesthesia data and those which do not, and there is the possibility of systematic differences. Nevertheless, this is the largest report of cardiac arrest in patients undergoing cardiac surgery to date. We also note that some subgroups are small, including syndromes (Down syndrome, Williams syndrome), along with some STAT categories, and we may be underpowered.

Cardiac programs should develop systems to identify high-risk patients preoperatively, review the cases ahead of time, and enhance existing internal systems to better support teams who are taking care of these patients. It may be that additional staffing is necessary including anesthesia or surgical support, or perhaps ensuring adequately experienced team members. In some cases of cardiac arrest, lack of available equipment has also been implicated.11 Additional studies would be helpful looking at individual clinicians’ volume and overall cardiac program volume on the relationship to cardiac arrest. Our study does not examine these particular issues. In addition, we did not examine the role of type of training of the anesthesia team in this study.

We are also limited by the lack of available granular detail on the management of the cardiac arrest including use of defibrillation, amounts and types of medications, length of arrest, and causes of arrests. These are not fields that are contained in the STS-CCAS database. As such, we cannot answer many important questions about how to best resuscitate these vulnerable patients.

In conclusion, the incidence of intraoperative cardiac arrest reported in the STS-CCAS database is low (0.38%). Future research and quality improvement efforts need to be directed at prevention, as well as optimal resuscitation strategies, in this unique population. Individual institutions should be closely monitoring their intraoperative cardiac arrest rate, given the association with poor clinical outcomes.
