Effects of Long‐Term Exposure to Ambient Formaldehyde on Hypertension and Angina Pectoris Symptoms: Evidence From the WHO SAGE Cohort Study
Authors: Jiahong Xu, Yan Shi, Guanhao He, Yanfei Guo, Ye Ruan, Jianxiong Hu, Qijiong Zhu, Zhiqing Chen, Shuru Liang, Yuan Zheng, Zhongguo Huang, Siwen Yu, Ruotong Zhu, Xiaomei Dong, Fan Wu, Wenjun Ma, Tao Liu
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWe aimed to investigate the associations of long‐term exposure to ambient formaldehyde with hypertension and angina pectoris symptoms in Chinese adults.Methods and ResultsParticipants' information was obtained from the WHO SAGE (World Health Organization Study on Global Aging and Adult Health) study. The Cox proportional hazards regression model was applied to estimate the associations of formaldehyde with hypertension and angina pectoris symptoms. Mediating effect analysis was used to investigate the mediating effect of hypertension between formaldehyde exposure and angina pectoris symptoms. Long‐term exposure to formaldehyde was positively associated with the risk of angina pectoris symptoms (hazard ratio [HR], 1.66 [95% CI, 1.29–2.13], per interquartile range [IQR], 3.33, 1015 molecules/cm2) and hypertension (HR, 1.17 [95% CI, 1.02–1.34], per IQR, 3.34, 1015 molecules/cm2). The associations between formaldehyde and angina pectoris symptoms were greater in participants aged ≥65 years (HR, 1.90 [95% CI, 1.29–2.80]) and in rural areas (HR, 2.71 [95% CI, 1.54–4.77]), whereas the associations of formaldehyde with hypertension were stronger in men (HR, 1.27 [95% CI, 1.02–1.58]), rural areas (HR, 1.22 [95% CI, 0.94–1.59]), and in ever smokers (HR, 1.33 [95% CI, 1.02–1.72]). The mediation effect analysis indicated that 18.44% (95% CI, 2.17–37.65) of the association between formaldehyde exposure and angina pectoris symptoms was mediated by hypertension.ConclusionsLong‐term exposure to ambient formaldehyde was positively associated with hypertension and angina pectoris symptoms. The effects of formaldehyde may be modified by age, sex, urbanicity, and smoking status. Hypertension might play a mediating effect in formaldehyde‐induced angina pectoris symptoms.

High blood pressure and hypertension are major contributors to cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) 1 and have been identified as the leading cause of disability and a significant risk factor for death worldwide. 2 , 3 In China, it is estimated that high systolic blood pressure is responsible for 2.47 million CVD deaths and 45.53 million years of life lost according to the Global Burden Disease Study 2019. 2 Meanwhile, the findings of other studies have also indicated a strong correlation between chronic hypertension and angina pectoris. 3 The increased prevalence of hypertension and angina pectoris has emerged as a significant public health concern worldwide. Identifying and reducing the risk factors is a major strategy to preventing CVD. 4

Previous research has demonstrated that CVD is influenced by many factors, with air pollution playing a pivotal role in the development of CVD. 5 , 6 , 7 A systematic review reported that exposure to air pollutants including particulate matter ≤2.5 μm in diameter (PM2.5), particulate matter ≤10 μm in diameter, and nitrogen oxides was strongly associated with the increased risk of CVD, especially in Asian countries and vulnerable subpopulations such as older people. 8 Air pollution was responsible for 4.75 million CVD deaths in 2021, ranking it as the fourth leading contributor to CVD. 1 Meanwhile, studies have shown that volatile organic compounds, key precursors to ozone and PM2.5, as well as a significant contributors to photochemical pollution, 9 were also important risk factors of CVD. 10

Formaldehyde is a typical volatile organic compound emitted from various sources, 11 and its pollution level has increased substantially in recent years in China, 12 which has attracted increasing public attention. Several studies have explored the cardiovascular effects of formaldehyde on populations with occupational and indoor exposure at a higher concentration and have identified its harmful effects on human health. 13 , 14 , 15 Additionally, animal experiments have demonstrated the potential mechanisms of formaldehyde exposures affecting cardiovascular health, including oxidative stress, cardiomyocyte apoptosis, and endothelial injury. 16 , 17 However, evidence on the effects of long‐term exposure to ambient formaldehyde on human health in the public is sparse, which may be partially due to the low concentration of formaldehyde in the ambient environment. 18 A few recent studies have suggested that even low levels of exposure to formaldehyde could cause irreparable damage to human organs. 19 For example, a time‐series analysis revealed that short‐term exposure to formaldehyde increased the risk of CVD mortality. 18 However, there is no epidemiological study that has investigated the association between long‐term exposure to ambient formaldehyde and CVDs.

CVD is a broad term encompassing many different types of diseases that exhibit distinct differences in their etiopathogenesis and symptom manifestations. 20 The associations of formaldehyde with subtypes of CVD should be separately investigated. Angina pectoris is a typical symptom of coronary artery disease and is prevalent in older people, causing significant harm to their health. Previous studies have suggested that formaldehyde exposure can stimulate cardiomyocyte damage, cardiovascular endothelial damage, and atherosclerosis, leading to heart muscle dysfunction. 17 This damage can cause insufficient oxygen supply to the heart muscle, causing ischemia and ultimately inducing angina pectoris. Additionally, formaldehyde exposure can increase the risk of hypertension by inducing oxidative stress, activating the renin‐angiotensin‐aldosterone system, and possibly sympathetic excitation, leading to vasoconstriction and increased blood pressure. 21 Moreover, formaldehyde has harmful effects on the cardiovascular system, such as causing arrhythmia and acute arterial constriction, which can further promote hypertension. 22 Hypertension is also an important risk factor for angina pectoris. 23 Therefore, this study hypothesized that long‐term exposure to ambient formaldehyde may increase the risks of hypertension risk and angina pectoris symptoms and that hypertension may act as an intermediate factor in formaldehyde‐induced angina.

To fill these research gaps, we extracted data from a national cohort study, WHO SAGE (World Health Organization Study on Global Aging and Adult Health), implemented in a Chinese population aged ≥50 years. We aimed to investigate the associations of long‐term exposure to tropospheric formaldehyde with the incidence of hypertension and angina pectoris symptoms and to further evaluate the mediation effect of hypertension on the association between formaldehyde exposure and angina.

Methods

The data that support the findings of this study are available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Population

Study data were obtained from the WHO SAGE study conducted in China by the World Health Organization, a national cohort study launched in 1 municipality (Shanghai) and 7 provinces (Guangdong, Hubei, Jilin, Shaanxi, Shandong, Yunnan, and Zhejiang) with face‐to‐face interviews with adults aged ≥50 years in China (Figure 1). The WHO SAGE cohort study was initially conducted during 2007 to 2010 (baseline investigation) and consequently implemented 2 waves of follow‐up surveys during 2014 to 2015 and 2017 to 2018, respectively. New participants were added in each wave of follow‐up to increase the sample size due to the loss of follow‐up. A total of 18 673 participants were initially included in the study. We excluded participants by the following criteria: loss to follow‐up, age <50 years, and missing information on key variables. As a result, 9341 participants were included in this study. We further excluded participants reporting hypertension at baseline investigation, and 3619 participants were finally included in the analysis of formaldehyde and hypertension. Similarly, based on the 9341 participants, we further excluded participants reporting angina pectoris symptoms at baseline investigation, and 8270 participants were left in the analysis of formaldehyde and angina pectoris symptoms (Figure S1).

Ethics Approval

The ethical review board of the World Health Organization has given approval to the WHO SAGE study (RPC146). In China, WHO SAGE wave 1 (approval number 200601) was approved by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention. WHO SAGE waves 2 and wave 3 were approved by the Shanghai Municipal Center for Disease Control and Prevention with approval numbers 2014–8 and 2018–1, respectively. Informed consent was obtained from each participant before the interview.

Outcome Measurement

Individuals who received a medical diagnosis of angina pectoris symptoms from a doctor before their interview or had undergone related therapies, such as taking medicine within the past 2 weeks preceding the interview, were identified as angina pectoris symptoms. Furthermore, during each wave of investigation, the participants were seated, and their arterial blood pressure was measured 3 times using a wrist blood pressure monitor on their right arm/wrist. The blood pressure metric for analysis was the mean of the latter 2 readings. Hypertension was defined as having either systolic blood pressure of ≥140 mm Hg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg. Additionally, participants who self‐reported a diagnosis of hypertension by a doctor or were taking antihypertensive medication within the past 2 weeks before the interview were also classified as hypertensive. 24 Taking into account the health outcomes assessed at baseline and during each follow‐up period, the health outcome of interest was defined as the first occurrence of the above conditions during the follow‐up period.

Assessment of Environmental Exposures

The formaldehyde data used in this study were obtained from the daily global grid level 3 formaldehyde tropospheric vertical column density product within the Belgian Institute of Space Aeronomy data set, featuring a spatial resolution of 28 × 28 km. Leveraging ozone monitoring instrument sensor data and differential optical absorption spectroscopy technology, this product enables the estimation of total formaldehyde content within specific spectral bands based on its absorption characteristics. Ultimately, the IMAGES global chemical transport model and radiation transport model were used to compute the concentration of tropospheric formaldehyde columns. 25

Previous studies have suggested that exposure assessment to formaldehyde based on the ground monitoring data may be biased at the regional level because of the limited number of ground monitoring stations. 26 Instead, the tropospheric formaldehyde concentrations estimated by the IMAGES global chemical transport model and radiation transport model provided more comprehensive spatial coverage, and its spatiotemporal changes were closely correlated with the ground level of actual formaldehyde concentration, 26 , 27 indicating the validation of applying tropospheric formaldehyde in studies on human health. Therefore, we applied the tropospheric formaldehyde concentration in this study.

Daily 8‐hour maximum ozone (MDA8 O3) concentrations at a spatial resolution of 7 × 7 km were estimated by an iterative random forest model across China. The iterative random forest model indicated that the cross‐validation R 2 was 0.84 for sample‐based and 0.79 for site‐based. 28 A satellite‐based spatiotemporal model was developed to estimate annual mean PM2.5 concentration with a spatial resolution of 1 × 1 km, which was established by the Atmospheric Composition Analysis Group. The annual mean geophysical PM2.5 estimates indicate high accuracy with R 2 were 0.82. 29 The daily temperature was obtained from the fifth generation European Centre for Medium‐Range Weather Forecasts atmospheric reanalysis of the global climate, with a spatial resolution of 28 × 28 km. 30

The daily data in each grid were used to calculate the annual average MDA8 O3 and temperature, respectively. We then allocated the environment exposure statistics to each participant based on their residential address coordinates, and calculated their 3‐year average concentrations before each calendar year of the follow‐up survey until the occurrence of health outcomes or the end of follow‐up as indicators of time‐varying exposures. In the exposure assessment to environmental factors, we also considered the changes in residential address during the follow‐up period.

Covariates

In accordance with previous studies, we included the following covariates: age (50–64 years, ≥65 years), sex (men, women), body mass index (normal weight [≤23.9 kg/m2], overweight [24.0–27.9 kg/m2], and obesity [≥28.0 kg/m2]), marriage status (married, unmarried), educational level (no formal education, primary school, and middle school or higher), family income (low, high), smoking status (ever, never), drinking status (ever, never), major type of household cooking fuel (clean fuel, unclean fuel), physical activity (low, middle, and high), fruit intake (sufficient, insufficient), vegetable intake (sufficient, insufficient), air pollution‐related occupations (related, not related), urbanicity (urban, rural), region (Southern, Northern), ambient temperature, MDA8 O3, PM2.5, and diabetes (yes, no) (Table S1).

Statistical Analysis

The collinearity among environmental exposures and covariates was evaluated using the variance inflation score. Our analyses revealed low collinearity, with variance inflation score values <10 (Table S2). 31 Given that covariates change over time during the follow‐up period, time‐varying covariance would occur. 32 To identify both internal variation and external variation of variables, we applied a time‐varying Cox proportional hazards regression model to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% CIs in the presence of health outcomes for each interquartile range (IQR) increment in the formaldehyde concentration. 32 We have examined influential observations and detected nonlinearity in relationship between the log hazard and the covariates of the Cox proportional hazards regression model (Figures S2–S4). We also applied a natural spline with 3 degrees of freedom to explore nonlinear effects and conducted subgroup analyses to examine potential modifications of some variables. We conducted a likelihood ratio test to assess potential nonlinearity by comparing a model with linear formaldehyde against a model with an ns function of formaldehyde. We adjusted age, sex, urbanicity, region, smoking status, drinking status, body mass index, marriage status, educational level, household income, indoor fuel type, MDA8 O3, PM2.5, and ambient temperature. Other confounders except for the subgroup category variable were adjusted in subgroup analyses. We further examined the statistical difference between subgroups by including an interaction term of formaldehyde exposure and the potential effect modifiers. 33 We also tested interaction of formaldehyde with PM2.5, MDA8 O3, and temperature on the incidence of angina pectoris symptoms and hypertension (Tables S3 and S4).

We conducted a mediation analysis to investigate whether hypertension plays a mediation role in the relationship between formaldehyde exposure and angina pectoris symptoms. To establish the mediation effect, the following conditions were considered: (1) Formaldehyde was significantly associated with hypertension. (2) Formaldehyde was significantly associated with an increased risk of angina pectoris symptoms. (3) Hypertension was significantly associated with an increased risk of angina pectoris symptoms. (4) After adding hypertension to the model, the association between formaldehyde and the risk of angina pectoris symptoms was weakened. 34 The total effect of formaldehyde on angina pectoris symptoms decomposed into 2 components: the indirect effect (HRIE, the effect of formaldehyde on angina pectoris symptoms mediated through hypertension) and the direct effect (HRDE, the effect of formaldehyde on angina pectoris symptoms). The standardized parameter estimates and their standard errors of these 2 separate models are combined to obtain estimates for the effect size of mediation. 35 , 36 Finally, the proportion mediated (PM) was calculated by the formula listed in Equation (1). 37 The 95% CI of the mediation effect is determined using 10 000 bootstrapped iterations. 38 The detailed process of mediation analyses is shown in Data S1. (1) PM=HRDEHRIE−1HRDE*HRIE−1.

Sensitivity Analysis

To test the robustness of our findings, we compared the major results with and without adjustment for several confounders with a large number of incomplete information such as physical activity, fruit intake, vegetable intake, and air pollution‐related occupations in participants with complete data. Then, considering the potential bias of other environmental exposures like ozone, PM2.5, and temperature, we conducted a series of 2‐exposure models adjustment only for each of these factors with consistent time‐varying exposures on a 3‐year time scale. Lastly, we also investigated the effects of applying different years (1 year, 5 years) of formaldehyde exposure in the sensitivity analyses. We also conducted sensitivity analysis in rural and urban subjects (Tables S5 and S6).

All analyses were conducted by R software (version 4.1.3; R Development Core Team 2019). All tests were 2‐tailed, and P<0.05 was considered statistical significance.

Results

Characteristics of Study Participants

A total of 9341 participants were included during the follow‐up period, with a mean 6.6 years of follow‐up and an overall 61 743 person‐years of follow‐up. Among the total participants, 3372 (36.10%) were aged >65 years, 4834 (51.75%) were men, 5282 (56.55%) resided in rural areas, 5705 (61.07%) lived in Southern China, and 4212 (45.09%) used unclean fuel as their primary household cooking fuel. There were 8270 and 3619 subjects included in the angina pectoris symptoms and hypertension analyses, respectively. Over the follow‐up, 439 (5.30%) participants reported angina pectoris symptoms, and 1543 (42.63%) participants reported hypertension (Table 1). There were 3619 participants finally included in the analysis of formaldehyde and hypertension, with a mean 6.4 years of follow‐up. There were 8270 participants left in the analysis of formaldehyde and angina pectoris symptoms, with a mean 6.7 years of follow‐up.

Spatial Distribution of Formaldehyde Concentration Across China

Figure 1 shows the spatial distribution of tropospheric formaldehyde across China. The concentration of tropospheric formaldehyde was higher in eastern regions and lower in western regions. Moreover, the areas in this study with elevated concentrations are primarily concentrated in several key regions, including the North China Plain (Tianjin, Shandong, Beijing), Yangtze River Delta (Shanghai, Jiangsu, Anhui), Pearl River Basin (Guangdong, Guangxi), and Sichuan Basin (Table S7). Among the total participants, the mean formaldehyde concentration was 8.68 (SD ±1.97) 1015 molecules/cm2.

Association of Long‐Term Exposure to Formaldehyde With Angina Pectoris Symptoms and Hypertension

We found positive exposure‐response associations of formaldehyde with the risks of angina pectoris symptoms and hypertension (Figure 2). Each IQR increment of formaldehyde was associated with a 66% increase in the risk of angina pectoris symptoms (HR, 1.66 [95% CI, 1.29–2.13]; IQR, 3.33, 1015 molecules/cm2) and a 17% increase in the risk of hypertension (HR, 1.17 [95% CI, 1.02–1.34]; IQR, 3.34, 1015 molecules/cm2) after adjusted for confounders. The associations between formaldehyde and angina pectoris symptoms were significantly stronger in participants aged ≥65 years (HR, 1.90 [95% CI, 1.29–2.80]) than in younger participants (HR, 1.50 [95% CI, 1.08–2.07]; P interaction=0.007), and in rural individuals (HR, 2.71 [95% CI, 1.54–4.77]) than in urban individuals (HR, 1.18 [95% CI, 0.90–1.53]; P interaction=0.012). Moreover, the associations of formaldehyde with hypertension were significantly greater in male participants (HR, 1.27 [95% CI, 1.02–4.77]) than in female participants (HR, 1.08 [95% CI, 0.90–1.30]; P interaction<0.001), in rural individuals (HR, 1.22 [95% CI, 0.94–1.59]) than in urban individuals (HR, 1.14 [95% CI, 0.95–1.36]; P interaction=0.016), and in ever smokers (HR, 1.33 [95% CI, 1.02–1.72]) than in never smokers (HR, 1.10 [95% CI, 0.93–1.30]; P interaction=0.008) (Table 2).

Mediation Effect of Hypertension on the Association of Formaldehyde Exposure With Angina Pectoris Symptoms

The direct and indirect standardized coefficients of formaldehyde on angina pectoris symptoms were estimated to be 1.34 (95% CI, 1.15–1.55) and 1.06 (95% CI, 1.01–1.12), respectively. Sensitivity Analyses

Results of sensitivity analyses showed no substantial changes in HRs after adjusting for different confounders and using different years' averages in the exposure assessment to formaldehyde (Tables S8 and S9).

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first national cohort study to evaluate the risk of angina pectoris symptoms and hypertension related to long‐term exposure to ambient formaldehyde. We found that long‐term exposure to formaldehyde was significantly associated with hypertension and angina pectoris symptoms and that hypertension might partially mediate the effects of formaldehyde on angina pectoris symptoms. In addition, the effects of formaldehyde may be modified by age, sex, urbanicity, and smoking status. Our findings have important implications for estimating the health effects of formaldehyde and formulating policies aimed at controlling its impact.

The sources of formaldehyde in the atmospheric environment are relatively extensive, with automobile exhaust, interior decoration material, incomplete combustion of fossil fuels, and industrial waste gas being the primary direct sources, whereas atmospheric photochemical reactions serve as a secondary sources. 39 , 40 Previous studies have suggested that the secondary formation is the major source of formaldehyde, in which vehicle exhaust, industrial emissions, and biogenic emissions also contributed significantly to the ambient formaldehyde in China. 27 , 41 These characteristics may lead to higher concentrations of formaldehyde in economically developed regions. 27 We found that the levels of formaldehyde are elevated in Southern China, such as in the Pearl River Delta, which may be attributed to the abundance of vegetation, the prevalence of higher temperatures, excessive emission of automobile exhaust, and industrial emissions in this region. 27 We also found that the ambient formaldehyde concentrations are notably higher in the Sichuan Basin, which may be due to the unique geographical environment resulting in less wind speed and difficult diffusion of pollutants. 42 Dense population and industrial development are also the primary contributors to the increased concentration of formaldehyde in the Sichuan Basin, where human‐made emissions play a significant role in its formation. 43

Our results suggested substantial variations of ambient formaldehyde concentrations across China, with higher concentrations in Northern, Eastern, and Southern China, and lower concentrations in Western China, 44 making it difficult to simply compare the nationwide formaldehyde concentration with other regions globally. However, previous studies have reported elevated formaldehyde concentrations in regions like India, Southeast Asia, Equatorial Africa, and the Amazon Basin, 45 , 46 which was likely associated with significant emissions of nonmethane volatile organic compounds, high‐density population, industrialization, weather conditions, and biomass burning in these regions. 47 The ambient formaldehyde concentrations in these regions were comparable to or greater than some regions in China, such as the Beijing‐Tianjin‐Hebei area, the Yangtze River Delta, and the Pearl River Delta. In contrast, the ambient formaldehyde concentrations in Europe and North Africa are relatively low, similar to those in Western China. 46

In this study, we observed positive associations of long‐term exposure to tropospheric formaldehyde with the incidence of hypertension and angina pectoris symptoms. Although few studies have estimated the chronic effects of exposure to formaldehyde on cardiovascular health in the general population, the results of some similar studies have supported our findings. 13 , 48 , 49 For example, a national birth cohort study in Japan showed that exposure to house decorations, formaldehyde, and organic solvents during pregnancy was associated with an increased risk of coronary heart disease in offspring. 13 A natural experimental study in the United States also found that acute exposure to formaldehyde adversely affected vascular function in women and ruled out other diseases. 49 Although it is difficult to directly compare our findings with these previous studies because of the differences in study population, study regions, exposure assessment, and analytical strategies, our findings combined with previous studies strongly suggested the adverse cardiovascular effects of ambient formaldehyde. Therefore, rigorous targeted measures are needed to control the formaldehyde in the atmospheric environment.

The stratified analyses revealed stronger effects of formaldehyde in rural residents than in urban residents. This rural–urban disparity may be related to several reasons. First, a rural population may have higher exposure and susceptibility to air pollution because of their lower levels of self‐protection and engagement in more outdoor activities. 50 , 51 Moreover, the more intensified physical activities in rural populations could increase the inhalation rates and depth, and hence increase their exposure dose to air pollution. 52 Second, people in rural areas have a higher prevalence of smoking and are usually exposed to greater indoor air pollution, such as unclean fuels for cooking and heating, and passive smoking, which may strengthen the adverse health effects of formaldehyde. 53 , 54 , 55 This study also observed slightly greater associations of formaldehyde with angina pectoris symptoms and hypertension in individuals using unclean household cooking fuel than those using clean fuel, and stronger associations of formaldehyde with hypertension in ever smokers than in never smokers. Third, rural populations usually have poorer socioeconomic conditions, health care systems, and health status, which may also increase their susceptibility to air pollution. 51 In this study, we also found stronger effects of formaldehyde on angina pectoris symptoms in individuals with low family income than those with high family income, indicating the plausible modifications of socioeconomic conditions.

Based on the associations among formaldehyde, hypertension, and angina pectoris symptoms, we found that hypertension may partially mediate the association between formaldehyde and angina pectoris symptoms. Although we did not find literature about the mechanism to explain this association, there were studies that showed that hypertension is the primary contributing factor for CVD events such as coronary heart disease (usually presenting as angina pectoris symptoms clinically), heart failure, and stroke. 5 , 56 For example, a prospective cohort study indicated that hypertension is the major risk factor for CVD, contributing to 22.3% of its occurence. 53 Moreover, chronic hypertension can result in coronary artery sclerosis, leading to stenosis of the coronary lumen and reduction of coronary reserve, thereby triggering angina pectoris symptoms. 57 Our findings may help to understand the mechanisms of ambient formaldehyde affecting cardiovascular health.

The biological mechanisms of formaldehyde exposures adversely impacting cardiovascular health were not totally clear. Several potential mechanisms have been proposed in several previous studies. 16 , 17 For example, exposure to formaldehyde can lead to a reduction in the activity of superoxide dismutase in myocardium, thereby impairing the antioxidant system of myocardium and inducing toxicity to myocardial cells and tissues. 16 Moreover, the reaction between formaldehyde and active cysteine‐free sulfhydryl groups may result in the inactivation of LCAT (lecithin cholesterol acyltransferase), potentially impairing high‐density lipoprotein‐mediated cholesterol reverse transport and promoting the formation of atherosclerosis. 58

Strengths and Limitations

There were several strengths in this study. First, this is the first prospective cohort study that investigated the associations of long‐term exposure to tropospheric formaldehyde exposures with hypertension and angina pectoris symptoms, and further investigated the mediation effect of hypertension in the association between formaldehyde and angina pectoris symptoms. Second, this prospective cohort study was conducted in 8 provinces or municipalities across China and included a representative sample of the Chinese population. Hence, our findings had a strong causal inference for recommendation. Third, the individual exposure to air pollutants was matched based on their residential address, and changes of address were also considered during the follow‐up, which could largely reduce the misclassification bias. Finally, we examined the modifications of several individual characteristics, which could provide significant information for identifying the most vulnerable groups and for making targeted intervention measures.

There are also some limitations in this study. First, a considerable number of participants were lost to follow‐up due to factors such as immigration and urbanization, which may introduce selection bias into the study findings. Second, the identification of symptoms relied on self‐reporting, potentially leading to misclassification bias. However, it is important to note that this was likely nondifferential and hence may lead to underestimation of the association. 59 Third, some potential confounding variables were not accounted for in the primary models due to a high rate of missing information including physical activity levels, occupational exposure to air pollution, diabetes, and intake of vegetables and fruits. Nevertheless, sensitivity analyses conducted in participants with complete information demonstrated the robustness of the results against these factors. Fourth, tropospheric formaldehyde concentrations may not directly reflect the actual formaldehyde exposure. However, it has been demonstrated that tropospheric formaldehyde concentrations could provide a more comprehensive spatial coverage than the ground data.

Conclusions

This study provided clear evidence that long‐term exposure to ambient formaldehyde was positively associated with angina pectoris symptoms and hypertension. In addition, the effects of formaldehyde may be modified by age, sex, urbanicity, and smoking status. We also found that hypertension might play a crucial mediation effect in formaldehyde‐induced angina pectoris symptoms. Given the limited number of studies, we call for more studies with high quality to further investigate the chronic effects of ambient formaldehyde on human health in the future.
