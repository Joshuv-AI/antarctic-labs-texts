Global maternal and infant health monitoring systems: a scoping review protocol
Authors: Khairat Al-Habbal, Zeinab Hassan, Rima Kaddoura, Sara Yousef, Jason Fetty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionEnhancing maternal and infant health is a cornerstone of global health advancement. This can be achieved by building sustainable health monitoring systems that can accurately and reliably generate high-quality data and produce evidence-based recommendations for policymakers. By identifying gaps and strengths in current systems, this review aims to highlight current practices in monitoring maternal and infant health outcomes, including low birth weight.Methods and analysisThe review will adopt the Arksey and O’Malley framework and the Joanna Briggs Institute’s Scoping Review Methods Manual. Three databases, including PubMed, Embase and CINAHL (Cumulative Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature), as well as relevant grey literature sources, will be searched for articles describing active global population-based maternal and infant health monitoring systems published in English from the year of database inception till 30 September 2025. Two reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts, followed by independent full-text screenings against predefined eligibility criteria, with data extracted using a data extraction form. After data extraction, a narrative synthesis will be performed. The findings will adhere to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines.Ethics and disseminationThis review is based on publicly available data; no ethical approval is required. The findings of this scoping review will be published in journals and presented at relevant conferences.

Introduction

Maternal and infant health is essential for global health and well-being.1 2 While significant efforts have been made globally, the number of deaths among neonates and mothers remains alarming.1 3 4 Approximately 260 000 pregnant women died during their pregnancy or delivery in 2023.5 These deaths, which are often caused by complications during pregnancy, such as severe bleeding, unsafe abortion, high blood pressure and pregnancy-related infections, are largely preventable with timely, high-quality healthcare.135 Maternal mortality is mirrored by the high incidence of neonatal mortality, with daily neonatal mortality rates reaching 6300 globally, and more than 2 million infant deaths in 2022.4 6 Premature birth and low birth weight are considered as main contributors to these high rates.4 6 According to the WHO, low-birth-weight infants are those weighing less than 2500 g at birth.7 A systematic analysis of national, regional and global prevalences of low birth weight has shown that more than nineteen million newborns were low birth weight in 2020.8 Low-birth-weight infants are more likely to develop disabilities, including stunted growth and cognitive impairment, as well as chronic conditions that typically develop in adulthood, such as obesity and diabetes.810

Countries are encouraged to accelerate efforts to reduce preventable maternal and neonatal mortalities and morbidities, thus contributing to the targets of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) by 2030.1 5 11 To support achieving the SDGs, global initiatives including Global Strategy for Women’s, Children’s and Adolescents’ Health, Every Newborn Action Plan, Ending Preventable Maternal Mortality and the Global Financing Facility for Every Woman Every Child11 established indicators and targets to eliminate preventable maternal and newborn deaths and stillbirths.12 However, funding allocated to maternal and infant health remains significantly insufficient, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). An analysis of the impact of funding cuts on maternal and infant mortality between 2009 and 2019 has reported an increase in maternal mortality of 6.4% and an increase in child mortality of 3.6%.13 Similar consequences are also anticipated due to the aid cuts implemented as part of the 2025 US foreign aid policy.13 Given the escalating challenges, it is more crucial than ever to efficiently collect, analyse and disseminate evidence-based data required for assessing progress towards achieving the SDGs5 11 14 and towards achieving universal health coverage for women and children at global, national and subnational levels.11 14 This could be accomplished through implementing reliable and consistent monitoring systems for maternal and infant health. A monitoring system is identified as a public health surveillance system which in turn is defined by the WHO as the continuous and systematic collection, analysis, interpretation, assessment and dissemination of health-related data to guide policymakers and public health interventions.15 These systems generate a wealth of data that can be used to produce evidence-based recommendations for policymakers to guide resource allocation.11 Nevertheless, generating evidence-based maternal and infant health data remains a challenge, particularly in LMICs. Research has shown that major data gaps exist for maternal and neonatal deaths in many countries. For instance, around forty million unregistered deaths and births exist every year.14 These gaps are due to several factors, including but not limited to poor health information systems (HIS), inconsistency in data collection and management and absence of data assessment tools.14

Generating high-quality data is critical to enhance the tracking and interpretation of maternal and infant health indicators.5 11 12 14 It requires sustainable HIS that accurately and reliably measure these health indicators.5 11 An initial step towards improving the tracking of maternal and infant health indicators is mapping the current maternal and infant health monitoring systems worldwide.16 A recent review described eight active maternal and infant health data collection systems in the LMICs.17 While it provides valuable information on active systems and their strengths and limitations, the search is limited to LMICs only.

Thus, we aim to conduct a scoping review to identify existing global maternal and infant health monitoring systems that collect information on maternal and infant health outcomes, including low birth weight. The review will highlight current practices in monitoring maternal and infant health outcomes. In addition, it will uncover gaps in knowledge, challenges and variations in the definition and tracking of maternal and infant health indicators. Ultimately, the review will lay the groundwork for designing future systems that are more effective, inclusive and responsive to maternal and infant health needs and highlight features of these systems that can be adapted in different contexts.

Stage 1: identifying the research question

To formulate a research question for this scoping review, we used the Arksey and O’Malley process.18 Our research question is as follows:

What are the available population-based maternal and infant health monitoring systems worldwide that collect data on maternal and infant health outcomes, including low birth weight?

Stage 2: identifying relevant studies

A thorough search strategy will be formulated to identify relevant studies following key inclusion criteria. The inclusion criteria are based on the ‘Population–Concept–Context’ framework recommended by the JBI for scoping reviews.

Population-concept-context framework

Population

The population includes pregnant women or women within the first-year post-birth, and their infants, globally.

Concept

The review will scope for active global maternal and infant health monitoring systems that continuously collect population-based data on mothers’ and infants’ health outcomes, including low birth weight.

Context

Population-based maternal and infant health monitoring systems are defined as systems that operate at district, national or global levels and that are either community-based or operating within healthcare systems worldwide.

We will include articles that describe maternal and infant health monitoring systems that have the following characteristics:

Population-based maternal and infant health monitoring systems, including digital tools and software, surveillance systems, maternal and infant health networks and electronic health records. Search strategy

The search strategies, including search terms, were co-developed by the research team which includes a medical librarian. The detailed search strategy is presented in online supplemental file 1. We will run an extensive search in three databases: Embase, CINAHL and PubMed using specific search terms outlined in table 1. The timeframe of the search will be from the year of database inception to 30 September 2025. The planned start and end dates for the study are September 2025 until June 2026. We will start by conducting a preliminary search using keywords in the PubMed database. The search strategy will then be applied to the other databases. In the last step, we will screen all references included to identify additional publications. In addition to searching databases, we will search grey literature, including websites of known HIS and governmental entities (ie, Ministries of Health). The complete search strategy will be reported and included in the final review paper.

Stage 3: selecting studies

After implementing the search strategy, all identified articles will be collected using Covidence, a reference management software that will also detect and remove any duplicates. Articles deemed relevant will undergo screening in two stages: (1) abstract and title screening and (2) full-text screening. Two reviewers will independently screen the abstracts and titles of all articles based on the inclusion criteria. The second stage involves full-text review, during which the reviewers will evaluate all retrieved articles and determine their eligibility. Any disagreements that arise during either screening stage will be resolved by a third reviewer and further discussed among the reviewers until a consensus is reached.

Stage 4: extracting data

Two reviewers will use a pretested data extraction form and will independently abstract key information from the included articles. Disagreements between the reviewers will be resolved through discussion. In case the disagreement was not settled, a third reviewer will independently resolve the conflict. The extracted data form is presented in online supplemental file 3.

Stage 5: collating, summarising and reporting the results

Our results will be reported using the PRISMA-ScR guidelines (online supplemental file 2). All identified global maternal and infant health systems will be combined, and their information will be summarised into tables to provide a comprehensive overview of the literature. Through our summary of findings, we aim to inform the advancement of future maternal and infant health monitoring systems by addressing challenges and gaps in the literature and comparing current methods used to collect maternal and infant health data across different regions of the world.

Ethics and dissemination

Since this review is based on publicly available data, no ethical approval is required. The institution’s ethics committee can be reached via ethics@ku.ac.ae. The findings of this scoping review will be published in journals and presented at relevant conferences. Moreover, the results will help improve maternal and infant health by offering an overview of existing global monitoring systems. This will enable researchers and policymakers to use the information to enhance the tracking of maternal and infant health outcomes—a step towards designing the required interventions to achieve the maternal and infant health SDGs.
