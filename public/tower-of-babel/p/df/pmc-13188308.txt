Immersive experience of online gaming among Chinese college students: the enhanced effect of sleep deprivation on emotion regulation and problematic gaming within the I-PACE model
Authors: Xia Zhao, Cheng Wang, Wei Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractOnline gaming addiction has become an important issue affecting the physical and mental health of college students, but its formation mechanism remains incompletely understood. Based on the I-PACE (Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution) model, this study explores the influence and mechanism of sleep deprivation on emotion regulation strategies (cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression) and problematic online gaming among Chinese college students through experimental design. The study recruited 300 college students (mean age 20.5 years, 48% male) in China, randomly assigned to a sleep deprivation group (24 h awake, n = 150) and a control group (normal sleep, n = 150). Measurements were conducted using the Gaming Addiction Scale, Emotion Regulation Questionnaire, Cognitive Bias Scale, and other instruments. Results showed that ERQ scores (M = 36.7, SD = 5.2) and problematic online gaming scores (M = 42.3, SD = 6.4) in the sleep deprivation group were significantly higher than those in the control group (ERQ scores: M = 28.1, SD = 4.1; problematic online gaming scores: M = 35.5, SD = 5.9), with p values all less than 0.001. Mediation analysis indicated that emotion regulation strategies (ERQ scores) partially mediated the relationship between sleep deprivation and problematic online gaming (indirect effect = 0.24, 95%CI[0.19, 0.29]). Moderation analysis revealed that cognitive bias significantly moderated the relationship between emotion regulation strategies (ERQ scores) and problematic online gaming (β = 0.32, p < 0.01), with the indirect effect under high cognitive bias conditions (0.33) being 2.2 times that under low cognitive bias conditions (0.15). The findings support and extend the I-PACE model, suggesting the mechanism by which sleep deprivation may increase problematic online gaming risk through impaired emotion regulation processes, emphasizing the important role of sleep health, emotion regulation training, and cognitive bias correction in preventing problematic online gaming.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-026-04408-6.Keywords: Sleep deprivation, Emotion regulation strategies, Problematic online gaming, I-PACE model, Cognitive bias

Introduction

With the rapid development of the digital era and the deepening popularization of Internet technology, online gaming has become an extremely attractive form of entertainment among contemporary college student populations. Its unique interactivity, immersiveness, and social attributes provide young people with unprecedented virtual experience spaces. However, excessive or maladaptive engagement in online gaming (i.e., problematic gaming) has been increasingly associated with a range of adverse outcomes, including poorer physical and mental health, impaired academic performance, and reduced social functioning. It is therefore important to distinguish between normative recreational gaming and problematic gaming/addictive patterns, which involve loss of control and functional impairment. Particularly in the university environment where sleep problems are common, the vicious cycle between insufficient sleep and gaming addiction is intensifying. In this study, we focus on acute sleep deprivation (i.e., experimentally induced prolonged wakefulness), which should be distinguished from sleep deficiency/insufficiency (i.e., chronically reduced sleep duration or poor sleep quality in daily life). The psychological mechanisms and cognitive processes behind this phenomenon urgently require in-depth investigation.

In recent years, domestic and international scholars have conducted extensive research on the causes and mechanisms of problematic online gaming, with the I-PACE (Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution) model providing an integrative theoretical framework for understanding addictive behaviors [1]. Related research has found that emotion regulation processes are core factors in predicting problematic gaming behavior [2]. According to Gross’s process model of emotion regulation, individuals commonly rely on strategies such as cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression, which may shape emotional experiences and subsequent behavioral choices. Sleep loss may disrupt affective stability and executive control, thereby reducing the capacity to flexibly implement adaptive emotion regulation strategies and increasing impulsive decision-making. Consistent with this perspective, accumulating evidence suggests that inadequate sleep is linked to heightened negative affect and impaired self-regulation, which may increase vulnerability to problematic online behaviors [3]. Cognitive bias, particularly attentional bias toward gaming-related cues, has been confirmed to play a key role in maintaining and reinforcing addictive behaviors [4, 5]. Meanwhile, emotional problems such as social anxiety and depression are closely associated with Internet addiction [6, 7]. In addition to sleep and emotion regulation mechanisms, broader psychological resources such as coping styles and resilience have also been identified as relevant correlates of problematic gaming, potentially buffering the development of addictive behaviors [8]. Although existing research has identified the independent roles of factors such as sleep, emotional regulation, and cognitive bias in gaming addiction [9, 10], the interactive mechanisms among these factors and their integrated influence pathways within the I-PACE model framework still lack empirical testing [11, 12].

Importantly, online platforms and digital engagement are not inherently detrimental. Under certain conditions, online activities may support learning and contribute to cognitive/academic performance among college students [13]. Nevertheless, when online gaming becomes a dominant coping strategy for stress or negative emotions, it may increase the risk of problematic gaming and related impairment. In addition to sleep and affective-cognitive mechanisms, broader sociocultural and interpersonal factors may also shape problematic online platform use. For example, religiosity and religious involvement may influence online behaviors through social support, coping resources, and value-based self-regulation, whereas bullying victimization may increase vulnerability to maladaptive coping and online addictive behaviors via heightened distress and avoidance motivations [14]. These contextual factors may interact with the person-affect-cognition pathways emphasized in the I-PACE model and deserve attention in future research.

Based on the deficiencies of existing research and the needs of theoretical development, this study innovatively incorporates sleep deprivation as a key antecedent variable into the I-PACE model, systematically examining the influence mechanism of sleep deprivation on emotion regulation strategies (cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression) and problematic online gaming among college students through experimental design, with particular focus on exploring the moderating role of cognitive bias in the relationship between emotion regulation and problematic gaming. This research design can not only reveal the specific pathways through which sleep deprivation affects gaming addiction but also provide scientific evidence for formulating targeted prevention and intervention strategies. It has important theoretical value and practical significance for promoting college students' mental health and preventing problematic online gaming.

Theoretical framework and literature review

Theoretical foundation of the I-PACE model

The I-PACE (Interaction of Person-Affect-Cognition-Execution) model, as a comprehensive theoretical framework for explaining problematic Internet-related behaviors, emphasizes that the dynamic interaction among individual characteristics, affective responses, cognitive processing, and executive functions jointly influences the development and maintenance of addictive and potentially addictive online behaviors [15]. The model posits that individuals undergo a complex psychological process when facing specific stimuli, wherein personal susceptibility factors (such as personality traits, biological vulnerability) interact with situational factors to produce affective and cognitive responses, which ultimately lead to specific behavioral decisions through the regulation of the executive control system [16]. In the context of problematic online gaming, the I-PACE model particularly emphasizes emotion regulation processes as a core mediating mechanism, linking individual vulnerability factors with addictive behaviors, while cognitive bias serves as a moderating variable affecting the intensity and direction of this process [17]. Importantly, within this framework, emotion regulation should be conceptualized as the use of specific strategies (e.g., cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression) rather than a clinical “disorder.”

The relationship between sleep deprivation and emotion regulation

The destructive impact of sleep deprivation on the emotion regulation system has received widespread empirical support. Research indicates that chronic or acute sleep loss may leads to impaired prefrontal cortex function, thereby weakening individuals' top-down control capacity over emotional responses [18]. When sleep deprivation occurs, the activity of emotion-related brain regions such as the amygdala significantly increases while prefrontal inhibitory function decreases. This imbalance in neural mechanisms directly leads to excessive emotional responses and decreased regulation capacity [19]. Adolescent and young adult populations are particularly susceptible to the effects of sleep deprivation due to physiological developmental characteristics and lifestyle influences. Research has found a bidirectional causal relationship between poor sleep quality and emotional problems such as depression and anxiety among college student populations, and these emotional problems further increase dependence on immediate gratification activities such as online gaming [20, 21]. From a self-regulation perspective, sleep deprivation may reduce cognitive resources and executive control, thereby undermining the flexible implementation of adaptive emotion regulation strategies (e.g., cognitive reappraisal) and increasing reliance on less adaptive strategies (e.g., expressive suppression), which may contribute to problematic online gaming. Accordingly, sleep deprivation may indirectly increase the risk of problematic gaming by impairing affective stability and self-regulatory functioning.

The moderating role of cognitive bias in problematic online gaming

Cognitive bias, particularly attentional bias, plays a key moderating role in the formation and maintenance of addictive behaviors. Research has found that individuals with problematic gaming exhibit significant attentional bias and memory bias toward gaming-related cues. This selective enhancement of cognitive processing increases the salience of gaming stimuli and weakens the processing of other information [22]. Attentional bias not only directly affects individuals' sensitivity to gaming cues but also amplifies the driving effect of emotional distress on addictive behaviors through interaction with emotion regulation processes. When individuals simultaneously experience greater difficulties in emotion regulation and high levels of cognitive bias, their risk of developing problematic gaming may increase substantially [23, 24]. The moderating effect of cognitive bias is also reflected in its impact on decision-making processes. Research shows that individuals with problematic gaming exhibit risk preference and impulsive decision-making tendencies when facing choices. This cognitive pattern reinforces the priority of gaming behavior and reduces consideration of long-term consequences [25]. Within the I-PACE framework, cognitive bias can be conceptualized as a cognitive mechanism that strengthens cue-reactivity and reward sensitivity, thereby intensifying the association between maladaptive emotion regulation and problematic gaming outcomes.

Formulation of research hypotheses

Based on the theoretical framework of the I-PACE model and findings from existing empirical research, this study constructs an integrative theoretical model to explain the relationships among sleep deprivation, emotion regulation strategies, cognitive bias, and problematic online gaming (as shown in Fig. 1). The model treats sleep deprivation as an exogenous variable, emotion regulation strategies (cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression) as a mediating variable, problematic online gaming as an outcome variable, and cognitive bias as a moderating variable affecting the strength of the relationship between emotion regulation strategies and problematic online gaming [26].

Research design and methods

Participant recruitment and experimental grouping

This study employs an experimental design method to explore the influence mechanism of sleep deprivation on college students' emotion regulation strategies and problematic online gaming. Through posting recruitment information at a comprehensive university in Anhui Province, China, a total of 300 participants meeting inclusion criteria were screened, including 144 males (48%) and 156 females (52%), with an age range of 18–23 years (M = 20.5, SD = 1.2). Participants were recruited via campus advertisements, classroom announcements, and online recruitment posts. Interested students completed an online screening survey assessing demographics, gaming habits, and health status. Participant inclusion criteria included weekly online gaming time exceeding 10 h, no history of sleep disorders, no history of mental illness, and normal or corrected-to-normal vision. Exclusion criteria covered individuals currently taking medications affecting sleep or mood, those who had recently experienced major life events, and those with substance dependence problems. All eligible participants provided written informed consent prior to enrollment, and the study protocol was approved by the institutional ethics committee.

The experiment adopted a randomized controlled design, dividing participants into a sleep deprivation group (n = 150) and a control group (n = 150). As shown in the experimental procedure in Fig. 2, it includes three stages: baseline measurement, experimental intervention, and post-test assessment. Participants were assessed at multiple time points (baseline, 12 h, and 24 h), resulting in a mixed design with a between-subject factor (group) and a within-subject factor (time). Participants in the sleep deprivation group were required to remain awake for 24 h in a laboratory environment, with video monitoring and activity recorders ensuring compliance with experimental requirements during this period, while the control group maintained normal sleep patterns and completed measurements at corresponding time points. To reduce potential bias introduced by different testing contexts (laboratory vs. home/online), standardized instructions and identical questionnaires were used across groups, and this procedural difference is acknowledged as a limitation. Measurement instruments and reliability and validity testing

In this study, we selected widely recognized and validated scales to measure key variables such as problematic online gaming, emotion regulation, and cognitive bias. These scales were chosen based on their proven psychometric qualities, relevance to the research questions, and frequent use in similar addiction and emotion regulation research. Each scale is well-established in the literature, making them suitable for the aims of this study.

The Gaming Addiction Scale (GAS) was used to assess problematic gaming behaviors, as it is one of the most commonly applied tools in addiction research. This scale has been shown to effectively capture the multifaceted nature of gaming addiction, including compulsive behavior and loss of control. Similarly, the Emotion Regulation Questionnaire (ERQ) was employed to assess two important emotion regulation strategies: cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression. These strategies are central to understanding emotional responses, which are important factors in the I-PACE model of addiction. The Cognitive Bias Scale (CBS) was developed to assess cognitive biases related to gaming, focusing on attentional and memory biases, which are critical in the maintenance of gaming addiction. Lastly, the Pittsburgh Sleep Quality Index (PSQI) was included to measure sleep quality, as sleep deprivation is a central variable in the study.

The reliability of all the scales used in this study was confirmed through internal consistency measures, ensuring that they were suitable for the research context. For the GAS, the scale demonstrated good internal consistency, consistent with previous studies. The ERQ showed acceptable reliability for both the cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression subscales, confirming its suitability for assessing emotion regulation strategies. The CBS also showed satisfactory internal consistency, with both attentional bias and memory bias subscales demonstrating good reliability. Finally, the PSQI exhibited good internal consistency, supporting its reliability for use in the study. All these scales are considered reliable and appropriate for measuring the relevant constructs in this research. PSQI was administered at baseline to assess participants' general sleep quality over the past month. However, since PSQI measures retrospective sleep quality, it may not be an ideal indicator of acute sleep deprivation effects. We also collected objective sleep data using wrist-worn activity recorders throughout the experimental period to monitor sleep patterns during the 24-h deprivation phase. Experimental procedure and data collection

The experimental procedure strictly followed a standardized process to ensure data quality and effective experimental control. As shown in Fig. 3, the arrangement of measurement time points fully considered the time course of sleep deprivation effects and between-individual variability in responses to sleep loss. Participants completed baseline measurements one week before the experiment, including demographic information collection, sleep quality assessment, and gaming habit surveys. During the baseline period, participants were required to maintain regular schedules (sleeping before 11 PM, waking after 7 AM) and record sleep conditions through sleep diaries. Compliance during the baseline week was monitored using sleep diaries and wrist-worn activity recorders. On the day of the experiment at 8 AM, participants arrived at the laboratory and completed pre-tests including assessments of emotion regulation strategies (ERQ: cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression), cognitive bias, and problematic online gaming. The sleep deprivation group remained awake from 8 AM, completing alertness tests and emotional state assessments every 4 h during this period, while the control group maintained normal routines at home and completed measurements through an online platform at corresponding times. To reduce demand characteristics, participants were informed that the study examined the effects of sleep schedules on cognition and emotion, without emphasizing gaming addiction as the primary outcome.

Schematic diagram of measurement time points and variable relationships

Data collection employed a mixed-mode approach. Laboratory measurements used E-Prime software to present stimulus materials and record reaction times and accuracy rates. Online measurements were implemented through the Wenjuanxing platform with attention check items to exclude invalid responses. In this study, we employed a true repeated measures design with three time points: baseline (T0), 12 h (T1), and 24 h (T2). This design allowed us to assess changes in emotional regulation and gaming addiction tendency over time in both the sleep deprivation and control groups. Repeated measures ANOVA was used to test within-group and between-group differences across time points. Physiological indicators were obtained through wrist-worn activity recorders continuously monitoring for 72 h to obtain objective sleep parameters. Objective sleep indices (e.g., total sleep time, sleep efficiency, and wake time) were used as manipulation checks to verify adherence to sleep deprivation and normal sleep conditions. To control for experimenter effects, all instructions were played using standardized recordings, and test personnel were uniformly trained and remained blind to the experimental hypotheses.

Because the sleep deprivation group was monitored in the laboratory whereas the control group completed assessments at home/online, potential context and administration-mode confounds cannot be fully ruled out; this issue is addressed as a limitation in the Discussion.

Impact of sleep deprivation on problematic online gaming

Measurement results for problematic online gaming also showed significant between-group differences. As shown in Fig. 6, the GAS score of the sleep deprivation group (M = 42.3, SD = 6.4) was significantly higher than the control group's (M = 35.5, SD = 5.9). This difference reached statistical significance (t(298) = 8.67, p < 0.001) with a large effect size (d = 1.10). These findings suggest that acute sleep deprivation may increase problematic gaming tendencies. Notably, 42.0% of participants in the sleep deprivation group scored above the suggested risk cutoff (GAS ≥ 45), compared with 14.7% in the control group. This proportional difference (OR = 4.20, 95%CI[2.51, 7.03]) suggests that sleep deprivation may be a relevant risk factor associated with elevated problematic gaming scores.

Mediation effect analysis

Mediation effect analysis used structural equation modeling (SEM) with bootstrapping to test the mediating role of emotion regulation strategies (ERQ scores) between sleep deprivation and problematic online gaming (GAS scores). As shown in the path analysis results in Fig. 8, they supported the hypothesized mediation model. The total effect of sleep deprivation on problematic online gaming was c = 0.47 (SE = 0.04, p < 0.001). When controlling for emotional regulation disorders, the direct effect decreased to c' = 0.23 (SE = 0.05, p < 0.001), and the indirect effect ab = 0.24 (95%CI[0.19, 0.29]) reached significance, indicating that ERQ scores partially mediated the impact of sleep deprivation on problematic online gaming. The proportion of mediation effect to total effect was 51.1%. These findings suggest that sleep deprivation may increase problematic gaming partly through changes in emotion regulation processes.

Mediation effect path analysis diagram

The Bootstrap test results in Table 4 further verified the robustness of the mediation effect. The confidence intervals for all path coefficients did not include zero, and the confidence interval for the indirect effect was relatively narrow (0.19–0.29), indicating high estimation precision. Overall, the results are consistent with the I-PACE framework, indicating that emotion regulation strategies may function as an important mechanism linking sleep deprivation and problematic gaming. In addition, because the ERQ assesses two distinct strategies (cognitive reappraisal and expressive suppression), supplementary mediation analyses were conducted to examine their unique mediating roles. Results indicated that sleep deprivation was associated with reduced reappraisal and increased suppression, and both pathways contributed to the indirect effect on problematic gaming.

Moderation effect analysis

Moderating role of cognitive bias

Moderation effect analysis tested the moderating role of cognitive bias on the relationship between ERQ scores and problematic online gaming (GAS scores). Hierarchical regression analysis results showed that the interaction term was significant (β = 0.32, SE = 0.06, t = 5.42, p < 0.01). As shown in the simple slope analysis in Fig. 9, under high cognitive bias conditions (+ 1SD), the predictive effect of ERQ scores on GAS scores was stronger (β = 0.58, p < 0.001), while under low cognitive bias conditions (−1SD), this relationship was relatively weaker (β = 0.26, p < 0.01). This moderation pattern supported the hypothesis that cognitive bias may act as a risk-amplifying factor.

Simple slope analysis provided in-depth exploration of the specific pattern of the moderation effect. This indicates that the association between emotion regulation strategy scores and problematic gaming was statistically significant at moderate-to-high levels of cognitive bias. Within this region, the effect of ERQ scores on GAS scores increased as cognitive bias increased, suggesting a strengthening effect of cognitive bias on this relationship.

Moderated mediation model testing

The final moderated mediation model integrated both mediation and moderation mechanisms. As shown in Fig. 11 and Table 6, conditional indirect effect analysis indicated that the indirect effect of sleep deprivation on problematic online gaming through ERQ scores was significantly moderated by cognitive bias (moderated mediation index = 0.09, 95%CI[0.04, 0.15]). Under high cognitive bias conditions, the indirect effect reached 0.33, which was higher than that under low cognitive bias conditions (0.15). These results indicate that cognitive bias strengthens the indirect pathway from sleep deprivation to problematic gaming via emotion regulation processes, highlighting the role of cognitive vulnerability in addiction-related outcomes.

Discussion and implications

Discussion

This study systematically examined the influence mechanism of sleep deprivation on problematic online gaming among college students based on the I-PACE model. The empirical results provided new perspectives for understanding the formation pathways of addictive behaviors. The research found that the ERQ scores of the sleep deprivation group (M = 36.7, SD = 5.2) was significantly higher than that of the control group (M = 28.1, SD = 4.1), with an effect size of 1.83, supporting hypothesis H1 and indicating that 24 h of sleep deprivation was associated with altered emotion regulation strategy patterns. This is consistent with neuroimaging research findings showing that sleep deprivation leads to weakened prefrontal-amygdala functional connectivity. The GAS score (problematic online gaming) of the sleep deprivation group (M = 42.3, SD = 6.4) was also significantly higher than that of the control group (M = 35.5, SD = 5.9), supporting hypothesis H2. Additionally, a higher proportion of sleep-deprived participants exceeded the suggested risk cutoff (GAS ≥ 45) compared with the control group. This difference indicates an elevated risk tendency rather than a clinical diagnosis, but it nonetheless suggests meaningful implications for student mental health and behavioral risk screening.

Importantly, these findings should be interpreted within a broader and balanced perspective. Online platforms are not inherently harmful; under certain conditions, digital engagement can support learning and enhance academic motivation. However, when gaming becomes a dominant coping strategy for negative affect or stress, sleep deprivation may further amplify vulnerability to problematic gaming behaviors.

As shown in Table 7, the proposed hypotheses received empirical support. Among them, the validation of hypothesis H3 regarding the mediating role of emotion regulation strategies is particularly important. The indirect effect (ab = 0.24) accounted for 51.1% of the total effect, suggesting that sleep deprivation may increase problematic gaming risk partly through changes in emotion regulation processes. This finding deepens understanding of the role of affective mechanisms in the I-PACE model. The confirmation of hypothesis H4 regarding the moderating role of cognitive bias provides an explanatory mechanism for individual differences. The moderated mediation index reached 0.09 (95%CI[0.04, 0.15]), indicating that individuals with higher cognitive bias may show stronger indirect effects of sleep deprivation on problematic gaming via maladaptive emotion regulation tendencies. These conditional effects have important implications for identifying high-risk groups and developing targeted prevention and intervention strategies, while also extending the application of the I-PACE model in explaining cognition–emotion interactions.

In addition to affective-cognitive mechanisms, broader contextual factors may also contribute to problematic online behaviors. For example, religiosity and spirituality have been linked to patterns of Internet use and addiction-related behaviors, potentially acting as protective factors through social support and value-based self-regulation. Bullying victimization may increase vulnerability to maladaptive coping and problematic online engagement by elevating distress and avoidance motivation. Future studies should incorporate such interpersonal and sociocultural variables into extended I-PACE pathways.

Several methodological considerations should be acknowledged. First, the control group completed assessments at home via an online platform, whereas the sleep deprivation group was monitored in the laboratory. This difference may introduce context- and administration-mode confounds (e.g., supervision, demand characteristics, attentional state). Although standardized instructions and attention checks were applied, future studies should implement fully comparable testing contexts across groups [32]. Second, PSQI reflects sleep quality over the past month; therefore, baseline PSQI group differences should be interpreted as a pre-existing characteristic rather than an acute effect of sleep deprivation. Third, although wrist-worn activity recorders were used to obtain objective sleep indices, future work should further strengthen manipulation checks and report additional objective indicators where possible.

Implications

The theoretical contribution of this study lies in incorporating sleep deprivation, a physiological factor, into the I-PACE model framework, constructing and validating an integrated physiology-emotion-cognition addiction development model, providing new theoretical perspectives for understanding the complex mechanisms of problematic online gaming. The moderated mediation model confirmed by the research not only enriches theoretical explanations of addictive behaviors but also reveals the interaction pattern between individual susceptibility factors (cognitive bias) and environmental stress factors (sleep deprivation). This theoretical integration has important significance for developing a more comprehensive addiction prevention and intervention framework, particularly emphasizing the need for multidimensional intervention strategies that simultaneously address physical health, emotional regulation, and cognitive correction.

Practical-level implications include that university mental health services should incorporate sleep health education as an important component of preventing problematic online gaming, establishing sleep quality monitoring and early warning mechanisms. For students who already experience sleep problems, timely provision of sleep hygiene guidance and cognitive behavioral therapy should be provided. For individuals with high cognitive bias, cognitive bias correction training should be implemented [33]. Through attention training and cognitive restructuring techniques, automatic processing tendencies toward gaming cues should be reduced. Meanwhile, emotional regulation skills training should be strengthened, teaching mindfulness meditation, emotional awareness, and adaptive emotional regulation strategies to help students establish healthy stress coping mechanisms as alternatives to the maladaptive pattern of escaping reality through gaming. In addition, interventions may benefit from addressing broader contextual risks such as bullying experiences and social support resources, and from promoting balanced digital use that leverages online platforms for learning while reducing maladaptive gaming behaviors. These comprehensive intervention measures should be integrated into university mental health education courses and counseling systems to form a systematic prevention network. In addition, future prevention programs may benefit from incorporating coping and resilience enhancement modules, as coping resources and resilience have been shown to relate to problematic gaming and may serve as protective factors.

Conclusion

This study employed an experimental design and systematic data analysis to investigate the relationship between sleep deprivation and gaming addiction tendency among college students. The results suggest that 24 h of sleep deprivation was associated with increased emotional regulation difficulties (approximately 30.5%) and a modest increase in gaming addiction tendency (approximately 19.2%). Mediation analysis indicated that emotional regulation difficulties partially mediated this relationship, accounting for about 51.1% of the observed effect, while cognitive bias appeared to moderate the link between emotional regulation difficulties and gaming behaviors. These findings provide preliminary empirical support for the I-PACE model by highlighting potential pathways through which physiological factors such as sleep deprivation may influence gaming-related behaviors. However, the results should be interpreted with caution. The observed effects are based on acute sleep deprivation under controlled laboratory conditions, which may not fully reflect chronic sleep patterns in naturalistic settings. Additionally, the cross-sectional nature of the measurements limits causal inference, and effect sizes, while statistically significant, should not be interpreted as indicating universal or large-scale impacts. Future research would benefit from longitudinal designs to track the temporal development of sleep problems and gaming behaviors, as well as examining additional moderating or protective factors, including social support and personality traits. Overall, this study contributes to understanding the potential links between sleep, emotional regulation, and gaming behaviors among college students, providing preliminary evidence that could inform targeted interventions. Nonetheless, conclusions regarding clinical significance or broader generalization should remain tentative until further replication and extended research are conducted.
