Dual-targeted lipid nanoparticles for TET3 siRNA delivery: nanobiotechnology strategy to remodel tumor immune microenvironment in hepatocellular carcinoma
Authors: Wei Peng, Jin Zhou, Zhiliang Chen, Chengjia Tang, Jia Kuang, Wenke Li, Zhao Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is characterized by a highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), where M2-polarized tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) and exhausted CD8+ T cells limit immunotherapy efficacy. However, the upstream epigenetic mechanisms driving these phenotypes remain poorly defined, and effective nanotherapeutic strategies to reverse this immunosuppression are lacking. This study aimed to identify key epigenetic regulators of TME dysfunction and develop a targeted nanoplatform to restore antitumor immunity in HCC.ResultsSingle-cell RNA sequencing identified ten-eleven translocation 3 (TET3) as highly expressed in M2 TAMs in HCC tissues. Mechanistically, TET3 promotes M2 polarization by enhancing interferon regulatory factor 4 (IRF4) transcriptional activity via hydroxymethylation, which in turn induces CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion through the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis. To target this pathway, we engineered dual-targeted lipid nanoparticles (TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo) modified with macrophage-specific peptides, enabling efficient and specific delivery of TET3 siRNA to M2 TAMs. In vitro experiments demonstrated the nanoparticles’ excellent biocompatibility, stability, and ability to silence TET3, inhibiting M2 polarization and restoring CD8+ T cell function. In vivo studies confirmed that TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo significantly suppressed tumor growth, reduced M2 TAM infiltration, and reversed CD8+ T cell exhaustion in HCC models.ConclusionsThis study identifies the TET3–IRF4 axis as a central epigenetic driver of immunosuppression in HCC and establishes TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo as a potent and translatable nanobiotechnology platform. Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) is the sixth most common malignancy worldwide and the third leading cause of cancer-related death, with a particularly high burden in China [1, 2]. Due to its insidious onset and lack of early specific symptoms, most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages, thereby missing the window for curative treatment [3, 4]. Although targeted therapies and immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) have improved clinical outcomes in a subset of patients, the majority derive limited benefit, and the 5-year survival rate remains below 20% [5–7]. A key contributor to this poor response is the highly immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME), which is marked by profound dysfunction of tumor-associated immune cells [8–10]. Consequently, strategies aimed at remodeling the TME and enhancing immunotherapy responsiveness have emerged as critical priorities in HCC research and treatment.

Macrophages are among the most abundant immune cells in the TME and are broadly classified into pro-inflammatory M1 and immunosuppressive M2 phenotypes [11, 12]. M2 macrophages promote tumor progression by secreting anti-inflammatory cytokines, facilitating angiogenesis, and suppressing antitumor immunity across various solid tumors [13]. In HCC, high M2 macrophage infiltration correlates with larger tumor size, vascular invasion, and poor prognosis. Moreover, M2 macrophages drive CD8+ T-cell exhaustion through the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis, thereby fostering an “immune-cold” TME [14, 15]. This signaling axis disrupts T cell migration and upregulates exhaustion markers such as PD-1, Tim-3, and LAG-3, leading to T cell dysfunction and even apoptosis [16, 17]. Modulating M2 polarization and restoring T cell functionality are considered crucial strategies for enhancing the efficacy of cancer immunotherapy.

Ten–eleven translocation 3 (TET3) is a core member of the TET family that catalyzes the conversion of 5-methylcytosine to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, thereby regulating gene transcription and maintaining genomic stability [18, 19]. Increasing evidence indicates that TET3 plays an important role in tumorigenesis and immune regulation by shaping both tumor cell phenotypes and immune cell functions [20, 21]. Notably, TET3 controls the hydroxymethylation of interferon regulatory factor 4 (IRF4), thereby modulating its expression and activity. IRF4 is a key transcriptional driver of macrophage M2 polarization and is closely linked to CD8+ T-cell exhaustion [22–24]. These findings highlight TET3 as a potential epigenetic regulator of tumor immunity, warranting further investigation into its roles in other immune cell populations and across diverse tumor models.

Small interfering RNA (siRNA) is a powerful gene-silencing tool with high specificity and efficiency, but its clinical translation in tumor immunotherapy is hindered by poor in-vivo stability, limited targeting capacity, and low cellular uptake [25–27]. Lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) have emerged as a promising RNA delivery platform, showing excellent biocompatibility and targeting potential in vaccines and cancer therapy [28]. In this study, we constructed dual-targeted LNPs (TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo) by modifying their surface with macrophage-specific ligands, namely α-macrophage peptides, enable efficient encapsulation and selective delivery of TET3 siRNA. Preliminary results demonstrate that this system exhibits good stability and high specificity for M2 macrophages, supporting its promise as a nanoparticle platform for precise remodeling of the tumor immune microenvironment (TIME) via epigenetic regulation, and for enhancing immunotherapy efficacy.

This study aims to systematically elucidate how TET3 drives macrophage M2 polarization and CD8+ T-cell exhaustion by regulating IRF4 hydroxymethylation, and to define its pivotal role in shaping the immune microenvironment of HCC. In addition, we constructed and optimized TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo, and evaluated their immunomodulatory and antitumor efficacy in both in vitro and in vivo models. The major innovations of this work include: (i) the first integration of single-cell transcriptomics with functional assays to uncover the immune-regulatory role of the TET3–IRF4 axis in HCC; (ii) the development of a highly efficient nanoparticle delivery system enabling precise epigenetic targeting of TET3; (iii) the coordinated modulation of macrophage and T-cell functions to overcome the limitations of conventional immunotherapy; and (iv) the proposal of a clinically translatable precision therapeutic strategy. Collectively, these findings provide new insights into the epigenetic control of tumor immunity and establish a theoretical and technical foundation for precision immunotherapy in HCC.

Materials and methods

Acquisition of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) data related to HCC

scRNA-seq data related to HCC were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO, https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) under accession number GSE149614, which includes two HCC tumor samples (GSM4505949 and GSM4505961) and two matched adjacent normal liver samples (GSM4505948 and GSM4505960). Data analysis was performed using the Seurat package in R software. Cells were retained for further analysis if they met the following quality control criteria: 200 < nFeature_RNA < 6000 and mitochondrial gene content < 25%. Highly variable genes were identified by selecting the top 2,000 genes based on variance.

Cell subpopulation clustering, cell-cell communication analysis, and differential expression analysis

After normalization of the gene expression matrix, dimensionality reduction was performed by principal component analysis (PCA) using the top 30 principal components. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was then applied for cell clustering and visualization. Distinct cell subpopulations within the TME were annotated based on canonical marker genes. To discriminate macrophage phenotypes and evaluate CD8+ T-cell functional status, the expression of specific marker genes was analyzed in relevant subclusters: Tlr2 for M1 macrophages, Mrc1 (CD206) for M2 macrophages, and Gzmk and Ifng (IFN-γ) for CD8+ T cells. Gene expression patterns were visualized using the VlnPlot and FeaturePlot functions in Seurat.

Cell–cell communication among M2 macrophages, tumor cells, and CD8+ T cells was inferred using the CellChat package. Ligand–receptor interactions were identified, and intercellular communication networks were constructed. Enrichment of key signaling pathways, including the CSF1–CSF1R axis, was analyzed to reveal potential mechanisms of cellular crosstalk.

To identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) between tumor-derived and normal-derived M2 macrophages, M2 macrophage subsets were extracted based on Seurat clustering. Differential expression analysis was performed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, with thresholds of |log2FC| > 1 and a false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05. The resulting DEGs were normalized and imported into Cytoscape for protein-protein interaction (PPI) network analysis using data from the STRING database (version 12.0, https://string-db.org/). Key regulatory genes were further identified using the “CytoHubba” plugin in Cytoscape.

Gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) functional enrichment analysis

GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed to characterize the biological functions and pathways associated with DEGs. Enrichment analysis was conducted using the clusterProfiler package in R, with statistical significance defined as p.adjust < 0.05. GO analysis included three domains: biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). KEGG pathway enrichment was carried out using the KOBAS database (http://kobas.cbi.pku.edu.cn). The results were visualized using bubble plots generated with the ggplot2 package in R.

Transcriptome sequencing and data processing

To identify downstream targets and pathways regulated by TET3, total RNA was isolated from TET3-overexpressing macrophages and corresponding control cells. RNA extraction was performed using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA), followed by purification with the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen, Germany). RNA quality was assessed with the Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA), and only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) > 7.0 were used for library construction. RNA sequencing libraries were prepared using the NEBNext Ultra RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina (NEB, USA) and sequenced on the Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Illumina, USA), generating approximately 20 million 150 bp paired-end reads per sample. The sequencing data were processed into sample-by-gene expression matrices.

Differential expression analysis

Differential gene expression between TET3-overexpressing and control macrophages was analyzed using the Limma package in R. Genes with |log2 fold change| > 2 and p < 0.05 were considered significantly differentially expressed. Identified DEGs were further subjected to GO and KEGG enrichment analyses using clusterProfiler to elucidate the principal biological processes and signaling pathways regulated by TET3. Visualization of transcriptomic alterations was performed using heatmaps generated with pheatmap and volcano plots created with ggplot2 in R.

HCC cell culture and grouping

To investigate the effects of TET3 overexpression on HCC cells, the following cell lines were used: human HCC cell lines Huh7 (CVCL_0336, JCRB0403, JCRB Cell Bank, Japan), Hep3B (HB-8064, ATCC, USA), and HCCLM3 (CL-0258, Procell, China); the human normal liver cell line THLE-3 (CRL-11233, ATCC, USA); and murine HCC cell lines Hepa1-6 (CRL-1830, ATCC, USA) and Hepa1c1c-7 (CRL-2026, ATCC, USA), along with the murine normal liver cell line AML12 (CRL-2254, ATCC, USA). All cells were cultured in either Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, 11965092, Gibco, USA) or RPMI 1640 medium (11875093, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, 10091148, Gibco, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15070063, Gibco, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO2. Hepa1-6 and Hepa1c1c-7 cells were transduced with either a TET3-overexpressing lentiviral vector (TET3 group) or an empty vector (Vector group) using a lentiviral transduction system (GenePharma, Shanghai, China). Stable transfectants were selected with puromycin (P8833, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) 48 h post-transduction [24].

Macrophage culture and grouping

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were isolated from the femurs and tibias of 6- to 8-week-old C57BL/6 mice. Bone marrow cells were seeded into RPMI 1640 medium (11875093, Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (10091148, Gibco, USA), 1% penicillin-streptomycin (15070063, Invitrogen, USA), and 20 ng/mL macrophage colony-stimulating factor (83869-56-1, PeproTech, USA) at 37 °C in a humidified 5% CO2 incubator for 6 days to induce macrophage differentiation. Macrophage polarization was induced by treatment with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (100 ng/mL; L4391; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for M1 polarization or interleukin-4 (IL-4) (20 ng/mL; 214-14-5UG; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for M2 polarization [29]. Subsequent experiments were conducted using M2-polarized macrophages.

After differentiation, macrophages were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 3–4 × 10⁵ cells per well and cultured overnight. When confluence reached approximately 50%, cells were subjected to lentiviral transduction for either TET3 overexpression or knockdown. Lentiviral vectors were obtained from GenePharma (Shanghai, China), and transduction was performed using polyethylenimine (PEI) (5 µg/mL; 408727; Sigma-Aldrich, USA) as the transfection reagent, with a viral titer of 1 × 108 TU/mL. After 48 h, the medium was replaced with fresh complete medium, and downstream experiments were carried out 24 h later [30].

Cells were divided into the following experimental groups: (1) TET3 overexpression group (TET3), empty vector control group (Vector), negative control group for lentivirus (sh-NC), TET3 knockdown group 1 (sh-TET3#1), and TET3 knockdown group 2 (sh-TET3#2); (2) lentiviral negative control group (sh-NC), IRF4 knockdown group 1 (sh-IRF4#1), and IRF4 knockdown group 2 (sh-IRF4#2); (3) sh-TET3 + vector group, and sh-TET3 + IRF4 group; (4) phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) group, TET3 siRNA@Lipo group, and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo group, with an siRNA concentration of 100 µg/mL.

The short hairpin RNA (shRNA) sequences used were as follows: sh-TET3: #1 CGCCCACAAGGACCAACATAA; #2 TACCTAGACACACCTACTAGG; sh-IRF4: #1 GCCAGACAACTGTATTACTTT; #2 CCAAGCATAAGGTCTGCTGAA [31].

CD8+ T cell isolation and culture

CD8+ T cells were isolated from the spleens of 6-8-week-old C57BL/6 mice using the EasySep™ Mouse CD8+ T Cell Isolation Kit (StemCell Technologies, Canada). TPurified cells were resuspended in complete medium and adjusted to a density of 2 × 106 cells/mL before being seeded into six-well plates. Cells were stimulated overnight with 2 µg/mL anti-CD3 antibody (A19017, ABclonal, China), followed by stimulation with 2 µg/mL anti-CD28 antibody (A20346, ABclonal, China) and 100 U/mL recombinant murine interleukin-2 (rmIL-2; RP01384, ABclonal, China) for 48 h. Subsequently, the cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycin, 10 mM HEPES, 2 mM L-glutamine, 1 mM sodium pyruvate, MEM non-essential amino acids, and 55 µM β-mercaptoethanol. The culture medium containing cytokines was replenished every 2–3 days to maintain cell density above 2 × 106 cells/mL [32].

Co-culture of macrophages and CD8+ T cells

CD8⁺ T cells were labeled with carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE; C34554, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at a final concentration of 5 µM per 1 × 106 cells. Cells were incubated at 37 °C in the dark for 20 min and washed three times with PBS. Labeled CD8+ T cells were then co-cultured with macrophages from each treatment group at a 1:1 ratio for 72 h. The co-culture medium consisted of RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% FBS, 1 mM sodium pyruvate (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), non-essential amino acids (Gibco, USA), 10 mM HEPES (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 55 µM β-mercaptoethanol (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) [33].

Western blot (WB) analysis of protein expression

Macrophages from different treatment groups were lysed in RIPA buffer (P0013B, Beyotime, China) supplemented with a protease inhibitor cocktail (B14002, Bimake, USA). Lysates were incubated on ice (30 min) and centrifuged (12,000 × g, 15 min) to collect supernatants. Protein concentrations were measured using the BCA Protein Assay Kit (23225, Pierce, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Equal amounts of protein (40 µg per sample) were separated by SDS–PAGE (10% gel) and transferred onto PVDF membranes (IPVH00010, Millipore, USA). Membranes were blocked with non-fat milk (5% in TBST containing 0.1% Tween-20) and incubated with primary antibodies (4 °C, overnight) (listed in Table S1). Subsequently, membranes were incubated for 2 h with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (ab205718, Abcam, UK; 1:5000 dilution). Protein bands were visualized using enhanced chemiluminescence reagents (RPN2232, Amersham, UK) and imaged with the ChemiDoc MP system (Bio-Rad, USA). GAPDH was used as the internal control.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from macrophages and tumor tissues of different treatment groups using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA; 15596018CN, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (1011U, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). cDNA was synthesized using the HiScript III RT SuperMix for qPCR (+ gDNA wiper) (R323-01, Vazyme, China). qPCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Q711-02, Vazyme, China) on a CFX96 Real-Time PCR Detection System (1845096, Bio-Rad, USA). GAPDH was used as the reference gene. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate. Relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method. Primer sequences are listed in Table S2.

Cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was evaluated using the EdU assay (C0078S, Beyotime, Jiangsu, China). Cells were seeded into 24-well plates (5 × 104 cells per well) and cultured (37 °C, 24 h). After washing with PBS, cells were incubated with fresh medium containing EdU (10 µM, 37 °C, 2 h). Cells were then fixed with paraformaldehyde (4%, room temperature, 15 min) and washed with PBS. Under light-protected conditions, cells were incubated with Click reaction solution (30 min) and counterstained with Hoechst 33,342 (1:1000, 10 min, room temperature). EdU-positive cells were visualized using a confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM; LSM 510 META, Carl Zeiss AG). Quantification of EdU-positive and Hoechst-stained cells was performed using ImageJ from three randomly selected fields per group [34].

Wound healing assay

To assess cell migratory capacity, HCC cells from each group were seeded into six-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well) and cultured until reaching approximately 90% confluence. A linear scratch was created using a sterile pipette tip, after which cells were washed with PBS to remove debris and incubated in a serum-free medium. Images of the wound area were captured at 0 h and 24 h using an inverted microscope (IXplore Pro, Olympus, Japan). The wound closure area was quantified using ImageJ to calculate the migration rate.

Transwell invasion and migration assay

For the invasion assay, Matrigel (50 µL; 356234, Corning, USA) was added to the upper chamber of a 24-well Transwell insert (8 μm pore size; 3422, Corning, USA) and incubated (37 °C, 30 min) to allow gelation. Cells were resuspended in serum-free medium and added to the upper chamber (1 × 105 cells in 200 µL), while medium containing FBS (600 µL, 10% FBS) was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After incubation (24 h), non-migrated or non-invaded cells on the upper surface were removed. Cells that had migrated or invaded to the lower membrane surface were fixed with methanol and stained with crystal violet (0.1%; C0121, Beyotime, China). Five random microscopic fields were selected for quantification. For the migration assay, the same procedure was followed except that the Matrigel coating step was omitted. Each experiment was performed in triplicate.

Flow cytometric analysis of immune cell populations

Tumor-infiltrating immune cells were isolated as single-cell suspensions using a gentleMACS™ Dissociator (130-093-235, Miltenyi Biotec Inc., USA) and a Tumor Dissociation Kit (130-096-730, Miltenyi Biotec Inc., USA). Co-cultured macrophages and CD8+ T cells from different treatment groups were collected and treated with 0.25% EDTA (25200-056, Gibco, USA) to obtain single-cell suspensions. Cells were stained with the following fluorophore-conjugated antibodies: FITC-conjugated F4/80 (1:200, 123107, BioLegend, USA), APC-conjugated CD206 (1:200, 141707, BioLegend, USA), APC-conjugated CD163 (1:200, 155305, BioLegend, USA), FITC-conjugated CD86 (11-0862-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), APC-conjugated CD86 (159215, BioLegend, USA), Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated IRF4 (1:200, 646405, BioLegend, USA), PE-conjugated GZMB (372208, BioLegend, USA), PerCP/Cyanine5.5-conjugated PD-1 (135208, BioLegend, USA), APC-conjugated Tim-3 (119706, BioLegend, USA), and APC-conjugated Lag-3 (125210, BioLegend, USA).

To block nonspecific Fc receptor binding, cells were pre-treated with an FcR blocking reagent (553142, BD Biosciences, USA). Surface staining was performed at 4 °C for 30 min using APC-conjugated anti-CD3 (11-0032-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and FITC-conjugated anti-CD8 (25-0081-82, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). For intracellular cytokine staining to assess CD8⁺ T-cell effector function, cells were stimulated with Brefeldin A (BFA; 420601, BioLegend, USA; 4 h). Fc receptor blocking was repeated, followed by fixation and permeabilization using a fixation/permeabilization kit (554714, BD Biosciences, USA). Data acquisition was performed using a BD LSRFortessa X-20 flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, USA), and data were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star, USA).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) detection of cytokines in supernatants

Supernatants from cultured macrophages or macrophage-CD8⁺ T cell co-cultures were collected for cytokine analysis. The concentrations of mouse IL-6 (ELISA MAX™ Deluxe Set Mouse IL-6, 431304, BioLegend, USA), TNF-α (430904, BioLegend, USA), IL-10 (431414, BioLegend, USA), TGF-β1 (436707, BioLegend, USA), and IFN-γ (88-7314-88, eBioscience, USA) were quantified using corresponding ELISA kits. Absorbance was read at 450 nm using a microplate reader (Varioskan™ LUX Multimode Microplate Reader, VLBLATD2, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cytokine concentrations were calculated based on standard curves generated from reference standards.

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)-qPCR for TET3 binding to the IRF4 promoter

ChIP was performed using the EZ-Magna ChIP™ A/G kit (17-10086, MilliporeSigma, USA). Cells were cross-linked with formaldehyde (1%; F8775, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), lysed, and sonicated to shear chromatin into fragments (200–500 bp). Chromatin was immunoprecipitated with an anti-TET3 antibody (61395, Proteintech, Wuhan, China) or an IgG control antibody (1:50; ab171870, Abcam, UK). Immune complexes were captured with magnetic beads, followed by reverse cross-linking and DNA purification. Enrichment of IRF4 promoter regions was quantified by qPCR, and specific binding was confirmed relative to IgG controls. The primer sequences used were as follows: Primer 1: 5′-AACGCTCCAAGTTGACCTGT-3′, 5′-GCTTTTGCCGCTGACTTTCT-3′; Primer 2: 5′-CTTCACAGGAACGGGCCATA-3′, 5′-AGGAACAGGAGCTTCGGACT-3′. NC Primers: 5′-AGCATGCACGGCTATATGGACTCA-3′, 5′-TGTGCAATGGCAAAGCCACAGA-3′.

Methylation-specific PCR

Genomic DNA was extracted from macrophages of different treatment groups and subjected to bisulfite conversion using the EZ DNA Methylation-Gold™ Kit (D5005, Zymo Research, USA). CpG islands within the IRF4 promoter were predicted using the MethPrimer tool, and methylation-specific primers were designed to span these regions. The methylation status of the IRF4 promoter was analyzed using the following primer sets: Methylated IRF4 primers: Forward: 5′-GTTTTTGTTTTTAGTTTGTGTACGT-3′; Reverse: 5′-CTTTAAACACCTTCTATCTATCGAA-3′; Unmethylated IRF4 primers: Forward: 5′-GTTTTTGTTTTTAGTTTGTGTATGT-3′; Reverse: 5′-CTTTAAACACCTTCTATCTATCAAA-3′.

Methylation and hydroxymethylation-sensitive qPCR

To determine hydroxymethylation levels at the IRF4 promoter, Tet-assisted bisulfite sequencing (TAB-Seq) was performed. Genomic DNA was isolated and quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (ND-2000, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; A260/A280 = 1.8–2.0). Each DNA sample (200 ng) was treated with T4 β-glucosyltransferase (M0357S, New England Biolabs, USA; 1 U enzyme, 1 µL 10× T4 BGT buffer, 1 mM UDP-glucose; total volume 20 µL) and incubated (37 °C, 1 h), followed by enzyme inactivation (65 °C, 20 min) to selectively protect 5-hydroxymethylcytosine residues. Quantitative PCR was performed using AceQ qPCR SYBR Green Master Mix (Q111-02, Vazyme, China) on a real-time PCR system. Amplification curves were analyzed to quantify the relative hydroxymethylation levels in the IRF4 promoter region.

Preparation of LNPs

LNPs were prepared using the Nano S microfluidics system (Unigen Inc., Suzhou, China). Ionizable lipids (HY-138170, MCE, USA), 1,2-dioleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine (HY-112005, MCE, USA), cholesterol (HY-N0322, MCE, USA), and DSPE-PEG2000-COOH (880135P, Avanti Polar Lipids, Merck, Germany) were dissolved in anhydrous ethanol (≥ 99.5%, 459844, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at a molar ratio of (20:30:40:0.75) to form the lipid phase. Simultaneously, TET3 siRNA or DiI-labeled TET3 siRNA (C1036, Beyotime, China) was dissolved in citrate buffer (50 mM, pH 4.0; HY-N1428, MCE, USA) to prepare the aqueous phase. The lipid and aqueous phases were mixed (volume ratio 3:1; flow rate ratio 1:3) and injected into the microfluidic device to generate TET3 siRNA@Lipo nanoparticles. After synthesis, the nanoparticles were dialyzed using 1× PBS (pH 7.4, 10010023, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 50 kDa centrifugal filter units (UFC905096, Millipore, Sigma-Aldrich) to remove unencapsulated siRNA and organic solvents. The final nanoparticle suspensions were stored at 4 °C.

The siRNA duplex targeting TET3 was designed using the siDirect platform and synthesized by GenePharma (Shanghai, China). The sequences (5′→3′) were as follows: Sense strand: AGAUAUAAAUAUUAAGAUCAA; Antisense strand: GAUCUUAAUAUUUAUAUCUCC.

Dual-peptide targeting modification

To enable M2 macrophage-specific targeting, TET3 siRNA@Lipo nanoparticles were conjugated with dual targeting peptides. An activation solution containing NHS (10 mM; 56480, Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, Germany) and EDC (10 mM; 39391, Sigma-Aldrich, Merck, Germany) was prepared in sodium acetate buffer (20 mM, pH 6.5). LNPs were incubated with this solution (room temperature, 30 min) to activate surface carboxyl groups. After removing excess EDC/NHS, 50 µg each of the M2-targeting peptide M2pep (YEQDPWGVKWWY; Genscript, China) and α-peptide (FAEKFKEAVKDYFAKFWD; Genscript, China) were added to the LNP suspension. The mixture was rotated (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; room temperature, 2 h) to generate the dual-ligand-modified TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo nanoparticles.

Characterization of morphology and particle size

Transmission Electron Microscopy (TEM): TET3 siRNA@Lipo and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo were diluted (0.1 mg/mL) and sonicated using an ultrasonic cleaner (Branson, USA). A sample aliquot (10 µL) was placed on a 300-mesh copper grid (Ted Pella, USA) and allowed to adsorb (3 min). Excess liquid was blotted with filter paper, followed by negative staining with phosphotungstic acid (1%, pH 7.0; 12501-23-4, Sigma-Aldrich, USA; 1 min). After drying, nanoparticle morphology was examined using a TEM (JEOL JEM-2100, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV.

Dynamic Light Scattering (DLS) Analysis: TET3 siRNA@Lipo and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo suspensions (1 mg/mL, 1 mL each) were loaded into quartz cuvettes and analyzed using a Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS (UK). Particle size, polydispersity index, and zeta potential were measured (25 °C; scattering angle 173°). Each sample was analyzed in triplicate, with each run lasting 90 s.

Encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of TET3 siRNA

The EE% of TET3 siRNA in the nanoparticles was determined using the RiboGreen assay (Quant-iT RiboGreen RNA Assay Kit, R11491, Invitrogen, USA). Nanoparticle samples (10 µL; TET3 siRNA@Lipo or TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo) were mixed with TE buffer (117 µL, 1×; 12090015, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) or Triton X-100 (117 µL, 2%; 9036-19-5, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) and vortexed (2 min). An equal volume of RiboGreen reagent (1:200 dilution) was added and incubated (5 min, room temperature). Assessment of RNase stability

The RNase stability of free TET3 siRNA and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo was evaluated by agarose gel electrophoresis (3%). Samples were incubated in PBS containing RNase A (10 µg/mL; Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA; 37 °C, 2 h), and reactions were terminated by addition of an RNase inhibitor. After mixing with loading buffer, samples were subjected to electrophoresis (Bio-Rad, USA), stained with GelRed (Biotium, USA), and visualized under ultraviolet illumination to assess siRNA integrity.

Stability of TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo in serum

To assess serum stability, TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo nanoparticles were dispersed in PBS containing FBS (10%; 10099141 C, Gibco, USA) under two pH conditions (7.4 and 6.8) to mimic physiological and TMEs. The suspensions were incubated at 37 °C with gentle shaking (100 rpm) for 24 h. Aliquots were collected at (0, 1, 2, 4, 8, 12, and 24 h), and particle size changes were measured by DLS (Malvern Zetasizer Nano ZS, UK).

In vitro release assay

The release kinetics of siRNA from nanoparticles were evaluated using a dialysis method. Nanoparticles (1 mg) were dispersed in PBS (1 mL; pH 5.0 or 7.4; Gibco, USA) and placed into a dialysis bag (MWCO 3,500 Da; Spectra/Por, USA). The bag was immersed in PBS (50 mL; pH 5.0) and incubated at 37 °C on a shaker at 100 rpm. Release medium (1 mL) was withdrawn at designated time points (every hour from 0 to 10 h, every 10 h from 10 to 50 h, and every 25 h from 50 to 125 h) and replaced with fresh PBS. Samples were stored at -20 °C until analysis. Released siRNA was quantified by qPCR (Applied Biosystems, USA) using a SYBR Green kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and RNA concentrations were measured with a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Hemolysis assay

Red blood cells (RBCs) were isolated from mouse whole blood and resuspended in PBS to a final concentration (2%, v/v). Cytotoxicity evaluation (cell counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay)

M2-polarized macrophages were seeded into 96-well plates (1 × 104 cells per well). After reaching appropriate confluence, cells were treated with TET3 siRNA@Lipo or TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo at various concentrations (6.25, 12.5, 25, 50, and 100 µg/mL) for 24 h. Cellular uptake analysis

M1 and M2 macrophages were seeded into 24-well plates (1 × 105 cells per well) in RPMI-1640 medium supplemented with FBS (10%). Cells were treated with DiI-labeled TET3 siRNA@Lipo or TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo (50 µg/mL). Samples were collected at (0.5, 1, 4, and 8 h) post-treatment. After washing with PBS (three times), cells were examined under a fluorescence microscope to visualize intracellular nanoparticle distribution. Uptake efficiency was evaluated by comparing fluorescence intensity between M1 and M2 macrophages.

For quantitative analysis, cells incubated for 8 h were washed three times with PBS and analyzed by flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, USA). The percentage of fluorescent cells and mean fluorescence intensity were used to assess nanoparticle uptake.

Endocytic pathway and endosomal escape analysis

To explore the uptake mechanism of TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo, M2 macrophages were pretreated with specific endocytosis inhibitors: cytochalasin D (20 µM; 6055-19-2, Sigma-Aldrich, USA), EIPA (10 µM; HY-101840, MCE, USA), and methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD) (5 mM; C4555, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Untreated cells served as controls. After pretreatment, cells were incubated with Dil-labeled TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo for 4 h. Endosomal and lysosomal compartments were labeled using LysoTracker Red (L7528, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and colocalization with nanoparticles was observed using confocal fluorescence microscopy. Endosomal escape efficiency was evaluated based on colocalization patterns, and quantitative image analysis was performed using ImageJ.

In vivo targeting evaluation of M2 macrophages

An HCC mouse model was established by subcutaneous injection of Hepa1-6 cells (5 × 10⁶ cells) into male C57BL/6 mice. Animals were maintained under standard conditions (22 ± 2 °C; 40%–60% humidity; 12 h light/dark cycle). Mice were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6 per group) and intravenously injected (tail vein) with DiI-labeled TET3 siRNA@Lipo or TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo. Tumor tissues were collected 6, 12, and 24 h post-injection for subsequent analyses [35].

Immunofluorescence staining was first performed to assess the in vivo targeting efficiency of TET3 siRNA@Lipo and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo toward M2 macrophages. Tumor tissues were cryosectioned (5 μm) and fixed with paraformaldehyde (4%). Sections were incubated with anti-CD206 (rabbit; 1 µg/mL; ab64693, Abcam, UK; 4 °C, overnight) followed by Alexa Fluor® 488–conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (1:1000; ab150077, Abcam, UK). Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI. Colocalization of nanoparticles with CD206+ M2 macrophages was visualized using CLSM.

To further validate the targeting specificity, tumors harvested at 6, 12, and 24 h were enzymatically dissociated into single-cell suspensions and stained with anti-F4/80 (FITC; 123107, BioLegend, USA) and anti-CD206 (APC; 141707, BioLegend, USA). Data were acquired using a BD flow cytometer and analyzed with FlowJo (BD Biosciences, USA) to quantify CD206⁺ macrophages and evaluate nanoparticle targeting efficiency [30].

In vivo biodistribution (in vivo imaging system (IVIS))

To evaluate the in vivo biodistribution of TET3 siRNA, mice were imaged using the IVIS-200 (PerkinElmer, Waltham, MA, USA). After intravenous injection of Dil-labeled TET3 siRNA@Lipo or TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo, tumor tissues were harvested 6, 12, and 24 h post-administration. Fluorescence signals were acquired and quantified using Living Image 4.7 software (PerkinElmer, USA). For quantitative analysis, regions of interest (ROIs) corresponding to tumors and major organs were selected, and fluorescence intensity was measured using ImageJ (NIH, USA). Data from different groups and time points were compared to determine the temporal and spatial distribution of TET3 siRNA, and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism (GraphPad Software, USA).

To investigate the biodistribution of TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo in various tissues, mice were administered 150 µL of DiD-labeled 6M1@C60 via tail vein injection. At 48 h after particle administration, five mice were euthanized. Major organs (liver, kidney, spleen, lung, and heart) were harvested, weighed, and homogenized in PBS (1 mL). The fluorescence intensity of each sample was measured using an Infinite M200 microplate reader.

In vivo safety evaluation

Healthy male C57BL/6 mice were randomly divided into three groups (n = 6 per group) and administered a single intravenous dose (0.3 mg/kg body weight) of PBS (control), TET3 siRNA@Lipo, or TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo. On day 14 post-injection, mice were anesthetized and blood (0.5 mL) was collected from the left ventricle. Serum was separated by centrifugation (13,000 × g, 5 min) and analyzed by Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd. to measure levels of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), gamma-glutamyl transferase (γ-GT), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), creatinine (CREA), and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) [36].

Mice were then euthanized, and major organs (heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney) were harvested, sectioned (8 μm), and subjected to hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining for histopathological examination [35, 37].

Establishment of animal models

Male C57BL/6 mice (6–8 weeks old; 20–22 g; n = 213) were obtained from Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. and maintained under SPF conditions (22 ± 2 °C; 50%–60% humidity; 12 h light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. Mice were acclimatized 1 week before experimentation.

To establish HCC models, mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 5 × 106 stably transduced Hepa1-6 cells and divided into vector and TET3. In another model, 5 × 106 Hepa1-6 cells were co-injected subcutaneously with 5 × 106 lentivirally transduced macrophages (sh-NC or sh-TET3) at a 1:1 ratio [38]. Four weeks after inoculation, mice were euthanized, and tumors were excised, photographed, and weighed.

Mice were further divided into two experimental cohorts. (1) sh-NC + anti-IgG, sh-TET3 + anti-IgG, sh-NC + anti-CD8a, and sh-TET3 + anti-CD8a (n = 6 per group). Anti-CD8a antibody (A2102, Selleck, USA) was administered intraperitoneally at 200 µg per dose every 3 days. An isotype control (anti-IgG, ab190475, Abcam, UK) was given under the same regimen [39]. (2) PBS + anti-IgG, TET3 siRNA@Lipo + anti-IgG, TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo + anti-IgG, TET3 siRNA@Lipo + anti-CD8a, and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo + anti-CD8a. Nanoparticles were administered intravenously via the tail vein at 0.3 mg/kg every 3 days. Anti-CD8a or anti-IgG was given intraperitoneally at 200 µg per injection at the same intervals [35, 36, 40].

Tumor size was measured weekly using a high-precision vernier caliper (Model 500-196-30, Mitutoyo, Japan; range: 150 mm; accuracy: 0.01 mm). Tumor volume (V) was calculated using the formula: V = (W² × L)/2, where W represents tumor width and L represents tumor length. Tumor growth was systematically recorded according to time point, treatment group, and tumor size [30, 41].

H&E staining

Tumor tissues were rinsed with saline and fixed in paraformaldehyde (4%, 24 h). Samples were washed, dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. Tissue sections were mounted on glass slides, dried (45 °C), deparaffinized in xylene, and rehydrated through graded ethanol to distilled water (2 min). Sections were stained with H&E solution (C0105S, Beyotime, China). Hematoxylin staining was performed (5 min), followed by rinsing under running water and differentiation with acid ethanol (1%, 3 s). Sections were counterstained with eosin (5%, ~ 2 min), dehydrated, cleared, and coverslipped. Tumor morphology, including cell proliferation and necrosis, was evaluated using a light microscope.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) staining

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tumor sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with H₂O₂ (3%; P0100, Beyotime, China). Antigen retrieval was performed in citrate buffer (pH 6.0; G1201, Servicebio, China). After cooling, sections were blocked with normal goat serum (C0265, Beyotime, China; 1 h, room temperature). Sections were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with the following primary antibodies: rabbit anti-TET3 (1:100; ab153724, Abcam, UK), rabbit anti-IRF4 (1:100; 62834, CST, USA), rabbit anti-Ki67 (1:100; ab15580, Abcam, UK), and rabbit anti-PCNA (1:100; ab92552, Abcam, UK). The following day, sections were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (1:1000; ab205718, Abcam, UK) for 1 h. Visualization was performed using DAB chromogen (G1211, Servicebio, China), and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Images were acquired using a light microscope (Leica Microsystems, Germany).

Immunofluorescence staining

To examine the colocalization of TET3 with the M2 macrophage marker CD163, tumor tissues were fixed in paraformaldehyde (4%, 24 h), dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (5 μm). Sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol, and subjected to antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0; G1201, Servicebio, China). After blocking with normal goat serum for 1 h at room temperature, sections were incubated overnight with rabbit anti-TET3 (1:100; ab153724, Abcam, UK), rabbit anti-IRF4 (1:100; ab315394, Abcam, UK), and mouse anti-CD163 (1:100; sc-58965, Santa Cruz, USA). After PBS washing, sections were incubated with Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit (1:1000; ab6939, Abcam, UK) and Alexa Fluor® 488-conjugated goat anti-mouse secondary antibodies (1:1000; ab150117, Abcam, UK), followed by nuclear staining with DAPI. Colocalization was visualized using a laser scanning confocal microscope (488 nm and 594 nm). Image acquisition and colocalization analysis were performed using ImageJ.

Apoptosis detection (TUNEL assay)

Apoptotic cells in tumor tissues were detected using a TUNEL assay kit (11684817910, Roche, Switzerland). Paraffin-embedded sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and treated with proteinase K (20 µg/mL; ST533, Beyotime, China; 30 min), followed by PBS washing (three times). Sections were incubated with TUNEL reaction mixture (37 °C, 1 h), and nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (C1005, Beyotime, China). Fluorescent signals were visualized using a fluorescence microscope to evaluate apoptotic cells.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD); an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t-test was used to compare the two groups. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s post hoc test was performed for comparisons among three or more groups when variance assumptions were met. The Mann-Whitney U test or Kruskal-Wallis H test was applied as appropriate for data that did not conform to normal distribution or exhibited unequal variances. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.5 (GraphPad Software, Inc.) and R software. A two-sided p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Single-cell transcriptomic analysis reveals TET3-mediated reprogramming of TAMs in HCC

TAMs are highly enriched in hepatic malignancies and are critical in establishing an immunosuppressive TME [42]. To investigate the role of TAMs in HCC progression, we analyzed scRNA-seq data from the GSE149614 dataset, which contains two HCC tumor samples and two matched adjacent normal liver tissues (Fig. 1A).

Single-cell and transcriptomic sequencing reveal functional reprogramming of macrophages in the HCC microenvironment.Note: (A) Schematic workflow of single-cell analysis, including single-cell suspension preparation, high-throughput sequencing, and data processing (Created in BioRender); (B) UMAP dimensionality reduction analysis identifying cell subsets including TAMs, tumor cells, and CD8⁺ T cells; (C) UMAP visualization showing significant enrichment of M2 macrophages and reduction of M1 macrophages in tumor tissues; (D) DEGs in M2 macrophages identified from single-cell transcriptomic data: green represents 80 downregulated genes, red represents 47 upregulated genes, and blue indicates non-significant genes (n = 2 tumor tissues, n = 2 adjacent normal liver tissues); (E) Workflow of bulk RNA-seq analysis (Created in BioRender); (F) Heatmap of the top 50 DEGs ranked by absolute logFC values across all samples; (G) Volcano plot showing DEGs between TET3-overexpressing and control samples: 98 downregulated genes (green) and 83 upregulated genes (red), with the top 10 most dysregulated genes annotated. Following normalization and dimensionality reduction using Seurat and UMAP, multiple cell populations were identified, including TAMs, tumor cells, and CD8+ T cells, and visualized in a two-dimensional space (Fig. 1B). Cell identities were annotated based on established marker genes: Cd68 and Cd163 for TAMs; Epcam, Cd24, Krt8, Krt18, and Krt19 for tumor cells; and Cd8a, Cd8b, and Gzmk for CD8+ T cells (Table S3). UMAP projections revealed a pronounced enrichment of M2-like TAMs in tumor tissues compared with normal liver, indicating macrophage reprogramming within the TME (Fig. 1C). Subtype analysis further confirmed that TAMs in tumors predominantly exhibited an M2 phenotype, as evidenced by high expression of the M2 marker Mrc1 (CD206) (Figure S1A), while expression of the M1 marker Tlr2 was markedly reduced (Figure S1B). CD8+ T cells in tumor tissues exhibited a dysfunctional phenotype, characterized by significantly reduced expression of the cytotoxic effector gene Gzmk (Figure S1C) and increased expression of the exhaustion marker PD-1 (Figure S1D), suggesting a dysfunctional and exhausted T cell state in the TME.

To further elucidate intercellular communication, CellChat analysis was conducted to examine signaling interactions among TAMs, tumor cells, and CD8+ T cells. The results indicated active signaling between TAMs and tumor cells via the CSF1-CSF1R axis (Figure S2A) and immunosuppressive signaling between TAMs and CD8+ T cells through the IL-10 and TGF-β pathways (Figure S2B-C).

Differential expression analysis of M2-like macrophages from tumor and adjacent normal tissues based on scRNA-seq data identified 127 DEGs, including 47 upregulated and 80 downregulated genes in tumor-derived M2 macrophages (Fig. 1D). GO enrichment analysis revealed that these DEGs were primarily involved in BP related to immune regulation, inflammatory responses, and macrophage proliferation and differentiation (Figure S3A). A PPI network constructed using the STRING database highlighted TET3 as a potential central regulator of M2 macrophage reprogramming (Figure S3B). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that these DEGs were significantly enriched in macrophage polarization–associated pathways, including Toll-like receptor, TNF, and IL-17 signaling, as well as other inflammation-related pathways (Figure S3C).

To investigate the functional role of TET3 in M2 macrophages, BMDMs were transduced with a lentiviral vector to overexpress TET3. RT-qPCR and WB analysis confirmed significantly elevated levels of both TET3 mRNA and protein compared to the control group (Figure S4A). Total RNA was extracted from TET3-overexpressing and control macrophages and subjected to bulk RNA sequencing (Fig. 1E). Differential gene expression analysis was performed using the Limma package, and the top 50 genes ranked by absolute |logFC| were visualized in a heatmap, which clearly separated TET3-overexpressing and control macrophages (Fig. 1F). In total, 181 genes were significantly affected by TET3 overexpression (|logFC| > 2, p < 0.05), with 83 genes upregulated and 98 downregulated (Fig. 1G). GO and KEGG enrichment analyses further demonstrated that the DEGs were mainly involved in macrophage differentiation and migration and were significantly enriched in Toll-like receptor, JAK-STAT, cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, and chemokine signaling pathways (Figure S4B-C).

These bioinformatics analyses suggest that TET3 mediates the reprogramming of TAMs in HCC, contributing to an immunosuppressive TME and potentially facilitating tumor immune evasion.

TET3 overexpression promotes HCC progression and Is Highly expressed alongside IRF4 in TAMs

Previous studies have reported that TET3 promotes M2 polarization of Kupffer cells and enhances the migratory and invasive capabilities of HCC cells [24]. To further investigate the role of TET3 in HCC progression, we examined the effect of TET3 overexpression in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 2A). RT-qPCR and WB analyses revealed that TET3 mRNA and protein levels were significantly elevated in multiple HCC cell lines (Huh7, Hep3B, HCCLM3, Hepa1-6, and Hepa1c1c-7) compared with normal hepatocyte lines (THLE-3 and AML12), with Hepa1-6 and Hepa1c1c-7 exhibiting the highest expression (Figure S5A). These two murine HCC cell lines were therefore selected for subsequent experiments. Lentiviral-mediated overexpression of TET3 was successfully achieved, as confirmed by RT-qPCR and WB (Figure S5B). Functional assays demonstrated that TET3 overexpression significantly enhanced HCC cell proliferation, as shown by EdU incorporation (Figure S5C). Scratch wound healing and Transwell migration/invasion assays revealed that TET3 markedly promoted cell motility and invasiveness compared with vector controls (Figure S5D-E).

To validate these findings in vivo, TET3-overexpressing Hepa1-6 cells were subcutaneously implanted into C57BL/6 mice to establish HCC xenograft models. At 4 weeks, tumors in the TET3 group were significantly larger than those in the control group (Fig. 2B), and excised tumors displayed a substantially greater mass (Fig. 2C). H&E staining showed higher tumor cell density and fewer necrotic areas in TET3-overexpressing tumors (Fig. 2D). IHC further demonstrated higher expression levels of proliferation markers Ki67 and PCNA in the TET3 group (Fig. 2D-E). Additionally, RT-qPCR and IHC confirmed significantly increased TET3 expression in tumor tissues of the TET3-overexpressing group compared to the vector group (Fig. 2E-F). These data demonstrate that TET3 is highly expressed in HCC and functionally drives tumor growth by promoting cancer cell proliferation, migration, and invasion, thereby accelerating HCC progression.

IRF4 has been identified as a key transcription factor regulating M2 macrophage polarization [43]. To explore whether TET3 influences IRF4 expression in TAMs, we analyzed their spatial distribution and expression patterns in HCC tissues. Immunofluorescence staining showed prominent colocalization of TET3 with the M2 macrophage marker CD163 in tumors from the vector group, which was further intensified in the TET3-overexpressing group (Fig. 2G). Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated a significant increase in CD163+IRF4+ double-positive cells in tumors from the TET3 group compared with controls (Fig. 2H). To validate these findings in vitro, we induced the polarization of BMDMs into M0, M1, or M2 subtypes using specific cytokines. WB analysis confirmed effective polarization, with CD206 and CD163 markedly increased in M2 macrophages and CD86 selectively upregulated in M1 macrophages (Figure S5F-G). Furthermore, both RT-qPCR and WB analyses showed that the mRNA and protein levels of TET3 and IRF4 were significantly upregulated in M2 macrophages compared to M0 and M1 subsets (Fig. 2I-J).

Collectively, our results support the notion that TET3 promotes M2 macrophage polarization by upregulating IRF4, thereby facilitating tumor progression and immune evasion in HCC.

TET3 promotes IRF4 expression and M2 polarization in macrophages via DNA hydroxymethylation

TET3, a member of the TET family of enzymes, catalyzes the conversion of 5-methylcytosine to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine, thereby regulating DNA methylation dynamics and gene transcription [44]. Previous studies have shown that TET enzymes bind to regulatory regions of the IRF4 locus and promote CpG demethylation to enhance IRF4 expression [45]. We hypothesized that TET3 epigenetically controls IRF4 expression in macrophages, thereby driving M2 polarization (Fig. 3A). RT-qPCR and WB analyses confirmed efficient manipulation of TET3 expression in M2-polarized macrophages. TET3 mRNA and protein levels were significantly increased in the TET3-overexpression group, whereas both sh-TET3#1 and sh-TET3#2 markedly reduced TET3 expression compared with the sh-NC group, with sh-TET3#1 showing superior knockdown efficiency and therefore used for subsequent experiments (Fig. 3B-C). WB revealed that M2 markers were strongly upregulated in TET3-overexpressing macrophages and suppressed following TET3 knockdown (Fig. 3D). ELISA assays showed that IL-10 and TGF-β were significantly increased, whereas IL-6 and TNF-α were decreased, in the TET3-overexpression group compared with vector controls. In contrast, TET3 knockdown led to a reciprocal cytokine pattern, with reduced IL-10 and TGF-β and elevated IL-6 and TNF-α relative to the sh-NC group (Fig. 3E).

To elucidate the epigenetic mechanism underlying TET3-mediated IRF4 regulation, ChIP-qPCR was performed. TET3 was found to directly bind to the IRF4 promoter (Fig. 3F). Moreover, bisulfite-based methylation analysis and hydroxymethylation-sensitive qPCR demonstrated that TET3 overexpression increased 5hmC and reduced 5mC levels at the IRF4 promoter, whereas TET3 knockdown produced the opposite effect (Fig. 3G-H).

Our findings highlight the role of TET3 in promoting M2 polarization by enhancing IRF4 transcription through hydroxymethylation.

TET3 knockdown suppresses M2 polarization via IRF4 downregulation and enhances the antitumor activity of CD8⁺ T cells

To further validate the regulatory role of TET3 in M2 polarization and its impact on CD8+ T cell function, we conducted a series of in vitro and co-culture experiments (Fig. 4A). First, IRF4 was silenced in M2-polarized macrophages using two lentiviral shRNAs. RT-qPCR and WB demonstrated that sh-IRF4#2 achieved more efficient knockdown than sh-IRF4#1 and was used for subsequent experiments (Figure S6A-B). Consistently, IRF4 knockdown markedly reduced the protein levels of the M2 markers CD206 and CD163, confirming effective inhibition of M2 polarization (Figure S6C).

When IRF4-silenced macrophages were co-cultured with CD8+ T cells, flow cytometric analysis revealed a significant increase in the proportion of CFSE+CD8+ T cells, indicating enhanced T-cell proliferation. Moreover, the frequency of GZMB⁺CD8⁺ cytotoxic T cells was significantly elevated, whereas PD-1+ exhausted CD8+ T cells were markedly reduced in the sh-IRF4 group compared with sh-NC controls (Figure S6D). ELISA assays showed significantly increased IFN-γ levels in the supernatants of sh-IRF4 co-cultures (Figure S6E). These findings demonstrate that IRF4 depletion in macrophages suppresses M2 polarization and restores CD8⁺ T-cell effector function.

To further establish the regulatory hierarchy between TET3 and IRF4, TET3 was silenced in M2 macrophages, with or without concomitant IRF4 overexpression. RT-qPCR and WB confirmed that TET3 knockdown significantly reduced IRF4 expression, whereas exogenous IRF4 restored its levels in the sh-TET3 + IRF4 group (Fig. 4B-C). WB analysis of M2 marker proteins revealed that TET3 knockdown led to a significant downregulation of CD206 and CD163 in M2 macrophages (Fig. 4D). ELISA analysis further revealed that IL-10 and TGF-β were significantly decreased, whereas IL-6 and TNF-α were increased in the sh-TET3 + vector group; these changes were substantially rescued by IRF4 overexpression (Fig. 4E), indicating that TET3 promotes M2 polarization in part through the regulation of IRF4.

CFSE-labeled CD8+ T cells were co-cultured with the treated macrophages for 72 h to evaluate the immunomodulatory effects on T cells. Flow cytometry analysis demonstrated that CD8⁺ T cell proliferation was significantly enhanced in the sh-TET3 + vector group, as indicated by an increased proportion of CFSE+CD8+ T cells. Additionally, the frequency of GZMB⁺CD8⁺ T cells increased, while the proportion of PD-1+CD8+ T cells decreased, indicating reduced exhaustion. These effects were partially reversed in the sh-TET3 + IRF4 group, suggesting that IRF4 re-expression restores CD8+ T cell exhaustion and diminishes their proliferative and cytotoxic capacity (Fig. 4F). Consistent with these findings, ELISA analysis of co-culture supernatants revealed a significant increase in IFN-γ production in the sh-TET3 + vector group, which was reduced upon IRF4 overexpression (Fig. 4G).

These results indicate that TET3 knockdown enhances CD8+ T cell proliferation and antitumor activity by suppressing IRF4-dependent M2 polarization, thereby improving the immunosuppressive TME.

TET3 knockdown in macrophages inhibits tumorigenesis by reducing CD8+ T cell exhaustion in vivo

To validate the functional impact of TET3 knockdown in vivo, we employed a subcutaneous HCC mouse model to assess whether targeting TET3 in macrophages could suppress tumor growth by alleviating CD8+ T cell exhaustion and inhibiting M2 macrophage polarization (Fig. 5A). Compared with the sh-NC + anti-IgG group, tumor volume was significantly reduced in the sh-TET3 + anti-IgG group, whereas tumors were markedly enlarged in the sh-NC + anti-CD8a group. Importantly, when CD8+ T cells were depleted, no significant difference in tumor size was observed between the sh-TET3 + anti-CD8a and sh-NC + anti-CD8a groups, indicating that the antitumor effect of TET3 knockdown is CD8⁺ T-cell dependent. Consistently, tumor volume in the sh-TET3 + anti-CD8a group was significantly larger than in the sh-TET3 + anti-IgG group (Fig. 5B). H&E staining further supported these findings. Tumors in the sh-NC + anti-IgG group displayed densely packed tumor cells with minimal necrosis, which was further aggravated in the sh-NC + anti-CD8a group. In contrast, the sh-TET3 + anti-IgG group showed loosely arranged tumor cells and extensive necrotic regions, whereas CD8+ T-cell depletion (sh-TET3 + anti-CD8a) significantly reduced tumor necrosis (Fig. 5C).

Notably, CD8+ T-cell depletion largely abolished the antiproliferative effect of TET3 knockdown (Fig. 5F). TUNEL staining showed markedly increased apoptosis in sh-TET3 + anti-IgG tumors, which was significantly reduced upon CD8+ T-cell depletion (Fig. 5G).

Flow cytometric analysis further demonstrated that the proportion of F4/80+CD206+ M2-like TAMs was significantly reduced in both sh-TET3 + anti-IgG and sh-TET3 + anti-CD8a groups, whereas anti-CD8a treatment alone had no effect on macrophage polarization (Fig. 5H). Moreover, TET3 knockdown markedly increased the frequency of GZMB⁺ cytotoxic CD8+ T cells and reduced the proportions of PD-1+, Tim-3+, and Lag-3+ exhausted CD8+ T cells. These effects were completely abrogated by CD8⁺ T-cell depletion (Fig. 5I).

Collectively, these data demonstrate that TET3 knockdown suppresses IRF4 expression in TAMs, reduces M2 polarization, and enhances the cytotoxic function of CD8⁺ T cells.

Preparation and characterization of TET3 siRNA@αmp-lipo

We successfully developed TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo, in which the core TET3 siRNA@Lipo nanoparticles were first assembled via electrostatic interactions and subsequently modified with M2pep and α-peptide to confer dual M2 macrophage–targeting capability (Fig. 6A). TEM revealed that TET3 siRNA@Lipo and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo exhibited uniform spherical morphology (Fig. 6B). DLS analysis indicated a narrow size distribution with low PDI, and negative Zeta potentials suggested good colloidal stability (Fig. 6C). Specifically, TET3 siRNA@Lipo showed an average diameter of approximately 96.9 nm with a PDI of 0.102 (green curve), while TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo had a slightly larger size of 122.9 nm and a PDI of 0.108 (red curve), indicating successful surface conjugation of M2-targeting peptides. The Zeta potential increased from − 41.2 ± 0.4 mV for TET3 siRNA@Lipo to -16.9 ± 0.3 mV for TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo (Fig. 6C), further supporting surface modification. RiboGreen assays demonstrated high EE% of TET3 siRNA (Fig. 6C). Agarose gel electrophoresis confirmed enhanced siRNA protection in TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo, as evident by the improved integrity of bands after RNase A treatment, compared to free TET3 siRNA (Figure S7A).

Stability studies showed that TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo maintained a stable particle size in PBS containing 10% FBS at both pH 7.4 and pH 6.8 (Figure S7B). In vitro release assays revealed a pH-dependent release profile, with approximately 85% siRNA released at pH 5.0 after 120 h, compared with only ~ 50% at pH 7.4 (Figure S7C), suggesting favorable release under the acidic TME.

The biosafety of the nanoparticles was further evaluated. Hemolysis assays showed that both TET3 siRNA@Lipo and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo induced hemolysis rates below 5%, comparable to the negative control and far lower than the positive control, indicating excellent blood compatibility (Figure S7D). In addition, CCK-8 assays demonstrated that neither formulation exerted significant cytotoxicity toward M2 macrophages over a wide concentration range (6.25–100 µg/mL), with high cell viability maintained even at the highest dose (Fig. 6D). Thus, our data demonstrate that TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo exhibits favorable biocompatibility and minimal cytotoxicity, supporting its applicability for further therapeutic and targeting strategies.

In the cellular uptake assay, M2 macrophages were incubated with Dil-labeled TET3 siRNA@Lipo or TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo, and fluorescence signal distribution was observed at various time points (0.5, 1, 4, and 8 h) using fluorescence microscopy. Compared to TET3 siRNA@Lipo, TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo showed significantly higher intracellular fluorescence in M2 macrophages, especially at 8 h post-treatment, indicating stronger targeting specificity and superior uptake efficiency (Fig. 6E). In a competitive uptake assay with mixed M1 and M2 macrophages (1:1 ratio), flow cytometry and confocal microscopy confirmed preferential accumulation of TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo in M2 macrophages, with markedly higher fluorescence intensity compared with M1 cells (Fig. 6F-G). These results demonstrate that TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo exhibits excellent safety, high cellular compatibility, and strong M2 macrophage-specific targeting, offering a promising strategy for intervening in immune escape mechanisms during HCC immunotherapy.

Endocytic pathway analysis revealed that, in untreated control cells, Dil-labeled TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo was evenly distributed throughout the cytoplasm with strong fluorescence intensity. In contrast, treatment with chlorpromazine (CPZ), an inhibitor of clathrin-mediated endocytosis, resulted in a marked reduction in intracellular fluorescence, indicating significantly impaired nanoparticle uptake. EIPA, an inhibitor of macropinocytosis, also reduced nanoparticle internalization, albeit to a lesser extent. Similarly, methyl-β-cyclodextrin (MβCD), which disrupts lipid rafts, decreased fluorescence intensity, suggesting that lipid raft–mediated endocytosis contributes to nanoparticle uptake (Figure S7E). Quantifying intracellular fluorescence using ImageJ showed that CPZ had the strongest inhibitory effect on TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo uptake, followed by MβCD and EIPA (Figure S7F). These findings suggest that TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo is primarily internalized via clathrin-mediated endocytosis, with additional contributions from lipid raft–mediated and macropinocytosis-like pathways in M1 and M2 macrophages.

To further evaluate endosomal escape, endosomes and lysosomes were labeled with LysoTracker Red, and the intracellular distribution of DiI-labeled TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo was visualized by confocal microscopy. After 4 h of incubation, substantial overlap between red (LysoTracker) and green (DiI) fluorescence was observed, indicating partial localization of nanoparticles within endo/lysosomal compartments. With prolonged incubation, the degree of colocalization markedly decreased, suggesting that a significant fraction of nanoparticles escaped from endosomes into the cytosol (Fig. 6H). Quantitative analysis of colocalization coefficients showed that M2 macrophages exhibited significantly lower colocalization between nanoparticles and LysoTracker compared with M1 macrophages, indicating more efficient endosomal escape in M2 cells (Fig. 6I).

These results demonstrate that TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo is efficiently internalized and capable of effective endosomal escape, particularly in M2 macrophages, providing a mechanistic basis for its superior intracellular delivery and therapeutic efficacy.

TET3 siRNA@αmp-lipo suppresses M2 polarization by downregulating IRF4 and enhances CD8⁺ T cell-mediated antitumor activity

We next evaluated the immunomodulatory efficacy of TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo in vitro, focusing on its ability to inhibit M2 macrophage polarization and to restore CD8+ T-cell function (Fig. 7A). M2-polarized macrophages were treated with PBS, TET3 siRNA@Lipo, or TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo. RT-qPCR and WB analyses revealed that TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo significantly downregulated both TET3 and IRF4 expression in macrophages, with a greater suppressive effect compared to TET3 siRNA@Lipo (Fig. 7B-C). WB analysis demonstrated a pronounced reduction in the M2 markers CD206 and CD163 in both nanoparticle-treated groups, with the strongest inhibitory effect observed in the TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo group (Fig. 7D). ELISA results of culture supernatants indicated that IL-10 and TGF-β secretion was significantly decreased, while IL-6 and TNF-α levels were increased in the TET3 siRNA@Lipo and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo groups (Fig. 7E), supporting the inhibition of M2 polarization.

TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo modulates CD8⁺ T cell function by regulating macrophage M2 polarization.Note: (A) Schematic diagram illustrating the experimental design investigating how TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo suppresses macrophage M2 polarization via IRF4 downregulation and enhances CD8⁺ T cell function (Created in BioRender); (B-C) RT-qPCR and Western Blot analysis of TET3 and IRF4 expression in macrophages under different treatments; (D) Western Blot analysis of M2 polarization markers in macrophages; (E) ELISA quantification of IL-10, TGF-β, IL-6, and TNF-α in macrophage supernatants; (F) Flow cytometry analysis of CFSE⁺CD8⁺ T cells to assess proliferation; (G) Flow cytometry detection of GZMB⁺CD8⁺ T cells and PD-1⁺CD8⁺ T cells; (H) ELISA measurement of IFN-γ levels in co-culture supernatants. n = 3 biological replicates. *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001

To assess the impact on CD8⁺ T-cell responses, CFSE-labeled CD8+ T cells were co-cultured with macrophages from the different treatment groups for 72 h. Flow cytometric analysis revealed significantly higher proportions of CFSE⁺CD8⁺ T cells in both nanoparticle groups, indicating enhanced T-cell proliferation (Fig. 7F). Furthermore, the percentage of GZMB+CD8+ T cells was markedly increased, whereas PD-1+CD8+ T cells were significantly reduced in the TET3 siRNA@Lipo and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo groups (Fig. 7G). ELISA detection of IFN-γ levels in co-culture supernatants showed a substantial elevation in both treatment groups relative to PBS (Fig. 7H).

Together, these findings suggest that TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo concurrently inhibits IRF4-mediated M2 polarization and restores CD8⁺ T cell effector function, thereby reversing immunosuppression in the TME.

TET3 siRNA@αmp-lipo inhibits M2 polarization of macrophages, reduces CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion, and enhances anti-tumor immunity in HCC

To evaluate the in vivo targeting efficiency and therapeutic potential of TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo, HCC-bearing C57BL/6 mice were intravenously injected with DiI-labeled TET3 siRNA@Lipo or TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo, and tumor tissues were collected at 6, 12, and 24 h post-injection (Fig. 8A). Immunofluorescence staining revealed strong colocalization between TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo and CD206+ TAMs within tumor tissues, with significantly higher colocalization signals at all time points compared to the TET3 siRNA@Lipo group (Figure S8A). Flow cytometry revealed a markedly higher proportion of TET3 siRNA+F4/80+CD206+ TAMs in the TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo group, confirming superior TAM-specific delivery within the TME (Figure S8B), indicating enhanced delivery specificity to TAMs within the TME.

Pharmacokinetic analysis further demonstrated that TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo exhibited prolonged circulation time in vivo, reflecting enhanced stability and protection of the encapsulated siRNA (Table S4). IVIS imaging showed that TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo accumulated preferentially in tumor tissues, with substantially stronger fluorescence signals at the tumor site than those observed for TET3 siRNA@Lipo (Figure S8C; Table S5). Quantitative ROI analysis confirmed that tumor fluorescence intensity in the TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo group was more than twofold higher than in the non-targeted group, indicating superior tumor accumulation and retention (Figure S8D). Importantly, biosafety evaluation demonstrated excellent systemic tolerance. H&E staining of major organs and serum biochemical analyses showed no evident pathological damage or abnormal changes in ALT, AST, γ-GT, BUN, CREA, or LDH levels across all treatment groups, indicating good systemic safety (Figure S9, Figure S10).

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo in vivo, C57BL/6 mice bearing HCC tumors were intravenously injected with TET3 siRNA@Lipo or TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo in combination with either anti-IgG or anti-CD8a antibodies. Tumor volumes were monitored weekly, and tumors were harvested at week 4 for further analysis. Compared to the PBS + anti-IgG group, both the TET3 siRNA@Lipo + anti-IgG and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo + anti-IgG groups exhibited significantly reduced tumor volumes and weights, with TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo showing the most pronounced antitumor effect. In contrast, administration of anti-CD8a antibodies markedly attenuated the therapeutic efficacy of both siRNA formulations (Fig. 8B-C). Histological analysis by H&E staining showed expanded necrotic areas and loosely arranged tumor cells in the TET3 siRNA@Lipo + anti-IgG and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo + anti-IgG groups compared with controls, with the most extensive necrosis observed in the TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo group. In contrast, CD8+ T-cell depletion substantially reduced tumor necrosis and restored dense tumor architecture (Fig. 8D). IHC analysis revealed that TET3 and IRF4 expression was markedly decreased in tumors from both siRNA-treated groups, independent of CD8+ T-cell depletion. Notably, the proliferation marker Ki67 was significantly reduced in the TET3 siRNA@Lipo + anti-IgG and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo + anti-IgG groups, with a stronger effect observed in the TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo group. However, anti-CD8a treatment reversed Ki67 suppression without affecting TET3 or IRF4 levels (Fig. 8E). TUNEL staining indicated a significant increase in apoptotic cells in the TET3 siRNA@Lipo + anti-IgG and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo + anti-IgG groups compared to PBS + anti-IgG, with the TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo group displaying the highest level of apoptosis. Anti-CD8a administration markedly reduced apoptosis in both nanoparticle-treated groups (Fig. 8F).

Flow cytometry analysis further demonstrated that, compared to the PBS + anti-IgG group, both the TET3 siRNA@Lipo + anti-IgG and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo + anti-IgG groups exhibited a significant reduction in the proportion of F4/80⁺CD206⁺ TAMs and an increase in F4/80⁺CD86⁺ macrophages within tumor tissues. Notably, TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo induced a greater reduction in M2-like (CD206⁺) TAMs and a more pronounced increase in M1-like (CD86⁺) TAMs than TET3 siRNA@Lipo. Anti-CD8a treatment did not significantly affect TAM polarization (Fig. 8G). In addition, the proportion of GZMB⁺CD8⁺ T cells was significantly elevated in the TET3 siRNA@Lipo + anti-IgG and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo + anti-IgG groups, while the frequency of exhausted CD8⁺ T cells was markedly decreased compared to PBS + anti-IgG controls. TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo exerted a stronger effect than TET3 siRNA@Lipo in enhancing cytotoxic CD8⁺ T cells and reducing exhaustion. However, CD8⁺ T cell depletion via anti-CD8a significantly reduced GZMB⁺CD8⁺ T cells and increased exhausted CD8⁺ T cells (Fig. 8H). Consistent with these observations, ELISA analysis revealed that IFN-γ and TNF-α levels were significantly increased in tumor tissues of the TET3 siRNA@Lipo + anti-IgG and TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo + anti-IgG groups, with TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo inducing a higher cytokine production than TET3 siRNA@Lipo. These effects were substantially abrogated by anti-CD8a treatment (Fig. 8I).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo reprograms TAMs by suppressing IRF4-driven M2 polarization, thereby relieving CD8+ T-cell exhaustion and restoring antitumor immunity, ultimately remodeling the HCC TME toward an immunostimulatory state with strong therapeutic potential.

Discussion

This study focuses on the dysregulation of macrophages and T cells within the immunosuppressive microenvironment of HCC, revealing that TET3 promotes M2 polarization and induces CD8+ T cell exhaustion through IRF4 hydroxymethylation. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis identified high TET3–IRF4 expression in M2-like TAMs, which suppress T-cell function via the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis. To therapeutically target this pathway, we developed TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo, a dual-targeted nanoplatform that selectively silences TET3 in M2 macrophages, effectively reversing their immunosuppressive phenotype and restoring antitumor immunity. This work uncovers an epigenetic mechanism underlying immune regulation in HCC and provide a translational strategy for precise TIME remodeling, offering a promising avenue to enhance the efficacy of immunotherapy in HCC.

Members of the TET family are well known for regulating DNA methylation and cell fate decisions, with TET1 and TET2 extensively studied in immune and neural systems. In contrast, the role of TET3 in tumor-associated immune cells remains largely unexplored [46]. While TET3 has been implicated in stem cell differentiation and tumor metabolism, its impact on macrophage phenotype has not been previously defined [23, 47, 48]. Our study provide the first evidence that TET3 promotes M2 macrophage polarization by epigenetically regulating IRF4 via hydroxymethylation, thereby filling a critical gap in our understanding of immune regulation in cancer. Unlike prior studies focused mainly on tumor-intrinsic pathways, our work identifies TET3 as a central immune regulator within the TME, highlighting its potential as an upstream target for combination immunotherapy strategies.

IRF4 is a pivotal transcription factor regulating macrophage polarization, T-cell differentiation, and plasma cell development, and has been extensively studied in multiple myeloma, autoimmune disorders, and chronic infections [49, 50]. In cancer, IRF4 transcriptionally activates M2-associated genes and is closely linked to the formation of tumor-promoting immune microenvironments [51, 52]. Our study identifies TET3 as an upstream regulator that promotes IRF4 expression through hydroxymethylation, thereby driving M2 polarization. This finding expands the IRF4 regulatory network from an epigenetic perspective and highlights a novel mechanism underlying immune suppression in cancer. Notably, targeting TET3 may provide a more durable and systemic strategy to reprogram the TIME than directly inhibiting IRF4 or its downstream targets, highlighting the therapeutic potential of epigenetic modulation in tumor immunotherapy.

Macrophages and T cells form a complex regulatory network within the TIME [53, 54]. M2-like macrophages are known to impair T-cell function through immunosuppressive cytokines (e.g., IL-10 and TGF-β), extracellular vesicles, and chemokine pathways—most notably the CXCL12/CXCR4 axis, a key driver of T-cell exhaustion [55–57]. However, the upstream regulators of this chemokine axis have remained poorly understood [58–60]. Our data reveal that the TET3/IRF4 axis upregulates CXCL12 expression in macrophages, thereby indirectly promoting CD8+ T cell dysfunction and exhaustion, uncovering an additional layer of macrophage-mediated regulation of T cell fate. In contrast to the traditional view that T-cell exhaustion is governed mainly by T-cell–intrinsic programs, our findings highlight the dominant role of macrophage-derived signals. Importantly, reprogramming M2 macrophages restored CD8⁺ T-cell effector function and enhanced antitumor cytotoxicity, providing a mechanistic rationale for combination immunotherapies and identifying a potential biomarker axis for immunotherapy resistance in HCC.

LNPs are among the most advanced siRNA delivery platforms, offering high transfection efficiency, low immunogenicity, and excellent biocompatibility [61]. Their clinical success in COVID-19 vaccines and rare disease therapies further underscores their strong translational potential [62, 63]. Although knockdown of TET3 in tumor cells may exert direct antitumor effects, this study preferentially targeted M2 macrophages because TAM-mediated immunosuppression plays a dominant and therapy-resistant role in HCC. Moreover, a macrophage-specific delivery strategy enables maximal restoration of CD8⁺ T-cell-mediated antitumor immunity while minimizing unintended effects on tumor cells. This study engineered a functionalized LNP delivery system, TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo, by modifying conventional LNPs with α-macrophage-targeting peptides to precisely deliver M2-like TAMs. This platform exhibits superior serum stability, RNase resistance, and cellular uptake compared with non-targeted LNPs. Importantly, it is modular and adaptable, providing a versatile strategy for delivering other epigenetic regulators to immune cells across diverse therapeutic applications.

ICIs are central to modern cancer immunotherapy, their efficacy critically depends on the immune status of the TME [64]. We propose a “TME modulation before immune activation” strategy to enhance ICI responsiveness. By silencing TET3, M2 macrophage polarization is suppressed and CD8+ T-cell function is restored, positioning this approach as an effective priming or combinatorial strategy for ICI therapy [65, 66]. Compared with conventional small-molecule inhibitors, our siRNA–nanoparticle platform offers higher specificity, controllability, and tunability. It provides a versatile foundation for combination regimens with PD-1 blockade, TGF-β inhibitors, and other immunomodulators, enabling multi-target synergistic interventions. This strategy holds promise for overcoming ICI resistance in HCC, as well as in immunologically “cold” tumors and non-responder populations.

Despite the antitumor efficacy of TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo in murine HCC models, several challenges must be addressed before clinical translation. First, the nanoparticle delivery system’s long-term in vivo stability and potential immunogenicity require further investigation. Second, the current study is limited to murine models, and additional validation in large animal models and preclinical studies is essential to confirm both efficacy and safety. Moreover, optimizing nanoparticle targeting specificity and delivery efficiency remains critical for future development to maximize therapeutic benefits. Finally, although the key conclusions of this study have been robustly validated through extensive and systematic in vitro experiments, bulk RNA-seq analyses, and multiple in vivo mouse models, the GEO dataset used for preliminary analysis included only two tissue samples. This limited sample size may restrict the comprehensive capture of intrinsic tissue heterogeneity. Future studies will expand clinical sample sizes, integrate multicenter datasets, and perform additional in vivo experiments to further validate and extend the present findings, thereby providing more robust and broadly applicable evidence for the underlying mechanisms.

In conclusion, this study identifies TET3–IRF4–mediated epigenetic regulation as a central mechanism driving M2 macrophage polarization and CD8+ T-cell exhaustion, thereby shaping the immunosuppressive TME in HCC. By engineering a TAM-targeted LNP delivery system, TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo, we achieved precise TET3 silencing, effectively reversing immune suppression and inhibiting tumor progression. Scientifically, these findings establish a new link between epigenetic control and tumor immunity. Clinically, this targeted delivery strategy offers a promising approach for treating immunologically “cold” tumors and enhancing immunotherapy responsiveness. Future studies integrating clinical specimens, multicenter validation, and combination regimens with existing immunotherapies will be essential to advance this platform toward precision immunotherapy for HCC.

Conclusion

This study uncovers a key mechanism by which TET3 promotes macrophage M2 polarization through IRF4 hydroxymethylation, ultimately suppressing CD8+ T cell function. By engineering TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo, we successfully achieved efficient delivery of TET3 siRNA into the TME. This strategy significantly inhibited the M2 polarization of macrophages and restored the antitumor activity of CD8+ T cells, resulting in robust tumor growth suppression in an HCC model. This is the first study to define the functional role of the TET3-IRF4 hydroxymethylation axis in regulating TAM polarization and CD8+ T cell exhaustion, offering new mechanistic insights into immune evasion in HCC. The TET3 siRNA@αmp-Lipo platform demonstrated precise targeting capability and effective remodeling of the TIME, providing a promising nanotherapeutic approach for HCC immunotherapy. Given its modular and adaptable design, this delivery system also offers broad potential for transporting other siRNAs or immunomodulators, supporting its future clinical translation.
