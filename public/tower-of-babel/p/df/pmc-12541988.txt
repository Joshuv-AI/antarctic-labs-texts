Sero-prevalence of hepatitis B among health facility staff in Salima district, Malawi – a cross-sectional study
Authors: George Dalitso Limwado, Yamikani Nuka, Kondwani Mpinga, Yanja Mawindo, Mark Chakwiya, Luciano Abraham, Victor Chikwapulo, Mzee Chalira, Dzimkambani Kambalame, Flexon Ngulinga, Collins Mitambo, Yonasi Chise
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimsHepatitis B (HBV) poses a significant public health challenge in Malawi with a pooled prevalence of 8% in the general population. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of HBV infection among healthcare workers in Salima District, Malawi, and assess the severity of positive cases.MethodsIn September 2020, a cross-sectional study was conducted in all public health facilities across Salima District and screened 854 healthcare workers for hepatitis B virus (HBV) using antigen point-of-care tests. Data on demographics and risk history were collected. Those who tested positive were linked to care, and further assessments were carried out by medical officers. Family members of positive cases were also screened for HBV. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize the data, with categorical variables presented as frequencies and percentages, and quantitative variables as means with standard deviations. The relationship between demographic factors and HBV prevalence was analyzed using unadjusted prevalence ratios (PR).ResultsThe prevalence of HBV infection among healthcare workers was 2.93% (25 infections, 95% CI: 1.90–4.29%). Infections were more common among males (95% CI: 0.81–5.98%), married participants (95% CI: 0.13–1.07%), and those unvaccinated against HBV. Environmental health personnel (95% CI: 0.78–69.46%) exhibited the highest chronic HBV prevalence. All positive cases were enrolled in the HBV clinic within two weeks of diagnosis, and none had signs of severe liver disease.DiscussionDespite lower prevalence than the general population, HBV infection rates remain concerning among healthcare workers, particularly those in non-clinical roles. The lack of vaccination history is a notable risk factor for infection.ConclusionThe study underscores the elevated prevalence of HBV infection among healthcare workers in the Salima District, emphasizing the importance of vaccination and prompt linkage to care.Keywords: Hepatitis b infection, Healthcare workers, Prevalence

Introduction

Hepatitis B (HBV) is a common communicable disease with a high prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa. The World Health Organization (WHO), estimates that 254 million people were living with chronic hepatitis B infection in 2022, with 1.2 million new infections each year [1]. HBV affects mostly the WHO African Region and the Western Pacific Region [2]. Sub-Saharan Africa has approximately 60 million chronically infected with HBV, and one of the highest mortality rates for the virus, estimated at 80,000 per annum [2–6].

HBV infections are associated with multiple risk factors, including occupational exposure among healthcare workers (HCWs), unprotected sex with multiple partners, sharing needles among intravenous drug users, and receiving blood transfusions. In highly endemic regions such as sub-Saharan Africa (SSA), early horizontal transmission and mother-to-child transmission also contribute significantly to the spread of HBV [1, 7, 8]. HCWs and other staff members who work in healthcare settings are at increased risk of contracting HBV. HCWs are four times more likely to be infected with HBV compared to the general population, due to needle stick injuries and contact with infected blood or blood products [9–13]. Furthermore, there is a low vaccination rate among healthcare workers in low-income countries, estimated at 2.3% [7, 8, 14]. In contrast, developed countries have implemented the same vaccination program to safeguard their healthcare workforce from HBV infection [8, 11, 12, 15].

Malawi has a pooled HBV prevalence (HBsAg) of 8.1%, (95% CI 6.1% − 10.3%) among adults in the general population [16]. Neighbouring countries also report high HBV prevalence rates, with Mozambique at 12.2%, Tanzania at 7.2%, and Zambia at 6.5% [13, 16–18]. To the best of our knowledge, there are no published reports on the prevalence of HBV infection specifically among healthcare workers in Malawi. However, a related study conducted among 62 foundational year medical students at Kamuzu University of Health Sciences (KUHeS), who are vaccinated against hepatitis B at the start of their clinical rotations, reported a prevalence rate of zero [19]. In contrast, neighbouring countries have documented HBV prevalence rates among healthcare workers, with Beira City, Mozambique, reporting a prevalence of 5.1% and a tertiary hospital in Tanzania reporting a rate of 7.4% [13, 20].

The Viral Hepatitis Unit at the Ministry of Health is actively aligning its efforts with the WHO’s Global Health Sector Strategy on Viral Hepatitis (2016–2022), with the ultimate goal of eliminating viral hepatitis by 2030 [2]. To achieve this, the unit is implementing evidence-based strategies, including the screening and vaccination of frontline healthcare workers (HCWs) and other health facility staff, who are at elevated professional risk of infection [21–23].

This study, conducted as part of a two-week vaccination campaign targeting healthcare workers (HCWs), is the first to investigate HBV prevalence among this key group in Malawi. These efforts are crucial in reducing occupational HBV transmission and improving the health and safety of HCWs, who play a vital role in combating viral hepatitis.

The study aimed to determine the HBV prevalence among all health facility staff, including both frontline HCWs (clinicians and nurses) and non-frontline HCWs (support staff) across health facilities under the Salima District Health Office. Additionally, it examined HBV prevalence across different healthcare worker cadres and assessed the severity of detected HBV infections, ensuring that those affected received appropriate care and support.

Methods

Study design

A facility-based cross-sectional study was conducted involving all staff members at health facilities under the Salima District Health Office in September 2020. This study took place during a two-week HBV vaccination campaign organized by the Environmental Health Department at the Salima District Health Office. The campaign was part of a national immunization campaign led by the Viral Hepatitis Unit at the Ministry of Health. Its goal was to vaccinate all frontline and non-frontline healthcare workers. Participants underwent initial HBV screening. Those who tested negative received the HBV vaccine, while those who tested positive were referred for further tests, treatment, and screening of their household members. The investigations included Liver Function Tests, Full Blood Count, provider-initiated HIV testing and counselling (PITC), and a Liver Fibroscan. These tests were conducted to assess the severity of the disease.

Study setting

Salima is a lakeshore district and a popular tourist destination in Malawi. It is situated along a lakeshore road that connects the northern and southern parts of the country. At the time of the study, the district had a population of 473,554 [24, 25]. The study was conducted in all 20 health facilities under the Salima District Health Office [26]. The study included 19 primary healthcare facilities, comprising 12 government-owned facilities, 5 facilities operated by the Christian Health Association of Malawi (CHAM), and 3 facilities run by non-governmental organizations (NGOs). The specific health facilities involved were ADMARC Dispensary, Baptist, Chagunda, Chinguluwe, Chipoka, Chitala, Kaphatenga, Katawa, Khombedza, Lifeline, Lifuwu, Maganga, Makioni, Malawi Armed Forces College (MAFCO), Mary Lou, Mchoka, Ngodzi, Parachute Battalion, and Thavite Health Centers, as well as Ndimoyo Palliative Care Clinic [26]. Additionally, Salima District Hospital (SDH), a secondary healthcare facility, was included in the study.

Sample size and sample selection

According to the records from the Human Resource Office of the Salima District Health Office, there were 1,200 staff members employed across 20 health facilities in the district (Fig. 1). Purposive sampling was applied, and the study included all staff who met the following criteria: currently employed at any of the 20 health facilities in Salima District and willing to provide informed consent. No additional criteria, such as vaccination history, were applied, as no prior vaccination records were available. A total of 854 staff members met these inclusion criteria and participated in the study. Staff members who were either unavailable or declined to participate were excluded.

Patient flow during screening

Data collection

The screening took place at the SDH laboratory for staff based at the district hospital. Additionally, a team of laboratory scientists visited all health facilities to conduct the screening. At these other health facilities, a separate room was allocated for testing. The farthest health facility, Makioni Health Center, is located 75 km away from SDH. After obtaining written informed consent, demographic data (age, gender, marital status, cadre), and additional details (years of service, number of household members, and history of prior HBV vaccination) were collected using a paper-based tool.

The study participants received pre-test counselling. Using an aseptic technique, a blood sample was collected through a finger prick for the HBV point-of-care test. A one-step rapid immunochromatographic test (Right sign rapid diagnostic test by Hangzhou Biotest Biotech Co., Ltd, China) was conducted to detect hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) [27]. The rapid diagnostic test has a sensitivity of 99.8% and a specificity of 99.6% [27]. The test results were recorded as positive or negative and read after 15–20 min. Staff members who tested negative received their first HBV vaccine (rDNA hepatitis B vaccine) on the same day and were given appointments for the remaining two doses scheduled after one and six months (Fig. 2). Those who tested positive received post-test counselling and were referred from the health centers to Salima District Hospital (SDH) for a review by a senior medical officer within a week. The counselling included recommendations for screening family members. At SDH, participants were provided with standardized, protocol-based care following the Guidelines for the Prevention and Management of Hepatitis B and C in Malawi [28].

A flow chart indicating how many staff members were approached and how many were included in the study

According to the protocol, chronic HBV patients who met specific criteria were initiated on a combination treatment of Lamivudine and Tenofovir Disoproxil Fumarate (TDF). The criteria for initiating treatment were as follows:

Criteria 1 - risk of fibrosis, defined as age over 30 years, Alanine Transaminase (ALT) levels persistently above the upper limit of normal on 2 to 3 occasions over a 6 to 12-month period, and HBV DNA levels exceeding 20,000 IU/ml [28].

Criteria 1 - risk of fibrosis, defined as age over 30 years, Alanine Transaminase (ALT) levels persistently above the upper limit of normal on 2 to 3 occasions over a 6 to 12-month period, and HBV DNA levels exceeding 20,000 IU/ml [28].

Criteria 2 - evidence of cirrhosis, identified through clinical signs, ultrasound findings indicative of cirrhosis, an APRI score greater than 1.5, or a liver fibroscan result above 9.4 kPa. However, due to resource limitations, HBV DNA testing was not conducted on those who tested positive [28].

Criteria 2 - evidence of cirrhosis, identified through clinical signs, ultrasound findings indicative of cirrhosis, an APRI score greater than 1.5, or a liver fibroscan result above 9.4 kPa. However, due to resource limitations, HBV DNA testing was not conducted on those who tested positive [28].

In line with national guidelines, chronic HBV is defined as a liver infection where hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) remains detectable in the blood for at least six months post-infection [28]. This definition was applied in the study.

Data analysis

The data was initially recorded using paper-based tools and subsequently transferred to a Microsoft Excel spreadsheet for cleaning. Statistical analysis was performed using STATA version 18 [29]. Descriptive statistics were utilized to summarize the variables, presenting categorical variables as frequencies and percentages, while quantitative variables were summarized as means with standard deviations. HCWs were classified into two main professional categories based on their roles in healthcare delivery: frontline HCWs (including clinicians such as medical doctors, clinical officers, clinical technicians, medical assistants, laboratory personnel, pharmacy personnel, and nurses, including nursing officers, nursing technicians, and community midwife assistants) and support staff (comprising of patient attendants, environmental health officers, health surveillance assistants, maids, administrative staff such as human resource officers, accountants, drivers, guards, and ground labourers, as well as the biomedical engineering team). Exact 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) for proportions were calculated to measure the precision of the estimates.

The relationship between demographic variables and HBV prevalence was assessed using logistic regression analysis. Previous studies from Ethiopia, Kenya, Mozambique, Nigeria, Tanzania, and Uganda informed the selection of independent variables. These studies demonstrated a relationship between HBV infection and factors such as age, cadre, sex, marital status, prior HBV vaccination, and years of service [11, 13, 20–23, 30].

Results

Participant’s demographic characteristics

Of the 1,200 staff members employed by the Salima District Health Office, 854 (71.2%) participated in this study. Of these, 457 (53.5%) were male. Participants were categorized into two age groups: 20–49 years, comprising 774 (90.6%) individuals, and those over 49 years, accounting for 80 (9.4%) individuals. The majority, 636 (74.5%), were married, and a significant portion of the group (250, 29.3%) were environmental health personnel. Most participants (370, 43.3%) were employed at Salima District Hospital (SDH). Years of service were divided into two categories: those with less than 10 years of service (416, 48.7%) and those with 10 or more years of service (438, 51.3%). Only 283 (33.1%) participants reported having been vaccinated for HBV prior to the study (Table 1). Since official vaccination records were unavailable, participants were asked to recall their vaccination history; while some recalled being vaccinated, many were uncertain about whether their vaccinations had been completed.

Prevalence of HBV among staff members

The overall prevalence of chronic HBV infection (HBsAg+) was 2.93% (25 infections, 95% CI: 0.019–0.0429%) (Table 2). HBV infection was more common among males (17 infections, 95% CI: 0.81–5.98%) and married individuals (18 infections, 95% CI: 0.13–1.07%). Among the different cadres, the highest prevalence of HBV infection was observed among environmental health personnel (11 infections, 95% CI: 0.78–69.46%) and hospital attendants (6 infections, 95% CI: 0.46–182.78.46.78%). Of the 25 positive cases, 6 participants (3 frontline and 3 non-frontline healthcare workers) reported having been previously vaccinated against HBV. No participants had indeterminate results, and none met the treatment criteria for HBV therapy.

All participants who tested positive for HBV were promptly linked to care, and none had a severe form of the disease (APRI Score: 0.5 to 1). Chronicity was confirmed through repeat HBsAg + testing at six months. Additionally, 32 household contacts of the positive cases were screened, of which 18 (56.25%) were female. Their ages ranged from 5 to 54 years, and none tested positive for HBV. All household contacts were subsequently vaccinated against HBV.

Analysis of HBV positivity and socio-demographic characteristics

When analyzing the relationship between HBV positivity and socio-demographic factors (age, cadre, sex, marital status, HBV vaccination history, and years of service), no significant associations were found (Table 3). This lack of association highlights the need for continued universal screening and vaccination efforts among healthcare workers, regardless of their demographic background.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this study is the first to comprehensively assess HBV prevalence among both frontline healthcare workers and support staff in a healthcare setting in Malawi. This investigation provides critical insights into the occupational risk faced by various cadres within healthcare facilities and highlights the importance of targeted prevention strategies to protect these essential personnel.

The prevalence of HBV found in this study, 2.93% (95% CI: 0.019–0.0429%), is notably lower than that of the general population, which stands at 8.1% (95% CI: 6.1%–10.3%) [16]. However, the prevalence is particularly high among lower cadres, such as support staff, highlighting occupational vulnerabilities within this group. Additionally, the observed prevalence is lower compared to similar studies conducted in Ethiopia (9.6%; 95% CI: 6.8–21.3%), Kenya (4.5%; 95% CI: 2.4–7.4%), Mozambique (5.1%; 95% CI: 2.9–8.1%), Uganda (8.1%; 95% CI: 5.3–10.9%), and Tanzania (5.7%–7.0%) [11, 13, 20–23, 30].

Our study did not identify any significant relationship between HBV positivity and demographic characteristics, a finding that contrasts with results from studies conducted in Ethiopia, Kenya, Mozambique, Uganda, and Tanzania [11, 13, 20–23, 30]. A key difference between our study and these others is the setting; while our research was conducted in primary and secondary healthcare facilities in a rural area, the other studies were based in tertiary healthcare facilities in urban settings. This difference in context may account for the variation in findings, underscoring the importance of setting-specific approaches when examining HBV prevalence.

Malawi has one of the lowest healthcare provider-to-population ratios globally, with just 0.019 physicians and 0.283 nursing and midwifery personnel per 1,000 people, significantly below the WHO-recommended ratio [15]. This shortage has led to increased task shifting, with support staff often performing duties typically reserved for trained clinicians [31–33]. While clinical and nursing students are vaccinated against hepatitis B (HBV) before starting their clinical rotations, non-frontline healthcare workers do not receive the same protection.

Malawi’s Extended Program on Immunization has incorporated the hepatitis B vaccine into the pentavalent vaccine, which also protects against diphtheria, pertussis, tetanus, and Haemophilus influenzae type b [34]. While this initiative has significantly enhanced immunization coverage for children, older generations remain at greater risk because they were not vaccinated in childhood. Consequently, many adults, particularly the elderly, are more vulnerable to hepatitis B infection due to the lack of early vaccination. In this study, none of our participants benefited from this updated immunization schedule.

Limitations.

Despite our study’s large sample size, the small number of HBV-positive cases limits the reliability of subgroup analyses, potentially reducing statistical power and making it challenging to identify significant trends or associations with demographic or clinical factors. This constraint affects the study’s ability to represent HBV prevalence accurately across different subgroups. Consequently, caution is necessary when interpreting these findings, and additional studies with larger numbers of positive cases are recommended to validate and expand upon our results.

Resource limitations also impacted our study. We could not perform specific serological tests, such as anti-HBs and anti-HBc assays, which are essential for distinguishing between acute and chronic HBV infections and assessing immunity. This limitation hindered our ability to evaluate natural immunity or resolved infections and compromised insights into infection resolution rates. Additionally, since HBV DNA testing was not feasible, our assessments relied on clinical evaluations and basic lab tests (FBC and LFTs), which may lack precision due to external influencing factors. Finally, vaccinating individuals who tested positive for HBV without accounting for the window period—the interval between infection and detectable levels—might have impacted outcomes, as individuals vaccinated during this period may not have benefited fully [35]. These limitations highlight the need for improved diagnostic resources and comprehensive testing in future research to optimize HBV management strategies in similar settings.

Conclusion

HBV infection among HCWs and support staff in Salima District is a significant public health issue, particularly affecting lower-cadre workers like environmental health personnel. While no significant links between HBV positivity and socio-demographic factors were found, the overall vulnerability of HCWs underscores the need for universal prevention measures. Prompt linkage to care for those testing positive has shown benefits in mitigating disease progression, yet the lack of comprehensive data on HBV prevalence among HCWs limits targeted prevention strategies. To meet the WHO’s 2030 elimination goal, the Malawi Ministry of Health must enhance screening and vaccination efforts, particularly for new recruits, integrate HBV vaccination into recruitment processes, and ensure access to personal protective equipment (PPE) for all staff. Additionally, further research is needed to explore patient-HCW transmission dynamics, providing localized data to inform national policies and improve interventions aimed at reducing transmission within healthcare settings and communities.
