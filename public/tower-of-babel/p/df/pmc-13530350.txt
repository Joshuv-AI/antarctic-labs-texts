How does the choice of food waste collection strategy affect the environmental performance of the food waste system?
Authors: Kari-Anne Lyng, Fabiana Frota de Albuquerque Landi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Separating food waste supports a more circular economy. As the most visible part of the value chain, the collection system plays a key role in waste management performance. This study quantifies the environmental impacts of various food waste collection methods using life cycle assessment, focusing on climate change and potential trade-offs across other impact categories. Two different treatment alternatives were explored: composting and anaerobic digestion (AD). The functional unit was defined as the management of the average amount of food waste generated per person during a year in Norway. The results show that the collection strategy affects both sorting rate and environmental performance. Climate change impacts range from −9.7 to −7.5 kg CO2 eq./person/year for AD and between −6.3 and 2.5 kg CO2 eq./person/year for composting. Collection in paper bags in separate containers results in the lowest climate change impact for both treatment pathways, but may increase other environmental impacts. For the AD pathway, there are relatively small differences between the results for the studied collection options. The use of paper bags, however, is likely to be the only option that eliminates the risk of plastic pollution in the soil from bags. The study identifies a potential “separate container effect”: a higher separation rate is achieved with separate containers compared to the optical bag sorting system. This emphasizes how crucial the choice of collection method is for effective food waste management.

Introduction

The European Union’s Circular Economy Action Plan (European Commission, 2020) significantly elevates the goals for municipal waste separation and recycling across Europe. In Norway, separate food waste collection became mandatory in January 2023, with a target of 70% separation by 2035. The national separation rate has been estimated to 47% (Syversen et al., 2018), highlighting a need for system enhancements.

Waste collection systems are complex, and a large variety of options exists. There are different waste collection bags (paper, biodegradable plastic, and conventional plastic) and container systems: separate containers for each waste type or centralized sorting systems, that is, optical bag sorting where the waste types are collected in coloured bags within a shared container and transported in the same vehicle, and subsequently separated using near-infrared technology. There is a need to increase knowledge about how different collection systems affect the overall environmental burdens and benefits of the food waste system.

Previous studies focusing on individual system components indicate that the waste collection strategy, including waste bags, container systems, and transportation, has an impact on the overall environmental performance of the waste system. Some studies show that availability, convenience, and existing infrastructure can impact the separation rate (Bernstad, 2014; Flygansvær et al., 2021). Sailer et al. (2022) found that storage duration and temperature affect the energy content of the food waste. Other studies indicate that increased separation may result in longer driving distances and more collection routes, thereby amplifying the environmental burdens associated with the collection strategy (Zbib and Wøhlk, 2019).

Observations from Norwegian municipalities have led to the hypothesis that separate containers for food waste could lead to higher sorting rates than shared containers with optical sorting. This “separate container effect” has been documented for plastic packaging in Norway (Hebrok et al., 2021), but not yet for food waste. There is a need to test and verify this hypothesis, and to explore how this potentially affects the environmental impacts.

Life cycle assessment (LCA) is widely used to assess the environmental impacts of waste collection systems (Laurent et al., 2014a, 2014b). Previous LCA studies have assessed how elements of the collection system affect environmental impacts. Dolci et al. (2021b) concluded that the bag type influences the environmental performance of the entire food waste management system. Some studies have found that the material of the waste collection bag can affect the properties of the food waste, such as reduced weight due to evaporation and reduced biogas potential (Dolci et al., 2021a; Påledal et al., 2018). Saibuatrong et al. (2017) compared the production of three waste bag alternatives, including conventional polyethylene (PE) plastic, bio-PE (bioethanol-based), and polybutylene adipate terephthalate (PBAT)/starch bags. Pérez et al. (2017) assessed different container systems and underlined the importance of container type and capacity.

Each of these studies contributes with valuable knowledge about decisive aspects regarding waste collection. To the authors’ knowledge, however, none of the studies has combined all the mentioned collection strategies for food waste and considered how different collection strategies can affect sorting rates and, consequently, the effectiveness of the recycling system.

This article aims to increase knowledge about how the collection strategy affects the environmental performance of food waste management, which collection strategies result in the lowest environmental impacts, and which factors affect the results. The intended audience is policymakers, municipalities, and waste management operators, as well as LCA practitioners assessing waste management and collection systems.

Materials and methods

The LCA was done in SimaPro 9.5 (PRé Sustainability, 2025). The ecoinvent database 3.9.1 cut-off by classification (Wernet et al., 2016) was applied to the background data. The following sections present the study according to the required (ISO, 2006) steps: goal and scope definition, life cycle inventory, and impact assessment. As the data used for the case study is based on Norwegian conditions, the results from the study may not be valid for other geographical scopes.

Goal and scope

Functional unit

The functional unit was defined as “management of the average amount of food waste generated per person during a year in Norway,” that is, 78 kg/person/year (Danielsen et al., 2021).

Scenarios’ description

The life cycle stages were defined as: waste collection bags and containers (including production), transport in waste collection vehicles, optical bag sorting (if relevant), pre-treatment (if relevant), treatment (incineration for non-separated food waste and composting or anaerobic digestion (AD) for separated food waste), use of secondary products (compost, digestate, biogas), and avoided emissions when secondary products substitute other products or energy carriers.

For composting, the removal is integrated in the composting process and involves sieving after the composting process. Life cycle inventory analysis

The main assumptions are described in the following sections, whereas the inventory data are described in detail in the Supplemental Material.

Household sorting rate

To test and explore the “separate container effect” hypothesis, that is, that there is a correlation between sorting system and sorting rate, waste composition data from Norwegian municipalities covering 45% of the Norwegian population were collected and categorized, calculating separated waste per person relative to average annual food waste generation.

The calculated average sorting rate was 73% for households with separate food waste containers (minimum 47% and maximum 85%) and 41% for households with coloured bags in shared containers (minimum 37% and maximum 53%). The minimum and maximum values were used to test the robustness of the results in the sensitivity assessments in section “Sensitivity analysis.”

No evidence for differences in sorting rates was found for different bag types; however, the bag choice may influence other factors. The weight of food waste can be reduced during collection due to water evaporation (Dolci et al., 2021a; Påledal et al., 2018). The weight loss is dependent on temperature, suggesting that data from Italy may not be relevant for Norway due to climatic differences. Weight reductions were also documented in a Swedish study (Påledal et al., 2018), who observed that storage of food waste in both paper and plastic bags at a temperature of 6°C resulted in little degradation, whereas storage at 22°C showed clear indications of degradation (Dolci et al., 2021a, 2021b).

Bags and containers

The paper bag is assumed to consist of kraft paper. The biodegradable plastic bag is assumed to be produced from the Materi-Bi® polymer, which, according to Dolci et al. (2021a) consists of 70% PBAT, 20% starch, and 10% additives. The additives are assumed to be pigments, of which 50% are titanium dioxide and 50% zinc sulphide, based on Dolci et al. (2021b).

The environmental impacts of production and waste treatment of grocery shopping bags, which are reused to collect residual waste, were not included, as the impacts were allocated to the shopping activity, 1 and were defined outside the system’s boundaries of this study.

The size of the residual waste container and shared container is assumed to be 240 L, consisting of 14.2 kg high-density polyethylene (HDPE). Separate containers for food waste are assumed to be 140-L HDPE containers weighing 10.6 kg. The service life of containers is assumed to be 10 years based on information from Norwegian municipalities. Chiew et al. (2015) assumed a service life of 20 years, which indicates that 10 years may be a conservative assumption.

Waste collection

Environmental impacts from waste collection include the production and use of the fuel consumed by the collection vehicles, in addition to infrastructure (production and maintenance of roads, vehicles, etc.). Fuel consumption was based on data collected from the waste company SIMAS, operating in rural Western Norway. Their total driven transport distance in 2021 was 226,116 km, and the amount of waste collected was 7006 tonnes, resulting in an average diesel consumption of 15.2 L/tonne of waste. This is outside the range found by Larsen et al. (2009) between 1.4 and 10.1 L diesel per tonne of waste collected, depending on the collection scheme. The high consumption reflects that this is a rural area with long transport distances and small amounts of waste. SIMAS has six waste collection vehicles, with an estimated service life of 10 years.

In the separate-container alternatives (FW1a–c), a 2-week collection frequency was assumed, with food waste and residual waste collected alternately by a single-chamber waste collection vehicle. In the FW2 scenario, coloured bags were collected weekly using the same vehicle type. Consequently, all scenarios assume identical vehicle travel distances and fuel consumption.

In cases where the same collection vehicle cannot be used for both residual and food waste, or if weekly food waste collection is required, the resulting transport demand would increase substantially (twice as high). This scenario was included in the sensitivity analysis (see the transport increase scenarios in section “Sensitivity analysis”).

Bag sorting and pre-treatment

Bag breakage can occur in the optical bag sorting facility (FW2). This can be caused by excessive compression in the waste collection vehicle, or that bags are not sufficiently tied by the households. According to Syversen and Schefte (2007), the sorting rate in Norwegian optical bag sorting facilities lies between 85% and 95%. As a basis, 90% efficiency was assumed in this study. Maximum and minimum values were tested in the section “Sensitivity analysis.” Energy consumption was assumed to be 12.7 kWh/tonnes of waste entering the sorting facility (Callewaert et al., 2022).

When food waste is sent to AD, pre-treatment is required to open and remove plastic bags and missortings to avoid sizeable objects in the biogas reactor and unwanted material in the digestate product. The amount of reject was assumed to be 20% of the input material based on Lyng and Saxegård (2020). Unlike the other bag types, the paper bags will dissolve in the pre-treatment process and follow the organic waste stream into the reactor. Pre-treatment may still be required to remove missortings, but the reduced need for bag removal may reduce the loss of organic material. No scientific studies examining how different bag types affect the loss of organic material in pre-treatment were found. An assumption of a reduction to 10% reject was tested in the sensitivity assessment.

When food waste is sent to composting, the use of paper and biodegradable bags (FW1a and b) does not require pre-treatment, as the bags will decompose during the composting process. The compost is sieved to remove unwanted objects before it is sent to the user.

The use of plastic bags (FW1c and FW2) requires pre-treatment for both treatment alternatives to open the bag and to avoid plastic in the compost. The pre-treatment process before composting was modelled in the same manner as pre-treatment before AD.

The non-separated food waste and the losses in the value chain were assumed to be sent to energy recovery in an incineration plant with fly ash extraction. The energy generated was assumed to substitute the Norwegian district heating mix based on the lower heating value of each material. The incineration plant was assumed to have an efficiency coefficient of 85%, and a 75% substitution rate.

Anaerobic digestion (biogas production)

AD was modelled using data from the operation of a Norwegian biogas plant (Lyng et al., 2021). This biogas plant is a typical example of the Nordic biogas model (Lindfors et al., 2022) where the waste resources are used to produce biomethane for transport, and the digestate is used as biofertilizer. The biogas was assumed to substitute diesel. The digestate was assumed to be used as biofertilizer and to substitute mineral fertilizer.

Composting

Data regarding production volumes and the use of energy and machinery during composting were collected from the Norwegian composting plant operated by SIMAS. As no data for direct emissions from the composting process was available, the ecoinvent data (Wernet et al., 2016) was used. Emissions from composting paper and biodegradable plastic bags were modelled based on data from Hermann et al. (2011), assuming that 57% of the carbon in the material is emitted as CO2 and 0.11% is emitted as CH4. As the biodegradable plastic bags consist of a combination of fossil-based and bio-based plastic (see Supplemental Table 2), the same split was used when modelling fossil and biogenic CO2 and methane emissions.

Carbon storage effect from the biogenic carbon present in the compost was estimated based on the approach of Hermann et al. (2011): from the carbon originally stored in mature compost, it was assumed that 51% contribute to humus formation. Part of the humus is mineralized, and the humus degradation rate was assumed to be 0.8% per year, which means that 23% of the humus from that carbon is still present in the soil after 100 years.

The compost is used as soil improvement in gardens and parks, and was assumed to substitute peat based on volume. The nutrients in the compost were assumed to reduce the use of mineral fertilizer, based on data from Boldrin et al. (2010).

Impact assessment

As the intended audience of this study (policy makers, municipalities, and waste management operators) have a large focus on climate impacts in their decision making, the emphasis of the analysis has been on the environmental impact category climate change (100 years). 2 Potential trade-offs between other environmental impact categories were assessed to identify and mitigate the risk of problem-shifting that may arise from prioritizing solutions solely based on climate change. The impact assessment method applied was Environmental Footprint 3.1 (Zampori and Pant, 2019) as adapted in SimaPro 9.5 (PRé Sustainability 2025). The Environmental Footprint method contains 16 environmental impact categories. As a first step, a screening of the most relevant environmental impact categories was performed by analysing the single score results when applying weighting factors from Zampori and Pant (2019). For composting, the five categories with the largest impacts were identified to be: climate change, particulate matter, human toxicity (cancer), eutrophication (terrestrial and freshwater), and resource use (fossil). For AD, the most relevant impact categories identified were climate change, particulate matter, acidification, eutrophication (freshwater and terrestrial), and resource use (fossil). As a result, these indicators were further analysed in detail.

Results

Food waste separated for anaerobic digestion

Potential impacts on climate change

The potential impacts on climate change for the four collection strategies when the food waste is sent to AD are shown in Figure 2. The values above zero represent the climate burdens from the collection and treatment, whereas the negative emissions are the avoided impacts associated with substitution. The net results are shown as a red dot.

Potential impacts on climate change for food waste separated for anaerobic digestion. The functional unit is the treatment of the average amount of food waste generated per person per year in Norway (78 kg).

Depending on the collection strategy, the net result from the collection and treatment of food waste ranges from −9.7 to −7.5 kg CO2 eq./person/year. This shows that the differences between the scenarios are relatively small and that the ranking is largely dependent on underlying assumptions. The largest burdens are from transport with waste collection vehicles. The avoided emissions – occurring when biogas substitutes diesel and digestate substitutes mineral fertilizer – are of large importance.

Collection of food waste in paper bags (FW1a) in a separate container results in slightly lower impacts. In all other alternatives, the bag is removed during pre-treatment and sent to incineration. As these bags consist of fossil-based material, the combustion creates greenhouse gas emissions. In addition, the impacts from the production of paper bags are lower than those of the other alternatives. Under the given assumptions, optical bag sorting (FW2) is the least beneficial option due to losses in the bag sorting facility and lower household sorting rates compared with a separate container. The losses lead to lower production of biogas and digestate, resulting in less substitution of diesel and mineral fertilizer.

Potential trade-offs between climate change and other environmental impact categories

The results for the selected environmental impact categories (see section “Impact assessment”) are shown in Figure 3. As the impact categories have different units, the results are shown relative to the most beneficial option in terms of climate change (FW1a) in percentage.

Results for food waste sent to anaerobic digestion, relative to the FW1a scenario.

Given the stated assumptions, the collection of food waste in paper bags (FW1a) has the lowest impact when considering the use of fossil resources, in addition to climate change. Conversely, it has the highest impact among the alternatives in the category of particulate matter. This is related to the avoided emissions of particles when substituting district heating mix from energy recovery, and specifically the use of natural gas for district heating. As incineration of plastics in the reject in the other alternatives increases the heating value and thus the amount of energy substituted, more energy from waste incineration is delivered, and the use of natural gas decreases. Improvements in certain impact categories (e.g., particulate matter) are partly driven by increased incineration, which may conflict with circular economy objectives.

Optical bag sorting (FW2), which had the highest impacts on climate change, performs better than the alternatives when considering terrestrial eutrophication, acidification, and particulate matter. Although the reduction in particle emissions is related to increased incineration, increased use of digestate as fertilizer can potentially lead to higher impacts from acidification and terrestrial eutrophication. It can also be noted that the collection with biodegradable and conventional plastic in a separate container performs similarly in all impact categories.

In all other impact categories, the use of paper bags is the option with the highest impacts, but performs equally to some of the other alternatives. The difference between the collection alternatives with separate containers (FW1a–c) is less than 2% for the acidification and terrestrial eutrophication impact categories.

Food waste separated for composting

Potential impacts on climate change

The climate change impacts for the four collection strategies when the separated food waste is sent to composting are shown in Figure 4. The results range from −6.3 to 2.5 kg CO2 eq./person/year.

Potential impacts on climate change for food waste separated for composting. The functional unit is the treatment of the average amount of food waste generated per person per year in Norway (78 kg).

With the given assumptions, the collection with paper bags in a separate container (FW1a) results in the lowest impacts on climate change, followed by collection with biodegradable plastic bags in a separate container (FW1b). Substituting peat with compost contributes to considerable benefits relative to the burdens associated with the collection and treatment of the waste. The largest avoided burdens are from potential methane emissions from land after removing peat, followed by mineralization of peat after use. The production of the bags and containers has relatively low impacts. The use of plastic bags to collect food waste in separate containers (FW1c) is less beneficial than paper bags and biodegradable plastic bags, due to emissions from pre-treatment as a consequence of sending the bags to incineration. The use of optical bag sorting systems, where the separated food waste is collected in coloured plastic bags (FW2), has the largest impact of all the alternatives. This is due to the lower household collection rates obtained and the loss caused by bag breakage. This results in less production of compost and consequently less substitution of peat, as well as emissions from incineration of the plastic bags.

Potential trade-offs between climate change and other environmental impact categories

When considering the ranking between the alternatives, large differences can be observed across the included environmental impact categories (Figure 5). The results are shown relative to the most beneficial option in terms of climate change (FW1a), in percentage.

Results for collection strategies sent to composting, relative to FW1a paper.

Although the collection of food waste in paper bags in a separate container (FW1a) obtains the lowest impacts with regard to climate change and use of fossil resources, paper and biodegradable bags (FW1a and b) obtain similar results or larger impacts on particulate matter, terrestrial eutrophication, and freshwater ecotoxicity. This is because these two options result in the highest production of compost, which can lead to increased impact on terrestrial eutrophication and freshwater ecotoxicity, and the lowest amount of heat from incineration of waste, which substitutes natural gas in the district heating mix and reduces the impact on particulate matter. For the same reasons, optical bag sorting (FW2) performs better than the alternatives in these categories.

Sensitivity analysis

The following aspects were tested in the sensitivity assessment: (1) minimum and maximum household sorting rates (HH sort), (2) minimum and maximum sorting performance in optical bag sorting in scenario FW2 (bag sort), (3) weight loss due to evaporation (10.2% for bioplastic bags and 12.9% for paper bags; Dolci et al., 2021a, 2021b) resulting in lower impacts from transport, (4) risk of methane leakage resulting in emissions and reduced production of biogas, (5) reduced reject (from 20% to 10% of input material) when using paper bags, and (6) transport increase if a weekly collection frequency is required for food waste (FW1a–c transport increase).

Figure 6 shows that for composting, using a separate container outperforms optical sorting with coloured bags, as FW2 consistently shows the highest impacts. For AD, the results are more complex: results are heavily influenced by household sorting rates, which can change the ranking between separate and shared containers. Separate container alternatives (FW1a–c) and high sorting rates (HH sort max) result in the lowest impacts on climate change, whereas minimum household sorting rates (HH sort min) lead to the least beneficial results.

Results from sensitivity assessment, results ranked from lowest to highest impacts on climate change for the two treatment methods.

HH: household; Trp: transport.

If the use of paper bags can be assumed to reduce the loss of organic material in the pre-treatment process, the results for FW1a are reduced from −9.7 to −12.0 kg CO2 eq./person/year when food waste is sent to AD. If, however, the use of paper bags results in increased methane loss compared with the other alternatives, their results are less beneficial. Weight loss due to evaporation can lead to decreased impact from transport, but not sufficient to compensate for the methane loss.

If the use of a separate container leads to increased transport needs, collection with paper bags in a separate container (FW1a) becomes equal to using coloured bags for optical sorting (FW2). Variations in losses in the optical bag sorting facility affect the results, but do not lead to as significant changes as the household sorting rates.

Discussion

Paper bag

The collection of food waste in paper bags in separate container appears to be the most beneficial option in terms of potential impacts on climate change regardless of waste treatment method. The difference between the collection options is, however, relatively small when the food waste is treated by AD and larger when the food waste is treated by composting. The difference between the minimum and maximum results is 2.3 and 8.7 kg CO2-eq./person/year for food waste treated by AD and composting, respectively. The better performance of paper bags with respect to biodegradable plastics is in line with a previous study, which found that paper had better performance than bioplastic, but that the differences in results were dependent on the LCA modelling approach (Dolci et al., 2021b).

Biodegradable plastic bags

The results from the LCA presented in this study show that paper bags obtain slightly better results than the biodegradable plastic bags in all environmental impact categories assessed. This can mainly be explained by the higher impacts from the production of the biodegradable bags. For some of the categories, the difference was less than 5%: particulate matter, terrestrial eutrophication, and freshwater ecotoxicity.

The climate change results when the separated food waste is treated by AD, show only marginal benefits of using biodegradable plastic compared with conventional plastic. This is because the biodegradable plastic was assumed to be removed in the pre-treatment process and sent to incineration. As the biodegradable plastic bag is partly produced from fossil resources, the combustion results in greenhouse gas emissions.

Plastic pollution in digestate and compost is a concern, as macroplastics can cause problems for animal life (Bucci et al., 2020). In addition, there is little knowledge about how microplastics can affect the ecosystems in the soil (Ding et al., 2022). Biodegradable plastics have been suggested as a possible solution to reduce plastic pollution.

Since biodegradable plastic bags are assumed to be removed during pre-treatment before AD, their degradability in AD does not impact the LCA results in this study. Still, there is a potential risk of plastic pollution when applying the digestate to soil that is not captured by the results.

The pre-treatment will remove most of the plastic, but small amounts can end up in the digester. Biodegradable plastic does not necessarily degrade in the AD process. For example, Bandini et al. (2020) found that starch-based bioplastic bags had a 85.79% of disintegration degree in AD. In another study done by Dolci et al. (2022), the results from biochemical methane potential tests indicated good degradability of bioplastic bags (71–93%); however, under semi-continuous co-digestion conditions, the degradability was significantly lower, confirming the importance of tests simulating realistic operating conditions.

Parts of the bags that are not removed during pre-treatment and not degraded in the AD process will end up in digestate, and subsequently end up in soil. The degradation rate is dependent on local conditions, and trials of biodegradable agricultural mulch film in Norway showed formation of biodegradable microplastics and found that accumulation is likely to happen in cold climatic regions when the biodegradable plastic is repeatedly added to the soil (Coutris et al., 2025). This suggests that, although the use of biodegradable plastic is likely to reduce the risk of plastic pollution compared with conventional plastic bags, only paper bags eliminate the risk of plastic pollution from collection bags.

Plastic bag

Plastic bag in separate container has the largest impacts on climate change among the separate container alternatives. In the other environmental impact categories, it performed better than biodegradable plastic bags. Conventional plastic poses the highest risk of pollution in compost and digestate. Although not covered by the EU single-use product (SUP) directive (European Commission, 2019), collection bags meet the definition of an SUP as they are used once before being discarded. Thus, using plastic bags for food waste collection may not be advisable.

The “separate container effect”

The background data collected in this study from waste composition analysis does indeed show an empirical observation of a correlation between the collection system and the sorting rate. This largely confirms the presence of a “separate container effect” that leads households with separate containers to achieve higher sorting rates. More extensive statistical analyses in future studies are recommended to draw a robust conclusion.

Although the data collection and assessment in this study indicate a correlation, the results do not provide any insight into causality. One possible explanation of the “separate container effect” is that the urban areas tend to offer shared containers, due to space constraints, and that inhabitants in urban areas tend to obtain lower sorting rates. Alternatively, the effect may reflect that users interact directly with the container, and the container system influences sorting behaviour.

Below, two possible theories for the lower sorting rates are proposed. Firstly, the individual consumers’ sorting behaviour becomes less noticeable when several waste types are thrown in the same bin. The visibility of individual actions can affect sorting behaviour (Bucciol et al., 2019). Secondly, throwing all waste types in the same bin and a subsequent sorting system that the consumer has limited knowledge about represents a risk of lower confidence that the sorted waste will go to proper treatment. The level of trust that waste is effectively recycled is also a factor that can affect sorting behaviour (Minelgaitė and Liobikienė, 2019). These aspects should be subject to further research.

The optical bag sorting system is seen as a way of reducing the number of containers and optimising transport. In the basis scenario in this study, it was assumed a weekly collection of coloured bags in shared containers. A collection system with alternating bi-weekly collection of food waste and residual waste in separate containers can be assumed to result in the same transport work. If, however, a weekly collection of food waste is required, the transport work will increase. Results indicate that separate containers still are the best solution, especially if food waste is sent to composting.

Sensitivity

The sensitivity assessment shows that household sorting rate largely affects the results and can change the ranking between separate and shared container. Separate container alternatives (FW1a–c) and high sorting rates result in the lowest impacts on climate change, whereas minimum household sorting rates (HH sort min) lead to the least beneficial results.

If the use of a separate container leads to increased transport needs, collection with paper bags in separate containers (FW1a) becomes equal to using coloured bags for optical sorting (FW2). Variations in losses in the optical bag sorting facility affect the results, but do not lead to as significant changes as the household sorting rates.

Limitations

The sensitivity assessment showed that, if the use of paper bags increase risk of methane emissions, it can potentially represent the worst scenario. Påledal et al. (2018) found that organic waste collected and stored at 22°C for 7 days had 10% higher biogas yield when collected in plastic bags than in paper bags. If the bags were stored for 21 days at 22°C, the waste collected in plastic bags obtained 60% higher biogas yield. This shows that the findings in this study have conditional applicability, as there is a risk of methane loss, affected by frequency and temperature, making the result dependent on both the collection system and climate. Consequently, decision-makers should interpret the results with caution: the climate benefits of paper bags observed here may not fully apply under conditions where organic waste is stored for longer periods or at higher temperatures. For collection using biodegradable bags, no studies were found that have investigated potential methane emissions. This highlights the need for more research on methane loss during waste collection, particularly in warmer climates. Although paper bags may reduce climate impacts, they can increase other environmental burdens, largely due to higher collection rates in separate containers. Methods for assessing plastic littering are still under development and were only addressed qualitatively in this study.

Conclusion

This study has shown that the choice of collection strategy can affect the overall environmental impacts of the waste management system. Especially important is the impact that the collection systems may have on the household sorting rates and other losses in the value chain. The hypothesis that a “separate container effect” positively affects household sorting behaviour should be explored in future research.

If the main purpose is to reduce climate change impact, the utilisation of paper bags appears to be the most beneficial option when assuming average household separation rates for the collection systems. It should be noted, however, that the conclusion is highly dependent on geographical context (transport distances) and underlying assumptions, as demonstrated by the sensitivity analysis. In addition, the collection of food waste in paper bags is the only alternative that eliminates the risk of plastic pollution of the soil when the food waste is sent to AD.

The choice of using paper bags can, however, lead to some trade-offs. Further research should look more into how to minimize methane losses during organic waste collection and how the choice of bags affects the losses of organic material in the pre-treatment.
