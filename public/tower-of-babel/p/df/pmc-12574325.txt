The Triple Hormone Enigma: Pituitary Macroadenoma With Bilateral Cortisol-Androgen Secreting Adenomas
Authors: Bhanu Malhotra, Vivek Jha, Amal Shaharyar, Md Sadam Hussain, Sanja Medenica, Pinaki Dutta
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe coexistence of a pituitary macroadenoma co-secreting growth hormone (GH) and adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) with bilateral adrenal adenomas autonomously producing cortisol and androgens represents an extremely rare and diagnostically complex clinical entity. We describe a 54-year-old woman who presented with acral enlargement, hirsutism, hyperpigmentation, and poorly controlled diabetes and hypertension. Hormonal evaluation revealed concurrent elevations in GH, ACTH, cortisol, dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate, and testosterone. Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging of the brain showed a pituitary macroadenoma, and abdominal imaging revealed bilateral adrenal masses. Despite undergoing transsphenoidal surgery and subsequent radiosurgery, the patient exhibited persistent hypercortisolism and androgen excess. Bilateral adrenalectomy was performed, and histopathology confirmed benign adrenocortical adenomas. The patient subsequently achieved biochemical remission and marked clinical improvement. This case highlights the need for comprehensive hormonal and imaging workup in patients with mixed clinical features and emphasizes the importance of a multidisciplinary approach to diagnosis and management in the context of multiple endocrine pathologies.

Introduction

Plurihormonal pituitary adenomas (PHAs) are an exceptionally rare group of tumors that secrete 2 or more anterior pituitary hormones, and they are classified into 2 main types: monomorphous adenomas, where a single cell type produces multiple hormones, and plurimorphous (or pleomorphic) adenomas, where distinct cell populations secrete different hormones [1]. These adenomas often present with a dominant hormonal syndrome—commonly acromegaly—while other hormone excess such as adrenocorticotropic hormone (ACTH) may be subclinical or masked [2, 3].

This dual secretory profile poses significant diagnostic challenges, as dynamic hormonal testing and imaging may yield ambiguous results. Aberrant transcription factor expression may underlie trans-lineage differentiation in these tumors, contributing to hormone diversification [4]. When coupled with bilateral adrenal adenomas autonomously producing cortisol and androgens, the clinical picture becomes even more complex [5, 6].

Our case, featuring a growth hormone (GH)–ACTH co-secreting macroadenoma alongside bilateral functioning adrenal adenomas exemplifies this complexity and emphasizes the importance of a structured, multidisciplinary diagnostic approach. The integration of dynamic suppression testing, high-resolution imaging, immunohistochemistry, and genetic analysis is essential to guide management and optimize long-term follow-up.

Case Presentation

A 54-year-old woman presented with complaints of persistent, dull, holocranial headache, generalized skin darkening, excessive sweating, and progressive hirsutism for the past 3 years. She also reported features of virilization for 6 months, characterized by temporal balding, deepening of voice, clitoromegaly, and breast atrophy.

She had been diagnosed with type 2 diabetes mellitus 10 years earlier, with persistently poor glycemic control despite multiple oral hypoglycemic agents. She had been initiated on insulin therapy 6 months prior to presentation due to worsening glycemia (glycated hemoglobin [HbA1c] 13.6% [SI: 125 mmol/mol]) (reference range, 4.0%-5.6% [SI: 20-38 mmol/mol]). She also had a 5-year history of hypertension that remained inadequately controlled despite dual antihypertensive therapy, including a calcium channel blocker and an angiotensin receptor blocker.

She had secondary amenorrhea for 10 years without menopausal symptoms or polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) history. There were no signs of galactorrhea, visual changes, weight loss, hyperthyroidism, or overt Cushing syndrome.

She had no history of steroid, herbal, or anabolic use; no renal/gallstones, fractures, or prior surgeries. Family history was negative for endocrine disorders. Medications included metformin, insulin, amlodipine, and losartan.

Diagnostic Assessment

On examination, she weighed 80 kg with a height of 175 cm (body mass index [BMI] 26.1 kg/m²) (reference range, 18.5-24.9 kg/m²). Notable findings included multiple skin tags, grade 3 acanthosis nigricans, diffuse hyperpigmentation, acneiform facial lesions, and a Ferriman-Gallwey score of 20/36, indicating significant hirsutism (Fig. 1). She had clitoromegaly (clitoral index: 308 mm²; reference range, < 35 mm²), acral overgrowth, and a deep, resonant voice. The abdomen was soft and protuberant. No signs of cushingoid features, goiter, or visual field defects were noted.

Evaluation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis demonstrated elevated midnight cortisol of 19.1 µg/dL (SI: 528 nmol/L) (reference range, < 7.5 µg/dL [SI: < 207 nmol/L]), confirming loss of circadian rhythm. Cortisol failed to suppress following overnight dexamethasone suppression test (ONDST; dexamethasone 1 mg orally at 23:00, cortisol 08:00: 9.1 µg/dL [SI: 252 nmol/L]; reference range, <1.8 µg/dL [SI: < 50 nmol/L]) and low-dose dexamethasone suppression test (LDDST; dexamethasone 0.5 mg orally every 6 hours for 48 hours, cortisol: 10.8 µg/dL [SI: 298 nmol/L]; reference range, <1.8 µg/dL [SI: < 50 nmol/L]). High-dose dexamethasone suppression test (HDDST; dexamethasone 2 mg orally every 6 hours for 48 hours) showed > 50% suppression with cortisol falling to 4.9 µg/dL (SI: 135 nmol/L), consistent with Cushing disease. Plasma ACTH was 35 pg/mL (SI: 7.7 pmol/L) (reference range, 5-60 pg/mL [SI: 1.1-13.2 pmol/L]), which was not suppressed in the setting of hypercortisolism, consistent with ACTH-dependent disease. She had biochemical evidence of androgen excess, with serum dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS) 996 µg/dL (SI: 27 µmol/L) (reference range, 44-331 µg/dL [SI: 1.2-9.1 µmol/L]) and total testosterone was 78.1 ng/dL (SI: 2.71 nmol/L) (reference range, 14.4-77.9 ng/dL [SI: 0.5-2.7 nmol/L]). Hormonal evaluation showed low gonadotropins with normal estradiol, likely secondary to hypercortisolism and androgen excess, rather than true central hypogonadism. Thyroid function was consistent with central hypothyroidism, while prolactin remained normal (Table 1).

Contrast-enhanced magnetic resonance imaging (CEMRI) of the sella revealed a 1.7 × 1.8 × 1.7 cm pituitary macroadenoma with left cavernous sinus invasion, consistent with Knosp grade 4 (Fig. 2A-2C). Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) of the abdomen demonstrated bilateral adrenal masses (Fig. 2D-2F): a right adrenal lesion measuring 2.4 × 1.6 × 1.1 cm with low unenhanced attenuation (10 Hounsfield units [HU]) and an absolute washout of 54%, consistent with a lipid-rich adenoma; and a larger left adrenal mass measuring 5.7 × 4.4 × 5.8 cm with higher unenhanced attenuation (52 HU) and absolute washout of 53%, suggestive of a lipid-poor adenoma. The adnexa were unremarkable.

Given the biochemical and radiological findings, bilateral adrenal lesions were provisionally considered as potentially functional. However, as ACTH was not suppressed at this stage, adrenal autonomy could not be established, and the possibility of pituitary-driven hypercortisolism with secondary adrenal enlargement was also considered. Subsequent dynamic testing and follow-up demonstrated persistent androgen excess with suppressed ACTH, confirming true adrenal autonomy. Differential diagnoses for androgen excess included ovarian hyperthecosis, virilizing ovarian neoplasm, and ACTH-dependent causes such as Cushing disease and non-classical congenital adrenal hyperplasia (NCCAH). ACTH-stimulated 17-hydroxyprogesterone and 11-deoxycorticosterone acetate were within normal limits, excluding NCCAH. A 4-day dexamethasone androgen suppression test (0.5 mg every 6 hours for 96 hours) confirmed persistent androgen excess with inadequate cortisol suppression (Table 2). The lack of androgen suppression despite partial cortisol reduction supported adrenal androgen autonomy.

As part of systemic evaluation, a transthoracic echocardiogram was performed to exclude cardiac myxoma, given the differential of Carney complex in the setting of bilateral adrenal disease. The study was normal, with no evidence of myxoma or other cardiac abnormalities.

Treatment

She underwent endoscopic transsphenoidal surgery (TSS) in November 2019. Histopathological examination of the resected pituitary tumor (Fig. 3A and 3B) revealed a Pit-1 positive adenoma with GH expression in approximately 90% of cells, ACTH expression in 10% of cells, and a low proliferative index (Ki-67 < 1%). T-pit immunostaining was negative, confirming absence of corticotroph lineage despite focal ACTH positivity.

Despite surgery, biochemical remission was not achieved, and imaging revealed a right parasellar residual lesion measuring 8 × 6 × 13 mm (Fig. 4). Consequently, she underwent Gamma Knife radiosurgery in January 2021 (dose: 20 Gy), and monthly long-acting octreotide (20 mg) was initiated to manage persistent GH excess.

Following TSS, hyperpigmentation and hirsutism persisted, though glycemic control and headaches improved. ACTH decreased to 1.5 pg/mL (SI: 0.33 pmol/L); however, hypercortisolism persisted, with nonsuppressible ONDST 7.5 µg/dL (SI: 208 nmol/L) and LDDST 9.3 µg/dL (SI: 258 nmol/L) on repeated testing. DHEAS and testosterone remained elevated even 2 years postoperatively, supporting adrenal secretion. Repeat CECT abdomen demonstrated stable adrenal morphology. Accordingly, total bilateral adrenalectomy was performed 2.5 years after pituitary surgery.

The resected adrenal glands contained multiple nodules ranging from 5 to 10 mm in diameter, with none exceeding 1 cm. Histopathological examination of both adrenal glands (Fig. 3C and 3D) revealed cortical adenomas composed of lobules and nests of polygonal cells with clear to eosinophilic cytoplasm, without evidence of atypia, necrosis, or capsular/vascular invasion. The Modified Weiss score was 0 in both glands, consistent with benign functional adrenal adenomas.

Comprehensive genetic evaluation using whole exome sequencing was performed. No pathogenic variants were detected in the multiple endocrine neoplasia type 1 (MEN1) gene, commonly associated with plurihormonal pituitary adenomas. Additionally, ARMC5 (Armadillo Repeat Containing 5), frequently implicated in primary bilateral macronodular adrenal hyperplasia (PBMAH), was analyzed and found to be negative for pathogenic variants. Protein kinase cAMP-dependent type I regulatory subunit alpha (PRKAR1A) sequencing was also performed and was negative, effectively excluding Carney complex. Moreover, no other likely pathogenic variant was found.

Outcome and Follow-Up

After adrenalectomy, the patient achieved clinical and biochemical remission of cortisol and androgen excess (Fig. 5), with normalization of DHEAS 25 µg/dL (SI: 0.7 µmol/L), testosterone 12 ng/dL (SI: 0.4 nmol/L), and IGF-1 290 ng/mL (SI: 38 nmol/L). Insulin was discontinued, blood pressure stabilized, and hirsutism improved. She remains on steroid replacement and octreotide for residual acromegaly, with stable imaging and ongoing endocrine follow-up.

Hormonal trends following transsphenoidal surgery (TSS) and total bilateral adrenalectomy (TBA). Serum dehydroepiandrosterone sulfate (DHEAS) and cortisol levels showed a gradual decline after TSS, with marked suppression observed following TBA. Plasma ACTH levels decreased sharply after TSS and remained suppressed thereafter; serum testosterone levels normalized only after adrenalectomy. These hormonal trends indicate biochemical remission of both hypercortisolism and hyperandrogenism following definitive surgical management.

Discussion

This case represents an exceptional and complex manifestation of endocrine pathology, characterized by a PHA secreting both GH and ACTH, in conjunction with bilateral adrenal adenomas autonomously producing cortisol and androgens. Such a constellation of hormone excess is exceedingly rare and presents significant diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. Among PHAs, co-secretion of GH and ACTH remains one of the least commonly encountered combinations, with only about 25 cases described in the literature to date [2]. In many of these reported cases, the clinical phenotype is typically dominated by acromegalic features, while the manifestations of Cushing disease are often subtle, absent, or obscured by the more prominent effects of GH excess [2]. This masking of hypercortisolism can delay diagnosis and complicate the interpretation of biochemical testing.

Double pituitary adenomas, reported in 0.4% to 2.6% of surgical series and ∼1% of autopsies [3], should be considered when discordant hormone profiles are present. They are usually functional, most often secreting GH and ACTH [7, 8].

Moreover, patients with MEN1 may have a predisposition to develop plurihormonal or multiple pituitary adenomas, particularly those secreting ACTH and prolactin [9]. In a large case series of 400 MEN1 patients, 19 were found to have hypercortisolism, and notably, 27% of those with Cushing disease harbored double pituitary adenomas [10]. This association emphasizes the importance of considering syndromic or familial etiologies in patients presenting with complex pituitary hormone excess.

Arita et al [11] reported a 29-year-old woman with a GH-ACTH co-secreting pituitary macroadenoma presenting with acromegaly and hyperandrogenism, who improved after surgery. GH adenomas often show plurihormonality, with one series noting co-expression of other pituitary hormones in 85% of cases, including ACTH in 25% [12]. These findings reinforce the need to consider hormonal co-secretion even when one hormonal axis clinically dominates, as in our case.

The precise prevalence and incidence of adrenal incidentalomas cannot be easily defined, since data from population-based studies are scarce. Age significantly influences detection rates, with prevalence being only ∼0.2% in individuals younger than 30 years, increasing to ∼3% by the age of 50, and reaching up to 10% in those older than 70 years [13-15]. Mild autonomous cortisol secretion (MACS) is the most common endocrine dysfunction associated with adrenal incidentalomas. Depending on study design, diagnostic protocols, and criteria applied, MACS has been reported in 5% to 30% of patients with adrenal adenomas [16], and in some cohorts, up to 50% of adrenal cortical adenomas demonstrate biochemical evidence of cortisol autonomy [17].

While cortisol-producing adenomas are well-documented, co-secretion of clinically significant androgens is exceedingly rare [18] and prompt evaluation for adrenocortical carcinoma [19]. However, our case distinctly illustrates that benign bilateral adrenal adenomas may occasionally present with combined hypersecretion, in the absence of imaging or histologic features of malignancy. A similar case report described a 42-year-old woman with progressive virilization due to bilateral cortisol and androgen secreting adrenal adenomas, supporting the plausibility of our patient's adrenal profile [20].

This case highlights a rare plurihormonal pituitary macroadenoma (secreting GH and ACTH) with bilateral adrenal adenomas producing cortisol and androgens. The overlapping features required extensive biochemical, dynamic, and imaging studies to localize hormone sources and guide surgery, achieving remission. MEN was considered but excluded on genetic testing.
