A case report of pediatric robotic adrenalectomy: pushing boundaries on the robotic platform
Authors: Ashwin Krishnamoorthy, Siva Ranjith Jayapalan, Guruprasad Chellappan Sojamani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMinimally invasive surgery has become widely adopted in various types of surgery and is associated with faster postoperative recovery. Similar benefits may apply to pediatric patients, in whom enhanced recovery can facilitate timely initiation or resumption of systemic therapy which is an important component of multimodality treatment. We report the case of a 2-year-old child with neuroblastoma of the left adrenal gland who underwent robotic adrenalectomy. This case suggests that a robotic approach may be feasible in carefully selected pediatric patients and can be performed safely under appropriate expertise.Keywords: Robotic surgery, Adrenalectomy, Surgical oncology, Neuroblastoma, Pediatrics

INTRODUCTION

In the last decade, minimally invasive surgeries have become increasingly popular. However, the application of robotic surgery in the field of pediatric oncologic surgery has been limited. The vast majority of pediatric solid organ tumors occur in the retroperitoneum and are suitable for excision through robotic surgery. However, the size constraints prevent the widespread application of this approach. Neuroblastoma frequently arises from the adrenal gland in children [1]. Adrenalectomy is a well-established procedure in adults; however, reports of robotic adrenalectomy in very young children remain limited.

Minimally invasive adrenalectomies have been shown to have faster postoperative recovery than open surgery, including in pediatric patients [2]. Shorter operating time, reduced transfusion requirements, and earlier resumption of oral intake may contribute to improved 30-day morbidity [3]. Earlier recovery may also facilitate timely resumption of chemotherapy, which forms the backbone of therapy for pediatric neuroblastomas.

The evidence for robotic surgery in the pediatric population is growing with reports of general abdominal, genitourinary, and gastrointestinal surgeries on the rise [4,5]. The application of robotic surgery for adrenalectomies is not widespread, with fewer than 10 cases reported in the literature [6,7]. In this case report, we present a case that underwent robotic adrenalectomy for neuroblastoma. The technical aspects of the procedure discussed below express the applicability of the robotic platform in the pediatric population.

CASE REPORT

A 2-year-old male child (height, 90 cm and weight, 12 kg) was evaluated for abdominal pain and was diagnosed with a neuroblastoma of the left adrenal (Fig. 1A) with metastasis to bone marrow, cervical lymph node, and bilateral iliac bones. Being Stage M, he was started on chemotherapy (rapid COJEC [cisplatin, vincristine, carboplatin, etoposide, and cyclophosphamide] regimen), and following three cycles, he was reassessed with a contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) scan (Fig. 1B), which showed a response at the primary site (initial imaging showed a lesion of size 8.5 × 7.7 cm, post-chemotherapy size was reduced to 3.3 × 2 cm) and resolution of metastatic lesions. As post-chemotherapy CECT demonstrated a small residual tumor amenable to extraction through a small incision, and because early resumption of chemotherapy was desired, robotic adrenalectomy was planned.

(A) Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) before initiation of chemotherapy showing a large tumor (arrow). (B) CECT following chemotherapy showing tumor response (arrow).

A lateral position with 15° lateral flexion at the hip joint was used. The docking was performed with the robotic cart on the patient’s left side. All the instruments and ports used were the same as those used for regular adult cases. Since the abdomen was relatively small and would not permit a ‘full’ four-port placement, we proceeded with a three-arm docking technique (Fig. 2A) with the ports placed in a curvilinear fashion (from xiphisternum to left anterior superior iliac spine) as opposed to the standard linear port placement used for the da Vinci Xi system (Intuitive Surgical). The fourth arm was collapsed and stowed away from the field. The available space was evenly distributed between the three ports, giving a space of approximately 7 cm between the ports. An assistant port was placed between ports one and two to facilitate field suction and vascular clip application.

(A) Port placement. Ports 1–3 were used for robotic arms, and port 4 served as the assistant port. The yellow line indicates the left subcostal margin. (B) Intraoperative view showing the tumor after dissection, with feeding vessels clipped. (C) Gross specimen. (D) Postoperative view after wound closure and drain placement.

After performing a left visceral rotation maneuver, the adrenal gland was visualized and excised with ligation of its vascular supply. In this case, most of the vasculature was controlled using the robotic bipolar arm (Fig. 2B). A laparoscopic bipolar vessel sealing device was also used as needed through the assistance port. The procedure was completed in 136 minutes from incision to port closure, including a 21-minute docking time and a 76-minute console time. The intraoperative blood loss was 20 mL. A drain was placed through port 3, and the specimen was extracted through port 2 after placing it in an endobag (Fig. 2C, D). The patient had an uneventful postoperative recovery and was discharged on postoperative day 3. The final histopathological report showed no residual neuroblastoma in the adrenal. The patient underwent high-dose chemotherapy with autologous stem cell transplantation 8 weeks after surgery. He has since completed systemic therapy and remains under follow-up. Surveillance imaging at 6 months showed no evidence of disease. Follow-up is planned every 6 months for 2 years, with annual visits thereafter.

DISCUSSION

Surgery forms one of the key modalities in the multidisciplinary care of neuroblastoma. Nevertheless, in pediatric malignancies, timely surgery and rapid recovery are especially important because chemotherapy often needs to be resumed without a prolonged break. Enhanced recovery following minimally invasive surgery can help meet this requirement by enabling timely resumption of chemotherapy. Laparoscopic resection would have been extremely difficult in this particular case, considering the space constraint and the fibrosis resulting from chemotherapy-related disease shrinkage. Advanced robotic surgical systems can help address challenging anatomy with improved ease compared with laparoscopic surgery. Features such as three-dimensional visualization, tremor filtering, and wristed articulating instruments may facilitate complex procedures such as adrenalectomy, which is often performed in a confined space adjacent to critical structures and involves delicate tissue handling [8].

The template for pediatric robotic adrenalectomy mirrors that of adult cases. The key differences lie in the planning of port placement and the usage of robotic arms in a judicious way, as access space remains the limiting issue when it comes to minimal access pediatric surgeries [6]. We used a three-arm docking technique, which is the most common method described in the literature currently [6,7,9]. Data from adult studies suggest that robotic adrenalectomy tends to require longer operative times than the laparoscopic approach [10]. In pediatric robotic procedures, reported mean operative times range from 165 to 194 minutes and appear to be longer in genitourinary cases [3]. Our operating time of 136 minutes (with a blood loss of approximately 20 mL) is significantly less than the data available from published series on pediatric robotic adrenalectomies [6,7]. In this case, the robotic platform’s enhanced dexterity was helpful in the setting of limited working space, supporting its use over a conventional laparoscopic approach. Further studies in pediatric patients are warranted to better define the potential benefits of robotic adrenalectomy compared with conventional laparoscopic adrenalectomy.

Our report supports the feasibility and safety of this procedure. Additional data across different pediatric age groups are needed to refine technical details such as port placement, docking, and patient positioning. When appropriate, minimally invasive approaches, including robotic surgery, may be considered in pediatric oncologic surgery.
