Quantitative RNA spatial profiling using single-molecule RNA FISH on plant tissue cryosections
Authors: Xue Zhang, Alejandro Fonseca, Konstantin Kutashev, Martina Leso, Adrien Sicard, Susan Duncan, Stefanie Rosa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSingle-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH) has emerged as a powerful tool for studying gene expression dynamics with unparalleled precision and spatial resolution in a variety of biological systems. Recent advancements have expanded its application to encompass plant studies, yet there remains a need for a simple and robust smFISH method adapted to plant tissue sections. Here, we present an optimized smFISH protocol, termed cryo-smFISH, for visualizing and quantifying single mRNA molecules in plant tissue cryosections. This method exhibits remarkable sensitivity, enabling the detection of low-expression transcripts, including long non-coding RNAs. By integrating a deep learning-based algorithm into our image analysis pipeline, our method enables precise assignment of RNA abundance in nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments. The method also enables robust integration with immunofluorescence, as cryosectioning enhances antibody penetration. This allows for the sequential visualization and quantification of both RNAs and endogenous proteins within the same cells. Finally, this study demonstrates the use of smFISH to validate single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) expression patterns in plant tissues. By extending smFISH to plant cryosections, plant scientists will be able to exploit the full potential of quantitative transcript analysis at cellular and subcellular resolution.Key words: smFISH, scRNA-seq, smFISH–IF, tissue cryosections, quantitative gene expression

Introduction

High-resolution analysis of spatiotemporal gene-expression dynamics requires precise quantification of RNA abundance and spatial distribution within cells and tissues. RNA in situ hybridization (ISH) is a classical technique that enables researchers to map RNA molecules within intact cells or tissues with spatial precision. This is achieved through the use of probes containing nucleic acid sequences that are complementary to a specific target RNA sequence (Pardue and Gall, 1969; Cox et al., 1984). ISH probes can be labeled with a variety of detectable molecules, such as radioisotopes (Harrison et al., 1973), biotin (Hutchison et al., 1982; Brigati et al., 1983), digoxigenin (Heiles et al., 1988; Panoskaltsis-Mortari and Bucy, 1995), and fluorescent dyes (Bauman et al., 1980; Singer and Ward, 1982). However, traditional ISH techniques suffer from limited spatial resolution and are primarily qualitative in nature.

Single-molecule fluorescence in situ hybridization (smFISH) is a variant of FISH that enables single-molecule resolution (Femino et al., 1998; Raj et al., 2008). It uses a set of 25–48 short DNA oligonucleotides (18–22-mers) to bind specific sequences in the target RNA, enabling the visualization and quantification of individual RNA molecules within intact cells and tissues at high spatial resolution. While smFISH has been successfully used in a variety of model organisms, including bacteria (Skinner et al., 2013), C. elegans (Raj et al., 2008), and mammalian cells (Lyubimova et al., 2013), its application to diverse plant tissues has been constrained by technical complexities arising from inherent autofluorescence and light-scattering properties of cell walls and vacuole-rich tissues. The adaptation of smFISH to the plant model species Arabidopsis thaliana in 2016 marked a pivotal development, enabling the imaging of individual RNA molecules within fixed, squashed root meristem cells (Duncan et al., 2016; 2017). Since then, additional smFISH protocols and other newly developed ISH-based methods have gradually emerged for studying gene expression in plants (Solanki et al., 2020; Yang et al., 2020; Huang et al., 2023; Nobori et al., 2023). We recently optimized smFISH for whole-mount intact plant tissues (Zhao et al., 2023). Despite the success of whole-mount smFISH in several tissues, this method is not ideal for very thick specimens, and physical sectioning may be required for thick plant samples. smFISH has been applied to plant tissue sections from paraffin-embedded samples (Huang et al., 2020). However, because paraffin embedding involves several steps and can be time-consuming, a more rapid and streamlined smFISH method for plant tissue sections would be beneficial.

In this study, we present an optimized smFISH protocol for the visualization and quantification of single RNA molecules in plant tissue cryosections. This method enables quantitative analysis of weakly expressed transcripts, such as long non-coding RNAs (lncRNAs), and provides precise information on transcript distribution across diverse cell types. By incorporating DAPI staining and a deep learning-based cell-segmentation pipeline, we achieved segmentation of subcellular compartments, allowing us to distinguish and quantify nuclear and cytoplasmic transcripts. Additionally, performing smFISH and immunofluorescence (IF) sequentially enables the visualization and quantification of RNAs and endogenous proteins within individual cells. Notably, this study demonstrates the application of smFISH for single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) validation in plants, enabling the investigation of spatial gene-expression patterns in relation to cell identity. In summary, our study illustrates how smFISH can be used for scRNA-seq validation and for the simultaneous detection of endogenous RNAs and proteins.

Results

Single-molecule RNA detection and quantification in Arabidopsis and barley tissue cryosections

Imaging plant tissues presents specific challenges because of their inherent autofluorescence. Although clearing techniques have greatly improved our ability to observe plant tissues in whole-mount settings (Zhao et al., 2023), their application remains limited in some plant species due to tissue thickness. In this context, tissue sectioning represents a valuable alternative. In contrast to optical sectioning, physical sectioning allows thicker specimens to be imaged without sacrificing the signal-to-noise ratio. The first step in developing our smFISH protocol for cryosections (hereafter referred to as cryo-smFISH) involved obtaining high-quality sections that preserve both plant tissue morphology and RNA integrity. To accomplish this, we adapted previously published protocols (Anjam et al., 2016; Stapel et al., 2018) for use in A. thaliana (Arabidopsis) root cryosections (Figure 1A). This included a cryoprotection step before cryosectioning to preserve tissue morphology (Supplemental Figure 1) and a re-fixation step to ensure RNA preservation. These adjustments, combined with the use of 5–10-μm-thick sections, enabled us to obtain well-preserved samples consisting of a single layer of cells. Although Arabidopsis roots are not thick specimens, they are extremely fragile, posing significant challenges for preserving tissue structure. Therefore, optimizing a cryosectioning protocol tailored to Arabidopsis roots provides a valuable initial step in developing a robust method that can be applied to other tissue types. To optimize and validate our cryo-smFISH protocol in Arabidopsis root cryosections, we focused on detecting transcripts from the housekeeping gene Protein Phosphatase 2A (PP2A), as previously reported (Duncan et al., 2016; 2017; 2023; Zhao et al., 2023). The resulting cryo-smFISH images from both longitudinal and transverse cryosections revealed distinct bright puncta corresponding to PP2A mRNA molecules, visible by both epifluorescence and confocal microscopy (Supplemental Figure 2), with confocal imaging providing clearer cell outlines. PP2A mRNA molecules displayed a ubiquitous distribution across various root cell types in the root meristem region, consistent with previous reports (Duncan et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2023). To enable precise allocation of transcripts to individual cells, we stained cryosections with the cell wall dye Renaissance 2200 (SR2200) (Musielak et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2023). Combined with our integrated image analysis pipeline (Zhao et al., 2023), this enabled the precise quantitative assignment of PP2A mRNA molecules to individual cells (Figures 1B and 1C; Supplemental Figure 3). Treatment with RNase A confirmed that the observed signals corresponded to true mRNA molecules (Supplemental Figure 4). It is important to note that cryosections capture only a fraction of a cell’s total 3D volume. Because many plant cells are highly elongated, we do not infer absolute “whole-cell” transcript numbers; instead, we report the number of transcripts detected per segmented cell within the imaged section as a proxy for relative expression. We empirically validated this two-dimensional (2D) sampling approach by demonstrating a highly significant correlation between transcript counts in adjacent optical slices of the same cells (Supplemental Figure 5). Nevertheless, if transcripts are unevenly distributed within cells, especially in cells longer than 10 μm, cross-sectional analyses should be complemented with longitudinal sections to accurately inspect transcript distributions.

We then assessed the applicability of our cryo-smFISH protocol in Arabidopsis leaves. Despite the challenges associated with autofluorescence in plant tissues, we were able to detect PP2A RNA signals in young Arabidopsis leaves (Supplemental Figure 6). We also attempted detection in adult Arabidopsis leaves, where high autofluorescence resulting from abundant chloroplasts, together with difficulties in preserving the spongy mesophyll structure, makes the process considerably more difficult. To mitigate autofluorescence, we used probes in the far-red region (Quasar670) and applied extended clearing steps (2 weeks with ClearSee). Under these conditions, RNA signals were detectable (Supplemental Figure 7); however, signal detection was inconsistent across cells, highlighting the technical limitations of the method in this tissue. We further sought to expand the application of cryo-smFISH to other plant species. Specifically, we applied our approach to the monocot Hordeum vulgare (barley) by detecting the housekeeping gene HvGAPDH in cryosections of roots and young leaves. HvGAPDH transcripts were clearly detected and quantifiable in both tissues (Figures 1B and 1D; Supplemental Figures 8A and 8B). Consistent with our earlier analyses, treatment with RNase A confirmed the specificity of the RNA signals (Supplemental Figure 8C). We further validated the specificity of the assay through cross-probe control experiments, using probes designed to detect PP2A from Arabidopsis and GAPDH from barley in both sample types (Supplemental Figure 9), and by using probes against VENUS mRNA in a VENUS transgenic line compared with wild type (Supplemental Figure 10). These results demonstrate that cryo-smFISH can be successfully used to detect and quantify specific RNA molecules with high resolution in cryosections obtained from both model and crop plant tissues.

Mapping the cell type distribution of transcripts in Arabidopsis roots using cryo-smFISH

scRNA-seq has emerged as a powerful genomic approach for the detection and quantitative analysis of messenger RNA molecules at single-cell resolution. Early scRNA-seq studies using high-throughput droplet-based technologies have revolutionized our understanding of plant biology by revealing the transcriptional states of many different cell types (Denyer et al., 2019; Zhang et al., 2019; Wendrich et al., 2020; Shaw et al., 2021; Otero et al., 2022; Shahan et al., 2022). However, gene expression estimates derived from scRNA-seq are not absolute but are influenced by capture efficiency, normalization strategies, and computational inference. Hence, complementary methods are needed to validate scRNA-seq results. Furthermore, scRNA-seq does not preserve spatial information, as tissue is disintegrated into individual cells before analysis (Shaw et al., 2021; Bawa et al., 2022). Complementary techniques, such as transgenic reporter lines and traditional ISH methods, are often utilized for scRNA-seq validation. Yet, these methods lack quantitative precision, fail to capture RNAs with particularly low expression, cannot visualize active transcription events, and are unsuitable for determining kinetic transcriptional parameters. These limitations highlight the need for complementary techniques that can validate scRNA-seq findings while retaining cellular spatial information. Here, we demonstrate the use of cryo-smFISH to map the spatial distribution of transcripts in plant tissues as a method suitable for scRNA-seq validation. As a proof-of-principle, we chose to study the nitrate transporter gene NRT1.9 (AT1G18880), which has been implicated in facilitating nitrate loading into phloem cells in Arabidopsis roots (Wang and Tsay, 2011). To investigate the cell-type-specific expression of this gene, we reanalyzed scRNA-seq data from 10-day-old Arabidopsis root tips, as previously described (Wendrich et al., 2020). Following clustering and cell type annotation, we subset the dataset to specifically investigate cell types readily distinguishable in root cryosections: epidermis, cortex, endodermis, pericycle, phloem, procambium, and xylem (Figure 2A). Analysis of differential gene expression (DGE) among these cell types indicated that NRT1.9 is preferentially expressed in the phloem, pericycle, and procambium (Figures 2A and 2B). To confirm these results, we designed a mixed smFISH probe set targeting both exonic and intronic regions of NRT1.9 RNA and performed cryo-smFISH on Arabidopsis root cross-sections (Figures 2C and 2D). The cryo-smFISH results closely aligned with the scRNA-seq expression pattern for NRT1.9 (Figures 2B–2F). However, a discrepancy was observed in the procambium, where scRNA-seq indicated higher levels of expression. Nevertheless, expression trends across the other cell types were highly similar between the two approaches, with phloem cells consistently exhibiting higher levels of expression in both scRNA-seq and cryo-smFISH analyses (Figure 2F). Detailed inspection of the images revealed RNA foci of higher intensity (Figure 2C, indicated by orange arrows). Through DAPI staining, we confirmed that these brighter foci were predominantly present in the nucleus (Supplemental Figure 11). Importantly, treatment with RNase A once again confirmed the specificity of the detected RNA signals (Supplemental Figures 12A and 12B). Overall, these results highlight the valuable application of cryo-smFISH as a method to validate and complement scRNA-seq findings at both qualitative and quantitative levels.

Detection of long non-coding RNA distribution with subcellular resolution

Long noncoding RNAs (lncRNAs) are a class of RNA molecules longer than 200 nucleotides that have garnered increasing interest due to their potential regulatory roles in various biological processes (Jha et al., 2020; Lucero et al., 2020; Statello et al., 2021). In contrast to coding RNAs, lncRNAs are recognized for their pronounced tissue specificity and, often, relatively lower abundance (Djebali et al., 2012; Wang et al., 2014; Cabili et al., 2015; Rosa et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2018). Despite their predicted contributions to multiple aspects of gene regulation, with subcellular localization being key to their function (Statello et al., 2021), the study of lncRNAs, especially at the single-cell level, remains limited (He et al., 2023). Here, we tested whether our cryo-smFISH method could be used to detect an uncharacterized low-abundance lncRNA. asSOFL1 is the antisense lncRNA of SOFL1 (Kim et al., 2022). We reanalyzed bulk RNA-seq data from wild-type Arabidopsis root tips (Choe et al., 2017). Among other genes, asSOFL1 RNA was found to be weakly expressed but detectable in root tips. We next designed smFISH probes targeting asSOFL1 transcripts and performed cryo-smFISH on Arabidopsis cross-sections. The results revealed bright dots corresponding to asSOFL1 transcripts (Figure 3A and Supplemental Figure 13). Counting transcripts per cell revealed that this lncRNA is expressed at low levels, with only a few transcripts detected per segmented cell within the section (Figure 3B). Despite its low expression, asSOFL1 transcripts were found ubiquitously across various cell types within the root (Figure 3C). We then employed RNase A treatment, which confirmed that the bright spots observed corresponded to true RNA signals (Supplemental Figure 12C). We additionally compared the expression profiles of asSOFL1 across root cell types obtained from scRNA-seq, bulk RNA-seq, and cryo-smFISH data (Supplemental Figure 14). This comparative analysis revealed that the results obtained by cryo-smFISH are consistent with those from the other two methods, with similar longitudinal trends also observed for NRT1.9 and PP2A.

Subcellular localization patterns of lncRNAs can provide insight into their function (Cabili et al., 2015). To elucidate the subcellular localization of asSOFL1 transcripts, we incorporated DAPI staining into our cryo-smFISH protocol. Visual inspection of the images revealed that asSOFL1 transcripts were predominantly localized in the nucleus (Figures 3D and 3E). To obtain a quantitative description of both nuclear and cytoplasmic compartments, we modified the cell-segmentation step in our image-analysis pipeline. This was achieved using custom-trained Cellpose models (Stringer et al., 2021), a deep learning-based algorithm for cellular segmentation. Using two models trained specifically for segmenting cells and nuclei in Arabidopsis and barley tissue cryosections, we created cell masks and nuclear masks required as input files for subsequent transcript quantification using the FISH-quant pipeline. With this adapted image-analysis pipeline, we were able to automatically assign transcripts to either nuclear or cytoplasmic compartments. Our cryo-smFISH results showed that most asSOFL1 transcripts were localized in the nucleus (Figures 3F and 3H; Supplemental Figure 13). In contrast, over 70% of PP2A transcripts were found in the cytoplasm, where translation occurs, as expected for a coding gene (Figure 3G). To further characterize these subcellular populations, we quantified the raw fluorescence intensity of individual smFISH foci. For both genes, cytoplasmic signals displayed a uniform intensity distribution characteristic of single, mature RNA molecules. Conversely, nuclear foci showed significantly higher variance (Levene’s test, p < 0.05) and a distinct subpopulation distribution (Welch’s test, p < 0.05), with high-intensity outliers corresponding to putative transcription sites where multiple nascent transcripts accumulate (Supplemental Figure 15). Together, these results illustrate that our cryo-smFISH protocol can be used to detect low-abundance transcripts, such as lncRNAs, and provide important data on their quantitative expression at the cellular and subcellular levels.

Simultaneous RNA and protein detection using a sequential cryo-smFISH/IF protocol

Examining the subcellular localization of proteins in conjunction with gene expression quantification and mRNA localization can shed light on the complex interplay between transcriptional and post-translational regulatory processes. To establish the proof-of-principle technical compatibility between cryo-smFISH and IF, we adapted a sequential smFISH-IF protocol as previously described (Rosa et al., 2016). Using probes against PP2A mRNA, we first performed cryo-smFISH, followed by IF using an antibody against the histone marker H4Ac. The results revealed that antibody staining was compatible with the cryo-smFISH procedure, with uniform signals across all cells and good tissue preservation, allowing straightforward overlay of cryo-smFISH and IF images (Figure 4A). This dual approach allowed us to visualize PP2A mRNA and H4Ac in the same cells and to quantify their levels per cell within the section (Figures 4B and 4C), as well as to assess their abundance within the same cells and across different cell types (Figure 4D). Importantly, this sequential cryo-smFISH/IF protocol can be extended to crop species, such as barley (Figure 4A). The development of this protocol provides a valuable tool for colocalization studies, especially in species for which transgenesis has not been achieved.

Discussion

In this study, we introduce an optimized smFISH protocol designed specifically for plant tissue cryosections. Our cryo-smFISH method provides an effective approach for precisely quantifying the spatial distribution of RNA with single-cell and single-molecule resolution in plant tissue sections. Compared with paraffin-embedding workflows, cryosectioning can reduce sample-processing time and avoid dehydration/clearing steps. While cryosections can be more sensitive to handling and may require careful optimization to preserve tissue morphology, they generally provide strong preservation of biomolecules, including protein epitopes and nucleic acids (Hira et al., 2019). Relative to our previous smFISH protocol for whole-mount tissue (Zhao et al., 2023), the choice of method will depend on the type of material and the final objective of the study. While whole-mount smFISH performs well in preserving tissue structure, it may yield inferior signal-to-noise ratios for thicker specimens. Furthermore, if the goal is to combine RNA detection with protein detection through immunofluorescence, cryosectioning offers a distinct advantage, as antibody penetration is notably more efficient in tissue cryosections. The use of thin sections also allows for the use of widefield microscopes, which can be invaluable when confocal microscopes are not readily available. Moreover, widefield systems for cryo-smFISH samples can significantly expedite image acquisition compared with confocal imaging, while still allowing for excellent segmentation results. A structured comparison of cryo-smFISH with whole-mount and other section-based smFISH workflows is provided in Supplemental Table 1.

The complexity of vascular tissue structure and development has historically made high-resolution gene expression profiling challenging. Detailed gene expression profiling in plant vascular tissues has predominantly been explored through scRNA-seq (Wendrich et al., 2020; Chen et al., 2021; Otero et al., 2022; Kułak et al., 2023). Here, we employed cryo-smFISH to physically map and quantify the vascular tissue-specific gene NRT1.9 within individual cells in a transgene-free manner. Our analysis, combining scRNA-seq with cryo-smFISH, highlights smFISH as a powerful method for validating scRNA-seq data while complementing it with cellular and subcellular spatial information. This further reinforces the importance of expanding the use of smFISH across several plant tissues and preparations, particularly as the demand for scRNA-seq validation methods continues to rise.

Lastly, cryo-smFISH is sensitive enough to detect low-abundance transcripts, such as lncRNAs, thereby expanding our ability to study RNA function at both cellular and subcellular levels. However, several limitations should also be noted. While some tissues, such as barley roots, yield good cryosections relatively easily, others, such as leaves or Arabidopsis roots, can be more challenging because tissue integrity is difficult to maintain and may require careful optimization and practice. Detection in highly autofluorescent tissues also remains challenging and requires optimization for each specific tissue type. This limitation is especially evident in adult Arabidopsis leaves, where high background fluorescence strongly limits robust RNA detection. Under our conditions, detectable signals were obtained only with Quasar670-labeled probes in the far-red range, where tissue autofluorescence is lower, underscoring the difficulty of applying cryo-smFISH reliably in this tissue. Additional optimizations, such as prolonged clearing treatments, may be necessary for different samples to reduce background autofluorescence and improve signal detection. Additionally, like most smFISH methods, our protocol has limitations in detecting RNAs shorter than 600 nucleotides and in co-detecting multiple RNA species (see Supplemental Table 2 for additional strengths and limitations of cryo-smFISH). Several technical advances could further improve the sensitivity and versatility of plant smFISH. For example, sensitivity could be enhanced through probe-design strategies that increase fluorescence output per transcript, such as increasing the number or density of probes targeting the same RNA (Wang and Guo, 2021). In parallel, other RNA-imaging approaches, including live-cell RNA-labeling systems such as MS2, provide complementary approaches for studying transcriptional dynamics in plants (Tantale et al., 2016; Alamos et al., 2021; Hani et al., 2021).

In summary, our cryo-smFISH method represents a valuable tool for precisely quantifying single RNA molecules in plant tissue sections with high spatial resolution. This innovative approach enables plant biologists to investigate RNA abundance and localization at both cellular and subcellular resolution, thereby greatly expanding the experimental toolkit for spatial studies of gene expression in plants.

Methods

Plant materials and growth conditions

Arabidopsis thaliana Col-0 and barley (Hordeum vulgare) were used in this study. Barley (Hordeum vulgare) seeds were a gift from Silvana Moreno (Department of Plant Biology, SLU, Uppsala, Sweden). The pHvpPIN1a:HvPIN1a-mVENUS barley line was a gift from Prof. Rüdiger Simon (Heinrich Heine University, Germany). Seeds were surface-sterilized in 1 ml of 70% ethanol in 2 ml Eppendorf tubes on a rotating wheel for 5–10 min, followed by three washes with 95% ethanol. The ethanol was then removed, and the seeds were allowed to dry in a laminar-flow hood.

After stratification for 2–3 days, seeds were sown on Murashige and Skoog (MS) agar plates. The plates were then transferred to a growth chamber under a 16-h light/8-h dark photoperiod, with temperatures of 22°C during the day and 20°C during the night. Arabidopsis root tips were collected 7 days after germination. Barley roots were collected 4 days after germination.

smFISH probe design

Probes targeting the genes of interest were designed using the online Stellaris Probe Designer from Biosearch Technologies: https://www.biosearchtech.com/stellaris-designer. For mRNA detection, the coding sequence of the target gene was entered into the program, which automatically generated a set of probes complementary to the target mRNA.

The sequences of the probes were then subjected to quality control using an automated local blast R script, available on GitHub at: https://github.com/xuezhang911/zhang_et_al_smFISH_cyrosections/tree/main/smFISHprobes. The smFISH probes used in this study and their respective fluorophores are shown in Supplemental Table 3. The probes were diluted in Tris-EDTA buffer to a final stock concentration of 25 μM.

Cryo-smFISH

Sample preparation

Tissues were dissected using a razor blade and fixed for 30 min for roots or 1 h for green tissues in 4% (w/v) paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution in nuclease-free 1× PBS buffer (pH 8.0; Sigma–Aldrich, cat. # AM9624). Following fixation, the tissues were cryoprotected in 34% sucrose in 1× PBS, followed by overnight incubation with mild shaking in a 1:1 mixture of 34% sucrose in 1× PBS and liquid optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT; Leica Biosystems, cat. # 14020108926) at 4°C. Subsequently, the samples were incubated in pre-chilled liquid OCT for 30 min to 1 h with mild shaking before tissue blocks were prepared. Using tweezers, the tissues (roots or leaves) were gently placed into 3D-printed Cryomolds (1 cm × 1 cm × 1 cm) filled with pre-chilled OCT. These OCT-embedded tissue blocks were frozen either by contact with dry ice or by indirect contact with liquid nitrogen. The cryoblocks were then wrapped in foil and stored at −80°C until cryosectioning. Cryosections of 10 μm for Arabidopsis and barley roots or 5 μm for barley leaves were cut using a cryostat (Leica CM1850) and mounted onto Polysine adhesion microscope slides (cat. # 48 382-117), followed by air-drying for up to 20 min and post-fixation for 10 min with 4% PFA at room temperature (RT). Subsequently, after rinsing three times with 1× PBS, the slides containing sections were permeabilized sequentially in methanol for 30 min, ethanol for 30 min, and ClearSee solution containing 10% (w/v) xylitol powder (Sigma–Aldrich, cat. # X3375), 15% (w/v) sodium deoxycholate (Sigma–Aldrich, cat. # D6750), and 25% (w/v) urea (Sigma–Aldrich, cat. # U5378) dissolved in RNase-free distilled water (Qiagen, cat. # 10-977-015). The slides were then stored at 4°C overnight until the subsequent smFISH procedure. To confirm RNA specificity, samples were incubated with RNase A (Sigma–Aldrich, cat. # R4642) for 1 h at 37°C in a humid chamber after the clearing step. Samples were then rinsed in 10 mM HCl for 5 min and washed twice in 2× saline-sodium citrate (SSC) for 5 min each before proceeding with hybridization.

Hybridization

The procedure was performed as previously described, with slight modifications (Duncan et al., 2016; Zhao et al., 2023). Briefly, slides prepared with cryosectioned samples were first rinsed 2–3 times with wash buffer containing 10% (v/v) nuclease-free 20× SSC (Thermo Scientific, cat. # AM9763), 10% (v/v) deionized formamide (Merck, cat. #S4117), and 0.1% (v/v) Triton X-100 (Sigma–Aldrich, cat. # T8787). A total volume of 100 μl of hybridization solution containing 10% (w/v) dextran sulfate (Sigma–Aldrich, cat. # RES2029D), 10% (v/v) deionized formamide, and probes at a final concentration of 250 nM was added to each slide. Plastic coverslips were placed over the samples to prevent buffer evaporation, and the probes were hybridized in a humid chamber at 37°C overnight in the dark.

Post-hybridization and mounting

Plastic coverslips were gently removed, and the hybridization solution containing unbound probes was removed by rinsing the slides twice with wash buffer. Subsequently, the slides were immersed in Coplin jars containing wash buffer for up to 1 h at 37°C. DAPI (1:10,000) or SR2200 dye (1:50,000; Renaissance Chemicals Ltd.; see Musielak et al., 2016 for recipes) prepared in wash buffer was applied for 10–15 min or 5–10 min, respectively. After washing for 5–10 min with 2× SSC buffer, the samples were mounted in freshly prepared GLOX mounting medium (0.4% glucose; 10 nM Tris–HCl, pH 8.0 [Invitrogen, cat. # AM9856]; and 2× SSC; supplemented with 1/100 [v/v] glucose oxidase [Sigma, cat. # G2133-10KU] and 2/100 [v/v] gently vortexed bovine liver catalase [Sigma, cat. # C3155-50MG]) and sealed with nail polish.

A step-by-step protocol for cryo-smFISH can be found in the Supplemental Information (Supplemental Methods 1).

smFISH followed by immuno-fluorescence (IF)

We conducted cryo-smFISH and IF sequentially; the cryo-smFISH protocol for RNA detection was performed first as described above, followed by immunofluorescence after cryo-smFISH image acquisition. After imaging, the coverslips were gently removed, and the samples were rinsed three times with 1× PBS. Samples were then incubated with enzyme mix (2% Driselase [Sigma–Aldrich, cat. # D8037], 1% cellulase [Sigma–Aldrich, cat. # C1184], and 2% macerozyme [Duchefa, cat. # M8002] in 1× PBS) for 15 min in a humid chamber at 37°C. After washing three times with 1× PBS, the samples were incubated with 5% BSA blocking buffer in a humid chamber for 30 min at 37°C. Subsequently, the samples were incubated with an anti-H4Ac antibody (Bio-Rad, AHP418) diluted 1:100 in 5% BSA in a humid chamber overnight at 4°C. The samples were then washed with PBST (0.1% Tween in 1× PBS) by incubation in Coplin jars at 37°C for 30 min. The samples were incubated with a secondary antibody (Agrisera, AS09633) diluted 1:200 in 5% BSA in a humid chamber at 37°C for 2 h. Excess antibody was removed, and the samples were washed with PBST in Coplin jars at 37°C in the dark for 30 min. The samples were then rinsed with 1× PBS and mounted in a drop of Vectashield mounting medium (Bionordika, cat. # VEH-1000). A step-by-step protocol for cryo-smFISH/IF can be found in the Supplemental Information (Supplemental Methods 1).

Image acquisition

A Zeiss LSM800 inverted confocal microscope controlled by ZEN Black software was used for imaging with a 63× water-immersion objective (1.20 NA). We acquired z-stacks with 0.2-μm spacing. The following channel settings were used: DAPI/SR2200, 353 nm excitation and 420–470 nm emission; Quasar570, 548 nm excitation and 570–640 nm emission; Quasar670, 650 nm excitation and 665–715 nm emission; and H4Ac, 488 nm excitation and 500–550 nm emission.

For widefield microscopy, we acquired 10–40 z-stack images with a cooled quad-port charge-coupled device (CCD) ZEISS Axiocam 503 mono camera using a 63× water-immersion objective (1.20 NA). The following channel settings were used: Quasar570, 533–558 nm excitation and 561 nm emission; Quasar670, 625–655 nm excitation and 673 nm emission; and DAPI/SR2200, 335–383 nm excitation and 465 nm emission.

Image analysis

To quantify transcript number and protein levels per cell within the cut section in an unbiased manner, we adapted the automated computational workflow used by Zhao et al. (2023). Our workflow integrates functions from four freely available software programs: Fiji (Schindelin et al., 2012), Cellpose (Stringer et al., 2021), FISH-quant (Mueller et al., 2013; Imbert et al., 2022), and CellProfiler (Carpenter et al., 2006; Stirling et al., 2021), together with a custom Python script. Analyses were performed using separate TIF images for each channel: SR2200, DAPI, smFISH, or IF. Shifts and misalignments between channels and images acquired from the same section were corrected using the BigWarp tool in Fiji (Bogovic et al., 2016). Maximum Z-projection or single-plane confocal or widefield images were used for analysis. A step-by-step image analysis protocol can be found in the supplemental information (Supplemental Methods 2).

Cell and nuclear segmentation

Image segmentation was performed using Cellpose-based algorithms (Stringer et al., 2021).

For cell segmentation, SR2200-channel images were used as input, and the “cyto” algorithm was selected as the pre-trained model for annotating individual cells within the tissue. To achieve accurate cell segmentation, semi-supervised training using SR2200-channel images was implemented. Following the annotation step, manual corrections and successive training cycles were applied until cell borders were precisely outlined. New models specifically designed to segment cells in Arabidopsis or barley tissue sections were generated and used to create cell masks for the subsequent transcript quantification step.

For nucleocytoplasmic-level analyses, we first trained a new segmentation model from the pre-loaded “nuclei” algorithm or from the cell segmentation model developed earlier from SR2200 images, using semi-supervised training as outlined above. As a result, we established two models specifically designed to segment cells with well-defined cell borders and nuclei within Arabidopsis or barley tissue sections. These two models were subsequently used to create cell and nucleus masks for the subsequent transcript quantification step.

The custom segmentation models are available on GitHub at: https://github.com/xuezhang911/zhang_et_al_smFISH_cyrosections/tree/main/smFISHprobes.

Image filtering and dot quantification

We employed the MATLAB version of FISH-quant v3 to quantify RNA dots in cryo-smFISH images (Mueller et al., 2013; Imbert et al., 2022). The software and its accompanying manual can be accessed on Bitbucket, as provided by Florian Mueller: https://bitbucket.org/muellerflorian/fish_quant/src/master/.

The “Cell Segmentation” tool was first used to generate text files containing FISH-quant-recognized nuclei or cell outline coordinates from the Cellpose-generated masks. After the outline text file for a given image was imported, the loaded cryo-smFISH image was filtered by applying a dual-Gaussian filter. For widefield cryo-smFISH images in Supplemental Figure 9, a preprocessing deconvolution step was performed using the open-source software DeconvolutionLab2 (https://bigwww.epfl.ch/deconvolution/deconvolutionlab2/) (Sage et al., 2017). The filtered image was then smoothed with a Gaussian kernel. The Gaussian kernel ratio required adjustment for each gene and different tissue contexts.

Subsequently, we conducted dot pre-detection in one selected filtered image. This was performed in the filtered image by defining threshold intensity and quality scores that enabled accurate detection of fluorescent foci. Next, the detected foci were fitted using Gaussian fluorescence fitting based on the point-spread function (PSF). The settings were then applied to run the analysis in batch mode using the batch analysis tool.

False positives were removed by thresholding the sigma–XY, amplitude, and pixel-intensity parameters, following the developer’s advice. The results were exported as tabulated files, indicating the number of transcripts per segmented cell or nucleus within each section for each object identifier.

Image visualization

Visualization Cells and nuclei: Cell and nuclear masks were created using Cellpose and imported into CellProfiler to generate cellular and nuclear outlines that trace the contours of individual cells and their nuclei. These outline images were then imported into Adobe Illustrator. The high-resolution images, while preserving fine detailed tissue structures, were finalized through image vectorization and manual adjustment of the outline thickness for phloem sieve elements from Arabidopsis and barley roots.

Cryo-smFISH dot visualization: An image displaying cryo-smFISH dots was generated either from the filtered image during the transcript quantification step or from manually plotted dots based on the coordinates provided by the final quantification output. This image was then overlaid with the original cryo-smFISH RNA channel to enhance visualization.

Protein quantification

The immunofluorescence images were analyzed using Cellprofiler (Carpenter et al., 2006; Stirling et al., 2021). Cellpose-generated masks were imported and used to identify each nucleus or cell as an independent object. From the IF images, we computed both the total intensity and the mean intensity for each object, allowing us to determine protein levels on a per-nucleus or per-cell basis. The results were exported as tabulated files.

Assignment of RNA or protein signals to individual cells within cell types

To assign gene transcript numbers to specific cell types, we first obtained the RNA mask and the cell/nuclei mask. For the RNA mask, we visualized RNA molecules by displaying their XY coordinates on a blank image. These coordinates, representing the centers of the dots, were obtained during the transcript detection step. For the cell/nuclei mask, we used Cellpose-generated masks. Subsequently, we imported both the RNA mask and the cell/nuclei mask into CellProfiler, and the number of RNA molecules per individual cell was counted and displayed on the cell/nuclei masks. Similarly, after we measured protein intensity with CellProfiler, the intensity within individual cells was quantified and displayed on cell masks. Consequently, images with well-defined tissue structures, cell/nuclei borders, and RNA masks as graphical representations of the single-cell results were produced.

scRNA-seq analysis

The normalized count matrix in HDF5 (.h5) format was downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO: GSE141730_aggregated_filtered_gene_bc_matrices.h5). Feature selection, dimensionality reduction, and clustering were performed as described in the original study (Wendrich et al., 2020). Cell types were manually annotated using the marker genes reported in that study. For downstream analyses, the full Seurat object was subsetted to include only epidermis, cortex, endodermis, pericycle, phloem, procambium, and xylem cells.

Bulk RNA-seq analysis

The WT FASTQ files were downloaded from the NCBI GEO study GSE96945. After rRNA removal with SortMeRNA (Kopylova et al., 2012), adapters were trimmed with Trimmomatic (Bolger et al., 2014). The raw gene-count matrix was obtained using the pseudoalignment software Kallisto (Bray et al., 2016). RNA-seq reads were normalized as transcripts per million (TPM).
