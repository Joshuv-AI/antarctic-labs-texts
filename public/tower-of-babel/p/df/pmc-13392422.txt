Template-assisted [6+6] cycloaddition for the construction of photothermally responsive functional materials
Authors: Lei Wang, Shu-Yu Wang, Jun-Fu Man, Xu-Hui Zhao, Rui-Dong Wang, Yuan-Yuan Guo, Lin Du, Qi-Hua Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
High-order cycloaddition offers a direct and powerful strategy for constructing medium-sized architectures, but it is often limited by low yields and poor stereoselectivity. Here, we report a template- assisted [6+6] photodimerization reaction, which is achieved via the self-assembly of a two-dimensional (2D) coordination polymer (CP) CP1. The coordination template precisely organizes the reactants. Under irradiation with visible light or natural sunlight, the [6+6] photodimerization proceeds in an environmentally friendly and selective manner. To demonstrate the universality and functionality of this strategy, we introduce methoxy-functionalized ligands to construct CP2, which not only undergoes the same [6+6] cycloaddition reactions but also exhibits temperature-responsive properties. The combination of CP1 and CP2 with Poly(vinyl alcohol) (PVA) and MXene (2D transition-metal carbides) further enhances their actuation properties, making them suitable for adaptive materials. This work establishes a milestone in template-assisted cycloaddition reactions, offering an approach to achieving higher-order cycloadditions and expanding the development of crystal adaptronics.

Introduction

Higher-order cycloaddition reactions (more than 6π-electrons)1–3, owing to their unique mechanisms and remarkable advantages in efficiently constructing complex cyclic systems, these reactions have become indispensable tools for the precise synthesis of highly selective and structurally diverse compounds, attracting sustained global attention in the field of organic synthesis chemistry4–7. In photochemical reactions, template-assisted strategies achieve classic cycloaddition processes such as [2+2] and [4+4] reactions by regulating the stacking arrangements of C=C double bonds8–11. However, template-assisted higher-order cycloaddition studies have thus far been limited to [4+4] cycloadditions involving 8π electrons, and no examples of higher-order cycloaddition beyond this level have been reported.

Coordination templates possess strong binding force and diverse coordination geometers, providing an ideal platform for the spatial pre-organization of alken-based substrates and thus offering a promising approach for efficient high-order photochemical cycloaddition12–20. According to the Woodward–Hoffmann rules, the [6+6] cycloaddition is thermally forbidden but photochemically allowed21,22, providing theoretical feasibility for template-assisted higher-order cycloaddition reactions. As a synergistic cycloaddition process similar to the [2+2] and [4+4] cycloaddition reactions, the [6+6] photodimerization also has the potential to generate materials with unique light response characteristics10,23–29. By combining the characteristic advantages of polyvinyl alcohol (PVA) and MXene, this light response performance can be further amplified30–33.

Hans–Dieter Becker et al. demonstrated that 9-alkenyl-substituted anthracene derivatives can undergo head-to-tail [6+6] cycloaddition reactions, however, these processes often have shortcomings such as low yields and poor stereoselectivity (Supplementary Fig. 3)34–36. To overcome these limitations, in this study, we employed (E)-3-(anthracen-9-yl)acrylic acid (aaa) as the core scaffold and designed the main ligand (E)-3-(anthracen-9-yl)-N-(pyridin-4-yl)acrylamide (3-apaa)(Supplementary Figs. 1, 2 and 4, Supplementary Methods), and applied a coordination-template strategy to precisely regulate its stacking arrangement37,38. Through solvothermal synthesis, we obtained two two-dimensional (2D) coordination polymers (CPs): {[Cd0.5(3-apaa)(ip)0.5]·1.5H2O}n (CP1, H2ip=Isophthalic acid) and {[Cd(3-apaa)2(MeOip)]·0.625(CH3OH)·1.25(H2O)}n (CP2, H2MeOip = 5-methoxyisophthalic acid). The coordination framework effectively fixes the 3-apaa ligand in a favorable head-to-head configuration, achieving an efficient and highly selective [6+6] cycloaddition reaction under visible light or daylight irradiation (Fig. 1). It is worth noting that both CP1 and CP2 exhibit distinct photosalient (PS) effects under light irradiation. Moreover, the introduction of methoxy substituents in CP2 further endows it with temperature response behavior, resulting in thermosalient (TS) effects. Furthermore, this study provides an in-depth analysis of the structure–activity relationships governing the light- and heat-induced responses of the crystal. By integrating CP1 and CP2 into PVA and PVA/MXene composite matrices, we amplified their photo- and thermally responsive behaviors. In summary, this work provides insights into the realization of higher-order cycloaddition reactions and opens avenues for the design and synthesis of intelligent functional materials.Fig. 1Schematic illustration.The optical dimerization structure transition from CP1 and CP2 to CP1a and CP2a. Results

Orange crystals of CP1 were obtained from solvothermal the reaction of CdSO4·8/3 H2O, 3-apaa, and H2ip (Methods). Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) analysis revealed that CP1 crystallizes in the Orthorhombic system, space group Cmce (Supplementary Table 1). The asymmetric unit contains one [Cd0.5(3-apaa)(ip)] moiety (Supplementary Fig. 5a). Each Cd2+ adopts a distorted octahedral [CdN2O4] coordination geometry, coordinated by two nitrogen atoms from two 3-apaa ligands and four oxygen atoms from three H2ip ligands. Adjacent Cd2+ ions are bridged by two H2ip ligands forming a secondary building unit (SBU) of [Cd2(3-apaa)2(ip)2] through end-to-end coordination with 3-apaa. These SBUs are further connected via chelating carboxylate groups of H2ip ligands, generating a 2D layered structure that extends along the bc plane (Supplementary Fig. 5b). The alkenes in adjacent 3-apaa are interlaced with each other, resulting in a geometric arrangement unfavorable for [2+2] cycloaddition. The distance between neighboring anthracene groups is 3.825 Å (<4.2 Å), and the distance between adjacent alkenes of anthracene groups is 4.095 Å (<4.2 Å), which satisfies the Schmidt’s topochemical criteria39–41. These structural features provide the potential for [6+6] cycloaddition reactions between adjacent anthracene units.

The solid-state UV–vis absorption spectrum of CP1 revealed a strong absorption band between 360 and 490 nm (Supplementary Fig. 7). The photoreactivity of CP1 was investigated using a custom-built photoreactor (λ = 475 nm). Upon visible light irradiation, the crystals broke apart, indicating the occurrence of a photoreaction (Fig. 2a). The reaction process was monitored by 1H NMR spectroscopy and PXRD (Supplementary Figs. 8a and 10a). In the 1H NMR spectrum, as light exposure time extended, the original olefinic proton signals at 6.78 and 8.28 ppm decreased, completely, accompanied by the emergence of a new olefinic signal at 6.60 ppm. Concurrently, cycloalkane proton signals appeared in the 4.60–5.10 ppm range. These results indicated that compound CP1 underwent a higher-order cycloaddition reaction, producing the cyclododecatetraene product {[Cd0.5(L1)0.5(ip)0.5]·1.5H2O}n (CP1a, L1 = (5E,7 R,8 R,9E,15 R,16S)-N7,N8-di(pyridin-4-yl)-7,8,15,16-tetrahydro-5,16:10,15-bis([1,2]benzeno)dibenzo[a,e][12]annulene-7,8-dicarboxamide). The reaction reached a 97% yield (using the proton of H of H2ip as an internal reference) after 210 min. PXRD data further confirmed the purity of the sample (Supplementary Fig. 10a). In the FT-IR spectrum, the blue-shift of the C=O stretching vibration at 1680 cm−1, the attenuation of the C=C–H characteristic peak at 989 cm−1, and the appearance of peaks at 1341 cm−1 and 682 cm−1 assigned to the out-of-plane and in-plane C–H bending vibrations of the bridge moiety in the anthracene cycloadduct dimer, all indicated the occurrence of the cycloaddition reaction (Supplementary Fig. 13). Moreover, the changes in the UV–vis absorption spectra and the band gap values (Eg) also verified the successful cycloaddition (Supplementary Fig. 7). To investigate the photoreaction process in detail, the structure of CP1a was determined by SCXRD. CP1a crystallizes in the Orthorhombic system, space group Cmce (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 11), which is consistent with CP1. We analyzed the bond lengths and angles of the cycloaddition product (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 17). The newly formed C1–C1’ (1.640 Å) and C8–C8’ (1.608 Å) bonds are consistent with the steric congestion and ring strain of the complex bridged macrocyclic system. The shortening of C6–C7 and elongation of C7–C8 reflect the expected C=C double-bond shift. The internal angles around the newly formed sp3 carbons deviate from the ideal tetrahedral angle (109.5°) due to macrocyclic constraints. Overall, these structural parameters are fully consistent with the expected geometry of the [6+6] photocycloaddition product. By breaking the coordination bond and hydrolyzing the amide bond, the dodecyl cyclic pyridine derivative L1 (Supplementary Methods), and the dodecyl cyclic carboxylic acid derivative (7 R,8S)−7,8,15,16-tetrahydro-5,16:10,15-bis([1,2]benzeno)dibenzo[a,e][12]annulene-7,8-dicarboxylic acid (L2), were successfully isolated respectively. All the isolated products were confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance, mass spectrometry (Supplementary Figs. 14–16).Fig. 2Photosalient effect of the crystal.Optical microscopic images of CP1 (a) and CP2 (b) exposed to visible light and optical images of CP1 (c) and CP2 (d) exposed to sunlight.

To demonstrate the universality and functionalization of the coordination template in achieving [6+6] cycloaddition, based on our previous research42,43, H2MeOip with a flexible alkoxy group was used to replace H2ip, and the yellow crystal CP2 was successfully synthesized (Methods). Single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) analysis revealed that CP2 crystallizes in the monoclinic system, space group C2/c (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 6). Its structure is similar to CP1. Due to the introduction of methoxy groups, the relationship between the 2D [Cd(MeOip)2]n-plane and 3-apaa is 94.918° in CP2, rather than the vertical relationship in CP1. Under visible light irradiation, it can also undergo a [6+6] photocycloaddition reaction to form [Cd2(L1)(MeOip)]n (CP2a)(Supplementary Fig. 9). To explore the specific photoreaction process, we determined the structure of CP2a via SCXRD. CP2a crystallizes in the Orthorhombic system, space group Cmce (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 12). Meanwhile, the changes in bond lengths and angles are similar to those in CP1, consistent with the expected product structure of the [6+6] cycloaddition (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Fig. 17). Notably, the cell volume of CP2 is approximately twice that of CP2a, indicating that the phase transition is driven by the photodimerization process.

Motivated by the series of optomechanical responses exhibited by CP1 and CP2 during their respective photoreactions, we performed further observations via optical microscopy. CP1 and CP2 crystallize as lamellar sheets, with their largest crystal plane indexed as (010) (the ac-plane), perpendicular to the b-axis (Supplementary Figs. 23a and 24a). Upon visible light irradiation, CP1 and CP2 will crack within 20 s, producing uniform cracks parallel to their edges (Fig. 2a, c, Supplementary Movies 1 and 3). Over time, the number of cracks increased progressively. We further examined the photochemical reactions of CP1 and CP2 crystals under white LED light and sunlight were studied (Fig. 2b, d, Supplementary Fig. 18, Supplementary Movies 2 and 4). Interestingly, the crystals also cracked within 1 h when exposed to white LED light and sunlight (outdoors, sunny, 10:00–14:00), indicating that CP1 and CP2 has photosaliency. It was observed that the cracks caused by light were uniform and all parallel to the shorter edge of the crystal (Supplementary Fig. 19). Through SEM characterization, the crystal’s cracks were flat and the cross-section was smooth (Supplementary Fig. 20). It was indicated that the [6+6] photocycloaddition process can be effectively applied to the precise and non-destructive cutting of crystals.

The optomechanical effect of CP1 and CP2 is well understood to arise from changes in the internal structure and arrangement of the polymer during their respective photocycloaddition process. To further elucidate the mechanism of light-induced crystal motion, we analyzed structural changes using confocal microscopy. Crystal structure analysis indicates that for CP1, the separation between adjacent planes along the a-axis decreases from 17.52 to 17.28 Å, the distance between adjacent Cd atoms along the b-axis increases from 3.831 to 3.931 Å, and the distance between adjacent carboxyl groups C coordinated with adjacent Cd decreases from 5.167 to 5.096 Å. (Fig. 3a). Such structural rearrangements generate significant internal stress, which is released through mechanical motion, causing the crystal to break parallel along the short sides. For CP2, this cycloaddition reaction not only caused changes in the internal molecular structure of the crystal but also led to the redefinition of its crystallographic axes. Specifically, the b-axis in CP2 became the c-axis in CP2a. Meanwhile, the distance between adjacent planes decreased from 17.63 to 17.44 Å, the distance between adjacent Cd atoms along the c-axis increased from 4.031 to 4.140 Å and the distance between adjacent carboxyl groups C coordinated with adjacent Cd was reduced from 4.995 to 4.891 Å (Fig. 3b). These structural and organizational changes in the crystal generate considerable internal stress, which is relieved through mechanical motion, causing the crystal to fracture perpendicular to the b-axis plane (Fig. 3c). Laser scanning confocal microscopy (LSCM) further revealed that under visible light irradiation, internal changes initiated at the side of the crystal closest to the light source and then propagated along the b-axis into the crystal until the reaction was complete (Fig. 3d and Supplementary Figs. 21–25).Fig. 3Structural evolution and splitting mechanism of crystals upon light irradiation.The changes in the distances between adjacent planes, adjacent Cd atoms, and adjacent carboxyl groups C coordinated with adjacent Cd during cycloaddition of CP1 (a) and CP2 (b). c The mechanism of crystal splitting during the photocycloaddition process. d The internal changes of crystals after exposure to light.

The reversible photo-induced cycloaddition of the crystal was further investigated using differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) (Supplementary Fig. 26). The DSC curves of CP1a and CP2a exhibited a distinct phase transition peaks at 461 K and 463 K, which can be attributed to the cleavage of the 12-membered carbon ring in its molecular structure, thereby regenerating C=C double bonds. This demonstrates that CP1a and CP2a can undergo a thermal ring-cleavage reaction at a specific temperature. To confirm this, CP1a and CP2a were heated to 473 K and maintained at this temperature for 6 h. 1H NMR and PXRD analyses verified that the CP1a underwent a complete ring-opening reaction, converting completely back to CP1 (Supplementary Fig. 27). Interestingly, the PXRD pattern of CP2a-473 K (generated via ring cleavage) differed from that of the original CP2 (Supplementary Fig. 28). This observation prompted further investigation into the thermal behavior of CP2. DSC measurements over different temperature ranges revealed distinct thermal events. From 300 to 425 K, a broad endothermic peak associated with solvent removal was observed at 345 K, followed by a baseline endothermic drift at 369 K during heating. These results suggest that CP2 undergoes a heat-induced structural transformation caused by solvent removal within this temperature range. Notably, the absence of corresponding peaks during cooling or subsequent cycles indicates that this transformation is irreversible (Supplementary Fig. 29b). In DSC measurements over the 100–300-K range, at the beginning of the second cycle, the sample’s heat absorption and release reach a stable state, an exothermic peak was observed at 280 K during cooling, accompanied by a corresponding endothermic peak at 280 K during heating. This reversible thermal behavior indicates that CP2 undergoes a continuous, reversible low-temperature crystal structural transition (Supplementary Fig. 29a).

The thermal saliency of CP2 was examined via optical microscopy. During heating, the crystal exhibited displacement at 323 K, accompanied by the formation of cracks parallel to its short edges (Fig. 4b). As the temperature increased to 333 K, additional cracks perpendicular to the short edges appeared. To further investigate the crystal’s low-temperature thermal response, we cooled the crystal in liquid nitrogen to create a low-temperature environment. As shown in Fig. 4a, cracks began to form in the crystal after 30 s, and by 60 s, partial fragmentation and movement of the crystal were observed. To elucidate the mechanism underlying thermomechanical motion–driven crystal structural changes, the unit cell parameters of the crystal CP2 were systematically collected and analyzed using variable-temperature single-crystal X-ray diffraction (VT-SCXRD) over the 120–420-K range, with data recorded at 20-K intervals (Fig. 5a). Between 120 and 340 K, the β-angle gradually decreased, stabilizing at 90° when the temperature reached 340 K, indicating that CP2 undergoes a structural transition upon heating (Supplementary Table 1, Supplementary Fig. 31). The PXRD pattern of CP2c (CP2−413 K) was consistent with that of CP2a-473 K generated via ring cleavage, further confirming the temperature responsiveness of CP2 (Supplementary Fig. 28b). Moreover, the PXRD pattern of CP2c-UV, obtained after irradiation of CP2c, matched that of CP2a, indicating that the [6+6] photocycloaddition reaction is reversible. Temperature-dependent structural variations were further analyzed by comparing the crystal structures of CP2 at 100 (CP2b), 298 (CP2) and 413 K (CP2c) (Fig. 5, Supplementary Table 1). Under low-temperature conditions (100–298 K), the crystal retained its original space groups. Upon heating, however, the monoclinic C2/c space group transformed into an orthogonal Cmce space group. This structural change is deeply associated with the synergistic effect of desolvation and skeletal rearrangement. As shown in thermogravimetric analysis (Supplementary Fig. 30), guest solvent molecules are released sequentially, first the smaller H2O molecules, followed by MeOH molecules confined by steric hindrance. The loss of solvent provides sufficient spatial freedom for the conformational rotation and vibrational adjustment of the alkoxy groups, leading to the irreversible transformation from CP2 to CP2c at elevated temperatures. When viewed along the c-axis, the [Cd(MeOip)2] planes exhibit a distinct corrugated structure at 100 K, with an angle of 3.411° between the MeOip2− plane and the [Cd(MeOip)2] plane. As the temperature increases, the [Cd(MeOip)2] planes gradually flatten, and the angle between the MeOip2− plane and the[Cd(MeOip)2] plane gradually decreases, until it is completely transformed into CP2c, and the Cd2+ ion forms a coplanar arrangement with the MeOip2− ligand (Supplementary Figs. 34–36, Supplementary Table 3). This conformational adjustment likely provides a structural basis for the observed intercrystal system transition of the space group.

Based on the classification of thermosalient crystals proposed by Panče Naumov, CP2 can be categorized as a Class I crystal44. Crystals in this class typically possess flat and rigid molecular structures, lack strong hydrogen-bonding groups, and adopt layered packing arrangements (e.g., pentenes and substituted benzenes). Their intermolecular interactions are generally weak, and thermal saliency arises when thermally induced strain exceeds the strength of interlayer interactions. VT-SCXRD data indicate that the temperature response of CP2 is intrinsically anisotropic. For this monoclinic system, the PASCal program was employed to calculate the thermal expansion coefficients and further investigate the crystal’s temperature-dependent behavior45,46. During cooling, analysis of crystal data in the 200–120-K range revealed that CP2 exhibits positive thermal expansion along the primary X1-axis [103], while negative thermal expansion occurs along the X2-axis [010] and X3-axis [−102] (Supplementary Tables 4 and 5, Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 37). During heating, using data in the 360–400-K range, CP2 transforms into the orthorhombic system, and the thermal expansion coefficients align with the changes in the crystal axes. Specifically, due to the increase in lattice symmetry, the oscillation of the crystal structure is suppressed, and positive thermal expansion phenomena are observed in the X1 axis [010], X2 axis [100], and X3 axis [001] directions of the crystal (Supplementary Tables 6 and 7, Fig. 5b and Supplementary Fig. 38). This change in crystal system generates new vertical cracks during heating, which are absent during cooling (Fig. 5c).

Although CP1 and CP2 has been confirmed to exhibit multi-stimuli-responsive properties, translating its mechanical motion into controllable responses to light and temperature for applications in areas such as soft robotics and biomedicine remains challenging. Hydrophilic polyvinyl alcohol (PVA), with abundant –OH groups, can interact with CP1 and CP2 through hydrogen bonds31,46. Meanwhile, MXene is known for its high photothermal conversion efficiency and good thermal conductivity, enabling it to absorb infrared radiation and locally increase the temperature32,33. Based on these properties, we mixed CP1 crystal powder with PVA to prepare the stimulus-responsive actuating element CP1-PVA. Additionally, we mixed CP2 crystal powder, PVA and Ti3C2TxMXene to fabricate the macroscopic stimulus-responsive actuating element CP2-PVA/MXene (Supplementary Methods). The SEM and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDX) analyses of the CP1-PVA and CP2-PVA/MXene composite film confirmed the formation of a homogeneous composite film (Supplementary Figs. 39 and 40). Upon irradiation with visible light and white LED light, the CP1-PVA films exhibited backlight bending, reaching the maximum bending angles at 120 s and 280 s, respectively (Fig. 6a and Supplementary Fig. 41a, Supplementary Movies 5 and 6). For CP2-PVA/MXene, it reached the maximum backlight bending at 24 s and 40 s, respectively. (Fig. 6b and Supplementary Fig. 41b, Supplementary Movies 7 and 8). As expected, upon absorbing infrared radiation, MXene efficiently increased the temperature of CP2 crystal in the CP2-PVA/MXene film. This triggered a temperature-responsive phase transition in CP2, resulting in backlight bending of the composite film, which reached its maximum bending angle at 120 s (Fig. 6c, Supplementary Movie 9). Liquid nitrogen was introduced into a centrifuge tube to create a low-temperature environment. When the liquid nitrogen approached the CP2-PVA/MXene film, the composite film bent toward it. Upon removal of liquid nitrogen, the composite film could quickly return to a vertical orientation, consistent with the reversible low-temperature structural changes observed in CP2 crystals (Fig. 6d and Supplementary Fig. 42, Supplementary Movie 10). Discussion

In summary, we devised a strategy for achieving higher-order cycloaddition reactions involving more than 8π electrons with the aid of coordination templates, and, based on this approach, we constructed stimulus-responsive macroscopic actuators. In CP1 constructed with isophthalic acid coordination modules, the arrangement of adjacent 3-apaa ligands is constrained by the crystal structure, enabling selective [6+6] photocycloaddition reaction under irradiation with visible light (λ = 475 nm) or sunlight, rather than the traditionally [2+2] or [4+4] photocycloaddition reactions. The CP2 obtained by introducing the methoxy functional group onto isophthalic acid not only maintains the [6+6] photocycloaddition activity but also exhibits temperature response characteristics, verifying the universality and functionalization potential of this template strategy Using these CPs, the composite films CP1-PVA and CP2-PVA/MXene constructed by integrating PVA and MXene can achieve macroscopic amplification of response behavior. This study represents the realization of a template-assisted [6+6] photocycloaddition, marking a significant advance in template-directed reactivity, and provides a promising platform for the precise construction and in-depth study of complex functional cyclic and macrocyclic compounds as well as for the development of multi-stimuli-responsive smart materials.

Methods

Synthesis of {[Cd0.5(3-apaa)(ip)0.5]·1.5H2O}n (CP1)

The concentration of HNO3 (1 M, 0.10 mL), CdSO4·8/3H2O (0.01 mmol, 2.56 mg), isophthalic acid (0.01 mmol, 1.70 mg), 3-apaa (0.01 mmol, 4.23 mg), 1 mL of H2O and MeOH were successively added into a hard glass tube (15 cm in length and 7 mm in diameter). The sealed glass tube was reacted at 100 °C for 24 h and cooled to ambient temperature at a rate of 3 °C/min to generate orange flaky crystals, which were collected by filtration, washed with H2O, and dried in air.

Synthesis of {[Cd(3-apaa)2(MeOip)]·0.625(CH3OH)·1.25(H2O)}n (CP2)

The conditions for synthesis of CP2 were similar to those for synthesis of CP1, and isophthalic acid was replaced by 5-Methoxyisophthalic acid (0.01 mmol, 1.96 mg).

Synthesis of {[Cd0.5(L1)0.5(ip)0.5]·1.5H2O}n (CP1a)

Orange crystals CP1 (0.20 g) were irradiated under visible light (λ = 475 nm) for 210 min to obtain orange crystal CP1a with a quantitative yield.

Synthesis of [Cd2(L1)(MeOip)]n (CP2a)

Yellow crystals CP2 (0.20 g) were irradiated under visible light (λ = 475 nm) for 390 min to obtain yellow crystal CP2a with a quantitative yield.
