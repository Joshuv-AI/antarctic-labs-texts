GSDME-N-induced mitochondrial neurotoxicity in early neurodegeneration was suppressed by nicotine via enhancing autophagic flux
Authors: Junjun Zhao, Saitao Qiu, Angyang Guo, Haixia Huang, Wei Wang, Jianliang Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNeurodegeneration is a chronic, progressive process initiated by early neurite retraction, a pathological hallmark preceding neuronal death in neurodegenerative disorders such as Parkinson’s disease (PD). Cleavage of gasdermin E (GSDME) releases its N-terminal domain (GSDME-N), which has been shown to mediate mitochondrial dysfunction and the onset of neurodegeneration. However, the therapeutic potential of targeting GSDME-N for early intervention in PD, and whether nicotine, a tobacco component with neuroprotective properties, acts through GSDME modulation remain unexplored.MethodsThe SH-SY5Y cells, primary neurons and C57BL mice were treated with rotenone to model neurodegeneration in PD. Mitochondrial accumulation of GSDME-N and activation of autophagy were assessed via immunoblotting and immunostaining. Mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using JC-1 and TMRM fluorescent dyes, while mitochondrial reactive oxygen species were detected with MitoSOX™ Red superoxide indicator. For morphological analysis, immunofluorescence staining was performed using antibodies against microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) to visualize neurites, along with MitoTracker to label mitochondria within neurites. In vivo, C57BL/6 mice were administered rotenone and provided with nicotine supplementation in their drinking water. Motor function was assessed using rotarod, hanging wire and pole tests. Tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive neurons and fibers were detected using immunohistochemistry.ResultsWe demonstrated that GSDME-N accumulation on mitochondria during early neurodegeneration was suppressed by nicotine, thereby maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and preventing neurite retraction. Mechanistically, nicotine activated autophagic pathway to promote GSDME-N clearance, which maintained mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and specifically rescued mitochondrial function. This protective mechanism significantly attenuated early pathological changes, including neurite retraction and loss. In a rotenone-induced PD model, nicotine treatment effectively reduced GSDME-N accumulation and decelerated neurodegenerative progression.ConclusionsThese findings reveal the critical role of GSDME-N in early-stage mitochondrial damage in PD and propose a novel therapeutic strategy targeting the nicotine-autophagy axis to counteract GSDME-N-mediated neurodegeneration.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07247-y.Keywords: GSDME, Neurodegeneration, Nicotine, Autophagy

Introduction

Neurodegeneration is a chronic, progressive process characterized by the gradual deterioration of neuronal structure and function [1]. A hallmark of this process is neurite retraction, which precedes overt neuronal death across various neurodegenerative disorders, including Parkinson’s disease (PD) [2]. Early in neurodegeneration, mitochondrial dysfunction emerges as a key pathological feature, driving the release of cytochrome c, which subsequently activates caspase-3 [3]. Notably, caspase-3 exhibits compartmentalized activation within neurites, where it initiates neurite retraction prior to cell death [4, 5]. Once activated, caspase-3 proteolytically processes multiple downstream substrates, including inhibitor of caspase-activated DNAse (ICAD), Rho-associated coiled-coil containing protein kinase 1 (Rock1), Gasdermin E (GSDME), resulting in DNA fragmentation, cell shrinkage and membrane rupture [6–8]. Caspase-3-mediated cleavage of downstream substrates may amplify local mitochondrial collapse and neurite degeneration, representing a critical mechanism driving the progression from early neurodegenerative changes to ultimate cell demise.

GSDME, a downstream molecule cleaved by caspase-3, is predominantly enriched in neuron and belongs to the pore-forming gasdermin superfamily that is associated with pyroptotic cell death [9, 10]. The gasdermin proteins possess the pore-forming N-terminal domains that are masked by their C-terminal domains [11]. Proteolytic cleavage between the N and C domains released the N-terminal of GSDME (GSDME-N), which induced pyroptotic cell death in renal, gastrointestinal, and cancer cells through plasma membrane pore formation, cellular swelling, and inflammatory mediator release [12–14]. In contrast to these non-neuronal cells, GSDME activation in neuron primarily tended to occur locally on mitochondria in neurites, much earlier than the apparent damage on the plasma membrane. These GSDME-N triggered mitochondrial dysfunction and subsequent neurite loss, which were regarded as an early step in neurodegeneration [15]. Since GSDME-N exhibited a pronounced affinity for binding to the mitochondrial membrane, preceding the subsequent rupture of the plasma membrane [16], the removal of these cleaved GSDMEs from the mitochondria could be a promising strategy for halting the progression of neurodegeneration and enabling early intervention in PD.

Nicotine, a psychoactive compound derived from tobacco leaves, has been reported to exhibit neuroprotective properties [17]. Epidemiological studies have demonstrated that smoking has the potential to reduce the incidence of PD mortality [18–22]. Despite the adverse health effects of smoking, the reduction in PD risk appears to depend on both the duration and intensity of tobacco use [23]. Notably, evidence indicates that the protective effect of smoking predominantly occurs prior to PD diagnosis [24, 25], implying the involvement of a nicotine-mediated pathway that acts before clinical symptoms emerge. However, the precise mechanism through which nicotine confers protection in PD remains unclear, and it is still unknown whether nicotine can regulate GSDME-N for early intervention.

Studies have revealed that nicotine can upregulate autophagy-related proteins and other protective factors [26–28]. Macroautophagy, also known as autophagy, is a major intracellular degradation pathway responsible for clearing large protein aggregates and damaged organelles. This process involves the formation of double-membrane vesicles termed autophagosomes and the subsequent fusion of the autophagosomes with lysosomes for degradation [29]. Autophagy defects have been associated with the accumulation of inclusion bodies and neurodegeneration [30, 31]. Therefore, pharmacological induction of autophagy by nicotine should represent a promising therapeutic approach for reducing the accumulation of GSDME-N and alleviating GSDME-N-induced mitochondrial neurotoxicity to mitigate neurodegenerative progression.

In this study, we demonstrated that GSDME-N accumulation on mitochondria in early-stage neurodegeneration was mitigated by nicotine, thereby maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and preventing neurite degeneration. Through activating the autophagic pathway, nicotine promoted GSDME-N clearance, which maintained mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and specifically rescued mitochondrial function. The neuroprotective mechanism effectively mitigated early neurodegenerative pathology, including neurite retraction and degeneration. In a rotenone-induced PD model, nicotine treatment effectively reduced GSDME-N accumulation and decelerated neurodegenerative progression. Our findings position GSDME-N as a promising therapeutic target for early-stage neurodegeneration and nicotine as a promising therapeutic candidate capable of targeting GSDME-N-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction to intervene in early-stage neurodegeneration.

Materials and methods

Antibodies and reagents

Anti-GSDME (ab215191) and anti-MAP2 (ab32454) antibodies were purchased from Abcam. Anti-p62 (A19700) and anti-PINK1 (A7131) were obtained from ABclonal. Anti-LC3B (L7543) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Anti-Parkin (sc-32282) was obtained from Santa Cruz. Anti-β-actin (G1001S, GXYbio) was purchased from GXYbio in Beijing. HRP-conjugated anti-Mouse and anti-Rabbit were purchased from Jackson. Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Rotenone (HY-B1756), 3-Methyladenine (HY-19312) and MitoSOX Red (HY-D1055) were purchased from MedChemExpress. Molecular probe JC-1 (420200) was purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, C11995599BT, Gibco), neurobasal medium (21103-049, Gibco), B-27 supplement (17504-044, Gibco), GlutaMAX (35050-061, Gibco), MitoTracker™ Red CMXRos (M7512) and propidium iodide (PI, P1304MP) were purchased from Thermo Fisher Scientific. Fetal bovine serum (MN012103, Mengma, China) was purchased from Mengmabio. Horse serum (S9050), penicillin-streptomycin liquid (P1400) and DNase I (D8071) were purchased from Solarbio. LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit (C0017) and Cell Mitochondria Isolation Kit (C3601) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (China). RIPA buffer (C1053) and 5 x SDS-PAGE protein sample loading buffer (B1012) were purchased from Applygen (China). BCA protein assay kit (ZJ102) and Omni-ECL™ Pico Light chemiluminescence Kit (SQ202L-2) were purchased from Epizyme (China). ProtLytic Protease and Phosphatase Inhibitor Cocktail (P002) was purchased from NCM Biotech (China). Mouse two-step detection Kit (PV-9002) and DAB colour reagent kit were purchased from Zhongshan Golden Bridge.

Animals

Eight-week-old male C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Vital River Laboratories (Beijing, China) and housed in microisolator cages under a 12-hour light/dark cycle. All of the experimental procedures were performed according to the guidelines of the Capital Medical University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee guidelines (Approval No.: AEEI-2022-197). The mice were randomly divided into three groups. Rotenone (4 mg/kg) was dissolved in DMSO and diluted using olive oil, and injected intraperitoneally, daily for two weeks as previously described [32]. The control group received the vehicle containing DMSO and olive oil. For nicotine administration, nicotine was added to tap water to create a concentration of 200 µg per milliliter [33].

Cell culture and treatment

Human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y cells were cultured in DMEM medium supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin liquid at 37 °C in a humidified incubator (Thermo Scientific, Germany) supplied with 5% CO2. For cell transfection, a mixture of plasmid GFP-GSDME and polyethylenimine (PEI) was added to the cultured cells for 24 h. For nicotine and/or rotenone treatment, nicotine (10 µM) was pretreated with SH-SY5Y cells for 1 h, and the rotenone (1 µM) was added to the cells for 12 h.

Primary cortical neuron culture and treatment

Embryonic mouse cortices from E15 C57BL/6 mice were dissected and digested with DNase I and trypsin in PBS at 37℃ for 15 min. The digested neurons were filtered with a nylon filter (FSTR040, Beyotime) and seeded with DMEM medium containing 5% FBS, 5% horse serum and 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin Liquid. After 4–6 h, the medium was replaced with neurobasal medium containing 2% B-27 supplement, 1% Penicillin-Streptomycin Liquid and 1% GlutaMAX. Half of the medium was replaced every 3 days. The neurons were cultured for 7–10 days. For cortical neuron transfection, plasmid GFP-GSDME was mixed with Lipofectamine 2000 (11668019, Invitrogen) and incubated with the neurons for 2 h at 37 °C. The medium was then replaced with Neurobasal/B27 growth medium for 2 more days. For nicotine and/or rotenone treatment, nicotine (10 µM) was incubated with neurons for 1 h, and the rotenone (0.05 µM) was added to the neurons for 12–24 h.

Mitochondrial fractionation

Mitochondria were isolated from SH-SY5Y cells using the Cell Mitochondria Isolation Kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, cells were digested with 0.25% trypsin and centrifuged at 200 g for 5 min at room temperature to collect cells. Then, cells were resuspended in pre-chilled PBS and centrifuged at 600 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. The cells were resuspended in Mito-Cyto extraction buffer and ground 30–40 times on ice. The cell lysate was centrifuged at 600 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and centrifuged at 11,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. The pellet was the mitochondrial fraction.

Western blotting (WB)

Samples were lysed in RIPA buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail. After sonication and centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and the protein concentration was measured using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein assay kit. Protein samples were mixed with 5 x loading buffer and boiled for 10 min. Samples of either mitochondrial and cytosolic extracts or total cell lysates, containing equal amounts of protein, were subjected to a 12.5% SDS-PAGE at 150 V, and then were transferred onto nitrocellulose (NC) and polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membranes for 80 min at 100 V. The proteins were blocked with 5% fat-free milk solution, incubated with the anti-GSDME, anti-p62, anti-LC3 B and anti-β-actin antibodies at 4 ℃ overnight. The blots were washed three times with TBST buffer for 10 min. The blots were then incubated with the corresponding HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies and subjected to Omni-ECL™ Pico Light Chemiluminescence Kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The western blotting images were analyzed using ImageJ software (NIH, USA).

Cell viability assays (MTT assays)

3-(4,5-dimethylthiazolyl-2)-2,5-diphenyltetrazoliumbromide (MTT, M5655, Sigma-Aldrich) is a water-soluble tetrazolium salt that can be converted into an insoluble purple formazan by mitochondrial succinate dehydrogenase. Cells were incubated with MTT (0.5 mg/mL) at 37 °C with 5% CO2 for 4 h. The absorbance of each sample was measured at 490 nm using a spectrophotometer (51119200, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Cell death assay (LDH release and PI uptake)

For lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release assay, the cell supernatant was assessed using the LDH Cytotoxicity Assay Kit in accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. The LDH release reagent was added to the wells as the maximum release, one hour before the desired endpoint for analysis. The supernatant was collected and incubated with LDH detection working solution at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance of each sample was measured at 490 nm.

Propidium iodide (PI) working solution was prepared by dissolving it in complete medium at a ratio of 1: 750. The cells were then incubated with the working solution at 37 ℃ for 20 min in a cell culture incubator. The nuclei were visualized with Hoechst. PI uptake was analyzed using the ArrayScan XTI High Content Analysis Reader (NX10002L, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and associated software (HCS Studio™ 2.0).

Measurement of mitochondrial membrane potential

The mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using JC-1 fluorescent probe. JC-1 is a membrane-permeant dye that selectively accumulates in the mitochondria and exhibits a shift in emission color from green to red as the mitochondrial membrane potential becomes more polarized. In healthy cells with high mitochondrial membrane potential, JC-1 accumulates within mitochondria and forms aggregates (J-aggregates) that emit intense red fluorescence. In cells with diminished mitochondrial membrane potential, JC-1 remains in its monomeric form in the cytoplasm, producing green fluorescence. The higher the ratio of red to green fluorescence, the higher the polarization of the mitochondrial membrane. The cells were then incubated with JC-1 staining working solution at 37℃ for 20 min in a cell culture incubator. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice with PBS and supplemented with culture medium. Images were captured using the ArrayScan XTI High Content Analysis Reader (NX10002L, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and associated software (HCS Studio™ 2.0). To measure the mitochondrial membrane potential in neurites, the mitochondria around the nucleus were first removed. Then, we used the plugin of Analyze particles in ImageJ software (NIH) to select the red puncta using ROI Manager. Finally, we analyzed the ratio of red to green intensity of the individual mitochondrial puncta.

The mitochondrial membrane potential was evaluated using TMRM (T668). Cells were incubated with 100 nM TMRM working solution for 30 min at 37 °C. The nuclei were visualized with Hoechst. Subsequently, the cells were washed twice with PBS. Images were captured using the LSM 880 Airyscan confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss).

Immunofluorescence and image analysis

Cortical neurons or SH-SY5Y cells were plated on polylysine-coated coverslips in 12-well plates and treated with rotenone and/or nicotine. The mitochondrial morphology and structure were evaluated using MitoTracker Red CMXRos (M7512, Thermo Fisher Scientific). The MitoTracker Red CMXRos was prepared by dissolving it with complete medium in 1: 5000. The cells were then incubated with the working solution at 37℃ for 20 min in a cell culture incubator. Cells were rinsed twice with PBS, fixed with a freshly prepared solution of 4% paraformaldehyde-sucrose in PBS for 5 min, and then permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 5 min. After washing with PBS three times, the cells were blocked with 5% BSA in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Then, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with antibodies against MAP2 or LC3B. After primary antibody incubation, the cells were washed and incubated with Alexa Fluor-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37 °C. Images were captured using the LSM 880 Airyscan confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss) using a 63x oil-immersion objective. The images were analyzed by ImageJ software (NIH). The length of neurites was measured using the plugin NeuronJ. The size of mitochondria in neurites was measured by Analyze particles. The colocalization was analyzed using the plugin JACoP or demonstrated by Plot Profiles.

Live cell imaging

Cortical neurons were cultured in glass-bottom dishes and imaged using the Zeiss LSM 880 Airyscan confocal laser scanning microscope equipped with a C-Apochromat 40x objective. Following treatment with nicotine and/or rotenone, mitochondria were labeled with MitoTracker Green (M7514, Thermo Fisher Scientific) for live-cell imaging. Images were captured every 6 s and time-lapse imaging was performed for around 5 min. Kymographs of selected neurites were generated and the mitochondrial motility was analyzed using Kymomaker software [34].

Mitochondrial ROS (mROS) measurement

mROS was measured using a MitoSOX™ Red mitochondrial superoxide indicator (HY-D1055, MedChemExpress) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cells were incubated with 5 µM MitoSOXTM reagent working solution for 20 min at 37 °C. After washing twice with PBS and supplementing with culture medium, we detected the ROS intensity with the LSM 880 Airyscan confocal laser scanning microscope (Zeiss). The nuclei were visualized with Hoechst. The fluorescence intensity was measured by ImageJ software (NIH).

Rotarod test

The rotarod test was used to assess the ability of motor coordination as previously described [35]. Briefly, training was performed for 3 days before the formal test. Mice were placed on the accelerating rod to test the time for mice staying on the rotating rod. The time that each mouse spent on the rod was recorded until it fell.

Wire hanging test

The wire hanging test aimed to evaluate muscle strength. A horizontal wire was suspended 50 cm above a foam carpet. The mouse was forced to grip the wire with its forelimbs. The hanging time on the wire was recorded until the mouse fell onto the foam carpet.

Pole test

The pole test was used to evaluate the motor coordination, balance, and bradykinesia as described previously [36]. The pole of 50 cm in length and 1 cm in diameter with a wooden ball on its top was set. The mouse was placed on the wooden ball to climb down to the bottom. The time until the mouse reached the ground was recorded.

Immunohistochemistry

After fix in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, the brains were transferred to 10%, 20%, and 30% sucrose in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 4 ℃. The coronal brain Sect. (40 μm) were made on a freezing sliding microtome (CM1860, Leica) and were collected in PBS. Next, the brain sections were permeabilized with 3% Triton X-100 (X100, Sigma-Aldrich) for 1 h and were immersed in 1× citrate buffer (AB64236, Abcam) at 95 °C for 10 min. After natural cooling to room temperature, the sections were washed three times with PBS for 5 min. The endogenous peroxidase of the brain section was quenched by incubation in endogenous peroxidase blocking agent for 20 min in the dark and the sections were washed three times with PBS for 5 min. The sections were blocked with 10% goat serum containing 0.3% Triton X-100 for 1 h. Then, the sections were incubated overnight at 4 ℃ with the primary antibodies against tyrosine hydroxylase (TH), (T1299, Sigma-Aldrich) in PBS containing 3% Triton X-100. After rinsing in PBS, the sections were treated with a reaction-enhancing solution for 20 min. Following washes in PBS, the sections were immersed in enhanced enzyme-linked goat anti-mouse lgG polymer for 1 h. After washing three times with PBS, the sections were incubated in DAB working solution for 4–5 min, then the DAB solution was aspirated. The colour development was terminated by adding tap water. The brain sections were mounted on glass slides and air-dried at ambient conditions. Sequential dehydration was performed through an ethanol gradient: 70% ethanol (3 min), 85% ethanol (5 min), 95% ethanol (7 min), followed by two 15-min immersions in absolute ethanol. Tissue clearing was achieved through two 20-min xylene treatments. The sections were mounted with neutral balsam (G8590, Solarbio) and scanned using a whole-slide imaging system (Aperio GT450, Leica). The TH positive neurons in substantia nigra and TH positive terminals in striatum were measured by ImageJ software (NIH).

Statistical analysis

Quantification and data analysis were performed with GraphPad Prism 10. Data were obtained from at least three independent experiments. All statistical values were displayed as means ± SEM. The statistical significance in this study was determined by Student’s t-test, One-way ANOVA or Two-way ANOVA. Differences were considered significant at p < 0.05.

Results

GSDME cleavage and mitochondrial dysfunction occurred before overt cell death

The primary neurons were exposed to rotenone, a toxin that targets the mitochondria and mimics neurodegeneration in PD [37]. Cell viability, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) release and propidium iodide (PI) uptake were measured after rotenone treatment with a series of concentration gradients at 12 h and 24 h. Rotenone induced a dose-dependent decrease in cell viability and increase in cytotoxicity (Fig. 1A and B and Supplementary Fig. 1). From these results, it is clear that rotenone at 0.05 µM induced only a slight decrease in cell viability without overt cell death, so we chose this condition to mimic the early stage of neurodegeneration. After rotenone (0.05 µM, 12–24 h) treatment, the cleavage of GSDME in primary neurons could be detected under a sublethal or pre-lethal condition and it also induced a significant increase in cleaved caspase-3 and GSDME-N in primary neurons after rotenone treatment (Fig. 1C), indicating that GSDME cleavage could occur before the obvious features of cell death appeared.

To test whether this detectable cleavage of GSDME was accompanied by mitochondrial dysfunction, a mitochondrial membrane potential assay was performed using JC-1. It revealed a significant decrease in the ratio of red/green fluorescence intensity in the rotenone treatment group in comparison to the control group (Fig. 1D), suggesting that rotenone treatment could induce the decrease in mitochondrial transmembrane potential. Furthermore, a significant increase in the GSDME-N domain was observed after rotenone treatment (0.5, 1, 10 µM; 12 h) in the human neuroblastoma cell line SH-SY5Y (Fig. 1E). Meanwhile, a significant decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential was detected after rotenone treatment (Fig. 1F), which did not result in LDH release under the experimental conditions (Supplementary Fig. 2), suggesting that rotenone under these conditions still did not cause obvious cell death. Collectively, these results showed that the used rotenone could induce GSDME cleavage and mitochondrial dysfunction, not with the evident hallmarks of cell death, mimicking the early stage of neurodegeneration.

Nicotine prevented GSDME-N accumulation on the mitochondria and rescued the mitochondrial dysfunction

To test the effect of GSDME-N on mitochondrial function, GFP tagged GSDME-N was overexpressed to label its location, and MitoTracker Red was utilized to stain the active mitochondria in SH-SY5Y cells. Confocal microscopy revealed that a large proportion of GFP-GSDME-N formed intracellular puncta and accumulated on mitochondria, whereas the full-length GFP-GSDME exhibited no discernible colocalization with MitoTracker Red and was distributed diffusely throughout the cell (Fig. 2A), suggesting that GSDME-N had a propensity to form puncta on the mitochondria. Moreover, subsequent to an extended period of transfection, a great percentage of GSDME-N was found to be localized to the plasma membrane (Supplementary Fig. 3A), implying that GSDME-N initially accumulated on active mitochondria.

To further test whether the accumulation of GSDME-N on mitochondria mediated mitochondrial damage, we stained transfected cells with Tetramethylrhodamine (TMRM) or MitoSOX to detect the mitochondrial membrane potential and mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mROS) prior to the onset of cell death. The results demonstrated a decrease in TMRM intensity and an increase in MitoSOX signal in the GFP-GSDME-N group, while the full-length GFP-GSDME group exhibited no obvious change. These observations suggested that GSDME-N binding to mitochondria led to a reduction in mitochondrial membrane potential and an increase in mROS in SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 2B and C). Collectively, these data suggested GSDME-N could accumulate on the mitochondria and induce mitochondrial dysfunction before the obvious cell death.

To test whether nicotine could serve as a preventative measure against mitochondrial dysfunction induced by rotenone, the SH-SY5Y cells were subjected to a range of nicotine doses. The results demonstrated that nicotine treatment could prevent the decrease of mitochondrial membrane potential induced by rotenone administration (Fig. 2D), indicating the protective role of nicotine on mitochondrial function. To determine whether nicotine could exert the protective function through targeting GSDME, we first compared the GSDME cleavage level in the presence of nicotine after rotenone treatment. We found a significant decrease in GSDME-N in the cell lysate after nicotine treatment (10, 50 µM), compared with the rotenone group, indicating that nicotine could downregulate the overall GSDME-N level in cell (Fig. 2E).

To investigate whether nicotine modulated rotenone-induced mitochondrial accumulation of GSDME-N, we expressed N-terminally GFP-tagged GSDME in SH-SY5Y cells. Confocal microscopy revealed that rotenone treatment triggered the formation of intracellular puncta, indicative of GFP-GSDME cleavage. These puncta colocalized with MitoTracker Red, confirming mitochondrial localization, and this effect was attenuated by nicotine (Fig. 2F and G). Furthermore, prolonged rotenone treatment led to GSDME enrichment in the plasma membrane, accompanied by cell ballooning (Supplementary Fig. 3B). To validate these findings, the mitochondrial fraction from SH-SY5Y cells was isolated, and it was demonstrated that nicotine significantly reduced mitochondrial GSDME-N levels compared to rotenone treatment (Fig. 2H). Overall, these results demonstrated that nicotine could reduce the accumulation of GSDME-N on mitochondrial fractions and exert neuroprotective effects.

Autophagy pathway was involved in the suppression of GSDME-N binding to mitochondria by nicotine

Unlike the ubiquitin-proteasome system which primarily targets soluble proteins, autophagy specializes in eliminating aggregated membrane proteins and compromised mitochondria. To determine whether nicotine could clear GSDME through autophagy pathway, SH-SY5Y cells were transfected with GFP-GSDME and subsequently labelled with autophagy-related marker LC3B and MitoTracker Red. The results demonstrated that nicotine induced the colocalization of LC3B puncta and GFP-GSDME puncta on the mitochondria (Fig. 3A), implying the involvement of the autophagy pathway for the degradation of GSDME puncta. Western blot analysis revealed that the conversion of LC3 from the soluble form (LC3-I) to the lower migrating autophagosome-associated form (LC3-II) was enhanced by nicotine treatment, accompanied with the degradation of P62, a marker for general autophagy. Particularly, rotenone-induced GSDME-N was significantly decreased by nicotine, and 3-MA, the autophagy inhibitor, could reverse the decrease of GSDME-N and the alteration of autophagy-related proteins in SH-SY5Y cells (Fig. 3B), indicating the involvement of autophagy pathway. Moreover, nicotine treatment increased the accumulation of PINK1 and Parkin in mitochondria, suggesting the enhanced PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagic flux (Supplementary Fig. 4).

Given that 3-MA inhibited GSDME-N degradation, we then tested whether 3-MA also blocked the protective effect of nicotine on mitochondrial dysfunction. The JC-1 assay revealed that 3-MA treatment mitigated nicotine-induced recovery of mitochondrial membrane potential after rotenone administration, suggesting that 3-MA abolished the protective function of nicotine on mitochondrial damage (Fig. 4A). Importantly, the mitochondrial accumulation of GSDME-N was exacerbated following autophagy inhibition (Fig. 4B), indicating that autophagy-dependent clearance of GSDME-N by nicotine was a key factor in the process. Functionally, 3-MA also reversed the decrease of mROS induced by nicotine treatment (Fig. 4C). Collectively, these observations demonstrated that nicotine could stimulate autophagy to prevent GSDME-N accumulation on mitochondria and thereby protect these organelles from toxin-induced damage in SH-SY5Y cells.

GSDME-N clearance from mitochondria by nicotine could maintain neurite integrity

To determine whether nicotine could regulate GSDME-N to protect mitochondria from toxin damage in primary neuron, gradient doses of nicotine were applied to the primary neuron. It demonstrated that nicotine (10 and 50 µM) downregulated the rotenone-induced GSDME cleavage in whole cell lysate (Fig. 5A), and nicotine administration was also able to prevent the decline of mitochondrial membrane potential caused by rotenone treatment (Supplementary Fig. 5). To ascertain whether nicotine also activated autophagy pathway to prevent GSDME-N accumulation on mitochondria in primary neurons, primary neurons were transfected with full-length GFP-GSDME and subsequently labelled with autophagy-related marker LC3B and MitoTracker Red. Confocal microscopy revealed that rotenone (0.05 µM, 12 h) induced the colocalization of GFP-GSDME puncta and mitochondria in the neurite, and LC3B was recruited to the GFP-GSDME clustered mitochondria in the neurite after nicotine treatment (Fig. 5B). To reconsolidate this observation, immunoblotting analysis was performed and it demonstrated nicotine increased the fraction of LC3-II and induced the degradation of P62. Meanwhile, the level of GSDME-N was found to decrease significantly with nicotine treatment, and the administration of 3-MA was found to reverse the decline of GSDME-N and the alteration of autophagy-related proteins (Fig. 5C), indicating the involvement of autophagic pathway after nicotine treatment in primary neurons.

Previous study suggested that the accumulation of mitochondrial damage in neurite was associated with neurite retraction, a hallmark of neurodegenerative disorders in the early stage [4]. To assess the effect of nicotine on the mitochondria in neurites after rotenone administration, the membrane potential of neuritic mitochondria was analyzed in the presence or absence of nicotine after rotenone treatment. We found that rotenone treatment led to a reduction in the mitochondrial membrane potential of neuritic mitochondria and nicotine reversed the rotenone-induced decrease in mitochondrial membrane potential within neurites (Fig. 5D).

The maintenance of neuritic mitochondria function is considered a promising therapeutic approach for addressing neurite retraction [4, 38]. Since nicotine could downregulate the excessive mitochondrial GSDME-N and balance the neuritic mitochondrial membrane potential, we questioned whether nicotine could attenuate the neuritic defects in early neurodegeneration stage. To this end, we overexpressed GFP-GSDME in primary neurons and used immunofluorescence to label microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2) to identify neurites and MitoTracker Red to label neuritic mitochondria (Fig. 6A). It was observed that rotenone treatment induced neurite shortening and even loss, whereas nicotine effectively attenuated these rotenone-induced neuritic alterations (Fig. 6B and C), indicating nicotine could attenuate the neuritic defects in early neurodegeneration stage.

Given that mitochondrial morphology change may be implicated in the neurodegenerative process [39, 40], we examined whether rotenone and nicotine affect mitochondrial morphology in neurites. To this end, we measured the area and perimeter of the mitochondrial punctum in neurite. The resultsdemonstrated that rotenone induced a significant increase in the area and perimeter of neuritic mitochondria in comparison with the control group. Conversely, nicotine treatment was observed to prevent the enlargement of neuritic mitochondria (Fig. 6D and F) and improve the retrograde mitochondrial transport (Supplementary Fig. 6). Furthermore, the colocalization of GFP-GSDME with mitochondria was observed after rotenone treatment, while nicotine treatment hindered this colocalization (Fig. 6G). Collectively, these results suggested that GSDME-N clearance from mitochondria by nicotine could maintain neurite integrity.

Nicotine downregulated GSDME-N and retarded the progression of neurodegeneration in rotenone-induced PD mouse model

To test whether nicotine could target GSDME-N to ameliorate the neurodegeneration in vivo, the C57BL mice were administered rotenone and provided with drinking water containing nicotine supplementation. Following a 7-day administration period, the level of GSDME-N was elevated in the midbrain in the rotenone treatment group compared to the control group. However, this elevation was reversed in the presence of nicotine (Fig. 7A and B), although at this timepoint, rotenone administration did not result in a significant alteration in the number of tyrosine hydroxylase (TH)-positive neurons in the substantia nigra and TH-positive fibers in the striatum (Figure C-E). Following a 14-day administration period, nicotine also induced a significant decrease in the level of GSDME-N compared to the rotenone-treated group (Fig. 7F and G). At this period, it was observed that rotenone treatment induced a loss of TH-positive neurons and fibers and administration of nicotine was found to mitigate this neurodegeneration (Fig. 7H-J). Collectively, these results indicated that rotenone administration induced GSDME cleavage prior to the loss of TH-positive neurons and fibers in the PD mouse model, and nicotine exerted protective effects against neurodegeneration.

To evaluate the motor function of mice, three distinct behavioral assessments were employed after a 2-week administration period, including rotarod test, hanging wire test and pole test. In the rotarod test and hanging wire test, mice after rotenone treatment exhibited a shorter time to fall compared to the control, whereas nicotine administration prolonged the duration on the rod or wire (Fig. 7K and L). The pole test revealed that nicotine shortened the descent time compared to the rotenone-treated mice (Fig. 7M). These results indicated that nicotine treatment decreased GSDME-N and retarded the progression of neurodegeneration in PD mouse model.

Discussion

The administration of nicotine effectively mitigated the accumulation of GSDME-N, a protein that has been linked to the aggregation of mitochondrial damage, oxidative stress and subsequent neurodegeneration. GSDME cleavage released the GSDME-N domain, which mediated mitochondrial dysfunction and the onset of neurodegeneration. However, whether targeting GSDME-N can be utilized for early intervention in neurodegenerative diseases such as PD remains unclear. Moreover, it is unknown whether nicotine exerts its neuroprotective effects by targeting GSDME. Our study demonstrated that GSDME-N accumulation on mitochondria during early neurodegeneration was suppressed by nicotine, thereby maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and preventing neurite degeneration. Through the activation of autophagic pathway, nicotine enhanced GSDME-N clearance, which maintained mitochondrial membrane potential, reduced reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and specifically rescued mitochondrial function. In a rotenone-induced PD model, nicotine treatment effectively reduced GSDME-N accumulation and slowed neurodegenerative progression. This pharmacological inhibition of GSDME-N by nicotine mitigated early neurodegenerative pathology, effectively preventing neurite retraction and further neurodegeneration.

GSDME-N accumulation on the mitochondria has been shown to induce mitochondrial dysfunction, an early hallmark of neuronal injury. Previous studies have primarily focused on the role of GSDME activation in pyroptotic cell death in cancer cells [8]. In neurons, however, activated GSDME rapidly translocated to axonal mitochondria following treatment with raptinal, a rapid inducer of mitochondrial damage and caspase-3 activation. Moreover, activation of GSDME has been detected in response to several neurodegeneration-associated proteins, including PR50 and TDP43, both of which are known to induce mitochondrial depolarization [15]. In our study, we found that rotenone—a mitochondrial toxin commonly used to model PD—induced GSDME cleavage in SH-SY5Y cells and primary neurons, leading to altered mitochondrial morphology and impaired mitochondrial mobility within neurites. Notably, before plasma membrane rupture, the N-terminal fragments of gasdermins preferentially targeted mitochondria owing to their strong binding affinity for cardiolipin, a mitochondrial membrane component [11, 41–43]. Based on these findings, we hypothesized that reducing GSDME-N accumulation in mitochondria could attenuate early neuronal damage. Consistent with this, nicotine treatment reduced GSDME-N colocalization with mitochondria, preserved neuritic mitochondrial integrity, prevented neurite loss in neuronal models, and alleviated neurodegeneration and motor deficits in a rotenone-induced PD mouse model via downregulation of GSDME-N.

These results broaden our understanding of the role of nicotine in mediating neuroprotective effects. Our findings indicated that nicotine had the capacity to target GSDME-N, thereby safeguarding mitochondria against damage inflicted by toxins. Previous studies have elucidated the neuroprotective function of nicotine in counteracting neurodegenerative diseases through a multifaceted mechanism, including the prevention of apoptosis, the enhancement of mitochondrial function, the inhibition of inflammation, and the degradation of deleterious proteins [28, 33, 44–46]. Specifically, nicotine has been shown to regulate the expression of anti-apoptotic proteins, including Bcl-2, Bcl-xL, and Mcl-1 [28]. The anti-apoptotic protein Bcl-2 could interact with the Bax/Bak oligomer on the outer mitochondrial membrane, thereby preventing mitochondrial permeabilization [47, 48]. In addition, nicotine has been shown to facilitate the degradation of alpha-synuclein and sirtuin 6, which may contribute to the alleviation of PD and neurodegeneration [33, 46].

Importantly, our experimental data revealed that nicotine stimulated autophagic flux and downregulated GSDME-N in both whole-cell and mitochondrial levels. It was documented that PD patients exhibited obstruction of autophagy flux, a process that could lead to the aggregation of alpha-synuclein and subsequent mitochondrial dysfunction [49, 50]. Previous studies demonstrated that nicotine treatment enhanced the expression of autophagy-related proteins in C. elegans and human neural cells [46, 51]. Furthermore, nicotine could activate α7 nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs) to modulate key autophagy regulators including the AMPK/mTOR and PI3K-AKT pathways [52]. More recently, nicotine was reported to promote the recruitment of PINK1 and Parkin to mitochondria and to modulate mitophagic flux [53]. In the present study, we further demonstrated that nicotine treatment increased the mitochondrial accumulation of canonical mitophagy markers, such as PINK1 and Parkin, and induced a clear colocalization of LC3B with mitochondria, indicating that activation of the PINK1/Parkin pathway contributes to nicotine-induced autophagic flux.

In summary, this study demonstrated that GSDME-N accumulation on mitochondria during early neurodegeneration was suppressed by nicotine, thereby maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and preventing neurite degeneration. Our findings position GSDME-N as a promising therapeutic target for early-stage neurodegeneration, with potential to delay or prevent irreversible neuronal loss. Nicotine served as a valuable pharmacological tool to demonstrate this neuroprotective pathway in this study. Future research should prioritize the development of more precise drugs or strategies to modulate GSDME. Elucidation of the specific role of GSDME in distinct phases of neurodegeneration will be instrumental in formulating strategies to avert or treat neurodegenerative disorders.
