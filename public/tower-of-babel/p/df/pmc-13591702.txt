The evolutionary landscape of host immunity genes involved in respiratory and other immune-related diseases, and the identification of TLR2 variation associated with severe COVID-19
Authors: Christopher N. Cross, Alessandro Lisi, Faith C. Simmonds, Marisol Fermin Flores, Kareem Washington, Thomas Heinbockel, Michael C. Campbell
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGiven its high mortality and broad societal impact, the COVID-19 pandemic is arguably one of the most consequential public health crises of the twenty-first century. Although previous studies have identified several genes associated with COVID-19 susceptibility, relatively little is known about the genes contributing to severe COVID-19, including their evolutionary histories. In the current study, we analyzed IL-4, TLR2, CCL2, and SLC11A1—immunity genes that have previously been implicated in severe COVID-19 and other immune-related diseases—in globally diverse populations from the 1000 Genomes Project. We also tested for associations between genetic variation at these genes and clinical COVID-19 phenotypes in nearly 4000 laboratory-confirmed COVID-19–positive individuals across two datasets from the GEN-COVID Multicenter Study in Italy.ResultsBased on our analyses, we identified striking signatures of positive selection within and around all four genes, including extensive haplotype structure, elevated population differentiation, and significant selection coefficients consistent with both ancient and more recent adaptive events. Notably, many of these signals were population-specific, highlighting the role of local selection in shaping immune gene diversity. Several selected alleles were also present in Neanderthal and/or Denisovan genomes, reflecting both shared ancestral polymorphism and archaic introgression within these genes. Functional predictions based on in silico analyses further revealed that a subset of selected alleles maps to transcription factor binding sites and is predicted to influence binding affinity. In addition, our genotype–phenotype analyses uncovered coding variants in TLR2 that were correlated with COVID-19 severity and a related comorbidity, with estimated effect sizes ranging from moderate to large. Interestingly, these significantly associated alleles occur at rare or low frequency in western European and East Asian populations but are absent in populations of African and South Asian descent, indicative of a relatively recent origin.ConclusionsOverall, our study provides new insights into the evolution of biologically relevant immunity genes in modern human populations and identifies genetic variation that may contribute to differences in risk for severe COVID-19.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12915-026-02623-6.

Background

Novel coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19), caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2), was a public health crisis that resulted in more than 700 million positive cases and over 7 million deaths worldwide (https://www.worldometers.info/coronavirus/). While SARS-CoV-2 infection can lead to severe respiratory problems, other symptoms can include cardiovascular and neurological impairment [1–5]. Furthermore, data have shown that host genetics play a role in SARS-CoV-2 infection [6–11]. In particular, studies have identified host genes encoding receptors for coronavirus binding (e.g., ACE2, ANPEP, DPP4) and proteases (e.g., TMPRSS2, FURIN, TMPRSS11D, CTSL, and CTSB) that facilitate the entry of SARS-CoV-2 into cells, increasing COVID-19 susceptibility [7, 12, 13]. Genetic analyses have also demonstrated that alleles within these genes vary in frequency among populations [7, 13], potentially contributing to differential risk for infection in the absence of vaccination.

In addition, accumulating evidence has indicated that host genes influence the severity of COVID-19 following initial SARS-CoV-2 infection [12, 14–38]. For example, mRNA expression levels of Toll-like receptor genes, including TLR2, have been correlated with severe disease (commonly characterized by dysregulated inflammation and a cytokine storm) in SARS-CoV-2–positive patients [19, 39]; in vivo mice experiments further confirmed that the TLR2 protein was the innate sensor that triggered inflammatory cytokine expression in response to SARS-CoV-2 infection [19]. Prior analyses have also shown that cytokines, such as IL-4, IL-6, TNF-α, CXCL8, CXCL9, CXCL10, and CCL2, are present at elevated levels in the serum of COVID-19 patients with severe respiratory symptoms [40–44]. These findings suggest that COVID-19 severity may have a different genetic architecture involving cytokine and interleukin genes than COVID-19 susceptibility [45, 46]. In addition, multiple lines of evidence, including a meta-analysis of ~ 400 host genetic susceptibility studies, have implicated a similar set of genes (specifically, IL-4, TLR2, CCL2, and SLC11A1) in the onset of other respiratory and immune-related diseases, such as psoriasis, osteoarthritis, pulmonary tuberculosis (TB), inflammatory bowel syndrome (IBD), influenza, pneumonia, and severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) [47–63]. Finally, a recent analysis identified 13 genome-wide loci associated with lung, autoimmune, or inflammatory diseases that were significantly correlated with severe COVID-19 [6]. Thus, we hypothesize that genes involved in respiratory and/or other immune-related illnesses, including their severity, may also contribute to acute manifestations of COVID-19.

Despite the far-reaching consequences of COVID-19, the role of host genetics in severe disease has not been extensively examined. Furthermore, little is known about the evolutionary histories of immunity genes that have been implicated in severe COVID-19 and other immune-mediated diseases [56–58, 64, 65]. To address these gaps in knowledge, we examined IL-4, TLR2, CCL2, and SLC11A1—genes associated with an array of immune-mediated illnesses and of long-standing interest to the authors—in 2039 individuals from globally diverse populations in the 1000 Genomes Project [66], with the goals of (1) identifying patterns of nucleotide and haplotype variation within and between populations and (2) inferring the microevolutionary forces that have shaped these genetic patterns over time. In addition, we performed association analyses of clinical phenotypes and known mutations in our four genes implicated in respiratory and/or other immune-related illnesses in nearly 4000 laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2–positive individuals across two datasets from the GEN-COVID Multicenter Study in Italy [67]. Overall, our study aims to characterize the evolutionary histories of key host immunity genes and provide new information regarding severe COVID-19 risk.

Results

Patterns of nucleotide variation

We identified 260 bi-allelic SNPs across the 8690-base pair (bp) region of IL-4 (Fig. 1; Additional file 1: Table 1), of which 20 were found in exons and 240 were present in non-coding regions (Fig. 1; Additional file 1: Table 1). Among the exonic SNPs, nine were synonymous, 10 were nonsynonymous, and one was a stop-gain mutation (rs550558636; Additional file 1: Table 1). Interestingly, we found that nonsynonymous SNPs were rare, exhibiting a minor allele frequency (MAF) below 1% in all populations (Additional file 1: Table 1). Similarly, synonymous variants were either rare (MAF < 1%) or absent in populations, with the exception of one SNP (rs2243251) which had an MAF that ranged from 14.2 to 27% in populations of African descent; the stop-gain mutation was present only in the Mende of Sierra Leone with an MAF < 1% (Additional file 1: Table 1).Fig. 1Gene structure and the distribution of polymorphisms across genic regions. In the non-coding regions of IL-4, we identified five common SNPs (MAF ≥ 5%) in pooled populations (Additional file 1: Table 1); one of these SNPs (rs2070874) was located in the 5′-UTR, whereas the other four (rs2243281, rs734244, rs2243253, and rs2243261) were found in intronic regions. Furthermore, the MAF at these SNPs varied considerably across populations. For example, the minor T-allele at rs2070874 exhibited frequencies that ranged from 37.5 to 58.1% in populations of African descent and from 72.6 to 86% in East Asians (Additional file 1: Table 1). Prior studies have also linked the rs2070874 T-allele to severe respiratory illness and heightened immune responses [57, 68–70]. Overall, genetic variation across IL-4 was dominated by non-coding polymorphisms, whereas coding variants were largely rare in populations.

Across the 21,981-bp length of the TLR2 gene, we identified 627 bi-allelic SNPs (Fig. 1; Additional file 1: Table 2). Specifically, we observed 23 synonymous variants, 47 nonsynonymous variants, and two stop-gain variants in the exonic regions. We also found 541 polymorphisms in the 5′-UTR and intronic regions, as well as 14 polymorphisms in the 3′-UTR (Additional file 1: Table 2). Furthermore, we identified 36 common SNPs (MAF ≥ 5%) within the 5′-UTR and introns, 20 of which were shared across all populations (none were present in the 3′-UTR). Interestingly, the MAF at these common sites differed widely among populations (Additional file 1: Table 2). For example, the minor T-allele at rs6811715 occurred at frequencies ranging from 33.9 to 42.9% in populations of African descent and from 25.8 to 33.3% in South Asian populations, but reached even higher frequencies in Europeans (47.8–53.3%) and East Asians (50–61.6%; Additional file 1: Table 2). The minor T-alleles at rs13115230, rs11099894, and rs7694512 also varied between 7 and 24.2% in populations of African descent but were observed at higher frequencies (≻ 28%) in non-Africans (Additional file 1: Table 2). Similarly, the derived G-allele at rs1898830—previously associated with respiratory disease [71–73]—was present at relatively high frequency (from 29 to 62.6%) in non-African populations, particularly in Asia (Additional file 1: Table 2). In contrast, the rs1898830 G-allele occurred at much lower frequency (7–23.3%) in African and African-descended populations (Additional file 1: Table 2). In summary, TLR2 exhibited extensive genetic diversity and pronounced allele-frequency differences primarily between African and non-African populations.

At CCL2, we identified 34 bi-allelic SNPs across the 1919-bp length of this gene (Fig. 1; Additional file 1: Table 3). Of these SNPs, four were in exons, 11 in introns, two in the 5′-UTR, and 17 in the 3′-UTR (Fig. 1; Additional file 1: Table 3). Among the four exonic variants, two were synonymous (rs4586 and rs368861512) and two were nonsynonymous (rs148285031 and rs190996557). Furthermore, only four of the 34 SNPs—namely, rs13900 (3′-UTR), rs2857657 (intron), rs4586 (exon), and rs28730833 (intron)—were common (MAF ≥ 5%; Additional file 1: Table 3). In addition, the MAF at these common SNPs varied dramatically across populations. For example, the minor T-allele at rs13900 ranged from 20.7 to 26.8% frequency in populations of African descent, while the same allele varied from 26.7 to 41% frequency in South Asians, from 28.5 to 35.9% in Europeans, and from 41.4 to 64.9% in East Asians (Additional file 1: Table 3). Similarly, the rs4586 T-allele ranged from 16 to 36.7% frequency in African and African-descended populations but varied from 35 to 63.6% frequency in non-Africans (Additional file 1: Table 3). Prior studies have also implicated rs13900 and rs4586 in gene expression regulation and/or respiratory disease susceptibility [65, 74]. Thus, despite harboring relatively few polymorphisms, CCL2 exhibited pronounced population differences in allele frequencies.

Finally, at SLC11A1, we identified 464 bi-allelic SNPs across the 14,622-bp length of this gene (Fig. 1; Additional file 1: Table 4). Of these SNPs, 75 were located in exons, 299 in introns, six in intronic splice sites, two in the 5′-UTR, and 82 in the 3′-UTR (Fig. 1; Additional file 1: Table 4). We also observed eight common SNPs (MAF ≥ 5%) in exons, 41 in introns, and eight in the 3′-UTR (Additional file 1: Table 4). Notably, the MAF at common exonic SNPs differed considerably between African and non-African populations. For example, the minor rs17221959 T-allele ranged from 28.3 to 40.3% frequency in populations of African descent but did not exceed 15% frequency in non-Africans (Additional file 1: Table 4). Similarly, the minor rs7576974 T-allele varied widely in frequency (7.5–25.9%) in populations of African descent but occurred at < 4.1% frequency in non-Africans (Additional file 1: Table 4).

We also noted frequency differences at common intronic variants across populations. For example, the minor rs2290708 T-allele varied between 19.2 and 35% frequency in Europeans but was present at < 18% frequency in other global populations (Additional file 1: Table 4). Likewise, the T-alleles at rs13011285 and rs17228995 ranged from 20.7 to 34.1% in Europeans but occurred at lower frequencies in populations of African, South Asian, and East Asian descent (Additional file 1: Table 4). Furthermore, the derived rs2279014 T-allele in the 3′-UTR—previously associated with decreased risk for Mycobacterium avium complex infection [75]—was observed at frequencies of ~ 31-46% in populations of African and European descent, whereas its frequency ranged from 18.8 to 36.7% in South and East Asian populations (Additional file 1: Table 4). In summary, we found pronounced population differences in allele frequency at several common SNPs, including variation implicated in infectious disease susceptibility.

Tests of neutrality

To assess whether patterns of nucleotide variation deviated from neutrality, we calculated Fay and Wu’s H (H) and Tajima’s D (DT) statistics for IL-4, TLR2, CCL2, and SLC11A1 in each population with DnaSP [76] (Additional file 1: Tables 5–8). We also simulated the expected H and DT values under different models of population growth to determine the statistical significance of observed values (Additional file 1: Tables 9–16). Significantly negative values indicate an excess of rare (for DT) or high-frequency derived variants (for H) congruent with a model of recent population expansion or positive selection, whereas significantly positive values denote an excess of intermediate-frequency variants (both DT and H) which can arise from balancing selection, population structure, or soft selective sweeps [77–83]. Our analyses revealed a mix of positive and negative H values at IL-4 in non-African populations, with significantly negative H statistics observed in East Asians. In contrast, populations of African descent generally exhibited positive but non-significant H values (Additional file 1: Table 9). At TLR2, H statistics were consistently negative in non-Africans, with significant values occurring in a subset of European, South Asian, and East Asian populations. Similarly, we observed significantly negative H statistics at TLR2 in populations of African descent (Additional file 1: Table 10). Our analyses of CCL2 revealed a pattern of negative H statistics in African and non-African populations (Additional file 1: Table 11), with significant values occurring in the East African Luhya and West African Yoruba. At SLC11A1, significantly negative H values were observed in both African and non-African populations (Additional file 1: Table 12).

Finally, although the DT statistics were a mix of negative and positive values at IL-4, TLR2, and CCL2 in both Africans and non-Africans, they were generally more positive than expected under different population growth models (Additional file 1: Tables 13–15). In contrast, at SLC11A1, we observed significantly negative DT statistics in populations of African descent and consistently negative DT statistics in non-Africans (though these latter values were not significant; Additional file 1: Table 16). Overall, both H and DT statistics indicated that patterns of variation at IL-4, TLR2, CCL2, and SLC11A1 have deviated from neutral expectations in multiple populations.

Long-range haplotype homozygosity

We calculated iHS and nSL statistics for each population to evaluate patterns of haplotype homozygosity surrounding the IL-4, CCL2, TLR2, and SLC11A1 genes using the selscan and Polaris software packages [84, 85]. Our analyses revealed outlier | nSL | values at several IL-4 variants, including rs56408050 and rs56174361, in non-African populations (Table 1; Additional file 1: Table 17; Additional file 2: Fig. S1; Additional file 3: Fig. S2). To further investigate these signals, we examined haplotype structure by plotting the decay of extended haplotype homozygosity (EHH) from these SNPs [86]. This analysis revealed relatively short EHH around the ancestral alleles at rs56408050 and rs56174361 in non-Africans, particularly in European and Asian populations (Additional file 4: Fig. S3). Selection coefficient estimates

As a complementary approach, we applied the CLUES2 algorithm implemented in the CLUES2 Companion package to IL-4, TLR2, CCL2, and SLC11A1 in each population at different time depths (i.e., 1000, 500, and 200 generations ago) to infer temporal shifts in selection intensity [87–89]. At IL-4, our results showed that selection pressure on SNPs likely originated deep in time. For example, we observed significant selection coefficient (s) estimates for the derived allele at rs56174361 in African populations at 1000 generations ago, corresponding to 28,000 years ago (ya; Fig. 4; Additional file 1: Table 24). We also detected significant s for ancestral alleles at rs2227282 and rs2227284 in African populations (Fig. 4; Additional file 1: Table 24). In contrast, selection appears to have favored the ancestral allele at rs56174361, as well as the derived alleles at rs2227282 and rs2227284 in different populations (Fig. 4; Additional file 1: Table 24). Similarly, at TLR2, we found evidence for selection acting on derived alleles at 1000 generations ago (28,000 ya) in African populations. For example, in the Yoruba from Nigeria, the s estimate for rs2083287 was 0.02942 (95% CI: 0.00664–0.0522; Additional file 1: Table 25), consistent with positive selection on the derived allele at this site. Notably, we did not observe extensive EHH around this SNP, suggesting the selective signal is relatively old and has been eroded by recombination over time. In contrast, we detected non-significant s estimates for derived alleles at other SNPs (e.g., rs12510167, rs7694512, rs11099874, rs7694512, and rs1311523), which exhibited extensive EHH in populations of Africa descent. This finding suggests that selection may have occurred more recently in time. One notable exception to this pattern was rs13123230 which showed long-range EHH and a significant s estimate at 1000 generations ago (28,000 ya) in the Esan from Nigeria (Additional file 1: Table 25; Additional file 7: Fig. S6). However, upon further examination, we found that s was 0.01405 (95% CI: 0.00612–0.02199) at 1000 generations ago, then declined to 0.00386 (95% CI: − 0.17166–0.17938) at 500 generations ago (14,000 ya), and subsequently increased dramatically to 0.01683 (95% CI: − 0.26538–0.29903) at 200 generations ago (5600 ya), though these two latter s estimates were not significant (Additional file 1: Table 25). Outside of Africa, multiple SNPs showed significant s estimates across time periods (i.e., from 28,000 to 5600 ya) in different populations (Additional file 1: Table 25). Collectively, these above findings suggest a dynamic history of selection at TLR2, with both ancient and more recent selection acting on multiple variants in African and non-African populations.

At CCL2, we found that the strength of selection at loci in this gene varied among populations (Additional file 1: Table 26). For instance, we observed a significant s estimate for the ancestral C-allele at rs4586 in Africans (Mende and Yoruba) and East Asians (Japanese) but not in other populations (Additional file 1: Table 26). In contrast, we found significant s estimates at rs13900 favoring the derived allele in Africans (Luhya) and East Asians (Han Chinese and Japanese), while rs2857657 exhibited a significant s estimate for the derived allele in the Tuscan Italian population (Additional file 1: Table 26). Thus, we observed a heterogeneous pattern of selection at CCL2, with selection favoring different allele states—either ancestral or derived—at multiple loci in distinct populations.

At SLC11A1, we detected significant s statistics at several polymorphic sites in African and European populations dating to at least 28,000 ya (Additional file 1: Table 27). This deeper timeframe of selection is further supported by the absence of extended haplotype homozygosity in African populations and the presence of only moderate haplotype structure in Europeans, consistent with older selection signals eroded by recombination [86]. In contrast, we observed instances of selection on sites at a more recent time depth in South Asian and East Asian populations (Additional file 1: Table 27). For example, rs2695343 exhibited significant s estimates favoring the derived allele at least 200 generations ago (5600 ya). We also detected significant s estimates for derived alleles at rs2279015 and rs1059823 that progressively increased over time (from 28,000 to 5600 ya) in South and East Asians (Additional file 1: Table 27). These events likely contributed to the long-range EHH observed in these populations (Additional file 1: Table 27). Additionally, we found very few instances of selection acting on ancestral and derived alleles at the same site in different populations (Additional file 1: Table 27). Overall, we observed divergent selective histories at SLC11A1, with older signals in African and European populations and more recent selection in South and East Asians.

Population differentiation

To identify loci showing population-specific allele frequency changes, we calculated the Population Branch Statistic (PBS). We then generated a genome-wide null distribution by calculating PBS for randomly selected SNPs and identified variants within IL-4, TLR2, CCL2, and SLC11A1 that exceeded the 95th and 99th percentiles of this distribution (Additional file 1: Tables 28–31). Based on this approach, we identified several loci with elevated differentiation along the focal population lineages (Additional file 1: Tables 28–31). In contrast, other variants—such as rs2243251 and rs2243253—exhibited extreme PBS values that were restricted to African populations (Table 2; Additional file 1: Table 28). Population differences in linkage disequilibrium

To further characterize the evolutionary dynamics of our immune genes, we examined intragenic patterns of linkage disequilibrium (LD) using pairwise SNP comparisons. These analyses revealed pronounced differences in LD across continental populations. More explicitly, LD blocks were generally shorter, and the number of SNP pairs in strong LD was smaller in populations of African descent compared to non-Africans across all four genes (Additional file 1: Tables 32–52). We also found that many SNPs inferred to be under selection consistently exhibited strong LD (r2 ≥ 0.8) with one another. For instance, rs56174361 and rs56408050 in IL-4 were in perfect LD (r2 = 1) in African and non-African populations. Similarly, other adaptive SNPs at IL-4 (e.g., rs2070874, rs734244, rs2243267, rs2243282, rs2243284, rs2243288, and rs2243290) were in near-perfect (r2 > 0.9) or perfect (r2 = 1) LD with each other in multiple populations (Additional file 1: Tables 32–52). We also detected strong LD among adaptive SNPs at TLR2, though the specific pairwise LD relationships varied across populations (Additional file 1: Tables 32–52). In contrast, while we observed strong LD (r2 ≥ 0.8) among adaptive loci at SLC11A1, the number of SNP pairs in strong LD was greater in non-Africans than in African and African-descended populations (Additional file 1: Tables 32–52). At CCL2, we found that rs4586 and rs13900 were either in near-perfect (r2 > 0.9) or perfect (r2 = 1) LD primarily in East Asians, whereas the LD between these variants was weaker (r2 ≤ 0.8) in populations of African, South Asian, and European descent (Additional file 1: Tables 32–52).

In addition, comparison of LD patterns with the PBS results showed that many of the strongest pairwise relationships involved SNPs that were identified as PBS outliers (Table 2 and Additional file 1: Tables 28–52). Notably, African and African-descended populations exhibited shorter blocks of LD even within regions containing PBS outliers, whereas Eurasian populations often showed larger clusters of PBS outliers in strong LD (Table 2 and Additional file 1: Tables 28–52). Taken together, these results indicate that population differentiation frequently involved blocks of linked variants rather than isolated mutations (though these blocks were shorter in populations of African descent compared to non-Africans).

Patterns of archaic introgression

To identify archaic-derived variants within our immune genes, we employed the archaicAllelePro approach implemented in the ArchaicSeeker 2.0 package [90]. This method detects archaic alleles by comparing modern human genomic variation to archaic hominin genomes (e.g., Neanderthal and Denisovan) and identifying variants that are shared between archaic genomes and non-African populations but absent or rare in African populations. Using this approach, we observed clear geographic structure in the distribution of Neanderthal-derived variants at TLR2. Notably, the largest number of archaic alleles was observed in South Asian populations (Fig. 5; Additional file 1: Tables 53–64), and the frequency of many of these alleles exceeded 5% (Fig. 5; Additional file 1: Tables 53–64). We also found similar Neanderthal-derived alleles in East Asian populations, though their number and frequency were much lower than those observed in South Asians (Fig. 5; Additional file 1: Tables 53–64). Interestingly, one of these SNPs, rs62323856, had a significant s estimate (s = 0.0194; 95% CI: 0.0083–0.0306) dating to at least ~ 28,000 ya in East Asia, but no signal of selection was observed more recently within this geographic region (Additional file 1: Table 25). Comparatively, European populations possessed an intermediate number of archaic variants at TLR2, many of which were common (MAF ≥ 5%; Fig. 5; Additional file 1: Table 2; Additional file 1: Tables 53–64); one of these SNPs, rs74341183, also showed a significant s estimate (s = 0.0365; 95% CI: 0.0187–0.0542) in the Tuscan Italian population dating to at least 5600 ya (Table 1; Additional file 1: Table 25; Additional file 1: Tables 53–64). In contrast, we detected clear geographic structure in the distribution of Denisovan-derived variants at SLC11A1. Specifically, a core set of Denisovan-derived SNPs (rs3731864, rs117769919, rs117018415, and rs79796505) was observed mainly in East Asian populations—most notably in the Han Chinese, Dai, Japanese, and Vietnamese (Fig. 5; Additional file 1: Tables 65–72). Furthermore, the above Denisovan-derived alleles occurred at a frequency greater than 1.5% in these populations (Fig. 5; Additional file 1: Tables 65–72). We also observed the same set of Denisovan-derived variants in South Asia at much lower frequencies (< 1%; Fig. 5; Additional file 1: Tables 65–72).

At IL-4, we found a single low-frequency Neanderthal-derived allele (rs200256232) in East Asian populations (Additional file 1: Table 1; Additional file 1: Tables 73 and 74). Furthermore, we identified derived alleles at several additional sites (e.g., rs2243288, rs2243290, rs2243284, and rs2243282) that were shared with Neanderthal and/or Denisovan genomes (Additional file 1: Table 1). However, because these derived alleles were not inferred to have entered modern human populations via introgression using the archaicAllelePro framework [90], their presence in both archaic and modern humans most likely reflects inheritance from a shared common ancestor rather than archaic admixture. In contrast, we did not detect Neanderthal- or Denisovan-derived alleles at CCL2 in any of our populations. Taken together, these results indicate substantial heterogeneity in patterns of archaic introgression across immune genes, with Neanderthal-derived variants at TLR2 broadly distributed across Asia, Denisovan-derived variants at SLC11A1 largely concentrated in East Asia, limited archaic signals at IL-4, and no detectable introgression at CCL2.

Distribution of haplotype variation

To further explore population-level differences in genetic variation, we examined fine-scale haplotype patterns within our immunity genes. At IL-4, we inferred 296 distinct haplotypes, nine of which were common (H44, H74, H85, H103, H115, H197, H234, H241, and H287) in populations of African descent (Additional file 1: Tables 75 and 76). Together, these common haplotypes represented ~ 51 to 58% of all haplotypes in these populations (Additional file 1: Tables 75 and 76). In contrast, eight common haplotypes (H44, H69, H82, H176, H197, H198, H234, and H241) comprised ~ 81 to 93% of all haplotypes in non-African populations (Additional file 1: Tables 75 and 77). Further analyses also showed that a subset of these haplotypes (H44, H69, H82, H176, and H234) accounted for more than 78.5% of all haplotypes in Europeans and South Asians; however, the same haplotypes only constituted between 47.4 and 74.6% of the total haplotypes in East Asian populations (Additional file 1: Tables 75 and 77). Overall, we observed greater haplotype diversity in populations of African descent and a smaller set of dominant haplotypes in non-African populations.

At TLR2, we identified a total of 599 distinct haplotypes in pooled global populations (Additional file 1: Tables 78–80); eight common haplotypes (H11, H30, H231, H265, H361, H402, H438, and H580) comprised ~ 38 to 44% of the variation present in African and African-descended populations (Additional file 1: Tables 78 and 79). In comparison, 12 common haplotypes (H2, H40, H65, H96, H98, H108, H227, H231, H482, H507, H532, and H540) constituted ~ 49 to 80% of the haplotypes in non-Africans (Additional file 1: Tables 78 and 80). Our analyses also showed that a few of these haplotypes disproportionately contributed to the variation in these populations. For example, H231 accounted for ~ 40 to 46% of the haplotypes in South Asians, while H231 comprised ~ 24 to 25% and ~ 24 to 36% of the haplotypes in Europeans and East Asians, respectively (Additional file 1: Tables 78 and 80). In contrast, the maximum frequency of haplotypes in populations of African descent was 15.4% (Additional file 1: Table 79). Overall, non-African populations were characterized by a smaller number of high-frequency haplotypes at TLR2 (particularly H231, which was most prevalent in South Asians), whereas African and African-descended populations exhibited a larger number of lower-frequency haplotypes.

At CCL2, we identified 35 distinct haplotypes in pooled global populations (Additional file 1: Tables 81–83), five of which were common (H2, H9, H11, H19, and H29). Among these haplotypes, H9, H11, H19, and H29 constituted 91.7–96.9% and 94.1–99.5% of the total variation in African and non-African populations, respectively (Additional file 1: Tables 81–83), indicating a strong skew toward a small set of dominant haplotypes at this gene. Thus, CCL2 haplotype variation was characterized by a small number of high-frequency haplotypes shared across global populations.

At SLC11A1, we found a total of 619 distinct haplotypes in pooled global populations (Additional file 1: Tables 84–86); 11 common haplotypes (H102, H159, H179, H204, H215, H254, H269, H334, H445, H484, and H491) comprised between ~ 28 and 54% of all haplotypes in populations of African descent (Additional file 1: Tables 84 and 85). In contrast, eight common haplotypes (H150, H223, H284, H321, H334, H340, H484, and H491) accounted for ~ 50–75% of all haplotypes in non-Africans (Additional file 1: Tables 84 and 86). Interestingly, only three common haplotypes (H334, H484, and H491) were shared between African and non-African populations (Additional file 1: Tables 84–86), revealing a high level of divergence between these broad groups at this gene.

Among these haplotypes, H231 contained the largest number of selected alleles and had the highest frequency within the network (Fig. 6). Furthermore, H98 and H540 showed a striking geographic pattern. For example, the derived alleles at rs13147967 and rs1898831 occurred on several haplotypes (e.g., H98, H231, and H540) that were widespread across Eurasian populations, while the derived alleles at rs2083287 and rs6811185 existed on haplotypes (e.g., H361, H402) that were more common in populations of African descent (Fig. 6; Additional file 1: Table 88). Comparatively, H29 carried the derived rs2857657 G-allele, likewise inferred to be under selection; the highest frequency of this haplotype occurred in Europe (Fig. 6). Specifically, H334 occurred at ≺ 14% frequency in populations of African descent (Additional file 1: Tables 84 and 85) but reached substantially higher frequency in non-Africans (i.e., ~ 41–55%, ~ 40–52%, and ~ 38–44% in East Asians, Europeans, and South Asians, respectively; Additional file 1: Tables 84 and 86). These African-specific haplotypes also exhibited reticulations, which suggest historical recombination (Fig. 6). Taken together, the SLC11A1 network showed strong geographic differentiation, along with evidence of recombination and selection.

Transcription factor binding analysis

Given that variation in gene expression is known to contribute to disease susceptibility and/or severity [93–95], we examined common SNPs inferred to be adaptive using the SNP2TFBS tool [96], which predicts whether polymorphisms occur in transcription factor binding sites (TFBSs) [96, 97]. This analysis identified two SNPs at IL-4 (rs56174361 and rs56408050) that were inferred to affect the binding of the EGR2 transcription factor (TF). Interestingly, these SNPs were consistently found to exist in perfect LD across geographically diverse populations. Furthermore, one SNP at CCL2 (rs2857657)—located within a TFBS—was predicted to influence the binding of the SOX10 and ZNF263 transcription factors (Table 1). At SLC11A1, rs2279014 and rs2279015 were predicted to alter the binding of C/EBPα and EGR1, respectively (Table 1). In contrast, none of the SNPs at TLR2 overlapped with known TFBSs in the SNP2TFBS database. Overall, these findings suggest that a subset of adaptive variants could play a role in gene regulation by modifying TF binding.

Genotype–phenotype associations

Because we hypothesized that loci associated with immune-mediated diseases could also influence severe COVID-19 outcomes, we focused our analyses on previously identified candidate loci associated with immune-related illnesses in two GEN-COVID cohorts of laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2–positive individuals [67]. First, we examined comorbidities known to increase the risk for severe COVID-19 (rather than COVID-19 severity directly) along with genotypes from our four genes in a cohort of 1112 SARS-CoV-2–positive individuals. Using a logistic regression approach, we identified a significant association between the missense rs139227237 mutation in the coding region of TLR2 and organ transplant status (P = 0.02979 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction; β = 88.7, 95% CI: 32.49–144.91; Additional file 1: Table 91). However, the unusually large β from this analysis suggested an inflated effect size, likely driven by sparse allele counts. To obtain a more reliable estimate, we analyzed the data using Firth logistic regression [98–101], which mitigates small-sample and sparse-data bias. This reanalysis showed that rs139227237 remained significantly associated with organ transplant status (P = 0.0056; β = 5.34, 95% CI: 1.56–9.12; Additional file 1: Table 92). Although β was still relatively large, the Firth correction provided a more stable estimate. Notably, in both regression analyses, the rs139227237 C-allele [p.(Phe217Ser)] was observed at 8.3% frequency in SARS-CoV-2–positive individuals who had received an organ transplant but was rare among infected individuals without a transplant history (0.09%; Additional file 1: Tables 91 and 92). This finding is particularly noteworthy given that the heightened vulnerability of organ transplant recipients to severe COVID-19 has been well documented in the literature [102, 103]. No significant associations were identified between genetic variation at the other immunity genes and comorbidities in this dataset.

We further explored the effect of the coding region on COVID-19 outcomes in a second dataset. Specifically, we analyzed whole exome sequencing data from a cohort of 2839 SARS-CoV-2–positive individuals from GEN-COVID [67]. These participants were classified by degrees of COVID-19 severity (i.e., not hospitalized, hospitalized with no oxygen therapy, hospitalized with oxygen therapy, hospitalized with CPAP/BiPAP, hospitalized with intubation, and deceased). When we examined these phenotypes as a binary trait (i.e., not hospitalized versus more serious outcomes), we detected a significant association at rs5743709, a coding SNP, in TLR2 [p.(Ala781Ala)] (P = 0.01119 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction; β = 1.15, 95% CI: 0.493–1.815; Additional file 1: Table 93), with a relatively large estimated effect size. In addition, when COVID-19 severity was modeled as a quantitative trait (i.e., graded levels of severity), the rs5743709 A-allele was significantly associated with increasing disease severity (P = 0.00288 after Benjamini–Hochberg correction; β = 0.574, 95% CI: 0.196–0.952; Additional file 1: Table 94) using a mixed linear regression model; within this context, the effect size was moderate, indicating a measurable contribution of this variant to severe COVID-19 (Additional file 1: Table 94). We did not detect significant associations at the other immunity genes.

Notably, the rs5743709 A-allele was not observed in the Tuscan (TSI) population, likely reflecting its low frequency and the limited sample size of this reference panel rather than a true absence in Italy more broadly. Discussion

Evidence for positive selection

Our analyses uncovered strong evidence for positive selection at IL-4, TLR2, CCL2, and SLC11A1 in African and non-African populations. Specifically, we detected significant deviations from neutrality, long-range haplotype homozygosity around either derived or ancestral alleles, elevated population differentiation, and significant s estimates at multiple sites within these genes. Taken together, these analyses led to the identification of common SNPs that were inferred to be under selection. Furthermore, several of these SNPs were found on different common haplotypes, suggesting these haplotypes could have risen to high frequency in populations due to multiple-locus soft sweeps [104–106]. Under this model, no single favored mutation sweeps to fixation [107, 108]; instead, beneficial alleles at different nucleotide sites increase in frequency, simultaneously undergoing incomplete selective sweeps. In this scenario, multiple alleles (and haplotypes) can co-exist at intermediate frequency, resulting in strongly positive summary statistics in populations, which we observed at IL-4 (in Africans and non-Africans), TLR2 (in non-Africans), and CCL2 (in both Africans and non-Africans).

Among these three genes, some of the most striking genetic patterns arguably occurred in or around IL-4 which harbored alleles that were inferred targets of selection, including those shared with Neanderthal/Denisovan species. Although it is likely that these shared IL-4 alleles were inherited from the common ancestor between archaic and modern humans, their presence prompts new questions about whether archaic and modern humans displayed similar phenotypic traits due to their shared alleles and whether these alleles were subject to selection in archaic humans. Furthermore, we observed extreme population differentiation at IL-4 variants and a pattern of divergent haplotypes at this gene. Sakagami et al., one of the few evolutionary studies of IL-4, reported finding divergent haplotypes in their dataset and proposed that this pattern of variation was likely the result of selection in response to different environmental pressures in populations [109]. Overall, our results and those from this previous study highlight the complex nature of adaptive evolution at this gene.

In comparison, the markedly negative H values and extensive EHH mainly around derived alleles at TLR2 in African populations suggest that this gene likely experienced a classic (or hard) selective sweep in Africa. Similar signals of positive selection have been identified at other loci, such as IFN-γ on Chromosome 12 [110] and the MCM6 region on Chromosome 2 associated with lactase persistence [85, 111, 112]. Likewise, the pronounced negative H values at SLC11A1 and the long-range haplotype structure around variation in this gene in non-African populations suggest SLC11A1 underwent a hard selective sweep, leading to the dominance of the H334 haplotype in these groups. Non-Africans also possessed high-frequency selected variants that occurred at low frequency or were absent in populations of African descent, suggesting local (or regional) adaptation outside of Africa. Although African and African-descended populations exhibited significantly negative H statistics at SLC11A1 indicative of a hard selective sweep, they lacked the extensive haplotype structure observed in non-Africans. One possible explanation for this pattern is that classic selective sweeps at this gene in ancestral Africans occurred much earlier in time than those in non-Africans, decreasing the likelihood of observing long-range EHH in African populations. In the end, it is feasible that a skew in the site frequency spectrum suggestive of positive selection may be present in modern human populations, but long-range haplotype structure may be absent [113]. In addition, we found haplotypes carrying selected variants that were specific to populations of African descent, likely reflecting local (or regional) adaptation.

Importantly, the evolutionary signals observed at these genes almost certainly predate the COVID-19 pandemic and therefore reflect deeper historical and ancient selective pressures—likely driven by past host–pathogen interactions or other environmental factors—rather than SARS-CoV-2 itself [114, 115]. Given their role as the primary interface between the host and the environment, it is not surprising that immunity genes have been frequent targets of selection [116, 117]. Consistent with this expectation, multiple studies have identified signals of positive selection at immune loci across populations [106, 110, 118–124]. For example, Barreiro et al. reported strong recent selection at the Toll-like receptor gene clusters likely in response to microbial exposure, with pronounced signals in Eurasian populations [121]. In line with these patterns, our analyses revealed substantial geographic heterogeneity in haplotype structure, allele frequencies, and significant s estimates at loci across different time depths, providing further evidence of recurrent and population-specific selection acting on our immunity-related genes.

Archaic introgression shaping variation in immune genes

Our analyses also revealed the presence of Neanderthal- and Denisovan-derived variants in IL-4, TLR2, and SLC11A1, indicating that introgression from archaic humans contributed to patterns of variation at these genes in modern populations. For example, we identified several Neanderthal-derived SNPs (rs74341183, rs75160897, rs5743700, rs62323856, and rs72731633) at TLR2 that were broadly shared across Eurasian populations. Furthermore, a small subset of these alleles was inferred to be under selection in southern Europe (Italy) and East Asia (Vietnam). Indeed, prior studies have shown that archaic ancestry is non-randomly distributed across the human genome and is often enriched in loci involved in environmental interaction, particularly genes related to immunity and host–pathogen defense [125–127]. Notably, studies have demonstrated that innate immunity loci, including the Toll-like receptor gene cluster (TLR6-TLR1-TLR10), have been frequent targets of archaic introgression in Eurasian populations [124, 128]. More broadly, these observations support the growing view that archaic introgression has played an important role in shaping the diversity at human immune genes and may continue to influence susceptibility or resistance to infectious diseases in modern populations [129]. Moreover, the signatures of selection observed at a subset of introgressed alleles raise the possibility that these variants could have been under selection in archaic humans.

Transcription factor binding at common adaptive loci

Using the SNP2TFBS tool, we identified several targets of selection that were predicted to alter the binding of multiple transcription factors, potentially affecting gene expression. These transcription factors included EGR2 (rs56174361 and rs56408050 in IL-4); SOX10 and ZNF263 (rs2857657 in CCL2); EGR1 and C/EBPα (rs2279015 and rs2279014, respectively, in SLC11A1). Previous studies have shown that EGR2 can influence susceptibility to autoimmune eye disorders [130]. Although the function of ZNF263 is not well-understood, this transcription factor has been implicated in basic cellular processes, such as transcriptional repression [131]. SOX10 has been correlated with rare genetic disorders, such as Waardenburg syndrome [132] and Hirschsprung disease [133]. Additionally, previous studies have reported that EGR1 regulates cell growth, proliferation, and apoptosis, as well as fibrotic gene expression [134, 135]. Finally, the C/EBPα transcription factor recognizes the CCAAT motif in the promoters of target genes. Collectively, these findings suggest that adaptive loci identified in our study could contribute to gene expression regulation, conferring a fitness advantage.

While computational approaches can be powerful for predicting the impact of nucleotide variation on transcription factor binding [136–142], it is important to recognize their current limitations. In particular, only a small fraction of transcription factors in the genome has been functionally characterized [143]. As a result, not all of the TFBSs, containing our SNPs of interest, have known corresponding transcription factors, limiting our ability to infer function in silico [106]. Therefore, future in vitro and in vivo experiments will be needed to determine the extent to which the loci identified in this study influence gene expression.

Selection, evolutionary trade-offs, and respiratory disease

Interestingly, we found that several common SNPs—previously implicated in respiratory disease risk—were inferred to be under selection. For example, studies have reported that the rs13900 C-allele located in the 3′-UTR of CCL2 was correlated with a decreased level of cytokine production [74, 144], predisposing individuals to infectious disease [145–147]. Our analyses showed that the rs13900 T-allele was under selection. Furthermore, rs1898830 G-allele in TLR2 and rs4586 C-allele in CCL2, each identified as targets of selection in our study, have been previously implicated in respiratory disease [65, 71–73]. More explicitly, a recent genetic analysis showed that the frequency of the derived rs1898830 G-allele was significantly lower in TB patients compared to controls in the Chinese Han population—a finding that was replicated in the Tibetan population [73]. In a separate study, the rs4586 T-allele at CCL2 also was significantly associated with an increased risk for chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, which is characterized by airflow obstruction, chronic inflammation, and a progressive decline in lung function [65]. Thus, the rs13900 T-allele, rs1898830 G-allele, and the rs4586 C-allele, all inferred to be under selection, are not associated with increased disease risk and may in fact be protective.

In contrast, we also found that some alleles under selection were correlated with increased disease susceptibility. For example, a case–control study of individuals from Japan showed that the derived rs2279014 T-allele in the 3′-UTR of SLC11A1 was associated with protection from Mycobacterium avium complex, a predominant contributor to disease burden in the region [148, 149]. However, our results showed selection on the ancestral rs2279014 C-allele (not on the rs2279014 T-allele) in East Asian samples, including the Japanese. Furthermore, several studies have reported that the ancestral rs2070874 T-allele at IL-4 was correlated with severe respiratory disease and an exacerbated immune response [57, 68–70]. Yet, our results indicated selection favoring the ancestral rs2070874 T-allele in populations of East Asian and African descent (in contrast, we detected selection on the derived rs2070874 C-allele in European and South Asian populations). While it may seem counter-intuitive for alleles contributing to disease susceptibility to be favored in populations, this pattern could be explained by antagonistic pleiotropy—a genetic phenomenon in which a single allele has opposing effects on fitness, conferring a benefit in one context while imposing a cost in another [150, 151]. Accordingly, these selected alleles may have conferred advantages under certain environmental conditions but later became deleterious as selective pressures shifted over time. Overall, our findings suggest that alleles shaped by selection may continue to influence susceptibility or resistance to modern infectious diseases.

Coding variation and severe COVID-19

Our analysis of SARS-CoV-2–positive cases from the GEN-COVID Multicenter Study in Italy uncovered rare and low-frequency polymorphisms in the TLR2 coding region that influence COVID-19 and COVID-19–related outcomes. These findings are consistent with other studies that have reported association between host polymorphisms, particularly in genes related to immunodeficiency or inflammasomes, and severe COVID-19 [152]. For example, mRNA expression levels of Toll-like receptor genes, including TLR2, have been associated with disease severity in SARS-CoV-2–infected individuals [19, 39]. Furthermore, rare mutations in the Toll-like receptor 7 (TLR7) gene—which is involved in the innate antiviral immune response—were correlated with severe COVID-19 in patients who were admitted to the ICU in Italy [153]. The authors also found that these mutations suppress the production of interferon, predisposing individuals to severe disease [153]. Similarly, a separate case–control study identified rare missense mutations at TLR7 in patients with life-threatening COVID-19 [154]. In addition, rare mutations in other immunity genes (e.g., TLR3, IL12RB1, TBK1, and IFNGR2) have been argued to play a role in the onset of severe COVID-19 symptoms [153–157]. In contrast, a recent large-scale, multiethnic study of > 500,000 individuals reported no significant associations between protein-coding variants (ascertained through whole exome sequencing) and COVID-19 severity after applying a stringent Bonferroni correction [158]. However, given this study design, we argue that this analysis could have inadvertently overlooked novel associations. By comparison, our study employed a hypothesis-driven, candidate-gene approach focused on loci previously implicated in immune-mediated diseases, enabling the detection of significantly associated variants that would otherwise be missed in broader genome-wide analyses. Nonetheless, given the limited sample sizes in the current study, we recognize that our findings will require replication in larger independent cohorts, along with functional validation, to confirm the biological relevance of the variants reported here (as well as those described in prior genetic association analyses).

Notably, our candidate loci have also been implicated in other immune-related diseases. For example, data have indicated that rs139227237 [p.(Phe217Ser)] plays a role in abnormal innate immunity signaling and the pathophysiology of myelodysplastic syndromes [159]. Furthermore, the synonymous rs5743709 [p.(Ala781Ala)] SNP has been associated with the risk of tuberculosis [160]. Although rs5743709 does not alter the amino acid sequence, it is plausible that this SNP could be in strong LD with a functional variant (or exist in a regulatory motif involved in splicing) that directly influences disease risk. Given the overlap in symptoms between severe COVID-19 and other acute illnesses [161, 162], it is possible that rs139227237 and rs5743709 (or other variants in strong LD with them) could affect multiple immune-related diseases (i.e., they could be pleiotropic). Indeed, the pleiotropic effects of variants have been reported in several association studies of complex traits, including disease susceptibility [163–165]. Finally, unlike common variants shaped by positive selection, the rare- and low-frequency candidate loci associated with COVID-19 comorbidity and severity did not exhibit clear signatures of selection. Instead, these rare- and low-frequency variants likely represent more recent mutations, distinct from older, geographically widespread variants influenced by past selective pressures.

Conclusions

In summary, our results indicate that the evolutionary histories of IL-4, TLR2, CCL2, and SLC11A1 have been shaped by multiple processes, including ancient and recent episodes of positive selection, as well as the introduction and persistence of archaic variation. Together, these forces have generated complex patterns of genetic diversity across populations. We suggest that ancient and historical selective pressures imposed by infectious diseases predating COVID-19 have shaped common variation that influence susceptibility or resistance to illnesses. We also identified rare- and low-frequency alleles in the TLR2 coding region that are correlated with COVID-19 comorbidity and severity. Although these mutations likely explain a small fraction of severe COVID-19 variability, a more detailed study of this genetic variation and others across the genome in a large cohort could lead to new insights into the pathophysiology of severe COVID-19. Given the persistence of COVID-19, there is a growing need to identify additional predictive biomarkers of risk for severe disease. In addition to acute disease, long COVID, which is characterized by lingering symptoms affecting multiple organ systems, remains a poorly understood consequence of SARS-CoV-2 infection. Expanding genetic studies to identify variants linked to post-viral conditions such as long COVID may help uncover pathways involved in long-term immune dysfunction.

One of the major challenges that countries worldwide will face over the coming decades, as humans increasingly transform their environments, is the emergence and global spread of infectious respiratory diseases. To minimize the impact of these respiratory illnesses, novel statistical methods and a concerted effort to include diverse populations in genomic studies will be needed. Such approaches will be essential for identifying additional risk alleles that influence disease susceptibility and severity in different populations. Ultimately, the findings from these studies will deepen our understanding of the genetic architecture underlying differential infectious disease risk and help guide the development of effective therapeutic interventions.

Methods

Population samples

We analyzed nucleotide and haplotype variation in four immune-related genes: IL-4 (8690 base pairs [bps]) on Chromosome 5, TLR2 (21,981 bps) on Chromosome 4, CCL2 (1919 bps) on Chromosome 17, and SLC11A1 (14,622 bps) on Chromosome 2 in 2039 unrelated individuals from the 1000 Genomes Project (human genome build GRCh37/hg19, Phase 3) [66]. These individuals originated from Africa, South Asia, Europe, and East Asia [166]. In this dataset, Africa consisted of 99 Esan (ESN, Nigeria), 113 Mandinka (GWD, Gambia), 54 Mende (MSL, Sierra Leone), 107 Yoruba (YRI, Nigeria), 99 Luhya (LWK, Kenya), 60 African Americans (ASW, USA), and 96 Barbadians (ACB, Barbados). Throughout this paper, the term “African populations” refers to indigenous African populations only, while the term “African and African-descended populations” refers to indigenous African and African American/Afro-Caribbean populations, respectively. In addition, we periodically refer to these populations collectively as "populations of African descent". South Asia included 84 Bengali (BEB, Bangladesh), 103 Gujarati (GIH, Gujarati Indian recruited in Houston, TX), 99 Indian Telugu (ITU, Indian Telugu recruited in the UK), 93 Punjabi (PJL, Pakistan), and 99 Tamil (STU, Sri Lanka). Europe consisted of 90 Great British (GBR, Great Britain), 99 Finnish (FIN, Finland), 107 Iberian (IBS, Iberian Peninsula), and 104 Toscani Italians (TSI, Italy). East Asia encompassed 93 Dai (CDX, China), 103 Han (CHB, Han Chinese in Beijing, China), 105 Han (CHS, Han Chinese in South China), 104 Japanese (JPT, Japan), and 99 Kinh (KHV, Vietnam). Individual chromosomes for the Altai Neanderthal genome were downloaded from http://cdna.eva.mpg.de/neandertal/altai/AltaiNeandertal (2013) [167]. Individual chromosomes for the Denisovan genome were downloaded from https://cdna.eva.mpg.de/neandertal/altai/Denisovan/ (2013) [168].

To investigate the genetic architecture of COVID-19 severity and related comorbidities, we examined genetic and clinical data from GEN-COVID, a multicenter network of more than 40 hospitals and affiliated partners that collected high-quality samples and data across Italy for COVID-19 research [67]. More specifically, we analyzed variants genotyped with the Illumina Global Screening Array-24 v3.0 + Multi-Disease BeadChip together with traits related to COVID-19 severity in a cohort of 1141 SARS-CoV-2–infected participants [67]. In addition, we analyzed whole exome sequencing data and graded levels of COVID-19 severity in a different cohort of 2920 SARS-CoV-2–positive individuals from GEN-COVID [67]. The whole exome data were generated using the Illumina NovaSeq6000 System (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA) at a minimum of 20 × coverage, and variant calling was performed following GATK best practices. Finally, because the genotype and whole exome sequencing datasets use distinct identifiers, and ancestry analyses revealed differences in population composition between them, we analyzed the datasets separately.

Nucleotide variation

We extracted variant calls (from VCF files; human genome build GRCh37/hg19) for the IL-4, TLR2, CCL2, and SLC11A1 genes in the 1000 Genomes Project (Phase 3) using VCFtools [169]. The genomic coordinates (i.e., the start and end positions) for these genes were obtained from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database. The minor allele frequency (MAF) was defined as the second most frequent allele at a given site in pooled populations (i.e., 2N = 4078). SNPs were also classified based on the MAF at each site, with variants categorized as common (MAF ≥ 5%), low frequency (1% ≤ MAF < 5%), and rare (MAF < 1%).

Tests of neutrality

To determine if IL-4, TLR2, CCL2, and SLC11A1 evolved neutrally, we calculated two summary statistics, Fay and Wu’s H (H) [83] and Tajima’s D (DT) [170], for each population using DnaSP [76]. These statistical tests analyze different aspects of the site frequency spectrum [171], providing information about the evolutionary forces that have influenced patterns of polymorphic variation in populations. We also simulated theoretical H and DT values within a coalescent framework under different demographic models [119, 172–176] using the msprime software [177, 178]. For African populations, we simulated a 2-, 4-, 6-, 8-, and 10-fold increase in population size (from an initial population size of 7300 individuals) starting at 148,000 ya until the present (2000 replicates per simulation).

For non-Africans, we simulated demographic models involving a population bottleneck (consisting of 1861 individuals) 51,000 ya, which corresponds to the approximate time anatomically modern humans migrated out of Africa [174, 179, 180], followed by a 20-, 40-, 60-, 80-, and 100-fold increase in population size starting at 23,000 ya until the present (2000 replicates per simulation) [172–176, 181]. For all simulations, we used a mutation rate of 2.5 × 10−8, recombination rate of 1 × 10−8, and a generation time of 28 years [174–176]. We also incorporated bidirectional migration between Africans and non-Africans at ~ 30,000 ya and ~ 20,000 ya using the number of migrants given in Gravel et al. [174]. Finally, we assessed the statistical significance of observed H and DT values by comparing them to a distribution of theoretical values. In cases where observed values were either negative or positive in populations, we tested for significance in one direction (i.e., a one-tailed test, P < 0.05). In cases where observed values were a mix of positive and negative numbers across populations, we tested for significant difference in both directions (i.e., a two-tailed test, P < 0.05).

Long-range haplotype homozygosity

We characterized the length of haplotype homozygosity on Chromosomes 2 (SLC11A1), 4 (TLR2), 5 (IL-4), and 17 (CCL2) for each population using the iHS statistic calculated with the selscan and Polaris software packages [84, 119, 182]. To identify outlier iHS values, the unstandardized scores for > 600,000 SNPs across the ~ 240 megabase (Mb) region of Chromosome 2, > 520,000 SNPs across the ~ 190 Mb region of Chromosome 4, > 470,000 SNPs across the ~ 180 Mb region of Chromosome 5, and > 180,000 SNPs across the ~ 80 Mb region of Chromosome 17 in each population were normalized using the norm program implemented in selscan [84]. Standardized | iHS | > 2 corresponds to scores exceeding the 95th percentile of the empirical | iHS | distribution. We further identified SNPs with more extreme signals by noting those with | iHS | values exceeding the 99th and 99.5th percentiles of the same empirical distribution. We also calculated nSL, another haplotype-based statistic, to estimate the length of haplotype homozygosity in populations using selscan [183]. The resulting nSL statistics were normalized using the norm software [84], and outlier nSL scores were identified using the same procedure as the one applied to the | iHS | values. By convention, selscan reports positive statistics when the derived allele (coded as “1”) is under selection and negative statistics when the ancestral allele (coded as “0”) is favored [84, 184–186], which is opposite to the orientation described in Voight et al. [119]. Because none of the variants at IL-4 surpassed the outlier thresholds, we did not include these results in Additional file 1; however, they are presented in Additional file 2: Fig. S1 for completeness.

Finally, we quantified the decay of identity of haplotypes with distance by calculating the extended haplotype homozygosity (EHH) statistic with selscan and Polaris [84, 86, 182] using loci with extreme | iHS | and/or | nSL | statistics identified above as core SNPs. If we observed outlier standardized | iHS | and/or | nSL | scores along with long-range EHH for a given SNP, we classified that SNP as a target of selection.

Selection coefficient estimates

The selection coefficient (s) quantifies the fitness effects of mutations that arise in populations [87], providing insight into the impact of selection at the genetic level. We estimated s for alleles with an MAF ≥ 5% in our immunity genes using the CLUES2 algorithm implemented in the CLUES2 Companion package [87, 89]. CLUES2 incorporates ancestral recombination graphs (ARGs)—which capture the history of genomic regions in a sample of sequences—along with importance sampling of ARGs to estimate s in different time periods [87]. Thus, CLUES2 utilizes the full set of allele histories in a sample of sequences to estimate s [87]. We calculated s (and the corresponding confidence interval) at different time depths (i.e., 1000, 500, and 200 generations ago, which correspond to 28,000 ya, 14,000 ya, and 5600 ya, respectively, assuming a generation time of 28 years) for each gene using CLUES2 Companion [89]. Because selection intensity can vary over time [87], we aimed to capture time points at which selection coefficients might change. SNP loci with significant s estimates (i.e., − log(p) > 1.3 or P < 0.05) at any time depth were classified as targets of positive selection, complementing the | iHS |, | nSL |, and EHH results.

Population differentiation

To detect population-specific allele frequency differentiation, we calculated the Population Branch Statistic (PBS) for SNPs within each gene using a custom Python script that first estimated pairwise FST with VCFtools [169] and then calculated PBS from those FST values with the standard transformation formula implemented in schikit-allel. More specifically, PBS was calculated per SNP using a three-population framework consisting of a focal population (POP1), a closely related sister population (Y), and a more distantly related population (Z). To evaluate whether SNPs exhibited high divergence in each focal population, PBS values were compared to a genome-wide empirical distribution. The genome-wide SNPs in the distribution were generated separately for each trio analyzed, resulting in trio-specific empirical distributions. SNPs with PBS values exceeding the 95th and 99th percentiles of empirical distributions were classified as outliers. A complete list of population trios and the number of genome-wide SNPs used in these analyses are provided in Additional file 1: Tables 28–31.

In addition, we calculated among-population FST at individual polymorphic sites across each gene using the Weir and Cockerham method in VCFtools [169, 187]. The observed FST estimates for SNPs in the IL-4, TLR2, CCL2, and SLC11A1 genes were then compared against an empirical distribution of FST values derived from 8.1 million genome-wide SNPs that were randomly chosen to identify outlier values (i.e., those exceeding the 95th percentile of the distribution). This FST empirical distribution was generated using the same 21 populations that were used to calculate the observed values. Based on this approach, we determined that FST = 0.286 represented the 95th percentile of empirical values.

Linkage disequilibrium

To characterize patterns of allelic associations, we calculated pairwise LD between SNPs within genes using the r2 statistic implemented in PLINK v.2.0. The r2 statistic measures the degree of non-random association between alleles at two sites, with values ranging from 0 (complete independence) to 1 (complete linkage). To focus on strong LD relationships, we retained SNP pairs with r2 ≥ 0.8 [188, 189]. These high-LD SNP pairs were subsequently used to identify clusters of tightly linked variants within IL-4, TLR2, CCL2, and SLC11A1.

Archaic introgression

To identify archaic-derived variants in genes, we applied the archaicAllelePro pipeline implemented in ArchaicSeeker 2.0 [90]. This method detects archaic introgression by integrating modern human genomic variation with archaic hominin reference genomes and an African reference panel representing non-introgressed modern human ancestry. For each retained archaic-derived variant, the pipeline reports several statistics, including the genomic position, reference and alternate alleles, ancestral and derived allele states, presence/absence of the derived allele in archaic reference genomes, and the posterior probability of archaic origin (ArchaicAlleleProb). We considered a posterior probability > 0.95 as strong evidence of archaic ancestry.

Haplotype inferences and relationships

Fully phased haplotypes for each gene were extracted from 2039 individuals in the 1000 Genomes Project using VCFtools [66, 169]. A custom script was then applied to the phased data to: (1) identify distinct haplotypes for each gene, (2) determine the number of times each haplotype occurred in the dataset, and (3) record the populations in which each haplotype was observed. To facilitate visualization of the phylogenetic networks, singleton haplotypes (i.e., haplotypes observed only once within a given gene dataset) were excluded from subsequent analyses. Next, the genealogical relationships among haplotypes were reconstructed for each gene using the median-joining method implemented in the Network v. 5.0 software [91]. The final tree for each gene was the one with the fewest number of mutational changes among all possible trees [190].

Transcription factor binding analysis

We applied the SNP2TFBS tool to polymorphisms that were inferred to be under selection [96]. This tool assesses whether or not SNPs of interest map to transcription factor binding sites (TFBSs) and outputs a list of SNPs that overlap with TFBSs along with the transcription factors that interact with them [96].

Genetic association analyses

We analyzed genotypic and clinical phenotype data from laboratory-confirmed SARS-CoV-2–positive individuals enrolled in the GEN-COVID Multicenter Study in Italy [67]. The GEN-COVID cohorts consisted of SARS-CoV-2–infected individuals from whom DNA and detailed clinical information were collected through a consortium of more than 40 Italian hospitals, 16 Continuity Assistance Special Units, and eight Departments of Preventive Medicine for host genetic analysis [67].

For our initial GEN-COVID cohort of 1141 SARS-CoV-2–positive individuals, we applied standard quality control (QC) procedures in PLINK v.2.0 [191], including assessment of Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (filtering at P < 1 × 10−6), identification of related individuals, detection of strand inconsistencies, and evaluation of overall genotyping rates. Following QC, all candidate SNPs of interest were retained, and the overall genotyping rate was 99.93%. We also identified 58 individuals who were related at closer than the third-degree level; to ensure independence, 29 of these individuals were removed. The final dataset consisted of 1112 unrelated individuals (638 males and 474 females) ranging in age from 18 to 99 years. Prior to testing for any genetic associations, we generated a genetic relationship matrix (GRM) between pairs of individuals using genome-wide SNPs in the 1112 SARS-CoV-2–positive individuals to account for population structure with the GCTA software [192]. We then applied a standard logistic regression approach implemented in GCTA [192] to test for associations between variants in our four genes and binary clinical phenotypes linked to severe COVID-19 (e.g., cancer, chronic kidney, chronic pulmonary, organ transplant, heart failure, diabetes, immunocompromised, asthma, hypertension, cardiac infarction, and stroke). In these regression analyses, the GRM was modeled as a random effect to account for population structure, while sex and age were included as covariates. Statistical significance was determined after applying (1) the saddle point approximation (SPA) method to correct for unbalanced samples sizes in comparative groups and (2) the Benjamini–Hochberg method to control for multiple testing. To further examine significant associations characterized by unusually large β estimates, we applied Firth logistic regression using SAIGE [98–101], which applies a penalized likelihood framework to mitigate bias, including small sample sizes and sparse data. For all significant associations, confidence intervals for β were calculated using the standard formula β ± 1.96 × SE, where SE is the standard error.

Additionally, we analyzed whole exome sequencing data in a second GEN-COVID cohort consisting of 2920 SARS-CoV-2–infected individuals. We first assessed relatedness within this cohort and excluded 80 individuals who were more closely related than third-degree relatives. We also removed one individual who had missing sex information. Our final dataset consisted of 2839 SARS-CoV-2–infected individuals (1688 males and 1151 females) ranging in age from days to 99 years. These 2839 SARS-CoV-2–positive individuals were also classified by degrees of COVID-19 severity as follows: score 0 = not hospitalized (N = 465), 1 = hospitalized with no oxygen therapy (N = 397), 2 = hospitalized with oxygen therapy (N = 930), 3 = hospitalized with CPAP/BiPAP intervention (N = 655), 4 = hospitalized with intubation (N = 202), and 5 = deceased (N = 190). In the current study, severe COVID-19 was defined as the stage of illness requiring hospitalization or resulting in death. Prior to our association analyses, we generated a new GRM using the whole exome sequencing data from the 2839 SARS-CoV-2–positive individuals. We then employed linear mixed model approaches implemented in GCTA [192] to examine the effects of genetic variation on COVID-19 severity. More specifically, we analyzed phenotypes as binary (i.e., not hospitalized versus more severe outcomes) and quantitative traits using logistic and linear mixed models, respectively. In both analyses, the GRM was included as a random effect to account for population structure, while sex and age were incorporated as covariates. Statistical significance was determined after applying SPA and Benjamini–Hochberg corrections, where appropriate. For all significant associations, confidence intervals for β estimates from both logistic and linear regression models were calculated using the standard formula β ± 1.96 × SE.
