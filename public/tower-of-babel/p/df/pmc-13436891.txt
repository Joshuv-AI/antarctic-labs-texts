Total intravenous anesthesia with propofol and remifentanil followed by planned sedation with propofol alone in Intensive care for a patient with glutaric acidemia type-1 undergoing complex thoracolumbar scoliosis surgery
Authors: Jonathan Mathers, Lars O. Sandnes
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 15-year-old boy with a severe phenotype of Glutaric Acidemia Type 1 (GA-1) presented for planned major thoracolumbar scoliosis correction under general anesthesia. Propofol is relatively contraindicated in patients with GA-1 according to latest guidance because of the risk of propofol infusion syndrome. Because of previous use of propofol in the patient when admitted to intensive care and the potential benefits as a sleep agent we decided to use propofol for his surgery and postoperative sedation. We report the successful use of propofol for general anesthesia and postoperative sedation on intensive care.

Introduction

Glutaric Acidemia Type 1 (GA-1) is a rare, autosomal recessive inherited error of metabolism characterized by the accumulation of organic acid metabolites. It was first reported in 1975 by Goodman et al.[1] as macrocephaly at birth and thereafter dystonia with degeneration of caudate and putamen. GA-1 is caused by heterogenous mutations in the glutaryl-CoA dehydrogenase gene and over 63 different mutations have been identified.[2] The global incidence of GA-1 stands at about 1:100,000 newborns.[3]

The phenotypic profile of children diagnosed with GA-1 can vary,[1] but most children present with macrocephaly at birth followed by psychomotor delay, dystonia, and thereafter spastic quadriparesis. Cognition appears relatively preserved but patients have trouble talking or performing commands due to muscle weakness or spasticity. Patients with GA-1 can present for a variety of surgical procedures related to the condition such a neurosurgery (VP shunts), orthopedic surgery (correction of scoliosis and spasticity operations), as well as general surgery (insertion of percutaneous gastrojejunostomy (PEG)) together with muscle biopsies, and radiological imaging.[4]

There are few case reports available on the best or optimal general anesthesia for patients with GA-1[456789] and only one case report of the use of total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA) with two twins requiring a ventriculosubperitoneal shunt but the duration of surgery and total propofol dose are not described.[7] A single case report of remimazolam with remifentanil has been described for general anesthesia where inhalational anesthetics were avoided to reduce postoperative nausea and vomiting but remimazolam is not licensed for general anesthesia in Norway.[5] Propofol anesthesia as a maintenance agent is described as contraindicated according to the latest guidance due to the risk of propofol infusion syndrome.[4]

We report on the anesthesia and postoperative course of a 15-year-old boy who underwent a planned thoracolumbar scoliosis correction under TIVA with planned postoperative ventilation on the intensive care unit (ICU) under propofol sedation.

Case Report

A 15-year-old boy with GA-1 was scheduled for corrective surgery for a severe thoracolumbar scoliosis. He was born in Norway to non-consanguineous parents, and his diagnosis was made in early infancy after he presented to hospital with severe metabolic acidosis combined with feeding difficulties and regression of his psychomotor milestones.

The patient was tetraplegic and had a neuromuscular scoliosis. He required a cough-assist device as well as the need for positive pressure ventilatory support at night via a tracheostomy. He had a deep brain stimulator implanted in addition to a spinal Baclofen pump to alleviate his spasticity.

An anesthetic plan of TIVA with planned postoperative ventilation was made due to experience from previous ICU admissions with the use of propofol sedation over long periods and that it would facilitate neurological monitoring under anesthesia. It was decided to ventilate the patient overnight on the ICU due to the potential for changes in his ventilatory profile after correction of the severe scoliosis, development of a metabolic crisis postoperatively as well as the daytime-only availability for reactivation of his deep brain stimulator. He had an established “SOS”- regime to be used in periods of metabolic crisis (for example when he had an infection) and this included Intralipid which is a component of propofol.

Induction of anesthesia was performed with a 100% oxygen and Sevoflurane via his tracheostomy whereupon peripheral IV access was obtained and TIVA was started. Invasive monitoring was established with an arterial line and subclavian central line. He was then placed in the prone position for surgery, and his deep brain stimulator was shut down. Eleveld pharmacokinetic models were used for both propofol and remifentanil to maintain anesthesia with targets under anesthesia of 3–4.5 mcg/ml and 4–8 ng/ml end target concentration respectively. Bispectral analysis (BIS) was not used to guide TIVA administration due to the concerns of inaccuracy of BIS with quadriplegic paresis.[10]

The surgery was long and lasted approximately seven and half hours during that he lost approximately 1,250 ml of blood. Red cell salvage was used during surgery, and he received 400 ml of autologous blood as well as two units of packed red cells. He was cardiovascularly stable under the whole procedure.

He was transferred sedated to the intensive care unit where his arriving blood gas was pH: 7.35 (normal range 7.36–7.44), pO2: 32.8 kPa, pCO2: 5.45 kPa, Base excess: −6.2 mmol/L, Lactate: 0.9 mmol/L, Sodium 139 mmol/L, and Potassium 4,3 mmol/L. His course on ICU was otherwise unremarkable with 22 hours of continual sedation and optimized ventilation under propofol sedation. His propofol infusion ran at between 3 and 4 mg/kg/hour with some intermittent boluses with him receiving a total of 1274 mg. He was transferred to the ward and discharged home 11 days after his operation. During anesthesia he received a total of 2,650 mg of propofol over seven and a half hours and a further 1,274 mg on intensive care. This amounts to 103 mg/kg over a 24-hour period and 70 mg/kg in the perioperative period. There was no evidence of metabolic disturbance because of his significant propofol load, and his perioperative and postoperative course were otherwise unremarkable.

Discussion

GA-1 is a rare autosomal-recessive inherited syndrome whose widespread effects can result in children and adults affected present for a variety of different surgeries. Because of the nature of metabolic dysfunction, published guidance recommends the avoidance of propofol as a maintenance anesthetic.[4] We present a report of a child with a severe phenotype of GA-1 who received a long anesthetic with TIVA followed by propofol alone sedation in ICU without any perceived problems. Whilst we do not claim that propofol can be considered completely safe for all patients in all scenarios this case helps to build a clearer picture of the available anesthetic options and can help support the decision to use TIVA where clinically indicated for patients with GA-1.
