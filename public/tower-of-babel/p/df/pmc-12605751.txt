Medical debt, financial risk factors, and deferred care among low-wage workers
Authors: Mathieu Despard, Sally A Hageman, Stephen Roll
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionMedical debt is widely regarded as a social problem that reflects growing out-of-pocket costs. Yet whether medical debt acts a social determinant of health by discouraging people to seek additional care may depend on one's ability to repay this debt.MethodsWe use data from the first wave of a survey of a nationally representative sample of 2090 low-wage workers in the U.S. We ran linear probability models to predict putting off filling prescriptions, receiving primary medical care, and receiving specialty medical care based on medical debt disposition.ResultsWe find that workers with medical debt they cannot afford to repay are more likely to defer three types of health care and are confronted with several other financial risk factors compared with workers with medical debt they are repaying. Also, concerning putting off needed health care, there is no difference between workers who are repaying their medical debt and those with no medical debt.DiscussionThese findings suggest the need to strengthen financial assistance policies and programs and ensure access to low-cost health coverage for low-wage workers.

Introduction

Nearly a fifth (16.4%) of U.S. households have medical debt with a median value of $2,000, which represents amounts owed that have not been paid in full or that were being repaid over time. Medical debt prevalence is higher among Black adults and households with children and/or households with members who lack health insurance, are living with a disability, and/or are in poor or fair health.1,2 Medical debt is an important indicator of patients’ out-of-pocket responsibility that remains after insurance or for care not covered by insurance. In 2022, 12% of U.S. households had medical debt that was in collections at a median value of $731,3 which may indicate problematic medical debt—that which patients may be unable to repay. Concerning additional needed care, 8% of U.S. adults said they deferred or delayed medical care and prescription drugs due to cost concerns.4 Additional signs of patient cost sensitivity include the link between high deductibles and decreased health care utilization5-7 and studies that show that cancer survivors defer care due to cost concerns.8 While medical debt is associated with poor health outcomes and mortality9,10 it is unclear whether this may be related to deferred or delayed additional care due to cost concerns. The link between medical debt—amounts owed for care already received—and health care hardship—delaying or deferring additional care not yet received due to anticipated costs—is unclear. People may be reluctant to seek additional care that could add to their debt until they finish paying what they currently owe. Yet having medical debt, like any other form of debt, may not be a problem if it is being repaid and does not affect one's decision to seek additional care they need. In this case, it may be medical debt people are unable to repay that is a driver of cost sensitivity that affects decisions to delay or defer additional needed care. In this brief report, we examine the relationship between having medical debt—including the disposition of this debt—and deferring or delaying three types of health care due to cost among a nationally representative sample of low-wage workers in the U.S. We examine these relationships controlling for insurance coverage, income, and liquid assets—resources that workers could use to pay for health care. We focus on low-wage workers as a vulnerable population for three reasons. First, groups that experience health disparities such as women of color are disproportionately segregated into low-wage work. Second, the nature of low-wage work is more likely than higher-wage work to entail activities and requirements for which maintaining physical health is vital to maintain employment. Third, low-wage workers have experienced important coverage gains via Medicaid expansion and Affordable Care Act (ACA) premium tax credits that may be reduced following recent legislation.

Data and methods

Data for this study are from Wave One of the Workforce Economic Inclusion and Mobility (WEIM) project, administered in November 2023 to a nationally representative sample (N = 2426) of U.S. households with lower-wage workers earning < 250% of the Federal Poverty Line. This sample was drawn from the AmeriSpeak panel, administered by the National Opinion Research Center (NORC). The Institutional Review Board at a university employing two of the authors gave approval for the study. After excluding respondents not currently employed, our analytic sample was 2090 low-wage workers. To measure medical debt as the predictor variable of interest, we asked whether respondents currently owed any money for medical or dental bills for themselves or another family member and the disposition of these bills (see Appendix A for survey wording). Medical debt was coded as “1” if respondents answered yes to any of the questions regarding bill disposition and “0” if the answer to all these disposition questions was no. To measure whether respondents were able to repay their medical debt, we coded responses as “1” if respondents said they had past due bills or bills they were unable to pay and “0” if they said they were paying their bills off over time directly to a provider or on a credit card. For the dependent variable of interest, we assessed deferred health care by asking whether in the prior 12 months respondents did not get primary care, specialty care, and/or prescription medication they needed because they could not afford it. We ran three sets of linear probability models to predict the likelihood of deferring three types of health care. The first set compared respondents with and without medical debt. The second set assessed ability to repay—comparing respondents who had medical debt they could not afford to repay with respondents who were repaying medical debt. The third set compared those who were repaying medical debt with those who had no medical debt. The margins command in Stata 18 was used to calculate predicted probabilities of deferred health care with respect to medical debt disposition. Covariates included age, gender, race/ethnicity, household income, liquid financial assets, partner status, number of children in the home, health insurance type (public, private, employer-based, or none), and health status as measured with the SF-12 general health question. Multi-step weighting was used to account for sampling probabilities, non-response, and U.S. population representativeness.

Results

The sample was 60% white, 12% black, 19% Hispanic, and 9% other race/ethnicity respondents, and 53% male and 47% female. The majority (92%) had household incomes below $70,000, over half (52%) had $1000 or less in liquid assets, 43% had employer-based health coverage, 29% public coverage (eg, Medicaid), 17% private coverage, and 11% no coverage. Over a third (35%) of respondents had medical debt, 60% of which (21% of the total sample) was past-due medical or dental bills they could not pay. Less than a fifth of respondents reported putting off primary medical care (17%), specialty medical care (15%), and prescription medications (17%) within the prior 12 months due to cost. Based on a comparison of unweighted adjusted R-squared values, adding the presence of any medical debt to models predicting the three types of deferred health care increased the proportion of variance explained by an average of 72% from baseline models with covariates only, including income, liquid assets, and insurance coverage. Table 1 show that respondents with medical debt had a greater probability of all three types of deferred health care compared with respondents with no medical debt (P < 0.001). For example, respondents with medical debt had a 31% predicted probability of putting off getting prescription medications compared with 10% of those without medical debt, controlling for income, health insurance, and other factors. Whereas Table 1 shows results comparing workers with and without medical debt, Table 2 shows results among workers with medical debt, assessing ability to repay by comparing those who were unable to repay their medical debt with those who were repaying their medical debt. Workers unable to repay had a greater probability of all three types of deferred health care compared with respondents with medical debt they were repaying (P < 0.001). For example, respondents with medical debt they could not repay had a 39% predicted probability of putting off getting primary medical care compared with 15% of those with medical debt they were repaying, controlling for income, health insurance, and other factors. Other factors with a statistically significant association with deferred health care across the two sets of models included liquid financial assets, health insurance, and health status (see Tables 1 and 2). Our final set of models compared those who had medical debt they were repaying with those who had no medical debt. We found no statistically significant differences in deferred health care between these two groups of low-wage workers. Lastly, to better understand findings reflected in Table 2, we examined differences between low-wage workers based on ability to repay medical debt. Those with past due medical or dental bills they could not pay had less in liquid assets and were less likely to have money saved for emergencies (P < 0.001), and were more likely to hold student debt (P < 0.01), personal loans from banks (P < 0.05) and online lenders (P < 0.001), and “buy now pay later” (P < 0.01), auto title (P < 0.05) and payday loans (P < 0.001). For example, 48% of those with past due bills had one or more student loans compared with 32% of those paying their bills. Those with past due bills were also more likely to have unstable jobs (P < 0.05) and have sold blood plasma for money (P < 0.05). Concerning meeting other basic needs, those with past due bills were also more likely than those paying medical or dental bills to have problems making housing payments, paying other bills, and to be uncertain they will have enough food to eat in the next 4 weeks (P < 0.001). Moreover, those with past due bills experienced an average of 3.35 income and expense shocks (eg, expensive car repair, reduction of work hours) compared with 2.10 among those repaying debt. However, there were no statistically significant differences with respect to household income. Regarding health status, those with past-due bills were more likely to say they were in fair or poor health (41%) compared with those paying their bills (23%) (P < 0.05). Though the prevalence of medical debt is greater among Black adults compared with adults with other race or ethnic identities,1 race and ethnicity was not statistically significant factor in any of our models predicting putting off health care.

Discussion

We find an association between medical debt and putting off needed health care among a nationally representative sample of low-wage workers in the U.S. Yet this association is related to ability to repay. Having medical debt in and of itself is not a risk factor for putting off additional needed health care among low-wage workers; it is having medical debt that some workers cannot afford to repay—workers who face a constellation of additional financial challenges such as unstable employment and greater incidence of income and expense shocks. One way to reduce the risk of deferred care among low-wage workers with medical or dental bills they cannot pay is to strengthen charity care policies and programs11 and medical debt protections12 including by standardizing eligibility criteria and making it easier to apply for charity care.13 Another strategy is to ensure low-wage workers have access to low-cost health coverage. Through Medicaid expansion and premium tax credits for marketplace plans, the ACA has helped reduce out-of-pocket costs14,15 and problems paying medical bills.16 However, Medicaid and premium tax credit spending reductions in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act that was signed into law on July 4, 2025 are projected to result in a loss of health coverage for 16 million people,17 many of whom are low-wage workers. Our findings highlight the critical importance of maintaining access to low-cost health plans.

Conclusion

The degree to which patients can absorb out-of-pocket health care costs should be considered based on whether their financial obligations discourage future health care utilization. Low-wage workers with past due medical and dental bills they cannot pay might be discouraged from seeking additional care they need and face a host of other problems like food insufficiency and student debt. Strategies to lower out-of-pocket health care costs among low-wage workers and other vulnerable populations should be a priority for policymakers.
