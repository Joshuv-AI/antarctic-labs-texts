Comparison between grip strength and relative grip strength in their relationship with allostatic load among adolescents
Authors: Huiqiong Xu, Xi Wang, Wan Xiao, Yang Xie, Xinyu Zhang, Shaojun Xu, Yuhui Wan, Fangbiao Tao
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo assess the associations of muscle strength, measure by grip strength and relative grip strength (grip strength divided by body weight), on the allostatic load (AL) index in adolescent boys and girls.MethodsA total of 1,323 students were recruited (boys = 776, girls = 547). Data on general demographic characteristics, anthropometric indicators, grip strength, blood pressure, pulse rate, and biological samples were collected. A 20-indicator-based AL index (using the AL1, AL2, and AL3 calculation methods) was used as the dependent variable. Multiple linear regression models were used to evaluate the association between grip strength, relative grip strength, and AL. All analyses were stratified by sex.ResultsThe mean age of the participants was 14.17 ± 1.45 years. Overall, the linear regression model adjusting for demografic characteristics showed that greater grip strength was associated with higher AL1 scores (R2 = 10.1%, β = 0.096, P < 0.001). Then, we further adjusted for body weight, and negative associations were observed between grip strength and AL1 (R2 = 35.3%, β = -0.027, P = 0.030). However, after sex stratification, this association was not statistically significant in girls. Moreover, relative grip strength was negatively associated with AL1 (R2 = 14.8%, β = -8.529, P < 0.001) after adjusting for demographic characteristics in the total sample. Specifically, relative grip strength was more strongly associated with the AL1 burden in boys than in girls. Finally, sensitivity analyses of AL2 and AL3 yielded similar results.ConclusionWe observed that compared with grip strength, relative grip strength might be a useful indicator for the identification of AL burden in adolescents. The results suggest that strengthening relative handgrip strength in adolescents might help reduce the AL burden, especially in boys.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-024-20129-0.Keywords: Grip strength, Alloatatic load, Biomarkers, Adolescent, Sex

Introduction

The allostatic load (AL) framework was proposed by McEwen and Stellar [1] to provide a conceptual and measurement model that considers the “cumulative wear and tear” of the body over time due to human responses to stressors. It is commonly measured in the older adult population. However, studies are now increasingly reporting AL in younger populations, including children and adolescents [2, 3]. In addition, several studies have documented that among all muscle function tests, grip strength is an estimate of isometric strength in the upper extremities, which has gained attention as it is easy to measure using a portable, simple, inexpensive, and non-invasive device [4, 5].

Previous research has identified that greater grip strength across the life course, including childhood, young adulthood, and mid-adulthood, could protect against adverse cardiometabolic health [6]. In contrast, there was also evidence that minimal significant association was observed between AL measurements and grip strength [7]. Furthermore, strength measures should be normalized to body weight. Therefore, grip strength normalized by body weight (relative grip strength) has been proposed as a useful tool for assessing functional capacity in post-menopausal women [8]. However, only a few studies have compared the relationship between grip strength and relative grip strength and health outcomes [9], especially in adolescence, which is a foundation for future health [10].

Notably, individual biomarkers and biological systems [11], and their computational methods [7] included in the AL differed widely across studies. The highest risk quartile (classical method) scoring for AL remains a popular method today, with a systematic review reporting that 73% of included studies used this approach despite the potential loss of information across the risk spectrum [11]. McLoughlin et al. used data from the Irish Longitudinal Study on Ageing (TILDA) to compare the utility of 14 different scoring methods that have been used to compose AL (such as high-risk quartiles, deciles, two-tailed cut-points, Z-scores, system-weighted indices, clinical cut-points, and sex-specific scores), and the results suggested that the choice of the AL scoring method has a relatively small effect on predicting some important health outcomes [7]. At the same time, previous studies [12, 13] indicated that the Z-score (continuity of preserved variables) and systematic weighting (reduced bias caused by an excess of certain system biomarkers) might be good choices for future research, with a particular emphasis on the use of sex-specific cutoff points [7]. However, the studies mentioned above were conducted in the adult population. Thus, this study was expanded to include an adolescent sample.

To address these gaps, the present study is one of the first attempts to compare grip strength and relative grip strength in terms of their relationship with different AL calculation scores among adolescents, as well as the sex differences. We hypothesized that greater grip strength and relative grip strength would be associated with a lower burden of AL in adolescents, then relative grip strength would be superior to simple grip strength in representing the AL burden, and we also sought to ascertain whether there were any apparent gender differences for these relationships.

Methods

Sample and procedures

Between November and December 2021, a cross-sectional survey was conducted among middle school students in grades 7 and 10 in Shenzhen City, Guangdong Province, China. Convenient sampling was used to extract study population. First, we purposely selected two districts in Shenzhen City (Baoan District and Guangming District) based on their economic level and geographical location. Next, in each of district we randomly selected two middle schools. Then, a minimum of 200 students were selected from each school for the survey. Visits to the study school lasted for 4 days, during which participants’ self-reported questionnaires, physical and fitness indicators, and biological specimens were collected. Afterward, 1,428 valid questionnaires and 1,328 valid biological and physical indicators were collected. Only those individuals who provided complete information regarding the variables were included in this study. This resulted in a final sample of 1,323 participants.

Written informed consent was obtained from the participants, their parents, and their guardians. Ethical approval was granted by the Ethics Committee of the Anhui Medical University (20200573).

Measures

Grip strength and relative grip strength

In this study, grip strength was assessed using an electronic hand dynamometer (WCS-10000, Shanghai, China) adjusted to fit the size of each participant’s hand according to sex [14]. Participants were asked to stand while kee.

ping their upper arms tight against the trunk and then squeeze the dynamometer as hard as possible for at least three seconds. The test was performed twice, and the highest score for each hand was recorded. The mean values of the right and left hands, expressed in kg, were used in the analysis. This was the same method used by López-Gil et al. [15, 16]. It should be noted that it is necessary to carry out physical preparatory activities before the test, but not strenuous exercise, at the same time, participants who don’t have experience in testing should be tutored and provided with opportunities to practice.

In addition, to eliminate the influence of body weight on grip strength, we calculated the relative grip strength = grip strength divided by body weight. The body weight in this study was measured by trained staff and recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg by an electronic scale.

Allostatic load

A total of 20 biomarkers across the immune and inflammatory systems (high-sensitivity C-reactive protein, Interleukin (IL)-6, IL-8, IL-1β, and tumor necrosis factor-α), metabolic system (fasting blood glucose, insulin, triacylglycerol, total cholesterol, high-density lipoprotein (HDL), low-density lipoprotein, body mass index, and waist circumference), cardiovascular system (systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and pulse rate), and kidney/liver function (creatinine, urea nitrogen, alanine aminotransferase, and total bilirubin) were used to construct the AL indicators in this study (Table S1). The AL scores were estimated based on three different calculations: AL1, AL2, and AL3.

AL1 was calculated using the group AL index method, originally proposed by Seeman et al. [17] and has become one of the most typical methods for AL measurement [18]. Dichotomous indicator variables were created for each biomarker based on gender; the high-risk quartiles (≥ P75) were defined as “1”, and those below this threshold were defined as “0”, and HDL was reverse-coded (the quartile cutoffs and assay types are presented in Table S1). These biomarkers were added to an index ranging from 0 to 20, with a higher score reflecting a higher AL burden. AL2 is based on AL1, which was systematically weighted to reduce deviations caused by an excessive proportion of biomarkers in some systems. For example, metabolic biomarkers tend to be the most populous in the AL literature [19]. AL3 is the Z-score method, which can maintain the continuous properties of each variable; each biomarker was standardized to a mean of 0 and a standard deviation of 1, and then summed the Z-scores of all indicators to generate an overall AL score [20].

Covariates

We used a literature review and directed acyclic graphs [21] to identify confounding factors (Figure S1) that were included in the analyses to prevent overfitting.

The covariates included age [22], sex (boy or girl) [22], parents’ education level (primary school or below, junior middle school, senior middle school, college or higher) [3], self-perceived family economic status (poor, moderate, or good) [3], as well as lifestyle-related behaviors, including smoking [23], drinking [23], physical exercise [24, 25], and screen time [26] in this study. Due to the low rates of smoking (1.3%) and drinking (5.5%) among middle school students, they were combined into one variable.

Statistical analyses

All analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0, and statistical significance was set at a P-value < 0.05. First, sociodemographic data, grip strength, relative grip strength, and different AL scores were described for both the total sample and for boys and girls separately. Sex differences were assessed using the chi-square test for categorical variables and independent samples t-test for continuous variables. Next, the distribution of each variable was checked using histograms, which indicated that they appeared normal (Figures S2-S3). Therefore, we used Pearson’s correlations to test the associations between grip strength, relative grip strength, and different AL scores, as well as the relationships between different subgroups. In general, after taking the absolute value, 0-0.1 indicates no correlation, 0.1–0.3 indicates a small or weak correlation, 0.3–0.5 indicates a medium correlation, and 0.5-1.0 indicates a strong correlation. Moreover, as age is the strongest predictor of health, we also examined which AL scores were most strongly correlated with chronological age [7]. Finally, multiple linear regression models were applied to analyze the relationship between grip strength, relative grip strength, and the AL score. We reported R square, non-standardized β coefficients, and P-value. In the total sample, for the analysis of grip strength, five separate models were run for each outcome with the addition of more stringent covariates: Model 1 unadjusted model with only the dependent variable, Model 2 adjusted model with age and biological sex, Model 3 adjusted model with age, biological sex, parents’ education level, self-perceived family economic status, and lifestyle-related behaviors; Model 4 adjusted model with weight; and Model 5 adjusted model with a combination of Model 3 and Model 4; for the analysis of relative grip strength, to prevent overcontrol, we used three models: Model 1 was unadjusted; Model 2 was adjusted for age and biological sex; and Model 3 was further adjusted for parents’ education level, self-perceived family economic status, and lifestyle-related behaviors based on Model 2, since weight is already included in relative grip strength. However, biological sex was not included as a covariate in the sex subgroup.

Pearson correlation

In the total sample, grip strength was positively correlated with immune1, metabolic1, kidney/liver function1, and AL1 levels (r = 0.152, 0.109, 0.210, and 0.209, respectively; P < 0.01). However, relative grip strength was negatively correlated with metabolic1, kidney/liver function1, and AL1 (r = -0.283, -0.094, and − 0.211, respectively; P < 0.01). Specifically, the results were similar for the sex subgroups, but the correlation was higher for boys than for girls.

Figure 1 shows that grip strength, relative grip strength, and AL1 were positively correlated with age (P < 0.01). However, in the AL subgroup, there was no statistically significant correlation between metabolic1 and age, and cardiovascular1 revealed negative correlations with age.

Multiple linear regression

The results for the AL1 are presented in Table 2, which shows that grip strength was positively associated with AL1, and the proportion of variance explained was 10.1% (Model 3: β = 0.096, P < 0.001) in the total sample after adjusting for demographic characteristics. The results were similar in the sex subgroups, with R2 being slightly higher for boys (R2 = 11.4%) than for girls (R2 = 9.1%). Specifically, there was significant association between grip strength and AL1 in boys (Model 4: R2 = 37.4%, β = -0.031, P = 0.029; Model 5: R2 = 38.8%, β = -0.032, P = 0.048); however, there was no significant association between grip strength and AL1 in girls (Model 4: R2 = 29.6%, β = -0.007, P = 0.776; Model 5: R2 = 30.2%, β = -0.007, P = 0.776).

Sensitivity analysis

To test the robustness of the results, the associations of grip strength and relative grip strength with AL2 and AL3 were used for the sensitivity analysis (Figures S4-S5 and Tables S2-S5). The overall results, similar to AL1. Specifically, in the total sample and sex subgroup after adjusting for demographic characteristics, the grip strength was positively associated with AL2 and AL3, and relative grip strength was negatively associated with AL2 and AL3, as shown in Model 3. These suggest that the different evaluation methods are relatively stable for AL construction. However, in the total sample, it is worth noting that there were no statistically significant associations between grip strength and AL2 or AL3 (P > 0.05) in Model 5. In the sex group, there were no statistically significant associations between grip strength and AL2 or AL3 (P > 0.05) in Model 4 and Model 5.

Discussion

For the first time, this study assessed the association of grip strength and relative grip strength with AL, a measure of biological multisystem dysregulation among adolescents from a relatively developed southern city in China. Stratification by sex highlighted that grip strength and biological indicators were relevant for identifying the boys and girls participants.

Specifically, our results revealed a positive association between grip strength and AL burden even after adjusting for demographic characteristics. The direction of the observed relationship was unexpected but not without precedent. Freire et al. observed that high handgrip strength was associated with high AL values in a sample of 256 older community-dwelling adults in Brazil, yet, they hypothesized that this contradiction could be due to survival bias [27]. It may also be due to a positive relationships between grip strength and weight in children and adolescents [28], while being overweight or obese can cause low-grade chronic inflammation, endocrine, and metabolic disorders [29]. Thus, in this study, the directionality of the results changed when weight was further controlled for as a confounder. It is especially important for adolescents, whose weight changes rapidly, and analyzing only absolute grip strength may limit the generalizability of the study.

We also demonstrated that greater relative grip strength was associated with lower AL burden in adolescents. In line with the results of previous studies [30], adolescents with greater grip strength normalized by body mass had a significant association with longitudinal health maintenance and health improvements. Furthermore, a longitudinal cohort study of 3,970 children aged 10–13 years revealed that normalized handgrip strength was independently associated with lower cardiometabolic risk in boys and girls [31]. These results suggest that relative grip strength may be better than simple grip strength in representing AL burden.

Another interesting finding was the sex differences in the above association analyses. Overall, compared with girls, boys’ grip strength or relative grip strength explained a higher proportion of the variance. It suggested that boys may benefit most from interventions to improve relative grip strength. Few studies have examined the effect of gender on the association between muscular strength and health outcome with inconsistent findings [30, 32]. A longitudinal cohort study reported that adolescents with low normalized grip strength at baseline had a higher prevalence of declining or poor health in boys than girls (60.2%>51.0%) [30]. Whereas evidence from the Cooperative Health Research in the Region of Augsburg (KORA)-Age Study has shown that the association between muscular strength and all-cause mortality tended to be stronger in women [32]. Although there is no consensus regarding the incorporation of sex-specific risk values in the calculation of the AL index, a growing number of studies have considered the potential impact of sex differences [7, 33]. Therefore, we recommend that future studies continue to examine the use of sex-specific biomarkers, especially in situations where the distribution of biomarkers varies by sex [34], or in fact, there may be large sex differences, such as physical or physical fitness [28].

Finally, to evaluate the stability of the AL score, we used three different calculation methods for sensitivity analyses, and similar results were obtained, that is, grip strength was positively correlated with three AL scores, and relative grip strength was negatively correlated with three AL scores. Therefore, we echoed the conclusion of other studies [12, 17], that the classical method used in most papers and perhaps the concept of constructing AL around this scoring method should be considered. Nevertheless, in the context of this study, it is necessary to critically consider whether the choice of the scoring method can greatly affect the results.

Although this study had significant strengths, such as the use of a multi-method assessment of AL, comparing grip strength and relative grip strength in their relationship with AL, and incorporating sex differences in the analysis, it also had several limitations that should be addressed in future research. First, this study did not include any neuroendocrine markers, which is an important limitation as they are hypothesized to play a central role in the stress response. However, a systematic review reported that only half of the studies included at least one hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis biomarker [11]. Second, this study used cross-sectional data; therefore, the causal relationships between the variables should be examined in a longitudinal study in the future, and the potential effects of different stages of adolescence can be further taken into account. Third, given the homogeneous sample of adolescents from a single geographic area, we were cautious in generalizing our results to the adolescent population at large, as greater heterogeneity in race/ethnicity and maturity may influence the associations between strength and various biochemical indicators. Future studies could expand the sample size, encompass adolescents from diverse regions and economic levels, thereby selecting a more representative adolescent cohort. Fourth, studies found that the social distancing measures and home confinement implemented during the COVID-19 pandemic may lead to decreased levels of physical activity [35, 36], and may affect grip strength and body weight. However, López-Bueno et al. [37] observed no significant change in cardiovascular health before and after the quarantine. In addition, there were also results suggested that physical fitness and coordination may been negatively affected during the confinement, and that the growth curve of physical fitness could return to normal values when adolescents were allowed to go out for sports and physical activities [38, 39]. Finally, the average grip strength of both hands was used in this study, while other studies have chosen the dominant hand. Currently reported summary measures of grip strength vary widely, including maximum or average values, from one to two to three attempts, with either one hand or the dominant hand alone [5], in addition, there exist diverse methodologies for calculating relative grip strength, not only in absolute terms (kilograms) relative to weight, but also relative to BMI, height, and body fat mass.

Implication

The current study, although not directly focused on clinical samples, can offer valuable insights for enhancing clinical care and possesses significant implications for public health interventions geared towards adolescent health improvement. Specifically, our findings suggest that relative grip strength, which takes into account an individual’s body weight, may serve as a more sensitive marker of AL in adolescents, compared to absolute grip strength. This underscores the importance of considering not merely absolute muscle strength but also body composition when assessing health risks among adolescents. Furthermore, the observed stronger correlation between relative grip strength and AL in boys underscores potential gender-specific differences in the efficacy of interventions aimed at enhancing handgrip strength. Consequently, future interventions designed to mitigate AL in adolescents, especially among boys, should prioritize strategies that not only fortify grip muscles but also foster healthy body weight and composition. This might encompass a multifaceted approach, combining physical activity programs, nutrition education, and weight management initiatives tailored to the distinct needs and characteristics of adolescent boys and girls.

Conclusions

Our findings provide insights into the extent to which relative grip strength may confer protection against AL burden in adolescents. The results of sex stratification should be considered in the development of targeted interventions in the future. Adolescents, especially boys, should be encouraged to participate in muscle-building exercises to promote their current health status. Additional studies, particularly cohort of large samples, are needed to better understand how grip strength status during childhood and adolescence tracks into and influences health throughout adulthood.
