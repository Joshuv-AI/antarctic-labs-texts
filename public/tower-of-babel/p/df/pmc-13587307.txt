The impact of public health and social measures on social contact patterns and transmission of COVID-19 in Hong Kong
Authors: Horace Choi, Kiesha Prem, Shirley Kwok, Eric Lau, Gabriel M Leung, Mark Jit, Kathy Leung, Joseph T Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPublic health and social measures (PHSMs) are key interventions during pandemics. The study period spanned the infection-naïve phase before 2022 through the city’s fifth wave of COVID-19 (January–April 2022) driven by the Omicron BA.2 variant. Background

Understanding social contact patterns, including age-dependence, frequency, location, and type of contacts, is crucial to projecting the spread of infectious diseases and developing effective public health and social measures (PHSMs) such as school and workplace closures, stay-at-home orders, and mass gathering restrictions [1, 2]. Contact patterns changed dramatically in many countries as the COVID-19 pandemic unfolded and evolved [3, 4]. Extensive social contact studies, such as POLYMOD and CoMix, have been conducted in Western Europe and North America before and during the pandemic, providing insights to explore the corresponding changes [5–9]. For example, a survey study in the United Kingdom reported that the average number of daily contacts dropped to 2.8 in the first four days when lockdown was implemented in March 2020, compared to an estimate of 10.8 from POLYMOD in 2005–2006 [5]. In a population-based Canadian study, the number of contacts decreased from 7.8 before the pandemic in 2018–2019 to 3.1 during the lockdown in spring 2020 [9]. In Asia, a Chinese study documented a decrease in contacts in Wuhan and Shanghai from 14.6 to 18.8 in 2017–2019 to just 2.0–2.3 per day in February 2020, when the number of reported cases of the first wave of the pandemic peaked across the Chinese Mainland and stringent interventions (e.g., social distancing policies) were implemented [10]. The sizeable reductions in social contacts underscored the global impact of PHSMs on contact patterns and, potentially, transmission dynamics [10].

Hong Kong offers a unique setting for studying social contact patterns during the COVID-19 pandemic. To control transmission, the government implemented a broad suite of PHSMs, including restrictions on group gatherings (e.g., not more than 2/4/8 people) and on inbound travel for more than three years [11]. Unlike many other regions (e.g., those in Europe), Hong Kong never enacted a full citywide lockdown. Despite these prolonged PHSMs, the city experienced five waves of COVID-19 between 2020 and 2022. The first four together resulted in a cumulative incidence of less than 0.2% of the population, markedly lower than that in many other parts of the world [12, 13]. The fifth wave, occurring in January–April 2022 and driven predominantly by the highly transmissible SARS-CoV-2 Omicron BA.2 variants, led to a sharp surge in cases with a peak daily incidence of nearly 80,000 cases and a cumulative incidence of 1.2 million cases (16% of the population) [12, 14]. Sero-epidemiological estimates indicated that 45% (posterior median; 95% credible interval: 41–48%) of the population were infected during the fifth wave, underscoring significant cases under ascertainment [15].

The fifth wave was accompanied by the most stringent PHSMs implemented in the city, including school closures and work-from-home arrangements, which substantially curtailed social interactions [16, 17]. After the fifth wave, some PHSMs were gradually relaxed, although mask mandates, restrictions on group gatherings, and the half-day requirement for face-to-face schooling remained in effect [11]. By March 2023, all PHSMs were lifted, and travel between Hong Kong and the Chinese Mainland resumed after nearly three years of interruption.

In this study, we examined age- and setting-specific contact patterns during the COVID-19 pandemic and quantified how these patterns changed over time.

Methods

Participants and the survey

We conducted 12 rounds of surveys from September 2021 to December 2023. We aimed to recruit 10,000 participants, comprising individuals from the general population reached by random digit dialling and members of eight selected high-contact groups (e.g., catering and retail staff, school teachers) given their comparatively high contact rates. Each participant reported contacts for two days (one weekday and one weekend day), and no participants contributed data to multiple survey rounds.

The survey design was modelled on the POLYMOD and CoMix studies conducted in Europe [5, 18–20], adapted to the Hong Kong context and aligned with our previous study conducted in 2015–2016 [21]. Participants reported their social contacts from the preceding day via online or paper questionnaires. To minimize recall bias, automated reminders were sent the day before each assigned reporting day to support prospective recording. Details of recruitment and implementation are provided in Additional file 1: Supplementary Information (pages 1–3) [5–7, 21].

Statistical analysis

We estimated the mean number of daily contacts and its 95% confidence interval (CI), with subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, vaccination status, and contact setting. While we included survey data collected before the fifth wave (from September 2021), all data were collected during the pandemic. As such, we compared these estimates with those from our 2015–2016 pre-pandemic contact survey, which used similar methods [21]. We used negative binomial regression models to examine associations between daily contact counts and covariates including sex and vaccination status.

When analysing the social contact matrices, we first divided participants into 16 age groups (0–5, 6–10, 11–15, …, 71–75, and 76 or above). We then constructed contact matrices with entries across time, where denotes the mean number of daily contacts that individuals in age group j make with individuals in age group i. The contact matrices were presented in a form that was adjusted for the age distribution in the study population and reciprocity of contacts [5, 22]. Specifically, for each contact matrix, we computed its dominant eigenvalue which, under the next-generation matrix (NGM) approach, is proportional to [24]. This proportional relationship between the dominant eigenvalue calculated from the contact matrix and is based on the assumptions that the population is fully susceptible to the circulating variants and that the transmission parameters remain constant [5, 23]. We then examined temporal changes in this metric to assess the potential impact of PHSMs on by comparing the calculated dominant eigenvalues over the study period with its corresponding value at the beginning of the study, a standard approach in studies on the association between social contact patterns and disease transmission [5]. We applied bootstrap resampling at the level of individual participants, with replacement, to generate 1000 replicated datasets. Using the replicated datasets, we estimated the 90% CIs of the relative changes in . Moreover, some earlier studies have suggested that a duration of 15 min or more (in a single contact or cumulatively) may be associated with an increased risk of SARS-CoV-2 transmission, although this threshold (15 min) may vary across different variants due to differences in transmissibility, viral load, and minimal infectious dose (e.g., the current Omicron family variants may be more transmissible) [25–28]. We performed a sensitivity analysis to estimate the changes in by reconstructing the contact matrices considering contacts lasting at least 15 min. This sensitivity analysis was intended to assess the robustness of the findings to a more restrictive contact definition.

Results

A total of 10,515 (51% male) participants enrolled in the study. Over 97% completed an online questionnaire by themselves. Overall, 3,462 (33%) participants belonged to one of the selected high-contact groups: among them, 22% worked in retail, 21% in construction, and 17% in catering. Detailed demographic data are provided in Additional file 1: Table S1.

Contact patterns

Figure 1 presents temporal changes in the average number of daily contacts among respondents from the general population, stratified by age, and location and type of contacts. The average number of daily contacts varied significantly throughout the study period. During the fifth wave, when PHSMs were most stringent (from January 7 to April 20, 2022), participants reported fewer contacts than in the pre-fifth wave (Table 1 and Additional file 1: Table S3). Specifically, in the pre-fifth wave period, participants from the general population reported an average of 6.2 (95% CI: (5.9, 6.5)) daily contacts, which dropped to 5.1 (5.0, 5.3) during the fifth wave. After the fifth wave, particularly from August 2022 to January 2023, the reported number of daily contacts (5.1 (5.0, 5.3)) remained similar to the levels during the fifth wave despite relaxation of some PHSMs, e.g., school closures and work-from-home arrangements. When all PHSMs were lifted from March 2023 onward in the post-pandemic period, the average number of daily contacts increased substantially to 8.4 (8.3, 8.5).

Location-specific trends suggested the impact of PHSMs on social behaviours. The average number of daily contacts made at home, at workplaces, and at school, and those made physically demonstrated a substantial drop during the fifth wave (Fig. 1f and g). The number of contacts made in public venues such as venues for entertainment and sports, restaurants, and public transport (“Other places” in Fig. 1f) and the number of non-physical contacts (Fig. 1g) increased greatly after March 2023 when all PHSMs were lifted, concurrent with the rise in the overall number of contacts in the post-pandemic period.

Participants in high-contact groups consistently reported more daily contacts than the general population (Fig. 1d), with a relative increase by 23% (21%, 26%) after adjustment for participants’ age, gender, and vaccination status (Table 2). Specifically, the average daily number of contacts made by participants in the high-contact groups was 8.8 (8.0, 9.6) before the fifth wave, decreasing to 7.8 (7.3, 8.2) during the fifth wave and 6.7 (6.5, 6.9) in post-fifth wave (Table 1). In the post-pandemic period, they reported 9.3 (9.1, 9.6) daily contacts on average.

Compared to our pre-pandemic survey in 2015–2016, which recorded an average of 8.1 (8.0, 8.2) daily contacts in the general population, respondents in this study generally reported fewer daily contacts in the pre-fifth wave period (6.2 (5.9, 6.5)) and during the fifth wave (5.1 (5.0, 5.3)) (Table 1) [21]. In the post-pandemic period, when all PHSMs were lifted and whole-day face-to-face schooling resumed, the average number of daily contacts rebounded to 8.4 (8.3, 8.5) which was similar to pre-pandemic levels.

Contact matrices

The contact matrices showed strong age-assortative mixing along the main diagonal for contacts made at home and at school (the second and third rows of Fig. 2). Off-diagonal bands in the contact matrices reflect intergenerational interactions typically in multi-generational households (home) and the student-teacher interactions (school). In contrast, contacts made at workplaces and other places (the fourth and the last rows of Fig. 2), as well as non-physical contacts (third row of Additional file 1: Figure S7), exhibited broader, cross-age mixing.

Regarding contact locations, the category “Other places” includes public transport, places of worship, sports facilities (e.g., gyms and pools), outdoor environments (e.g., streets and country parks), entertainment and retail venues (e.g., cinemas, supermarkets, and malls), and food and beverage venues (e.g., restaurants and bars)

The contact matrices mirrored the potential effect of PHSMs across the study period. The marked reduction in school contacts during the fifth wave compared to the pre-fifth wave period (Fig. 2k–l) reflects complete school closures. From April 2022 onwards, schools reopened progressively, initially with half-day classes, and later with whole-day schooling permitted contingent on vaccine uptake among students and teachers [11]. Across different phases of school resumption, contacts among school-aged children gradually rebounded (Fig. 2m–n). Particularly, after March 2023, when all schools resumed whole-day face-to-face classes without additional requirements (Fig. 2n), school contacts increased sharply, underscoring the central role of educational institutions in shaping age-specific mixing. For example, the average number of daily intra-age-group contacts made at school among 6–10-year-olds dropped from 2.1 before the fifth wave to 0.2 during the fifth wave, and rebounded to 2.2 when whole-day schooling resumed in the post-pandemic period.

Figure 1h presents the relative changes in the dominant eigenvalue of the contact matrix relative to the pre-fifth wave baseline over time. This metric can be considered to reflect relative changes in the of the Omicron BA.2 variant attributable solely to changes in contact patterns associated with PHSMs (i.e., excluding the effect of susceptible depletion and the emergence of new variants). Under this interpretation, accounting for all contacts (the blue line in the plot), declined throughout the fifth wave and the subsequent months, reaching a nadir of 20% (median; 95% percentile interval: 1% to 40%) below the pre-fifth wave level. Thereafter, increased steadily. By December 2023, nearly a year after all PHSMs had been lifted, was 2.1 (1.6, 2.5) times the pre-fifth wave level. In the sensitivity analysis that reconstructed the contact matrices with contacts lasting at least 15 min only, the estimated (Additional file 1: Figure S9) showed a similar pattern to the original analysis based on all contacts regardless of contact duration (Fig. 1h). Specifically, the estimated dropped during the fifth wave, remained low in the subsequent months, and reached a nadir of 26% (11%, 42%) below the pre-fifth wave level. The estimated then increased gradually when PHSMs were lifted after March 2023, and was approximately 2.1 (1.6, 2.6) times the pre-fifth wave level by December 2023.

Discussion

The COVID-19 pandemic substantially altered how people interact. Implementation of PHSMs, availability of pharmaceutical interventions such as vaccines and antiviral medications, and behavioural responses to fear of contagion were among the factors that reshaped contact patterns in populations [29–31]. Assessing changes in social contact patterns across phases of PHSM implementation is critical for understanding the effectiveness of these interventions in reducing disease transmission [3]. We examined this issue in Hong Kong, which implemented prolonged PHSMs, and observed temporal changes in contact patterns that differed from those reported in other countries and regions.

Throughout the entire acute phase of the COVID-19 pandemic of over three years in Hong Kong, the stringency of PHSMs varied during different waves, leading to significant changes in transmission opportunities, as reflected by the estimated effective reproduction numbers [14]. To examine how the contact patterns changed across the pandemic and with PHSMs implementation, we surveyed over 10,000 participants in more than 28 months, which covered the fifth wave with the highest incidence in the city during the period from January to April 2022 [11]. Among the general population, compared to our pre-pandemic survey in 2015–2016 (8.1), we observed a reduction of 37% in the number of daily contacts during the fifth wave (5.1 on average) [21]. In the post-pandemic period, the mean number of daily contacts rebounded to 8.4, a level similar to the pre-pandemic estimate. The relative changes in the number of daily contacts in our study were less pronounced than those reported in European regions and the Chinese Mainland [21]. For example, the CoMix Europe Working Group estimated that the average number of daily contacts during the pandemic between March 2020 and March 2022 dropped by 66–74% in Belgium, Germany, the Netherlands, and the United Kingdom, when compared to the POLYMOD study in 2005–2006 [6, 29, 30]. In China, the number of daily contacts decreased by 70–88% in Wuhan, Shanghai, Shenzhen, and Changsha during the first-wave outbreak period in January–April 2020, compared to their respective baseline estimates [10, 32].

Moreover, the number of contacts in many countries/regions after the pandemic remained considerably lower than the pre-pandemic levels. Compared to the POLYMOD study, the number of contacts in the post-pandemic period (November–December 2022) was lowered by 30–45% in the United Kingdom, Belgium, and the Netherlands [33]. Another study in the Netherlands reported a drop of 13% in the number of reported daily contacts after the pandemic (May 2023) compared to the pre-pandemic estimate in 2016–2017 [34].

Many European countries had lockdowns that mandated citizens to stay at home and greatly reduced contact between people [35]. The work-from-home practice may also introduce a new norm in contact patterns, particularly after the pandemic [36]. In China, stringent social distancing policies such as containment measures and movement restrictions were implemented during the first wave of the outbreak in 2020 [37]. However, there was no lockdown in Hong Kong although the government and many companies recommended staff work from home. The government also adjusted the stringency of PHSMs according to the severity of infections in the community. This may partly explain the less pronounced drop during the pandemic in our survey when compared to the CoMix European project and the Chinese case.

Furthermore, while the stringency level of PHSMs impacted social contacts, contact patterns also evolved throughout the epidemic due to changes in health risk perception and PHSM fatigue. A local study reported that people may perceive a lower severity of the disease and show a lower intention to avoid going out as time passed during the pandemic [14]. Consequently, timely data on social contacts and adherence to PHSMs are essential to assess the effectiveness of PHSMs and enhance public health messaging. Such information could offer actionable insights for tailoring public health strategies in future pandemics. Notably, over 97% of our responses were from self-completed online surveys, demonstrating its feasibility for the prompt assessment of PHSM effectiveness. With clear instructions, self-reported online contact surveys can effectively track changes in contact patterns. This can help policymakers evaluate and adjust PHSMs as needed.

Based on the changes in the dominant eigenvalues of contact matrices, we estimated that in late 2023 (about one year after all PHSMs were lifted) was approximately 2.1 times the pre-fifth wave level in late 2021. The increase was smaller when was based on physical contacts only than when non-physical contacts were considered. This may reflect the resumption of routine social activities while some caution regarding close physical contact remained (such as maintaining social distancing) [38]. This may also indicate that physical contacts, often concentrated within households or close networks, changed less than non-physical contacts in public settings [39]. These findings, however, should be interpreted cautiously, because this approach of estimation assumes a fully susceptible population and constant transmission parameters, assumptions that did not hold throughout the pandemic [40, 41]. For example, the more pronounced increase in based on non-physical contacts likely indicated greater recovery of broader social mixing, rather than a proportional increase in actual transmission risk through non-physical contacts.

Our study has several limitations. First, respondents were asked to record their contact patterns for the day before they were contacted for the survey. We sent out automated text reminders to the mobile phones of participants on the day before the proposed contact recording day [42, 43]. While this approach may have helped participants remember their contacts, recall bias regarding contact details (e.g., the age of the contacted persons and respective contact duration) may remain, particularly for participants who had a lot of contacts (e.g., participants from the high-contact groups). Second, social desirability bias may have led to underreported contacts even though the research data did not include personal identifiers. Third, our study started recruitment one and a half years after the first emergence of the pandemic. The changes in contact patterns and the correlation with PHSMs in the early phase of the pandemic were not captured in our study. Fourth, our previous pre-pandemic study conducted in 2015–2016 utilized both online and paper-based questionnaires, among which the average number of contacts based on the online questionnaire (5.1) was notably lower than that reported using the paper-based format (9.9) [21]. In the current study, the average number of contacts when all PHSMs were lifted (8.4) was similar to the estimate from the previous study (8.1, after the adjustment to the mode of questionnaire). Participants in 2015–2016 may have been less familiar with completing online questionnaires compared to participants in the current study (conducted in 2021–2023). The reported number of contacts from the online survey in the previous study may have been underestimated, and thus comparisons between the current study and the pre-pandemic survey should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, comparisons within the current study, such as those before and after the fifth wave, were not affected by this issue. Fifth, we estimated the changes in by referring to the changes in the dominant eigenvalues calculated from social contact matrices. The proportional relationship between and the calculated dominant eigenvalues is based on the assumptions that the population is fully susceptible to the circulating variants and the transmission parameters are constant across the time horizon. However, during the study period, changes in vaccination coverage in the population, levels of natural infection, and the circulating variants invalidated these assumptions. As such, the corresponding findings should be interpreted carefully. For example, the estimated relative changes in based on the changes in contact patterns may overestimate the actual impact on observable transmission if the probability of disease transmission per contact had increased substantially over time or if the circulating variants were more transmissible than the variant circulating when the baseline dominant eigenvalue was estimated [44, 45].

Conclusions

During the COVID-19 pandemic in Hong Kong, our data and analyses suggest that PHSMs (including school closures, work-from-home arrangements, and restrictions on group gatherings and inbound travel) were temporally associated with reduced social contacts and hence may potentially affect disease transmission. These quantitative estimates may indicate the potential value of PHSMs for informing future pandemic preparedness and response.
