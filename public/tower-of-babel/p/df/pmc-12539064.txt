Arterialization of the Portal Vein: A Potentially Graft‐Saving Strategy to Optimize Veno‐Arterial Inflow in Liver Transplantation
Authors: Teresa Diago Uso, Chase J. Wehrle, Giuseppe Iuppa, Luca Del Prete, Sangeeta Satish, Masato Fujiki, Federico Aucejo, Koji Hashimoto, Charles Miller, Luis Campos, Cristiano Quintini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionThe dual vascular inflow of liver grafts in liver transplantation (LT) is increasingly recognized as crucial to achieving adequate graft function in deceased donor LT (DDLT). Portal hyperperfusion is the most common, leading to decreased intrahepatic adenosine and arterial vasoconstriction via the hepatic artery buffer response. Splanchnic vasodilators, splenic artery embolization, or splenectomy are all recognized among other treatments. However, the management of portal hypoperfusion is less well described, with only isolated case reports describing the augmentation of portal venous (PV) inflow. This is the largest series describing PV arterialization as a strategy to save liver grafts by bolstering portal inflow.MethodsPatients at a single transplant center (Cleveland Clinic Abu Dhabi, UAE) were included (2018–2024). Intra‐operative portal vein and hepatic artery flows are measured in all cases using a MediStim vascular flow device, and liver‐vascular ultrasounds are obtained routinely post‐transplant. Surgical PV arterialization is used as a short‐term measure when adequate portal vein flow cannot be achieved using conventional techniques. Cases were performed using end‐to‐side anastomosis of a cadaveric iliac artery graft from the recipient infra‐renal aorta (n = 3) to the recipient PV as an additional inflow in Y‐configuration or using a conduit from the donor splenic artery to the donor PV (end‐to‐side) with the standard PV‐anastomosis also functioning.ResultsFour patients were deemed to have inadequate portal flow in the early post‐LT (range POD1‐7, n = 3) or intra‐operative (n = 1) periods. All patients had laboratory and/or visual evidence of inadequate liver function secondary to portal hypoperfusion at the time of the decision to pursue arterialization. PV flows before arterialization were 500–590 mL/min [31–39 mL/min/100 g] and 1.4–2 L/min [93–133 mL/min/100 g] after arterialization. Arterialization grafts (n = 3) were ligated on post‐arterialization days 2–7. At the conclusion of modulation, PV flows were 1.1‐2.3 L/min [73–147 mL/min/100 g] with maintained HA flows 120–495 mL/min, highlighting the relative adaptability of liver graft and PV compliance after augmentation.All four patients are symptom‐free with intact liver graft function at a range of 6–18 months post‐LT. Graft survival of 100% compares favorably to all‐comers after LT in the same setting (1‐year GS = 96%, log‐rank p = 0.874).ConclusionsArterialization of the portal vein is a viable and graft‐saving strategy for PV hypoperfusion in LT. Surgeons may pursue this approach in the scenario of inadequate portal vein perfusion without other identifiable causes, such as PV thrombus or port‐systemic shunts, with a plan for arterial shunt reversal 1–7 days after placement based on flow assessment.

Introduction

Liver transplantation (LT) is the only cure for cirrhosis, but the shortage of available grafts outpaces demand, affecting both Western countries and the Middle East [1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6]. This shortage, along with the challenges of retransplantation, highlights the importance of preserving liver grafts to improve outcomes [7, 8].

The dual vascular inflow of liver grafts is increasingly recognized as crucial to achieving adequate graft function, particularly for those grafts that are either of marginal quality or undergoing intense regeneration (i.e., partial grafts). More specifically, the hepatic artery (HA) and portal vein (PV) demonstrate a relative competition for graft inflow via the hepatic artery buffer response (HABR) [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. The most common clinically relevant scenario is portal hyperperfusion, which leads to increased washout of intrahepatic adenosine, in turn, arterial vasoconstriction and arterial hypoperfusion. Splanchnic vasodilators, splenic artery embolization (SAE), or splenectomy are all recognized graft inflow modulation strategies [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. However, the opposite conundrum may also exist, wherein PV flow is itself inadequate. The PV typically carries about 75% of hepato‐petal blood flow, and livers generally require 90–130 mL/min/100 g of liver weight [15, 16, 17].

The consequences of portal hypoperfusion are unfortunately dire, leading to near‐universal graft loss if not corrected. The management of portal hypoperfusion is generally to improve flow if possible. This has historically been described through ligation of porto‐systemic collaterals and/or use of alternative inflows, such as spleno‐renal or reno‐portal anastomoses [18, 19, 20, 21]. However, the management of this issue in the absence of such collaterals is essentially unreported, with only isolated case reports describing the augmentation of portal venous (PV) inflow using manners other than porto‐systemic venous shunts [22, 23].

This is the largest series describing the utility of short‐term PV arterial augmentation as a strategy to save liver grafts by bolstering portal inflow while leaving the arterial graft inflow intact. There are notably many studies describing liver arterialization in the nontransplant setting secondary to liver devascularization, but the post‐LT setting is clearly a far different situation requiring careful and unique considerations. Thus, we report a novel strategy that can save liver grafts, which would have otherwise been lost with good long‐term outcomes. The literature of hepato‐petal vascular flow in the transplant setting is also reviewed, and the international Cleveland Clinic Enterprise approach to the measurement and management of hepatic inflow is presented.

Methods

Patients at a single academic transplant center in Abu Dhabi, United Arab Emirates (UAE), were included (2018–2024). Patients receiving PV arterial augmentation (n = 4) are described in detail, including the rationale for arterialization, the time course of this procedure and its reversal, and the HA and PV flows measured intra‐operatively during the sequential operations. Representative liver vascular ultrasounds (LVUSs) are provided again, preceding and following treatments. Surgical PV arterialization is used as a short‐term measure when adequate portal vein flow cannot be achieved using conventional techniques. Cases were performed using end‐to‐side anastomosis of a cadaveric iliac artery graft from the recipient infra‐renal aorta (n = 3) to the recipient PV as an additional inflow in Y‐configuration or using a conduit from the donor splenic (end‐to‐end, n = 1) to the donor PV (end‐to‐side) with the standard PV‐anastomosis also functioning.

Study outcomes were collected and reported according to the Core Outcome Sets (COS) methodology for outcomes reporting in LT [24, 25]. This study was approved by the UAE regulatory body (DOH) and conducted according to the Declaration of Helsinki.

Approaches to Graft Inflow

Intra‐operative PV and HA flows are measured routinely in all cases using a MediStim (MediStim, Oslo, Norway) vascular flow device. The presence of porto‐systemic shunts is assessed pre‐operatively with contrast‐enhanced imaging and intra‐operatively by clamping of the inferior vena cava (IVC), which will result in increased PV flow only when porto‐systemic shunts are present. Additionally, the potential for PV hyperperfusion is assessed by measuring HA augmentation, which refers to temporary clamping of the PV to assess for increased HA flow regulated by the HABR, thus giving an indirect estimate of the impact of PV hyperperfusion on arterial flow. PV/HA flows, both in this study and, generally speaking, represent findings at a mean arterial pressure (MAP ≥ 65 mmHg), which is also our target blood pressure during the case. The impact of vasopressor and/or inotropic agents on HA/PV flow is not clear, but we do feel this should at least be considered in the surgeon's assessment.

LVUSs are also obtained daily post‐transplant for at least 7 days post‐transplant in all cases. Portal hyperperfusion frequently presents as arterial vasoconstriction evidenced by increased resistive indices (RI). This is managed as previously described with an escalating protocol characterized by somatostatin analogues, splenic artery ligation (SAL), or SAE, depending on the timing of discovery, or splenectomy. Portal hypoperfusion is managed intra‐operatively by the ligation of all major porto‐systemic shunts, which we perform routinely when shunts are present. However, this series represents a new escalation of the management of hypoperfusion when such shunts are not present.

Our collective Cleveland Clinic Transplant Enterprise protocol for the assessment, monitoring, and surveillance of liver graft hemodynamics, as well as options for management of portal hypo‐ and hyperperfusion are provided (Figure 1).

Sample Case Presentations

Patient 1

Patient 1 is a male in his early 60s with a history of autosomal dominant polycystic liver and kidney disease for which a simultaneous liver–kidney transplant (SLK) was required. Listing MELD was 22. The patient underwent LT in September 2022 with a 40‐year‐old DBD liver with 6 h, 5 min of cold ischemia time. At initial transplant, the portal vein anastomosis was conventional, and the arterial inflow was performed via a recipient common hepatic (CHA)–gastroduodenal (GDA) branch patch with a donor celiac–CHA branch patch. Intra‐operative hepatic vascular inflows were measured to be PV = 575 mL/min and HA = 280 mL/min; the graft weight was 1800 g, and GRWR was 2.4. The patient was on very high pressor support due to blood loss during the challenging hepatectomy, and the low PV flow was felt to have potentially resulted from a low‐flow state. The bile duct was exteriorized, and the patient was brought to the intensive care unit (ICU).

On POD2, the patient was re‐explored. At this time, the AST/ALT remained >1500 IU/mL, the internal normalized ratio (INR) was >2.5 despite adequate resuscitation, and lactate was >5 mmol/L, raising concern for possible inadequate graft function. Visual inspection of the liver noted a very hard and seemingly hypoperfused liver graft, though the bowel and abdomen appeared healthy. PV flow was 550 mL/min and HA = 250 mL/min. Of note, there were no portosystemic shunts identified either on pre‐operative imaging or intra‐operatively, and PV flow did not increase with clamping of the IVC. Thus, the decision was made to augment PV inflow. In the absence of any shunts or varices, this was done using a cadaveric iliac artery jump graft from the infra‐renal aorta (end‐to‐side) to the portal vein (end‐to‐side) on the donor graft side of the original PV anastomosis. After arterialization, inflow was PV = 1.8L/min and HA = 80 mL/min. A drop of 5–10 mm of mercury in the systolic blood pressure of the patient was noted, consistent with the physiologic systemic hemodynamic response to the creation of a high‐flow artero‐venous shunt (PV arterialization). The patient then underwent renal graft implantation and was returned to the ICU. After this period, over the coming days, liver enzymes and INR down‐trended appropriately, and the vasopressor requirement decreased and was stopped. Representative LVUS from after arterialization (POD3) is provided with notably very high portal flow and velocity, and low arterial flow (Figure 2).

Sample liver vascular ultrasound images from Patient 1 after arterialization and after ligation.

On POD7, the patient was re‐explored for biliary reconstruction and ligation of the arterialization conduit. ALT and AST were fully normalized at this point. PV flow at this time was 5 L/min before clamping of the arterialization conduit and 2.3 L/min post‐ligation with HA = 120 mL/min, which was optimal for a 1.8‐kg graft. The bile duct was then reconstructed.

The patient is now doing very well at 18 months post‐LT with normal liver and kidney function. He has no ascites, stigmata of portal hypertension, or abdominal complaints.

Patient 2

Patient 2 is an early 60s male with ETOH cirrhosis complicated by mixed hepatocellular (HCC) and cholangiocarcinoma (CCA), for which he had received multiple prior liver resections and trans‐arterial radioembolization. There were no stenoses or thromboses noted in any of the PV systems on contrast‐enhanced pre‐LT imaging. LT was performed in June 2024 with a 35‐year‐old 1.5‐kg DBD graft (GRWR = 2.0) with 2.5‐h CIT using venovenous bypass, given the projected complexity of the hepatectomy. Intra‐operatively, the case was initially uneventful, with standard caval replacement (upper and lower IVC anastomoses), main donor–recipient PV–PV anastomoses, and recipient GDA–CHA to donor celiac artery arterial inflow. Reperfusion was uncomplicated, and the patient was on only low‐dose norepinephrine support. However, during the hemostasis and biliary reconstruction, the patient was noted to have worsening lactic acidosis with escalating vasopressor requirement. Lactate was elevated as high as 19 mmol/L on intra‐operative blood gas; LFT measurements were not available due to the intra‐operative decision‐making timeframe. Visually, the liver graft was not well perfused. Vascular measurements were notable at this time for a PV flow = 550 mL/min and HA = 80 mL/min. The anastomosis was patent with no narrowing or kinking, and no other reasons for this low PV flow state were identified. There was no change in portal vein flow with clamping of the IVC, demonstrating indirectly that no porto‐systemic shunts were present. Post‐arterialization flow was PV = 1.5L/min and HA = 50 mL/min, with excellent systolic and diastolic waveform, and the patient's hemodynamic status improved significantly after the arterialization.

The patient was brought back to the operating room on POD2 for planned re‐exploration with improved transaminases and LFTs. The liver graft was well perfused and viable at this time. The preligation flow in the PV = 2L/min, with 730 mL/min flow in the arterialization graft and 1.3L in the standard PV anastomosis; HA = 64 mL/min with an excellent waveform (IR: 0.7–0.8). After ligation, HA = 495 mL/min and PV = 1.1L/min (Figure 3). Post‐operative course was notable for an anastomotic bile leak on POD6, which was managed with an endoscopic stent.

Sample liver vascular ultrasound images from Patient 2 before arterialization with acute portal vein thrombus, after arterialization, and after ligation.

The patient is currently doing well with intact liver synthetic function at 8 months post‐transplant. He has no symptoms, including ascites or other signs of portal hypertension. Notably, this patient had a biopsy performed at 2 months post‐LT, which was notable for T cell–mediated acute cellular rejection (ACR) but was without signs of other vascular or biliary damage noted. The episode of ACR resolved with intravenous corticosteroids.

Patient 3

Patient 3 is an early‐20s female with acute fulminant liver failure of unknown etiology. The patient was quite sick at the time of transplant, including intubation, on continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) + plasma exchange and multiple vasopressors. She underwent LT in October 2023 using a 1.5‐kg DBD liver graft from a 29‐year‐old DBD donor with 3‐h CIT; GRWR was 2.7. The initial case was uncomplicated with standard inflow via PV (main–main) and HA (rCHA–GDA to dCHA–splenic patch) anastomoses and outflow using standard caval replacement techniques. HA and PV flow intra‐operatively were quite reassuring (PV = 1.5L/min and HA = 850 mL/min) despite the patient being at high risk of portal hypoperfusion due to the lack of pre‐LT portal hypertension. The patient was transferred to the ICU after standard biliary reconstruction.

Our protocol is to perform daily LVUSs, which was done on POD1, notable for a portal vein thrombosis in the donor right–left PV bifurcation extending into the left and right branches of the liver graft. AST was 1711 IU/L, and ALT was 1147, with lactate 2.5 mmol/L at this time. The patient was re‐explored for planned PV thrombectomy. The PV was transected; both e‐version and suction thrombectomy and Fogarty maneuvers were performed. The PV clot was removed from the donor PV bifurcation, yet the intra‐hepatic peripheral left‐sided PVT could not be removed. No PVT was identified at the anastomosis. Tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) was injected into the portal vein for attempted chemical thrombectomy, but this was also unsuccessful. We performed re‐anastomosis of the PV, after which PV flow was only 500 mL/min, with HA = 250 mL/min. This flow was inadequate for the liver graft, and in the absence of another strategy to remove the thrombus, the decision was made to augment the venous inflow. A cadaveric iliac artery graft was anastomosed again using end‐to‐side techniques from the infra‐renal aorta to the portal vein. Post‐arterialization flows were PV = 2 L/min and HA = 190 mL/min. The patient did excellent after this procedure with down‐trending LFTs and stable hemodynamics.

The patient was taken back to the operating room on POD7 for planned vascular assessment. Flows were PV = 3 L/min (conduit = 2.1 L/min and PV = 900 mL/min) and HA = 120 mL/min. Occlusion of the conduit yielded adjusted flows of PV = 1.1 L/min and HA = 292 mL/min, and thus the conduit was ligated.

The patient is currently asymptomatic and demonstrates intact liver function at 14 months post‐transplant, with no requirement for intervention in this time after the closure of the conduit.

This patient had a biopsy performed at 6 months post‐LT for a potential rejection event, which was notable for no rejection and intact hepatic architecture with no evidence of sinusoidal injury or parenchymal congestion.

Patient 4

This is an early 40s male with fulminant liver failure secondary to untreated hepatitis A virus. He received a liver transplant using a 1.5‐kg liver from a 22‐year‐old DBD donor with 1.5 h of CIT and a GRWR of 1.9. There were no shunts identified on pre‐operative imaging. The vascular reconstruction was performed using standard PV anastomosis and a recipient GDA–CHA patch to a donor GDA–CHA patch. The vascular flows were noted to be PV = 590 mL/min and HA = 350 mL/min. The biliary reconstruction was performed duct‐to‐duct with a plan to remeasure flows immediately prior to closure. At this time, the graft appeared grossly malperfused, and PV flow was unchanged. Lactate was increasing from 5 mmol/L at the time of reperfusion to 12 mmol/L after completion of biliary anastomosis. There was no PV flow augmentation with clamping of the IVC. Thus, the decision was made to perform an arterial augmentation, which was performed using the donor splenic artery branching off the celiac axis in an end‐to‐side fashion. As before, the standard PV anastomosis was left intact. The PV flow after arterialization was 1.4 L/min and HA = 220 mL/min.

PV flow on POD1 LVUS was 1.8L/min, and HA RIs were 0.7–0.8. The patient was taken back to the operating room on POD7 for planned conduit ligation. However, intra‐operative measurements were notable for a PV = 2.2L/min, but only 400 mL/min after test clamping of the arterialization conduit. HA flow was 250 mL/min. While we believe routine planned ligation of the conduit is critical, in this case, without the arterialization, there was persistent portal hypoperfusion. Thus, as PV and HA flows were actually optimal in this case, the arterialization conduit was left patent.

On POD15, PV flow was 2.5 L/min and RIs were 0.7–0.8. Post‐operative course was otherwise notable for a chyle leak from the peri‐aortic dissection during the conduit placement; this was repaired during the same operation in which the conduit was evaluated. This patient is currently alive with an intact functioning liver graft at 11 months post‐LT. At present, with intact graft function, there is no plan for conduit ligation, as we conclude that the liver graft has physiologically accommodated this in‐ and outflow. Of note, the patient has no symptoms of portal hypertension at present, including the absence of ascites, varices, or edema.

Discussion

This is the largest series in the literature describing arterial augmentation of PV inflow as an early graft‐saving strategy to resolve peri‐transplant portal hypoperfusion. Admittedly, the authors adopted this technique very recently, and we were skeptical about PV arterialization and its potential complications. This technique was used only because of the clear evidence that low PV flow was responsible for the rapidly deteriorating patient's clinical conditions, highlighting the importance of intra‐operative flow measurement.

All presented cases had, to our knowledge, no other method to improve PV inflow and were at high risk of graft loss, yet are now symptom‐free with intact graft function. Three aspects of this strategy were striking to us: Arterialization of the portal vein was a simple and very safe technique to augment PV flow with little to no systemic hemodynamic impact on the patient's hemodynamics.The clinical improvement of the patient was nearly immediate after arterialization, demonstrating the severe consequences of suboptimal early graft perfusion but also its reversibility when early intervention is implemented.In all cases, the arterialization of the graft resulted in a near physiologic graft hemodynamic state as demonstrated by intra‐operative flow measurement. This highlights the ability of the liver to self‐regulate intrahepatic hemodynamics.

We suspect that this will translate to long‐term graft survival as these patients have now fully normal hepatic function; three after ligature of the augmentation conduit and one with the conduit remaining intact. This point emphasizes arterialization as a planned temporary augmentation, but re‐assessment of hepatic inflow on a daily basis should guide this decision‐making. Further, we have presented our own protocol for measurement of hepatic flows as a routine intra‐ and immediate post‐operative practice, which can allow for early graft‐saving interventions before hypoperfusion leads to irreversible graft damage. Finally, we review the literature on arterial augmentation, which has been described but almost exclusively for alternative indications.

Arterialization of the portal vein has been described, with the majority of cases from two series from Colombia (n = 25) and France (n = 8), yet the indication for these descriptions is notably very different from the presented study [29, 30]. In both studies, a single arterial conduit was connected from the recipient arterial system to the donor PV in a permanent fashion as either an alternative strategy to arterial reconstruction or in the case of severe portal vein thrombosis. However, our cohort is the largest to describe a short‐term arterial augmentation with planned conduit ligation with generally excellent results, though we acknowledge sample‐size limitations with a small cohort. Furthermore, this is the only study describing intra‐operative flow measurement pre‐ and post‐arterialization in any capacity, with special reference to how flow can guide decision‐making. In three cases, with increased liver graft compliance and microcirculatory recovery, the PV flow became adequate after just a short period of supplementation, highlighting again how sensitive the liver graft is to vascular flow during the immediate post‐transplant period. However, we also note one case where the augmented inflow was left intact, emphasizing the importance of continuous re‐assessment of the relative inflow. In the absence of porto‐systemic shunts, there are no other identified ways to increase portal vein inflow quickly, and all four presented cases would have resulted in graft loss without the described augmentation. Our conclusion of impending graft and/or patient loss without arterialization is supported in each case by escalating biochemical evidence (lactate and/or LFTs), escalating inotropic requirement, and hepatic hypoperfusion without other identifiable cause for the rapidly deteriorating patient clinical conditions This is notably in the presence of aggressive resuscitation, which led us to conclude additional resuscitation would not be adequate without portal flow supplementation, although we acknowledge that this cannot be proven definitively. Two patients had post‐transplant biopsies, which did not demonstrate clear evidence of microvascular damage, although this is not definitive, and core biopsies are prone to sampling error. However, this does provide evidence supporting temporary arterialization as a reasonable strategy.

We hypothesize that in some cases, portal hypoperfusion might be the cause of primary graft nonfunction, initiating a rapidly vitious hemodynamic cycle of hepatic hypoperfusion and ischemia followed by systemic hemodynamic compromise, causing worsening liver ischemia. Yet, we believe that hypoperfusion could have, indeed, a reversible course if the cycle is interrupted at an early stage. Conversely, highlighting the delicate nature of this balance, we also highlight that mild hypoperfusion might not require intervention if HA flow is sufficient to augment total graft inflow. Specifically, in a study by Sasaki et al., the lowest quartile of PV flow (PVF < 1 L) demonstrated reduced graft survival unless HA flow was also in the top quartile (HA flow > 359 mL/min) [32]. Such a balance can guide assessment both intra‐ and post‐operatively, although we highlight that these findings were in a subacute to chronic setting, where graft loss did not appear imminent. The opposite problem, portal hyperperfusion, does remain a more common problem. Though not the primary focus of this work, we do feel that a review of hepatic arteriovenous flow will augment our general understanding of the management of either condition. Portal and hepatic arterial blood flow mix in the hepatic sinusoids, which creates a feedback loop that regulates the relative inflow of the PV and HA. Specifically, an increase in PV blood, which is relatively de‐oxygenated, increases adenosine washout in the space of Disse. Adenosine is a potent vasodilator; thus, increased washout leads to HA vasoconstriction, increasing the RIs of the HA [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. This process is termed the HABR. It is this mechanism, and not the previously termed “splenic steal syndrome,” which most commonly leads to inadequate HA perfusion in liver transplant. The relative PV and HA requirements depend on the parenchymal weight, with optimal PV flows of 90–130 mL/min per 100 g of liver [15, 16, 17]. Decreased splenic‐to‐portal blood flow, such as achieved in SAL or SAE, reduces PV flow, which in turn prevents adenosine washout and reduces the RI in the HA [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14]. The weight‐dependent nature of this phenomenon makes it most relevant to living donor liver transplant (LDLT), wherein our group has previously described a protocol for SAL and/or splenectomy to modulate portal inflow depending on the graft weight, intra‐operative flow measurements, and spleen volume [33, 34]. This response also depends on the graft outflow, which is the final mediator of the HABR, and should be optimized regardless of graft type, though again this is most commonly relevant for LDLT [35, 36]. The larger nature of deceased donor grafts makes portal flow modulation less commonly required, though it is still needed at times to mitigate hyperperfusion [14].

Of note, portal hyperperfusion could be accidentally created if the arterialization conduit is not ligated, manifesting in symptoms of portal hypertension and even potential graft‐threatening hemodynamic compromise. Case 4 is unique in the persistence of portal hypoperfusion; however, we emphasize that cases of arterialization should include planned ligation after re‐assessment of portal and arterial flow unless there is compelling evidence to the contrary. In these cases, the relative HA and PV flows were measured with q6 h LVUS. This is critical to assess for the balance of maintained PVF while avoiding arterial compromise associated with portal hyperperfusion, as this could be a consequence of the arterialization. Signs of arterial compromise would warrant urgent intervention.

The relative issues of portal hyper‐ and hypoperfusion are complex, and this work is intended to summarize such findings to aid surgeon decision‐making. However, we also aim to advocate simply for the routine assessment of such findings, both intra‐ and post‐operatively, specifically intra‐operative inflow measurement during surgery and in‐ and outflow assessment after transplant using the LVUS. We advocate for routine intra‐operative PV and HA flow measurements in all cases. Such measurements are routinely performed in all Cleveland Clinic centers globally and allow for early intervention prior to irreparable graft damage, and are the result of the evolutionary understanding of hepatopetal blood supply. Further, they provide definite evidence of etiologies of graft dysfunction that can allow targeted intervention. For example, portal hypo‐ and hyperperfusion may lead to graft dysfunction that appears similar to primary nonfunction, yet have three distinct etiologies and three distinct managements. Without a numeric assessment of the hepatic vasculature, however, it would be impossible to determine which intervention (portal inflow modulation or simple retransplantation) would be most effective in reducing patient morbidity. This also highlights the need for research to report not just graft loss, but the etiology of graft loss, as each of these three examples could be impacted differently from different pre‐ and intra‐operative interventions, thus comparative research across research studies must critically rely on both the etiology and cause of such complications [24, 25].

This study has limitations. The most notable is the small sample size: With only four patients, it is challenging to perform a comparison between the outcomes of these patients and those with different challenges or those managed differently. Although the follow‐up for these patients is relatively long, considering the etiology of the complication, the true long‐term graft survival cannot be reported at this time. Our target blood pressure during the case is a MAP ≥ 65 mmHg and flow measurements reflect findings at this point. Data are not available on vasopressor support required at the time of flow measurements, and further, the impact of vasopressors on these measurements is unknown at this time. Previous studies balancing PV and HA flows are used to extrapolate how PV flow may recover; however, we acknowledge that these are not directly applicable in the acute or hyperacute settings described, and it would be optimal to match these grafts to a set with borderline hypoperfusion that may have recovered without arterialization, though this data was not available. The reported follow‐up is sufficient only to comment on initial graft function or survival, and not on the long‐term impact of potential sinusoidal damage or microvascular graft injury that could be associated with arterial flow into the venous system. Portal hypoperfusion does not have clearly defined thresholds, in our study or the literature in general. Our decision(s) to proceed with arterialization were based on clinical judgment primarily supported by laboratory values and flow measurements; thus, this study cannot provide definitive guidance as to when arterialization should be performed. Larger multicenter studies with longer follow‐up and comparative arms are critical to solidify definitive indications and confirm both safety and efficacy.

In summary, we conclude that arterialization is a safe, viable, and sometimes a graft‐saving procedure that can be added to the tool‐kit of the liver transplant surgeon in extreme cases. While this procedure should only be considered when there is clinically relevant extreme graft‐threatening portal hypoperfusion (as HA flow can compensate for mildly inadequate PV flow) [32], we advocate for its more liberal use due to its safety profile and profound beneficial effects.
