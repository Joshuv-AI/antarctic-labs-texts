Management of localized anterior tooth wear: a minimally invasive approach using the Dahl concept
Authors: Hong-Ye Liu, Yan-Ru Wu, Jia-Wei Wang, Hua-Ling Sun
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Dahl concept has been widely recognized and applied in the management of localized anterior tooth wear for several decades, although its clinical implementation remains a topic of ongoing discussion. This case report describes a practical application of the Dahl concept in a patient presenting with severe wear of the lower anterior teeth, accompanied by reversible pulpitis. The report outlines a minimally invasive restorative strategy, guided by the principles of the Dahl concept, using composite resin to achieve occlusal elevation. This approach avoids extensive tooth reduction or the need for root canal therapy, thereby preserving the vitality of the anterior teeth. By fulfilling the patient’s functional, financial, and aesthetic needs while minimizing invasiveness, this technique highlights the significant clinical relevance of the Dahl concept in managing localized tooth wear. It reinforces the concept’s value in achieving effective and conservative treatment outcomes that prioritize the preservation of tooth structure and pulp vitality.Keywords: Tooth wear, Dahl concept, Occlusal reconstruction, Mini-invasive, Resin restoration

Introduction

Tooth wear is a non-caries defect characterized by the progressive loss of dental hard tissues due to chemical and mechanical etiologies [1]. When wear exceeds average levels for a patient’s age, it can lead to pain, functional impairments, and aesthetic concerns, potentially resulting in complex complications such as a reduced vertical dimension of occlusion, increased tooth sensitivity, and temporomandibular joint disorders [2, 3]. Management of localized anterior tooth wear presents challenges, including insufficient restorative space and compromised tooth structure, necessitating effective, minimally invasive approaches.

The Dahl concept, introduced in 1975, offers a minimally invasive solution for managing localized anterior wear by creating space for restoration through relative axial tooth movement [4, 5]. This method involves placing an anterior bite-raising splint to increase vertical dimension, allowing passive posterior elongation and anterior tooth adjustments over time. Studies have shown that this technique can successfully reestablish occlusal balance within an average of six months, with success rates ranging from 83–100% [6, 7]. However, traditional splints require continuous patient compliance, which poses a limitation.

A 10-year retrospective study demonstrated median survival times of 5.8 years for primary restorations and 4.75 years for replacements, with high patient satisfaction [8]. Composite resins are repairable and cost-effective but are prone to wear and demand stringent adhesion techniques, especially in cases of severe wear. A review evaluating the effectiveness of the Dahl concept found that its success rate could reach 94% − 100%, and stable space augmentation could be achieved in all age and gender groups [9].

This paper discusses a clinical case employing a multi-step approach under the Dahl concept, combining composite resins for initial occlusal elevation and ceramics for definitive restoration. The gradual bite-raising process allowed the patient to adapt to changes in the vertical dimension, preserving pulp vitality and minimizing tooth structure removal. This approach achieved functional and aesthetic rehabilitation while reflecting the clinician’s proficiency and patient-centered care. The case underscores the significance of the Dahl concept in guiding minimally invasive management of localized anterior tooth wear.

Case presentation

History and chief complaint

A 40-year-old male patient presented with complaints of heightened sensitivity and pain localized to his lower anterior teeth, accompanied by muscular tension and discomfort in the masticatory muscles. The patient expressed a desire for treatment aimed at alleviating his symptoms and restoring normal jaw function.

Clinical findings

The patient exhibits a discrepancy between the facial midline and dental midline, in conjunction with a high smile line (Fig. 1).

Facial appearance during initial visit

Teeth 32–42 display a significant loss of normal morphology, characterized by extensive dentin exposure and classified as grade 3 wear according to the Tooth Wear Index (TWI). These teeth exhibit transient sensitivity during temperature testing. Similarly, teeth 33 and 43 show notable dentin exposure, graded as TWI grade 2, and also experience transient thermal sensitivity. In contrast, teeth 13–23 demonstrate normal temperature sensitivity responses and are graded as TWI grade 1, while all other posterior teeth remain clinically unremarkable.

Non-carious cervical lesions (NCCL) are observed in teeth 22 and 32–42.

Teeth 33–43 exhibit gingival erythema and swelling, accompanied by Bleeding on Probing (BOP), which is indicative of underlying periodontal pathology (Fig. 2).

Treatment plan

Upon comprehensive examination and analysis, we proposed orthodontic treatment for the patient to align the dental arch, open the bite, and create adequate space for subsequent fixed restorations. However, due to time and financial considerations, the patient declined this treatment plan. We recommended that the patient undergo simultaneous double-occlusion restoration treatment for the maxillary and mandibular anterior teeth areas and informed the patient of the risks associated with single-occlusion restoration treatment. The patient was well - informed but still chose to only address the painful lower anterior teeth and postpone the restoration of other teeth. Taking all factors into account, we decided to apply to the Dahl technique to re-establish new anterior occlusal contacts in appropriate positions, thereby elevating the vertical dimension and enhancing the occlusion. The final restoration of teeth 33–43 will be performed using all-ceramic materials. To reduce the wear on the opposing teeth caused by all-ceramic restorations, a molar splint is fitted for the patient after final restoration, and regular follow-up monitoring is strengthened.

In the original Dahl technique, partial metal occlusal plates are typically placed on the palatal surfaces of the upper anterior teeth to create an occlusal space of 1.8–4.7 mm. The average time for most individuals to achieve an ideal occlusion using this technique is about six months. However, issues of poor patient compliance often lead to the failure of the final restoration [9]. Therefore, we employ an alternative modified method, substituting composite resin for the traditional anterior occlusal plate, to elevate the patient’s vertical dimension. This is expected to promote posterior tooth elongation and establish a new stable occlusion, thereby creating restorative space for the anterior teeth.

The strength and wear resistance of resin materials are significantly lower than those of all - ceramic materials. Resin materials are more suitable for medium - and short - term restorations. When complications occur, patients need to return for timely treatment. In this case, obvious wear and chipping of the resin material occurred within just two months. The patient stated that they were unable to return for a timely follow - up due to being out of town. Therefore, after communicating with the patient, we decided to use all - ceramic materials as the final restoration. However, considering that the patient’s opposing teeth are natural teeth and the patient has nocturnal bruxism, we informed the patient to keep wearing the occlusal splint and strengthened the follow - up of the tooth wear situation. If further tooth wear occurs, corresponding treatment should be carried out as soon as possible.

Treatment process

To achieve an optimal mandibular position, the vertical dimension of the oral cavity was increased by approximately 1.5 mm. Study models were taken and transferred to a facebow and articulator for accurate spatial replication. A diagnostic wax-up and intraoral mock-up were then created, followed by meticulous adjustments and polishing (Figs. 4, 5 and 6).

Considering the patient’s periodontal condition, the guide plate and resin(Germany, VOCO) were specifically designed to avoid the gingival margins of the anterior teeth, minimizing the risk of gingival irritation. Adjustments were carefully made to ensure uniform contact of the upper and lower anterior teeth during centric occlusion and protrusive movements, while maintaining no posterior tooth contact during various functional movements. This approach effectively created space for posterior tooth eruption and facilitated bite opening.

After two months of wearing the resin restoration, a follow-up examination was conducted to evaluate the condition of the posterior tooth construction. During the visit, intraoral examination revealed significant wear and partial loss of resin material on teeth 33–43, leading to the loss of initial canine guidance (Fig. 7). Due to the severe wear of the anterior teeth and the posterior teeth not yet achieving the desired occlusal position, insufficient space was observed for anterior restoration.

In response, a new diagnostic wax-up and intraoral mock-up were created based on the updated clinical findings (Fig. 8), and further elevation of the anterior occlusion was performed. The patient was advised to continue wearing the restoration for an additional month before the next follow-up assessment.

The patient was advised to continue wearing the restoration for an additional two months.

Four months after the second occlusion elevation, a bite-paper test showed that the posterior teeth of the patient had completely established occlusion. with the distance from the gingival margin apex of the left teeth 3–3 measured at 16.0 mm (Fig. 9d). Intraoral examination reveals transient sensitivity to cold stimulus on tooth 43, while other lower anterior teeth show no significant symptoms. Radiographic examination shows normal findings for teeth 33–43. The resin restoration on the anterior teeth was then removed, and it was observed that the space for anterior restoration now met the requirements for minimally invasive preparation (Fig. 10). A CT scan showed symmetrical bilateral joints without abnormalities, and no issues were detected at the apices of teeth 13–23 and 33–43 (Fig. 11). Under local anesthesia, minimally invasive preparation was performed on the mandibular teeth 3–3. Due to the patient’s lingually inclined anterior teeth and deep overbite, the axial direction of the teeth was slightly adjusted during the preparation(Fig. 12). After trying on a temporary crown, the fit was adjusted, polished, disinfected, and cemented using zinc oxide.

Discussion

This case report describes the treatment plan for a patient with severe tooth wear in the anterior region leading to pulp and masticatory muscle symptoms. The treatment process often requires orthodontic intervention to align the teeth and create space for restoration by opening the bite. However, due to factors such as the patient’s time constraints and financial conditions, some patients have a strong demand for short-term, efficient recovery of certain biting functions and facial aesthetics. In such cases, the Dahl approach offers an alternative treatment option. The irreversible nature of tooth preparation necessitates careful selection of restoration plans for each patient.

Previous studies have demonstrated that the application of the Dahl Concept should strictly adhere to indications, including localized anterior tooth wear, stable periodontal status, and absence of TMD history [10]. Another study proposed expanding the indications to include Angle Class I occlusal relationships [11]. Absolute contraindications include severe active periodontitis, untreated TMD, bone metabolic disorders and adjacent implant-supported restorations [10, 11]. The current patient fulfills all inclusion criteria for Dahl Concept application.

For this particular patient, we respected their wish to preserve as much pulp vitality and affected teeth as possible, fully embodying a spirit of caring and humanistic consideration. Utilizing minimally invasive Dahl techniques, we raised the bite incrementally, ultimately maintaining pulp vitality while restoring and establishing new anterior tooth contact, in line with the principles of minimally invasive restoration.

Additionally, we opted for the Dahl approach as an alternative treatment and recommended simultaneous restoration of the anterior teeth of both jaws; however, the patient declined. Considering the impact of a single-jaw all-ceramic restoration on the natural teeth of the opposing jaw, and recognizing that the proposed solution did not fundamentally address the issue of anterior dental crowding with deep overbite, we took preventive measures to prevent further tooth wear. Specifically, immediately after the final restoration, we created a night grinding bite plate for the patient, instructing them to wear it during sleep, and strengthened follow-up observations to minimize continued tooth wear.

For patients with localized severe tooth wear, compensatory growth of the alveolar bone often leads to a lack of space for restoring the worn teeth. The amount of restorative space that can be created using the Dahl technique varies from person to person, and different studies report varying outcomes [5, 9, 12, 13]. In this case, approximately 2–3 mm of occlusal space was created, providing sufficient space for the full crown restoration of teeth 33–43. Currently, the limits of the occlusal space that can be generated using the Dahl concept remain unclear, with reports indicating that it may reach up to 4.7 mm [14].

The irreversible nature of tooth preparation necessitates a careful selection of restoration plans for each patient. In the case of this patient, we honored their desire to preserve the vitality of the pulp and the affected tooth as much as possible, reflecting a spirit of compassion and humanistic care. We employed the minimally invasive Dahl technique to restore and establish new anterior tooth contact.

Some individuals may worry that during the process of opening the bite, the relatively elevated anterior crowns could lead to chewing difficulties for the patient. The masticatory muscles that have adapted to the original level of wear might experience some degree of discomfort or soreness, potentially affecting the joints in an unpredictable manner. However, research seems to indicate that in similar restorations across multiple patients, there have been almost no reports of such signs or symptoms [13]. In this case, we raised the bite incrementally, and by the time of final restoration, the patient experienced no discomfort in the teeth, muscles, or temporomandibular joint except for an initial reduction in chewing efficiency.

We conducted the Oral Health Impact Profile-14 (OHIP-14) assessments for the patient both before treatment and at 15 months post-repair. Higher scores indicate more pronounced discomfort. As supported by Dahl and Krungstad’s study involving 20 patients with jaw base metal markers, cephalometric analysis revealed that patients across all age groups (18–50 years) exhibited tooth eruption potential through alveolar bone remodeling, with created occlusal spaces resulting from combined tooth extrusion (40%) and intrusion (60%) [6], while subsequent studies demonstrated concomitant condylar repositioning during this process. In our current case, the pre- and immediate post-treatment CBCT sections and 3D model superimposition (Figs. 22, 23 and 24) partly confirmed the immediate periodontal ligament adaptation post-restoration and demonstrated measurable mandibular repositioning and alveolar bone remodeling. These findings are aligned with the established biological mechanisms of Dahl therapy. However, this study’s reliance on single-case CBCT analysis limits the generalizability of its findings to broader populations. Additionally, the resolution of CBCT imaging and the accuracy of 3D model registration may introduce measurement errors.

In this case, the researchers analyzed the patient’s occlusal condition primarily based on clinical imaging data and articulating paper measurements, demonstrating the applicability of these recording methods in basic restorative treatment. For managing complex cases or requiring high-precision occlusal assessment, the T-scan technology exhibits more significant advantages in the accuracy and reliability of occlusal evaluation compared to traditional methods such as wax bite registration and articulating paper marking [15]. Digital occlusal analysis using T-Scan III can also guide splint thickness design to minimize subjective errors [10]. For such patients in the future, it is recommended to introduce digital occlusal analysis systems like T-scan to achieve precise recording of occlusal conditions, facilitating the evaluation and pre-post comparison of treatment outcomes.

In this case, composite resin was selected to fabricate the temporary restoration, but abnormal wear and debonding of the restoration in the canine area occurred two months after placement. Although the choice of temporary material balanced aesthetics and fabrication efficiency, its susceptibility to wear indicates that more wear-resistant alternatives are needed for long-term temporary restorations. A study has confirmed that among various composite resin materials, hybrid-filled (large particle + nanoscale particle) resins exhibit better wear resistance [16]. Sufficient curing and fine polishing of composite resin restorations can significantly reduce the risk of early wear [17]. Existing studies have confirmed that applying a silica (SiO2) nanocoating to the surface of computer-aided design/computer-aided manufacturing (CAD/CAM) resin blocks can significantly enhance their wear resistance. This finding suggests that exploring the application of silica or other similar coating technologies to the surfaces of other types of composite resin restorations may enhance the overall wear resistance of the restorations in the future [18]. Appropriate material selection determines the final restorative outcome. As discussed above, considering the patient’s clinical conditions, resin materials might be insufficient to support the long-term stable retention of the restoration. Therefore, all-ceramic materials were ultimately chosen for the final restoration, achieving satisfactory restorative effects and long-term prognosis.

This case was followed up for 15 months, and only the progression of tooth wear at the 15 - month mark was analyzed. There was no obvious progression of tooth wear, and no problems with the restoration or the tooth apex were observed during the follow - up period. However, tooth wear is a long - term process. It is recommended to increase the frequency and duration of follow - up to further monitor potential root resorption, apical changes, or continuous tooth wear.

Healthy periodontal tissues ensure a relatively stable gingival margin position in patients. This stability reduces the likelihood of bleeding during tooth preparation and impression taking, thereby enhancing the fit of the final restoration [19]. Upon initial consultation, the patient presented with mild redness and swelling of the gingiva in the lower anterior teeth region. Since no obvious biological irritants such as dental calculus and soft plaque were observed during the oral examination, it was speculated that the condition was caused by the irritation of wedge - shaped defects. During the subsequent temporary restoration, the normal shape of the tooth necks was restored, and the patient’s periodontal condition was observed to return to a healthy state. In the subsequent follow - up, the patient’s periodontal condition remained stable. During the subsequent follow - up of patients, corresponding assessments and monitoring of the progression of tooth wear and periodontal status of the affected teeth should be strengthened.

Limitations and future directions

In this case, the worn maxillary anterior teeth and NCCLs of the patient were not restored. Moreover, the method for judging occlusal establishment is relatively simple. Currently, spraying glass-ceramic powder on the tissue surface of zirconia lingual veneers can enhance the bonding force between zirconia and dental tissues [20]. This method may be more suitable for minimally invasive restoration of such patients and can also reduce the wear of all-ceramic materials on natural teeth. In the future, it may be necessary to combine digital technology to improve the monitoring and judgment of each procedure in this treatment plan.

Although the successful management of this case further supports the clinical efficacy of the Dahl concept in localized anterior tooth wear, this study is limited to a single-case report and fails to elucidate how variables such as age, occlusal force distribution, or individual bone metabolism rates influence alveolar bone remodeling efficacy. Its universal applicability across diverse populations with partial tooth wear remains unproven. For future patients with localized tooth wear, subsequent research will systematically collect, organize, and analyze the treatment processes and restoration outcomes, and compile a series of case reports. In the future, a prospective study is planned to enroll multiple similar patients, with a focus on comparing and validating the reproducibility and clinical efficacy of the Dahl concept across different populations.

Conclusion

The Dahl technique can serve as an alternative solution for addressing localized tooth wear issues. This treatment approach involves relatively straightforward clinical techniques and does not require orthodontic intervention. Additionally, resin restorations are more prone to wear during the initial bite elevation process, allowing for better adaptation to the intraoral occlusion. They can also be easily adjusted or removed at any time, providing greater control over treatment outcomes. Moreover, this method maximally preserves tooth vitality and structure, resulting in a final restoration that is both minimally invasive and aesthetically harmonious.
