A retrospective analysis of 266 cases: clinical features of Chlamydia pneumoniae pneumonia in children
Authors: Qiao Qian, Shi Jinghua, Wang Jiani, Zhang Yuling
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChlamydia pneumoniae is a significant pathogen in pediatric community-acquired pneumonia, with increasing incidence globally, particularly in East Asia. However, gender disparities and the role of bronchoscopy in disease management remain underexplored. This study aimed to analyze the clinical features, risk factors, and outcomes of C. pneumoniae pneumonia in children.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective analysis of 266 children with C. pneumoniae pneumonia admitted to a tertiary maternal and child health hospital from January 2024 to June 2025. Data included age, gender, fever at admission, cough duration, length of stay, bronchoscopy performance, hematological parameters (white blood cell count, neutrophil ratio, lymphocyte ratio), C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, pulmonary involvement, co-infection status, and medication use. Statistical analyses involved t-tests, chi-square tests, and logistic regression.ResultsA significant surge in cases was observed since January 2025. Gender-based analysis showed girls (n = 127) had longer hospitalization (mean difference:7.0, p < 0.05) and cough duration at admission (median value: 7, p < 0.05), with higher co-infection rates (48% vs. 30.9% in boys, n = 139, p < 0.05). Among 102 co-infected cases, rhinovirus was predominant (37.3%, n = 38). Logistic regression identified younger age as an independent risk factor for co-infection (OR 0.91 per year, p < 0.05). Children undergoing bronchoscopy (n = 86) had prolonged hospital stays, lower lymphocyte and monocyte ratios, and higher neutrophil ratios (p < 0.05). Bronchoscopic findings revealed viscous yellowish secretions, which were looser than Mycoplasma pneumoniae-induced plastic bronchitis and effectively managed with lavage. Younger age and lower neutrophil ratios predicted multi-lobar involvement (p < 0.05).ConclusionC. pneumoniae pneumonia in children exhibits gender-specific severity, with girls showed trends of prolonged hospitalization and cough duration. Younger age increases co-infection risk, and bronchoscopy indicates more severe disease but aids in targeted therapy. These findings underscore the need for early diagnosis and personalized interventions in high-risk groups.Keywords: Chlamydia pneumoniae, Children, Bronchoscopic features

Introduction

Community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) remains a leading cause of morbidity and hospitalization among children worldwide, posing a significant burden on global health systems [1, 2]. Among the diverse etiological agents, atypical pathogens, particularly Mycoplasma pneumoniae (MP) and Chlamydia pneumoniae (CP), are responsible for a substantial proportion of pediatric CAP cases [3–5]. Epidemiological data have demonstrated that MP infections often occur in cyclical epidemics every 3 to 7 years, with pronounced seasonal peaks, highlighting the persistent challenge they present in pediatric medicine [6–8]. While CP infections are also recognized as a common cause of respiratory tract infections in children, their precise epidemiological patterns and clinical impact are often less distinctly characterized, sometimes overlapping with those of other pathogens [8, 9].

The diagnosis of atypical pneumonia presents considerable clinical challenges. The clinical manifestations, such as fever and persistent cough, are often nonspecific and can overlap significantly with viral or typical bacterial pneumonia [10]. Conventional laboratory markers, including white blood cell counts and C-reactive protein (CRP), frequently lack sufficient specificity for a definitive etiological diagnosis [11]. Furthermore, standard diagnostic methods for atypical pathogens have inherent limitations. Serological testing for IgM antibodies, a cornerstone of diagnosis, requires time for an immune response to develop, often delaying diagnosis beyond the acute phase of illness [6]. While nucleic acid amplification tests (NAATs), such as PCR, offer higher sensitivity and earlier detection, their utility can be affected by factors like sample type, timing of the test, and the potential for detecting colonization rather than active infection, leading to diagnostic uncertainty [6, 12]. These diagnostic hurdles underscore an urgent need for more reliable, early indicators to guide targeted therapy and improve clinical outcomes.

In recent years, flexible bronchoscopy with bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) has emerged as a crucial diagnostic and therapeutic tool, especially in cases of severe, refractory, or complicated pneumonia [13]. Its application has significantly advanced our understanding of the endobronchial manifestations of specific pathogens. For Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP), a growing body of literature has delineated a distinct spectrum of bronchoscopic findings. These include diffuse mucosal inflammation, edema, and hyperemia, but more characteristically, the presence of viscous mucus plugs, airway mucosal necrosis, and the formation of plastic-like casts, a condition known as “plastic bronchitis” [14–17]. These specific airway lesions are now considered hallmarks of severe MPP and are closely associated with disease severity and the development of post-infectious complications like airway stenosis or occlusion. This detailed characterization of MPP’s endoluminal landscape has provided clinicians with invaluable visual cues for diagnosis and intervention.

Despite these advances in understanding MPP, the bronchoscopic features of Chlamydia pneumoniae pneumonia (CPP) in children remain largely under-investigated and poorly defined in the literature. To our knowledge, few studies have systematically described the endobronchial appearance of CPP, and crucially, direct comparative analyses of the bronchoscopic findings between CPP and MPP are scarce. This represents a significant knowledge gap, as both pathogens are common causes of atypical pneumonia yet may require different management strategies, especially concerning immunomodulatory therapies or specific antibiotic choices. Differentiating them early in the disease course based on distinct endoluminal patterns could therefore have profound clinical implications.

Therefore, this retrospective study was designed to investigate the clinical and, most importantly, the bronchoscopic characteristics in a large cohort of 266 children diagnosed with Chlamydia pneumoniae pneumonia. We hypothesized that CPP presents with a unique set of bronchoscopic findings that are distinguishable from the well-documented features of MPP. By systematically analyzing and comparing these features, this study aims to identify potential endoscopic markers for the early and accurate differential diagnosis of these two prevalent atypical pneumonias, thereby providing clinicians with novel insights to optimize therapeutic decision-making and improve patient prognosis.

Method

Study design and participants

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region of maternal and child health hospital in China. A total of 266 pediatric patients diagnosed with Chlamydia pneumoniae pneumonia (CPP) between January 2024 and June 2025 were enrolled. The inclusion criteria comprised: (1) children aged 0–16 years diagnosed with pneumonia; (2) confirmed CPP diagnosis via nucleic acid testing; (3) complete clinical records for all study variables. Exclusion criteria included immunodeficiency, congenital organ dysfunction, or recent immunosuppressive therapy. The study period was selected to capture the significant increase in CPP cases observed since March 2025, aligning with regional reports of rising C. pneumoniae incidence. Co-infection was defined as concurrent detection of ≥ 1 additional pathogen (viral/bacterial) via PCR or culture within 72 h of admission. Respiratory specimens (nasopharyngeal swabs or BAL fluid) were tested for C. pneumoniae DNA using the BGI Genomics Co., Ltd. (Shenzhen, Guangdong, China) multiplex targeted sequencing kit. The indications for bronchoscopy include: (1) refractory pneumonia; (2) infections with unidentified pathogens obtained through upper airway nucleic acid sampling that cannot guide treatment; (3) lobar consolidation on imaging. All bronchoscopies were performed by 3 pediatric pulmonologists with > 5 years’ experience. Procedure details were standardized via institutional protocols.

Data collection

This study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Inner Mongolia Autonomous Region Maternal and Child Health Hospital (Approval No. 2025016-1). The requirement for informed consent was waived by the IRB due to the retrospective nature of the study, and all data were anonymized prior to analysis.

Data abstraction followed a standardized protocol to ensure consistency, with ambiguous cases reviewed by two independent pediatricians.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were represented byproportions (%) while continuous variables were described by the mean (standard deviation, SD) or median (interquartile range, IQR), as appropriate. To compare the differences across groups, one-way analyses of variance (normal distribution), Kruskal–Wallis tests (skewed distribution), and chi-square tests (categorical variables) were undertaken. Logistic regression models were used to determine the odds ratios (OR) and 95% confidence intervals (95% CIs) for the relationship between co-infection and age.

All the analyses were performed with the statistical software packages R (http://www.R-project.org, The R Foundation) and Free Statistics software versions 2.1.

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was approved by the hospital’s Institutional Review Board (IRB approval number: 2025016-1). Waiver of informed consent was granted due to the retrospective nature and anonymized data handling, complying with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

A total of 266 children with Chlamydia pneumoniae pneumonia (CPP) were included in this study. Since January 2025, a significant increase in the incidence of CPP has been observed, highlighting a recent epidemiological shift. The cohort comprised 139 boys and 127 girls. Statistical analysis revealed significant differences between genders: girls exhibited longer hospital stays (7.0 ± 2.4 vs. 6.1 ± 2.1 for boys, p < 0.05), prolonged duration of cough at admission (7.0 (4.0, 14.0) days vs. 6.0 (4.0, 9.5)days, p < 0.05), and a higher incidence of mixed infections (rate: 48% vs. 30.9%, p < 0.05)(Table 1). Among the 102 cases with mixed infections, rhinovirus co-infection was the most prevalent, accounting for 38 cases (37.3%)(Fig. 1). Logistic regression analysis identified younger age as an independent risk factor for mixed infections, with older children demonstrating a lower risk (OR = 0.91, 95% CI: 0.84–0.99, p < 0.05)(Table 2).

Logistic regression analysis for mixed infections

When stratified by bronchoscopy performance, patients undergoing bronchoscopy had significantly longer hospital stays (7.1 ± 2.0 vs. 6.2 ± 2.3, p < 0.05), lower lymphocyte and monocyte ratios, and higher neutrophil percentages (Table 3). Bronchoscopic findings secretions were thick, tenacious, and adherent to airway walls(n = 86)(Fig. 2). Additional findings included: mucosal congestion(n = 72, 83.7%), bronchial wall edema (n = 64, 74.4%). In contrast to Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia, which can sometimes forms plastic bronchitis, the secretions in CPP were looser and effectively managed with lavage therapy, achieving optimal airway clearance. Regarding the extent of infection, multivariate analysis indicated that younger age and lower neutrophil percentage were significantly associated with multi-lobar involvement (p < 0.05,Table 4), suggesting a distinct pattern in younger children.

Discussion

Epidemiological trends and gender differences

Our study reveals cases increased significantly after March 2025, aligning with global reports of rising C. pneumoniae infections in children [18, 19]. Notably, gender-based stratification showed distinct clinical patterns: girls exhibited longer cough duration, extended hospitalization, and higher rates of co-infection compared to boys. This may reflect sex-specific immune responses, as estrogen has been linked to enhanced humoral immunity but potentially delayed pathogen clearance in respiratory infections [20, 21]. Further prospective studies with larger sample sizes and standardized outcome measures (e.g., ICU admission, oxygen requirement) are needed to confirm gender-specific severity.

Bronchoscopic findings: mechanisms of secretion characteristics

A critical observation is the endoscopic distinction between CPP and Mycoplasma pneumoniae pneumonia (MPP). However, due to ethical and feasibility constraints, a direct comparison group with MPP was not included in this study.In CPP, bronchoscopy revealed yellowish-white, viscous secretions obstructing airways.While CPP secretions appeared less cohesive than those described in severe MPP cases, this comparison is based on indirect literature evidence. Direct comparative studies are needed to validate these observations.This difference likely stems from:

Pathogen-specific inflammatory responses

C. pneumoniae triggers a neutrophilic-predominant inflammation [22, 23], leading to thick but friable secretions rich in neutrophils and cellular debris. In contrast, MPP induces lymphocyte-dominated inflammation with fibrin deposition, forming rigid casts [18, 23].

Reduced mucin cross-linking

C. pneumoniae may downregulate mucin glycoprotein polymerization, resulting in looser secretions more amenable to lavage [24, 25]. This explains why therapeutic bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) effectively cleared CPP secretions without mechanical complications.While mucin cross-linking patterns were not directly measured, the observed secretions’ consistency suggests potential differences in mucin biology between CPP and MP infections, warranting further investigation.

Age-dependent severity

Younger children with lower neutrophil counts developed multilobar infections, suggesting immature immune responses fail to contain localized infection [18, 26].

Co-infections and risk factors

Co-infections occurred in 102 cases (38.3%), predominantly with rhinovirus (37.3%), supporting rhinovirus’s role as a common co-pathogen in pediatric pneumonia [26, 27]. Logistic regression confirmed younger age as an independent risk factor for co-infection (OR = 0.91), consistent with studies showing immature mucosal immunity in infants increases susceptibility to multiple pathogens [20, 21, 28].

Bronchoscopy utility and inflammatory markers

Children undergoing bronchoscopy had prolonged hospitalization, lower lymphocyte/monocyte ratios, and higher neutrophilia. This subgroup likely represents severe cases requiring invasive intervention. Elevated neutrophils correlate with tissue damage and prolonged recovery, while lymphopenia indicates immune exhaustion [22, 23].

Therapeutic implications

The efficacy of BAL in CPP underscores its value for severe cases with secretion obstruction. Macrolides (e.g., azithromycin) remain first-line therapy, but co-infection screening—especially for viruses—is crucial for targeted treatment [1].

Strengths and limitations

This study preliminarily describes bronchoscopic features of CPP and compares them with existing literature on MPP, highlighting potential distinctions, alongside identifying clinically significant gender disparities with girls showed trends of prolonged hospitalization and cough duration, and documenting a recent epidemiologic surge in a large pediatric cohort (n = 266). However, the retrospective nature introduces selection bias toward severe cases due to non-standardized bronchoscopy criteria, the non-randomized bronchoscopy selection may introduce confounding by indication, limiting causal inference.The absence of quantitative secretion metrics(e.g., viscosity, mucin content) limits objective characterization of pathogen-specific differences. Generalizability may be constrained by the single-center design, warranting validation in diverse populations.Inter-observer variability in bronchoscopic interpretation was not formally assessed, which may limit generalizability.Qualitative assessment of secretion consistency introduces observer bias. Future studies should incorporate quantitative mucin assays.

Conclusion

This study highlights C. pneumoniae as a re-emerging threat with unique age and gender specific severity patterns. Cases increased significantly after March 2025, high co-infection burden in young children, and endoscopic features distinguishing CP from MP provide novel insights into disease pathophysiology. Future studies should explore CP–rhinovirus interactions and validate bronchoscopy criteria for optimizing outcomes.Further prospective studies with larger sample sizes and standardized outcome measures (e.g., ICU admission, oxygen requirement) are needed to confirm gender-specific severity.
