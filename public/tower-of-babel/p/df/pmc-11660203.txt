The value of assessing deep disease healing by probe‐based confocal laser endomicroscopy and histology for long‐term prognosis of ulcerative colitis
Authors: Yue Zheng, Jixin Zhang, Jinwei Wang, Junxia Li, Huahong Wang, Yu Tian
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and AimThe benefits of deep disease healing need evaluation by long‐term clinical research in different populations. Confocal laser endomicroscopy (CLE) is a superior method for evaluating deep disease healing.MethodsThis prospective study enrolled ulcerative colitis (UC) patients in clinical remission who underwent colonoscopy, CLE, and histological assessment. Patients were monitored for relapse by patient‐reported outcomes and colonoscopy evaluation of mucosal healing. The ability of different methods of mucosal healing to predict long‐term disease recurrence was assessed using Kaplan–Meier estimation and Cox proportional hazard regression.ResultsForty‐two patients in clinical remission were assessed by colonoscopy. Those with Mayo endoscopic subscores (MES) ≤ 1 were enrolled. The 48‐month recurrence rates in present healing group, assessed by CLE (colonic barrier assessment and ENHANCE index) and by histological examination (Geboes scale), were 20.0%, 26.7%, and 11.1%, respectively, and were significantly lower than absent healing group (P < 0.05). Univariate Cox proportional risk regression analysis in absent of healing disease, determined by the ENHANCE index and Geboes scale, indicated an increased risk of recurrent events, with hazard ratios (HR) of 3.87 (95% CI: 1.18, 12.62) and 8.20 (95% CI: 1.06, 63.30), respectively. Multivariate Cox proportional hazard regression analysis adjusted for the extent of inflammation (E3 or not) showed a significant difference only for the ENHANCE index, with an HR of 3.53 (95% CI: 1.03, 12.10), P = 0.045.ConclusionsDeep disease healing has a lower recurrence rate. The colonic barrier healing assessment, ENHANCE index, and histological Geboes scale have superior long‐term prognostic value for UC patients.Keywords: confocal laser endomicroscopy, deep disease healing, histological remission, ulcerative colitis

Introduction

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic nonspecific intestinal inflammatory disease that includes ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD). Its pathogenesis is still unclear, and the disease recurs frequently. 1 , 2 In recent years, the incidence of IBD has increased worldwide, especially in Asia and economically developing countries. 3 , 4 Many studies have shown a significant decrease in the recurrence rate of IBD patients following endoscopic mucosal healing. 5 The STRIDE II consensus clarified that endoscopic mucosal healing was the long‐term goal for UC treatment while also proposing the concept of histological relief for deep disease healing. 6 Recent research has found that IBD patients who achieve intestinal barrier healing show superior endoscopic and histologic remission and obvious decreases in their long‐term major adverse outcomes (MAOs). 7

In the past decade, confocal laser endomicroscopy (CLE) has gradually been applied in the field of IBD diagnosis, and clinical research has continued to report satisfactory results. 8 Many CLE scoring systems has been established to evaluate IBD mucosal healing, including Watson grade, 9 endomicroscopic mucosal healing (eMHs), 10 impaired intestinal permeability (CLSs), 11 ENHANCE index, 12 and various mucosal healing evaluation systems. 13 , 14 , 15 But most of these studies only confirm the important role of CLE in predicting the recurrence of IBD in 1‐year follow‐up. There were few studies for long‐term follow‐up period to demonstrate the value of deep mucosal healing (DMH) for this disease.

At present, prospective validation studies are lacking for the evaluation of different indicators of DMH for long‐term follow‐up period in patients with UC. The aim of this pilot study was to compare several main criteria for DMH to determine which method is more accurate and effective for predicting long‐term outcomes. The overall goal is to conduct long‐term clinical research in different populations of patients with UC to extend knowledge of the potential benefits of treatment strategies using efficient methods aimed at DMH.

Patients and methods

Patients

This study was designed as a single‐center prospective observational study conducted at Peking University First Hospital from December 2018 to June 2023. The study was approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Peking University First Hospital and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (registered at https://www.chictr.org.cn, and the registry number is ChiCTR1800020205).

Patients who were established to have an ulcerative colitis diagnosis according to consensus 16 on the diagnosis and treatment of IBD by the Digestive Branch of the Chinese Medical Association for at least 1 year were screened between December 2018 and June 2019. Of the screened patients, 42 patients in the clinical remission stage agreed to participate in the study and underwent colonoscopy and CLE examinations. After the colonoscopy examination, 28 patients remained whose Mayo endoscopic subscore (MES) was ≤1, and those 28 patients were enrolled in the study. All 28 patients were older than 18 years of age, and they all signed informed consent forms before joining the study. Patient exclusion criteria included tumor history, thromboembolic diseases; renal dysfunction, severe heart and lung diseases, and known allergy to fluorescein and women who were pregnant or breastfeeding.

Endomicroscopy assessment

Prior to colonoscopy, the patients underwent routine bowel preparation using polyethylene glycol in the evening and morning before the examination. The colonoscopy was performed using intravenous sedation, with constant monitoring of vital signs. When the colonoscope entered the terminal ileum, an intravenous injection of 10% fluorescein (4 mL) was performed. A probe‐based confocal laser endomicroscopy system (pCLE, Cellvizio, Mauna Kea Technologies, Paris, France) capable of passage through the working channel of the colonoscope was used. The pCLE was performed by two physicians who were proficient in the procedures. The physicians explored and recorded at least four specific segments of the ileum, ascending colon, sigmoid colon and rectum, as well as any other sites deemed necessary by the physicians. Targeted biopsies were conducted at these exploration sites.

The quality of the pCLE images were analyzed in real time by the operator and at least 30 s of video recordings were digitally stored for each segment. Further judgments on a wider field of view were made based on reconstruction images completed using the Mauna Kea Technologies toolkit software. Two expert operators reviewed the video and reconstruction images in a blinded fashion to avoid information bias. If there was any disagreement of two operators that affected the final results, the third expert participated in the discussion. The pCLE images were evaluated according to the criteria of Watson grade, 9 colonic barrier healing assessment, 7 and ENHANCE index. 12 The Watson grade system uses cell shedding and local barrier dysfunction of the terminal ileum to divide the disease into I–III grades to predict the relapse of IBD. The absent of colonic barrier healing for UC was described as a change in the appearance of the dark center in the crypt of the colon to an intensity brighter than the surrounding epithelium. The value of the ENHANCE index was accumulated from four different score point aspects: large vessel diameter, dilated crypt lumen, fluorescein leakage, and irregular crypt architecture. The four categories were graded from A to D using the ENHANCE index, with patients who achieved a D category deemed to have very low inflammation, which was further validated by the histological Nancy index.

Histological inflammation assessment

Using the Geboes scale items, 17 mucosal histological inflammation was independently assessed by a professional IBD pathologist. The Geboes scale included seven features: architectural changes, chronic inflammatory cell infiltration, lamina propria neutrophils and eosinophils, neutrophils in the epithelium, crypt destruction, and erosion or ulcers. Each of the seven features was evaluated using scores from G0 to G5, and the severest inflammation observed was regarded as the Geboes scale score for the patient.

Follow‐up

The follow‐up period extended from December 2018 to June 2023. During the observational period, patients applied the standard of practice for UC management based on the current guidelines, 16 and the researchers carefully enquired about the patient's symptoms and consulted the medication records. Expert gastroenterologists (J.X.L. and H.H.W.) supervised each decision‐making process. According to each patient's situation, clinical follow‐up was generally arranged every 1–3 months. An observational follow‐up colonoscopy for the study was scheduled once a year. Patients without relapse also underwent colonoscopy examination at the end of the follow‐up period.

Primary endpoint

Clinical recurrence is the primary endpoint event of this study. The clinical follow‐up assessed the patient‐reported outcomes (PROs) as signs of recurrence, including symptoms of more than two bowel movements or visible rectal bloody stool occurring for 3 days or more within a week after ruling out infectious and other inflammatory conditions. Patients with clinical relapse underwent clinical evaluation, laboratory examination and colonoscopy at the soonest possible time. MES ≥ 2 points was used as an objective criterion to determine UC relapse. Clinical follow‐up also assessed the occurrence of major adverse outcomes (MAOs), such as treatment escalation (defined by the use of systemic steroids or biologic agents) and UC‐related hospitalization or surgery. Clinical relapse confirmed by colonoscopy and MAOs occurring within the follow‐up period was recorded as the endpoint event of the study.

Statistical analysis

Data are shown as frequencies with proportions. The differences in the recurrence rates between present and absent of healing groups, as determined by the different indexes, were computed using the chi‐square test. Kaplan–Meier curves were constructed for the estimated 4‐year rate of clinical relapse or the occurrence of MAOs, and the rates between present and absent of healing groups were compared using the log‐rank test.

A Cox proportional hazard regression model was used to estimate the association between the different indexes and disease recurrence, with the hazard ratios (HR) reflecting the degree of the association. Both univariate and multivariable analyses were conducted, adjusting for the inflammation region (E3 or not), to explore the independence of the association from inflammation region factors. The predictive ability of each index for clinical recurrence was assessed using the time‐dependent receiver operating characteristic (ROC) method to draw the ROC curve for 48 months of follow‐up and calculate Harrell's C statistic to reflect the predictive ability. A value of P < 0.05 (two‐sided) was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 22 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA) or SAS 9.4 (SAS Institute, CA, USA), as appropriate, by a blinded statistical analysis professional (J.W.W.) who was not involved in the clinical, histological, endoscopic, or endomicroscopic data acquisition.

During the 4‐year follow‐up, the visit schedule planned for at least every 3 months was not fully maintained due to the COVID‐19 epidemic, especially in 2020. In the first half of 2020, the patients were followed up mainly by telephone or online, whereas in the latter half of 2020, the patient follow‐up gradually returned to the normal scheduled in‐person visits. Ultimately, an accumulative total of 1065 follow‐up months and 76 colonoscopy examinations were included in the analysis. On average, the number of follow‐up colonoscopies was 0.9 times per 12 months per case.

Among the 28 patients, 15 cases reported no recurrence and maintained treatment during follow‐up. At the end of the study, all 15 of these patients underwent colonoscopy, and their MES scores remained between 0 and 1 point. At the 4‐year follow‐up, 13 cases had recurrence or had experienced MAOs, for a recurrence rate of 46.4%. Each of these 13 patients suffered a flare‐up of UC at different times. One patient had a severe relapse and was hospitalized to undergo biological therapy (infliximab). One case who had UC combined with primary sclerosing cholangitis experienced a UC recurrence with biliary tract infection and was admitted to the emergency room for sepsis. Another five cases increased their drug dosages; three cases added a mesalazine enema combined with oral mesalazine; and three cases did not accept escalation treatment, although their chronic intestinal inflammation was persistent. A comparison of the clinical recurrence rates among the patients grouped by different mucosal healing indexes revealed that the recurrence rates determined by MES scores and Watson grade had no statistical significance, as the chi‐square P values were 0.10 and 0.23, respectively. The recurrence rates of present of healing group confirmed by colonic barrier healing assessment, ENHANCE index, and Geboes scale were 20.0%, 26.7%, and 11.1%, respectively, and were significantly lower than the rates of absent of healing group: 61.1%, 69.2%, and 63.2% (P < 0.05). A comparison of the 4‐year survival curves using Kaplan–Meier analysis log‐rank tests indicated significant differences of disease recurrence between patients with present or absent colonic barrier healing, ENHANCE index D category or not, and Geboes scale less than 2 or not at baseline (with log‐rank P 0.036, 0.015, and 0.015, respectively), as shown in Table 3. Discussion

In 2015, the STRIDE committee defined a treat‐to‐target approach for IBD, which shifted the goal of UC treatment to long‐term prevention of disease complications. 18 Since then, clinical studies have begun to pay more attention to the histological improvement by biological agent treatment and have found that microscopic features of inflammation activity may persist in macroscopically inactive disease. 19 Recent clinical trials have identified deep mucosal healing as a therapeutic secondary endpoint. 20

The STRIDE II consensus proposes that histological healing can be used as an auxiliary method of endoscopic relief in UC to represent deep disease healing. 6 In recent years, the CLE features of IBD images have gradually become clear. 14 , 21 , 22 Computer‐aided analysis software 23 and auxiliary biomarker technology 24 can now generate sufficient data for evaluating the inflammation of IBD. CLE, as a noninvasive real‐time histological‐level examination method, can provide clinical information in detail for deep healing of IBD. 25 Nevertheless, the efficacy of CLE scoring systems or histological indexes still requires validation by long‐term prospective follow‐up studies. 26

In our research, UC patients in clinical remission, especially those who achieve MES 1 or 0 score, have relatively simple recognition of their colonic and terminal ileum pCLE image. The Watson grade is based on the observation of the terminal ileum mucosa, avoiding interference from local inflammation in the colon, so it represents overall intestinal barrier function. However, there are some confusions in its judgment, such as the difficulty of determination of Watson II: cell shedding confined to single cell per shedding sit, which resulted in a small amount of disagreement in our team. The colonic barrier healing is exactly opposite to the Watson grade, directly evaluating the colonic mucosa, so it is easier to operate and determine. Regardless of where fluorescein leakage of the colon occurs, it is judged as absent of colonic barrier healing. There was almost no difference in the score within our team. The ENHANCE index is more comprehensive and complex scoring system. There are often different opinions on the specific scoring in our team. However, the differences in subitem scores hardly affect the judgment of patients in the D category. So there were very few disagreements in our team when using the ENHANCE index for judgment.

Our study is a prospective 4‐year follow‐up study, and it has provided data confirming that deep disease healing would bring about a lower UC recurrence rate. In this study, the patients who achieved pCLE deep healing (present of colonic barrier healing and ENHANCE index D category) or histological healing (Geboes scale <2) had a lower recurrence rate and better prognosis. The Watson grade had a low efficacy for predicting long‐term UC recurrence, and the UC assessment was more effective using colonic barrier healing than the Watson grade and was a simpler procedure. The ENHANCE index had the best efficacy for predicting UC relapse, but this procedure is slightly more complex. The Geboes scale, as a classic pathological scoring for UC, showed a predictive ability similar to that of the ENHANCE index, with the same Harrell's C statistic. However, the Geboes scale is a more complicated assessment method and requires the judgment of a pathology expert.

The patients who showed endoscopic mucosal healing in this study had a lower recurrence rate (46.4%) than the overall rate reported in the literature for patients with UC. 27 However, this study also excluded patients with incomplete barrier function determined by PRO, and this could have acted as another factor giving rise to a lower recurrence rate. 11 Research on the relationship between PRO and the endoscopic and histological appearance of UC has shown that endoscopically inactive UC was associated with an absence of rectal bleeding but not with complete normalization of stool frequency. 28

The limitations of this study need to be acknowledged. First, the number of enrolled patients was small, so the study power was insufficient to estimate even a large effect size (e.g. HRs of 7) and effects of disease characteristics, such as age, gender, and medical history, were not completely eliminated in the multivariate Cox regression analysis. Large‐scale, prospective multicenter clinical studies should be established to explore the benefits of deep disease healing. The second limitation was that the proportion of patients with limited inflammation involvement (E1 accounting for 50%) was relatively high, fewer patients had extensive colon involvement and previous severe episodes, and most of the cases were using mesalazine rather than immunosuppressants or biological agents. Third, the follow‐up was initially affected by the COVID‐19 epidemic, and subsequent follow‐up was constrained by those conditions. Lastly, laboratory indicators, such as C‐reactive protein and fecal calprotectin, were not collected uniformly, and the patients' quality of life, 29 which is an important long‐term prognostic evaluation for IBD, was not investigated.

Although there were still different research conclusions on the value of deep disease healing by using CLE, 30 it was gradually recognized that functional barrier healing should be considered for the future management of patients with IBD in routine clinical practice. 31 Recently, CLE has been used to conduct in‐depth molecular biology research to explore barrier healing, changes in intestinal inflammatory cell subsets, and mesenchymal stem cell function. 32 , 33 There are reasons to believe that new tracers with high safety in the human body will be developed for CLE applications in the future. When assisted by computer deep‐learning models, 34 this approach would provide plentiful connotations and more accurate assessment of the deep healing of IBD.

Conclusion

Deep disease healing shows a lower long‐term recurrence rate and a better prognosis for UC patients. The colonic barrier healing assessment, ENHANCE index, and histological Geboes scale have high clinical diagnostic value for deep disease healing of IBD.
