Novel VSMC-associated biomarkers in intracranial aneurysm pathogenesis: a multi-omics and machine learning study
Authors: Ruoyu Liu, Bin Xu, Zhenqiang Huang, Lujun Chen, Xiao Zheng, Tianwei Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aims to identify characteristic genes linked to vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) and intracranial aneurysm (IA) formation and rupture, providing insights for early diagnosis, risk prediction, and treatment of IA.MethodsWe analyzed the GSE75436 and GSE13353 datasets from the GEO database, performing differential expression analysis with |log2FC|> 1 and adj p < 0.05. Gene Ontology (GO), KEGG, and Reactome pathway enrichment analyses were conducted. Using the GSE122897 dataset, Weighted Gene Co-expression Network Analysis (WGCNA) identified gene modules and hub genes associated with IA. Machine learning algorithms (LASSO, SVM-RFE, and Random Forest) were applied to select differentially expressed hub genes. The expression patterns of marker genes were explored using the GSE193533 single-cell RNA sequencing dataset. Lastly, we assessed the predictive value of these genes in IA formation and rupture by plotting Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the Area Under the Curve (AUC), thereby validating their sensitivity and specificity in clinical diagnostics.ResultsWe identified 1000 upregulated and 806 downregulated genes in IA compared to normal arteries, and 468 downregulated and 405 upregulated genes in ruptured IA tissue. WGCNA revealed key gene modules associated with IA. Machine learning identified genes such as COL5A2, CDH11, PLOD1, P3H4, PLIN2, and PLAUR. Single-cell analysis showed a phenotypic transition in VSMCs from contractile to synthetic, with these genes predominantly expressed in synthetic VSMCs. ROC analysis validated their excellent diagnostic performance for IA formation and rupture.ConclusionCOL5A2, CDH11, PLOD1, and P3H4 were identified as genes associated with IA formation, while PLIN2 and PLAUR were linked to IA rupture. These genes provide potential biomarkers for IA diagnosis and risk assessment.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04720-z.

Introduction

Intracranial aneurysms (IAs) are widely prevalent among the adult population worldwide. It is estimated that approximately 3–5% of adults globally harbor unruptured intracranial aneurysms. In China, the prevalence of unruptured aneurysms in individuals aged 35 to 75 is around 7% [1]. An intracranial aneurysm is a pathological outpouching of the arterial wall. The tunica media and adventitia of cerebral vessels are less elastic than those of extracranial vessels. Additionally, the middle layer of smooth muscle is sparse, the outer membrane is thin, and the internal elastic lamina is more developed [2]. Large cerebral vessels also run through the subarachnoid space, which lacks connective tissue support. These anatomical features are thought to contribute to aneurysm formation, although the precise pathophysiological mechanisms remain unclear. Over 80% of spontaneous subarachnoid hemorrhages (SAH) are caused by the rupture of intracranial aneurysms [3]. Aneurysmal subarachnoid hemorrhage (aSAH) has a global incidence of 7.9 cases per 100,000 person-years across all midyears. The incidence of aSAH is 1.3 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.98–1.7) times higher in women than in men [4]. Mortality and morbidity rates following aSAH are high; at least 25% of patients will die within 48 h of the event, with death before hospital admission occurring in 10–15% of patients and in-hospital mortality reported in 20% of admitted patients, while around half of survivors will experience ongoing neurological deficit [4–6].

The pathogenesis of intracranial aneurysms is complex and involves multiple factors, with hemodynamics, immune responses, and vascular remodeling playing critical roles in disease initiation and progression. Abnormal hemodynamic shear stress is a critical driver of aneurysm formation and progression, triggering pathological changes through endothelial dysfunction and structural damage to the arterial wall. Animal model studies of intracranial aneurysms have revealed that aneurysm formation arises from an imbalance between the tensile strength of the cerebral arterial wall and the hemodynamic stress it endures [7]. Cerebral arterial wall inflammation may weaken its tensile strength, increasing the risk of developing intracranial aneurysms.

During vascular development, the primary functions of vascular smooth muscle cells (VSMCs) are proliferation and the production of matrix components for the vessel wall. Mature VSMCs are highly specialized cells with a primary role in contraction, characterized by a low proliferation rate [8]. VSMCs contract in response to increased intracellular Ca2⁺ concentrations. In VSMCs, the Ca2⁺-calmodulin complex activates myosin light chain kinase (MLCK), which facilitates ATP hydrolysis through phosphorylation, providing the energy required for myosin-actin sliding during cell contraction [9]. Actin polymerization and cytoskeletal dynamics play critical roles in regulating contractile function [10].

VSMCs are highly dynamic, and their phenotypic switch between a contractile and synthetic state is influenced by multiple factors in the vascular microenvironment. This phenotypic transition is implicated in vascular injury, atherosclerosis, and aneurysm formation [11–13]. Previous studies have shown that VSMCs contribute to the formation and progression of intracranial aneurysms by remodeling the extracellular matrix through the secretion of inflammatory cytokines and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs) [12]. Furthermore, certain studies have suggested that mediators such as TNF-α can induce phenotypic transitions in cerebral VSMCs [14]. Kilic et al. provided morphological evidence of disordered VSMC alignment and reduced staining of contractile proteins in the tissues of intracranial aneurysms and ruptured aneurysms [15].

A significant challenge in the management of unruptured intracranial aneurysms (UIAs) lies in accurately assessing their rupture risk and making informed decisions between active intervention and conservative observation. Hypertension and smoking are well-established risk factors for aneurysm rupture [16].Additionally, a history of spontaneous subarachnoid hemorrhage (SAH), aneurysm diameter greater than 7 mm, posterior circulation aneurysms, irregular aneurysm morphology (e.g., daughter sacs or multilobular shape), significant aneurysm growth or morphological changes, and a family history of aneurysms are recognized as high-risk factors for rupture [17]. A follow-up study of over 400 intracranial aneurysms revealed that aneurysms with significant wall enhancement had a markedly higher risk of rupture within two years compared to those without wall enhancement [18]. Therefore, high-resolution vessel wall MRI imaging, which assesses aneurysm wall enhancement and its degree, holds promise as a tool for rupture risk assessment. However, prospective cohort studies are needed to validate this approach.

In addition, metabolic features such as lipid metabolism have been shown to be closely associated with intracranial aneurysm formation. A plasma proteomics study identified several potential plasma biomarkers linked to aneurysm development, including apolipoprotein A-1 (ApoA-1), serpin family A member 1 (SERPINA1), and profilin 1 (PFN1) [19]. These findings provide critical insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying aneurysm pathogenesis and open avenues for the development of novel diagnostic and risk assessment tools.

Bioinformatics analysis techniques, including Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA), machine learning algorithms, and single-cell sequencing, have become indispensable tools for understanding complex biological systems and uncovering molecular mechanisms underlying various diseases. WGCNA is widely used to identify gene modules with highly correlated expression patterns and to explore the relationships between these modules and specific phenotypic traits. It facilitates the discovery of hub genes that may serve as critical regulators or biomarkers [20, 21]. Machine learning algorithms enable the extraction of high-dimensional patterns from large-scale biological datasets, offering robust tools for classification, prediction, and feature selection. These algorithms, including support vector machines (SVM), random forests, and neural networks, have been successfully applied to prioritize disease-associated genes, predict disease outcomes, and optimize diagnostic models [22]. Single-cell sequencing provides a high-resolution approach to analyze the transcriptomic heterogeneity of individual cells within complex tissues, allowing the identification of rare cell populations, cell-state transitions, and lineage relationships. In this study, publicly available gene expression datasets related to intracranial aneurysms were retrieved from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO) database. Through comprehensive bioinformatics approaches, including differential expression analysis, WGCNA, and machine learning algorithms, feature genes associated with intracranial aneurysm formation and rupture were systematically investigated. This research holds significant value in identifying novel signature genes in vascular smooth muscle cells linked to aneurysm formation and rupture, as well as in predicting the risks of aneurysm development and rupture.

The workflow of the study

Differential expression analysis

Gene differential expression analysis was performed using the "limma" package in R [23]. After removing potential batch effects and normalizing the gene expression data, a linear model was constructed for each gene to estimate the relationship between gene expression levels and grouping conditions. The “lmFit” function was used to fit the linear model, followed by empirical Bayes moderation using the "eBayes" function to improve the accuracy of statistical inference. Differentially expressed genes (DEGs) were identified based on an adj. P.Val(adjusted p-value) < 0.05 and |logFC(log2 fold change)|> 1. A volcano plot was generated to visualize DEGs, and a heatmap was created to display the expression patterns of the top differentially expressed genes across the samples.

Enrichment analysis of differentially expressed genes

We performed gene ID conversion for differentially expressed genes using the "org.Hs.eg.db" R package. Gene Ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analyses [24] were conducted using the "clusterProfiler" R package [25], while Reactome pathway enrichment was performed using the "ReactomePA" R package [26]. A cutoff of adjusted p-value < 0.05 was applied as the significance threshold for all analyses. The enriched biological functions and pathways of the differentially expressed genes were identified, and the results were visualized using the "ggplot2" and “enrichplot”R packages.

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis

We downloaded the expression matrix of the GSE122897 dataset from the GEO database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/) and obtained corresponding clinical information using the "GEOquery" R package [27]. Sample GSM3487858 was excluded due to unclear intracranial aneurysm status. To standardize the data, we first retrieved gene length information for the human reference genome using the "GenomicFeatures" and "geneLenDataBase" R packages, and converted the raw count data to TPM values [28]. Next, we calculated the variance of each gene and selected the top 25% of genes with the highest variance for further analysis. To ensure data quality, we applied the "goodSamplesGenes" function from the "WGCNA" R package for quality control, and performed hierarchical clustering using average linkage to remove outlier samples [29].

Subsequently, the clinical traits (including normal cortical arteries, aneurysms, and ruptured aneurysms) were binarized for group comparisons using the binarizeCategoricalVariable function from the "WGCNA" R package. Based on a scale-free topology fit index (topological overlap) greater than 0.85 and good average connectivity, we selected a soft threshold (power = 16). We then constructed a scale-free co-expression network using a one-step method, calculated module eigengenes (MEs) for each module, and performed correlation analysis with clinical traits to identify modules significantly associated with clinical characteristics.

To further assess the functional relevance of gene modules, we calculated the Module Membership (MM) and Gene Significance (GS) for each gene. We then computed the Module Eigengene-based Connectivity (KME) values for each gene, and selected genes with high KME values as hub genes for the key modules. Finally, we performed GO and KEGG enrichment analyses of these hub genes, summarized the enrichment results through simplified clustering, and compared the differential enrichment across different modules to reveal their potential biological functions [30].

Machine learning based feature gene screening

In this study, we applied three machine learning algorithms: LASSO regression (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator), SVM-RFE (Support Vector Machine-Recursive Feature Elimination), and Random Forest for feature selection on key differentially expressed genes identified through WGCNA and differential expression analysis. The objective was to further identify feature genes associated with the formation and rupture of intracranial aneurysms. The specific steps for each algorithm are as follows:

LASSO regression

LASSO regression is a typical linear regression method that performs both feature selection and model fitting simultaneously. We utilized the "glmnet" R package to implement LASSO regression. By introducing an L1 regularization term, LASSO shrinks the regression coefficients of certain features to zero, thereby selecting the relevant features. In this study, we used tenfold cross-validation to select the optimal regularization parameter (λ). The model minimizes the residual sum of squares while controlling for model complexity, ultimately selecting significant feature genes associated with the formation and rupture of intracranial aneurysms. The genes with non-zero regression coefficients are considered potential biomarkers and provide a list of candidate genes for further investigation.

SVM-RFE

SVM is used as the base learner, and each feature’s importance is assessed based on its weight in the model. In this study, we used tenfold cross-validation to evaluate the classification error and accuracy for different feature subsets. Using the featsweep function, we constructed SVM models for different feature subsets and calculated the classification performance for each subset. By selecting the feature subset with the lowest classification error, we identified the most important feature genes associated with intracranial aneurysm formation and rupture.

Random forest

The "randomForest" R package was used to implement Random Forest, while the "caret" package was applied for recursive feature elimination, iteratively removing the least important features to improve model performance.

Single-cell sequencing data analysis

In this study, we obtained the h5 files for three samples from the GSE193533 dataset on the GEO database and read the data using the "hdf5r" and "Seurat" R packages [31]. We then merged the three Seurat objects using the "JoinLayers" function and calculated and visualized the proportions of mitochondrial, ribosomal, and erythrocyte genes. Based on these proportions, as well as the number of genes and UMI counts, we filtered out low-quality cells. Cells were retained only if they exhibited a mitochondrial gene percentage < 25% and a hemoglobin gene percentage < 1%. Next, we applied the "harmony" R package to correct for batch effects and performed dimensionality reduction using the UMAP (Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection) algorithm [32]. We then applied the Louvain clustering algorithm at various resolutions to classify the cells into subpopulations. To further investigate the characteristic genes of the cell subpopulations, we utilized the "COSG" R package for feature gene identification and performed cell annotation using the online tool (http://xteam.xbio.top/ACT/) [33]. We extracted the vascular smooth muscle cells subpopulations and performed subsequent normalization, dimensionality reduction, and clustering. Based on marker genes associated with contractile and synthetic phenotypes of VSMCs reported in relevant literature, we calculated the contractile and synthetic phenotype scores for each cell cluster. Additionally, we utilized the “monocle” R package for pseudotime analysis to model the developmental trajectories of VSMCs. This approach allowed us to predict the differentiation dynamics of these cells, classify VSMC subtypes, and calculate the relative proportions of each subtype within the dataset. Finally, gene expression was visualized using functions such as FeaturePlot for spatial mapping of gene expression patterns across the vascular smooth muscle cells.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R (version 4.4.2), with statistical significance defined as p < 0.05. The “pROC” R package was utilized for receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve construction. Calculate the area under the curve (AUC) values to evaluate the predictive performance of potential biomarkers in distinguishing between IAs and control samples, as well as between IAs and ruptured IAs.An AUC > 0.7 was considered indicative of good predictive performance.

The analysis revealed a significant positive correlation between the MEgreen module and the comparison between aneurysmal and normal arterial tissues (r = 0.62, p = 2e-07). Additionally, the MEturquoise module showed a significant negative correlation in the comparison between ruptured and unruptured aneurysms (r = −0.45, p = 4e-04). This suggests that the hub genes of the MEgreen module are closely associated with aneurysm formation, while the hub genes of the MEturquoise module are significantly linked to aneurysm rupture. These modules likely play pivotal roles in the mechanisms underlying aneurysm formation and rupture progression.

Module eigengene-based connectivity (KME) values were calculated to identify hub genes in the MEgreen and MEturquoise modules, with thresholds set at |KME|> 0.80 and > 0.85, respectively. This analysis resulted in the identification of 68 hub genes in the MEgreen module and 348 hub genes in the MEturquoise module (Supplementary Material 1).

Functional enrichment analysis of the hub genes was performed using GO and KEGG. The GO biological process enrichment results were clustered and simplified into 10 major categories, including immune responses, inflammatory responses, cell proliferation, and muscle contraction (Fig. 4D). Further analysis revealed that the hub genes in the MEgreen module were associated with extracellular matrix-receptor interactions, cell–matrix adhesion, focal adhesion, actin filament assembly, muscle cell differentiation, cytoskeleton organization in muscle cells, and growth factor beta signaling pathways. Meanwhile, the hub genes in the MEturquoise module were primarily linked to vascular smooth muscle contraction, calcium signaling pathways, muscle cell development, and cAMP signaling pathways (Fig. 4E-F). Finally, 37 overlapping genes were extracted between the hub genes in the MEgreen module and the upregulated genes in aneurysmal tissue compared to normal arterial tissue, and 100 overlapping genes were identified between the key genes in the MEturquoise module and the DEGs associated with different aneurysm progression states (Supplementary Material 2).

Discussion

The structural integrity of the arterial wall depends on the differentiation state of VSMCs and the extracellular matrix (ECM) [12]. During the early stages of intracranial aneurysm formation, VSMCs undergo migration and proliferation while secreting ECM components. Studies have shown that minimal aneurysms exhibit significant neointimal hyperplasia resembling atherosclerosis, characterized by VSMCs proliferation. Additionally, luminal pads of neointimal hyperplasia originating from the arterial wall have been observed in experimental saccular aneurysm models [34, 35].

Throughout the process of IA formation and rupture, significant changes occur in the differentiation and synthetic functions of VSMCs, accompanied by increased release of matrix metalloproteinases, which are crucial events driving aneurysm rupture. During this progression, VSMCs shift from a contractile phenotype, primarily involved in vascular constriction, to an undifferentiated, pro-inflammatory, and ECM-remodeling phenotype. Common markers of the contractile phenotype in IA studies include smooth muscle 22 alpha (SM22α), smooth muscle alpha actin (α-SMA), smooth muscle myosin heavy chain (SM-MHC), h1-calponin, and smoothelin. The synthetic phenotype is typically validated by assessing gene or protein levels of CRBP-1, MYH10, chemokine MCP-1, VCAM-1, and matrix metalloproteinase MMP-2 [36, 37].

Research has also demonstrated that both VSMC phenotypes are lost during aneurysm rupture. The aneurysmal walls of ruptured lesions exhibit a higher degree of VSMC degeneration compared to unruptured lesions, with increased numbers of apoptotic bodies. VSMC apoptosis has been strongly associated with aneurysm rupture [38]. However, current studies on VSMC phenotypic transitions and their marker genes during different stages of IA progression remain limited. Further refinement and exploration of these mechanisms are urgently needed.

This study systematically investigated the potential molecular mechanisms and characteristic genes involved in IA formation and rupture by integrating bioinformatics analysis, machine learning algorithms, and single-cell transcriptomics, with a particular focus on the critical role of vascular smooth muscle cells in these processes. We identified numerous DEGs associated with IA formation and rupture. Functional enrichment analysis revealed that upregulated genes were primarily involved in inflammatory responses, immune processes, and cytokine signaling pathways, whereas downregulated genes were significantly enriched in pathways related to VSMC contraction, actin cytoskeleton regulation, and focal adhesion. These findings suggest that the activation of inflammation and immune responses, along with VSMC dysfunction, contributes to the pathological changes in IA formation and rupture. Consistent with previous studies, this further underscores the pivotal roles of inflammatory responses and VSMC phenotypic switching in the pathogenesis of IA.

Subsequently, we performed WGCNA to construct gene co-expression networks related to IA formation and rupture and identified two key modules: MEgreen and MEturquoise, which were significantly associated with sample traits. Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that the MEgreen module was primarily involved in ECM remodeling, focal adhesion, and actin cytoskeleton regulation; the MEturquoise module focused on VSMC contraction and calcium signaling pathways. These modules and their hub genes may act synergistically in IA formation and rupture, indicating that the pathological progression of IA is a multifactorial and complex process.

We employed LASSO regression, SVM-RFE, and random forest algorithms to identify characteristic genes associated with IA formation and rupture from DEGs and hub genes in co-expression networks. Single-cell transcriptomic analysis revealed that, as IA progresses, the proportion of immune cells significantly increases, whereas the proportion of VSMCs decreases, with VSMCs undergoing a phenotypic shift toward synthetic VSMCs. COL5A2, CDH11, PLOD1, and P3H4 were primarily expressed in synthetic VSMCs and were significantly upregulated in IA tissue. Similarly, PLIN2 and PLAUR were also expressed in synthetic VSMCs. The ROC analysis validated that the six feature genes demonstrated excellent diagnostic performance in predicting both aneurysm formation and rupture.These genes may serve as important molecular drivers of VSMC phenotypic transformation during IA formation and rupture.

COL5A2 encodes the α2 chain of type V collagen, a key regulator of fibrillogenesis [39]. Arick et al. found that a deficiency in the α2(V) chain exacerbates abdominal aortic aneurysm formation and predisposes to aortic arch dissection and ruptured aneurysms [40]. CDH11 encodes cadherin-11, a cell adhesion molecule that plays a critical role in cell–cell adhesion. Its cytoplasmic tail typically interacts with p120-catenin and β-catenin, facilitating the connection to the cytoskeleton through α-catenin-mediated binding to actin filaments [41]. CDH11 is involved in various cellular processes, including cell migration, invasion, epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT), and wound healing. Studies have shown that CDH11 is also closely associated with diseases related to fibrosis and inflammation. In bone marrow-derived immune cells, the deficiency of CDH11 impairs macrophage migration and promotes the proliferation of helper T cells (Th cells), thereby exacerbating atherosclerosis and inflammation [42]. PLOD1 encodes lysyl hydroxylase 1, an enzyme that plays a critical role in procollagen formation by post-translationally modifying lysine residues into hydroxylysine. PLOD1 is associated with vascular smooth muscle cell phenotype transformation in adult aortic diseases, such as familial thoracic aortic aneurysms. Sara et al. found that overexpression of PLOD1 leads to upregulation of procollagen gene expression, enhancing the secretory function of VSMCs. In contrast, silencing PLOD1 results in the upregulation of contractile markers in aortic vascular smooth muscle cells [43]. P3H4 encodes prolyl 3-hydroxylase family member 4, which can form a complex with the proteins encoded by P3H3 and PLOD1. It catalyzes the hydroxylation of lysine residues in the collagen α-chain, a process essential for the proper assembly and cross-linking of collagen fibers [44]. PLIN2 encodes the lipid droplet protein Perilipin 2, which plays a role in regulating intracellular lipid metabolism. Studies have shown that PLIN2 enhances macrophage inflammatory responses by upregulating the expression and secretion of various inflammatory cytokines, and it plays a crucial role in pathological conditions such as atherosclerosis and cerebral ischemia–reperfusion injury [45]. PLAUR encodes the urokinase plasminogen activator receptor (UPAR), a glycosylphosphatidylinositol (GPI)-anchored membrane protein that is expressed on a variety of cell types. UPAR is a key mediator in plasminogen activation and fibrinolysis. The interaction between urokinase-type plasminogen activator (UPA) and UPAR is linked to actin-cytoskeleton reorganization and cell adhesion, which regulates the function of VSMCs involved in vascular remodeling. This process plays a critical role in the progression of atherosclerotic plaque formation [46]. These findings provide important theoretical insights into the molecular mechanisms underlying IA and offer potential therapeutic targets for future research.

The results of the present study indicate that inflammation, immune regulation, and dysfunction of VSMCs play a critical role in the formation and rupture of IAs. Moreover, the identified feature genes provide important insights for further elucidating the molecular mechanisms associated with IAs and lay the foundation for the development of molecular diagnostic markers and potential targeted therapies for IAs. In addition, this study integrates multiple bioinformatics approaches, offering a reference model for the systematic study of complex diseases. However, some limitations remain in the study. For example, the data used were sourced from publicly available databases, and the sample size is relatively limited; the functions of the identified feature genes and their specific roles in IAs still need to be validated through further experimental studies. We attempted to build a diagnostic evaluation model using LASSO regression. However, due to the limited sample size of publicly available sequencing data for intracranial aneurysms (training set ≤ 49 samples), only threefold cross-validation could be performed to ensure the rationality of the sample size in each fold. Additionally, the model exhibited obvious overfitting during validation, resulting in insufficient generalization ability(Figure S4). Given that the core objective of this study is to screen characteristic genes associated with intracranial aneurysm formation and rupture related to vascular smooth muscle cell phenotype, rather than developing a clinical diagnostic tool, we did not further force the construction of an integrated diagnostic model.Future research should involve larger patient cohorts and in vivo and in vitro experiments to further confirm the clinical application potential and value of these feature genes. Furthermore, due to the availability of only mouse IA model-derived single-cell sequencing data from public databases, there are inherent species differences, which may affect the biological interpretation and clinical translational application of the findings. Therefore, future studies should further expand and optimize the research design, incorporating more human samples and high-resolution single-cell sequencing data to provide more comprehensive evidence for unveiling the molecular mechanisms of IA.

Conclusion

COL5A2, CDH11, PLOD1, and P3H4 were identified as upregulated genes in vascular smooth muscle cells associated with IA formation, while PLIN2 and PLAUR were associated with IA rupture.The identification of these genes provides a foundation for further investigation into the molecular mechanisms underlying the role of vascular smooth muscle cells in IA development and progression. Additionally, these genes offer potential biomarkers for early diagnosis and risk assessment of intracranial aneurysms.
