A Prospective Study of Marsh PK‐PD Model and Schnider PK‐PD Model During Anesthesia Induction for Obese Patients Undergoing Elective Heart Surgery
Authors: Jianhong Ye, Weina Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis prospective study compared the Marsh and Schnider pharmacokinetic models for propofol target‐controlled infusion (TCI) during anesthetic induction in obese patients undergoing elective cardiac surgery. A total of 118 patients were randomly assigned to either the Marsh or Schnider group (n = 59 each). The primary outcome was the performance error (ΔC = measured minus predicted plasma concentration) during and after TCI. Secondary outcomes included hemodynamic pharmacokinetic‐pharmacodynamic (PK‐PD) model, anesthetic depth (BIS), cardiac function (LVEF, SV), electrocardiographic PK‐PD model (QTc, QTcd), and recovery profiles. While no significant differences in ΔC were observed during TCI, the Marsh model demonstrated significantly lower ΔC at all time points after TCI cessation (p < 0.05). Compared to the Schnider group, the Marsh group also showed higher BIS values, better preserved LVEF and SV, shorter QTc/QTcd intervals, reduced propofol requirements, and shorter recovery times (all p < 0.05). In conclusion, under the conditions of this study, in which propofol TCI was configured using total body weight for the Marsh model and lean body weight for the Schnider model, the Marsh model provided more accurate plasma concentration control, better hemodynamic stability, and improved recovery profiles compared with the Schnider model for anesthetic induction in obese cardiac surgery patients.Keywords: elective cardiac surgery, hemodynamics, induction of anesthesia, marsh PK‐PD model, myocardial contractile function, obesity, Schnider PK‐PD model

Introduction

Elective cardiac surgery is a common clinical intervention for the treatment of heart disease; however, surgical procedures may induce myocardial ischemic injury and hemodynamic fluctuations, thereby compromising therapeutic outcomes [1, 2]. Previous studies have indicated [3] that pathophysiological alterations in morbidly obese patients can influence pharmacokinetics, modifying drug distribution, absorption, metabolism, and excretion, leading to differences in drug dosing compared to non‐obese individuals. Therefore, optimizing anesthesia management in obese patients undergoing elective cardiac surgery is of critical importance. Currently, target‐controlled infusion (TCI) systems for anesthetic drugs primarily incorporate two pharmacokinetic‐pharmacodynamic (PK‐PD) models: the Marsh PK‐PD model and the Schnider PK‐PD model. The Marsh PK‐PD model, derived from pediatric pharmacokinetic data using steady‐state venous sampling [4], calculates drug disposition based solely on total body weight without incorporating age or gender as covariates. In contrast, the Schnider PK‐PD model was developed from healthy, non‐obese adult volunteers using arterial blood sampling for PK‐PD analysis [5], and incorporates age, gender, and lean body mass as covariates. Notably, the Schnider PK‐PD model's derivation from non‐obese individuals may limit its accuracy in obese patients, where altered body composition and volume of distribution significantly affect propofol pharmacokinetics. While newer general‐purpose models such as the Eleveld model have been developed to accommodate diverse populations, the Marsh and Schnider PK‐PD models remain the most widely available and clinically implemented TCI platforms worldwide. This study therefore compares these two standard models to provide evidence‐based guidance for their application in obese patients undergoing cardiac surgery, a population in which their relative performance has not been systematically evaluated.

Methods

General Data

This prospective study enrolled 118 consecutive obese patients undergoing elective cardiac procedures between May 2022 and September 2024, with computer‐generated randomization allocating 59 participants to each study arm. Sample size calculation was performed a priori based on the primary outcome of performance error (ΔC) at 5 min after TCI cessation. Based on a pilot study of 10 patients per group, the mean ΔC at this time point was −0.10 ± 0.03 μg/mL in the Marsh group and 0.52 ± 0.17 μg/mL in the Schnider group. Using a two‐sided significance level of α = 0.05 and power of 90% (β = 0.10), the minimum required sample size was calculated as eight patients per group. To account for a potential 20% dropout rate, the target sample size was increased to 10 patients per group. However, to ensure adequate power for secondary outcome analyses and to enhance the robustness of the findings, all 118 consecutive eligible patients presenting during the study period were enrolled, with 59 patients allocated to each group. This final sample size exceeds the minimum required and is consistent with sample sizes reported in comparable TCI comparative studies.

Selection Criteria

Inclusion Criteria

Body mass index (BMI) ≥ 30 kg/m2; New York Heart Association (NYHA) functional grading II–III; Meeting the indications for elective cardiac surgery; Provision of informed consent for participation in the study.

Exclusion Criteria

Severe hemodynamic instability requiring pharmacological intervention; psychiatric disorders; patients presenting with acute coronary syndrome or those who had experienced decompensated cardiac failure or acute myocardial ischemia within the preceding 30 days were excluded from participation; hematologic or immune system diseases; previous coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG) or valve replacement surgery; cognitive impairment; active severe infection; malignancy.

Anesthetic Management and Data Collection

All enrolled subjects underwent upper limb venous access establishment upon entering the operating room, with simultaneous radial artery catheterization on the ipsilateral side connected to a GE Healthcare B650 multiparameter patient monitor for vital sign surveillance (heart rate, invasive arterial blood pressure). Anesthetic depth monitoring was performed using the GE Healthcare S/5 Entropy Module to record SE and RE values. Propofol TCI was administered using Fresenius Base Primea infusion pumps equipped with integrated TCI software (Fresenius Kabi, Germany), which include both Marsh and Schnider pharmacokinetic models. The Marsh model was configured using total body weight (TBW) for dose calculation, while the Schnider model was configured using lean body weight (LBW) as the weight scalar, consistent with their respective pharmacokinetic algorithms. Anesthesia was delivered via a Drager Fabius GS anesthesia machine (Drager, Germany).

Premedication consisting of 0.5 mg penehyclidine HCl and 10 mg dexamethasone IV was administered 30 min before initiating anesthesia. During induction, sufentanil (0.25 μg/kg) was injected intravenously, followed by TCI of propofol (Fresenius Kabi Deutschland GmbH; National Drug Approval No.: HJ20150660). A target plasma concentration of 2 μg/mL was selected for induction based on standard clinical practice for cardiac surgery patients, as this concentration provides adequate anesthetic depth while maintaining hemodynamic stability in obese populations. Following induction of unconsciousness, 0.3 mg/kg cisatracurium was given IV to facilitate tracheal intubation when SE measurements were maintained under 60. Ventilator parameters were then established with a respiratory frequency of 12–14 cycles per minute, I:E ratio of 1:1.5, and tidal volumes adjusted to 6–8 mL/kg of ideal body weight.

Anesthesia maintenance was achieved via total intravenous anesthesia (TIVA). Propofol was administered via TCI with target concentrations adjusted manually by the anesthesiologist based on real‐time Entropy (SE) values and hemodynamic responses. The target plasma concentration was initially maintained at 2 μg/mL and adjusted in increments of 0.2–0.5 μg/mL to maintain SE values between 40 and 60 and hemodynamic variables within 20% of baseline. If signs of inadequate anesthesia (hypertension, tachycardia, or increased SE values) occurred, the target concentration was increased; conversely, if hypotension or bradycardia developed, the target was reduced. Remifentanil was infused continuously at 5–10 μg/(kg × h). During the surgical procedure, physiological parameters were maintained within predetermined ranges: PETCO2 at 30–50 mmHg, SpO2 between 98%–100%, and hemodynamic variables (arterial pressure and cardiac rhythm) within 20% of preoperative measurements. Target‐controlled propofol administration was terminated 10 min before wound closure, with remifentanil delivery discontinued after the final surgical intervention. The predicted effect‐site concentration at the moment of emergence from anesthesia (CeROC), defined as the time when patients first opened their eyes to verbal command, was automatically recorded by the TCI pump.

For determination of propofol plasma concentrations, 3 mL arterial blood samples were collected from the radial artery catheter at predefined time points during TCI (5, 10, 15, 30, 45, 60 min) and after TCI discontinuation (1, 5, 10, 30, 45, 60 min). Samples were collected into heparinized tubes and immediately centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. Plasma was separated and stored at −80°C until analysis. Propofol concentrations were measured using high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with fluorescence detection (Agilent 1260 Infinity II, Agilent Technologies, USA). The assay linear range was 0.05–10 μg/mL, with intra‐ and inter‐assay coefficients of variation < 8%. All measurements were performed in duplicate by an independent laboratory technician blinded to group allocation.

For electrocardiographic analysis, standard 12‐lead ECGs were recorded at each time point (T0–T3) using the GE Healthcare B650 multiparameter monitor with a paper speed of 25 mm/s and gain of 10 mm/mV. QT intervals were measured manually by two independent investigators blinded to group allocation, from the onset of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave (defined as the return to the isoelectric line). In the presence of U waves, the QT interval was measured to the nadir of the curve between T and U waves. Three consecutive cardiac cycles were measured in each lead, and the average value was calculated. QT intervals were corrected for heart rate using Bazett's formula: QTc = QT/√¯(RR interval). QT dispersion (QTd) was defined as the difference between the maximum and minimum QT intervals across all 12 leads, and corrected QT dispersion (QTcd) was calculated as the difference between maximum and minimum QTc intervals. Intra‐observer and inter‐observer variability were assessed in 20 randomly selected ECGs, with intra‐class correlation coefficients > 0.85 indicating good reliability.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS version 22.0, with normally distributed continuous variables reported as mean ± SD (x¯±s) and analyzed by Student's t‐test, while categorical data were expressed as frequency (%) and evaluated using chi‐square tests, adopting a significance threshold of p < 0.05.

Discussion

During cardiac surgery, anesthetic depth and hemodynamics are prone to fluctuations. TCI of anesthetic agents facilitates hemodynamic stability; however, in obese patients, altered tissue distribution due to obesity significantly affects the apparent volume of distribution of anesthetics, potentially complicating the maintenance of appropriate sedation depth during anesthesia [6, 7]. Therefore, selecting an appropriate anesthetic sedation management protocol is critical to ensuring the safety of obese patients undergoing elective cardiac surgery during anesthesia induction and minimizing myocardial functional impairment.

Existing research has established that the precision of target‐controlled infusion performance is fundamentally dependent on the underlying pharmacokinetic model parameters [8, 9]. The pharmacokinetic parameters for propofol TCI include the Marsh and Schnider PK‐PD models. The Marsh PK‐PD model exhibits an overshoot phenomenon during initial TCI and target concentration adjustments [10], whereas the Schnider PK‐PD model, a modified version of the Marsh PK‐PD model, demonstrates higher system accuracy and safety [11, 12]. However, the accuracy of these pharmacokinetic parameters in TCI systems for obese patients undergoing elective cardiac surgery requires further investigation.

The differential performance observed between the two models can be attributed to their respective weight‐scaling strategies. The Marsh model, which calculates propofol dosing based on total body weight (TBW), inherently accounts for the increased distribution volume in obese patients, which may explain its more favorable performance in this study. In contrast, the Schnider model was originally developed using lean body weight (LBW) as the weight scalar in non‐obese volunteers; when applied in clinical practice with total body weight as the input, as is common in many TCI systems, the model systematically overestimates drug requirements in obese individuals. This mismatch between the model's underlying weight metric and the patient's body composition likely explains the higher measured‐to‐predicted concentration differences, deeper anesthetic levels, and greater hemodynamic suppression observed in the Schnider group. Thus, rather than indicating an inherent superiority of the Marsh pharmacokinetic structure, the present findings reflect the consequence of appropriate versus mismatched weight scaling in the obese population under the specific TCI configuration used in this study.

The present study revealed no statistically significant differences in ΔC (measured plasma concentration minus predicted concentration) across time points during propofol TCI, with all ΔC values being positive, indicating that measured concentrations exceeded predicted concentrations but gradually approached them over time. Following termination of target‐controlled infusion, the Marsh pharmacokinetic model consistently demonstrated reduced performance errors (ΔC) compared to the Schnider protocol across all postoperative measurement intervals. In the Schnider group, ΔC remained positive (measured > predicted), declining slowly toward predicted concentrations, whereas in the Marsh group, ΔC initially registered negative values (measured < predicted) before gradually approaching zero. These findings suggest that the Marsh PK‐PD model achieves closer alignment between measured and predicted propofol concentrations, demonstrating superior accuracy for propofol TCI in obese cardiac surgery patients compared to the Schnider PK‐PD model. Prior research highlights that failure to recognize distinctions between these models may result in inadequate dosing (insufficient anesthesia) or excessive dosing (excessive depth) [13, 14]. To mitigate such risks, this study employed routine hemodynamic and bispectral index (BIS) monitoring. While hemodynamic profiles did not differ significantly between groups, BIS values at T1–T3 were higher in the Marsh group, indicating shallower anesthesia depth versus the Schnider group. The T1 BIS disparity may stem from the Schnider PK‐PD model's characteristic overshoot, whereas T2–T3 differences likely reflect divergent target‐control algorithms.

Cardiac surgery entails unique pathophysiological challenges. Continuous assessment of cardiac output and vascular resistance facilitates precise hemodynamic optimization throughout anesthetic phases, effectively minimizing perioperative complications [15, 16]. The observed preservation of ventricular function (higher LVEF and SV from T1–T3) in the Marsh cohort implies that the Schnider algorithm may potentiate ischemia–reperfusion damage in obese individuals, ultimately compromising circulatory efficiency. Although certain anesthetics are known to prolong QTc and QT dispersion (QTcd) [17, 18], few studies have compared the electrophysiological effects of these TCI models. Here, the Marsh group exhibited shorter QTc and QTcd at T2–T3 than the Schnider group, implying a protective role against ischemia–reperfusion injury. However, the precise mechanisms remain unclear and warrant further exploration.

Additionally, both TCI models provided adequate anesthesia, but the Marsh group required lower propofol doses per unit time and cumulatively, with shorter recovery‐to‐awakening and extubation times than the Schnider group. The propofol concentration required for unconsciousness induction using the Marsh PK‐PD model demonstrated a significant correlation with postoperative recovery times, highlighting its particular clinical utility for obese patients undergoing cardiac procedures.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size, while sufficient for the primary outcome, may limit the generalizability of secondary findings. Second, the single‐center design and specific inclusion criteria (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2, elective cardiac surgery) restrict extrapolation to other populations or clinical settings. Third, although the Marsh and Schnider models were compared, newer general‐purpose models (e.g., Eleveld model) were not evaluated. Finally, the study focused on induction and early recovery phases; long‐term outcomes were not assessed. Further multicenter studies with larger cohorts and extended follow‐up are warranted to validate these findings.

In summary, while both PK‐PD models are applicable for anesthesia induction in obese cardiac surgery patients, the Marsh PK‐PD model offers superior performance in maintaining target propofol concentrations, regulating anesthetic depth, myocardial protection, and recovery profiles, meriting broader clinical adoption.
