Delocalized Redox Framework of Indanthrone Enables Low‐Strain and Durable Mn2+/H+ Storage in Aqueous Batteries
Authors: Hyeongseok Lee, Hyeju Kwon, Seunghyeop Baek, Yoo Sei Park, KwangJin Kim, Chung‐Yul Yoo, Munseok S Chae
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAqueous manganese batteries (AMBs) are promising alternatives to lithium‐ion systems due to their safety and cost‐effectiveness. However, organic cathodes often face challenges regarding material dissolution and slow ion kinetics. In this work, we introduce indanthrone (IDT) as a strategic organic cathode for high‐performance Mn2+/H+ batteries. IDT features a massive eight‐ring π‐conjugated framework that enables extensive electronic delocalization. Unlike systems with excessively concentrated active groups, the IDT framework provides a more dispersed distribution of redox‐active sites. This arch\itecture promotes smooth ion transport through enhanced charge delocalization across the extended aromatic system. The IDT electrode delivers a high specific capa\city of 194.8 mAh/g at 0.1 A/g, while the Mn‐IDT full‐cell exhibits 85.5% capacity retention after 3000 cycles at 0.3 A/g. Spectroscopic and crystallographic analyses confirm a low‐strain pathway, where b‐axis expansion (2.09%) is effectively compensated by minor contractions in the a and c‐axes. This unique anisotropic response leads to minimal overall volume fluctuation, ensuring structural integrity during prolonged operation. This work provides a molecular‐level design principle for constructing low‐strain organic cathodes for high‐voltage aqueous multivalent batteries.Keywords: aqueous batteries, hybrid battery, Indanthrone, manganese batteries, organic cathode

Introduction

Recently, lithium‐ion batteries (LIBs) have achieved superior energy densities and broad accessibility through the accelerated evolution of energy storage technologies [1]. Nevertheless, their progress is increasingly hampered by the finite availability of essential minerals, specifically lithium and nickel [2]. Furthermore, the inherent toxicity and flammability of conventional organic electrolytes present significant environmental risks and persistent safety vulnerabilities, including thermal runaway and fire hazards [3]. To mitigate these concerns, aqueous batteries employing water‐based electrolytes have emerged as a formidable alternative, ensuring economic feasibility, non‐flammability, and operational safety while offering exceptionally high ionic conductivity [4, 5].

Among various aqueous systems, manganese (Mn) stands out as a promising anode material [6, 7, 8]. It is characterized by its natural abundance and a markedly lower standard reduction potential (−1.19 V vs. SHE) relative to other conventional metal anodes. This deeper potential theoretically facilitates the construction of batteries with substantially higher operating voltages. Moreover, given its high theoretical specific capacity of 976 mAh/g, aqueous manganese batteries (AMBs) have garnered considerable attention as a focal point for next‐generation energy storage systems.

Despite these advantages, the development of suitable cathode materials remains a major hurdle. Conventional transition‐metal‐based inorganic materials, such as vanadium oxides (e.g., CaV6O16 [9], H2V3O8 [10], and Al0.1V2O5· 1.5H2O [11]) and Prussian blue analogues (e.g., MnHCF [12], ZnHCF [13], and NiHCF [14]), suffer from limitations such as active material dissolution, structural instability, and the presence of interstitial water or lattice defects. These issues lead to rapid capacity fading, thereby limiting their practical applicability.

Organic cathode materials (OCMs) have recently attracted attention as a sustainable alternative, offering versatile structural designability to effectively accommodate the intercalation of ions [15]. While these attributes are encouraging, translating such theoretical advantages into practical applications remains a formidable challenge. Within the diverse class of OCMs, conjugated carbonyl (C═O) compounds are widely utilized due to their superior specific capacities. Anthraquinone is a representative example, yet its dissolution in electrolytes results in continuous capacity decay and poor cycling stability [16]. To suppress dissolution, extending the aromatic framework through the incorporation of pyrazine units has been proposed, as seen in 5,7,12,14‐tetraaza‐6,13‐pentacenequinone (TAPQ) for rechargeable aqueous zinc batteries [17]. Nonetheless, TAPQ exhibits a significant discrepancy between its theoretical and delivered capacities, likely due to the excessively concentrated distribution of C═O and C═N active groups that hinders efficient ion transport.

To overcome these structural and electrochemical limitations, indanthrone (IDT) as a strategic solution to these structural and electrochemical limitations. IDT is a well‐known commercial blue dye industrially derived from 2‐aminoanthraquinone, its insolubility in organic solvents and established production routes ensure excellent chemical stability and cost‐effectiveness. Structurally, IDT integrates two Anthraquinone units via a dihydropyrazine ring, forming a massive eight‐ring π‐conjugated framework and a stable π–π stacking architecture. This configuration facilitates extensive electronic delocalization [18]. Furthermore, by promoting electronic delocalization and effectively lowering the energy barrier for ion diffusion, this conjugated framework achieves a higher specific capacity and a low‐strain pathway compared to non‐conjugated systems [19, 20]. Unlike TAPQ, the IDT framework provides a more dispersed distribution of redox‐active sites, which promotes smooth ion transport and prevents localized electrostatic trapping of ions [21].

In this work, we demonstrate a new concept of an aqueous manganese battery by pairing the IDT cathode with a Mn metal anode. This configuration allows IDT to operate safely within its stability window while achieving high capacity. The IDT electrode delivers a high specific capacity of 194.8 mAh/g at 0.1 A/g, approaching 80.4% of its theoretical capacity (242.3 mAh/g). The Mn‐IDT full cell maintains 85.5% of its initial capacity after 3000 cycles at 0.3 A/g. Our results confirm that the delocalized π‐conjugated structure of IDT enables a low‐strain pathway through a unique anisotropic lattice response. Specifically, the expansion along the b‐axis (2.09%) during ion intercalation is effectively compensated by minor contractions in the a and c‐axes, resulting in minimal overall volume fluctuation. This cooperative structural behavior effectively minimizes structural degradation during cycling, proving the potential of IDT as a robust and durable redox framework for high‐voltage aqueous manganese‐ion energy storage.

Results and Discussion

Investigating the Molecular and Crystalline Features of Pristine IDT

Figure 1a,b illustrates that indanthrone exhibits a layered structure oriented along the b‐axis. Site A, centered around the carbonyl oxygen atoms, serves as both a diffusion pathway for protons (H+) and a primary redox‐active site for reversible storage via chemical interactions with oxygen. Site B facilitates the insertion and transport of Mn2+ ions, where oxygen and nitrogen atoms each contribute a single electron to stabilize the Mn2+ ions through a coordination mechanism, thereby enabling stable storage and smooth ionic migration. Site C, defined by the interlayer space between molecular layers, acts as a dual‐ion channel where both Mn2+ and H+ ions can be stored and transported. It accommodates simultaneous ion insertion and ensures rapid diffusion along the interlayer pathways. Such a structural configuration demonstrates that IDT possesses a three‐dimensional framework with integrated two‐dimensional interlayer channels, providing a highly favorable environment for the facile transport and efficient storage of ions within the electrode. Figure 1c and Table S1 display the X‐ray diffraction (XRD) pattern along with the Rietveld refinement results obtained using the GSAS program [22] to verify the phase purity and structural characteristics of IDT. All major diffraction peaks are well‐indexed to a monoclinic structure with the P21/a space group [23, 24]. The refined lattice parameters were determined to be a = 30.94(2) Å, b = 3.872(1) Å, c = 7.823(2) Å, and β = 92.11(3)°. The high phase purity and crystallinity, as evidenced by the diffraction data, facilitate favorable charge‐storage reactions. Additionally, the relatively long a and c axes signify the presence of wide diffusion channels and ample open space within the crystal lattice. These structural attributes demonstrate that the IDT framework is well‐suited for achieving exceptional long‐term durability and high reversible capacity.

The molecular vibrations and functional groups of IDT were further characterized via Raman spectroscopy, as illustrated in Figure 1d. The spectrum exhibits distinct peaks in the range of 1227.9–1435.5 cm−1, which are assigned to the C─N stretching vibrations of the amine linkages. The aromatic C═C stretching modes are observed between 1509.3 and 1608.3 cm−1, confirming the robust π‐conjugated framework of the molecule. Notably, the vibrational bands in the 1609.8–1666.8 cm−1 region correspond to the C═O carbonyl stretching, while the signals spanning 3082.5 to 3293.1 cm−1 represent the N─H amine stretching vibrations. Figure 1e presents the Fourier‐transform infrared (FTIR) spectrum of IDT, showcasing its characteristic functional groups. The IR spectrum exhibits prominent absorption bands in the range of 1440–1079cm−1, which are assigned to C─N stretching vibrations. Aromatic C═C stretching modes are observed within the 1600–1502cm−1 region. Notably, the absorption bands between 1696 and 1604cm−1 are identified as C═O carbonyl stretching, while the bands spanning 3281–3064cm−1 correspond to N─H stretching vibrations. These FTIR results, in conjunction with the Raman data, successfully characterize the indanthrone framework and confirm its chemical structure [18].

Figure 1f presents the transmission electron microscopy (TEM) image of IDT, revealing a well‐defined rod‐like morphology. The individual structures exhibit lengths of approximately 1–2 µm, characterizing them as microrods with a high aspect ratio that form an interconnected network. Such structural attributes, particularly the elongated and spacious architecture, are highly advantageous for providing efficient pathways for ion transport. As shown in Figure 1g, a single microrod displays a consistent width of approximately 100 nm and a smooth surface. Additionally, the high‐resolution TEM (HR‐TEM) image in Figure 1h confirms a uniform surface texture without discernible structural defects at the edge regions. This high degree of structural integrity ensures intimate contact between the IDT surface and the conductive additives, which is crucial for facilitating efficient interfacial charge transfer during electrochemical processes. More detailed images are available in Figure S1. The elemental composition and distribution of IDT were further analyzed using TEM‐based energy‐dispersive X‐ray spectroscopy (EDX) mapping, as presented in Figure 1i. The elemental maps reveal that C (green), N (purple), O (blue), and Mn (orange) are homogeneously distributed throughout the entire structure of the microrods. This uniform distribution confirms the high chemical homogeneity of the synthesized IDT and the successful incorporation of all constituent elements within the framework. The quantitative elemental composition of the pristine IDT, further confirming the presence of C, N, O, and Mn is provided in the TEM‐EDX spectra (Figure S2).

Electrochemical Charge Storage Performance of IDT

The electrochemical properties of IDT were investigated using cyclic voltammetry (CV) within a potential range of −0.7 V to 0.7 V (vs. Ag/AgCl) at a scan rate of 0.1 mV/s, as shown in Figure 2a. The CV curve displays distinct oxidation peaks at −0.29, −0.22, and 0.51 V during the anodic scan. In the subsequent discharge process, corresponding reduction peaks are observed at 0.32 and −0.69 V. These features indicate the reversible redox reactions of the carbonyl and amine groups within the IDT framework, enabling efficient energy storage and release. Furthermore, the sharp current response near the oxidation and reduction boundaries demonstrates that the extended conjugated backbone facilitates rapid ion intercalation and de‐intercalation. It is established that potential beyond 0.8 V leads to severe water decomposition (OER), which can cause structural severing, a significant decrease in electrical conductivity, and the collapse of ion storage sites. Consequently, an operating window of −0.7 to 0.7 V is optimal for maintaining stable electrochemical performance.

(a) CV curve of IDT at a scan rate of 0.1 mV/s, (b) GCD profiles of IDT at various current densities ranging from 0.1 to 0.8 A/g, (c) Rate capability, (d) Long‐term cycling stability of the IDT electrode at 0.2 A/g over 2000 cycles, (e) Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) of the IDT electrode in its pristine state and after 2000 cycles.

Figure 2b illustrates the galvanostatic charge‐discharge (GCD) profiles of the IDT electrode at various current densities. At a low current density of 0.1 A/g, the electrode delivers a remarkably high maximum specific capacity of 194.8 mAh/g with a prominent and long voltage plateau. This feature signifies that the electrode is capable of providing a stable energy output at a constant potential, which is a desirable characteristic for practical battery applications. As the current density increases to 0.2, 0.4, and 0.8 A/g, the discharge capacities are recorded as 95.4, 24.5, and 8.27 mAh/g, respectively. The origin of this high capacity is attributed to the extended π‐conjugated architecture of the IDT framework. This structural property effectively stabilizes the redox‐active carbonyl and amine groups during the ion intercalation process. Furthermore, the conjugation facilitates efficient electron delocalization, which ensures a high degree of active site utilization throughout the organic framework. However, a sharp decline in capacity is observed at elevated current densities (e.g., 0.8 A/g). This kinetic limitation is primarily governed by an interfacial passivation phenomenon rather than bulk structural collapse. During the rapid discharge process, protons are instantly consumed at the electrode surface. Because this rapid proton consumption outpaces the diffusion rate of H+ from the bulk electrolyte, the local pH at the immediate electrode‐electrolyte interface spikes momentarily [25]. This sudden alkaline microenvironment triggers the immediate precipitation of irreversible Mn(OH)2 byproducts. At 0.8 A/g, this Mn(OH)2 layer forms rapidly and physically blocks the active sites and ion diffusion channels at the interface. Consequently, it causes severe cell polarization, leading to the premature reaching of the designated voltage limit before the bulk IDT framework can be fully utilized, which ultimately results in a drastic reduction in capacity. To evaluate the rate capability and structural robustness of the IDT electrode, a step‐wise cycling test was conducted by increasing the current density from 0.1 to 0.8 A/g and subsequently returning it to 0.1 A/g, as depicted in Figure 2c. The electrode initially delivers a specific capacity of 194.2 mAh/g at 0.1 A/g. As the current density increases to 0.2, 0.4, and 0.8 A/g, the capacities gradually decrease to 93.2, 24.0, and 8.90 mAh/g, respectively. Crucially, when the current density is reverted to the initial 0.1 A/g, the capacity successfully recovers to 192.9 mAh/g, which is nearly identical to its original state (99.3%). This remarkable recovery demonstrates the superior reversibility and durability of the IDT framework. The GITT results further support the favorable ion transport kinetics of the IDT framework (Figure S3), exhibiting ion diffusion coefficients on the order of ∼10−9 cm2/s throughout most of the discharge‐charge process, which indicates relatively stable and efficient ion diffusion behavior within the delocalized π‐conjugated framework. Additional theoretical studies, including density functional theory (DFT) calculations and ion diffusion barrier analyses, may provide deeper insight into the effect of the delocalized π‐conjugated framework on ion transport behavior. It further proves that the IDT electrode maintains its structural integrity without significant degradation even after exposure to elevated current densities, ensuring stable electrochemical performance. The long‐term cycling stability of the IDT electrode was evaluated at a current density of 0.2 A/g over 2000 cycles, as depicted in Figure 2d. The electrode maintained a discharge capacity of 50.6 mAh/g at the 2000 cycle, corresponding to a capacity retention of 61.1%. with an average Coulombic efficiency of 102.3%. This capacity fade is primarily attributed to the gradual formation of Mn(OH)2 on the electrode surface through parasitic side reactions; this byproduct acts as a passivating layer that increases internal resistance and hinders efficient ion transport, a phenomenon that is more pronounced during long‐term cycling at lower current densities. The Coulombic efficiency slightly exceeding 100% is mainly attributed to minor oxygen evolution reactions (OER) and the subsequent reduction of generated oxygen species during discharge in the aqueous electrolyte system. This effect is further amplified by the beaker‐type cell configuration with a relatively large electrolyte volume and should therefore be considered an intrinsic artifact of aqueous systems. Such sustained performance after extensive cycling demonstrates that the IDT electrode possesses remarkable structural stability and reliable capacity retention during prolonged charge‐discharge operations. To further corroborate these findings, Figures S4 and S5 illustrate that the characteristic peaks associated with the core IDT framework and its functional groups remain virtually intact, showing no significant disappearance or deformation. Furthermore, SEM analysis of the pristine and cycled electrodes (Figure S6) confirms that the overall morphology remains well‐preserved without any structural fracture or deformation. These results validate the exceptional chemical durability and high structural reversibility of the IDT framework, even after rigorous long‐term electrochemical testing.

Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was employed to further investigate the origin of the performance degradation through the change in internal impedance, as shown in Figure 2e. The Nyquist plots comparing the electrode in its pristine state and after 2000 cycles reveal an increase in both the internal resistance (Rir, from 0.672 to 1.55 Ω) and the charge‐transfer resistance (Rct, from 17.1 to 65.8 Ω). This impedance growth is primarily attributed to the formation of byproducts, such as Mn(OH)2, on the electrode surface, induced by the aqueous electrolyte during long‐term cycling [26]. The presence of these byproducts is further evidenced by the ex situ Mn 2p XPS spectra (Figure S7). While no Mn signal is detected in the pristine IDT cathode, a distinct Mn signal emerges after cycling, even in the fully charged state. This serves as definitive proof of the deposition of irreversible Mn‐based side products on the IDT framework. Notably, the attenuation of C─O─Mn related features indicates a decline in the reversible intercalation performance of Mn2+ and H+ ions, as the active storage capability of the framework degrades after extensive cycling (Figure S7). The C 1s and N 1s spectra further confirm that the core IDT structure remains intact, as the characteristic peaks for C─C, C─N, C═O, and C─N─C bonding states are well‐preserved. To further substantiate the structural integrity of the IDT framework, the XRD patterns of the electrode were examined before and after cycling (Figure S8). The diffraction profile after cycling remains highly consistent with the pristine state, indicating that the characteristic peaks of the IDT framework are preserved without any irreversible phase transformations or significant loss of crystallinity. These results demonstrate that the crystalline structure remains intact even after extensive electrochemical stress, providing further evidence for the exceptional durability mentioned earlier. However, the accumulation of surface byproducts effectively passivates the active sites and hinders charge transfer. These surface modifications ultimately contribute to the significant increase in Rct and the observed capacity fade. The low‐frequency region shows a relatively unchanged Warburg slope, indicating that bulk ion diffusion remains largely unaffected despite interfacial resistance growth [26].

Investigation of Reversible Redox Chemistry and Mn2+/H+ Ions Intercalation

To confirm the reversible storage of Mn2+ within the IDT electrode, ex situ TEM‐EDX mapping and XPS analyses were performed, as presented in Figure 3. Figure 3a illustrates the designated voltage steps during the discharge‐charge process within the potential range of 0.7 to −0.7 V. The TEM‐EDX elemental mapping (Figure 3b,c) provides visual evidence of Mn2+ intercalation, with the distribution of C (green), N (purple), O (blue), and Mn (orange) clearly identified. In the fully discharged state, small Mn is uniformly distributed across the electrode, signifying the successful insertion of Mn2+ from the electrolyte into the host framework. The results suggest that proton‐related reactions play a dominant role in the overall charge storage, while Mn2+ insertion contributes partially. Notably, the Mn signal nearly disappears in the re‐charged state, indicating the effective de‐intercalation of Mn2+. Specifically, the signals for C, N, and O remain consistent throughout the cycling process, which confirms the superior structural integrity and durability of the organic IDT framework even after rigorous electrochemical reactions. For further verification of Mn insertion, the TEM‐EDX elemental spectra in Figure S9 provide evidence of a small amount of Mn presence within the IDT framework at the fully discharged and re‐charged state.

(a) GCD curve during the discharge process from 0.7 to −0.7 V. TEM‐EDX elemental maps of the electrode in the (b) pristine state and (c) fully discharged state. Ex situ XPS spectra of: (d) C 1s, (e) N 1s, (f) O 1s, and (g) Mn 2p. Electrode at various discharge states within the potential range of 0.7 to −0.7V: (h) FTIR and (i) Raman spectra.

X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was performed to investigate the change of the electronic state within the IDT framework (Figure 3d–g). In the C 1s spectra during the discharge process (Figure 3d), characteristic peaks corresponding to C─C (282.75 eV), C─N (284.25 eV), and C═O (288.4 eV) exhibit distinct shifts in binding energy upon discharging to −0.7 V. Specifically, these changes indicate that the impact of Mn2+ and H+ intercalation is not localized but propagates throughout the entire carbon backbone, facilitated by the extended π‐conjugated structure. Notably, the transition from C═O to C─O─Mn2+/H+ alters the local chemical environment, while efficient electron delocalization ensures that these electronic modifications are reflected across the entire aromatic framework rather than being confined to specific functional groups.

Similarly, in the N 1s region (Figure 3e), the C─N─C signal at 398.05 eV is maintained; however, noticeable shifts in binding energy are observed. These shifts originate from the delocalized redox framework, where electron density dissipates across the aromatic rings rather than being localized on specific oxygen sites during redox reactions. In the O 1s spectra (Figure 3f), the transition of the C═O signal at 529.45 eV and the emergence of a new component at 530.75 eV are attributed to the insertion of Mn2+ and H+, forming C─O─Mn2+/H+ species. This confirms that Mn2+ and H+ successfully coordinate with the carbonyl oxygens of the IDT framework, establishing the reversibility of the molecular system. The robust intensity of the Mn 2p XPS peaks further signifies efficient Mn2+ intercalation (Figure 3g). As described in Figure 3d, the extended π‐conjugated structure enables even the relatively large Mn2+ ions to be accommodated effectively. Notably, the delocalized charge distribution prevents ions from being trapped by intense localized electrostatic forces, facilitating smooth and reversible ion storage. This enhanced intercalation/de‐intercalation kinetics results in the strong signal intensity observed, validating the superior performance of the delocalization‐based redox framework. The electrochemical reversibility of this delocalized system is further confirmed by the ex situ XPS spectra of the fully re‐charged electrode (Figure S10). Upon re‐charging, the C 1s, N 1s, and O 1s spectra almost entirely revert to their pristine profiles, indicating that the electronic states of the IDT framework are successfully restored. Most notably, the Mn 2p signal, which was prominent in the discharged state, nearly vanishes in the re‐charged state. This nearly complete disappearance of the manganese signal provides direct evidence of the effective and reversible de‐intercalation of Mn2+ ions. To supplement these findings, the presence of manganese was further examined through SEM‐EDX elemental mapping and spectra (Figures S11 and S12). Consistent with the XPS results, the SEM‐EDX mapping shows a minimal distribution of manganese across the IDT micro‐rods in the re‐charged state. The corresponding EDX spectrum further confirms the successful extraction of ions from the framework, validating the overall reversibility of the electrode. Such high reversibility is attributed to the delocalized charge distribution, which prevents ions from being trapped and allows for a smooth transition back to the initial state. The slight residual intensities observed in the re‐charged spectra are likely due to minor side reactions, which do not significantly impact the overall structural robustness of the IDT‐based redox system.

The reversible structural and chemical evolution of the IDT electrode is depicted in Figure 3h,i. Upon reaching the fully discharged state, the ex situ FTIR spectra (Figure 3h) exhibit broad and intense absorption bands in the ranges of 3468–3264 cm−1 and 3171–3024 cm−1. These signals, which are absent in the pristine state, are attributed to the O─H and N─H stretching vibrations induced by the co‐intercalation of hydrated H+ and Mn2+ ions from the aqueous electrolyte. Simultaneously, the characteristic C═O stretching modes of the IDT backbone (1591–1543 cm−1) are significantly attenuated, while a new C─O stretching peak emerges at 1283 cm−1. This transition signifies the reduction of carbonyl sites and subsequent ion intercalation, which is stabilized by the delocalized π‐conjugated structure. As shown in Figure S13, the near‐complete restoration of these spectral features to their pristine‐like states upon recharging demonstrates the exceptional electrochemical reversibility and structural robustness of the IDT electrode. The reversible structural and chemical evolution of the IDT framework was further scrutinized via ex situ Raman spectroscopy (Figure 3i). At the pristine state (0.7 V), the Raman spectrum exhibits characteristic vibrational modes corresponding to the aromatic C═C stretching (1509.1–1608.3 cm−1) and C─N stretching (1228.3–1435.7 cm−1), alongside signals originating from the intrinsic N─H groups (3082.9–3293.3 cm−1). Upon discharging to −0.7 V, the spectrum undergoes substantial modifications, most notably the emergence of a new C─O stretching peak at 1230.5cm−1. Simultaneously, the N─H band displays a marked increase in both intensity and width, signifying the successful co‐intercalation of H+ and Mn2+ ions into the redox‐active sites. The skeletal vibrations of C═C and C─N also show discernible intensity reductions and subtle positional shifts. These alterations are attributed to the redistribution of electron density across the delocalized π‐conjugated framework, which facilitates molecular stabilization during charge storage. As supporting evidence, the re‐charged state shown in Figure S14 reveals that these spectral features almost completely revert to their original profiles. This near‐complete restoration indicates the effective extraction of extra protons and the successful preservation of the intrinsic N─H backbone, thereby validating the excellent electrochemical reversibility and structural robustness of the IDT‐based redox system.

Ex Situ Crystallographic Analysis and Quantitative Unit Cell Change of the IDT Framework

To investigate the structural evolution of the IDT framework during the discharge process, ex situ XRD analysis was performed (Figure 4a). As the potential decreases from 0.7 to −0.7 V, the diffraction peaks corresponding to the (001) and (20‐1) planes exhibit a continuous and gradual shift toward higher 2ϴ. This systematic shift indicates that the framework accommodates the incoming Mn2+ and H+ ions without undergoing any discrete phase transitions. Notably, the maintenance of sharp and well‐defined diffraction peaks throughout the discharge process signifies the well‐preserved long‐range crystalline order of the IDT backbone. This structural robustness confirms that the organic framework can effectively mitigate the internal strain induced by ion intercalation, providing a solid foundation for its exceptional cycling stability.

(a) Ex situ XRD patterns and the corresponding potential profile. (b) Evolution of unit cell parameters (a, b, and c) as a function of potential. (c,d) Schematic illustrations of the crystal structure showing the anisotropic lattice expansion and contraction in the a‐c and a‐b planes.

The quantitative evolution of the unit cell parameters (a, b, and c) as a function of the discharge potential is illustrated in Figure 4b. Upon the intercalation of Mn2+ and H+ ions, the IDT framework undergoes a distinct anisotropic lattice change. Specifically, the b‐axis, which represents the stacking direction of the IDT molecules, expands by 2.09% (from 3.872 to 3.953Å). This expansion is primarily attributed to the successful occupancy of guest ions within Site C, identified as the interstitial channels between the molecular stacks in Figure 1b. Such interlayer expansion confirms the effective accommodation of ions, while the robust π‐π stacking ensures that the overall structural integrity is maintained. In contrast, the a and c‐axes exhibit minor contractions of 0.26% and 0.28%, respectively. These findings are in excellent agreement with the subtle shift of the (001) and (20‐1) diffraction peaks toward higher 2ϴ observed in Figure 4a. As visualized in the structural models (Figure 4c,d), the simultaneous expansion and contraction along different crystallographic directions result in a negligible overall volume change. This characteristic low‐strain behavior effectively mitigates mechanical degradation and preserves the structural integrity of the IDT electrode, underpinning its exceptional durability and superior long‐term cycling stability.

Electrochemical Performance of the Mn‐IDT Full‐Cell

Figure 5 presents the electrochemical performance of aqueous full cells employing a Mn metal anode and an IDT cathode. To evaluate the electrochemical behavior of the Mn‐based cell, CV measurements were conducted at a scan rate of 0.2 mV/s. As shown in Figure 5a, well‐defined and pronounced redox peaks are clearly observed, indicating highly reversible electrochemical reactions based on a Mn2+/H+ dual‐ion insertion mechanism. The rate capability of the Mn‐based cell was evaluated by increasing the current density from 0.1 to 0.3 A/g and subsequently returning it to 0.1 A/g (Figure 5b). The cell delivered a maximum discharge capacity of 195.3 mAh/g at 0.1 A/g and maintained stable capacities even as the current density increased. Notably, when the current density was restored to 0.1 A/g, a discharge capacity of 194.47 mAh/g was recovered, which is nearly identical to the initial value. This behavior demonstrates the excellent structural durability and high reversibility of the IDT‐based framework. Additionally, the GCD profiles recorded at various current densities (Figure 5c) show discharge capacities of 157.5, 141.6, and 131.2 mAh/g at 0.15, 0.2, and 0.3 A/g, respectively. Despite the increase in current density, the voltage plateaus remain well preserved. This demonstrates efficient charge‐transfer kinetics and indicates that polarization effects are effectively suppressed, confirming the robust ion‐diffusion pathways within the IDT framework. This result confirms the fast ion diffusion kinetics and robust structural integrity of the IDT electrode.

(a) CV passivation curves at 0.2 mV/s in the Mn‐IDT full‐cell system. (b) Rate capability at various current densities from 0.1 to 0.3 A/g. (c) GCD profiles at different current densities. (d) Long‐term cycling performance at 0.3 A/g over 3000 cycles. (e) EIS spectra of the electrode in the pristine and cycled states.

The long‐term cycling stability of the Mn‐based cell was evaluated at 0.3 A/g for 3000 cycles (Figure 5d). The discharge capacity decreased from an initial value of 131.8 to 112.8 mAh/g after 3000 cycles, corresponding to a high capacity retention of 85.5%. Compared to the previous experiments employing a capacitive counter electrode (Figure 2), a higher capacity retention was observed. This improvement is attributed to the different charge voltage cutoff, indicating that the charging voltage has a significant impact on cycling stability. Moreover, the average Coulombic efficiency remained close to 100% throughout the cycling process, suggesting highly durable and reversible redox reactions enabled by the extended π‐conjugated aromatic structure of the IDT molecule. The GCD profiles of the full cell using a Mn metal anode and an IDT cathode are shown in Figure S15. To further investigate the origin of this stability, EIS measurements were performed on the electrodes in the pristine state and after long‐term cycling (Figure 5e). The internal resistance (Rir) exhibited only a slight increase from 2.16 to 2.68 Ω after cycling, whereas the charge‐transfer resistance (Rct) increased significantly from 76.3 to 350.9 Ω, corresponding to an approximately 4.6‐fold increase. As previously discussed in Figure 2b, the proton co‐intercalation process leads to a localized increase in pH due to the consumption of H+ ions and the subsequent accumulation of residual OH‐groups. This induced alkaline microenvironment triggers the precipitation of irreversible Mn(OH)2 byproducts not only on the cathode but also at the Mn metal anode interface. Consequently, the formation of these side products significantly contributes to the large resistance increase observed after cycling in Figure 5e. Furthermore, repeated ion insertion and extraction processes, along with long‐term contact between the organic electrode surface and the electrolyte, may have led to the formation of a thin interfacial layer that physically hinders charge transfer.

Nevertheless, considering the extensive cycling duration of 3000 cycles, the overall increase in resistance can be regarded as effectively suppressed. These results demonstrate the excellent structural robustness and electrochemical stability of the IDT cathode in the Mn‐based cell. Since hydrogen evolution reactions can induce interfacial instability and increased internal resistance, further investigations into interfacial stabilization strategies for the Mn metal anode, including HER suppression and surface passivation mechanisms, remain important challenges for future studies [26].

Conclusions

This study provides the first experimental demonstration that indanthrone can serve as a high‐performance organic cathode material for Mn2+/H+ hybrid aqueous batteries. The IDT electrode delivers a high specific capacity of 194.8mAh/g at 0.1A/g and exhibits remarkable durability, retaining 85.5% (112.8mAh/g) after 3000 cycles in a Mn‐IDT full‐cell at 0.3A/g. These results clearly confirm the exceptional structural robustness of the IDT framework under prolonged electrochemical operation.

The favorable electrochemical performance originates primarily from the extended π‐conjugated redox framework, which promotes efficient electron delocalization and prevents localized electrostatic trapping of ions. Combined spectroscopic and crystallographic analyses elucidate a highly reversible Mn2+/H+ dual‐ion insertion mechanism. Notably, the IDT framework accommodates ion insertion through anisotropic lattice changes, characterized by expansion along the b‐axis (2.09%), accompanied by minor contractions along the a and c‐axes. This cooperative lattice response effectively minimizes the overall volume change, giving rise to a low‐strain pathway that underpins the long‐term cycling stability.

Despite an increase in charge‐transfer resistance (Rct) associated with the formation of an interfacial Mn(OH)2 layer and minor HER activity, the fundamental molecular backbone of IDT remains structurally intact. The Mn‐IDT full‐cell further exhibits a high operating voltage and superior rate performance, confirming the practical viability of this system. Overall, IDT emerges as an intrinsically durable and efficient redox framework for Mn‐ion storage. Future studies should focus on practical cell configurations, including high‐mass‐loading electrodes and pouch‐cell systems, to further evaluate the commercial viability of the proposed manganese battery system. Future efforts focusing on interface engineering and the suppression of parasitic HER are expected to further enhance the performance and fully realize the potential of high‐voltage aqueous Mn‐ion energy storage systems [27].
