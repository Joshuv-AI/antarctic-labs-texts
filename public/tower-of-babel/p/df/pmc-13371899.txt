Single-cell transcriptomic unveils tumor-mediated reprogramming of neutrophils and their unique vulnerability that inhibits metastasis
Authors: William Putzbach, Maria P. Zappia, Ahmed Magdy, Jing Li, Veronique Nogueira, Kevin Lou, Kevan M. Shokat, Abul B. M. M. K. Islam, Maxim Frolov, Nissim Hay
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Neutrophils are protumorigenic and prometastatic in tumor-bearing mice. Comprehensive single-cell RNA sequencing analyses on various tissues of naïve and tumor-bearing MMTV-PyMT (mouse mammary tumor virus–polyoma middle T antigen) mice unraveled a neutrophil reprogramming process and uncovered a strategy to selectively target reprogrammed neutrophils. Transcriptional reprogramming of neutrophils in tumor-bearing mice is initiated in the bone marrow (BM) by granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF). Among the seven neutrophil clusters in the BM, the abundance of one cluster is markedly increased whereas another cluster is diminished in tumor-bearing mice. This reprogramming endures across tissue types including blood, tumor microenvironment, and the metastatic niche in the lungs. Thus, in all tissues tested, neutrophils derived from tumor-bearing mice are transcriptionally distinct from those derived from naïve mice. Among the genes that are markedly induced are members of the Ifitm family. We leverage this facet to selectively target reprogrammed neutrophils through treatment with Rapalink-1—an mTOR (mechanistic target of rapamycin) inhibitor—whose intake is greatly enhanced by IFITM proteins. Reprogrammed neutrophils selectively uptake Rapalink-1, which selectively eradicate them in vivo and normalized the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio in tumor-bearing mice without affecting naïve neutrophils. Last, Rapalink-1 inhibited metastasis of a Rapalink-1–resistant tumor. Thus, Rapalink-1 not only could affect the primary tumor but also selectively eliminates tumor-reprogrammed neutrophils to diminish metastasis.

INTRODUCTION

Neutrophils are the most numerous of the granulocyte lineages in the mammalian immune system and constitute the first line of defense during the innate host response. They combat pathogens through the release of hydrolytic granules, production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), and release of chromatin through NETosis (1–4).

In the bone marrow (BM), neutrophils undergo a tightly orchestrated series of transcriptional events that drive their maturation even under basal conditions. However, in response to certain pathological conditions, such as tumorigenesis or systemically disseminated infection, this process expands considerably through so-called emergency granulopoiesis (5–8). Neutrophils can exhibit considerable transcriptional plasticity driven by signals in the local environment (9). With the advent of single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), the number of discrete transcriptional states found for neutrophils has grown considerably (10–14).

It was reported that neutrophils can take on an antitumor N1 or protumor N2 subtype, and numerous studies have shown tumor burden shifts the balance toward the latter (15). This reprogramming is aptly demonstrated in clinical breast cancer cases through the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), with a higher value correlating with a worse prognosis (16). It was established that neutrophils can promote metastasis in mouse models (17–19). Neutrophils support metastasis by a variety of mechanisms including physically escorting metastasis-initiating cells (20), releasing growth-promoting metabolites and signaling molecules (17, 20), and releasing chromatin-derived neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) that adhere circulating tumor cells to the premetastatic niche to seed metastasis (21). In addition, ample evidence supports the existence and prometastatic role of so-called myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which are a distinct subgroup of neutrophils that inhibit antitumor T cells (22, 23).

Comprehensive understanding of neutrophil reprogramming during tumorigenesis is an active area of research. Although there are clinical trials to generally target neutrophils in patients with cancer (24, 25), scientists and clinicians are more interested in whether tumor-reprogrammed neutrophils can be selectively targeted. An ideal regimen would also spar canonical, unreprogrammed neutrophils so they can continue functioning in the innate immune system. In this work, we conducted scRNA-seq experiments on various tissues of naïve and tumor-bearing mouse mammary tumor virus–polyoma middle T antigen (MMTV-PyMT; or just PyMT) mice (26) and find that granulocyte colony-stimulating factor (G-CSF) drives a stable transcriptional shift in mature neutrophils that endures across tissues types and is enriched in the primary tumor and premetastatic niche in the lungs. Although reprogramming in the BM is sustained in other tissues, an additional level of transcriptional reprogramming occurs exclusively in neutrophils from the tumor microenvironment (TME) and lungs. Among the genes that are markedly induced in the reprogrammed neutrophils are members of the Ifitm family. Tumor-reprogrammed neutrophils have the highest expression of Ifitm genes compared to any other cell type in tumor-bearing mice. We leverage this facet to selectively target these reprogrammed neutrophils to show they promote breast cancer metastasis to the lungs. We accomplished this through treatment with Rapalink-1—a bivalent third-generation mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) inhibitor—whose intake is greatly enhanced by IFITM proteins, which act as noncanonical carriers of linked chemotypes (27). Rapalink-1 selectively targets neutrophils in MMTV-PyMT mice and not in naïve mice and thus reverses the NLR. Consequently, Rapalink-1 inhibits metastasis independently of its effect on the primary tumor.

RESULTS

Breast tumor reprograms neutrophils in the BM

We performed scRNA-seq on single-cell suspensions isolated from the BM of tumor endpoint (2 cm in total tumor diameter) MMTV-PyMT mice, in which human breast cancer is faithfully recapitulated (26), and age-matched naïve mice. No prior antibody or gradient-mediated neutrophil enrichment step was performed because this would risk excluding certain neutrophil subtypes that do not meet the criteria for the selection method used. Instead, we identified neutrophils and other cell types in silico using a panel of published scRNA-seq biomarkers (see Materials and Methods) specific for immune cells found in the BM (28) (fig. S1 and table S1). Seurat’s integration pipeline was then used to produce uniform manifold approximation and projections (UMAPs) (29), which, broadly speaking, depict the transcription profile of each cell in two-dimensional space such that cells with similar transcriptomes are closer in proximity than are cells with unrelated profiles. Doing this on the “isolated” BM neutrophil lineage [from pre–granulocyte-monocyte progenitor (GMP) to mature neutrophils] revealed the existence of seven transcriptional clusters labeled N0 to N5_(N and P) (Fig. 1A and table S2) in order of increasing maturity (fig. S2) as defined by well-established neutrophil differentiation markers (11). Because naïve and MMTV-PyMT mice-derived BM cells were collected in replicate pairs (each pair consisting of a naïve and age-matched MMTV-PyMT mouse), we could assess transcriptional differences between the two genotypes with a high level of confidence. When we grouped the UMAP according to genotype, we clearly see BM neutrophils’ transcription profiles are shifted between those derived from naïve versus MMTV-PyMT mice; this shift begins in N3, or the immature neutrophil stage, and becomes more obvious in N4 (Fig. 1, A and B). Fully mature neutrophils are the most transcriptionally distinct—so much so that they form two separate clusters, N5_N and N5_P in Naïve and PyMT mice, respectively (Fig. 1, B and C). Consistently, this tumor-induced transcriptional shift occurs only in the neutrophil clusters expressing G1 cell cycle phase genes (N3 to N5), which represent nondividing and committed neutrophil-fated cells; in contrast, the N0, N1, and N2 clusters express genes associated with G2, S, or M (mitosis) phases of the cell cycle, which is characteristic of noncommitted and/or dividing cells, and represent GMPs (and possibly pre-GMP), proNeutrophils, and preNeutrophils, respectively (Fig. 1D). Quantifying cell frequency in each neutrophil cluster demonstrated a marked increase in the N5_P cluster with almost a complete diminishment of the N5_N cluster in MMTV-PyMT mice versus naïve mice (Fig. 1E). Thus, mature naïve neutrophils can be characterized by the N5_N cluster whereas tumor-reprogrammed neutrophils by the N5_P cluster. Biomarker identification using the FindAllMarkers function in Seurat revealed the distinguishing mRNAs for each N cluster (Fig. 1F and table S3). Among the distinguishing biomarkers for the tumor-specific N5_P cluster are (i) Ifitm members, which are involved in cell autonomous antiviral immunity and trans-cell migration (30–33) (discussed below); (ii) the murine-specific Wfdc17, whose function has yet to be demonstrated but is predicted to act as a serine protease inhibitor (34); (iii) Igfbp6, or insulin-like growth factor binding protein 6, which has a role in activating various neutrophil functions including oxidative burst, migration, and degranulation (35); (iv) Prok2, which is a well-known proangiogenic factor produced by neutrophils that can aid in metastasis (36, 37); (v) Il-1β, an important proinflammatory cytokine that also plays crucial roles during tumorigenesis and metastasis (38); and (vi) JunB, a transcription factor (TF) that is instrumental to neutrophil differentiation, survival, and functionality (39, 40). N5_N is characterized by relatively high expression of (i) the rodent-specific Ccl6, a chemoattractant for monocytes, macrophages, and T cells that has several homologs in human including CCL9, CCL18, and CCL14 (41), and (ii) Sell or L-selectin, whose expression in neutrophils is associated with potent antitumor activity and favorable response to immunotherapies (42). Although N5_N and N5_P displayed the greatest extent of gene expression differences, we also observed transcriptional changes begin earlier in the N3 and N4 stages too (Fig. 1, A and B). Therefore, we pseudobulked the mRNA counts from all the N clusters for each sample and performed differential expression analysis between naïve and MMTV-PyMT–derived neutrophils (Fig. 2A and table S4). MMTV-PyMT–derived neutrophils exhibit drastic up-regulation of certain genes in addition to the genes indicated above including (i) members of the Ifitm family (Ifitm1, Ifitm2, Ifitm3, and Ifitm6); (ii) Tspo, which is a mitochondrial outer membrane protein involved in cholesterol transport (43, 44), metabolism (45), and neutrophil migration (46); and (iii) Upp1, or uridine phosphorylase-1, whose activity in neutrophils was recently shown to promote premetastatic niche formation and inhibit antitumor T cells (47). In addition, there are several genes that are transcriptionally suppressed. These genes were then compiled into a tumor-induced reprogramming gene expression signature in neutrophils (Fig. 2B). The validity of some of these differentially expressed genes was verified by quantitative real-time polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR) (Fig. 2C) and Western blot (Fig. 2D). We also show that similar tumor-induced changes occur in the BM of mice orthotopically transplanted with E0771 cells (fig. S3, A and B), including notable enrichment of the N5_P and diminishment of N5_N clusters in tumor-bearing mice (fig. S3C) and notably similar gene expression changes compared to the MMTV-PyMT mice (fig. S3, D and E, and table S5). This suggests that the tumor-reprogramming we observed in the MMTV-PyMT mice is extendable to other models of breast cancer.

Gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) of the genes differentially expressed between naïve and MMTV-PyMT mice (table S4) revealed enrichment/depletion of Hallmark gene sets including interferon signaling, IL-2-Stat5 and IL-6-Jak-Stat3 signaling, hypoxia, and mTORC1 signaling, among others (fig. S4 and table S6). These gene sets play important roles in neutrophils’ differentiation and function: (i) Interferon signaling modulates neutrophil expansion, function, and the N1/N2 transition (48–50). (ii) Stat5 and Stat3 function antagonistically during neutrophil differentiation in the BM with the former promoting neutrophil depletion/monocyte expansion and the latter augmenting neutrophil expansion and driving expression of various protumorigenic factors (51–53). (iii) Hypoxic conditions drive protumorigenic activity in neutrophils and MDSC transition in tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs) (54, 55). (iv) mTORC1 signaling is necessary for many neutrophil functions including migration, activation, and formation of NETs (56, 57).

We then used univariate linear model (ULM) analysis to infer the activity of TFs known to regulate neutrophil differentiation at different stages. To find TFs that could potentially regulate these tumor-transcriptionally reprogrammed genes, we looked for enrichment/depletion of curated TFs’ target genes [from the CollecTri database (58)] among the differentially expressed genes, thereby inferring the activities of these TFs. We performed the ULM TF analysis on individual cells, and the top 40 TFs that exhibited the highest differential average activity between naïve and PyMT BM neutrophils are displayed in the heatmap (Fig. 2E and table S7). Among the elevated TF activities in MMTV-PyMT neutrophils are Dlx1, Sin3A, and Stat3. Whereas the function of Dlx1 in neutrophils has not been described previously, Stat3 was reported to support a protumorigenic phenotype in neutrophils through gene expression changes (52, 53). Tspo and Prok2 are both direct targets of Stat3 according to the CollecTri database. Sin3a was previously reported to regulate signal transducers and activators of transcription (STATs) and, thereby, regulate expression of Ifitm1 to Ifitm3 (59). Among the TFs with suppressed activity in MMTV-PyMT neutrophils are Runx1, Klf4, and Foxo3—all of which have previously described function in neutrophils (60–62).

We also performed a Monocle analysis to infer how neutrophils from naïve versus PyMT mice differentiate through time and find any branch points where naïve and PyMT-derived neutrophils depart from their common course and follow separate trajectories. This analysis yielded an inferred pseudotimeline beginning with the youngest N0 progenitors and ending with the most mature N5_N and N5_P neutrophils (fig. S5, A to C). There is one main branch point, corresponding to when naïve and PyMT-derived neutrophils begin to follow separate trajectories with distinct expression profiles (fig. S5, A and B). We see that this branch point mostly overlaps with the immature neutrophil N3 cluster (fig. S5C). We next had Monocle define discrete gene expression modules that are present beginning at and following the branch point in clusters N3 to N5_N/P (fig. S5A), each of whose constituent genes’ mRNA expression changes occur simultaneously along Monocle’s inferred trajectory; these concurrent expression changes are indicative of genes that are part of a common gene regulatory network. We found numerous modules that were exclusive to either the N5_P or N5_N clusters (fig. S5D). Closer inspection revealed that genes within these modules do exhibit differential expression between the N5_N versus N5_P clusters similar to that found between the pooled neutrophils derived from the naïve versus PyMT mice (Fig. 2F and table S8). Enrichment analysis of the genes included in these modules revealed enrichment in Hallmark gene ontology (GO) terms, some of which overlap with those described in fig. S4, including interferon signaling, glycolysis and apoptosis (fig. S5E). Besides transcriptional reprogramming, we also observed a phenotypic change of neutrophils in MMTV-PyMT mice as both spontaneous and chemokine-induced motility are greatly increased in neutrophils from MMTV-PyMT mice (Fig. 2G). Together, the results showed a clear transcriptomic reprogramming of BM neutrophils, mediated by multiple TFs, in the tumor-bearing mice.

Neutrophil reprogramming in the BM is driven primarily by distal G-CSF in tumor-bearing mice

Our next step was to uncover the tumor-induced signals responsible for driving the neutrophil reprogramming in the BM. These alterations occurred with virtually no infiltration of MMTV-PyMT–expressing cancer cells into the BM (fig. S6). This implicated distal signaling through a circulating cytokine released by the tumor, either directly or indirectly. Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) of the blood plasma showed that G-CSF is the most robustly induced cytokine in the plasma of MMTV-PyMT mice (Fig. 3A). It is also noteworthy that IL-6 was also elevated, which is likely connected to the enrichment of the IL-6 JAK-STAT3 GO gene set in PyMT-derived neutrophils we identified in fig. S4. Cxcl1 and Mip1 are known chemoattractants for neutrophils and monocytes and were also up-regulated. Regardless, G-CSF is still, by far, the most elevated cytokine. G-CSF was also previously implicated in generating protumorigenic neutrophils in the BM (8). However, it is not known how G-CSF reprograms neutrophils at the single-cell level. Therefore, we probed whether G-CSF has the primary role in reprogramming BM neutrophils in tumor-bearing mice. To accomplish this, we conducted a series of scRNA-seq experiments on the BM of MMTV-PyMT mice injected with either control isotype immunoglobulin G (IgG) or a neutralizing anti-G-CSF antibody and naïve mice injected with either vehicle or recombinant murine G-CSF. We generated a UMAP and identified all the BM cell types using expression of canonical mRNA markers (fig. S7 and table S9). We then selected for BM neutrophils in silico as before and applied Seurat’s label transfer pipeline (63) to label cells based on their similarity to the transcription profiles identified for the N clusters in Fig. 1, which produced the UMAP shown in Fig. 3B. When we split the UMAP according to genotype/treatment groups, we saw notable similarities between (i) the naïve vehicle-injected mice and MMTV-PyMT anti-G-CSF–injected mice and (ii) between the naïve G-CSF–injected mice and MMTV-PyMT IgG-injected mice (Fig. 3C). This strongly suggested that G-CSF is (i) necessary for tumor-induced reprogramming to occur in BM neutrophils and (ii) is sufficient to drive these alterations even when the tumor is absent. Analysis of the cell frequencies in each cluster of the BM neutrophils revealed the same features observed in Fig. 1E. Namely, naïve mice injected with G-CSF and MMTV-PyMT mice both displayed almost exclusive expression of the N5_P cluster, whereas naïve mice injected with vehicle and PyMT mice injected with anti-G-CSF antibody predominantly expressed the N5_N cluster (Fig. 3D). Differential gene expression analysis showed that most of the mRNAs up-regulated or down-regulated in BM neutrophils from MMTV-PyMT mice were similarly up-regulated or down-regulated in naïve mice after G-CSF administration (Fig. 3E and table S10); this was also true at the protein level (Fig. 3E, inset). Reciprocally, we found the opposite when we injected MMTV-PyMT mice with the anti-G-CSF antibody (Fig. 3F and table S11)—namely, the up-regulated and down-regulated genes induced in PyMT IgG-injected control mice were mostly down-regulated or up-regulated, respectively, in MMTV-PyMT mice injected with the antibody. When we generated an expression heatmap of the top 70 up-regulated/down-regulated signature genes from Fig. 2B, we find that mature neutrophils from untreated naïve and anti-G-CSF–injected MMTV-PyMT mice exhibit the same expression profile, whereas the IgG-injected MMTV-PyMT and G-CSF–injected naïve mice share far more transcriptional similarity (Fig. 3G). Together, these results demonstrate that G-CSF, likely produced by distal tumor cells (not excluding indirectly through activated nontumor cells), is the predominant cytokine that reprograms neutrophils in the BM of tumor-bearing mice and engages neutrophils directly and not through any cellular intermediate.

Tumor-induced reprogramming is preserved in neutrophils outside the BM

Thus far, all our data are derived from BM neutrophils, but it is unknown whether this reprogramming would persist in neutrophils once they enter other tissues. Maintaining these gene expression alterations would be expected if these reprogrammed neutrophils directly support metastatic cells and/or promote premetastatic niche formation. Therefore, we conducted further scRNA-seq experiments on single-cell suspensions isolated from numerous tissue types in naïve and MMTV-PyMT mice in addition to the BM, including the blood, lungs, and primary tumor. As before, we classified all cell types in these tissues (64–66) using canonical cell type mRNA markers (fig. S8, A to C, and table S12). We then isolated the neutrophils in silico, and then we used the same integration and label transfer pipelines in Seurat to generate a UMAP for all in silico–derived neutrophils derived from the blood, lungs, and primary tumor (fig. S9A). As expected, the progenitor N0 to N2 clusters are largely diminished because these extra-BM tissues do not produce neutrophils de novo. When we group the UMAP by genotype, we see the same drastic cluster shift between the naïve and MMTV-PyMT mice (fig. S9B). When we separated the UMAP for neutrophils according to each tissue/genotype combination, we observed that most prominent cluster in all tissues tested in PyMT mice is N5_P, whereas in naïve mice, the most prominent cluster is N5_N (Fig. 4, A and B). This clearly distinguishes naïve neutrophils from tumor-reprogrammed neutrophils in all tissues tested. We also see that TANs are mostly composed of the tumor-reprogrammed N5_P cluster, consistent with these reprogrammed neutrophils being mobilized to the tumor to play a protumorigenic role within that microenvironment. Differential gene expression analysis showed, quite convincingly, that the tumor-induced signature genes found in the BM are, likewise, differentially expressed in the neutrophils from the blood and lungs tissues (Fig. 4, C and D, and tables S13 and S14). We also observed similar reprogramming in the spleen, with the N5_P cluster being much more prevalent than the N5_N cluster in MMTV-PyMT mice (fig. S10A) and tumor-induced transcriptional reprogramming occurring in neutrophils derived from tumor-bearing mice (fig. S10B and table S15). As expected, this reprogramming closely mirrors the gene expression changes that occur in BM neutrophils from MMTV-PyMT mice (fig. S10C).

Thus far, in the clustering of naïve and reprogrammed neutrophils, we have relied on integration to analyze our data. During this process, we split the samples into groups—each composed of a naïve and MMTV-PyMT sample pair that was gathered at the same time from the same tissue (see Materials and Methods). This approach allows us to find anchors and integrate neutrophils between groups; this coerces neutrophils from different groups to cluster together if they are transcriptionally similar, which allows us to combine neutrophils from the same neutrophil stage, even if they have divergent transcription profiles because of differences in tissue origin or sample processing. Because no anchoring is done within each group, the gene expression differences between the paired naïve and MMTV-PyMT samples are preserved. Although this approach provides the advantage of negating unintended gene expression changes that result from disparate sample handling techniques for different tissues when generating the UMAP, it may also obfuscate biologically relevant transcriptional changes that genuinely occur due to signals from the tissue itself. Therefore, to determine changes in gene expression among the different tissues we reanalyzed our neutrophil datasets using integration but, this time, splitting the datasets according to genotype. This means all naïve neutrophils are in one group and are anchored and integrated to the MMTV-PyMT neutrophils, which are in the other group; this means neutrophils from different tissues within each genotype group are not anchored. This approach allows gene expression changes between neutrophils from different tissues to be highlighted in the resulting UMAPs (Fig. 4E). From this UMAP, we see that neutrophils from the BM and blood share transcriptional similarity, which would be expected because these neutrophils have just left the BM and entered the blood and not had any exposure to any resident tissue signaling molecules. We see that the lung neutrophils and TANs are clustered together and are distinct from neutrophils in the BM and blood. As before, we also observe a transcriptional shift in neutrophils between naïve and MMTV-PyMT mice within each tissue (Fig. 4F), which becomes more obvious when the UMAPs are split according to both genotype and tissue residency (fig. S9C). An expression heatmap of the top 70 tumor-induced signature genes (derived from Fig. 2B) shows that neutrophils from the BM, blood, and lungs all undergo similar reprogramming in PyMT mice (Fig. 4G).

According to the UMAPs depicted in Fig. 4E, lung neutrophils and TANs share a high degree of transcriptional similarity because they cluster together. Furthermore, we found that previously defined mRNA biomarkers that are specifically enriched in neutrophils from certain tissues (9) are appropriately expressed in neutrophils from matching tissue origins in our datasets (fig. S11, A to C). However, TANs expressed enrichment of biomarkers attributed to lung neutrophils (fig. S11C). Together, these results could support the notion that lung neutrophils in tumor-bearing mice are derived from the TANs that migrate to the lungs as a cluster of cells together with circulating tumor cells (20).

To further investigate the shared and distinct transcriptional states of lung neutrophils and TANs, we first isolated neutrophils only from the N5_P cluster derived from PyMT mice; we did this so we can compare neutrophils from the same genotype and level of maturity and focus only on changes induced by tissue residency. We next performed differential gene expression analysis on pseudobulked neutrophils from each of the tissues (fig. S11D and table S16). We observed four distinct subclusters in the heatmap dendrogram within the N5_P cluster, with each subcluster being uniquely expressed in neutrophils from a specific tissue. Subcluster #1 is specific for TANs and, to a lesser extent, lung neutrophils demonstrating the similarity between TANs and lung neutrophils in MMTV-PyMT mice. Subcluster #2 is unique to lung neutrophils, subcluster #3 is unique to blood neutrophils, and subcluster #4 is unique to BM neutrophils (fig. S11D). We then isolated both N5_N and N5_P from naïve and PyMT mice and performed the ULM TF enrichment analysis to compare/contrast TF activities among different tissues and genotypes (fig. S11E and table S17). As expected, we see many common TFs between TANs and lung neutrophils. Among the TFs that display differential activity in naïve versus MMTV-PyMT neutrophils is Runx1, which showed reduced activity in BM neutrophils of MMTV-PyMT mice (Fig. 2E) and showed reduced activity in blood and lung neutrophils and TANs of MMTV-PyMT mice.

Together, these data show that distal tumor reprogramming (likely through G-CSF) begins in the BM and is maintained in neutrophils across all tissues tested. Once neutrophils enter the TME, they acquire an additional layer of tumor proximal reprogramming that shares many features in common with lung neutrophils in MMTV-PyMT mice. The transcriptional similarity between TANs and lungs’ neutrophils raised the possibility that either (i) neutrophils are regulated via similar signals common to both environments or (ii) the lung neutrophils in tumor-bearing mice are derived from TANs, which is consistent with a previous report showing that TANs escort tumor cells to the metastatic site (20). Last, our results have postulated several TFs that may form the basis of this reprogramming.

Targeting tumor-reprogrammed neutrophils and metastasis with Rapalink-1

The tumor-reprogrammed neutrophils are clearly distinguished from naïve neutrophils in all tissues tested (Fig. 4, A and B), raising the possibility of selectively targeting them. A common feature of the tumor-reprogrammed neutrophils is elevation of IFITM family members in neutrophils from all tissues in MMTV-PyMT mice, as validated by Western blot (Fig. 5A). We also found that IFITM expression is highest in mature CD101+ compared to immature CD101− neutrophils (fig. S12A), consistent with our scRNA-seq analysis. In addition, scRNA-seq analysis of whole blood and lung samples showed that neutrophils from MMTV-PyMT mice express the highest level of Ifitm mRNA compared to other cell types (fig. S12, B and C). Recently, it was shown that IFITM proteins facilitate the transport of chemically linked drugs (linked chemotypes) (27). This raised the possibility that neutrophils of tumor-bearing mice could be more susceptible to chemically linked drugs such as Rapalink-1, in which the mTORC1 inhibitor rapamycin is linked to the mTOR kinase inhibitor sapanisertib (67). To further assess this possibility, we first used the fluorescent analog of Rapalink-1, RapaTAMRA-PEG8, which is created by the adenosine triphosphate (ATP) site binding element being replaced with tetramethylrhodamine (TAMRA) (27). We observed that the uptake of RapaTAMRA-PEG8 is much higher in blood neutrophils derived from MMTV-PyMT mice than those from naïve mice (Fig. 5B). Furthermore, we showed that BM neutrophils isolated from MMTV-PyMT mice are more sensitive to ex vivo Rapalink-1 treatment as evidenced by their increased sensitivity to mTORC1 inhibition (Fig. 5C). mTORC1 activity was measured by determining the ratio between phosphorylated S6 (pS6) and total S6 normalized to the vehicle control samples. Notably, for unknown reason, the level of total S6 is much higher in neutrophils derived from MMTV-PyMT mice when compared to neutrophils derived from naïve mice.

Injection of Rapalink-1 in vivo led to a drastic decrease in Ly6GHIGH neutrophils from the blood and lungs in MMTV-PyMT but not as much for naïve mice (Fig. 5D). In addition, Western blot revealed that IFITM1 expression is much lower in blood neutrophils isolated from Rapalink-1–injected versus vehicle-injected PyMT mice (Fig. 5E), indicating that the reprogrammed neutrophils are selectively eliminated. Rapalink-1 treatment also drastically reduced tumor-associated Ly6GHIGH neutrophils, as measured by Ly6G expression (Fig. 5F). The selective effect of Rapalink-1 on the reprogrammed neutrophils was clearly demonstrated by differential blood counts. Blood counts after in vivo Rapalink-1 treatment of naïve and MMTV-PyMT mice showed a marked reduction of neutrophils in the blood of MMTV-PyMT mice but not in naïve mice, as well as a decrease in the NLR (Fig. 5G), which is associated with a more favorable prognosis (16). Rapalink-1 also did not reduce the cell frequency of other blood cells except PyMT neutrophils (fig. S12D). Together, these results strongly suggest that Rapalink-1 selectively eliminates the reprogrammed neutrophils in tumor-bearing mice but not the neutrophils in naïve mice.

Next, we wanted to determine whether the elimination of the reprogrammed neutrophils by Rapalinik-1 could inhibit metastasis independently of its effect on the primary tumor. Therefore, we generated E0771 cells refractory to Rapalink-1 treatment by deleting Fkbp12 (68) (fig. S13, A and B). These cells were orthotopically implanted into female mice, and when tumors reached endpoint, mice were euthanized to quantify metastases. As shown in Fig. 5 (H and I), Rapalink-1 markedly decreased the number of macroscopic and microscopic (fig. S13C) metastases in the lungs, respectively. As expected, immunohistochemical (IHC) staining of lungs with anti-Ly6G, showed a marked decrease in neutrophils in the lungs in Rapalink-1–treated mice (Fig. 5J and fig. S13D). Primary tumors were approaching or were at tumor endpoint when mice were euthanized for analysis. However, we observed a mild inhibition (not statistically significant) of primary tumor growth after Rapalink-1 treatment (fig. S13E). This is consistent with the Fkbp12 knockout (KO) E0771 cells being refractory to Rapalink-1 (fig. S13B) but still being reliant on nontumor cell autonomous effect by neutrophils, which are, themselves, negatively affected by Rapalink-1.

Last, we analyzed human scRNA-seq datasets to see whether IFITM expression is a feature of neutrophils in patients with cancer. We first analyzed a dataset containing premenopausal normal breast and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) tissues (GSE161529) (69, 70). After initial UMAP generation with all the breast/tumor cell types, we selected for neutrophils in silico using various human neutrophil biomarkers (Fig. 6, A and B). After quantifying the mRNA counts in the neutrophil subset, we found that IFITM1, IFITM2, and IFITM3 were all up-regulated in the breast cancer TANs compared to normal breast resident neutrophils. We also saw drastic up-regulation of another signature gene TSPO (Fig. 6C). We then analyzed another breast cancer scRNA-seq dataset produced from the cells in the blood, primary tumor, and adjacent normal breast tissue collected from eight patients with breast cancer (GSE114727) (71). We found several neutrophil clusters (Fig. 6D), with one cluster exhibiting biomarkers associated with MDSCs (Fig. 6E), as previously described by Alshetaiwi et al. (22). We further found that this MDSC cluster also has the highest expression of IFITM mRNAs (Fig. 6F). To extend our findings to other cancer types, we also analyzed a glioblastoma scRNA-seq dataset where primary tumor biopsies were compared to normal brain biopsies taken from patients undergoing anterior temporal lobectomy for seizures (GSE278456) (72). Plotting the distribution of combined IFITM expression (i.e., IFITM1, IFITM2, and IFITM3 together) reveals that glioblastoma TANs harbor a population of neutrophils that express elevated IFITM and lack neutrophils with low IFITM expression compared to normal brain resident neutrophils (Fig. 6G).

Together, these data suggest that the Ifitm expression signature that makes reprogrammed protumorigenic neutrophils susceptible to Rapalink-1 treatment in mice may also be exploited to selectively eliminate prometastatic MDSCs and reduce metastasis in patients with human breast cancer.

DISCUSSION

In recent years, accumulating evidence established that neutrophils in tumor-bearing mice and patients with cancer are protumorigenic and, particularly, prometastatic. They exert their effects through multiple mechanisms including immunosuppression (22), escorting tumor cells to the metastatic site (20), capturing circuiting metastasis-initiating cells through NETosis (73), preparing the premetastatic niche in the lungs (74, 75), and by providing growth factors directly to cancer cells in the TME (17). However, thus far, there are no studies that elucidate, at the single-cell level, how neutrophils in tumor-bearing mice are reprogrammed. The recognition of the role of neutrophils in promoting tumor growth and metastasis led to clinical trials in patients with cancer to target neutrophils with CXCR2 inhibitors and others (24, 25). However, these targeted therapies affect other functions of neutrophils and are not selective for the reprogrammed neutrophils. Whether these reprogrammed neutrophils can be selectively targeted to inhibit metastasis is not known. Here, we uncovered the evolution of neutrophils’ reprogramming in tumor-bearing mice, which begins in the BM and endures in the blood, TME, and the metastatic site. The transcriptional reprograming is principally mediated by G-CSF and a host of TFs, including some previously known to the neutrophil field and others unknown. G-CSF was shown to be required for normal neutrophils’ maturation and mobilization (76), but it appears that, above a certain threshold level, G-CSF induces a transcriptional reprogramming in neutrophils, which is different from that of normal neutrophils. This is of critical importance to the clinic as patients with breast cancer on chemotherapy are often administered recombinant G-CSF to boost their neutrophil numbers (77). Although this combats neutropenia, it may also reprogram neutrophils to become prometastatic.

The diversion of neutrophils clusters begins in the BM by a substantial increase of one cluster of mature neutrophils (N5_P) and diminishment of another (N5_N). The neutrophils in blood and lungs of naïve mice primarily originate from cluster N5_N, whereas the neutrophils in blood, lungs, and TME of tumor-bearing mice are primarily originate from the N5_P cluster. Thus, the neutrophils of tumor-bearing mice are transcriptionally distinct from neutrophils of naïve mice. A second level of reprogramming occurs outside the BM. Specifically, we showed murine TME neutrophils and lung neutrophils in tumor-bearing mice are transcriptionally similar. This similarity could support previously published results showing that TME neutrophils escort cancer cells to the metastatic site (20). However, we cannot completely exclude the possibility that additional reprogramming of TME, and lung neutrophils evolved independently. We find that both TANs and lung neutrophils are regulated by both common and distinct TFs. Future investigations will have to determine their roles and importance in neutrophils and how they contribute to tumorigenesis.

The distinction between naïve and tumor-bearing mice neutrophils could unravel selective vulnerability of the reprogrammed neutrophils. Our studies showed substantially high levels of Ifitm genes and proteins in the reprogrammed neutrophils when compared to naïve neutrophils. IFITMs are small transmembrane proteins, and several distinct functions were attributed to them including antiviral defense (30, 31), and germ cell development (33). As their name suggests, IFITMs are induced by interferons, and numerous groups have shown interferons to modulate neutrophil function during tumorigenesis (49, 78). Our GSEA presented in fig. S4 also suggests that interferon signaling may underlie, at least partially, the reprogramming we observe. However, interferon signaling is known to bias neutrophils toward an inflammatory tumorcidal phenotype, not a prometastatic phenotype as we see with tumor-induced reprogramming. Furthermore, our data show that G-CSF injection alone can up-regulate IFITM in neutrophils (Fig. 3E). This is also supported by a study showing that recombinant G-CSF up-regulates IFITM ex vivo (79). Together, this suggests that G-CSF can partially mimic interferon signaling in neutrophils—at least with respect to IFITM up-regulation—without biasing them toward an antitumor phenotype. Future work will decipher whether G-CSF and interferon signal through a common transcriptional regulator or through completely divergent mechanisms. Recently, it was shown that IFITM proteins serve as conduits that facilitate cell entry of chemically linked large drug molecules such as Rapalink-1, in which the mTORC1 inhibitor rapamycin is linked to the mTOR kinase inhibitor sapanisertib (27, 67). Thus, raising the possibility that Rapalink-1 can selectively target the reprogrammed neutrophils. The uptake of Rapalink-1 by neutrophils from tumor-bearing mice is markedly higher than that by naïve neutrophils. In vivo Rapalink-1 treatment also markedly decreased the number of neutrophils in tumor-bearing mice but not of neutrophils from naïve mice, and it also greatly reduced the NLR in tumor-bearing mice without affecting other lymphocytes. The selective elimination of reprogrammed neutrophils by Rapalink-1 enables the diminishment of metastasis. This effect is independent of the effect of Rapalink-1 on the primary tumor because the inhibition of metastasis occurred with the primary tumor resistant to Rapalink-1.

In addition to Rapalink-1, there are other chemically linked high-molecular-weight molecules, including PROTACs (PROteolysis Targeting Chimera), whose entry into cells is facilitated by IFITM proteins (27), and therefore could be used to selectively target the reprogrammed neutrophils. Our results are likely extendable to patients with human breast cancer as we showed that MDSCs, which are prometastatic, have the highest expression of IFITM and would be selectively targeted by drugs like Rapalink-1.

There are other genes that are markedly induced in the reprogrammed neutrophils, and if it turns out they are required for the reprogrammed neutrophil’s protumorigenic functionality, then they could be targeted as well. For instance, Tspo is markedly elevated at the RNA and protein levels. TSPO is an outer membrane mitochondrial protein responsible for the transport of cholesterol to the mitochondria (43, 44). Germline deletion of Tspo in mice does not exert any adverse consequences (80), and inhibitors of TSPO are available (81). Therefore, if TSPO is required for the functionality of reprogrammed neutrophils, it could be targeted to induce their disfunction.

Last, it should be noted that the neutrophils’ reprogramming we observed in tumor-bearing mice resembles neutrophils’ reprogramming after infection (11) and thus might explain, at least in part, why bacterial infection or lipopolysaccharide (LPS) administration exacerbate metastasis (82, 83).

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

All animal protocols and mouse strains were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the University of Illinois at Chicago (UIC) (animal protocol: ACC2023049), in accordance with the US Animal Welfare Act and the policies of the National Institutes of Health (NIH). MMTV-PyMT FVB/N mice were purchased from the Jackson Laboratory. MMTV-PyMT mice were euthanized at endpoint (12 to 16 weeks old), when the combined diameter of all over mammary tumors reached 2 cm in diameter. Although lung metastasis is a relatively early event, the mice would always reach tumor endpoint before metastasis would become so burdensome that respiration was affected, which would otherwise necessitate euthanasia. Twelve- to 16-week-old age-matched female naive mice were also used in studies. C57BL/6 were also purchased from the Jackson Laboratory and used for orthotopic injections and Rapalink-1 experiments (see below). All tumor-bearing mice were euthanized upon reaching humane endpoint (2 cm in diameter of combined tumors). All experiments were done with pairs of tumor endpoint mice and age-matched naïve mice. Only females were considered. All animal experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the UIC, as required by the US Animal Welfare Act and the policy of the NIH. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane for all nonterminal surgical procedures. Injection with ketamine (90 to 120 mg/kg)/xylazine (8 to 12 mg/kg) and CO2 asphyxiation were used for terminal procedures.

Cell lines

Murine E0771 parental and E0771 FKBP12 KO (discussed below) cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% Pen/Strep antibiotics. Isolated neutrophils from mice were cultured in RPMI 1640 with 10% FBS and 1% Pen/Strep antibiotics. 293FT cells (Invitrogen, #R70007) were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) with 10% FBS and 1% Pen/Strep antibiotics.

FKBP12 CRISPR KO in E0771 cells

Parental E0771 were infected with a lentivirus (lentiCRISPRv2-puro; Addgene, #52961) generated in 293FT cells using the Virapower packaging system (Invitrogen, #K497500) that expresses wild-type Streptococcus pyogenes Cas9 and a guide RNA (gRNA) expression scaffold. We followed the standard BsmBI-based cloning procedure to insert our own custom gRNA spacer sequencing (5′-AAAGAAATTTGATTCCTCTC-3′) that targets mouse Fkbp12. Western blot confirmed KO of FKBP12 in these cells. To confirm resistance to Rapalink-1, we plated the parental and FKBP12 KO E0771 cells at 9K cells per well on a 96-well plate and performed a toxicity curve titration experiment using a threefold serial dilution of Rapalink-1 starting at 100 nM. After 3 days, cells were counted using Hoechst/propidium iodide (PI) staining.

Tissue processing

BM-derived cells were isolated from tibia and femur as previously described (19, 84) with modification. Briefly, BM cells were harvested by flushing the femur and tibia with RPMI 1640 containing 10% FBS, 1% Pen/Strep, and 2 mM EDTA using a 25-gauge needle. Cells were passed through a 100-μm strainer and centrifuged at 429g for 5 min at 4°C. Red blood cells (RBCs) were lysed by adding 3 ml of room temperature ammonium chloride potassium (ACK) lysis buffer to the cell pellet. Ten milliliters of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was added to stop the lysis before centrifuging the cells again. BM cells were cultured in complete RPMI 1640 media. Mouse spleens were harvested and pulverized, before being subjected to RBC lysis and several subsequent washes with complete RPMI 1640.

Tumor and lung tissues were isolated with the Miltenyi Biotec tumor dissociation kit. Briefly, tumors and lungs (perfused through the right ventricle with 10 ml of PBS and through the trachea with 1 ml of PBS) were isolated from anesthetized mice. Tissues were then washed in PBS. For preparation of single-cell suspensions, the tissues were resuspended in the digestion enzymes provided in the kit as directed by the manufacturer in 2 ml of Eppendorf tubes. Only 20% of enzyme R was used to preserve leukocyte antigens for downstream fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) or flow cytometry. The tissues were minced using scissors within the tube. Tissues were homogenized by rotating the samples at 37°C for 45 min. During that time, the cell mixtures were intermittently pipetted with a 1-ml tip to help facilitate cell dissociation. After dissociation, the cells were run through a 100-μm filter and pelleted by centrifugation at 350g. RBC lysis was performed with ACK buffer as described above. Last, the cells were washed several times with complete RPMI media before culturing or experiments.

Otherwise, if the tumor and lung tissues were to be used for hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining or IHC staining, then the samples were fixed in 10% formalin overnight with shaking at 4°C and then washed with 70% ethanol for 1 hour before being stored at 4°C until paraffin embedding (see below). Blood samples were collected through the vena cava of anesthetized mice using a 25-gauge needle. Afterward, whole blood samples were immediately placed in EDTA-coated microtainers to prevent coagulation. RBC lysis was performed by adding 2 ml of room temperature RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend) per 100 μl of whole blood. Afterward, samples were centrifuged at 350g for 5 min. Cells were washed and resuspended in complete RPMI 1640.

H&E and IHC staining

Formalin-fixed tissues were embedded in paraffin wax using the Micron STP 20 tissue processor. Paraffin tissue blocks were sectioned using a microtome set at 5 μm. After baking the slides overnight, sections were rehydrated through sequential 3-min washes in the order: xylene, 100% ethanol, 85% ethanol, 75% ethanol, 50% ethanol, and finally water.

For H&E staining, the slides were stained with hematoxylin for 1 min, washed in water briefly, washed in acidic ethanol (1% HCl/ethanol) for 5 s, and then washed in bluing agent (0.1% sodium bicarbonate in water) for 1 min. Following another wash in water, the slides were stained with eosin for 45 s and then washed in 75% ethanol. Afterward, slides were dehydrated by subsequential 3-min washes in the order: 75% ethanol, 85% ethanol, 100% ethanol, and finally xylene. Following this, coverslips were mounted using Permount (Fisher).

For IHC, citrate antigen retrieval was performed after rehydration by boiling sections in an acidic sodium citrate buffer [10 mM sodium citrate and 0.05% Tween 20 (pH 6.0)] in the microwave until bubbles appear and then transferring the slides to a sub-boiling water bath for 20 min. After subsequent washes in tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 (TBST), peroxidase quenching was performed by incubating the slides in 3% hydrogen peroxide/methanol for 10 min at room temperature. The sections were washed again and then blocked with 5% normal goat serum for 1 hour. Afterward, the primary antibody was added overnight in the blocking buffer. Next day, slides were washed three times with TBST and then incubated with the goat anti-rabbit IgG-HRP (horseradish peroxidase) secondary antibody for 1 hour. Following three more washes with TBST, the 3,3′-diaminobenzidine (DAB) substrate was added; the reaction was monitored under a light microscope and quenched in water when the desired staining intensity was reached. The slides were then stained with hematoxylin as before and then washed and dehydrated into xylene before coverslips were mounted. Staining was observed and recorded using the Leica Dmi8 microscope using the 20x objective lens. Manual counting of the entire tissue section was used to quantify the H&E staining for metastases. For IHC, roughly 5 to 10 portions of the tissue sample were chosen at random to take images. IHC quantification was performed using ImageJ to count the number of DAB+ cells and nuclei.

Neutrophil isolation from the BM with Histopaque gradient

Following the RBC lysis step and centrifugation, the BM cell pellet was resuspended in 1 ml of cold PBS and layered on top of a Histopaque gradient, consisting of two layers: 3 ml of Histopaque 1119 on the bottom and 3 ml of Histopaque 1075 on top. After centrifuging at 2000 rpm for 30 min (with brakes off), neutrophils were recovered at the interface of the two layers. Neutrophils were washed several times with complete RPMI 1640.

Complete cell count

Whole blood was collected from anesthetized mice through the vena cava using a 25-gauge needle. Complete cells count of murine blood samples to identify was performed by UIC’s Biologic Resources Lab. Cell types were identified by electrical impedance to measure cell size and morphology.

Transwell migration assay

BM neutrophils were isolated from naïve and PyMT mice. A 96-well Transwell plate (Neuro Probe) with a 3-μm pore size membrane was used for the assay. Briefly, 100K neutrophils were plated on the top well in 23 μl of complete RPMI 1640. The bottom well contained 30 μl of RPMI 1640 media with either dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) or 30 nM recombinant murine CXCL1. Neutrophils were allowed to traverse the membrane over 1.5 hours. Traversed neutrophils were counted in the bottom well to quantify mobility.

Flow cytometry and FACS

Neutrophils from tumors and lungs were isolated or quantified via flow cytometry or FACS using the following protocol. Following tissue digestion and RBC lysis, the single-cell suspensions were washed in PBS with 2% FBS and 2 mM EDTA (FACS buffer). Following resuspension in FACS buffer, the cells were blocked with anti-mouse CD16/32 TruStain FcX antibody (BioLegend) according to the manufacturer’s recommendation for 5 min on ice. Then, the samples were incubated with fluorophore-conjugated antibodies directed against the following surface antigens: CD45, CD11b, LY6G, and CD101. All fluorophore-conjugated antibodies used were purchased from BioLegend and used at the concentration specified by the manufacturer. Incubation with antibodies proceeded for 20 min on ice in the dark. Samples were then washed twice in FACS buffer and then analyzed on the flow cytometer (Beckman CytoFLEX S) or processed via the FACS machine (SONY cell sorter MA900) to isolate neutrophils. Neutrophils were identified as CD45+;CD11b+;LY6G+ cells. In addition, CD101 staining was used to distinguish mature (CD101+) versus immature (CD101−) neutrophils.

For surface staining of blood samples, the protocol is similar except whole blood samples (collected in EDTA-coated microtainers) from the vena cava were directly blocked with TruStain FcX, incubated with antibodies, and then treated with RBC lysis buffer (BioLegend).

For quantification of surface Ly6G expression, the mean fluorescence intensity of the Ly6G-binding conjugate antibody was measured in CytExpert v2.5 (Beckman Coulter).

Quantitative real-time PCR

RNA was isolated from cells using the GenJET RNA purification kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). One microgram of RNA was used to generate cDNA using the iScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific). cDNA was diluted three times, and 2 μl was used for a 10-μl qRT-PCR reaction. qRT-PCR was performed using the iQ SYBR Green Supermix (Bio-Rad) in the Bio-Rad CFX Connect Real-Time PCR System. Primers used are listed in Table 1. Relative RNA expression level was calculated as 2−ΔΔCt (cycle threshold) for the difference between the Ct value for the gene of interest and the respective Ct value for β-actin in the Bio-Rad CFX manager 3.1 software. Fold changes (FCs) were calculated by ratio the relative expression of target gene in the experimental group compared to the control group.

Cytokine profile assay

Serum was isolated from mouse whole blood using serum-separating tubes (BD microtainer). After letting the blood sit in the tube for 30 min, the sample was then centrifuged at 1200g for 10 min at 4°C. The cytokine levels were detected using a proteome profiler mouse cytokine array kit (R&D Systems) following a standard manufacturer’s instruction.

Immunoblotting

The cells were washed once with cold PBS and lysed in radioimmunoprecipitation assay (RIPA) buffer containing a Pierce protease inhibitor mini tablet inhibitor and a Pierce phosphatase inhibitor mini tablet inhibitor (one tablet per 10 ml of lysis buffer) on ice. Protein containing the supernatant was collected by centrifugation at 13,000g at 4°C for 10 min. Equal amounts of protein were loaded on an SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) gel and transferred to a polyvinylidene difluoride (PVDF) membrane. The membrane was blocked with 5% skim milk in TBST or 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in TBST for phosphorylated proteins and incubated with different primary antibodies and processed by standard Western blot procedures. ImageJ was used for data quantification.

Orthotopic injection

Parental E0771 and FKBP12 KO E0771 cells were used to establish orthotopic tumors in C57BL/6 mice. Cells were resuspended in cold PBS at 250K cells/25 μl. The cell/PBS suspension was mixed with ice-cold Matrigel at 1:1 and kept on ice. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and their eyes were covered in Vaseline petroleum jelly to prevent them from drying out. Then, 50 μl of the cell/PBS/Matrigel mix was injected with an insulin syringe just below the fourth nipple into the breast tissue. Mice were monitored daily for a week following the injection. Mice were euthanized at tumor endpoint.

In vivo treatments

Recombinant mouse G-CSF (PeproTech) was dissolved in 0.1% BSA/PBS and injected subcutaneously daily with a 25-gauge needle with 2 μg (in 200 μl of 0.1% BSA/PBS) per mouse. Treatment was carried out for 5 days in naïve mice. Reciprocally, a neutralizing anti-mouse G-CSF antibody (R&D Systems) was injected intraperitoneally into PyMT mice at 50 μg (in 100 μl of 0.1% BSA/PBS) per mouse every other day for ∼1 week. Control PyMT mice were injected with an IgG isotype control.

Rapalink-1 was administered via intraperitoneal injection at 1.5 mg/kg [dissolved in 100 μl of 1:2:2 DMSO:polyethylene glycol, molecular weight 300 (PEG-300):PBS solution] as described previously (85). Near-endpoint tumor-bearing MMTV-PyMT and age-matched naïve mice were injected and euthanized 3 days later.

Uptake of fluorescent Rapalink-1 analog in peripheral white blood cells

Blood was collected from the vena cava from naïve or endpoint MMTV-PyMT mice and added to EDTA-coated microtainers before being transported to sterile FACS tubes. Then, a fluorescent analog of Rapalink-1 (RapaTamra-PEG8) (27) was incubated with whole blood at a concentration of 100 nM for 4 hours at 37°C. Then, flow cytometry was used, as described above, to analyze the neutrophil population in the blood. In addition, RapaTamra-PEG8 emits light in the phycoerythrin (PE) channel, allowing us to assess its uptake into neutrophils. In this way, we could determine relative RapaTamra-PEG8 uptake by looking directly at the intensity at the mean fluorescence intensity of PE positivity in the CD45+;CD11b+;LY6G+ neutrophil subset as measured using CytExpert v2.5.

Ex vivo mTOR inhibition with Rapalink-1 in BM neutrophils

We conducted a Rapalink-1 titration assay for naïve and PyMT-derived BM neutrophils in Fig. 5C. Briefly, BM neutrophils were isolated via FACS (CD45+;CD11b+;LY6G+;CD101+) and then resuspended in complete RPMI. Mature CD101+ neutrophils were isolated because these neutrophils are the ones that express elevated IFITM proteins (fig. S12A). After resuspension, neutrophils were treated with vehicle only or Rapalink-1 at varying concentrations for 1 hour at 37°C. Neutrophils were then pelleted and treated with RIPA buffer with protease and phosphatase inhibitors to inhibit dephosphorylation from occurring. Western blot for pS6 and total S6 revealed the level of mTOR inhibition.

Single-cell RNA sequencing

Each sample type was collected in duplicate for scRNA-seq experiments. The exceptions are for lung samples from MMTV-PyMT mice, which were collected in triplicate, and the orthotopic E0771 experiments, for which a single BM sample was collected from a naïve or E0771-injected mouse. Samples were processed for scRNA-seq using the Chromium Single Cell Library and Gel bead kit v3; the manufacturer’s recommended protocol was followed (10x Genomics, CG000183_ChromiumSingleCell3__v3_UG_RevB). All neutrophil samples were resuspended in complete RPMI media. Viability was determined via Trypan blue staining and counting on a hemacytometer. Only samples showing viability above 90% were processed further. Chromium’s Controller was used to make nanoliter-sized droplets that harbor uniquely barcoded beads called Gel Bead-InEMulsions or GEMs. After GEM generation, the mRNA was reverse transcribed, and the resulting cDNA was amplified with 11 PCR cycles. The Chromium i7 Samples Index plate (10x Genomics) was used to PCR amplify the final library for 11 to 14 cycles. Quality control (QC) of the libraries was assessed using the High Sensitivity D5000 ScreenTape (TapeStation, Agilent Technologies). Last, the libraries were sequenced using a NovaSeq 6000 SP flow cell (Illumina). The facilities at Illinois Urbana-Champaign conducted the sequencing.

Alignment for scRNA-seq analyses

CellRanger (v6.0.2; 10x Genomics) was used to map the scRNA-seq data. In brief, CellRanger’s mkfastq module was used to preprocess/demultiplex the raw sequences in the FASTQ files and convert the BCL files to FASTQ files. Then, CellRanger’s align module was used to align the reads to the murine reference genome (GRCm38/mm10) with the appropriate gene model from Ensembl (v 109). This module uses the STAR aligner (v2.7.10b) and generates a BAM file, which contains all the mapped reads (86).

The feature-barcode matrices, which contain the quantified expression of each gene in each cell, was generated using CellRanger’s count module. This module identifies the cell barcodes and UMIs (unique molecular identifiers) within each BAM files and creates a filtered BAM file, which only contains cell-associated reads. The filtered BAM file is used to generate a digital expression matrix (DEM), which quantifies the number of UMIs that correspond to genes in each individual cell. Last, CellRanger’s agger module was used to perform QC of the mapped data. This involves combining the DEMs from multiple scRNA-seq libraries to generate a normalized expression matrix, which is used for downstream analyses.

Quality control

Seurat (v4.3.0) and R (v4.2.2) were used to analyze the scRNA-seq datasets (87). First, QC was performed by filtering cells based on the number of detected genes (nFeature_RNA) and the percentage of mitochondrial RNA reads (%mito). Specifically, only cells that met the following criteria were kept: nFeature_RNA > 200, nFeature_RNA < 6000, and %mito < 5.

Analysis of scRNA-seq data

After QC and filtering, we analyzed the single-cell data from all the different cell types (i.e., BM, blood, lungs, spleen, and primary tumor). We first grouped all datasets of common tissue origin together. Each tissue group contained datasets derived from samples taken from paired MMTV-PyMT and age-matched naïve mice that were processed at the same time. Nevertheless, to account for nonbiological variability between the samples, we used Seurat’s integration and unsupervised clustering pipeline to analyze the samples within each tissue dataset. First, we used Seurat’s SplitObject function to divide each group into individual sample objects; the mRNAs in each cell from each object was then normalized, using the NormalizeData function (scale factor of 10,000 by default), and analyzed for variable features using the FindVariableFeatures function (“vst” method; 1000 or 2000 variable features) individually. Next, we used Seurat’s SelectIntegrationFeatures to select features that was be used for integration. These features were then used as the input for scaling the data and running the principal components analysis (PCA) using Seurat’s ScaleData and RunPCA functions, respectively, with default settings. Next, the FindIntegrationAnchors function identified anchor genes among the objects, followed by the IntegrateData function to integrate them together. Reciprocal principal component analysis (RPCA) integration was used for this latter step. After another round of scaling and PCA calculation, we performed the RunUMAP function to create the UMAP to perform the nonlinear dimensional reduction of the integrated object. The UMAPs were visualized using the DimPlot function. The FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions determined the K-nearest neighbors and a shared nearest neighbor (SNN) graph to cluster the cells. Consult Table 2 for the parameters used for each experiment’s integration used to generate UMAPs depicted in the figures.

Last, we used a resolution of 1, 0.5, and 0.3 for the FindClusters function for the spleen, GSE114727, and GSE278456 datasets, respectively.

To perform in silico isolation of neutrophils from each tissue group, we set the DefaultAssay to “RNA” and used the FeaturePlot function to visualize which clusters express murine markers including Elane, Mpo, Ly6G, S100a8, and S100a9. Also, we used Seurat’s AddModuleScore function to calculate module scores for gene sets that are specifically expressed in neutrophils and myeloid progenitors, which were used previously by Xie et al. (11). These module scores basically measure the combined expression of multiple genes from a gene set. We then used the FeaturePlot function to visualize which clusters express these sets of genes. We then used the subset function to isolate clusters that are part of the neutrophil lineage. We excluded clusters that (i) were contaminated with platelet mRNAs, including expression of Pf4, NGRN, and/or PPBP; (ii) expressed mRNAs from a contaminating cell type; or (iii) express a diminutive number of and/or lack adequate representation of genes.

Because the BM contains the whole neutrophil lineage beginning with hematopoietic stem cells, we further analyzed the in silico–isolated neutrophils within these datasets to determine how tumor burden affects neutrophil differentiation along the entire trajectory. To that extent, we split the partitioned BM neutrophils into experimental groups, each composed of a pair or samples from a naïve and a PyMT mouse that were age-matched and harvested in parallel. This is appropriate because technical variability is lowest between samples collected simultaneously versus between samples collected on different days and/or with different tissue isolation techniques. This also has the advantage of not obscuring differential clustering between naïve and PyMT mice that is due to genuine biological differences. We then performed RPCA integration as described above using parameters outlined in Table 2. The resulting seven clusters were labeled N0 to N4 and N5_N and N5_P. The clusters are numerically ordered according to maturation, which was determined by using the AddModuleFunction to calculate module scores for expression of gene sets that are specific to different levels of neutrophil differentiation, as shown in fig. S2. The two N5 clusters are the most mature; the “N” suffix denotes a cluster mostly confined to naïve mice, and the “P” suffix denotes a cluster mostly confined to PyMT mice.

We also used the same approach in the preceding paragraph to analyze in silico–isolated neutrophil datasets derived from (i) the BM of G-CSF–treated and anti-G-CSF antibody–treated mice (Fig. 3C) and (ii) the combined datasets taken from the blood, lungs, and primary tumors from naïve and PyMT mice (Fig. 4A). Again, RPCA integration was performed after splitting the data into experimental groups, each containing a matched pair of naïve and PyMT-derived samples from the same tissue (Table 2).

We used an alternative integration pipeline after combining neutrophils from the BM, blood, lungs, and primary tumor tissues taken from both naïve and PyMT mice (Fig. 4F). Again, we performed RPCA integration using Seurat. However, this time, we split the combined dataset according to genotype (Table 2). In other words, we combined the different tissue samples derived from the naïve mice in one group and all the datasets from PyMT mice in another group. Doing this, we found anchors between the two naïve and PyMT-derived objects and coerced clustering between the two genotypes to ensure that neutrophils at the same level of maturation or subtype will cluster together, despite gene expression changes that may be induced by tumor burden. This also allows us to visually highlight differences in transcription originating from tissue itself because anchors are not found between different tissue types.

Some datasets were analyzed using the standard Seurat unsupervised clustering pipeline without integration. In this case, samples were normalized using Seurat’s NormalizeData function. Then, we identified 2000 variable features using the FindVariableFeature function. We used the number of principal components as suggested by Seurat’s Elbow plot, PCA heatmaps, and the Jack Straw plot using the RunPCA function. The FindNeighbors and FindClusters Seurat functions were used to cluster the cells, followed by the RunUMAP function to visualize the nonlinear dimensional reduction. All parameters used for analyses depicted in figures are shown in Table 2. In addition, in silico–isolated neutrophils from the spleen (related to fig. S10) and human patients (related to Fig. 6, B and G) had 20 PCAs calculated and used (npcs = 20 for the RunPCA function and dims = 20 for RunUMAP and FindNeighbors functions) and a resolution of 0.5 for the FindClusters function.

The N clustering determined from the original BM neutrophil samples (Fig. 1A) was assigned to neutrophil cells from all other scRNA-seq experiments using Seurat’s Label Transfer pipeline. Briefly, we used the UMAP and accompanying N cluster calculated for the BM samples in Fig. 1A as a reference because BM contains all stages of neutrophil differentiation. Then, we set the DefaultAssay to “RNA” and normalized the data from the reference BM neutrophil object using Seurat’s NormalizeData function with default parameters. Then, we used FindVariableFeatures to find 2000 variable genes in the reference object. Next, the FindTransferAnchors (30 dimensions) was used to find anchors. Last, we provided the anchors as input for Seurat’s TransferData function to transfer the N cluster assignments from the reference dataset to the query datasets. Cells with prediction scores below 0.5 were discarded.

Downstream gene expression scRNA-seq analysis

We identified distinguishing biomarker mRNAs for each cluster (Fig. 1F) using Seurat’s FindAllMarkers function (Wilcoxon rank sum test with Bonferroni correction test; adjusted P value < 0.05; log2FC ≥ 1.0, and min.pct = 0.20). Top 10 biomarkers (sorted by log2FC) were displayed in the heatmap.

We used Seurat’s AddModuleScore function to calculate module score for each cell for a particular set of genes. Similarly, we identified which phase of cell cycle each cell is in by using Seurat’s built-in gene sets for “S,” G2M,” and “G1” phases and the CellCycleScoring function. Expression for individual genes, module scores, and cell cycle module scores were visualized using Seurat’s FeaturePlot and VlnPlot functions.

Differential gene expression analysis was performed by pseudobulking neutrophils from individual samples. For determining the tumor-induced signature in PyMT-derived BM neutrophils (Fig. 1A), we pooled the reads from all the clusters within each sample; this pseudobulking effectively reduced the scRNA-seq dataset to a bulk RNA seq dataset. The pseudobulking approach allows us to take advantage of the statistics we could use by having replicate samples. This offered a more conservative approach than using Seurat’s built-in FindMarkers function, which, by default, does not take into account replicate samples. Only genes that expressed over 10 reads across all samples were considered. We used the edgeR package (90) to determine differentially expressed genes. The trimmed mean of M values (TMM) method was used to normalize the counts. After estimating the dispersion using edgeR’s estimateDisp function, we performed differential gene expression testing by using a quasilikelihood F test (glmQLFTest function) to test the fit of a quasilikelihood negative binomial generalized log-linear model (glmQLFit function) with individual factors for genotype and batch. The Benjamini-Hochberg method was used to adjust P values. Each batch consisted of paired naïve and PyMT samples that were processed at the same time. Doing this allows us to mitigate any batch effects. A significant adjusted P value was considered at 0.05. A similar method was used for differential expression testing between vehicle-treated and G-CSF–treated BM neutrophils (Fig. 3E) and naïve and PyMT neutrophils from the blood and lungs (Fig. 4, C and D).

We also performed differential expression testing on neutrophils from different tissues (fig. S11D). To focus purely on changes induced by the tissue-of-origin, we wanted to analyze neutrophils at the same level of maturation and from the same genotype. To that end, we first isolated neutrophils only from PyMT mice and from cluster N5_P. Afterward, we performed a similar pseudobulk differential gene expression analysis as described in the preceding paragraph, except our model used a factor for tissue instead of genotype. We counted any mRNA that was differentially expressed (adjusted P value < 0.05) in any of the tissues as significant and plotted it on the heatmap.

We used scatterplots to the visualize differential gene expression results. First, we pooled all neutrophil clusters across samples into a single group such as genotype or tissue origin. Then, Seurat’s Average Expression function was used to find the average mRNA expression within each group, which was then transformed using the log1p function [ln(mean expression + 1)]. Last, scatterplots with labeled genes were generated using the R package ggplot2 and the geom_point and LabelPoints functions. For the tumor-induced signature visualized in Fig. 2A, the blue and red dots represent all significantly differentially expressed mRNAs (adjusted P value < 0.05) that are up-regulated (logFC >1.0) or down-regulated (logFC < −1.0) in the PyMT-derived BM neutrophils. The labeled genes are the top 50 differentially expressed (sorted by adjusted P value) mRNAs. The same top 50 signature genes labeled in Fig. 2 (A and B) were also labeled on the scatterplots shown in Figs. 3 (E and F) and 4 (C and D) and figs. S3D and S10B to show how the expression of these particular genes change. The blue and red dots represent the mRNAs identified as significantly up-regulated or down-regulated, respectively, when comparing the two groups labeled in the scatterplot. The same criteria (adjusted P value < 0.05 and logFC < −1.0/logFC > 1.0) were used to identify significance. For analyses that only had one sample per group (Fig. 3F and figs. S3D and S10B), Seurat’s FindMarkers function was used to perform differential expression testing. Significance criteria were defined as an adjusted P value of <0.05 and log2FC < −0.5/log2FC > 0.5.

All gene expression heatmaps were generated using the heatmap function from the ComplexHeatmap package. Briefly, normalized reads were z-scaled using R’s scale function. Hierarchal clustering was performed using Euclidean distance. The heatmap in Fig. 2B contains the top 70 significant tumor-induced neutrophil reprogramming signature genes (sorted by adjusted P value). The same genes were analyzed for the heatmaps shown in Figs. 3G and 4G and figs. S3E and S10C. For each gene expression heatmap, z-scaling was done for each gene across all samples, but for Fig. 4G, z-scaling was done separately for each tissue so that differential gene expression between genotypes within each tissue would be the effectively visualized. Last, the heatmap in fig. S11D was split into four dendrogram subclusters using the heatmap function’s row_split parameter.

Gene set enrichment analysis

GSEA was performed on the scRNA-seq data to identify GO terms that are enriched in differentially expressed genes. All Hallmark GO gene sets were extracted from the msigdbr package. For fig. S4, differentially expressed genes were sorted according to −log(P value), and the GSEA was performed using the fgsea function from the fgsea package, with the scoreType set to “pos” because all ranking metric values were positive (91).

TF inference analysis

TF-target interactions were extracted from the CollecTri database from the decoupleR package (92). The Seurat object was first converted to an expression matrix, and then all genes that were not expressed in any cells were filtered out. ULM analysis was performed on individual cells using the run_ulm function (source: TFs; target: target genes) from the decoupleR package. The inferred scores for TF activity enrichment were then scaled using Seurat’s ScaleData function and deposited into a metadata slot. Last, the mean TF activity enrichment score was calculated for all neutrophils across samples that belong to a particular genotype or tissue group. Differential activity between different groups was assessed by sorting the TFs’ inferred activity scores from highest to lowest Sd among the groups being compared; TFs that exhibit the highest degree of variable activity between groups were considered top candidates for driving differential gene expression between those groups. Comparison of the mean TF activity between replicates was visualized with a heatmap using the ComplexHeatmap package. The Pearson correlation coefficient was calculated from each cell between the scaled TF activity and that TF’s normalized mRNA expression and then displayed alongside the gene name in the heatmap.

Monocle3 analysis

The Monocle3 (version 2.28.0) package (93–95) for R was used to infer neutrophil trajectories and identify gene modules (i.e., groups of genes whose expression changes occur concurrently and therefore might be under the control of the same regulatory signals) that occur along that trajectory and might be affected by tumor burden. Briefly, we used Monocle3’s as.cell_data_set function to convert our Seurat object (used to build the UMAP in Fig. 1A) into a Cell Data Set (CDS) object that Monocle3 uses. This way, we can preserve the original UMAP structure. Then, we clustered cells using Monocle3’s cluster_cells function with a resolution of 0.0001. We then used the learn_graph (ncenter = 800) and order_Cells functions to create the pseudotime trajectories. We set the root node within the N0 cluster. The plot_cells function was used to visualize the trajectory and the pseudotime.

We were interested only in temporally coregulated gene modules that are differentially expressed between naïve and PyMT mice. To that end, we used Monocle3’s choose_cells function to select only for neutrophils in the N3 to N5 clusters because these occur during and after the transcriptional branch point (fig. S5A). To find coregulated genes, we used Monocle3 to find gene modules—groups of genes whose expression is temporally linked according to the inferred pseudotime. This was done using Monocle3’s test_graph function and find_gene_modules function. We then used the aggregate_gene_expression function to aggregate each module’s inferred expression score according to “N” cluster. Then, we plotted these aggregated scores for each cluster in a heatmap (fig. S5D) using the pheatmap function (scaled according to row) from the pheatmap package. We focused on gene modules that exhibited the greatest difference in aggregated expression between the N5_N and N5_P clusters. For generation of the heatmap in Fig. 2F, we used the used the heatmap function from the ComplexHeatmap package to display the z-scored normalized expression of the top 10 correlated genes with the highest Moran’s I statistic and q value < 0.05 within each indicated gene module in clusters N3 to N5. To perform the enrichment analysis in fig. S5E, we modified our protocol to use the enricher function from the enrichR package to calculate enrichment/depletion of Hallmark GO terms; this function takes two lists: one for genes of interest such as the genes from each gene module and a list of background genes. We then plotted the −log(FDR) (false discovery rate) for each GO term’s enrichment/depletion for each gene module using the heatmap function from the ComplexHeatmap package.

Analysis of human datasets

To determine whether transcriptional reprogramming occurs in neutrophils from patients with human cancer, particularly elevated IFITM expression, we first retrieved human scRNA-seq data (GSE161529) from myeloid cells isolated from breast tissue of healthy volunteers and primary tumors from patients with TNBC (69). RPCA integration allowed us to cluster all congruent cell types together; plotting expression of known human neutrophils markers allowed us to identify the neutrophil cluster (Fig. 6, A and B). We extracted the average mRNA expression of IFITM1, IFITM2, IFITM3, and TSPO from neutrophils from each individual patient using the AverageExpression function and transformed it using the log1p function. We then made a bar graph to display their relative expression levels in healthy versus TNBC-derived neutrophils (Fig. 6C). We also looked at another scRNA-seq dataset (GSE114727) that isolated cells directly from the breast tumor, lymph nodes, and blood from patients with breast cancer (71). After in silico neutrophil isolation as done before, we performed a standard nonintegrative clustering scheme to come up with three neutrophils clusters (Fig. 6D). We then used the addmodule function to plot the combined expression of genes associated with an MDSC subtype (Fig. 6E), as defined by Alshetaiwi et al. (22), and combined IFITM1, IFITM2, and IFITM3 expressions (Fig. 6F) within each of the three clusters. Last, we performed a similar analysis on cells isolated from noncancerous brains versus glioblastoma tumors (GSE278456) (72). Given the large number of patients and isolated cells in this dataset, it was first necessary to downsample to 20,000 random cells before integration. Then, after isolating the neutrophil cluster as done before, we combined the IFITM1, IFITM2, and IFITM3 using the addmodule function and plotted the expression using Seurat’s RidgePlot function.

All QC and filtering steps used for preprocessing these human scRNA-seq datasets were the same as those used for the mouse datasets produced in this study.

Statistics

GraphPad Prism 8 was used for all statistics presented in bar and line graphs. Parametric Student’s two-tailed or one-tailed t tests were used to calculate P values for difference in mean calculations. The Western blot band intensity was quantified using pixel intensity as measure by ImageJ. IHC results were also quantified by ImageJ using the FIJI iteration to count the number of DAB+ cells and total number of nuclei. Nonparametric Mann-Whitney tests were also used for non-normally distributed data. A two-factor mixed-effects analysis was used to test significance for an effect of Rapalink-1 on tumor size over time (fig. S13E).
