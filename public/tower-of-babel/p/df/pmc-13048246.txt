Nuclear translocation of β-catenin in Wg/Wnt signaling via the IFT-A microtubule–associated complex requires Pasovec/Gid8 proteins
Authors: Linh T. Vuong, Marek Mlodzik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Wg/Wnt signaling is critical throughout development and homeostasis and associated with many diseases, including cancer. Wg/Wnt signaling is mediated by β-catenin (Armadillo/Arm in Drosophila) with the IFT-A/Kinesin2 complex promoting nuclear translocation of β-catenin/Arm. Existing information suggests that additional proteins are involved. Here, we demonstrate that a conserved protein, Pasovec (Psv; Gid8 in mammals), with loss-of-function mutants resembling wg and arm/β-catenin mutant phenotypes, is required for nuclear β-catenin/Arm localization. Psv interacts with the IFT-A/Kinesin2 complex, physically binding IFT140, a core component of IFT-A. The Psv/Gid8-IFT140 association is independent of Wg/Wnt-signaling activation. Psv/Gid8 contains a CRA domain, identified as interacting with RanBPM, which mediates its nuclear localization. Mutations in CRA affect Psv/Gid8’s own nuclear localization and that of β-catenin/Arm upon Wg/Wnt-signaling activation. Psv with a mutated CRA can act as an inhibitor of Wg/Wnt signaling. Together, this study describes a previously unidentified factor, required for Wg/Wnt signaling, that functions during the nuclear translocation process of β-catenin/Arm.

INTRODUCTION

The Wnt/Wingless (Wg)-β-catenin (β-cat) signaling pathway is highly conserved across metazoans and required in the regulation of many processes, ranging from embryonic development and organogenesis, including tissue growth, cell fate specification, survival, and migration, to stem cell maintenance and regeneration [reviewed in (1–5)]. Wnt signaling is also critically linked to several diseases, including developmental abnormalities, cilium-associated defects, and initiation and progression of various types of cancers [reviewed in (6–9)]. In the absence of the Wnt signal, β-cat is phosphorylated by the so-called “destruction complex” (DC) and therefore targeted for proteasomal degradation. The DC is composed of Axin, APC (adenomatous polyposis coli), and the kinases glycogen synthase kinase 3β (GSK3β) and casein kinase 1α (CK1α) [reviewed in (1, 3, 5, 10)]. In Wnt/Wg-β-cat signaling, Wnt binds to the Frizzled-LRP5/6 (low-density lipoprotein receptor–related protein 5/6) receptor/co-receptor pair, leading to the disassembly of the DC, upon which cytoplasmic β-cat [Armadillo/Arm in Drosophila (11)] is released and stable [e.g., reviewed in (3–5, 12)]. Nuclear translocation of β-cat/Arm is then the rate-limiting step in Wnt signal transduction for transcriptional target gene activation, with β-cat/Arm being the key coactivator of the T cell factor/lymphoid enhancer factor (TCF/LEF) transcription factor family [reviewed in (1, 3, 13)].

While the membrane proximal Wnt-signaling receptor complexes, resulting in pathway activation, and the composition and function of the DC are reasonably well understood [reviewed in (1, 5, 10, 12)], the dissection of the nuclear translocation process of β-cat remains a work in progress (14, 15). Recent work has identified a critical role for the IFT-A [intraflagellar transport-A complex (16, 17)] and Kinesin2 in nuclear translocation of β-cat/Arm (18–20). It has been established that an IFT-A/Kinesin2 complex binds to β-cat/Arm, as it is released from the DC, forming a transport complex that is required for nuclear translocation of β-cat/Arm (18, 20). IFT-A is generally known for its essential role during ciliogenesis and ciliary retrograde transport (16, 17). In Wnt signaling, IFT-A acts in the cytoplasm, and in particular, it binds an N-terminal region of β-cat/Arm (residues 34 to 87 in Arm, equivalent to the β-cat24–79 peptide in mammals) via its subunit IFT140 (21). IFT-A also binds to Kap3 connecting it to Kinesin2/Kif3a (18). The resulting complex associates with cytoplasmic microtubules and promotes nuclear translocation of β-cat/Arm upon Wnt/Wg-pathway activation (18, 20). The loss of function (LOF) of IFT-A complex components or Kap3 and Kinesin2 results in impaired nuclear translocation of β-cat in vivo in Drosophila, as well as in mouse embryonic fibroblasts and human cell lines (18, 19, 21). The binding of β-cat to IFT140 within IFT-A is critical for nuclear translocation. Expression of the respective N-terminal β-cat peptide interferes with the IFT-A interaction of endogenous β-cat by competing for/blocking its binding site, thus acting like an interference tool and suppressing Wg/Wnt signaling in vivo during development and in cancer cell lines (21).

The unexpected observation that the stable β-cat/Arm mutant isoform, ArmS10 (22), which has this interaction region deleted, is still capable of localizing to the nucleus (18) and activating Wg-target gene expression suggested that there are likely other β-cat binding proteins required for its nuclear translocation, either as cytoplasmic retention anchors, competing with IFT140 to inhibit the process, or additional positively acting β-cat/Arm–interacting factors promoting its nuclear translocation in parallel to or together with IFT-A. This hypothesis is further supported by the fact that nuclear localization of ArmS10 is not visibly affected by reduced IFT140 levels (18). To identify additional factors required in this process, we searched the literature for proteins with a suggested function in nuclear β-cat/Arm localization and/or its nuclear function. This identified a gene called Twa1 (for “Two hybrid-associated protein 1 with RanBPM”) (23) as a potential candidate, with Lu et al. (23) suggesting that it is required for nuclear retention of β-cat in cancer cell contexts (see below for more details).

Drosophila wing development serves as a paradigm for Wnt/Wg-signaling dissection. In larval wing imaginal discs, Wg is expressed as a two-cell stripe at the dorsoventral (D/V) compartment boundary of the wing pouch, where it acts as a morphogen and activates target genes in a concentration-dependent manner (24–27). Wg protein is detected in a gradient up to several cells away from its expression source (26, 27), leading to a stereotypic pattern of gene expression governing wing development with its highest levels specifying wing margin cells (28, 29). Wg signaling targets in cells adjacent to the D/V boundary include senseless (sens), a gene responsible for specification of wing margin sensory bristles (30), while lower Wg threshold target genes include Distalless (Dll), expressed in a graded fashion, decreasing away from the Wg expression stripe (28, 29). The use of wing imaginal disc patterning has thus served as a tremendous resource to dissect the Wnt/Wg signaling pathway and associated cell biological features (2). In addition to the wing, Drosophila embryogenesis and segmental patterning have served as an excellent model for Wg/Wnt-pathway assembly, including the identification of arm/β-catenin, as a key component of Wg signaling [e.g., (22, 31)].

Here, we identify a conserved gene, Drosophila CG18467 [mentioned as Twa1 or Gid8 (glucose-induced degradation protein homolog 8) in mammalian cells (23, 32, 33)], which cooperates with the IFT-A/Kinesin2 complex in β-cat/Arm nuclear translocation. On the basis of the embryonic LOF phenotype of CG18467, closely resembling that of armadillo (arm) mutants, we have named the gene pasovec (psv; the Czech word for armadillo). Our data indicate that psv is required for Wg/Wnt signaling in all tissues tested and critical for nuclear translocation of β-cat/Arm. Our results are consistent with a model where Psv directly associates with β-cat/Arm to promote its nuclear localization, at least in part in a cooperative manner with the IFT-A complex. Notably, Psv contains a domain [C-terminal RanBPM–associated (CRA)] required for its nuclear localization, which we show to be critical for its role in Wg/Wnt signaling and β-cat/Arm nuclear localization, both in Drosophila and in human cells (with the human ortholog Gid8 performing an equivalent function). Our work described here not only identifies a previously unidentified conserved factor in Wg/Wnt signaling but also provides additional insight into an emerging mechanistic model for nuclear transport of β-cat/Arm upon Wnt-signaling activation.

RESULTS

CG18467/pasovec knockdown resembles Wg-signaling defects

As mentioned above, the observation that the stable ArmS10 mutant isoform (22) is capable of translocating into the nucleus led us to screen for potential additional factors in the process of nuclear β-cat/Arm translocation. A literature search identified a gene called Twa1 [for Two hybrid-associated protein 1 with RanBPM (Ran binding protein M)] (23) as a candidate. Notably, Twa1, also known as Gid8 in mammals, has two orthologs in the Drosophila genome, CG18467 and houki (34). This gene family is conserved, exhibiting >60% sequence identity between the Drosophila and human proteins (fig. S1A). It contains a C-terminal domain proposed to interact with RanBPM [CRA; as defined by Lu et al. (23)] (see sequence alignment in fig. S1A). The houki (hou) gene has been recently analyzed and shown to function as a core scaffold component of the Drosophila C-terminal to LisH (CTLH) E3 ubiquitin-ligase complex, where it mediates the regulated degradation of maternal RNA binding proteins during early embryogenesis (34). We thus tested the other Drosophila ortholog, CG18467, for a potential role in Wg signaling. First, we used the UAS/Gal4 system (35) for RNA interference (RNAi)–mediated gene knockdown at the wing margin (using C96-Gal4; see expression domain in fig. S1B) [also (35)], which serves as a rigorous model for Wg-signaling dissection. RNAi-based knockdown of CG18467 (referred to as pasovec/psv; see below for explanation) with three independent RNAi lines resulted in the loss of wing margin cells and associated notching phenotypes [Fig. 1, A to C; see quantification and additional RNAi lines in fig. S1 (C to Eb)].

To further define the role of CG18467/psv in wing development, we examined its requirement for expression of key Wg/Wnt-signaling target genes senseless/sens (30) and Distal-less/Dll (36). RNAi knockdown of CG18467/psv (using C96-Gal4) led to a marked reduction in the number of Sens-expressing cells and Dll expression at the D/V boundary (Fig. 1, Aa to Cb; quantifications in figs. S1C and S3B). Similarly, knockdown of CG18467/psv using en-Gal4 caused partial loss of the posterior wing margin in adult wings and a corresponding reduction in Sens expression (fig. S2, A and B; quantification in fig. S2C). In contrast, Wg expression itself was not affected (fig. S2, A to C), excluding a psv role in Notch signaling and confirming that the observed defects are due to impaired Wg-signaling activity, not Wg expression. As mentioned above, Drosophila encodes two Gid8 paralogs, Psv (this study) and Hou/CG6617 (34). Knockdown of hou (C96-Gal4>houIR or en-Gal4>houIR) did not detectably affect Wg/β-cat signaling (fig. S2, D to Ea). Unexpectedly, broader hou depletion (en-Gal4>houIR) resulted in misorientation of wing hairs, a Wnt/planar cell polarity (PCP) defect (fig. S2, D and Da). These results demonstrate that the two Gid8 paralogs in Drosophila have distinct functions, with Psv affecting Wg/Wnt signaling and Hou possibly contributing to PCP regulation (see Discussion). The psv-associated loss of wing margin fate was not due to cell death, as there was no detectable Caspase3 induction resulting from psvIR expression (fig. S1, F to Fb). Together, these results suggest that CG18467/psv, as a member of the Gid8 protein family, is specifically required for Wg/Wnt-signaling activity and Wg target gene activation during wing patterning.

As the premise of this study was to identify additional factors required in the nuclear translocation process of β-cat/Arm, we next assessed CG18467/psv in the salivary gland (SG) assay. SG cells, because of their large cell and nuclear size, facilitate detection of nuclear β-cat/Arm upon Wg induction (18). Notably, Wg-induced β-cat/Arm translocation was eliminated by RNAi-mediated knockdown of CG18467/psv (Fig. 1, D to F; quantification in fig. S2I; note that without Wg induction, Arm is mainly localized at cell junctions irrespective of psvIR knockdown; fig. S2, F to H). Consistently, Western blot analysis of SG nuclear extracts revealed a loss of nuclear Arm in psvIR backgrounds (Fig. 1G; note that total Arm protein levels remained unchanged by psvIR). On the basis of the above functional requirements, along with the embryonic LOF phenotype of CG18467/psv (see below), we have named the gene pasovec (psv; the Czech word for armadillo).

pasovec (psv) LOF mutant phenotypes display Wg-signaling defects

To confirm and further define the role of psv as a Wg-signaling factor, we have generated LOF mutants (using the CRISPR-Cas9 methodology; see Materials and Methods), thus obtaining two independent LOF alleles, designated as psvnull and psvΔC (see fig. S3A for allele information) with both alleles showing indistinguishable phenotypes. Analyses of psv mutant tissue in wing disc clones revealed that expression of Wg targets Sens and Dll was largely eliminated in psv mutant cells (Fig. 2, A to F, and figs. S3, B to F). Dlg as a cellular junctional marker was not affected (Fig. 2G and fig. S3F), indicating that psv does not interfere with epithelial cell architecture. Quantification of Sens and Dll expression in psv mutant cells revealed that these target gens were largely lost (Fig. 2B and fig. S3B). A knockdown of IFT140 in psv mutant cells did not enhance the effect of psv−/− on Sens expression, consistent with both genes being fully required in Wg signaling and affecting the same process together or in parallel (quantified in Fig. 2B). These data were consistent with the RNAi experiments for psv shown above (Fig. 1), defining psv as an essential component of the Wg/β-cat pathway.

In addition to wing patterning, Wg signaling in Drosophila is essential in many other contexts; for example, it is critical for establishing segmentation, as a “segment polarity gene,” during embryogenesis, giving the armadillo gene its name [e.g., reviewed in (37, 38)]. In the embryo, the loss of arm or other Wg signaling components disrupts the anterior-posterior segmental patterning and associated denticle belt specification on the ventral epidermis of embryos and larvae (39). Knockdown of psv using daughterless-Gal4 (da-Gal4; driving expression in all embryonic cells) resulted in a consistent segment polarity phenotype, characterized by ectopic denticles and partial loss of naked cuticle (fig. S3G), very similar in strength and penetrance to the phenotype observed in da-Gal4, UAS-armRNAi embryos (fig. S3G). Confirming this phenotype and the requirement of psv for embryonic Wg signaling, both zygotically homozygous psv LOF alleles, psvnull and psvΔC, caused segment polarity–type segmentation defects with disrupted denticle belt patterning (Fig. 2, H and I; compare to Fig. 2G for wild-type control, segments A3 to A5), again very similar to arm LOF alleles that specifically affect Wg signaling (11). This phenotype gave pasovec (psv) its name. As several Wg-signaling factors share a substantial maternal contribution, we also tested the knockdown of maternal psv (with nanos[nos]-Gal4) or combined maternal-zygotic (da-gal4 and nos-Gal4). Both scenarios displayed stronger phenotypes with higher penetrance than the purely zygotic mutants or knockdown (Fig. 2, J to L, and fig. S3H; 27 to 29% penetrance). Together with the requirements in wing development and the SG assay, these data indicate that psv plays a general role in Wg/Wnt signaling, likely by affecting the nuclear translocation of Arm/β-cat.

psv functionally interacts with the IFT-A/Kinesin2 complex

Given that a psv knockdown disrupts the nuclear translocation of Arm/β-cat (Fig. 1, D to F), we next tested whether it acts together with the IFT-A/Kinesin2 complex. To assess this, we examined whether psv functionally interacts with components of this complex using RNAi and overexpression in the wing margin specification assay. Very similar to psvIR knockdown (Fig. 3, A and B; via C96-Gal4), knockdown of IFT140, the Arm/β-cat binding component of IFT-A, resulted in a partial loss of the wing margin (Fig. 3C; quantified in Fig. 3G). Reducing IFT140 level in the psvIR background enhanced the respective phenotype, with an increase in loss of margin cells and wing tissue (Fig. 3E; compare to control in Fig. 3D). Overexpression of IFT140 (in the C96>psvIR background) largely suppressed the margin defects (Fig. 3, F and G; C96>IFT140 alone did not cause detectable defects in adults or Sens and Dll expression in wing discs; fig. S4, E and Ea).

Consistent with adult wing phenotypes, C96>psvIR analyzed in wing discs resulted in reduction of Sens-expressing cells and Dll expression along the D/V boundary [Fig. 3, Ba, Bb, Da, and Db; compare to wt control in Fig. 3 (Aa and Ab); Sens expression quantification in Fig. 3H]. The reduction in Sens and Dll expression caused by psvIR was either enhanced by concomitant knockdown of IFT140 (Fig. 3, Ea, Eb, and H) or rescued by its overexpression [Fig. 3, Fa, Fb, and H; compare to control in Fig. 3 (Da and Db)]. Similar interactions were observed with Kinesin2 (Klp64D) and the Kap3 adaptor (fig. S4). Together, these data suggested that Psv functions together with the IFT-A/Kinesin2 complex in Wg/Wnt signaling and, although all are essential, either of the components can be rate limiting.

Psv directly associates with IFT140 and Arm/β-cat

To establish the mechanism of interactions between Psv and the IFT-A/Kinesin2/β-cat complex, we tested whether Psv physically associates with any of its components. Notably, glutathione S-transferase (GST)-Psv displayed direct physical interaction with IFT-A, via IFT140 (Fig. 3I, lanes 1 and 2) and Arm/β-cat (Fig. 3I, lanes 7 and 8), in in vitro pulldowns of the respective maltose-binding protein (MBP) fusions (Fig. 3I; odd lanes represent 10% input, and even lanes represent the pulldowns). These interactions were specific, as Psv did not pull down Kinesin2 (Klp64D, Drosophila Kif3a; Fig. 3I, lanes 3 and 4) or a control kinesin (Klp61F; Fig. 3I, lanes 5 and 6), or Arm did not pull down GST (lane 9). To confirm the Psv and IFT140 interaction in vivo, we performed coimmunoprecipitation (co-IP) assays with Drosophila wing imaginal disc extracts expressing IFT140-myc (or IFT144-myc as a negative control; expressed throughout the wing pouch with nub-Gal4). Extracts from nub>IFT140-myc and nub>IFT144-myc wing discs were immunoprecipitated with anti-Psv antibodies (see Materials and Methods and below) and probed with anti-myc (Fig. 3J), revealing that IFT140 readily coimmunoprecipitated with Psv (Fig. 3J), suggesting that they form a physiological complex. Together, these results support the conclusion that Psv directly associates with both IFT140/IFT-A and Arm/β-cat and further suggest that Psv plays a role in the nuclear translocation of Arm/β-cat by participating in the IFT-A/Kinesin2 complex–mediated process.

Psv shows overlapping subcellular localization with the IFT-A complex in vivo

The genetic and physical interactions between Psv and the IFT-A/Kinesin2 complex suggested that these proteins function together in the same cellular complex. To address this, we generated a specific antibody against Psv, which recognizes endogenous Psv protein in both Western blots and in fixed tissues in vivo (fig. S5; see Materials and Methods for details). We costained Psv with IFT140-myc or Klp64D-hemagglutinin (HA) (Kinesin2) in Drosophila wing imaginal discs and naïve SG cells. In wing discs, Psv was detected in intracellular puncta in cells near the wing margin (Fig. 4, A and B), where Wg signaling is active (also fig. S5). Notably, these puncta costained quite extensively with IFT140-myc (Fig. 4, A to Ac; also fig. S5E; quantified in fig. S5D) and also displayed partial overlap with Klp64D-HA staining (Fig. 4, B to Bc, and fig. S5F; quantified in fig. S5D). While the observed colocalization in wing imaginal disc cells is in a tissue that is exposed to Wg signaling, we also analyzed this in “naïve” cells that are not exposed to Wg. SG cells serve this purpose, as they only get exposed to Wg when it is induced via the Gal4 system. In “naïve” SG cells, endogenous Arm is only detected at adherens junctions (AJs) (Fig. 4, Cb and Db). Notably, in these cells, partially overlapping localization of Psv and IFT140-HA is observed in cytoplasmic puncta (Fig. 4, C to Cc). Similarly, partial cytoplasmic colocalization to puncta was detected between Psv and Kinesin2/Klp64D-HA (Fig. 4, D to Dc; note that Klp64D-HA also associates with AJs, unlike Psv and IFT-A; Fig. 4Da).

Together, these data indicate that (i) Psv associates with the IFT-A complex (IFT140) in the cytoplasm in the presence or absence of active Wg signaling, and (ii) Psv does not localize to AJs and therefore does not associate with junctional Arm/β-cat in either cell type tested. These observations suggest that Psv forms complexes with IFT140 independently of Wg/Wnt signaling and that it only associates with Arm/β-cat upon Wg/Wnt-signaling exposure (see below).

The CRA of Psv is required for its role in Wg/Wnt signaling in vivo and in cancer cells

In a previous study with Twa1/Gid8, the protein was forced into the nucleus using an exogenous C-terminally added nuclear localization signal (NLS) to attempt to distinguish whether Twa1 regulates β-cat nuclear import or nuclear retention (23). Human Twa1/Gid8 was extended at its C terminus (downstream of the CRA domain) with a classical NLS, generating a Twa1-NLS construct that localized constitutively to nuclei independent of Wnt stimulation (23). Expression of Twa1-NLS in human embryonic kidney 293 cells was sufficient to increase nuclear β-cat levels, leading to the suggestion that forced nuclear localization of Twa1 promoted nuclear β-cat retention (23). To test whether the CRA domain plays a role in this, a related construct lacking the CRA (Twa1-ΔCRA-NLS) was generated. This mutant transgene, despite having an NLS, failed to promote nuclear β-cat accumulation (or transcriptional activity), suggesting that the CRA domain is essential for Twa1/Gid8–mediated nuclear β-cat retention (23).

As we show here that Psv is essential for nuclear Arm localization in a Wg/Wnt signaling–dependent manner, we wished to identify the specific amino acids within the CRA domain required for this function. To this end, we mutated the longest conserved motif present within the CRA, LLAFD (fully conserved in all Gid8 family members, except houki; fig. S1A) to AAAFD (shown in fig. S6A as “LLAFDRPE to AAAFDRPE”), and compared the localization of wild-type Psv-green fluorescent protein (GFP) and the mutant protein Psvmut-GFP in SGs. Notably, overexpression of wild-type GFP-tagged Psv (C805-Gal4, UAS-Psv-GFP) displayed predominantly nuclear localization (fig. S6B). In contrast, (over)expressed Psvmut-GFP displayed cytoplasmic localization and even nuclear exclusion in the same SG assay (fig. S6B), indicating that the CRA domain mediates nuclear localization and that the LLAFD motif is critical. Consistently, Western blot analysis of SG nuclear extracts revealed nuclear enrichment of Psv-GFP, whereas Psvmut-GFP was not detected in nuclear fractions (fig. S6C). To assess whether Wg signaling can induce nuclear localization of endogenous Psv, we tested SGs that expressed Wg (with LepB treatment). Under these conditions, endogenous Psv accumulated in the nucleus (fig. S6, D and F), similar to nuclear Arm localization [see above and (18)]. Nuclear localization of Psv depended on Wg induction and was absent in control SGs (fig. S6, E and F).

As Twa1/Gid8 was identified as a RanBPM binding protein and the Ran guanosine triphosphatase is critical for nuclear translocation, we next asked whether Ran can affect Wg signaling–induced nuclear Arm localization in SGs (via C805-Gal4>UAS-RanIR). While knockdown of Ran had no effect in naïve SGs (in the absence of Wg) on junctional Arm (fig. S6G; comparable to psvIR; fig. S2, G and H), in the presence of Wg induction, Ran knockdown abolished nuclear Arm accumulation (fig. S6H; compare to control in Fig. 1D). Together, these results indicate that the CRA domain (and a specific motif within it) was essential for its Wg/Wnt–induced nuclear localization and that this function is Ran-dependent (see Discussion).

Next, we tested whether a mutant CRA can interfere with the assumed Psv function in Wg/Wnt signaling in vivo by expressing Psvmut in developing wing imaginal discs (Fig. 5, A to Ca). Notably, expression of Psvmut in developing wings leads to a loss of wing margin cells and marked reduction in cells expressing Sens (Fig. 5, C and Ca; note that equivalent expression of wild-type Psv had no effect; Fig. 5, B and Ba; quantified in fig. S8A). To confirm its function in the Arm nuclear translocation context, we compared the effect of wild-type Psv and Psvmut on a mild armIR knockdown [Fig. 5 (D and Da) shows armIR alone as control]. While overexpression of wild-type Psv largely rescued the C96>armIR defects (Fig. 5, E and Ea), coexpression of Psvmut markedly enhanced wing margin and wing tissue loss in adults and loss of Sens-expressing cells in discs (Fig. 5, F and Fa; quantified in fig. S8A). These data indicate that the CRA motif in Psv is critical for its function in the Wg/Wnt-signaling context for β-cat/Arm localization.

To address whether this role of the Psv CRA and CRA motif is conserved in a mammalian setting, we used Wnt signaling–dependent/mutant human cancer cell lines and assayed here the equivalent mutant isoform to Psvmut within the human ortholog Gid8 as Gid8mut. We tested cancer cell lines that are either addicted to autocrine Wnt signaling (HCC1395; with autocrine Wnt3a expression; Fig. 5G) or cell lines that are mutant showing constitutive Wnt-signaling activity independent of ligand [HCT116 cells with a mutant stable β-cat or DLD1 cells with mutant APC; (40–43)]. Expression of the mutant form, Gid8mut, in any of these cell lines markedly reduced the expression of the TOP/FOP Wnt reporter (Fig. 5, I, K, and M). Consistently, transfection of Gid8mut blocked proliferation in these cancer cell lines in a functional growth assay [Fig. 5 (H and J) for HCC1395 cells and Fig. 5 (L and N) for HCT116 and DLD1, respectively].

Together with the Drosophila in vivo data, this confirmed a conserved requirement for Psv/Gid8 in Wnt signaling in general. Furthermore, these experiments established that the conserved motif within the CRA domain is functional and critically required for its Wg/Wnt signaling–associated function, both in Drosophila and human contexts. They further provide insight that Psv/Gid8 mutants can serve as inhibitors of Wnt signaling in vivo and mutant cancer contexts.

Psv affects nuclear localization and signaling activity of stable β-cat/Arm mutations

As the premise of this study originated from the observation that the very stable deletion mutant of β-cat, ArmS10 [which cannot bind to IFT140 (18), in contrast to Arm*, which is a point mutation in the priming kinase target site (44)], is still at least partially capable of nuclear translocation, we asked whether interfering with both the IFT-A complex and psv could block ArmS10 nuclear entry. We first tested this in the wing margin patterning assay (Fig. 6, A to Da), where ArmS10 expression induced many ectopic margin bristles (Fig. 6C) and supernumerary Sens-expressing cells near the prospective wing margin in wing discs (Fig. 6Ca). Notably, RNAi-mediated double knockdown of both psv and ift140 in an ArmS10 background (C96>ArmS10, >psvIR, >ift140IR) rescued the ArmS10 gain-of-function (GOF) defects [Fig. 6, D and Da; quantified in fig. S8 (B to D); similarly, C96>psvIR, >ift140IR fully suppressed the Arm* phenotype; fig. S7 (A to Da); quantified in fig. S8 (B to D)]. We next analyzed nuclear translocation of ArmS10 in SGs using the reliable nuclear translocation assay. Without Wg expression, β-cat/Arm is only detected at AJs, while when Wg expression is induced, nuclear β-cat/Arm is detected (Fig 1D) [also (18, 21)]. Psv and IFT140 physically associate with distinct regions of β-cat/Arm

To get additional mechanistic insight, we dissected the Psv association with β-cat/Arm and requirement for specific regions in β-cat/Arm in this physical interaction (Fig. 6I). Direct in vitro pulldown assays with Psv and β-cat/Arm established that full-length β-cat/Arm and an N-terminal β-cat/Arm fragment bind Psv (Fig. 6I), while the Psv interaction with β-cat/Arm was not mediated by the Arm34–87 N-terminal Arm region (Fig. 6I, lane 5), previously shown to be necessary and sufficient for interaction with IFT140 (21). In contrast, we detected Psv to directly bind to Arm residues 76 to 150 within the N-terminal domain (Arm76–150; Fig. 6J, lane 4), whereas no interaction was detected with Arm1–75 (Fig. 6J, lane 3). Thus, the Psv-binding region within the N-terminal domain of Arm is distinct from and adjacent to Arm34–87, which binds to IFT140 (21). Together, these data suggest that β-cat/Arm interacts simultaneously with Psv and IFT140 through neighboring (nonoverlapping) sequences in its N-terminal region.

To get functional insight, we tested the relationship of Arm34–87, which acts as dominant negative for Wg/Wnt signaling by interfering with IFT140 binding to endogenous β-cat/Arm (21). According to the dominant-negative behavior of Arm34–87, overexpression of IFT140 in the Arm34–87 background rescued its effect (Fig. 6, K, L, and N). In contrast to IFT140, overexpression of Psv did not rescue the phenotype caused by Arm34–87 expression (Fig. 6M; compare to Fig. 6L; Psv alone showed no defects in a wt background; Fig. 5E). Together, all these data are consistent with a model that IFT140 and Psv are both required for nuclear β-cat/Arm translocation and that they act together, not redundantly or interchangeably, and bind to adjacent regions within the N-terminal β-cat/Arm domain (see the model in Fig. 7 and Discussion).

DISCUSSION

In this study, we define the function of a conserved protein, Pasovec (Psv in Drosophila and Gid8 in humans), within the Wg/Wnt-β-cat pathway and its requirement for nuclear translocation of β-cat/Arm. Our data reveal that Psv and its human ortholog Twa1/Gid8 [suggested to interact with RanBPM via its CRA domain (23)] contain a specific motif, within the CRA, required for its nuclear localization. Psv directly binds to both the N-terminal region of β-cat/Arm and IFT140 in the process. β-cat is a multifunctional protein that (i) associates with AJs and links these to the actin cytoskeleton and (ii) is required in canonical Wg/Wnt signaling, where it acts as the key nuclear effector. Although entry of cytoplasmic β-cat/Arm into the nucleus is critical in Wnt-signal transduction, β-cat/Arm does not contain an NLS. We show that Psv is required for the nuclear localization of β-cat/Arm. When, in Drosophila Psv or the human ortholog Gid8, the respective motif within the CRA is mutated, it serves as a dominant inhibitor of Wnt signaling. Psv also associates with IFT140 within the IFT-A complex [which is itself required for nuclear translocation of β-cat/Arm (18)] in the cytoplasm independent of Wnt-pathway activation and is a critical component of the machinery promoting nuclear β-cat/Arm translocation. Our data indicate that Psv and IFT-A need to work together to promote nuclear β-cat/Arm localization upon Wg/Wnt-signaling activation.

Psv/Gid8 proteins are required for Wg/Wnt signaling

The earlier failure to identify Psv as a component of Wg/Wnt signaling might be unexpected. It is encoded by a small gene and thus could have been missed in genetic screens for patterning mutations, which were not fully saturating (45). Moreover, psv has a significant maternal contribution, and thus, its zygotic-only LOF phenotype is milder than its full (maternal and zygotic) LOF feature. Many of the screens and definitions of critical components of canonical Wg/Wnt signaling were associated either with genetic modifier screens in Drosophila or by molecular and biochemical assays in Xenopus [reviewed in (46–48)]. For example, in Drosophila, a GOF misexpression Wg phenotype in the eye screened for modifiers identified the TCF family transcription factor pangolin and associated transcriptional cofactors Legless and Pygopus (49, 50). Similarly, GOF molecular screens identified several Wnt-pathway components in the frog system (46–48). While the function of Psv/Gid8 would have likely been missed in GOF assays in Xenopus, it could have been found in a suppressor screen in the Drosophila eye (49, 50). Nonetheless, no screening approach—genetic or molecular—is likely to identify all factors and components of a process, and as such, it is expected that additional pathway components, in any context, can still be found. As such, genetic screens in model organisms like Drosophila remain very valuable to complement the vast data coming from molecular “omics” approaches.

The Gid8 family has been studied extensively as a core component of the CTLH/GID ubiquitin-ligase complex in yeast and mammals, with roles in diverse processes including cell proliferation, metabolism, and regulation of adhesion proteins (51, 52). LisH domains in other proteins have also been shown to bind cytoplasmic dynein, raising the possibility that Psv/Gid8 family proteins may also engage in microtubule-associated trafficking (53–57). Prior work has also proposed that Twa1/Gid8 promotes nuclear retention of β-cat, providing a link to Wnt signaling (23). Our data here reveal that its role in Wnt/β-cat signaling is to promote nuclear translocation of β-cat, not its retention, which, depending on the assay, might have been confused. Our data also suggest that Psv/Gid8 acts independently from its CTLH-mediated ubiquitination function in the Wnt/β-cat nuclear localization context. Notably, Drosophila encodes two Gid8 paralogs, Psv (this work) and Houki (Hou) (34). Knockdown of hou in developing wings (either via C96-Gal4>houIR or en-Gal4>houIR) does not visibly affect Wg/Wnt β-cat/Arm signaling, whereas it perturbs Wnt/PCP–mediated patterning. Recently, a proteomic screen in Drosophila wings identified Hou (CG6617) as a candidate regulator of Wnt/PCP establishment (58), consistent with a CTLH-associated function in cytoskeletal dynamics, polarity establishment, and spatial protein (de)stabilization. In addition, Gid8 was independently identified as a Dishevelled-associated protein via mass spectrometry analysis in human cells (59), consistent with the notion that Gid8 proteins might participate in a broader regulatory network controlling PCP, in line with a CTLH-complex role. Together with our results, these studies suggest that the two Drosophila paralogs, Psv and Hou, despite their shared homology, have distinct contributions, with Psv acting in nuclear localization of β-cat/Arm (via a CRA-associated motif that is less conserved in Hou), independent of a CTLH-linked function, and Hou acts (possibly) via CTLH in other outputs, including regulating Dsh in PCP signaling [(58); for reviews on Dsh and the Wnt/PCP pathway, see (60–65)]. This further suggests that Psv and Hou differentially couple CTLH-complex activities and/or dynein-based processes, which can be tested further in future work.

What is the functional relationship of IFT-A and Psv in Wnt signaling?

Classic work revealed that β-cat can accumulate in the nucleus independently of conventional NLSs or importin-mediated mechanisms (66). More recently, the definition and mechanistic dissection of the cytoplasmic role of IFT-A in Wnt signaling and nuclear β-cat/Arm localization have been a breakthrough to start understanding the process (18). However, the observation that the stable deletion mutant ArmS10, which lacks the N-terminal region required for IFT140/IFT-A binding, can still enter the nucleus suggested that other factors and/or mechanisms are possibly participating in β-cat/Arm nuclear import (18). Potential additional insight might be associated with the C-terminal region of Arm/β-cat, where a PY-NLS motif (67) has been proposed and suggested to be required for β-cat nuclear localization (68), although this motif is not conserved in Drosophila Arm/β-cat. Similarly, the C terminus of region of β-cat has been linked to nuclear transport via Importin 11 in APC-mutant CRC cell lines, which are Wnt ligand independent and constitutively active (69). However, the reduction of activity via the C-terminal region appears partial in both scenarios, and while intriguing, it remains unclear how universal these requirements within the C-terminal β-cat region are.

We demonstrate here that in a double-mutant scenario, with both Psv and IFT-A impaired, nuclear localization of ArmS10 is inhibited. At first, this might suggest that the IFT-A complex and Psv share partially redundant roles in the process. However, in mutant clones of either psv (this study) or IFT140 (18), Wg-signaling targets, like Sens, are not expressed, indicating a nonredundant function for both factors. Similarly, psv mutant embryos strongly resemble the phenotypic features of arm mutant embryos that affect Wg signaling, and in the SG nuclear localization assay, knockdown of either blocks nuclear translocation of endogenous β-cat/Arm. Moreover, (over)expression of the N-terminal region of β-cat/Arm, Arm34–87, or human β-cat24–79 peptides, which are necessary and sufficient to bind to IFT140, inhibit Wnt signaling very effectively in Drosophila and in human cancer cell lines by blocking nuclear β-cat/Arm translocation, including that of stable β-cat mutations that bind to IFT-A (21). Together, these results establish a nonredundant function. The observation that Psv binds to IFT140 and β-cat/Arm is consistent with the notion that it acts together with the IFT-A complex. Psv binds the β-cat/Arm N-terminal region in sequences adjacent to and nonoverlapping with the Arm34–87 fragment, which binds IFT140, implying a simultaneous association (see the model in Fig. 7).

The outlier in the logic of nonredundant functions of IFT140/IFT-A and Psv is again the ArmS10 deletion. However, ArmS10 might behave somewhat differently, as it would escape cytoplasmic retention mediated by N-terminal sequences because of the nature of its deletion. In this context, it is noteworthy that while the effects of ArmS10 are strongly suppressed by the double knockdown of IFT140 and psv, the suppression in vivo does not drop below “wild-type levels,” as represented by a normal wing margin (see Fig. 6D), suggesting that some ArmS10 still escapes the suppression, providing signaling levels near wild-type levels. As ArmS10 is extremely stable, it is possible that low background levels of translocation, not using the regular mechanism, are sufficient to maintain some signaling.

The Psv CRA provides a unique function within the IFT-A/Psv/β-cat complex

While IFT-A is critical for the nuclear translocation of β-cat/Arm and its association with cytoplasmic microtubules, it lacks any means to engage the nuclear import machinery. Thus, within the IFT-A/Psv/β-cat complex described here, Psv likely serves as a key factor for providing this function via its CRA domain. The CRA has been proposed to interact with RanBPM (23), and we show here that a Ran knockdown interferes with nuclear translocation of β-cat/Arm in the SG assay, with the CRA appearing critical in the process. The observation that (over)expression of Psv/Gid8 with a mutant CRA can interfere with nuclear translocation of β-cat/Arm in Drosophila and human cells, in analogy to a dominant interference tool, provides additional evidence for its critical requirement besides the IFT-A/Kinesin2 complex. In summary, nuclear translocation of β-cat/Arm can be effectively interfered with from both sides, (over)expressing either (i) the N-terminal peptide of β-cat/Arm, Arm34–87, or human β-cat24–79, which blocks its interaction with IFT-A, or (ii) Psv/Gid8 with a mutant CRA. The definition of an importin-independent mechanism for nuclear translocation of β-cat/Arm (66) and the critical IFT-A involvement maintain the unique mechanism required for β-cat/Arm nuclear import.

Cuticle preparation and wing mounting

Embryos were collected from the indicated crosses on apple agar plates supplemented with fresh yeast paste. Embryos were dechorionated with 50% bleach for 2 min and rinsed extensively with water. For cuticle preparation, dechorionated embryos were mounted in Hoyer’s medium (73), incubated at 60°C overnight, and viewed with a Zeiss Axio-Imager microscope. All adult images were acquired using a Zeiss Axiocam color-type 412-312 camera and Zeiss Axiocam software.

Immunostaining and histology

Imaginal discs were dissected at the third instar larval stage in PBS and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (EMS, 15714-S) in PBS. Discs were washed two times in PBS and 0.1% Triton X-100 and incubated in primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. After washing in PBS and 0.1% Triton X-100, incubation with secondary antibodies was at room temperature for 2 hours. Samples were mounted in Vectashield (Vector Lab, H-1200). Wing disc images were acquired with a confocal microscope (20× to 40×, oil immersion, Leica SP8 or Zeiss LSM980 system). Images were processed with ImageJ (National Institutes of Health) and assembled in Photoshop (Adobe).

SGs were dissected at the third instar larval stage in PBS and treated with 0.1% Leptomycin B (Sigma-Aldrich, L2913-5UG) for 5 to 10 min before fixation in PBS and 4% paraformaldehyde. All subsequent steps were as described for wing imaginal discs.

Immunoprecipitation

Lysates from 30 wing imaginal discs of nub>IFT140myc, nub>IFT144myc; Psv or GFP or SGs C805>Psv; C805>PsvIR or C805>lacZ were precleared by incubating with protein A-Agarose beads (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 20333) for 1 hour at 4°C followed by centrifugation. Protein A-Agarose beads were immunoprecipitated with specific antibodies at 4°C for 1 hour. A polyclonal anti-myc antibody and anti-Psv or anti-Arm antibody were used. Immunoprecipitates were resuspended in SDS sample buffer, boiled for 5 min, separated by SDS–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis, and transferred to nitrocellulose for immunoblotting. Protein was detected by the Immobilon Forte Western HRP Substrate kit (Millipore, WBLUF0500).

Cell lines and culture conditions

Wnt3a-expressing L-cells and control L-cells were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (Gibco, 11965092) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Thermo Fisher Scientific, A5256701) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin (Gibco, 15140-122). Wnt3A and control L-cells were prepared as described by a protocol provided by American Type Culture Collection. Human cancer cell lines DLD1 and HCT116 were grown in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium supplemented with 10% FBS. The HCC1395 cell line was grown in RPMI 1640 medium (Millipore Sigma, R8758) supplemented with 10% FBS. All cells were cultured at 37°C in 5% CO2. All cell lines were provided by S. Aaronson.

Luciferase reporter assay for β-cat activity (TOP/FOP reporter assay)

Human full-length β-cat, Gid8mut, or lacZ was cloned into pcDNA4/TO plasmids (gifts from S. Aaronson). For all cancer cell lines, cells were seeded at a density of 4 × 105 cells per 12-well plate 1 day before transfection. Cells were then transfected by Lipofectin (Invitrogen, A12621) with plasmid constructs [LacZ, full-length β-cat (Addgene, 14717), or Gid8mut: 1 μg each)] together with 2 μg of pGL-TOP or its negative control vector pGL-FOP. For HCC1395, HCT116, or DLD1 cell lines (gifts from S. Aaronson), after 24 hours, Wnt3A condition medium was added to the cells and incubated for an additional 24 hours. The treated cells were then washed and lysed using 20 μl of luciferase lysis buffer per well, and luciferase activities were performed and measured in 96-well plates using the Dual-Luciferase Reporter Assay (Promega E1910) system according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The measurement was conducted on a Synergy MX luminometer. Experiments were carried out in triplicate and repeated at least three times, shown as the means ± SD of the ratio between the TOP/FOP.

Cell proliferation assay

All cancer cell lines were seeded in 24-well plates (2 × 104 cells per well). After 24 hours, cells were transfected with lacZ or Gid8mut. At the indicated time points posttransfection (1 to 5 days), cell proliferation was determined by the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyl-tetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Abcam, Ab211091). Briefly, the MTT reagent was added to each well to a final concentration of 0.5 mg/ml, and plates were incubated for 2 hours at 37°C to allow the formation of formazan crystals. The supernatant was then removed, and formazan crystals were dissolved in 750 μl of MTT solvent with gentle shaking for 15 min. The absorbance at 570 nm was read on a microplate reader. All assays were performed in triplicate.

Quantitative and statistical analyses

Wing discs

Wing imaginal disc staining was processed using Image J. To establish an appropriate quantification of expression/staining signal, Sens intensity was normalized by “average Sens/Dll pixel intensity” (“+” cells) minus “average background pixel intensity outside Sens/Dll expression domain” (“−” cells). Mean measurements were plotted for 10 wing discs or 3 different clones. For statistical analyses, a two-tailed test was performed on normalized mean intensity measurements to compare genotypes.

Adult wing margin loss

Wing margin defects were quantified form bright-field images of adult wings using ImageJ. For each wing, the scale was calibrated on the basis of the imaging metadata. The entire wing margin was traced from the anterior to posterior wing region to obtain the total margin length. Individual missing (notched) margin regions were similarly traced to measure the length of missing margin tissue. The percentage of missing margin was calculated as (notched length/total margin length) × 100. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism software. Comparisons between two groups were conducted using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test, while comparisons among multiple genotypes were performed using a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s multiple comparisons test. Statistical significance was defined as *P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, and ***P < 0.001.

Embryo genotyping, identification, and quantification

Embryos carrying balancer chromosomes were identified using associated fluorescent markers, including Kruppel-GFP (Kr-GFP) or actin-GFP (act-GFP), depending on the cross. Embryos positive for the GFP signal were excluded from analysis. Embryos lacking GFP expression were the experimental genotype for each cross and were used for quantification. For each genotype, a minimum of 80 to 100 experimental embryos were analyzed. Penetrance was calculated as the proportion of experimental embryos displaying the phenotype out of the total number scored.
