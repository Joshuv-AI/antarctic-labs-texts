Performance and Cost-Efficiency of an MPT64 Assay for Detecting Mycobacterium tuberculosis Complex in a Setting With Frequent Nontuberculous Mycobacteria Isolation
Authors: Hui-Jin Yu, Jung-ah Kim, Hyun Mi Song, Kyoung Ah Lee, Heejung Kim, In Young Yoo, Tae Yeul Kim, Hee Jae Huh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundRapid and accurate detection of Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC) in acid-fast bacilli (AFB)-positive cultures is crucial for effective patient management and transmission control. This study assessed the diagnostic performance and cost-efficiency of the Bioline MPT64 assay for detecting MTBC in AFB-positive cultures in a setting with frequent nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) isolation.MethodsBetween August 2024 and February 2025, AFB-positive cultures were tested in parallel using the Bioline MPT64 assay and real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR). The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) of the Bioline MPT64 assay were calculated using real-time PCR as the reference standard. Cost-efficiency of diagnostic strategies incorporating the Bioline MPT64 assay was also evaluated.ResultsBased on real-time PCR results, among 494 AFB-positive cultures, 192 (38.9%) were MTBC-positive, 299 (60.5%) were NTM-positive, 2 (0.4%) were positive for both MTBC and NTM, and 1 (0.2%) was negative for both. The Bioline MPT64 assay exhibited a sensitivity of 99.0% (192/194), specificity of 100% (300/300), PPV of 100% (192/192), and NPV of 99.3% (300/302). Two false-negative results occurred in MTBC-NTM co-positive cultures. Using the Bioline MPT64 assay alone could reduce costs by 66.7% compared to real-time PCR alone but carries a risk of missing approximately 1.0% of MTBC-positive cultures. A stepwise strategy—initial screening with the Bioline MPT64 assay followed by real-time PCR for MPT64-negative cultures—could offer diagnostic accuracy comparable to real-time PCR alone but result in only a modest 5.5% cost reduction.ConclusionThe Bioline MPT64 assay shows strong concordance with real-time PCR and offers substantial cost savings when used alone, though it may miss MTBC-NTM co-positive cultures. PCR confirmation of MPT64-negative results mitigates this risk but provides only modest cost savings in NTM-prevalent settings.

INTRODUCTION

Tuberculosis (TB), an infectious disease caused by the Mycobacterium tuberculosis complex (MTBC), remains a leading cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide. According to the World Health Organization’s Global Tuberculosis Report 2024, an estimated 10.8 million TB cases and 1.25 million TB-related deaths occurred in 2023.1 In Korea, although the number of TB cases has steadily declined in recent years, the disease continues to pose a major public health threat, with 19,540 cases and 1,331 deaths reported in 2023.23 Despite being preventable and curable, delayed diagnosis can lead to prolonged treatment and increased transmission, resulting in substantial economic and social challenges.4 Given this significant burden, implementing rapid, simple, and affordable TB diagnostic methods is essential.

Various molecular diagnostic techniques, including real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR), PCR-restriction fragment length polymorphism, and DNA probe-based assays, are widely used to detect MTBC in acid-fast bacilli (AFB)-positive cultures. These methods offer high sensitivity and specificity and enable reliable differentiation between MTBC and nontuberculous mycobacteria (NTM), as well as identification of specific NTM species.56 However, they are often complex, expensive, and require specialized equipment and trained personnel, limiting their use in resource-limited settings.7 As an alternative, immunochromatographic assays targeting the MPT64 protein secreted during the active growth phase of MTBC can be considered.8

MPT64 assays offer several advantages: they are low-cost, require no specialized equipment or advanced skills, and yield results within 30 minutes, enabling prompt decision-making.91011 Commercially available MPT64 assays—such as Bioline TB Ag MPT64 (Abbott Inc., Yongin, Korea), Capilia TB-Neo (TAUNS Laboratories Inc., Izunokuni, Japan), and MGIT TBc Identification Test (BD, Sparks, MD, USA)—have demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity in previous studies.6911121314151617181920 However, false-negative results may occur in MPT64-negative MTBC strains or cultures with low MTBC loads, such as MTBC-NTM co-positive cultures.9112122 As a result, many laboratories use real-time PCR alone or adopt a stepwise strategy—initial screening with the MPT64 assay followed by real-time PCR for negative results—for accurate MTBC detection. Nevertheless, the diagnostic performance and cost-efficiency of these strategies have yet to be compared.

This study aimed to assess the diagnostic performance of the Bioline TB Ag MPT64 assay (Bioline MPT64 assay) and, based on our data, to compare the cost-efficiency of diagnostic strategies for MTBC detection in hospital settings where NTM is more frequently isolated than MTBC.

METHODS

AFB cultures

This study included respiratory specimens submitted for routine AFB culture at the Clinical Microbiology Laboratory of Seoul Medical Center, Seoul, Korea, between August 2024 and February 2025. Respiratory specimens were decontaminated using the standard N-acetyl-L-cysteine–2% NaOH method, followed by centrifugation at 3,000 × g for 20 minutes at 4°C. After discarding the supernatant, the sediment was resuspended in 2 mL of phosphate buffer (pH 6.8). Aliquots of 500 μL of the resulting suspension were inoculated into mycobacterial growth indicator tubes (MGIT; BD) and incubated in the BACTEC MGIT 960 system (BD) for up to 6 weeks. Additionally, 200 μL aliquots were inoculated onto 2% Ogawa agar (Kyokuto Pharmaceutical Industrial Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan) and incubated for up to 8 weeks. For positive cultures, Ziehl-Neelsen staining with 1% carbol fuchsin was performed. AFB-positive cultures were then tested in parallel using the Bioline MPT64 assay and real-time PCR (Anyplex Plus MTB/NTM Detection Kit; Seegene, Seoul, Korea). Cultures that showed growth but were confirmed as AFB-negative by Ziehl-Neelsen staining were considered contaminated and excluded from this study. In our hospital, because Ziehl-Neelsen staining, the MPT64 assay, and real-time PCR were performed once a week, the maximum time gap between MGIT positivity and MPT64 testing (and real-time PCR) was 7 days. MPT64 assay

The Bioline MPT64 assay was conducted following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, 100 μL of liquid culture was directly applied to the sample wells. For solid cultures, three to four colonies were suspended in 200 μL of extraction buffer, and 100 μL of the suspension was transferred to the wells. The test kits were incubated at room temperature for 15 minutes. A positive result was indicated by a band on the test line in addition to the control line. If a band did not appear in the control line, the test was considered invalid. Interpretation of the MPT64 assay was conducted in a blind manner by technicians who were unaware of the real-time PCR results.

Real-time PCR

Real-time PCR was performed using the Anyplex Plus MTB/NTM Detection Kit (Seegene, Seoul, Korea) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, DNA was extracted using the boiling method. The PCR reaction was carried out in a final volume of 20 μL, comprising 5 μL of extracted DNA and 15 μL of master mix. Amplification was performed on a CFX96 real-time PCR system (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA) using the following cycling conditions: 95°C for 15 minutes, followed by 45 cycles of 95°C for 30 seconds and 60°C for 1 minute.

This assay targets the IS6110 region and mpb64 gene for MTBC detection (FAM channel) and the 16S rRNA gene as a pan-Mycobacterium target for NTM detection (Cal Red 610 channel). A culture was classified as MTBC-positive when the MTBC target was positive (Ct value ≤ 40), regardless of the pan-Mycobacterium target result. It was considered NTM-positive when the pan-Mycobacterium target was positive (Ct value ≤ 38) and the MTBC target was negative. If both targets were positive and the Ct value for the MTBC target was higher than that for the pan-Mycobacterium target, the culture was classified as MTBC–NTM co-positive.

Analysis of cultures with discordant results

In cultures where the results of the Bioline MPT64 assay and real-time PCR were discordant, both tests were repeated. Cultures with persistent discordant results after retesting were classified as discordant. Additionally, AFB-positive cultures that tested negative for both MTBC and NTM by real-time PCR were identified using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS; MALDI Biotyper, Bruker, Bremen, Germany) and confirmed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing, as previously described.23

Analytical specificity evaluation of the Bioline MPT64 assay

Cross-reactivity of the Bioline MPT64 assay was evaluated using 73 reference strains: 64 NTM and 9 non-mycobacterial AFB-positive strains (Gordonia spp., Nocardia spp., Rhodococcus spp., and Tsukamurella spp.) (Supplementary Table 1). The reference strains were first subcultured onto Middlebrook 7H10 agar, and suspensions adjusted to a McFarland 0.5 standard were inoculated into MGIT tubes and incubated in the BACTEC MGIT 960 system. The Bioline MPT64 assay was performed in duplicate on positive cultures.

Data analysis

The diagnostic performance of the Bioline MPT64 assay for detecting MTBC was evaluated using real-time PCR as the reference standard. Sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Agreement between the two methods was assessed by Cohen’s kappa test and interpreted according to established criteria.24 Cost estimates were based exclusively on the local purchase prices of the Bioline MPT64 assay and real-time PCR assay (USD 4 and USD 12 per test, respectively), with labor, overhead, and instrument costs excluded. Additionally, we modeled how cost estimates vary with the proportion of MTBC. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software version 4.4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Ethics statement

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul Medical Center (approval No. SEOUL 2024-05-011), which waived the requirement for informed consent due to the use of deidentified patient data and the absence of additional sample collection.

RESULTS

Patient demographics and specimen characteristics

A total of 494 AFB-positive cultures—269 from liquid media and 225 from solid media—were included in the final analysis. These cultures were obtained from 204 individual patients, of whom 55.4% were male and 44.6% female. Patient ages ranged from 13 to 96 years, with a median of 75 years. The most common specimen type was sputum (85.2%), followed by bronchial washings (13.4%), pleural fluid (1.0%), and bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (0.4%). Based on real-time PCR results, of the 494 AFB-positive cultures, 192 (38.9%) were identified as MTBC-positive, 299 (60.5%) as NTM-positive, 2 (0.4%) as MTBC–NTM co-positive, and 1 (0.2%) as negative for both MTBC and NTM.

Performance of the Bioline MPT64 assay

The performance of the Bioline MPT64 assay compared with real-time PCR was assessed separately for solid and liquid cultures, as well as for the combined total, as shown in Table 1. When results from both culture types were combined, the sensitivity of the Bioline MPT64 assay was 99.0% (192/194; 95% CI, 96.3–99.9), and specificity was 100% (300/300; 95% CI, 98.8–100). The PPV was 100% (192/192; 95% CI, 98.1–100), and the NPV was 99.3% (300/302; 95% CI, 97.6–99.9). The kappa coefficient between the two methods was 0.992 (95% CI, 0.980–1.000), indicating almost perfect agreement. The performance was equivalent for solid and liquid cultures, except for two liquid culture cases showing discrepancies: real-time PCR confirmed MTBC–NTM co-infection, while the MPT64 assay returned negative results. Upon retesting, these discrepant results persisted. The Ct values obtained by real-time PCR for the MTBC target were 30.0 and 37.3, respectively, indicating a low MTBC burden. Of note, one AFB-positive culture tested negative for both MTBC and NTM by real-time PCR and was also negative by the MPT64 assay. Further MALDI-TOF MS analysis identified the isolate as Nocardia farcinica, which was confirmed by 16S rRNA gene sequencing. No cross-reactivity was observed in the Bioline MPT64 assay when tested with 73 reference strains (Supplementary Table 1).

Cost-efficiency analysis

Fig. 2 illustrates the cost-efficiency of three diagnostic workflows currently used in clinical microbiology laboratories in Korea for detecting MTBC in AFB-positive cultures. The first strategy is to use real-time PCR alone for MTBC detection. Although this approach incurs the highest total cost (USD 5,928) due to exclusive use of the more expensive PCR assay, it offers high sensitivity and enables accurate detection of MTBC in MTBC–NTM co-positive cultures, which represent approximately 1% of MTBC-positive cases. The second strategy is to use the MPT64 assay alone. This approach reduces the total cost by 66.7% (from USD 5,928 to USD 1,976) by avoiding the use of real-time PCR; however, it carries a risk of false-negative results in MTBC–NTM co-positive cultures. Additionally, unlike strategies incorporating PCR, it cannot distinguish NTM from non-mycobacterial AFB-positive organisms, such as Nocardia species, which accounted for approximately 0.3% of MPT64-negative cultures. The third strategy is a stepwise approach using the MPT64 assay as a screening test, followed by real-time PCR for MPT64-negative results. While this approach eliminates the risk of false-negative results, it yields only a modest cost reduction of 5.5% (from USD 5,928 to USD 5,600), as the majority of AFB-positive cultures are MPT64-negative (302/494, 61.1%) and therefore require additional PCR testing. The cost of this strategy increases as the proportion of MTBC decreases, exceeding that of the real-time PCR–only strategy when the proportion falls below 33.3% (Fig. 3).

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the Bioline MPT64 assay and cost-efficiency of diagnostic strategies incorporating this assay in a hospital setting with frequent NTM isolation. We found that the MPT64 assay has high diagnostic accuracy, and using it alone could offer substantial cost savings compared to real-time PCR alone or a stepwise strategy (initial screening with the MPT64 assay followed by real-time PCR for negative results). However, it may miss MTBC-NTM co-infections, potentially delaying appropriate treatment and increasing the risk of transmission. These limitations should be carefully considered when using the assay alone.

In our study, the Bioline MPT64 assay demonstrated outstanding diagnostic accuracy, achieving a sensitivity of 99% and a specificity of 100% for identifying MTBC, consistent with the performance of MPT64 assays reported in previous studies.6911121314151617181920 The high specificity of MPT64 assays suggests that additional confirmatory testing for MPT64-positive cultures is generally unnecessary. However, Chikamatsu et al.16 reported cross-reactivity with certain rare NTM species, including M. brumae (Bioline MPT64 assay) and M. aichiense, M. chitae, M. gadium, and M. marinum (MGIT TBc Identification Test). The possibility of false-positive results due to such cross-reactivity should be taken into consideration when using MPT64 assays. Notably, unlike the previous report, our study observed no cross-reactivity of the Bioline MPT64 assay with the same type of strain of M. brumae (ATCC 51384 = KCTC 19711). To better assess this potential cross-reactivity, further studies involving various strains are warranted.

MPT64 assays are rapid, simple, and affordable diagnostic tools; however, the potential for false-negative results limits their utility in clinical practice. In our study, the Bioline MPT64 assay failed to detect MTBC in two cultures that were MTBC-NTM co-positive by real-time PCR, representing 1% of all MTBC-positive cultures. This limitation may be due to the relatively high limits of detection of MPT64 assays, which have been reported to range from 1 × 104 to 5 × 105 CFU/mL—substantially higher than those of real-time PCR.111920 Therefore, in settings where MTBC-NTM co-infections are common, MPT64-negative results should be interpreted with caution, and additional confirmatory testing is recommended, especially when clinical or radiological findings strongly suggest MTBC infection. In high-resource settings, MPT64 assays may be replaced by molecular methods such as real-time PCR, which, although more costly, offer superior sensitivity.

False-negative MPT64 assay results may also occur in MTBC strains with absent or reduced expression of the MPT64 protein, typically due to mutations in the mpb64 gene.131620212526272829 These mutations are often linked to specific MTBC lineages21272829; therefore, in regions where such lineages are prevalent, MPT64-negative cultures should be confirmed with additional identification methods before being classified as NTM. In Korea, only one case report has documented a mutation in the mpb64 gene (a 63-bp deletion).30 This deletion is linked to Lineage 4.2.2, particularly clade A strains found in Vietnam and China.21 Although a Lineage 4.2.2 strain has been identified in Korea, its classification within clade A has not been reported.31 Our study identified no MPT64-negative MTBC strains, suggesting that such strains, including those belonging to Lineage 4.2.2 clade A, are likely rare. However, because MPT64-negative strains can evade detection by MPT64-based assays, there is a risk of clonal selection and a gradual decline in assay performance.32 Ongoing surveillance is needed to monitor the emergence and spread of these strains.

Molecular methods such as real-time PCR are considered reliable for detecting MTBC in AFB-positive cultures, with the highly conserved insertion sequence IS6110 being the most commonly used target.33 However, using IS6110 as the sole target carries a risk of false-negative results, as some strains do not harbor this sequence; therefore, additional targets such as mpb64 should be included to improve detection accuracy.343536 Despite their high accuracy, the high cost of test kits used in molecular methods remains a major barrier to widespread adoption in clinical practice, particularly in resource-limited settings. In our study, a strategy relying solely on real-time PCR for MTBC detection was estimated to incur the highest cost. An alternative strategy using a less expensive MPT64 assay alone was expected to reduce the total cost by 66.7%. When labor and instrument costs are also considered, overall cost savings are likely to be even greater. However, despite the substantial cost savings, this strategy carries the risk of missing MTBC-NTM co-infections and MPT64-negative MTBC strains. Although these strains are currently rare in Korea, their prevalence could increase with rising international travel or immigration, and reliance solely on the MPT64 assay could lead to more missed TB diagnoses. Missed diagnoses delay treatment, a major predictor of poor clinical outcomes in TB patients.3738 Furthermore, each untreated person with active TB can infect, on average, 10–15 people per year,39 highlighting the substantial financial and public health burden of transmission from missed diagnoses. Future studies should estimate the potential costs of missed TB diagnoses and determine whether the cost savings from using the MPT64 assay alone outweigh these costs.

To mitigate the risk of false-negative results, an initial MPT64 assay followed by real-time PCR for MPT64-negative cultures may be used. In our study, this stepwise approach was expected to provide diagnostic accuracy comparable to that of real-time PCR alone, but the cost reduction was modest (5.5%) due to the high proportion of AFB-positive cultures requiring additional PCR testing. A previous study from a referral laboratory receiving specimens from multiple hospitals nationwide reported a higher MTBC proportion (53.8%) than the 38.9% observed at our institution,40 suggesting that the cost reduction from the stepwise strategy could be greater in most hospitals. Given the rising prevalence of NTM in Korea,4041 the cost burden of additional PCR testing is likely to grow over time, gradually reducing the cost-efficiency of the stepwise strategy. In hospitals with an MTBC proportion below 33.3%, the stepwise strategy may be more expensive than real-time PCR alone.

One key limitation of using an MPT64 assay alone is its inability to distinguish NTM from non-mycobacterial AFB-positive organisms such as Nocardia species. These organisms test negative on the MPT64 assay and may therefore be misclassified as NTM. As clinical outcomes and treatment strategies differ,42434445 MPT64-negative results should be confirmed using additional methods in settings where non-mycobacterial AFB-positive organisms are frequently encountered. In our study, only one such isolate (N. farcinica) was detected, accounting for approximately 0.3% of MPT64-negative cultures—a finding consistent with previous reports.4647 Given their low prevalence, the associated risk is considered modest.

As a single-center investigation, this study has limitations in the generalizability of its findings. Variations across institutions in the prevalence of MTBC, NTM, and MTBC-NTM co-infections, as well as differences in species and strain distribution, may affect the diagnostic performance and cost-efficiency of MPT64 assays. Although we modeled how costs vary with the proportion of MTBC, further multicenter studies using real-world data are needed to evaluate the diagnostic performance and cost-efficiency of MPT64 assays in diverse settings. Another limitation of this study is that cost estimates were based solely on the purchase prices of test kits. Comprehensive cost-efficiency analyses, including labor, overhead, and instrument costs, are needed to accurately assess the real-world utility of MPT64 assays. Lastly, the relatively small sample size and short study period limited our ability to detect MPT64-negative MTBC strains and evaluate cross-reactivity with rare NTM species. Larger, longer studies are needed to fully characterize assay performance under real-world conditions.

Our study demonstrates that the Bioline MPT64 assay shows strong concordance with real-time PCR and offers substantial cost savings when used alone, although it may fail to detect MTBC-NTM co-positive cultures. PCR confirmation of MPT64-negative results reduces this risk but yields only modest cost savings in hospital settings with frequent NTM isolation. Clinical laboratories should consider the local prevalence of MTBC, NTM, and MTBC-NTM co-infections, along with species and strain distribution, and adopt diagnostic strategies that achieve an optimal balance between accuracy and cost-efficiency in their settings.
