FOXC1 and Smooth Muscle Cell Plasticity in Human Atherosclerosis
Authors: Michal Mokry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corresponding Author

Despite extensive work in experimental systems, the clinical relevance of smooth muscle cell (SMC) plasticity in atherosclerosis remains only partially resolved. SMCs were long considered structural, stabilizing components of the vessel wall, but it is now clear that they can adopt a range of phenotypes that contribute both to plaque stability and progression. Translating this complexity into clinically meaningful insights has proven challenging.

In this issue of JACC: Basic to Translational Science, Rykaczewska et al1 identify FOXC1 as a transcriptional regulator linked to SMC phenotype and atherosclerotic disease. By integrating genetic, transcriptomic, and functional data, the authors show that FOXC1 associates with coronary artery disease risk; is down-regulated in human plaques, particularly in symptomatic lesions; and correlates with SMC-rich, more stable plaque characteristics.1 Functional experiments suggest that FOXC1 supports a quiescent, contractile SMC phenotype, whereas its repression promotes proliferation, migration, and cytoskeletal remodeling, consistent with SMC activation. FOXC1 is an evolutionarily conserved transcription factor with known roles in vascular development and in controlling cell state transitions, including quiescence, proliferation, and cytoskeletal organization.2,3 These functions are consistent with the phenotype described here. At the same time, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely defined, particularly how FOXC1 regulates these transitions and how this translates to plaque stability.

Beyond the identification of FOXC1, the study stands out for its integrative design. By combining genetic association data with plaque transcriptomics and cellular deconvolution, the authors link variation at the FOXC1 locus not only to disease risk but also to plaque cellular composition, particularly the balance between SMCs and macrophages.1 This creates a direct link between genetic signals and tissue-level biology, moving beyond association toward a more mechanistic interpretation, which is essential for translational relevance.

SMC plasticity has been well documented in mouse lineage-tracing studies, showing transitions toward macrophage- and fibroblast-like states.4 Human plaque studies, however, point to a broader and more complex range of SMC-derived phenotypes, often present within the same lesion and linked to clinically relevant features such as plaque stability and symptoms.5 The present study does not introduce a new form of plasticity, but rather places its regulation, through FOXC1, within the complexity of human disease. Although FOXC1 itself is conserved among vertebrates,6 the ability to connect its expression to plaque composition and clinical phenotype depends on human tissue. Experimental models remain essential for mechanistic work, but the aspects of SMC plasticity that matter clinically are best captured in human atherosclerosis. Human plaques reflect decades of disease progression, with accumulated extracellular matrix, lipid debris, and calcification, and are shaped by long-term exposure to systemic risk factors and local remodeling processes. These features, and their link to clinical presentation, are difficult to reproduce in current experimental systems. At the same time, as in much of SMC biology, these observations raise as many questions as they answer, particularly regarding whether modulation of these pathways ultimately stabilizes or destabilizes human plaques.

These insights rely on large, well-characterized human biobanks such as the Biobank of Karolinska Endarterectomies,7 which combines detailed plaque phenotyping with transcriptomic profiling. Other large-scale efforts in the field extend this framework in different directions. STARNET, eg, links vascular and metabolic tissues across large patient cohorts, enabling integration of genetic variation with tissue-level gene regulation in a way that is difficult to achieve in genetically homogeneous model systems.8 Athero-Express, in turn, connects plaque composition with clinical outcomes and has enabled systematic evaluation of molecular signatures in relation to disease progression.9 Together, these resources illustrate how human data sets can capture both interindividual variability and clinically relevant phenotypes that are largely inaccessible in experimental models. They also make it possible to study domains that have traditionally relied on animal systems, such as drug response, directly in human tissue, an approach that is only beginning to emerge.10 Experimental systems, by contrast, necessarily simplify this biology. It is therefore notable that the central observations of this study, linking FOXC1 expression to plaque composition and clinical phenotype, come directly from human data.1

Several limitations should be acknowledged. Although the association between FOXC1 and SMC phenotype is consistent across data sets, causality at the cellular level is not fully established. The focus on late-stage carotid plaques, while clinically relevant, limits insight into earlier disease stages. In addition, although pathways related to cytoskeletal organization, adhesion, and cell cycle control are implicated, the underlying mechanisms remain incompletely defined.

The experimental data complement the human findings. The dynamic regulation of Foxc1 in vascular injury models, together with the effects of FOXC1 silencing in cultured SMCs, are consistent with a role in maintaining SMC quiescence and contractile function.1 These observations help anchor the human results and show how experimental systems can be used to probe mechanisms identified in patient-derived data. Looking ahead, integrating human genetics, tissue-level omics, and functional validation offers a strong framework for target discovery in atherosclerosis. Expanding such approaches to larger and more diverse cohorts, incorporating longitudinal data, and using emerging technologies such as spatial transcriptomics will further strengthen the link between molecular mechanisms and clinical outcomes. Approaches such as organoids or plaque-on-chip systems may help bridge this gap, although they still capture only selected aspects of the disease and remain inherently reductionistic.

FOXC1 adds to a growing group of transcriptional regulators that influence SMC state and may represent a potential entry point for therapeutic intervention. As with many such targets, translation will depend on understanding context-specific effects and downstream pathways.

This study provides new insight into the regulation of SMC plasticity and highlights the value of human tissue-based approaches. Rather than relying solely on simplified models, we are increasingly able to study atherosclerosis in its full biological context, a shift that will shape future discoveries.
