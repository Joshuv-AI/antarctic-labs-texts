Eosinophil counts as a prognostic marker in glioblastoma: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Eric Giannaris, Geoffrey Sedor, Catherine S. Spina, Kristin Hsieh, Carl Elliston, Simon K. Cheng, Fabio M. Iwamoto, Guy M. McKhann, Michael B. Sisti, Jeffrey N. Bruce, Tony J. C. Wang, Matthew Gallitto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioblastoma is a highly aggressive primary brain malignancy characterized by poor median survival despite trimodal therapy. While established prognostic factors exist, significant heterogeneity in patient outcomes persist. This study aims to evaluate whether white-cell differentials predict oncologic outcomes in glioblastoma (GBM).MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed 85 GBM patients treated with standard surgery, radiotherapy (60 Gy in 30 fractions), and temozolomide. Clinical data (age, Karnofsky performance status, extent of resection, MGMT methylation, corticosteroid use, dosimetric data) and hematologic indices (absolute lymphocyte count (ALC), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), absolute eosinophil (AEC) and basophil counts, etc.) were collected at baseline and up to 3 months post-chemoradiation.ResultsMedian overall survival (OS) in our cohort was 22.5 months (95% CI, 20.4–32.2). On univariate analysis, higher AEC correlated with improved OS at baseline (HR 0.68; p = 0.013) and at 2 months (HR 0.58; p = 0.019). In the combined multivariable model—controlling for age, extent of resection, MGMT status, KPS, steroid use, and % of brain volume receiving 60 Gy) —baseline AEC remained independently prognostic (aHR 0.64; p = 0.021).ConclusionsBaseline eosinophils were independently associated with improved survival. Such findings highlight the importance of host immunity and baseline eosinophils as a potential prognostic marker of OS in GBM and warrant larger, prospective studies on prognostic marker validation.

Introduction

Glioblastoma (GBM) is the most common malignant primary brain tumor in adults. Despite extensive trimodal therapy, including surgical resection, radiation, and chemotherapy, median overall survival is only 15 months, with a 5-year survival rate of 5% [1, 2]. Prognosis is often well-established by clinical and molecular factors such as age, KPS status, MGMT methylation, IDH mutation, and extent of resection [2–6]. Yet survival can still vary widely among GBM patients with similar clinicopathologic features.

GBM patients are frequently immunosuppressed, reflecting both tumor-mediated immune evasion and therapy-related effects [7]. Lymphopenia is a common complication during and after chemoradiotherapy as circulating lymphocytes are extremely radiosensitive and undergo apoptosis within hours of systemic irradiation [8]. While many hematologic factors recover, lymphocyte depletion can persist for months [9]. Lymphopenia has been associated with inferior survival outcomes in several studies, suggesting that patients’ immune function during and after treatment could potentially impact long-term survival [10–12]. Similarly, the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR) and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio (LMR) have been associated with worse outcomes in gliomas and GBM [13–16].

Beyond these markers, interest has recently expanded to other immune subsets, including eosinophils and basophils. In vitro, eosinophils have been associated with attraction towards glioblastoma cells and promotion of Fas/FasL–mediated apoptosis [17]. Clinically, in an analysis of GBM patients treated with bevacizumab, an increase in eosinophils during treatment correlated with improved survival [18]. Similarly, in a retrospective cohort of GBM patients, higher absolute eosinophil counts, and elevated lymphocyte-to-basophil ratios independently predicted longer overall survival [19].

In this study, we perform a retrospective analysis of hematologic parameters in a cohort of GBM patients to identify predictors of survival. While prior retrospective studies have implicated eosinophils as prognostic markers in GBM, these studies have included molecularly heterogeneous populations that incorporated IDH-mutant tumors alongside IDH-wildtype GBM. By restricting our analysis to IDH-wildtype patients, we provide a more homogeneous cohort. In clarifying the prognostic impact of different white cell counts (including lymphocytes, eosinophils, and basophils), therapeutic strategies may be targeted to preserve and improve immune function during standard treatment.

Methods and materials

Patients

This single-institution retrospective analysis included 85 adult patients with pathology-proven glioblastoma (GBM) enrolled on an institutional IRB-approved database between 12/20/2011 and 06/20/2016. Patient inclusion criteria included patients treated with maximally safe surgical resection followed by conventionally fractionated radiation therapy to a total dose of 60 Gy delivered in 30 daily fractions – with concurrent and adjuvant temozolomide. Exclusion criteria included infection treated with antibiotics, prior radiation therapy to the central nervous system (CNS), incomplete course of radiation therapy, and a lack of recorded follow-up in the initial 3 months post-chemoradiation therapy.

Patient demographics and treatment details, including use of chemotherapy and steroids, were obtained from the electronic medical record. Hematologic assessment was conducted using the electronic medical record, collecting the complete blood count (CBC) with differential within 2 weeks of starting chemoradiation therapy (pre-CRT), within 2 weeks of completion of chemoradiation therapy (post-CRT) and monthly for the first three months after treatment. Dosimetric analyses were performed using Eclipse→ or PinnacleⓇ software and patient pre-treatment CT scan generated at time of simulation. The volumetric analyses included evaluation of the following structures: planning target volume (PTV), brain parenchyma and calvarium. All data was stored according to IRB-approved best practices.

Statistics

All statistical analyses were conducted in R (v4.3.1). Overall survival (OS) was defined as the time from the date of diagnosis to date of death or last known alive date, with patients last known alive censored at that date. Steroid exposure was quantified as the cumulative number of days on dexamethasone across the concurrent chemoradiation period and the first three months post-treatment. Univariate analyses were conducted using Cox proportional hazards regression, computing hazard ratios with 95% confidence intervals and p-values with statistical significance defined as p<0.05. Each univariate Cox regression was performed using all patients with available data for that specific variable and time point (complete-case analysis per variable); the sample size for each model is reported in the results. The multivariable Cox model was fit on patients with complete data across all included covariates. We included one timepoint per biomarker and did not co-model composite ratios with their component cell counts. Covariates that were significant on univariate analysis or deemed clinically important in established literature were included in the multivariable model: extent of resection, MGMT methylation status, age at diagnosis, KPS, steroid usage, and % of brain volume receiving 60gy. The Kaplan-Meier analysis was used for survival analyses, using the log-rank test for significance.

Results

Patients

This cohort consisted of 85 patients with glioblastoma, 62.3% male and 37.6% female, with a median age of 56.1 years and median Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS) of 90 (Table 1). All patients received standard concurrent chemoradiotherapy, and the median radiotherapy duration (“package time”) was 42.8 days. All patients underwent maximally safe surgical intervention, with gross total resection (GTR) in 34.1%, subtotal resection (STR) in 58.8%, and biopsy alone in 7.0%. MGMT promoter methylation was present in 23.5% of patients, absent in 61.2%, and unknown in 16.5%. The median total duration of steroid use was 62.8 days.

At the time of analysis, 69 patients had died (81.1%). The median overall survival was 22.5 months (95% CI 20.4–32.2 months). Three patients did not have IDH testing performed; given the 2011–2016 study period, diagnosis was based on histopathologic criteria predating the 2021 WHO classification. Due to the retrospective nature of the study, hematologic data availability varied by time point: baseline (pre-CRT) values were available for 83 patients; post-CRT, n = 85; 1-month post-CRT, n = 69; 2-month post-CRT, n = 69; 3-month post-CRT, n = 55 (65% of cohort). Each Cox model reflects the complete-case sample available for that specific variable and time point.

Overall Survival

On univariate Cox proportional hazards analysis, several clinical and hematologic variables demonstrated significant associations with overall survival (Table 2). Extent of resection was strongly associated with survival, as patients undergoing subtotal resection or biopsy had significantly worse outcomes compared with those undergoing gross total resection (HR 2.26, 95% CI 1.33–3.85, P = 0.0034).

MGMT status showed a trend toward improved survival among patients with methylated tumors; however, the comparison of unmethylated/unknown versus methylated MGMT did not reach statistical significance (HR 1.75, 95% CI 0.97–3.16, P = 0.064).

Steroid exposure demonstrated a borderline association with survival when treated as a continuous variable, with increasing total steroid days associated with worse outcomes (HR 1.00 per day, 95% CI 1.00–1.01, P = 0.051).

Radiation-related factors significantly associated with poorer survival included longer radiotherapy duration (HR 1.12, 95% CI 1.01–1.24, P = 0.033), greater percent brain volume receiving ≥ 60 Gy (Brain RT V60) (HR 1.19 per 5% increase, 95% CI 1.04–1.35, P = 0.004), and larger PTV 6000 volumes (HR 1.02 per 10 cm³, 95% CI 1.00–1.03, P = 0.018).

From a hematologic perspective, absolute eosinophil counts (AEC) demonstrated consistent associations with survival. Higher baseline AEC was associated with improved overall survival (HR 0.68, 95% CI 0.50–0.92, P = 0.013), as were higher eosinophil counts at 2 months post-treatment (HR 0.58, 95% CI 0.36–0.91, P = 0.019). In contrast, higher absolute neutrophil counts at 2 months post-treatment were associated with worse survival (HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.01–1.37, P = 0.034).

Other hematologic parameters, including monocytes, basophils, platelets, total white blood cell count, and red blood cell indices measured at baseline and subsequent post-treatment time points, were evaluated but did not demonstrate significant associations with overall survival on univariate analysis.

On multivariable Cox regression analysis adjusting for relevant clinical covariates (Table 3), baseline absolute eosinophil count remained an independent prognostic marker of overall survival when modeled per standard deviation (SD) increase (aHR 0.64, 95% CI 0.44–0.94, P = 0.021; baseline AEC SD = 0.108 × 10³/µL).

Extent of resection remained a strong independent predictor, with patients undergoing subtotal resection or biopsy demonstrating significantly worse survival compared with those undergoing GTR (aHR 2.48, 95% CI 1.32–4.67, P = 0.005). MGMT unmethylated/unknown status was also independently associated with poorer outcomes (aHR 3.15, 95% CI 1.48–6.70, P = 0.003). The percentage of total brain volume receiving 60 Gy during radiotherapy was also significantly associated with worse survival (aHR 1.30, 95% CI 1.08–1.56, P = 0.005).

Age, KPS, and steroid use were not independently associated with overall survival in the multivariable model. Radiation dose-volume metrics and steroid exposure were included as covariates but did not materially alter the observed association between baseline eosinophil counts and survival.

Kaplan–Meier analysis demonstrated a separation in overall survival by baseline absolute eosinophil count (AEC) when dichotomized at the cohort median (Fig. 1). Patients with high baseline AEC (≥ median, 0.065 × 10³/µL) had a median overall survival of 31.8 months (95% CI, 22.2–40.8) compared with 21.1 months (95% CI, 15.3–35.8) among those with low baseline AEC (< median, 0.065 × 10³/µL) (P = 0.05).

Discussion

In this study, we comprehensively evaluated the prognostic significance of hematologic parameters in a cohort of GBM patients treated at a single institution. We found that baseline eosinophils were a significant independent predictor of survival, highlighting novel associations involving peripheral eosinophil count and overall survival in GBM.

One of the novel findings of our study is the protective association of higher eosinophil counts with patient survival. Only a few studies have examined eosinophils in GBM prognosis. Madhugiri et al. found that both absolute eosinophil count and the neutrophil-to eosinophil ratio were different between short-term and long-term survivors, with higher eosinophils in the latter [19]. However, these prior studies included molecularly heterogeneous populations that mixed IDH-mutant and IDH-wildtype tumors. Our study extends these findings in several ways: by restricting the primary analysis to IDH-wildtype patients, we evaluate eosinophil prognostic value in a biologically homogeneous cohort in which confounding by IDH mutation status is eliminated, and we evaluate eosinophil counts across multiple time points from baseline through three months post-chemoradiation. In our cohort, the clinically applicable cutoff of AEC ≥ 0.065 × 10³/µL at baseline identified patients with meaningfully longer median OS (31.8 vs. 21.1 months). Importantly, this association for baseline eosinophils remained robust after adjusting for clinical confounders.

Eosinophils are associated with allergic and parasitic infections via the Th2 pathway and IgE-mediated mechanisms. In cancer, tumor-associated eosinophilia has been observed in certain malignancies to improve outcomes. In GBM, it has been observed that patients with increased IgE and CD23 have better prognoses [20, 21]. There is also evidence that eosinophils can exert cytotoxic effects on tumor cells and facilitate the recruitment of T cells to tumor sites in other cancers. In GBM, Vieira et al. reported that in situ eosinophil infiltration in GBM specimens was associated with tumor cell apoptosis [17]. Moreover, corticosteroids are known to decrease eosinophils [22], and the potential interplay between steroid use and eosinophil-mediated immunity represents an area of interest for future investigation. Furthermore, it is important to note that the hematologic associations observed in this study were evaluated during from baseline and limited to the first three months following the completion of chemoradiation. This timeframe is notable, as it may reflect the window of greatest immune perturbation in GBM patients. During the post-treatment phase, patients experience the compounded immunosuppressive effects of standard trimodal therapy, reflecting both tumor-mediated immune evasion and therapy-related effects. Because circulating immune cells are highly radiosensitive and corticosteroids are known to decrease eosinophils, this early window captures the host’s most vulnerable immunologic state. Therefore, baseline and post-treatment eosinophil counts may serve as a particularly sensitive marker of the host’s capacity to maintain an immune response during peak physiological stress. Further research to clarify the role of eosinophils in GBM prognosis is warranted and should continue to be an area of interest.

Limitations of our analysis include the retrospective nature of the study. Additionally, patients were not excluded from our analysis with incomplete hematologic follow-up, thus our evaluation of hematologic parameters at 1 and 2 months after treatment included 81% (n = 69) and at 3 months included 65% (n = 55) of the total cohort. Another limitation is that we did not have longitudinal data on subsets of lymphocytes (CD4, CD8, etc.), which are established markers of immunosuppression. Moreover, the observed median OS of our relatively smaller cohort was higher than historically reported, which may reflect potential selection bias for those fit to receive standard of care conventionally fractionated chemoradiation. Lastly, our cohort includes 13 MGMT-status unknown and 3 IDH-unknown patients which were treated as GBM based on histopathologic data predating the 2021 WHO molecular classification. These data should be viewed as hypothesis-generating as numerous markers and timepoints were explored. Confirmation of our findings warrants a larger, multicenter prospective study.

Conclusion

In this retrospective cohort of GBM patients receiving standard trimodal therapy, lower baseline eosinophils were independently associated with shorter overall survival. These findings support the clinical relevance of peripheral eosinophil count and its role in host immunity when assessing outcomes in GBM patients. These findings are hypothesis-generating and support the need for larger, prospective studies to validate eosinophils as a potential prognostic marker in GBM.
