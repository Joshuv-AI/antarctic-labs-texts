Kangaroo mother care and maternal bonding 1 year after childbirth: a nationwide birth cohort—the Japan environment and children’s cohort study
Authors: Kayoko Saito, Hitomi Inano, Akiko Tsuchida, Haruka Kasamatsu, Kyoko Sasano, Kanako Shimada, Kenta Matsumura, Kei Hamazaki, Hidekuni Inadera, Tomomi Hasegawa, Michihiro Kamijima, Michihiro Kamijima, Shin Yamazaki, Maki Fukami, Reiko Kishi, Chiharu Ota, Koichi Hashimoto, Chisato Mori, Shuichi Ito, Ryoji Shinohara, Takeo Nakayama, Ryo Kawasaki, Yasuhiro Takeshima, Seiji Kageyama, Narufumi Suganuma, Shoichi Ohga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo prevent bonding disorder, support should be provided both immediately after childbirth and in the long term. This study focuses on the prevention of bonding disorder and aimed to clarify the effect of early mother–infant contact on mother-to-infant bonding 1 year after childbirth.MethodsThe participants were 81,634 mothers involved in an ongoing nationwide birth cohort study called the Japan Environment and Children’s Study. Mother-to-infant bonding was measured using the Mother-to-Infant Bonding Scale, Japanese version (MIBS-J). Mother–infant contact after childbirth was classified as “no contact”, “clothed or swaddled contact”, and “skin-to-skin contact”. Mother–infant bonding disorder was identified based on an MIBS-J score of ≥ 5, and analyses were also performed based on the sub-scores for “Lack of Affection” and “Anger and Rejection”. A generalized linear mixed model analysis was used to derive adjusted odds ratios (AORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for cases of bonding disorder and subscale scores according to mother–infant contact, with no contact as the reference group and while controlling for potential confounders.ResultsOne year after childbirth, the clothed or swaddled contact group (AOR 0.93, 95% CI 0.87–0.99) and the skin-to-skin contact group (AOR 0.85, 95% CI 0.81–0.90) had a significantly lower odds of bonding disorder compared with the no contact group. By subscale, the odds of bonding disorder on the Lack of Affection subscale was lower in the skin-to-skin contact group (AOR 0.91, 95% CI 0.89–0.93) than in the no contact group. For the Anger and Rejection subscale, the odds of bonding disorder was lower in the clothed or swaddled contact group (AOR 0.94, 95% CI 0.92–0.96) and in the skin-to-skin contact group (AOR 0.92, 95% CI 0.91–0.94) than in the no contact group.ConclusionsEarly skin-to-skin contact between mother and child immediately after childbirth is associated with a reduced likelihood of long-term bonding disorder in mothers.

Introduction

The concept of maternal bonding was introduced by Reva Rubin in the 1960s and later popularized by Klaus and Kennell in the 1970s. They argued that “bonding” does not mean mutual attachment between an infant and the mother but the mother’s unilateral interest in her attachment to the infant and should therefore be distinguished from infant–mother attachment [1]. Therefore, “bonding” refers to the unilateral emotional bond from caregivers to their infants, whereas “attachment” refers to the basic sense of trust that infants develop toward their caregivers [2]. The lack of an emotional bond from a parent toward a child is called bonding disorder and was first discussed as a theme in perinatal psychiatry in the 1990 s [3, 4]. Klaus et al. [5] argued that the first few minutes and hours after delivery constitute a sensitive period during which bodily contact between the mother and infant plays an important role in building a bond between them.

Kangaroo mother care was first used in 1979 in San Juan de Dios Hospital, Bogotá, Colombia, to prevent nosocomial infection of premature babies and promote breastfeeding [6]. In developed countries, kangaroo mother care was introduced to promote mother-to-infant bonding and breastfeeding and, today, the provision of kangaroo mother care immediately after childbirth is encouraged not only for premature infants but also for healthy term infants [7, 8]. However, the impact of kangaroo mother care on maternal bonding is of low certainty [9]. Additionally, because this topic has not attracted much attention in the past decade or so, further research is needed [10]. In addition, kangaroo mother care is associated with good bonding in systematic reviews that only include low-birth-weight and preterm infants [9].

Better interaction between the mother and child at 6 months after childbirth has been associated with a lower likelihood of bonding disorder [11], and maternal postnatal depression has been correlated with the occurrence of bonding disorder [11–16]. There are various reports on treatments for bonding disorders, such as mother–child contact after delivery [17], video reflection [18], and baby massage [19], but there is no generally established method [20].

Therefore, we analyzed data from approximately 100,000 mother–child pairs who participated in the Japan Environment and Children’s Study (JECS), a national birth cohort study, to investigate the effect of early mother–child contact immediately after childbirth on maternal bonding at 1 year after childbirth. In this study, we evaluated the relationship between early skin-to-skin contact after childbirth and maternal bonding 1 year after childbirth by using the Mother-to-Infant Bonding Scale, Japanese version (MIBS-J).

Methods

Study design and sample

The aim of this study was to clarify the relationship between early skin-to-skin contact and maternal bonding in the first year after birth using JECS data. The JECS is a nationwide government-funded birth cohort study of the relationship between various environmental factors and children’s health and development. Details of the study design and baseline characteristics of the JECS participants have been reported by Kawamoto et al. [21] and Michikawa et al. [22]. The dataset used included data from the first trimester, from the second or third trimester, and then from 1 month and 1 year after childbirth. A total of 103,062 pregnancies were registered between January 2011 and March 2014 via face-to-face recruitment at public agencies, including both rural and urban locations throughout Japan. The present study is based on the jecs-an-20180131 dataset (published in March 2018) and the complete dataset contained 103,062 records. The sample size was predetermined to guarantee adequate statistical power for rare diseases with a prevalence of ≤ 1%.

This study included data from mothers with singleton live births. Hence, data from mothers with multiple participations (5,647), multiple births (949), and miscarriages/still births (3,676) were excluded. Data from 81,634 mother–infant pairs were included in the analysis (Fig. 1), excluding 2,609 who did not respond to the item on early mother-to-infant contact, 8,192 who did not respond to an item on the MIBS-J, and 355 responses by someone other than the mother.Fig. 1Study flow chart. “Multiple participations” refers to individuals who participated more than once in the study

Measures

Exposure

A questionnaire was developed to ask mothers about early mother–child contact 1 month after childbirth. The question “Did you hold your baby soon after it was born?” had three response options: “Held the baby with skin-to-skin contact” (skin-to-skin contact), “Held the baby without skin-to-skin contact” (clothed or swaddled contact), and “Did not hold the baby immediately after the birth” (no contact). Three groups were formed based on the responses, and these groups were analyzed to examine early mother–child contact.

Outcome

The MIBS-J used data from questionnaires completed by mothers when their children were 1 year old. The MIBS-J consists of 10 items on a four-point Likert scale, and the total score ranges from 0 to 30. Higher scores for the MIBS-J indicate worse maternal bonding, and it consists of two subscales, Lack of Affection and Anger and Rejection [23, 24]. The Lack of Affection and Anger and Rejection subscales each have four items, for a total of 12 points. The items in the Lack of Affection subscale are as follows: “Feel loving towards my baby” (reverse-scored), “Enjoy doing things with my baby” (reverse-scored), “Feel protective towards my baby” (reverse-scored), and “Feel close to my baby” (reverse-scored). “Anger and Rejection” includes the items “Feel scared or panicky when I have to do something for my baby”, “Feel resentful towards my baby”, “Feel angry with my baby”, and “Wish my baby was different”. The remaining two items—“Feel nothing towards my baby” and “Wish I did not have my baby”—do not belong to either subscale [23, 24].

The MIBS-J has been reported to be useful for identifying mothers with severe bonding disorder requiring clinical intervention using a cutoff score of 4/5 [25]. Accordingly, we adopted the 4/5 cutoff point to identify a case of bonding disorder. In addition, as a generalized linear model was used for the two subscales, no cutoff values were set.

The Cronbach's alpha for this study was almost the same as that of previous studies (total = 0.76, Lack of Affection = 0.65, Anger and Rejection = 0.63) [23, 24].

Covariates

We used the following variables derived during pregnancy as potential confounders: maternal age (years, continuous); parity (primiparity or multiparity); marital status (married, single, divorced, or widowed); highest educational level (≤ 12, > 12 to < 16, and ≥ 16 years); annual household income (< 4, ≥ 4 to < 6, and ≥ 6 million JPY); smoking status (never, past, or current); alcohol intake (never, past, or current); physical activity in terms of continuous metabolic equivalents; history of physician-diagnosed depression, anxiety disorder, schizophrenia, autonomic imbalance, or other mental disorders (yes or no); and maternal psychological distress [26, 27] in the second or third trimester (Kessler Psychological Distress Scale [K6] score ≥ 13 [27]). The K6 was developed by Kessler et al. [28], and the validity of the Japanese version was determined by Furukawa et al. [27]. The six items are assessed on a 5-point scale (0–4) that yields a total score of 0–24. Psychological distress was assessed over the past 4 weeks, with a higher score representing greater psychological distress. Severe mental illness is defined as a K6 score ≥ 13, a score proposed by Kessler et al. [28] as the optimal cutoff for achieving a balance between false positives and false negatives. In addition, for children’s characteristics, we used sex of the child (male or female), gestational age at birth (weeks, continuous), and congenital anomaly (yes or no). These variables were categorized according to the medical practice or common practice in Japan and/or guidelines from earlier studies [12, 13, 28–33].

Statistical analysis

This study treated the three early skin-to-skin contact groups as the exposure variable and cases of bonding disorder and MIBS-J subscale scores for Lack of Affection (4 items) and Anger and Rejection (4 items) at 1 year as outcome variables. We used separate generalized linear mixed models, with unit number as a random effect and logit as the link function, to calculate crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs and AORs, respectively) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for clothed or swaddled contact and for skin-to-skin contact, respectively, using no contact as the reference group and while controlling for potential confounders. To address missing values, multiple imputation was performed using chained equations [34] to generate five imputed datasets, which were then combined for the final analysis. Data were analyzed using SAS version 9.4 software (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC).

Discussion

This study classified early mother–infant contact into three categories (no contact, clothed or swaddled contact, and skin-to-skin contact) and examined their relationship with MIBS-J scores at 1 year after childbirth. The number of mothers who had skin-to-skin contact with their infants after delivery was 46,690 (57.2%), while 15,748 (19.3%) had clothed or swaddled contact. Thus, 76.5% of mothers had mother–infant contact immediately after delivery in Japan. According to a study conducted at a small number of facilities in Japan, the implementation rate of early mother–infant contact is 82.0% [35], which is in accordance with our result.

Here, the prevalence of bonding disorder varied among groups defined by early skin-to-skin contact, with 12.8% for no contact, 11.6% for clothed or swaddled contact, and 10.9% for skin-to-skin contact. Early skin-to-skin contact was significantly associated with a decrease in the incidence of bonding disorder (AOR 0.85, 95% CI 0.81–0.90). Even though a prevalence difference of 1.9 percentage points, at most, may not seem particularly large, differences of this magnitude can still be relevant when considered at the population level. Furthermore, it was encouraging to find that, even among mothers with high Lack of Affection and Anger and Rejection scores, skin-to-skin contact was significantly associated with fewer cases of bonding disorder. The same directional association was even found to be true for clothed or swaddled contact (AOR 0.93, 95% CI 0.87–0.99). Thus, our findings indicate that skin-to-skin contact immediately after childbirth is related to mother–infant bonding at 1 year after childbirth.

The mean (SD) score of the MIBS-J was 1.95 (2.29) for mothers at 1 year after childbirth. In addition, the mean (SD) score of the MIBS-J Lack of Affection subscale was 0.63 (0.99) while that of the Anger and Rejection subscale was 1.17 (1.40). A previous study conducted in Japan with 554 mothers in a maternity clinic reported that the mean (SD) scores of the Lack of Affection and Anger and Rejection subscales were 0.50 (1.20) and 0.70 (1.10), respectively, at 4 months after delivery [23]. In contrast, our study revealed higher scores for both subscales. One possible explanation for this difference is that the previous study was conducted in a small clinic-based sample, whereas our study included a large, more heterogeneous population, which may better reflect variability in maternal bonding scores.

The Lack of Affection subscale score was lower in the skin-to-skin contact group compared with the no contact group. This raises the question of how early skin-to-skin contact might relate to a mother’s developing feelings of affection toward her baby. Rubin (1984) suggested that, when the mother goes through a verification process after giving birth, she uses touch to confirm the information that she has gained through sight and, by doing so, obtains more information on the newborn [36]. Our previous qualitative study also suggested that kangaroo mother care immediately after delivery may deepen a mother’s sense of having given birth, as she perceives the baby with multiple senses while holding the baby skin-to-skin [37]. In other words, early skin-to-skin contact may provide mothers with a more immediate and multisensory experience of their infant, which might contribute to a greater sense of familiarity and emotional connection. This can evoke positive emotions, which might help explain the lower likelihood of lack of affection. Similarly, the Anger and Rejection subscale score of mothers who held their clothed baby was significantly lower than that of mothers who did not hold their baby immediately after delivery (AOR 0.94, 95% CI 0.92–0.96). Furthermore, the score of mothers who held their baby with skin-to-skin contact was lower than that of mothers who did not hold their baby (AOR 0.92, 95% CI 0.91–0.94).

In his review article “Attachment and Loss: Retrospect and Prospect,” Bowlby (1982) comments on retrieving behavior, which is observed in all mammals [2]. Retrieving behavior among primates involves holding the child close to the body with both hands. Among humans, this is referred to as “maternal protection”. This behavior may reduce not only physical distance but also the emotional distance between mother and child. Tactile stimulation has been shown in animal experiments to play an important role in the formation of bonds between mothers and their offspring [38]. Even when the mother holds the baby with her clothes on, the action of holding the child with both arms lessens the physical distance between mother and child. This might encourage the mother to recognize her child soon after delivery, enhance maternal protection, and lead to a lower Anger and Rejection score, even with clothed or swaddled contact.

The limitations of this study are as follows. First, we used the 4/5 cutoff point to identify bonding disorder. However, strictly speaking, this is an optimal cutoff value not for 1 year postpartum but for 1 month postpartum. Thus, further study will be necessary to validate the use of this cutoff for 12-month postpartum data. Second, although we adjusted for a number of covariates, residual confounding cannot be ruled out and there is still a possibility of unadjusted covariates. Potential unmeasured factors—such as the mother’s own upbringing experience or childcare support from individuals other than the partner—may influence mother–infant bonding. Third, one of the selection criteria of the JECS is being able to read and write in Japanese. This single-language specification homogenized the cultural background of the sample. Because giving birth and childrearing is subject to strong cultural influence, it is necessary to test whether similar results are obtained in a different cultural setting. Finally, the questionnaires used in this study were collected from 2011 to 2014, and therefore reflect maternal and infant conditions during that period.

On the other hand, this study has several strengths. First, because it is a large-scale national study, the sample size is large. For example, the male-to-female ratio in this study is almost the same as that in a population-based study [22], indicating that this is a high-quality nationwide study. Second, being a birth cohort study (a longitudinal study), the relationship was examined prospectively. Third, because the main covariates were included in the model, the possibility of spurious correlations in the observed relationship was largely eliminated.

Conclusions

In this large nationwide cohort, early skin-to-skin mother–infant contact immediately after delivery was associated with better bonding outcomes at 1 year postpartum, including a lower prevalence of overall bonding disorder and difficulties related to affection and anger/rejection. These findings suggest a potential benefit of early postnatal contact for subsequent mother–infant bonding, supporting its continued incorporation into perinatal care practices.
