Aryl and Alkyl Radiotrifluoromethylation via Metallaphotoredox‐Mediated Radical Cross‐Coupling
Authors: Lukas Veth, Albert D. Windhorst, Danielle J. Vugts
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTGiven the short half‐life of radionuclides used for positron emission tomography (PET), such as fluorine‐18 (t 1/2 = 109.8 min), efficient and mild methods for the radiolabeling of PET tracers are highly sought after. Previous studies have shown how photoredox catalysis can be used to unlock unique reactivity by enabling radiofluorinations via radical formation of either non‐radioactive substrates or carbon‐centered 18F‐labeled reagents. However, the cross‐coupling of two different radical species, one of which is radiolabeled, remains thus far elusive. Herein, we report the metallaphotoredox‐mediated radiotrifluoromethylation of arenes and alkanes via radical cross‐coupling reactions (RCCR). All protocols proceed under mild conditions and use a commercially available Ru‐based photoredox catalyst and copper(I) iodide to mediate the radical coupling. The reactions are operationally simple and can be set up without the use of a glovebox or Schlenk line. The good functional group tolerance and applicability to PET tracer synthesis is demonstrated by the radiotrifluoromethylation of a range of substrates, incl. bioactive boscalid and PET tracer [18F]SL25.1188. In a broader context, this work sets the stage for the further exploration of RCCR for radiolabeling reactions beyond radiotrifluoromethylation.

Introduction

Radiolabeled compounds have found use in numerous applications in life sciences [1, 2]. Among them, positron emission tomography (PET) is a non‐invasive imaging tool used in daily clinical practice for routine diagnosis of medical conditions and drug development [3, 4]. The most commonly used PET radionuclide is 18F owing to its favorable properties: [2, 5] the half‐life (t 1/2 = 109.8 min) allows scanning over several hours and shipping between different facilities and the low positron energy (E βmax = 0.634 MeV) results in a relatively high spatial resolution in vivo. To explore the full potential of PET, reliable methods for the precise incorporation of 18F into organic molecules are required. Despite the availability of a multitude of synthetic tools for fluorination reactions using 19F, their translation to radiolabeling presents a formidable challenge [6, 7]. As a consequence of the production of 18F via the 18O(p,n)18F nuclear reaction in (medical) cyclotrons, it is typically obtained as [18F]F−. Consequently, most methods for radiofluorination aim to make use of [18F]F− directly. Alternatively, the use of simple 18F‐labeled reagents, derived from [18F]F−, has also found widespread application [8]. For the incorporation of multifluorinated moieties, in particular difluoromethyl (CHF18F) and trifluoromethyl (CF2 18F) groups, such reagents have proven to be invaluable to provide access to radiolabeled compounds in high molar activity (Am) [9, 10, 11].

Radicals are reactive intermediates with great usefulness for synthetic chemistry. The emergence of photoredox catalysis has enabled the access to radicals under mild reaction conditions and with broad applicability [12, 13, 14]. As such, it has also found applications in radiochemistry for the incorporation of 18F, for example, by activation of the non‐radioactive substrates to radical (ion) species that can react with a 18F‐fluorination reagent, in most cases nucleophilic [18F]F− (Scheme 1a) [15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. Alternatively, 18F‐labeled carbon‐centered radicals can be accessed that can then react with non‐radioactive substrates [10, 21, 22].

Radical cross‐coupling reactions (RCCR), that is, the reaction of two different radical species for the construction of single bonds, are attractive yet challenging chemical transformations [23] that have also profited from the renaissance of photoredox chemistry [24]. Despite the low activation energy for the reaction of two radicals, mediating the selectivity presents a considerable challenge. It is generally dictated by the persistent radical effect [25], requiring one of the two radical species to be somewhat persistent. As a consequence, the reaction of two transient radicals is difficult to achieve. The use of transition metals has shown to provide a potential solution to this problem by stabilizing one of the radicals and consequently making a selective coupling reaction possible. This has contributed to the expansion of the scope of radical cross‐coupling reactions and enabled Csp2─Csp3 and Csp3─Csp3 bond‐forming reactions under mild reaction conditions [24]. Despite this, the application of RCCR in radiolabeling of PET tracers remains thus far elusive.

To probe the feasibility of a RCCR, we considered the use of our recently developed photoredox‐mediated radical trifluoromethylation chemistry based on 18F‐labeled trifluoroiodomethane (Scheme 1b) [22]. CF3‐groups are commonly found in drug compounds and are therefore attractive synthetic targets for PET tracer synthesis [26]. We envisioned that RCCR could be used for the radiotrifluoromethylation of arenes and alkanes. Arenes are the most commonly trifluoromethylated moieties in drug compounds and are therefore of high synthetic interest to radiochemists. Apart from strategies utilizing [18F]F−, the use of CuCF2 18F has enabled the radiofluorination in higher molar activity and starting from a range of different substrates [9, 27, 28, 29, 30, 31]. However, most strategies require either high temperatures (>100°C) [27, 28, 29, 30] or substrates that are of limited stability, e.g., boronic acids [29, 30]. For alkanes on the other hand, protocols for their radiotrifluoromethylation [32] are either limited to 2,2,2‐[18F]trifluoroethyl groups [33] or suffer from low molar activity (<1 GBq/µmol) [34]. A RCCR could address both issues and broaden the radiochemical space of available motifs for PET tracer synthesis. We speculated that a RCCR could be achieved via the reaction pathway shown in Scheme 1c. Non‐radioactive aryl or alkyl radical R· and radiolabeled radical ·CF2 18F would be formed via photoredox chemistry using photoredox catalyst PC, followed by the coupling of both radicals mediated by a suitable transition metal catalyst TM, vide supra.

In this report, we present the first metallaphotoredox‐mediated radical‐radical cross‐coupling reaction for the use in radiofluorination chemistry. Specifically, our strategy is exploited to deliver a general approach for the radiotrifluoromethylation of arenes and alkanes to be used for the synthesis of PET tracers.

Results and Discussion

We commenced our study with the radiotrifluoromethylation of arenes. Based on the reaction design, the choice of leaving group X, and catalysts PC and TM are crucial. Given the plethora of available substrates for aryl and alkyl radicals, we considered leaving groups to be ideal that are generally late‐stage compatible, easy to install, and undergo radical formation using the same catalyst as for the ·CF2 18F formation. Given these criteria, we chose thianthrenium salts (TT) [35]. Apart from their ease of preparation through late‐stage functionalization [35, 36, 37], they have been shown to undergo formation of radicals under Ru(bpy)‐catalysis [38, 39, 40]. Further, we hypothesized that a CuI‐complex would be the catalyst of choice for mediating the radical coupling. For reactions utilizing 19F, it has been shown that CF3‐radicals can react with CuI‐species to form CuII‐CF3 complexes [41]. These, in turn, have been shown to react with aryl and alkyl radicals to form the trifluoromethylated products likely by formation of CuIII‐intermediates that readily undergo reductive elimination [38, 41, 42, 43, 44, 45].

Upon evaluation of a range of reaction conditions, we found that model substrate 1a could be converted to 18F‐labeled trifluoromethyl arene [18F]2a in 81 ± 2% radiochemical conversion (RCC) (Table 1, entry 1). The reaction proceeds using CF2 18FI, DBU as base, [Ru(bpy)3]Cl2·6H2O as photocatalyst, and CuI as coupling reagent under blue light irradiation in 10 min. We found that the choice of base is crucial as it is required for the activation of CF2 18FI and for reductive quenching. The reaction performance differed drastically when using similar bulky, non‐nucleophilic bases like DABCO and TMG, while comparable when using DIPEA (Table 1, entries 2–4). In the absence of base, product formation was reduced significantly (Table 1, entry 5). A potential reason for the residual reactivity could be that thianthrene acts as reductive quencher [46]. This observation is further supported by control experiments for the reactions shown in Scheme 3, which indicate that no product formation occurs in the absence of base and thianthrene (cf. Tables S2 and S3). Using a stronger photocatalyst, [Ir(ppy)3], led to the formation of more by‐product, mainly [18F]fluoroform, and consequently diminished the yield of [18F]2a (Table 1, entry 6). Importantly, no formation of [18F]2a could be observed in the absence of photocatalyst, 1a, or blue light (Table 1, entries 7–9). Only traces of product were formed in the absence of CuI, indicating its crucial role in this transformation (Table 1, entry 10). Although an inert gas atmosphere is required for the reaction to proceed (Table 1, entry 11), the reaction does not require handling in a glovebox or using a Schlenk line. With the optimized reaction conditions in hand, we sought out to investigate the reaction scope. We found the method to be applicable to a wide range of substrates (Scheme 2). The presence of several electron‐donating groups, such as methoxy‐, acetoxy‐, and acetamides, were well tolerated delivering the respective products in 56–85% RCC ([18F]2b‐[18F]2e). Likewise, substrates bearing electron‐withdrawing substituents, such as aldehyde‐, nitrile‐, trifluoromethyl‐, ester‐, halide‐, and sulfone‐groups delivered products in excellent RCC of 75–85% ([18F]2e‐[18F]2k). Gratifyingly, we found that the presence of substituents in ortho‐position did not affect the reaction performance ([18F]2l‐[18F]2m, 65%–75% RCC). Substrates bearing dimethylamino‐, hydroxyl‐, and nitro‐substituents did not deliver any product (see Figure S5). Multiple heterocyclic substrates, namely xanthones ([18F]2n), pyridines ([18F]2o), and dibenzofurans ([18F]2p), could be functionalized in 62%–87% RCC. Importantly, the method is also applicable to the functionalization of bioactive compounds. A derivative of boscalid underwent radiotrifluoromethylation to furnish the corresponding product [18F]2q in satisfactory RCC (37 ± 6%). To determine the molar activity of the products, experiments with full batches of CF2 18FI (1.14 ± 0.06 GBq, starting from 5.46 ± 0.15 GBq [18F]fluoride) were conducted. [18F]2k was then isolated by semi‐preparative HPLC and the Am of the isolated product was found to be 12.2 ± 5.7 GBq/µmol (for more details, see Supporting Information). This Am is in the expected range [22] and is sufficient for routine PET tracer productions, making the developed protocol a viable option when designing radiotracers. It should be noted that the Am increases approximately linearly when using higher starting activities, as previously reported [11]. Therefore, clinically relevant molar activities can be reached when using higher starting activities of [18F]fluoride, as is common practice for human productions (typically 50–500 GBq [18F]F− at the start of the synthesis).

Next, we turned our attention to the radiotrifluoromethylation of alkanes. We decided to start the investigation by using the analogous alkyl TTs, which have been shown to undergo radical formation as well [47]. To our delight, we found that model compound 3a readily underwent radiotrifluoromethylation to give access to 18F‐labeled trifluoromethyl alkane [18F]5a. Upon further optimization of the reaction conditions, we found that the use of DIPEA enabled the formation of [18F]5a in excellent 87 ± 4% RCC. A full scale experiment delivered product [18F]5a with a molar activity of 8.2 ± 0.6 GBq/µmol (starting with 5.89 ± 0.39 GBq [18F]F−), similar to the arenes, vide supra.

Alkyl TTs are generally synthesized from the corresponding alcohols and the radiotrifluoromethylation is a formally homologative transformation. Therefore, we wondered whether a decarboxylative reaction [48] could provide a complementary synthetic route while maintaining the overall carbon chain length. In particular, we were interested im N‐hydroxyphthalimide (NHPI) esters. This class of compounds can be easily accessed from abundant carboxylic acids and has been demonstrated to provide facile access to alkyl radicals [49, 50]. We found that under slightly modified reaction conditions using DABCO as base, [18F]5a could be synthesized from model substrate 4a in 77 ± 5% RCC (Scheme 3). This does not only provide a second synthetic handle for alkanes but also demonstrates the potential generality of the radical‐cross coupling concept developed herein.

The evaluation of the substrate scope revealed that apart from similar substrates bearing halide‐substituents ([18F]5b – [18F]5c, 81%–85% RCC), benzylic positions can be radiotrifluoromethylated in good RCC ([18F]5d, 53 ± 9% RCC). The radiolabeling of tertiary and quarternary compounds [18F]5e and [18F]5f also proceeded in good RCC (60%–72%). Furthermore, the developed protocols could be utilized to access β‐trifluoromethyl ether [18F]5g and ketone [18F]5h. The presence of heterocycles, such as benzothiophenes and phthalimides, was also tolerated, delivering the corresponding products [18F]5i‐[18F]5j in 67%–76% RCC. Substrates delivering product in insufficient RCC are depicted in Figure S5 (see Supporting Information). 11C‐labeled SL25.1188 is a PET tracer that is used for imaging monoamine oxidase B (MAO B) [51] and serves as a marker for astrogliosis. It is typically synthesized via an intramolecular cyclization reaction using [11C]CO2 [52]. Although this established radiosynthesis works well, it does come with the inherent drawbacks of 11C. Compared to 18F, 11C provides a lower spatial resolution and its shorter half‐life (t 1/2 = 20 min) results in a more limited clinical availability. Given the presence of an aliphatic CF3‐group in SL25.1188, we considered that the herein developed RCCR could provide access to the analogous [18F]SL25.1188. To our delight, the corresponding NHPI ester 4k underwent radiotrifluoromethylation smoothly to deliver [18F]SL25.1188 [18F]5k in 70 ± 9% RCC. This is one of the first examples of the radiofluorination of SL25.1188 [32] and demonstrates the potential impact of the herein developed radical cross‐coupling approach.

In order to gain further insight into the reaction mechanism and to investigate whether the reaction does indeed proceed via the coupling of two radicals, we performed radical trapping experiments (Scheme 4). When conducting the three model reactions under standard reaction conditions in the presence of radical trap (2,2,6,6‐tetramethylpiperidin‐1‐yl)oxyl (TEMPO), no formation of [18F]2a or [18F]5a could be observed in all three cases (Scheme 4a). Instead, CF2 18FI was almost exclusively converted to a new species, TEMPO‐CF2 18F. This observation confirms the formation of CF2 18F radicals and their crucial character for the product formation. The formation of TEMPO‐CF2 18F also occurred in the absence of CuI but not in the absence of [Ru(bpy)3]Cl2·6H2O, making an involvement of Cu‐species in the activation of CF2 18FI unlikely. Analogous products stemming from a reaction of TEMPO with non‐radioactive aryl or alkyl radicals could not be observed. Instead, HPLC analysis showed the formation of biphenyl and ethylbenzene. To further investigate the formation of non‐radioactive radicals, we conducted a radical clock experiment using substrate 1r (Scheme 4b). We found that only cyclized product [18F]2r’ was formed in 83% RCC while formation of trifluoromethylarene [18F]2r was not observed. This result strongly supports the intermediacy of an aryl radical. Additional trapping experiments with PINO radicals [53] (see Scheme S1) also indicated the presence of non‐radioactive aryl and alkyl radicals. Taken together, these mechanistic experiments support the proposed mechanism via radical cross‐coupling, as shown in Scheme 1c.

Conclusions

In summary, we have developed protocols for the radiotrifluoromethylation of arenes and alkanes via photoredox‐mediated radical‐cross coupling. All protocols are operationally simple, occur under mild reaction conditions, and possess a good functional group tolerance. All products can be isolated in good molar activity. The applicability to routine PET tracer synthesis was shown by the radiolabeling of different bioactive compounds, incl. the 18F‐labeling of astrogliosis marker SL25.1188. The radical mechanism is supported by mechanistic investigations. Overall, the work shows the potential of radical cross‐coupling chemistry to expand the radiochemical space of available radiofluorinated motifs for PET tracer synthesis. We expect this work to set the foundation for the development of more methods exploring this radical cross‐coupling approach.
