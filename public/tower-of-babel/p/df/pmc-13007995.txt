Reassessing Transketolase Assays: Methodological Considerations for Detecting Functional Thiamine Deficiency
Authors: Katie A Edwards
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThiamine (vitamin B1) deficiency due to degradation, inadequate intake, impaired absorption, or increased metabolic demand remains prevalent in both human and animal populations. In its diphosphate form (thiamine diphosphate, TDP), thiamine serves as an essential cofactor for metabolic enzymes, including transketolase (TKT), a rate‐limiting enzyme of the pentose‐phosphate pathway. Measurement of TKT activity with and without exogenous TDP provides a functional assessment of thiamine utilization that serves as a surrogate for or complements direct quantification of thiamine forms. Beyond its historical role as a deficiency‐screening tool, the TKT assay offers opportunities to interrogate enzyme function and cofactor dependence. Strategic variation of assay conditions, such as higher TDP concentrations to identify low‐affinity TKT variants or including or omitting magnesium to assess functional cofactor limitation, can distinguish true deficiency from impaired enzyme utilization or altered enzyme properties. This review evaluates preanalytical variables, assay methodologies, and data presentation strategies used for TKT measurements in erythrocytes and other tissues across human and nonhuman studies. Emphasis is placed on biological and methodological determinants that shape measured activity and responsiveness. Improved interpretation frameworks and thoughtful assay design can expand the utility of TKT measurements as indicators of functional thiamine status across clinical, translational, and ecological contexts.Keywords: enzyme assays, latency, pentose‐phosphate pathway, thiamine deficiency, transketolase, vitamin B1

Introduction

Thiamine (vitamin B1), in the form of thiamine diphosphate (TDP, also called thiamine pyrophosphate [TPP]), is an essential cofactor for enzymes that provide the link between glycolysis and the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, metabolize sugars and amino acids in the TCA cycle and pentose‐phosphate pathway (PPP), and are involved in fatty acid α‐oxidation [1]. One such enzyme is transketolase (TKT), a TDP‐dependent enzyme that catalyzes rate‐limiting reactions in the nonoxidative phase of the PPP. Beyond its role in assessing thiamine status, altered TKT activity is implicated in several disease states. Deficits in TKT activity resulting from reduced TKT expression, sequence differences, or post‐translational modifications that impair enzyme kinetics or cofactor affinity have been associated with Wernicke−Korsakoff syndrome [2−5]. This progressive neurological disease is most often linked to chronic alcohol use and thiamine deficiency, but underlying TKT deficits may predispose some individuals to a poorer ability to respond to circulating levels of thiamine. Reduced TKT activity has also been reported in the brains of patients with Alzheimer's disease and erythrocytes of diabetic patients [6, 7]. Alzheimer's disease and diabetes share convergent oxidative‐stress pathways in which impaired glucose metabolism and altered TKT‐dependent PPP activity can limit nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) generation, weaken antioxidant defenses, and contribute to neuronal and vascular injury [8, 9]. By contrast, increased TKT activity has been associated with poor prognosis in several cancers, where increased PPP flux is thought to support rapid nucleotide synthesis and enhanced NADPH production, thereby facilitating tumor growth and conferring resistance to endogenous cellular oxidative stress defense mechanisms [10, 11].

Historically, thiamine deficiency in humans has often been linked to diets consisting primarily of white rice, in which the removal of the thiamine‐rich hulls during processing, combined with limited intake of complementary thiamine sources lead to inadequate intake [12]. However, thiamine requirements correlate more closely with caloric intake (0.3 mg thiamine per 1000 kcal consumed) rather than a fixed mass of thiamine [13]. Diets with a high carbohydrate intake increase metabolic demand for thiamine, making individuals consuming refined grains particularly vulnerable [12, 13]. Although thiamine deficiency is often presumed uncommon in developed countries, this assumption largely reflects fortification practices in select countries (e.g., the United States, Canada, Australia, and the UK) rather than a universal policy. Even where fortification of staple foods, including rice, cereals, pasta, and bread, is implemented, thiamine requirements may be elevated in populations consuming high‐carbohydrate diets. Other common risks for deficiency in developed countries include obesity [14], bariatric surgeries [15], Crohn's disease [16], prolonged emesis such as in hyperemesis gravidarum [17], and alcohol use disorder [18]. These deficiencies are usually attributed to poor absorption of available thiamine, but they may also be due to low intake and altered expression of thiamine‐activating and metabolizing enzymes [19]. Consumption of foods containing thiaminases (enzymes that break down thiamine) [20, 21], preserved or processed under degrading conditions [22], or that complex with thiamine and reduce its bioavailability also can cause deficiencies [23−26]. Notably, prolonged thiamine deficiency can reduce TKT expression [27, 28]. Together, these factors indicate that thiamine deficiency and insufficiency remain clinically relevant yet likely under‐recognized in both developed and resource‐limited settings, particularly among populations with elevated metabolic demand or impaired absorption.

Despite these risks, blood thiamine levels are relatively infrequently measured compared to other vitamins. For example, the United States CDC National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey includes dietary thiamine intake data through patient surveys and reports circulating levels of various vitamins, including vitamin A, C, D, and folate, but not thiamine. In the NIH All of Us Research Hub, thiamine measurements were available for only ∼2% of participants with at least one vitamin‐related laboratory result in their electronic health record, compared to ∼29% and 23% of the participants with measurements for vitamin B12 and 25‐hydroxyvitamin D3 in serum or plasma, respectively (Figure 1).

Methods of Assessment of Thiamine Status

Assessment of long‐term thiamine status is preferably performed in whole blood, as plasma levels primarily reflect recent intake (free thiamine in plasma has a half‐life of only a few hours after intake [29, 30]). Further, because phosphorylation of thiamine to TDP occurs intracellularly, whole blood provides a more complete representation of the biologically active pool. Free thiamine predominates at low concentrations in plasma (≤∼10 nM), whereas approximately 80% of the total body TDP is contained within the erythrocytes [31−34]. Absent of intake, whole body thiamine stores are depleted over 2−3 weeks [35], which reflects in large part the high intracellular concentration and slower turnover from erythrocytes than plasma. TDP concentrations in whole blood and washed erythrocytes showed strong correlations [36]; however, washing erythrocytes is laborious, time‐consuming, can lead to losses of TDP and other thiamine forms, and introduce additional analytical variability, making whole blood preferable for concentration‐based analyses [34, 36, 37, 38].

Laboratory assessments of thiamine status are usually carried out using high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) with fluorescence detection following oxidation to thiochrome, or increasingly by mass spectrometry after chromatographic separation of native species [37, 39−41]. Chromatographic separation allows for the quantification of the parent and phosphorylated forms of thiamine, which yields valuable insights into active versus total concentrations. However, direct quantification remains challenging due to the polarity and lability of thiamine forms, susceptibility to dephosphorylation, adsorption to surfaces, and the requirement to release bound TDP from intracellular enzymes prior to analysis [39, 42]. Despite efforts to develop more rapid or simplified approaches, chromatographic methods remain the standard practice and are confined to relatively few centralized laboratories, contributing to higher cost and delays in result availability [39, 43].

An alternative approach is the determination of the erythrocyte transketolase activity coefficient (ETKAC) or ratio, which requires assessment of functional thiamine availability by measuring the activity of TKT in the presence and absence of exogenous TDP [36, 41, 44, 45, 46, 47, 48]. When thiamine is limiting, supplementation with TDP increases TKT activity, and the percent increase relative to basal conditions (often termed the TPP effect or percent activation) reflects the degree of saturation by endogenous TDP [49]. Throughout, references to the supplemented (activated erythrocyte TKT activity [AETK]) to basal activity (erythrocyte TKT activity [ETK]) ratio refer to the ETKAC (AETK/ETK). In human erythrocytes with adequate thiamine status, the supplemented to basal TKT activity ratio, or ETKAC, should be ≤1.15 (percent activation ≤15%); increases above 1.15 (>15% activation) indicate thiamine insufficiency, while those greater than 1.25 (25% activation) are indicative of severe thiamine deficiency [50, 51].

TKT assays can be carried out in a microtiter plate format using standard plate readers, enabling parallel analysis of multiple samples with reduced instrumentation and maintenance costs, and broader availability relative to chromatographic approaches for thiamine. Assays can also be automated using standard clinical chemistry analyzers [52]. Notably, the extensive UK National Diet and Nutrition Survey does not include blood thiamine levels but does report ETKAC data as a measure of thiamine status [53]. As discussed further below, washed erythrocytes are the preferred sample type for TKT assays in humans, although these assays are not exempt from the caveats mentioned above for this sample matrix, including the need to wash erythrocytes soon after sample collection and before freezing [54]. TKT‐based assay detractors similarly include limited availability at clinical testing laboratories and assay variability between laboratories [32, 37, 45, 55].

A limitation shared by both chromatographic and enzymatic approaches is the lack of universally applied standards and reference materials. Although isotopically labeled internal standards are available for mass spectrometry, internal standards are infrequently used in fluorescence‐based HPLC assays [40]. Comparatively, TKT‐based assays lack standardized controls with defined specific activities or known degrees of thiamine deficiency, contributing to interlaboratory variability. The absence of harmonized calibration materials and reference ranges complicates cross‐study comparisons and underscores the need for improved standardization across both analytical frameworks. Further, while the results and reference values of TDP are often reported as a concentration (e.g., reference values from the Mayo Clinic Laboratories 70–180 nM [56]), normalization to the hemoglobin content (e.g., ∼270–675 ng/g Hb) is a more accurate practice [36, 38].

Comparisons of the direct measurement of thiamine or TDP and TKT activity have yielded mixed findings on the efficiency of the detection of thiamine deficiency [36, 41, 45, 46, 48, 54, 57]. Some report greater sensitivity of direct TDP measurements in erythrocytes versus the determination of the ETKAC for detecting early deficiency [41, 46]. Given that the determination of the ETKAC requires measuring the relative rate of change of substrate depletion via ultraviolet (UV) light absorbance over time in the presence and absence of exogenous TDP, these differences are much more minor, and thus, the assay imprecision may be greater in nutritionally adequate or mildly deficient subjects [41]. Others have observed strong agreement between the methods, though similarly, some low thiamine levels may be missed using traditional ETKAC cutoffs [36]. Others have noted poor correlations between TDP and TKT‐based measures and expected thiamine status based on dietary intake, clinical symptoms of thiamine deficiency, or clinical response to supplemented thiamine [54, 58, 59, 60]. Basal TKT activity may have a stronger correlation with erythrocyte TDP concentration for the assessment of thiamine deficiency than the ETKAC in certain populations [45, 61]. A basal TKT activity cutoff of 0.59 U/g Hb has been used in studies [45, 61], though this has not been universally established [62]. Reference ranges for TDP concentrations, ETKA ratios, and specific TKT activities relevant to different age groups or populations at greater risk for thiamine deficiency may be beneficial in more accurately determining thiamine deficiency status [32, 45, 59, 62].

Low TKT activity may reflect inadequate phosphorylation of thiamine [63, 64], low expression of TKT [7, 59, 63], presence of endogenous inhibitors [65], and altered affinity for thiamine [66], thus chromatographic assays of thiamine and its phosphorylated forms can provide a more direct measure of thiamine availability [37, 41]. Conversely, measurement of basal and supplemented TKT activity, and the change in enzyme activity before and after thiamine supplementation in vitro or in vivo [37], offers insights into functional metabolic capacity and the ability to utilize available thiamine. Using both assays in concert can provide a powerful approach to understand the physical availability of systemic thiamine versus its functional utilization.

TKT measurements have been carried out for decades and were the predominant method of assessment of thiamine deficiency before direct thiamine chromatographic analysis became available. In addition to human erythrocytes, TKT activity is also measured in other tissues in laboratory animals for studying mechanisms and disease processes [67, 68] in both domesticated [69] and wild animals, particularly fish and bird populations, where thiamine deficiency is of ecological concern [46, 70, 71, 72, 73]. These nonerythrocyte tissues provide a valuable opportunity to examine how TKT behavior changes when tissue physiology, protein content, and cofactor availability differ from erythrocytes, highlighting both the strengths and limitations of applying traditional ETKA assumptions more broadly. TKT has been assayed in multiple tissues, including brain, kidneys, liver, and digestive gland tissue [46, 71, 72, 74, 75, 76, 77, 78]. Because most TKT protocols were historically optimized for human washed erythrocytes, implementing and interpreting their results across differing tissues requires consideration of baseline total protein concentrations, magnesium availability, and tissue‐specific TDP saturation. Expected sufficiency and deficiency ranges for nonhuman samples and nonerythrocyte tissues have not been well established in the literature, limiting translational interpretation. Moreover, in nonerythrocyte tissues, TKT activity can remain stable even after tissue TDP has fallen, indicating a delayed functional decline [46]. Accordingly, these tissues may better reflect later‐stage deficiency [46, 78]. The aim of this review is, therefore, not only to summarize assay formats, but also to clarify how biological context and methodological variation influence interpretation. This review encompasses foundational work identifying key assay limitations, methodological advances, with recent efforts toward assay standardization.

An Overview of the Biochemical Role of TKT

TKT and the availability of D‐ribulose‐5‐phosphate (Ru5P) are the key modulators of the nonoxidative phase of the PPP, with TKT reactions being rate‐limiting [49, 79]. The upstream enzymes ribose phosphate isomerase (RPIA) and ribulose phosphate‐3‐epimerase (RPE) convert Ru5P into ribose‐5‐P (R5P) and xylulose‐5‐phosphate (X5P), respectively (Figure 3) [44, 80]. These sugars serve as substrates for TKT, where the enzyme transfers a two‐carbon unit from X5P (donor) to either R5P or erythrose‐4‐phosphate (E4P) (acceptors), forming sedoheptulose‐7‐phosphate (S7P) and glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate (G3P) or fructose‐6‐phosphate (F6P) and G3P, respectively. The direction of these reversible reactions depends on the metabolic needs of the cell, where downstream products of the nonoxidative phase of the PPP can re‐enter glycolysis or the oxidative phase of the PPP. The PPP supplies both R5P for nucleotide biosynthesis and NADPH, which is critical for maintaining redox balance, supporting fatty acid, DNA, amino acid, and cholesterol synthesis, and xenobiotic metabolism [79]. Thus, TKT activity directly influences anabolic capacity, antioxidant defense, and cellular growth. Because the PPP shifts dynamically in response to metabolic needs, interpreting TKT activity requires understanding not only the substrate availability, but also the enzyme's structural organization and cofactor interactions.

An overview of the pentose‐phosphate pathway (PPP). In the oxidative phase of the PPP, glucose‐6‐phosphate (G6P) is irreversibly converted to 6‐phosphogluconolactone (6PGL) by glucose‐6‐phosphate dehydrogenase (G6PD), then to 6‐phosphogluconate (6PG) by 6‐phosphonogluconolactonase, and finally to ribulose‐5‐phosphate (Ru5P) by 6‐phosphogluconate dehydrogenase (6PGD), generating NADPH. In the nonoxidative phase, Ru5P is converted to ribose‐5‐phosphate (R5P) by ribose‐5‐phosphate isomerase (RPIA) or to xylulose‐5‐phosphate (X5P) by ribose‐5‐phosphate epimerase (RPE). Transketolase (TKT) catalyzes thiamine diphosphate (TDP)‐dependent carbon transfers in the nonoxidative phase, converting R5P and X5P to sedoheptulose‐7‐phosphate (S7P) and glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate (G3P), and erythrose‐4‐phosphate (E4P) and X5P to fructose‐6‐phosphate (F6P) and G3P, respectively. These products can feed back into glycolysis directly, or F6P can be converted back to G6P using glucose‐6‐phosphate isomerase (GPI) to feed back into either glycolysis or the oxidative phase of the PPP.

TKT Structure and Interactions

TKT from mammalian, bacteria, yeast, and plant sources has a monomeric mass of ∼70–75 kDa, but functions as a 140–150 kDa homodimer, with each monomer contributing an active site coordinated by a divalent cation and TDP [68, 81, 82, 83, 84, 85]. Dimerization is concentration‐dependent, and TDP binding between monomers stabilizes the holoenzyme and prevents dissociation into inactive apoforms [81, 83].

The Saccharomyces cerevisiae (baker's yeast) enzyme has been the most extensively studied [81, 86, 87, 88, 89, 90], whereas TKTs from other sources have been comparatively less characterized. Plant, yeast, and bacterial sequences share ∼50% identity, while human TKT shares only ∼27% (Table 1) [84, 91]. Approximately 50 amino acids are highly conserved across taxa and primarily support substrate and cofactor binding [84, 91]. Thorough studies have examined the kinetics and affinity of yeast TKT under various conditions [86, 87, 89, 93], which are beyond the scope of the present review. However, some takeaways are briefly mentioned to present the context of carrying out TKT activity measurements, with the caveat that properties of the well‐studied yeast TKT may not be well conserved with those from higher‐order species [94−96].

Reconstitution kinetics of the purified yeast apoenzyme depend on both TKT protein concentration and coenzyme availability [89, 93]. Yeast TKT requires a divalent cation for optimal activity, although once reconstituted, it shows relatively modest cation preference [81, 86]. The two active sites exhibit distinct metal affinities, one high and one low [83, 87, 97]. During reconstitution, substrate binding rates have been reported to decrease in the order Ca2+>Mn2+>Co2+>Mg2+>Ni2+ [86]. Ca2+ was found bound in the native holoenzyme of the S. cerevisiae TKT [87], and substitution of Mg2+ for Ca2+ reduced holoenzyme stability and TDP affinity [88, 89]. Negative cooperativity for TDP has been observed with both Mg2+ and Ca2+, accompanied by kinetically distinct Km values [89]. The interaction among TKT, Mg2+, and TDP is reversible, and maximal activity for the yeast TKT has been reported when the constituents were preincubated at pH 5.0 [86]. Chelation experiments highlight the importance of divalent cations: ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA) markedly reduced apoenzyme activity, and more gradually suppressed holoenzyme activity [89], consistent with preferential retention of divalent cations by the holoenzyme and its greater stability in their presence. With sufficiently high TDP, yeast TKT can retain appreciable activity in the absence of a divalent cofactor, albeit with a higher TDP Km [86, 87]. However, Cu2+ abolished activity [86], a property we recently also observed in certain TDP‐degrading microbial thiaminase I enzymes [98].

TDP forms hydrogen bonds with residues in the active site, along with electrostatic interactions with the divalent cofactor [81, 97], while its pyrimidine ring participates in hydrogen bonds and hydrophobic interactions. Oxythiamine pyrophosphate is a potent inhibitor (Ki = 30 nM) and binds more tightly to the apoenzyme than TDP itself (Km = 1.0 µM) [86]. Like thiamine, oxythiamine requires phosphorylation to become active [99]. Some inhibition due to phosphate ions alone has been reported, with a Ki for sodium diphosphate of 4.2 mM [86]. Hydroxypyruvate and hydroxyphenylpyruvate, by contrast, act as competitive donor substrates [100, 101]. Ongoing efforts aim to develop clinically useful, nonthiamine‐based TKT inhibitors due to the lack of specificity of thiamine analogues and risk of inhibition of other thiamine‐dependent enzymes [102, 103].

Two kinetically distinct yeast TKT populations, one with low and one with high affinity, have been described, and their interconversion contributes to an initial lag phase in activity [89]. In Escherichia coli, analogous high TDP affinity and low TDP affinity subpopulations of TKT enzymes have been identified, with their relative abundance modulated by oxidative stress [83, 104]. Oxidation of specific cysteine residues increases TDP affinity ∼35‐fold [83], suggesting a mechanism for rapidly enhancing PPP flux during oxidative stress [104]. Although this residue is conserved in yeast, bacteria, plants, and protozoa, it is not present in TKT from humans or mice [104].

Temperature optima reflect the physiology of the organism, with TKT from yeast being optimal at 28°C, consistent with the optimal temperature for growth of yeast cells [86], whereas that from the rat and human brain exhibiting an optimal of 37°C, consistent with their body temperatures [96]. For deeper mechanistic discussion of cofactor interactions and catalytic mechanisms, readers are directed to foundational structural and kinetic studies [81, 82, 90, 91, 95, 97, 104, 105].

Together, these biochemical properties frame the constraints of any TKT‐based functional assay, motivating a closer examination of how TKT measurements capture mechanistic properties across sample types and how methodological variation can shape result interpretation.

Monitoring Thiamine Deficiency Using TKT Activity

Determination of the ETKAC requires the measurement of TKT activity in hemolysates under both basal conditions and after supplementation with exogenous TDP. Erythrocytes are a practical sample type for assessing functional thiamine status as they deplete thiamine at approximately the same rate as other less accessible tissues [51, 67]. Further, these cells lack mitochondria and rely solely on the oxidative phase of the PPP for NADPH, making their reliance on the PPP critical for maintaining NADPH levels. Erythrocytes are incapable of protein synthesis [106], thus their TKT levels (and other thiamine‐dependent enzymes or transporters) reflect expression in their precursor cells (erythroblasts and reticulocytes) [107]. Since most thiamine within erythrocytes is in the form of TDP [59], ETKA measurements provide a reasonable proxy for biologically available thiamine [55].

TKT Assay Procedures

Most modern TKT assays follow the scheme in Figure 4A, in which NADH oxidation is monitored through a coupled enzyme reaction involving α‐glycerophosphate dehydrogenase‐triosephosphate isomerase (α‐GDH‐TPI). With excess R5P, alone or in concert with X5P, the rate of exogenous NADH depletion at 340 nm (or via fluorescence at λex = 340 nm, λem = 465 nm) reflects TKT activity [80, 108]. Selecting an appropriate window for slope determination is essential. The window chosen needs to be long enough to allow the samples to reach thermal equilibrium, but short enough to avoid the risk of substrate depletion. Slopes from basal and TDP‐supplemented reactions are background‐corrected, pathlength‐normalized, and converted to NADH consumption rates using the extinction coefficient, which is ideally determined empirically in the laboratory‐specific assay buffer (Figure 4B) [62]. The percent increase in activity upon TDP addition serves as an index of functional thiamine status. Earlier reported methods monitored R5P disappearance via orcinol or hexose formation via anthrone, both reliant on colorimetric measurements proportional to the sugar content [109].

Although this review is centered on erythrocyte‐based clinical assays, TKT is widely measured in other tissues and species where thiamine deficiency is biologically consequential. Fish liver provides a particularly informative comparative system in which large basal‐supplemented differences, clear Mg2+ sensitivity, and storage‐related effects are highly resolvable, allowing assay behaviors that are subtle in erythrocytes to become mechanistically explicit [110]. Importantly, fish represent one of the few vertebrate systems in which population‐level thiamine insufficiency has clear ecological consequences, making liver TKT activity both a practical probe of assay performance and a biologically relevant window into tissue‐level thiamine utilization. In fish liver, the absolute magnitudes and differences between basal and TDP‐supplemented slopes are much larger and more easily resolved than in human erythrocytes using the same procedure (Figure 4B). The relatively small differences between basal and supplemented rates likely complicate the assessment of mild deficiency in humans.

Although this spectrophotometric framework is broadly conserved, methodological variation remains substantial, particularly regarding substrate choices, preincubation steps, and buffer composition. Some protocols include both R5P and X5P as TKT substrates [76, 94], whereas others use R5P alone [44, 62, 80, 108, 111]. X5P is very expensive (as of this writing, $313 per mg for the lithium salt) and has historically had low purity and poor commercial availability [94, 112, 113]. At one point, it was commercially unavailable, prompting the development of an enzymatic approach using xylulokinase to convert inexpensive xylose to X5P [113].

Endogenous nonoxidative PPP enzymes RPIA and RPE interconvert Ru5P, R5P, and X5P [44, 80]. Physiologically, TKT activity is contingent on the availability of these enzymes to convert endogenous precursors to its substrates R5P and X5P [114]. Providing an excess of R5P in the reaction mixture avoids the dependence on upstream enzymes in glycolysis or the oxidative phase of the PPP. R5P was saturating at 8 mM with no benefit to further increases in the analysis of erythrocytes [80]. The Km values for R5P and X5P in a purified human erythrocyte TKT preparation are 0.16 and 0.3 mM, respectively [94]. Although variations in RPIA or RPE could theoretically influence TKT activity, the responsiveness of erythrocyte TKT to TDP supplementation indicates that coenzyme availability is the limiting factor rather than upstream enzyme activity [109]. These reactions are reversible, and when supplied with exogenous R5P, RPIA can reversibly convert R5P to Ru5P, and RPE can convert Ru5P to X5P, providing X5P within the sample. These enzymes are highly expressed in erythrocytes, allowing the formation of X5P in situ, and the addition of exogenous X5P is not required [111, 113, 115, 116]. Our own data likewise indicated that exogenous X5P was unnecessary for fish liver TKT assays [110]. Per the Human Protein Atlas, both RPIA and RPE exhibit broad cytoplasmic expression, suggesting that the exogenous X5P is likely unnecessary in other tissues as well [117, 118]. However, further interpretive information may be realized by running the assay with R5P and both with and without X5P to ensure that RPIA and RPE are consistently available across the same tissues within a sample population [110].

Beyond substrate considerations, coupled assays are supplied with exogenous α‐GDH‐TPI. TPI is endogenously involved in glycolysis and reversibly converts D‐glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate to dihydroxyacetone‐phosphate (Figure 3). α‐GDH reduces this product to glycerol‐3‐phosphate (G3P), oxidizing NADH in the process. Early procedures included monoiodoacetate to inhibit endogenous glyceraldehyde‐3‐phosphate‐dehydrogenase (GAPDH), which had the potential for conversion of G3P to diphosphoglycerate and thus limiting the oxidation of NADH. However, inclusion of this compound reduced measured TKT activity and was found to be unnecessary in erythrocytes [80]. The commercially supplied α‐GDH‐TPI enzymes typically used in TKT assays are supplied as rabbit muscle preparations and with activity units defined at 25°C [119]. In practice, most erythrocyte TKT assays are run at 37°C [44, 62, 94], which better approximates physiological conditions and ensures consistency with the physiological temperature of the coupling enzyme source. By contrast, reducing assay temperature to match ectothermic species (e.g., 10–15°C for cold‐water fish) risks shifting the rate‐limiting step from TKT to the coupling enzymes and suppressing apparent TKT activity. Thus, assay temperature should be chosen within a range that has been empirically validated for both TKT and the auxiliary enzymes, rather than simply mirroring the body temperature of the organism under study. Aside from absolute temperature, thermal consistency is key, which is especially a consideration for assays run in a microplate format [44].

For human erythrocytes, the incubation is optimally carried out at 37°C [44, 62, 94] and at pH 7.6 in 100 mM Tris‐HCl buffer [80, 108]. These conditions also yield maximal apoenzyme stability for TKT from the human brain [96]. Higher Tris concentrations inhibit purified yeast TKT, while phosphate, glycine, or triethanolamine buffers at similar concentrations and pH yield significantly decreased activity [80, 86]. Moderate concentrations of sulfate ions (4.9 mM) can be activating and yield a reduced Km for TDP, though higher concentrations and phosphate ions can be inhibitory [86, 94]. Dithiotreitol (DTT) has been shown to increase activity, likely by reducing cysteine residues [94]. Although the cysteine‐mediated oxidative activation mechanism described in yeast and bacteria is absent in human TKT, inclusion of reducing agents such as DTT can improve human erythrocyte TKT reconstitution primarily by preventing nonspecific oxidative inactivation during assay handling, rather than representing a physiological regulatory mechanism [83, 104].

Some extraction protocols include protease and phosphatase inhibitors to preserve enzyme integrity [120]. NADH is saturating for human erythrocytes at 10 mM, and higher concentrations are avoided to ensure that its losses are in a detectable range of the spectrophotometer [80]. Tween‐20 is sometimes included to improve the consistency of the meniscus and pathlength homogeneity when the assay is carried out in a microplate format [44], although its concentration may require adjustment for different sample types. In hemoglobin‐rich samples, potassium dichromate is used as a reference to compensate for the color imparted by hemoglobin and its degradation products [80, 121]. Attempts to chromatographically isolate TKT from hemoglobin increase complexity and reduce throughput [121], though they do help to elucidate the presence of TKT variants with varying isoelectric points and reduced affinity in hemolysates [121].

Preincubation procedures prior to measurement differ widely across studies. Some protocols preincubate samples with TDP, NADH, and α‐GDH‐TPI before substrate addition [70, 76], others incubate only with TDP [44], and some include all reagents in the preincubation step [80]. Although systematic comparisons are lacking, a preincubation of 15 min with TDP appears sufficient for apoenzyme reconstitution in lysed erythrocytes [80, 122].

Despite the historic variability, recent efforts to standardize methods in human erythrocytes will undoubtedly increase the assay consistency [44, 62]. Similar efforts are needed to validate TKT assays and establish standardized protocols in other tissue types [110].

Impact of Magnesium

Among the variables influencing TKT activity, magnesium availability warrants particular attention. Although Mg2+ is required for holo‐TKT assembly, its effect on measured activity depends strongly on the baseline TDP saturation state of the enzyme and on the tissue‐specific biochemical context. Some TKT procedures include magnesium chloride as an exogenous Mg2+ source [6, 76, 94, 120]. Although this is necessary for maximal spinach TKT activity, it has been reported not to be a requirement for TKT activity in rat liver or erythrocytes [80, 123]. However, this distinction is likely reflective of the endogenous availability of Mg2+ in the samples. Mg2+ is usually not added to erythrocyte TKT assay protocols [44, 62, 116], and as either whole‐blood anticoagulant EDTA or lithium heparin collection tubes may be used [44], this suggests that endogenous Mg2+ remains sufficiently bound to support holoenzyme stability even in EDTA‐anticoagulated samples. In another human erythrocyte report, the addition of Mg2+ to the assay at a high concentration (30 mM) resulted in decreased TKT activity [80].

Our work in lake trout liver similarly showed inhibitory effects at high added Mg2+ concentrations (>10 mM), but a clear increase in TKT activity by adding ∼0.5 mM Mg2+, both in the presence and absence of supplemental TDP (Table 2) [110]. We cannot rule out a dietary Mg2+ deficiency in these fish, and the apparent variability in Mg2+ requirement across assays likely reflects differences in its endogenous availability. Clinical data support this context dependence. In a study on human patients with alcohol use disorder, intravenous thiamine plus magnesium increased ETK more than thiamine alone [124], and patients with low serum magnesium concentrations (<0.75 mM) exhibited a poorer correlation of basal TKT values with erythrocyte TDP concentrations than those with normal magnesium concentrations [125].

Not adding Mg2+ to the assay in vitro means that the specific activity reflects the availability of both TDP and Mg2+ [110]. In purified systems, Mg2+ lowers the Km for TDP [86], meaning that the same level of TKT activity can be attained with lower TDP concentrations when Mg2+ is abundant. The stabilization imparted on the holoenzyme by Mg2+ is more critical at low TDP levels than when TDP is available at higher concentrations. Running samples without added Mg2+ can thus yield TKT values that are affected by the lower Km for TDP and thus are highly dependent on endogenous Mg2+ levels. By contrast, adding Mg2+ to TKT assays can simplify interpretation by isolating TDP responsiveness, but doing so yields a nonphysiological measure of functional thiamine status because it overrides activity derived from endogenous, circulating Mg2+ levels [110].

Our lake trout liver data highlight a practical consequence: Mg2+ addition increased both basal and TDP‐supplemented activities in absolute terms, but not proportionally. In this data, Mg2+ proportionally increased the basal activity to a greater extent. As a result, Mg2+ artificially reduced the supplemented:basal ratio and the calculated latency (Table 2), which could potentially mask a functional thiamine deficiency [110].

Without added Mg2+, samples with high endogenous Mg2+ (e.g., from dietary sufficiency) may show a higher basal TKT activity than Mg2+‐deficient samples, even if TDP levels are similar. This may help to explain the imperfect correlation between directly measured TDP and TKT‐based functional assays. A sample with low TDP could still meet the ETKAC cutoff criteria if its endogenous Mg2+ is high enough to maintain acceptable activity.

For cases with borderline ETKAC values, evaluating Mg2+ status of samples may, therefore, improve the agreement between laboratory measurements and clinical symptoms of thiamine deficiency. Magnesium also affects the activity of several other thiamine‐dependent or thiamine‐processing enzymes, including thiamine pyrophosphokinase 1, pyruvate dehydrogenase, 2‐hydroxyacyl‐CoA lyase 1, branched chain ketoacid dehydrogenase, and alpha‐ketoglutarate dehydrogenase, and Mg2+ deficiency can mimic symptoms of thiamine deficiency in humans [124, 126, 127]. A paired assay framework in which borderline or deficient samples are tested both with and without added TDP and Mg2+ can, therefore, help to distinguish true thiamine deficiency from functional impairment due to inadequate Mg2+ availability. Importantly, serum magnesium represents a small fraction of total body magnesium and does not necessarily reflect intracellular or erythrocyte magnesium availability [128], the compartment most relevant to TKT function. This discrepancy may help explain why patients classified as “magnesium‐replete” on serum testing may still demonstrate limited TKT responsiveness—a gap that a paired testing scheme can help resolve.

Data Presentations of TKT Activity

Given the number of biochemical and procedural differences across TKT assays, the way results are mathematically expressed is critical for accurate interpretation. This section, therefore, distinguishes commonly used reporting metrics, including specific activity, activation ratios, latency, and TPP effect, and clarifies how each reflects different aspects of enzyme behavior.

Although the terms ETK and AETK originate in erythrocytes, they are synonymous with basal and TDP‐supplemented activities, respectively, in other tissue types. These rates may be reported on a tissue mass–normalized basis (e.g., pmol/min/mg tissue) or as specific activity when normalized to total protein or hemoglobin (e.g., pmol/min/µg protein) [110]. Tissue‐normalized rates are more reflective of the integrated metabolic capacity within a given sample, whereas protein‐normalized activities account for extraction efficiency and relative TKT abundance. For erythrocytes, more commonly, results are expressed as the ETKA ratio (AETK/ETK) (also known as ETKAC), which serves as a functional index of thiamine availability that is less sensitive to absolute enzyme content (Equation 1).

The concentration of TDP needed to achieve maximum activity can be determined by titrating the TDP concentration until saturation [110]. Latency is linearly related to basal activity only if the TDP‐supplemented activity remains constant across samples; the TPP effect is nonlinear and disproportionately influenced by small basal values, potentially overweighting high TPP values in population analyses [130]. Further, latency typically exhibits stronger statistical power than the TPP effect when the same data set is used [130].

Limitations of Ratio‐Based Presentations

Ratio, percent, latency, and TPP effect metrics indicate relative change, not the absolute specific activities. Two samples can have the same latencies, yet differ substantially in specific activity (Equations 8 and 9).

In our human erythrocyte data, basal specific activity varied sixfold across samples, while lake trout liver samples varied 108‐fold [110]. The broad differences in specific activity were similar when comparing TDP‐supplemented data. When basal and supplemented activities were regressed against each other, the data were highly correlated [110, 129]. Samples with higher basal activity showed proportionally higher supplemented activity, indicating consistent abundance across tissues. The positive intercept of this regression on the y‐axis, representing the TDP‐stimulated data, suggests that even samples with low basal activity retain a pool of apo‐TKT that can be activated by exogenous TDP [129]. However, error propagation is a concern using the TPP effect presentation due to the additive effects of the data error when first calculating the difference and then the ratio of the supplemented and basal rates [129]. This is magnified by the low TKT activity in erythrocytes and instrumental limitations in its precise detection [129]. The same propagation of error could be assumed with the presentation of the data as percent latency.

Considerations on the Implementation and Interpretation of TKT Assay Results

Determining the ETKA ratio is straightforward, but determining specific activities for basal and activated TKT activity depends on the conditions used in a given laboratory, which can make intralaboratory comparisons challenging [59]. Differences in the hemolytic procedure are one potential source of variation, as well as preincubation time, temperature, and composition, reagent purity, and stability concerns of R5P when stored in an aqueous solution, and TKT itself when the purified enzyme is stored at 0°C overnight [7, 80, 94, 122]. Normalizing activities to hemoglobin or total protein concentration introduces other assays with their own potential variation. It is advisable to carry out total protein concentration assays on the same diluted lysate used for TKT assays to ensure accurate normalization [44]. Further, our recent results have shown that specific activity can be underestimated if the total protein concentration is too low, presumably due to insufficient dimerization of the available TKT [110]. It would be of interest to understand if low hemoglobin concentrations as a marker of total protein concentration in some human erythrocyte samples are associated with low latency, paralleling underestimates of ETKA ratios and possibly masking a thiamine deficiency.

Leukocytes also contribute to TKT activity, with approximately 100‐fold greater activity per cell than erythrocytes [134], making thorough washing of erythrocytes crucial when reporting specific activities [80]. Leukocytes, monocytes, and neutrophils have markedly higher TKT expression than B cells, lymphocytes, and natural killer cells [135]. ETKA ratios are less sensitive to leukocyte contamination and can, therefore, be applied to whole blood hemolysates, which offers easier preparation than washed erythrocytes [134]. Because leukocytes have short half‐lives, their TKT activity likely reflects short‐term status and is less suitable as a marker of long‐term thiamine status [44]. Similarly, while the age‐adjusted value can improve the reliability of the interpretation of specific activity measurements as a measure of deficiency, the ratio remains simpler as both basal and supplemented TKT activity decline equivalently with age [132]. Presumably, collectively, this is why the ETKA ratios are more commonly reported in published works and large databases than are specific activities.

Tissue‐specific handling and storage considerations can substantially influence measured TKT activity. In human whole blood, ETKA ratios did not differ significantly when samples were stored in lithium‐heparin tubes for 2 versus 24 h at 4°C prior to erythrocyte washing [44]. Washed erythrocytes are typically stored frozen, a process that induces hemolysis in addition to subsequent reconstitution in a low osmotic strength solution [80]. Storage‐related effects have been reported, including reduced erythrocyte activity and TPP effect within 2 weeks at −20°C, particularly in nutritionally abnormal patients [136]. By contrast, no significant stability concerns were noted after at least 1 month of storage at −20°C and at least 6 months at −70°C. Losses in activity have been noted after more than two freeze‐thaw cycles of hemolysate [80], although conflicting findings exist [108]. Protease and phosphatase inhibitors may mitigate degradation [120], although their influence on assay performance requires validation.

Our recent work further underscores the importance of interpreting specific activities alongside ratios, latencies, or TPP effects. In preprint findings from our group, fish liver stored for 24 h at 4°C or −20°C exhibited reduced latency compared to −80°C storage, driven by disproportionately greater suppression of supplemented versus basal activity [137]. This pattern is consistent with earlier reports of greater apoenzyme instability relative to holoenzyme, as previously observed in rat and human brains [74, 96]. Although considered a preanalytical artifact, preferential loss of apoenzyme may also occur in vivo, potentially contributing to reduced functional PPP capacity under deficiency conditions rather than reflecting assay behavior alone. Once assembled, the holoenzyme appears relatively stable and may be preferentially retained, which may help explain reports of preserved liver TKT activity despite substantial reductions of TDP pools [46], effectively buffering early biochemical deficiency. Conversely, another study reported increased latency when fish livers were frozen prior to extraction versus immediate processing [76]. Restoration of activity upon TDP supplementation suggests that some storage effects are reversible and may reflect degradation of endogenous TDP or selective destabilization of low‐affinity enzyme populations. Collectively, these observations underscore the need for careful control and reporting of preanalytical handling conditions.

Jones et al. reported basal TKT activity in human erythrocytes ranging from as little as 0.09 to 1.37 U/g Hb, with mean values of 0.48 and 0.75 U/g Hb, respectively, in cohorts from Gambia to the UK [62]. Using the definition of one unit as µmol/min and recognizing that hemoglobin comprises the vast majority of erythrocyte protein, this range corresponds approximately to 0.09–1.37 pmol/min/µg protein. Basal TKT activities in lake trout liver (typically ∼1.6–7.6 pmol/min/µg protein [Table 2] [110]) are generally higher, but remain roughly within an order of magnitude of human erythrocyte activity. This suggests broadly comparable baseline catalytic capacity across tissues despite substantial physiological and metabolic differences.

By contrast, the magnitude of TDP‐stimulated activation is far greater in the liver, where supplemented activities can exceed basal activities by ∼2.5‐ to 6‐fold, compared with the modest activation typically observed in erythrocytes (1.15‐ to 1.25‐fold). This larger dynamic range makes liver a particularly informative system for resolving biochemical determinants of TKT function. Expanding the information content of the assay to include varying TDP concentrations and paired ±Mg2+ conditions enables determination of Km values and characterization of functional Mg2+ dependence that may be difficult to discern in a limited dynamic range tissue, such as erythrocytes. Importantly, these insights emerge because both tissues were evaluated using a standardized assay framework, allowing differences in biological behavior, rather than methodological variation, to be revealed. Together, these findings reinforce that TKT activity is inherently tissue‐context dependent and that assay reference values and interpretive thresholds cannot be assumed to translate across species or tissues.

Conclusions

TKT assay measurements offer a uniquely informative window into functional thiamine status by directly probing the activity of a thiamine‐dependent enzyme central to PPP flux, redox homeostasis, and biosynthetic capacity. Because TKT activity integrates cofactor availability, enzyme abundance, and cellular context, these assays capture aspects of thiamine sufficiency that are not always apparent from static measurements of circulating thiamine or TDP concentrations. This functional perspective is particularly relevant in clinical settings where metabolic demand, illness, or altered enzyme regulation may uncouple thiamine levels from cellular utilization.

At the same time, the interpretation of TKT‐based measurements requires careful consideration of what the assay is designed to report. TKT activation assays are indirect, functional measures that do not quantify thiamine or TDP concentration directly, but instead assess the capacity of TKT to utilize TDP within its biochemical context. As such, the assay inherently integrates enzyme expression, cofactor availability (including magnesium), affinity, and structural state. These features represent a strength of the assay for evaluating functional thiamine status, but also mean that apparent discrepancies across studies may arise when differences in sample type, total protein concentration, magnesium availability, preincubation conditions, or data presentation are not explicitly considered. Collectively, these factors can substantially influence measured specific activity and responsiveness to TDP, with direct implications for clinical classification of sufficiency, insufficiency, or deficiency. Adoption of standard protocols is key. Importantly, assays developed and validated for erythrocytes cannot be assumed to translate directly to other tissues without appropriate validation, essential for meaningful tissue‐specific or disease‐focused interpretation.

From a clinical perspective, reliance on relative metrics alone, such as activation ratios, latency, or TPP effect, may obscure meaningful differences in absolute enzyme activity, potentially leading to under‐recognition of impaired nonoxidative PPP function and its broader relevance to disease. Reporting basal and supplemented specific activities alongside ratios would improve diagnostic clarity, facilitate interlaboratory comparisons, and enhance the ability to distinguish between true thiamine deficiency, impaired thiamine activation, and reduced enzyme expression. This distinction is particularly relevant in populations at elevated risk for thiamine‐related pathology, including individuals with alcohol use disorder, malnutrition, gastrointestinal disease, bariatric surgery, critical illness, or neurodegenerative disease.

There remains a pressing need to establish clinically meaningful, context‐specific reference ranges for TKT activity that account for age, dietary fortification, metabolic demand, and comorbid disease state. Such frameworks would improve the utility of TKT assays not only for identifying deficiency, but also for guiding therapeutic decisions, monitoring response to thiamine supplementation, and understanding interindividual variability in clinical outcomes. For nonhuman systems and translational models, the lack of validated tissue‐specific reference values similarly limits interpretation and cross‐species comparison. Recent efforts to standardize human ETK measurements provide a useful methodological foundation for assay harmonization (e.g., Refs. [44, 62]), but extension of such frameworks to other species and tissues will require context‐specific validation, reference ranges, and interpretive criteria.

In summary, TKT assays are a valuable clinical and investigative tool when applied with appropriate methodological rigor and interpretive care. The diagnostic utility can be further enhanced through expanded assay strategies, including paired conditions with and without magnesium to identify functional magnesium‐dependent thiamine underutilization, and higher TDP concentrations to identify low‐affinity enzyme variants. A clearer understanding of the biochemical determinants of TKT activity, coupled with standardized methods, reporting practices, and reference criteria, is essential for integrating these assays into modern approaches to diagnosing and managing thiamine‐related disorders across diverse patient populations and biological systems.
