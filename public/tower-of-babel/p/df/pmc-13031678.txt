CD146 + interstitial cells contribute to the dystrophic skeletal muscle phenotype in vitro
Authors: Bartosz Mierzejewski, Zuzanna Michalska, Dominika Kulma, Aleksandra Bos, Wladyslawa Streminska, Zbigniew Bartoszewicz, Edyta Brzoska
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
CD146 + interstitial cells are myogenic progenitors which contribute to skeletal muscle regeneration. However, their role in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) remains unclear. We compared CD146 + cells from wild-type and mdx mice, revealing distinct molecular and functional differences. Mdx CD146 + differentiated less efficiently into myoblasts and multinucleated myotubes but more efficiently into fibroblasts and adipocytes in vitro. Conditioned medium from mdx CD146 + cells impaired endothelial cell differentiation, through dysregulation of Sdf-1 and Angpt2 secretion. Transcriptomic analyses further reveal a dysregulation of key muscle-related and pro-angiogenic genes, along with an upregulation of fibrosis-associated factors. Mechanistically, we identify changes in the NF-κB, c-Jun, and c-Fos pathways activity as contributors to these pathological changes. These findings suggest that CD146 + cells may play a previously unrecognized role in promoting muscle fibrosis and vascular dysfunction in DMD, rather than aiding regeneration as observed in wild-type.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-38311-2.

Introduction

Skeletal muscle regeneration relies on population of stem cells associated with muscle fibres, called satellite cells (SCs), which become activated upon injury. These cells demonstrate an exceptional ability to regenerate injured skeletal muscle. However, in disease, for example, in patients suffering from Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), the regeneration process is disrupted. DMD is caused by a mutation in dystrophin encoding gene that results in dystrophin protein deficiency. The lack of dystrophin alters the function of dystrophin-associated protein complex (DAPC), which is responsible for anchoring muscle fibres in extracellular matrix (ECM). As a result, the sarcolemma, i.e., the muscle fiber membrane, is damaged during muscle contraction. It leads to an influx of calcium and other molecules leading to muscle fiber degeneration and initiation of repair process. Thus, constant and repeated muscle injury, inflammation, and recurring rounds of regeneration are observed in dystrophic muscles. Dystrophic pathogenesis involves the disruption of SC polarity, the loss of asymmetric cell division and a reduction in properly activated myogenic progenitor cells, eventually leading to the less efficient regeneration, and replacement of skeletal muscle by connective tissue1. Such changes result in progressive muscle wasting and impairment of contraction force. As a result, the respiratory failure, pulmonary infections, and cardiomyopathy lead to patient death2.

As we mentioned above, in dystrophic muscle, sarcolemma permeability is increased what leads to muscle fiber degeneration. In mature muscle, SCs remain mitotically quiescent until activated in response to the injury. Upon activation, they begin to proliferate and differentiate into myoblasts, which then fuse to form myotubes and new muscle fibers. SCs and differentiating myogenic cells express several transcription factors typical for myogenic cells such as Pax7, Myf5, Myod, myogenin, or Mrf4, which play crucial role in myogenic differentiation. SCs can divide asymmetrically and self-renew, which is crucial for skeletal muscle homeostasis. Importantly, the lack of dystrophin protein also has direct impact on the function of satellite cells. Satellite cells isolated from dystrophic muscles are characterized with abnormal cell divisions, altered metabolism, and mitochondrial dysfunction3–5.

It is well established that within skeletal muscles, not only SCs but also many other cell types play important roles in development, homeostasis, and pathogenesis6,7. Among them are immune cells, endothelial cells, nerve cells, pericytes associated with blood vessels, fibro-adipogenic progenitors (FAPs) and other interstitial cells (reviewed in8). Some of these cells directly participate in muscle regeneration by restoring vasculature, innervation, and ECM reconstruction. Other cells are responsible for regulation of SC and myoblast fate through secretion of growth factors and cytokines, such as, Hgf, Vegf, Igf-1 or Il-6, which support SC and myoblast proliferation and differentiation. Some of abovementioned cell populations were investigated as a potential source of cells for skeletal muscle therapies9. One of them are pericytes surrounding small caliber vessels. This cell population was described as a heterogenic and capable of differentiation into myogenic, fibrogenic, and adipogenic lineages. Mouse Tbx18 + pericytes were shown to differentiate in vitro into adipoblasts, chondroblasts, and osteoblasts. However, endogenous Tbx18 + cells maintained their primary identity and in injured mouse muscles did not contribute to other cell lines, such as, fibroblasts or myoblasts10. On the other hand, Alp+ pericytes present in mouse skeletal muscles were shown to be highly myogenic, able to fuse with myofibers, and enter the SC niche during postnatal skeletal muscle growth and regeneration11. These cells, also called mesoangioblasts, were successfully used to improve skeletal muscle function in dystrophic mice and dogs12. Further studies showed that pericytes residing in mouse muscles could be divided into two subpopulations: type-1 (nestin-/NG2+/CD146+/PDGFRα+) and type-2 (nestin+/NG2+/CD146+/PDGFRβ+). Type-2 pericytes were able to follow the myogenic programme in vitro and in vivo, after transplantation into injured muscles, while type-1 cells were more efficient in following the adipogenic programme12–14. More recent data indicate that CD146 marks a population of myogenic pericyte-like cells both in mouse and human skeletal muscles15,16. In mice, intramuscular injection of CD146 + pericytes resulted in improved muscle recovery after limb immobilization, mostly due to the formation of type IIa muscle fibers, increasing capillary content and collagen remodeling17. Another population that significantly affects skeletal muscle function are FAPs defined as cells expressing Pdgfrα and as a main source of fibrotic and adipose tissue in skeletal muscles. FAPs are progenitor cells crucial for maintaining proper muscle function. They are characterized by the ability to differentiate into fibroblasts and adipocytes, contributing to the production of ECM, muscle regeneration, and maintenance of the neuromuscular junctions. Depletion of FAPs resulted in decreased number of SCs, skeletal muscle atrophy, and impaired skeletal muscle regeneration18. Since FAPs are responsible for ECM deposition they contribute to pathological changes in the muscle. Under pathological conditions, FAP proliferation and differentiation leads to ectopic formation of adipose tissue or bone in the skeletal muscles19,20. Furthermore, FAPs can contribute to progression of the disease in mdx mice, mostly by regulating SC function, collagen deposition and adipose tissue formation21.

The purpose of this project was to analyze how the lack of dystrophin affects the properties and function of CD146 + interstitial myogenic pericyte-like cells. For this reason, we isolated CD146 + cells from the skeletal muscles of mdx mice and compared them with CD146 + isolated from the skeletal muscles of healthy mice. The phenotype and differentiation potential of WT and mdx CD146 + cells was analyzed as well as impact of their secretome on tubulogenesis. Next, the changes in transcriptome, secretome and selected signaling pathways activation were studied. The results allowed us to conclude that CD146 + cells contribute to the pathological phenotype of mdx mouse skeletal muscles.

Materials and methods

Animals and CD146 + cells isolation

Animal studies were approved by the Warsaw Local Ethics Committee No. 1 (permit number 1404/2022 and 1517P1/2023) and perfromed under the EU regulations. All details such as animal care and monitoring or experimental procedures were included in the permit. All methods were performed in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines and relevant regulations. Animals were anesthetized with isoflurane administered via a precision vaporizer (5% isofluran in O2, 1.0 L/min), and euthanasia was performed by cervical dislocation under deep general anesthesia. CD146 + cells were isolated from Gastrocnemius, EDL, Tibialis anterior, and Soleus muscles of 3–4-month-old transgenic Mcam/2xHA or C57BL/10ScSn-Dmdmdx/J (mdx) mice obtained from and maintained by Animal Facility at Faculty of Biology, University of Warsaw. Mcam/2xHA is a genetically modified mouse strain derived by us. These animals express the Mcam (CD146) protein with the 2xHA tag at the C-terminus. In this study, Mcam/2xHA animals were used as healthy animals (WT), which do not show dystrophic or any harmful phenotype. The mdx mice served as a model for DMD. Some isolated Gastrocnemius muscles from both Mcam/2xHA or mdx mice were snap frozen using liquid nitrogen cooled isopentane and stored at −80 °C for further cryosection and analysis. CD146 + cells from both mouse strains were isolated as previously described. Briefly, isolated muscles were cut into smaller pieces, incubated in 0.2% collagenase type I in DMEM at 37 ° C for 1.5 h, followed by the additional 30 min of incubation in dispase solution in DMEM solution (2 U/ml). Cell suspension was filtered, centrifuged, and cells expressing CD146 were selected using magnetic columns (MACS; Miltenyi Biotec) and antibodies against CD146 conjugated with ferromagnetic particles, according to the manufacturers protocol. Sorted CD146 + cells were subjected to RNA isolation or suspended in growth medium, that is, DMEM (glucose 4.5 g/l) supplemented with 15% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, seeded in culture plates and expanded under standard conditions: 37 °C, 5% CO2. The medium was replaced every 2 days.

Flow cytometry

Before sorting cells isolated from the hind limb muscles from of Mcam/2xHA or mdx mice were analyzed by flow cytometry for the presence of surface markers: CD146, Pdgfrβ and Pdgfrα. Briefly, after incubation in collagenase and dispase, cell suspension was filtered, centrifuged, and incubated with 3% BSA (bovine serum albumin) in PBS for 20 min at 4 °C. Then, cells were suspended in 3% BSA in PBS containing appropriate primary antibodies conjugated with fluorochromes against: CD146 (PE conjugated; 562196; BD Pharmigen), Pdgfrβ (APC-conjugated; 136008; Biolegend) or Pdgfrα (APC-conjugated; 135908; Biolegend). Cells were incubated with antibodies for 1 h at 4 °C, centrifuged, washed with PBS to remove remaining antibodies, centrifuged again and resuspended in PBS. Cells were subjected to flow cytometry analysis (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter) using CytExpert software. Unlabelled cells served as a negative control.

Quantified real-time PCR

Total RNA was extracted from CD146 + cells directly after isolation and sorting or 5-day long in vitro culture using High Pure Isolation Kit (Roche). RNA-based cDNA was synthesized using the RevertAid First-Strand cDNA synthesizer kit (ThermoFisher), according to the manufacturer’s protocol, under the following conditions: 25 ° C for 5 min, 42 °C for 90 min, and 70 ° C for 5 min. mRNA levels were evaluated using quantitative real-time PCR analysis (qRT-PCR) with TaqMan assays (ThermoFisher) for the following genes: Mcam (Mm00522397_m1), Alp (Mm00475834_m1), Pdgfrb (Mm01262487_m1), Cspg4 (encoding Ng2, Mm0050725_m1), Nes (Mm00450205_m1), Pdgfra (Mm00440701_m1), Peg3 (Mm01337379_m1), Pax7 (Mm01354484_m1), Myod (Mm00521984_m1). The expression of hypoxanthine phosphoribosyltransferase 1 (Hprt1; Mm03024075_m1) was used as a reference gene expression for further calculations. The reaction was carried out with TaqMan Gene Expression Master Mix (ThermoFisher) using LightCycler96 (Roche) under the following conditions: preincubation 2 min, 50 °C; preincubation 10 min, 95 °C; amplification 40 cycles 15 s, 95 °C and 1 min, 60 °C. Experiments included at least three independent biological replicates, each performed in two technical replicates. Expression levels were calculated using 2-(ΔCt) formula based on the relative expression of Hprt1.

Immunofluorescence

Frozen Gastrocnemius muscles from both Mcam/2xHA or mdx mice were sectioned using cryostat (Microm HM 505 N; Microm International GmbH) to obtain 7 μm thick cross sections which were then air dried and fixed with 3% PFA (paraformaldehyde) in PBS. CD146 + cells were cultured on cover slides covered with a 0.1% collagen solution in 0,1 M acetic acid. After 5 days of expansion in vitro cells were fixed with 3% PFA in PBS. Then, both muscle sections and cells were treated with 1% BSA/0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS and finally with 10% FBS/1% BA/0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS. The samples were incubated with the following primary antibodies: anti-CD146 (Rat; 134702, Biolegend), anti-Alp (Rabbit; bs-1535R, Invitrogen); anti-Pdgfrβ (Rabbit; 3169 S, CellSignaling), anti-skeletal muscle myosin (M7523, Sigma), anti-perilipin (9349 S, Cell Signaling), anti-laminin (Rabbit, L9393, Sigm-Aldrich) diluted 1:100 in 1% BSA/0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS o/n at 4 ° C. The samples were washed with 0.2% Triton-X100 in PBS solution and incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies conjugated with AlexaFluor 488 or AlexaFluor 594: anti-Rat (A21208, ThermoFisher), anti-Rabbit (A21207, ThermoFisher), or anti-Rabbit (A11037, ThermoFisher) diluted 1:200 in in 1% BSA/0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS for 1 h at room temperature. Cell nuclei were visualized using nuclear counterstain agent Hoechst 33,342 (Thermo Scientific). Specimens were mounted with fluorescent mounting medium (Dako Cytomation) and analyzed using a confocal microscope LSM 700 (Zeiss) and ZEN software (Zeiss).

Proliferation assay

CD146 + cells cultured in vitro were incubated with 0.5 µM carboxyfluorescein succinimidyl ester (CFSE, ThermoFisher) solution in PBS at 37 °C for 10 min. Cells were rinsed in PBS and cultured for 24–48 h in growth medium under standard conditions. The cells were then rinsed in PBS, trypsinised and subjected to flow cytometry analysis (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter) using CytExpert software (Version 2.4; https://www.mybeckman.pl/flow-cytometry/research-flow-cytometers/cytoflex/software). Unlabelled cells (negative control) and cells analyzed immediately after CFSE labeling (positive control) were included in each experiment.

Myogenic, fibrogenic, and adipogenic differentiation

CD146 + cells were cultured in vitro to reach at least 80% confluency (approx. 5 days). The growth medium was replaced with one of the following media: (1) skeletal muscle differentiation medium (C-23061, Promocell), containing 5% FBS, 1% penicillin-streptomycim, 50 mg/ml fetuin, 10 ng/ml epidermal growth factor (EGF), 1 ng/ml fibroblast growth factor (FGF), 10 ug/ml insulin and 0,4 ug/ml dexamethasone; (2) mesenchymal stem cell adipogenic differentiation medium 2 (pre-designed, ready-to-use; C-28016, Promocell); (3) fibrogenic differentiation medium containing DMEM (glucose 4,5 g/l) supplemented with 15% FBS, 1% penicillin/streptomycin and 10 ng/ml recombinant TGFβ (100 − 21; ThermoFisher). The cells were further cultured for 3 days in media inducing myogenic differentiation, 12 days in media inducing adipogenic differentiation, and 6 days in media inducing fibrogenic differentiation. Then, the cells were fixed and stained.

Cell medium preparation for in vitro angiogenesis assay

To prepare conditioned cell medium for the angiogenesis assay, CD146 + cells isolated from WT or mdx mice, were cultured in endothelial cell medium (ECMed, H1168, Cell Biologics) for 2 days. Next, the medium was collected, sampled, and stored at −20 ° C until angiogenesis assay. ECMed collected from CD146 + cell culture is referred to as ECMed-WT and ECMed-mdx, respectively.

Angiogenesis in vitro

The tube formation assay was performed on a 96-well µ-plate (89646, Ibidi). The inner wells of the plate were covered with 10 µl of Matrigel (354234, Corning) and incubated for 4 h at 37 ° C to allow polymerization. Then, the HUVECs (C2519AS, Lonza) were seeded at a density of 70 000 cells/cm2, according to the cell provider’s instructions. Control cells were cultured in Endothelial Cell Medium (ECMed, H1168, Cell Biologics), while experimental variants (WT, mdx) in medium collected from WT CD146+ (ECMed-WT) or mdx CD146+ (ECMed-mdx) cell culture, as described above. The HUVECs were maintained in culture for 18 h, at 37 ° C with 5% CO2, and then immediately photographed with the Nikon eclipse TE200 microscope and the Nikon Digital Sight DS-U2 camera. Measurement of parameters critical for angiogenesis such as: number of nodes, number of junctions, number of meshes, number of segments, total segment length, and total length (see Supplementary Fig. 2) was performed using Angiogenesis Analyzer plugin for Image J22 (available online http://image.bio.methods.free.fr/ImageJ/?lang=en).

Next-generation sequencing (NGS) of mRNA

For the total RNA-seq procedure, 275 ng of RNA (RIN > 9) obtained from cultured WT or mdx CD146 + cells was purified using KAPA Magnetic Beads (KAPA Biosystems, cat no. 07983298001) and then subjected to ribodepletion and library construction using the KAPA RNA HyperPrep Kit with RiboErase (KAPA Biosystems, cat. no. 08098140702) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The adapters used were KAPA_Univeral adapter and KAPA UDI primer mixes (KAPA Biosystems, cat. no. 9063781001, 9329838001). The samples were fragmented: 94 ° C 5 min, 12 enrichment cycles were performed. The resulting libraries were subjected to fragment length control using an Agilent TapeStation 4150 and a High Sensitivity D100 Reagent Kit (Agilent, cat. No.5067–5585) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 instrument using NovaSeq 6000 S1 Reagent Kit v 1.5 (200 cycles) reagents (Illumina, Cat. No. 20028318), in a pair-end read mode of pair-end reads 2 × 100 cycles using the standard procedure recommended by the manufacturer with the 1% addition of the Phix control library (Illumina, cat. no. FC-110–3001). Raw sequences were trimmed according to quality using Trimmomatic (version 0.39) using default parameters, except MINLEN, which was set to 50. The cut sequences were mapped to mouse reference genome provided by ENSEMBL (version grcm38_snp_tran) using Hisat2 with default parameters23,24. Optical duplicates were removed using the MarkDuplicates tool from GATK package (version 4.2.3.0) with default parameters except for OPTICAL_DUPLICATE_PIXEL_DISTANCE set to 12,00025. The mapped reads were associated with transcripts from the grcm38 database (Ensembl, version 102) using HTSeq-count (version 2.0.1) with default parameters except stranded set to “reverse”26,27. NGS raw data was deposited in the ArrayExpress repository (accession no. E-MTAB-16523). Some NGS data was visualized using MetaScape (https://metascape.org) to generate GO and transcription factors enrichment heat maps28.

Western blot

Proteins were isolated from WT or mdx CD146 + cells cultured in vitro using RIPA buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). An equal amount of total protein lysate was denatured by boiling in Laemmli buffer, separated by SDS-Page electrophoresis, and transferred to PVDF membranes (Roche). The membranes were blocked with 5% milk/Tris buffered saline (TBS) for 1 h and incubated with primary antibodies diluted 1:1000 in 5% milk/TBS, at 4 °C, overnight, followed by secondary antibodies diluted 1:10,000, at room temperature, for 1 h. The protein bands were then visualized with the SuperSignal West Dura Extended Duration Substrate (Thermo Scientific) and exposed to a chemiluminescence positive film (CL-XPosureTM Film; Thermo Scientific). The film was developed using a developer and fixer (Fuji). The density of the examined bands was compared with the density of α-tubulin bands. The following primary antibodies were used: rabbit monoclonal anti-NF-κB (#8242, Cell Signaling), anti-phosphorylated NF-κB (#3033, Cell Signaling), rabbit monoclonal anti-c-Fos (#2250, Cell Signaling), rabbit monoclonal anti-phosphorylated c-Fos (#5348, Cell Signaling), rabbit monoclonal anti-c-Jun (#9165, Cell Signaling), rabbit monoclonal anti-phosphorylated c-Jun (#5348, Cell Signaling), anti-PPARγ (#2435, Cell Signaling) and mouse monoclonal anti-α-tubulin (T5168; Sigma-Aldrich). Secondary antibodies used: rabbit anti-mouse IgG conjugated with peroxidase (A9044; Sigma-Aldrich) and goat anti-rabbit IgG conjugated with peroxidase (#7074; Cell Signaling). Three independent experiments were performed. Blots were visualized using Gel Doc XR + and analyzed using Image Lab 5.1 (BioRad). The bands intensity was calculated as relative to the corresponding α-tubulin bands (see Supplementary Fig. 3).

Growth factor analysis

Before analysis, thawed cell culture supernatants were centrifuged at 16,000 × g for 4 min immediately before use and diluted twice with test calibrator diluent. Concentrations of angiopoietin-1 (Angpt-1), Angpt-2 and vascular endothelial growth factor (Vegf) were measured using the custom Luminex Discovery assay kit number LXSAHM (R&D Systems) on the Bio-Plex MAGPIX Multiplex Analyzer (Luminex Corp., Austin, TX). A Bio-Plex Manager 6.1 (Bio-Rad Lab Inc., Hercules, CA) was used for the generation of a 5-log standard curve, the fitting of the curve, and the calculation of concentrations.

Statistical analysis

The Gaussian distribution of values was analyzed with the Shapiro–Wilk normality test. Data were analyzed using the t-test, one-way ANOVA or two-way ANOVA test and post hoc with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test. Differences were considered statistically significant when p < 0.05 (marked with asterisks, *p < 0.05; **p < 0.005; ***p < 0.001; ****p < 0.0001). All raw data and statistical analysis have been deposited in open, easily accessible Dane Badawcze University of Warsaw repository (10.58132/NWQZBI). Each value, together with mean value and standard deviation, is shown in each graph presented. All analyses were performed in at least 3 independent biological replicates. All statistical analyzes and graphs were performed with GraphPad Prism version 10.6.0 for MacOS, GraphPad Software, Boston, Massachusetts USA, www.graphpad.com.

Results

The skeletal muscles of mdx mice have fewer CD146 + and Pdgfrβ + pericytes than WT animals

First, we analyzed the localization of CD146 + cells within mouse skeletal muscle (Fig. 1A). We found that in WT mice, CD146 + cells are mostly located close to blood vessels, which is expected due to the typical perivascular localization of pericytes. Some cells were also present in the interstitial space. However, as indicated by co-staining with laminin, CD146 + cells in mdx mice are additionally found within fibrotic areas of dystrophic muscle (Fig. 1A). Next, we analyzed the percentages of CD146+, Pdgfrβ+, and Pdgfrα + cells in cell suspensions isolated from the hind limb skeletal muscles of WT and mdx mice using flow cytometry (Fig. 1B and C). CD146 is a cell adhesion molecule involved in angiogenesis and vascular stability. Pdgfrβ is also important for vascular development and maintenance8. In contrast, Pdgfrα is expressed by fibroblast-like cells, including FAPs, and regulates cell proliferation, migration, and differentiation. Pdgfrα is involved in wound healing and tissue remodeling processes that contribute to both muscle regeneration and pathological fibrosis and fat infiltration in dystrophic conditions29,30. In WT muscles, approximately 9% (± 0.7%) of the cells were CD146+, while in mdx muscles, this proportion was reduced to approximately 6.3% (± 1.7%) (Fig. 1C). CD146 was utilized as a marker for interstitial myogenic cells within skeletal muscles. Similar trends were observed when comparing the percentage of Pdgfrβ + cells: 40.2% (± 7.9%) of cells isolated from WT mice were Pdgfrβ+, compared to 31% (± 11.0%) in mdx mice (Fig. 1C). Pdgfrβ is commonly recognized as a marker for pericytes, along with Alp and Ng2. These data indicate a significant reduction in the number of cells that express CD146 and Pdgfrβ in skeletal muscles compared to WT muscles. Similar reduction was observed in the percentage of Pdgfrα + cells, with WT muscles containing approximately 35.4% (± 5.5%) and mdx muscles containing approximately 25.6% (± 5%) Pdgfrα + cells (Fig. 1C). Since Pdgfrα is a principal marker of fibro/adipogenic progenitor (FAP) cells, these findings suggest that the numbers of FAP are lower in mdx skeletal muscles. Finally, we evaluated the percentage of double positive CD146+/Pdgfrβ + cells (i.e. pericytes type-1) and CD146+/Pdgfrα+ (i.e. pericytes type-2) in WT and mdx mice skeletal muscles. We found approx. 9.4% (± 4.3%) of CD146+/Pdgfrβ + cells in WT muscles and 5,6% (± 0,9%) in mdx muscles and approx. 8.6% (± 2.9%) of CD146+/Pdgfrα + cells in WT muscles and 4.9% (± 2%) in mdx muscles (Supplementary Fig. 1). However, the differences were not statistically significant.

CD146 + isolated from WT or mdx mice are characterized by different gene expression profiles

Next, we isolated and cultured both WT and mdx CD146 + cells, subsequently analyzing the expression of genes associated with pericytes (Mcam, Alp, Pdgfrb, Cspg4), progenitor cells (Nes), fibro/adipogenic progenitors (FAP) (Pdgfra), and satellite cells/myoblasts (Peg3, Pax7, Myod) (Fig. 2). First, we analyzed the expression of genes encoding pericyte markers (Mcam, Alp, Pdgfrb) and the FAP marker (Pdgfra) in freshly isolated CD146 + cells or after 5 days of in vitro expansion (Fig. 2A). Mcam expression, which encodes CD146, was significantly higher in freshly sorted WT cells compared with mdx cells. However, Mcam expression markedly decreased during in vitro culture, and after 5 days no significant difference was observed between WT and mdx cells (Fig. 2A). Similarly, the expression of Alp and Pdgfrb showed no significant differences between freshly isolated or cultured cells in either genotype (Fig. 2A). In contrast, Pdgfra expression was comparable between freshly isolated WT and mdx cells but became significantly higher in mdx cells after 5 days of culture (Fig. 2A). We next assessed Cspg4 expression, which encodes Ng2, and found it to be lower in mdx cells compared with WT cells after 5 days of culture (Fig. 2B). Furthermore, mdx CD146 + cells exhibited reduced expression of Nes, which encodes nestin, relative to WT CD146⁺ cells.

The CD146 + subset isolated from WT or mdx mice continues to express pericyte-specific surface proteins

Subsequently, we aimed to confirm whether skeletal muscle-derived CD146 + cells retain the expression of surface markers after in vitro culture (Fig. 3A). We observed that a subset of cells in WT and mdx CD146 + cultures continued to express CD146, Alp or Pdgfrβ (Fig. 3B). 35,23% (± 19,56%) of cells obtained from WT mice retained CD146 expression in culture. CD146 was detected on the surface of 22,43% (± 9,30%) mdx mice derived cells. On the contrary, slightly higher proportion of mdx-derived cells retained the expression of Alp (61,18% ± 7,22%) and Pdgfrβ (52,79% ± 20,33%) after 5 days in culture, compared to WT-derived cells, respectively 52,73% ± 24,85%, and 48,50% ± 14,41%). However, none of these differences have been described as statistically significant.

We also calculated the fusion index to evaluate the ability of CD146 + cells to form multinucleated myotubes. WT CD146 + cells exhibited significantly greater potential to differentiate into myotubes than those derived from mdx mice. The fusion index, i.e., the percentage of cell nuclei within multinucleated myotubes, was 44.7% (± 4.2%) for WT CD146 + cells and 23,5% (± 2.3%) for mdx CD146 + cells (Fig. 5A). On the contrary, mdx CD146 + cells demonstrated a markedly higher capacity to differentiate into fibroblasts and adipocytes compared to WT CD146 + cells. Cell differentiation capabilities were evidenced by increased collagen deposition and lipid droplet formation, respectively, in the in vitro assays. The collagen deposition area was approximately 27.5% (± 6%) for WT cells and 46.4% (± 5.1%) for mdx cells (Fig. 5B). Similarly, more perilipin positive cells were detected in mdx CD146+ (40.3% ± 3.4%) than in WT cell cultures (10.3% ± 7%) (Fig. 5C). Collectively, these data indicate that CD146 + cells isolated from mdx mice proliferated more efficiently, have a lower ability to differentiate in myogenic lineage, but more efficiently follow the adipogenic and fibrogenic path.

Mdx-derived CD146 + cell secretome impair angiogenesis in vitro

Proper vasculature restoration is an essential step during skeletal muscle regeneration, securing the delivery of oxygen and nutrients to the injury site31. Neovasculogenesis, that is, the formation of new vessels, can be mimicked in vitro using the tube formation assay. In vitro capillary formation is preceded by endothelial cell migration and alignment, and each of these steps can be influenced by the environment. We hypothesized that the secretome of CD146 + cells isolated from WT and mdx mice differs, thus, affecting human ubilical cord vein endothelial cells (HUVECs) tube formation efficiency (Fig. 6A). Selected parameters describing tube formation were analyzed using Angiogenesis Analyzer, i.e. number of nodes (number of pixels having at least three neighbours), number of junctions (number of nodes or group of nodes forming bifurcations), number of segments (lines enclosed by two junctions), number of meshes (areas enclosed by segments), total segment length (summary length of all segments), total length (summary length of segments, isolated elements and branches)22. Almost no significant differences were observed between HUVECs cultured in ECMed control and ECMed-WT, except for a slightly lower total number of meshes in HUVECs cultured in ECMed-WT. However, these differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 6B). In contrast, significant inhibition of tube formation was observed, reflected by all analysed parameters, when HUVECs were cultured in ECMed-mdx, compared to cells maintained in control ECMed (Fig. 6B). Overall, significantly lower number of nodes, junctions, meshes, and segments observed in HUVECs cultured in ECMed-mdx, compared to HUVECs cultured in ECMed, indicated inhibited capillary sprouting.

Differences in gene expression between CD146 + cells derived from Mdx and WT

We compared the general differences in the gene expression profile between WT-derived and mdx-derived CD146 + using gene ontology (GO) annotations. According to our functional analyses, we found that genes up-regulated in mdx CD146 + cells compared to the organization of the WT cells were related to extracellular matrix but also immune response, the production or vascular development (Fig. 7A). Among the top 50 genes highly overexpressed in mdx-derived CD146 + there were those encoding extracellular matrix proteins for example Col3a1, Vcan, Fn1, Col6a2, Tnc, Col5a1, Col6a1, Col1a1, Col1a2, metalloproteinase and metalloproteinase inhibitors: Mmp2, Timp1, but also transcripts encoding HIF1A and cyclin D: Hif1a, CcnD (Fig. 7C). Genes down-regulated in mdx CD146 + cells compared to WT cells were mostly related to muscle-related terms such as muscle structure development (GO:0061061), cytoskeleton in muscle cells (mmu04820), muscle system process (GO:0003012), regulation of myotube formation (GO:0010830) (Fig. 7B). More specifically among the top 50 mostly down-regulated genes were genes encoding typical muscle proteins: Myod1, Myog, Des, Acta1, Ttn and also genes encoding light and heavy myosin chains such as Myh1, Myh3 or Myl1 (Fig. 7D). Furthermore, we found that among the genes down-regulated in mdx CD146 + cells were these encoding pro-angiogenic factors, for example Egfl7 (fold change: −2,07), Vwf (fold change: −2,13), or Angpt2 (fold change: −1,64). Finally, we found that WT and mdx CD146 + expressed different levels of genes that encode proteins involved in cell migration, such as Cxcl12 encoding Sdf-1 and Cxcr4 coding Sdf-1 receptor. Specifically, we found that Cxcl12 was up-regulated in mdx CD146+ (fold change: 1,76) while Cxcr4 was down-regulated (fold change: −3,5). Finally, using MetaScape analysis we found that observed changes in the gene expression level might be related to changes in the different transcription factors activity. Genes up-regulated in mdx CD146 + cells were annotated to be regulated among others by Sp1, Jun, or NFκB (Fig. 7E). At the same time, down-regulated genes were predicted to be regulated by among others Myod, Myog or Myf5 (Fig. 7F).

Discussion

DMD is a lethal X chromosome-linked neuromuscular disorder characterized by progressive muscle weakness and degeneration. Lack of functional dystrophin results in loss of muscle integrity, impaired contraction activity, and tissue degeneration. Progressive fibrosis, chronic inflammation, vascular aberrations, and adipose tissue accumulation characterizes dystrophic skeletal muscles2. However, knowledge how the lack of dystrophin impacts a different population of skeletal muscle resident cells is still limited. This issue could be crucial for potential DMD therapy. Rudnicki and co-workers described direct consequences of dystrophin loss on the function of satellite cells3,32. The dystrophin protein is expressed at high levels in activated satellite cells4. Importantly, dystrophin participates in the distribution of proteins responsible for asymmetric cell division i.e., PAR complex4. Thus, DMD satellite cells are characterized by the reduced ability to asymmetric cell division and generation of progenitor cells that follow myogenic differentiation. DMD patients and mdx mice show a significantly elevated numbers of Pax7 + SCs, but reduced number of myoblasts33. Furthermore, dystrophic myoblasts differentiate in vitro more rapidly than control cells34,35. In addition, mitochondria of the mdx mouse muscle fibers are dysfunctional, characterized by aberrant morphology and large mitochondrial vacuoles36. Mdx myoblasts exhibit reduced oxygen consumption and mitochondrial membrane potential, increased formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and oxidative stress37. mdx SCs are characterized by impaired epigenetic control and cell signaling. Moreover, mdx SC and DMD SCs could undergo a mesenchymal-like/fibrogenic transition and synthesize greater amounts of collagen playing profibrotic role38. Enhanced Tgfβ signaling in mdx muscle could also lead to adipogenic conversions of myoblasts39.

The lack of dystrophin could also affect the function of FAPs. FAPs are the primary source of ECM in both wild-type and mdx mice. Under ‘dystrophic conditions’, the number of FAPs increases and these cells undergo aberrant differentiation and activation, leading to increased fibrosis and fat accumulation. Control FAPs, on the other hand, maintain a balance between contributing to regeneration and undergoing apoptosis which leads to lack of fibrosis; however, dystrophic FAPs lose this regulatory balance, resulting in excessive fibrosis and fat deposition. Specific signaling pathways (such as those dependent on Notch, Wnt, Tgf-β, and Il-4) involved in the regulation of FAP are also disrupted under dystrophic conditions40. In mdx mice the number of FAPs in the diaphragm is increased compared to wild-type mice, although, in case of quadriceps such differences are less visible41. FAPs isolated from mdx muscles exhibit increased expression of genes that regulate ECM production and degradation, what leads to their enhanced profibrogenic activity. Finally, such mdx diaphragm FAPs show stronger signs of cellular stress, indicating a different response to the dystrophic muscle environment compared to wild-type FAPs. The juvenile D2-mdx mouse model exhibits severe DMD, characterized by significant muscle degeneration and impaired regeneration42. Such phenotype is linked to an increased inflammatory response to muscle injury, resulting in excessive fibrosis. Interestingly, the severity of muscle damage and degeneration in D2-mdx mice diminishes significantly in adulthood, coinciding with a restoration of inflammatory and FAP responses to normal levels42. This improved response allows for enhanced regenerative myogenesis, reaching levels similar to those of the milder B10-mdx DMD model. In vitro studies revealed that FAPs isolated from juvenile D2-mdx muscles negatively affect the efficacy of fusion of myoblasts derived from control SCs. These findings highlight the critical role of aberrant stromal cell responses (specifically, the exaggerated inflammatory response and FAP accumulation) in the severe muscle pathology observed in juvenile D2-mdx mice. Normalization of these responses in adulthood suggests that targeting these components of stromal cells could be a promising therapeutic strategy for DMD. Other studies also showed that stromal cells have different roles in chronic muscle degeneration. The findings suggest that the mechanisms driving fibrosis are more complex than previously understood, with the specific injury model playing a crucial role43.

In this manuscript, we focus on mouse muscle-derived CD146 + cells that are a myogenic pericyte-like population present within mouse and human skeletal muscles15. Pericytes are specialized cells connected with capillaries and post-capillary venules6. They play a role in blood vessel stabilization and permeability, vascular development/maturation, and regulation of blood flow44. They also function as tissue-resident progenitors; however, pericytes isolated from different sites present different differentiation potential15. Muscle-isolated pericytes are capable of differentiation into myogenic, fibrogenic, and adipogenic lineage45–47. The subpopulation of pericytes (nestin+/NG2+/CD146+/PDGFRβ+) could follow the myogenic program in vitro and in vivo, and after transplantation into injured muscles. In the present study we showed an important difference between wild-type or mdx CD146 + cells and their contribution to the pathological phenotype of the skeletal muscles of mdx mice. Little is known about the role of pericytes in dystrophic muscles48. Tedesco et al. showed that the number and myogenic capacity of muscle pericytes decreased in mice and patients with Limb-Girdle Muscular Dystrophy (LGMD2D)49. Pericytes isolated from muscles of patients suffering from myotonic dystrophy type 1 (DM1) maintained myogenic potential similar to cells from not affected controls50,51. However, the number of pericytes was higher in the muscle biopsies of dystrophic patients, including DMD patients, but not of neuropathic patients compared to healthy controls52. Pericytes from DMD patients could follow the myogenic program and participate in muscle fiber regeneration52.

In the present study we showed that the level of pericyte-like and myogenic markers is lower in mdx CD146 + cells than in WT CD146 + cells. We found that mdx CD146 + express much lower levels of Nes and Cspg4, coding markers of myogenic pericytes. At the same time, mdx CD146 + cells were characterized by increased level of the genes encoding FAP marker Pdgfra and decreased Myod. Simultaneously, mdx CD146 + cells expressed higher levels of factors involved in the regulation of muscle fibrosis and extracellular matrix remodeling. CD146⁺ cells isolated from mdx muscles followed the myogenic program less efficiently in vitro but showed enhanced differentiation into fibroblasts and adipocytes compared to CD146⁺ cells from healthy mice. Thus, mdx CD146 + cells seem to be more similar to FAPs than myogenic progenitors in the context of gene expression profile, and differentiation potential. Although, it not necessarily means definitive lineage conversion, but rather a shift away from a myogenic/pericytic phenotype to a FAP-like one. Our data showed elevated levels of phosphorylated (active) NF-κB and c-Jun, along with decreased levels of phosphorylated c-Fos in mdx CD146⁺ cells compared to WT. These findings, together with transcriptomic predictions of regulatory influence by NF-κB, Jun, and Fos family members, and literature suggest the potential involvement of these pathways in modulating the CD146 + cells identity. NF-κB, was shown to be aberrantly activated in DMD, which affects the inflammatory environment in dystrophic muscles53. The canonical NF-κB signaling pathway is activated by Bcr, Tcr, Tlr, Il-1R and TnfR, non-canonical NF-κB signaling is activated by CD40, Rank, Lt-βR, and Baff-R and both pathways exert complex effects in various biological processes, including inflammation, cell proliferation, survival, and gene expression54. NF-κB signaling interacts with other signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt, Mapk, Jak/Stat, Tgf-β, Wnt, Notch, and Hedgehog signaling55. In the context of DMD, NF-κB activation plays a significant role in muscle inflammation and fibrosis53. In DMD muscles NF-κB signaling pathway is activated by molecules released from injured muscle fibers, such as, DAMPs or Tnf-α56. Thus, NF-κB activation leads to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and promotes the expression of genes involved in extracellular matrix deposition, leading to increased fibrosis and impaired muscle regeneration53,57. c-Jun and c-Fos are part of Activator Protein-1 (AP-1) that is a transcription factor complex that plays a role in the control of tissue-specific genes58. In myogenic cells, AP-1 is a negative regulator of differentiation because c-Jun dimerizes with Myod and inhibit its activity58,59. Moreover, Myod is required for down-regulation of c-Fos which allows differentiation progression60. Therefore, activation of NF-κB signaling promotes fibrogenesis and activation of c-Jun and c-Fos inhibits myogenesis of mdx Cd146 + cells. Therefore, we concluded that mdx CD146 + cells exhibit altered in vitro characteristics that correlate with key pathological features of dystrophic muscle, including enhanced ECM deposition capacity, increased adipogenic potential, reduced myogenic differentiation capability, and inhibitory effect on EC tube formation in vitro. These cellular alterations may be associated with elevated NF-κB and c-Jun activity and reduced c-Fos signaling compared to WT CD146 + cells.

Furthermore, our results show that conditioned medium from mdx CD146⁺ cells compromises the in vitro differentiation and tube-forming ability of endothelial cells. The lower secretion of Sdf-1 and higher secretion of Angpt-2 were noticed. These factors play important role in angiogenesis regulation. Sdf-1 functions primarily as a chemoattractant, recruiting endothelial progenitor cells to the site of vascularization61. Angiopoietins focus on stabilizing newly formed blood vessels, regulating endothelial cell survival, maturation, and interaction with pericytes62. Angpt-1 promotes the recruitment and adhesion of pericytes to nascent blood vessels, strengthening their structure and preventing leakage. Angpt-2 has a similar function. However, Angpt-2 has proangiogenic properties only in the presence of Vegf, while in the absence of Vegf it inhibits tubulogenesis, promotes endothelial cell death, and vessel regression63. We did not detect Vegf in conditioned medium collected either from mdx or WT CD146 + cells (data not shown). Thus, down-regulation of Sdf-1 and Angpt-1 in CD146 + cells could affect vessel formation in dystrophic muscle. Importantly, dysfunctional angiogenesis is involved in Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) pathology64. Evidence from both DMD patients and mdx mice models consistently points to morphological changes in blood vessels. These include swollen and pale endothelial cells with impaired function, increased areas of capillary and endothelial cells, nearly occluded capillary lumens, and irregularities in the basement membrane. These vascular defects, observed in both DMD patients and mdx mice, suggest that impaired angiogenesis contributes to the pathogenesis of DMD, although not as the sole causative factor64. Furthermore, pericytes regulated SC activation and myofiber hypertrophy in Angpt-1 and Igf-1 dependent manner65. Ablation of pericytes led to increase in SC activation and decrease in myofiber diameter, suggesting their role in SC quiescence and myoblast differentiation65. Therefore, we suggested that the decrease in pro-angiogenic factors such as Sdf-1 and angiopoietins in mdx CD146 + cells could impact not only vasculature but also muscle fiber reconstruction in mdx mice.

Interestingly, Bianco and coworkers showed that human CD146 + muscle-derived cells injected intramuscularly into the left tibialis anterior of 2-month-old mouse were distributed to the interstitium and in a satellite cell-like position. Furthermore, human CD146 + muscle-derived cells transplanted into SCID/mdx mice form myofibers that express human dystrophin15. Thus, CD146 + muscle-derived cells could follow the myogenic program after transplantation and are a promising source of myogenic cells for DMD therapy66. Importantly, myogenic pericyte transplantation has also been tested in preclinical models of muscular dystrophies and showed the contribution of pericytes to muscle regeneration48. As shown by Cossu and coworkers the most promising source of such cells is a subpopulation of pericytes called mesoangioblasts14. Mesoangioblasts can differentiate and express essential proteins, such as, dystrophin and improve muscle function after transplantation. However, translating these findings into clinical efficacy has proven complex, as illustrated by the initial human trial, which, while safe, resulted in limited beneficial effects possibly due to the age of the patient age and existing treatments67. Therefore, muscle pericytes are an important population in DMD pathology, both because of their participation in DMD pathology and as a potential source of myogenic cells.

In conclusion, we showed that muscle-derived CD146 + pericytes reveal their altered differentiation capabilities under dystrophic conditions, characterized by lower myogenic potential and increased fibrogenic and adipogenic tendencies. Our and literature data suggest that dysregulation of signaling pathways such as NF-κB, c-Fos, and c-Jun within these cells might be correlated with the pathological conditions observed in dystrophic muscles, which affects muscle regeneration and perpetuates fibrosis. Additionally, muscle-derived pericytes negatively affect the ability of endothelial cells to form tubes in vitro, which corresponds to downregulation of pro-angiogenic factors, including angiopoietins and Sdf-1, suggesting their role in regulating angiogenesis. The findings underscore the need for an integrated understanding of muscle-derived cell populations and their roles in DMD pathology. Targeting dysregulated signaling pathways and cellular behaviours may offer novel therapeutic strategies aimed at restoring muscle integrity and function in dystrophic patients.
