Evaluation of Girls Invest, an economic empowerment intervention to address social and economic risks associated with intimate partner violence among adolescent girls in Ibadan, Nigeria
Authors: Olufunmilayo I. Fawole, Omowumi O. Okedare, Mobolaji M. Salawu, Susan M Kiene, Rebecka Lundgren, Elizabeth Frost, Tesmer Atsbeha, Chi-Dean Lin, Elizabeth Reed
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe high prevalence and associated negative consequences of intimate partner violence (IPV) (23–49%) against girls has been well-documented in Nigeria. This study evaluated Girls Invest, an app-based economic empowerment intervention, to address social and economic factors associated with IPV among girls ages 15–19 in low-income communities in Ibadan, Nigeria. Satisfaction, acceptability, and feasibility of the intervention were also assessed.MethodsWe conducted a 2-armed randomized-controlled trial (16 schools, 8 schools per arm) among girls ages 15–19 (n = 258) to compare the Girls Invest intervention to a wait-list control condition on social and economic risk factors associated with IPV. Quantitative analyses compared survey data at baseline and 6 months follow-up using a difference-in-differences approach. Five focus groups were also conducted among a subset of participants (n = 62) after completing the intervention; focus group data were analyzed and independently coded by two researchers for themes related to participants’ satisfaction, acceptability, and impact on economic and social factors associated with IPV.ResultsParticipants averaged 16 years old, were predominantly from the Yoruba tribe, and most reported Islam as their religion. Participants reported high levels of acceptability and satisfaction with the app-based intervention. Survey findings indicate statistically significant improvements in gender equitable attitudes (p = 0.02) and non-significant trends toward reduced endorsement of traditional gender roles (p = 0.07) and decreased perception of economic vulnerability (p = 0.20) among Girls Invest participants relative to the waitlist control group. These survey findings were further substantiated by focus group findings with the following themes reported: 1) satisfaction with the app-based platform and financial incentives, 2) greater awareness of issues regarding gender equity and women’s rights, 3) increased awareness of healthy versus unhealthy relationship behaviors, 4) increased knowledge and confidence regarding financial decision-making, and 5) improved expectations for the future regarding vocational opportunities. Notably, participants reported discussing topics they learned with friends and siblings.ConclusionsWhile a full-scale trial is needed, our findings suggest that Girls Invest holds promise in mitigating social and economic factors associated with IPV. Given the increasing use of smartphones in Nigeria and globally, mobile technology may be a useful platform to support scalability and is lower in cost compared to traditional in-person approaches.Clinical trial registrationClinical trials unique ID: NCT06942481 (Retrospective registration: 4/9/2025).Data available upon request; study ongoing.

Introduction

Gender-based violence, in the form of intimate partner violence (IPV), is associated with multiple poor mental, physical, and behavioral health outcomes, including depression, anxiety, substance use, HIV/STI infection, and adolescent pregnancy [1–4]. Two decades of research across multiple regions have documented the high global prevalence of IPV (23–49%) among girls and young women [1–3]. In sub-Saharan Africa, approximately one in three women experience physical or sexual violence by an intimate partner, contributing substantially to maternal morbidity, HIV risk, and lost educational and economic opportunities among women and girls [4].

Building on this global evidence as well as high rates reported in sub-Saharan Africa, country-level data from Nigeria reveal similarly alarming patterns of IPV among women and girls. In Nigeria, 30–40% of women report experiencing IPV in their lifetime [1, 2]. Among adolescents and young women in Nigeria, IPV prevalence remains high, particularly in low-income settings. A study conducted among girls aged 15–24 years in low-income communities in Ibadan found that 21% reported experiencing IPV in the past 12 months [3]. The notably high rates of IPV within this urban, low-income population of adolescent girls and young adult women in Nigeria underscores the need for IPV prevention efforts in this population.

Poverty and economic vulnerability are risk factors for IPV, increasing girls’ risk in two main ways. First, economic vulnerability creates scenarios where girls may be pressured to seek out relationships with male partners as a means to obtain financial security [5, 6]. Relationships in which girls are financially reliant on a male partner decreases girls’ control over decisions within the relationship (e.g., sexual decision-making) and heightens their risk for IPV [7–9]. Economic vulnerability and financial reliance on males also decreases girls’ capacity to leave abusive relationships [4, 7, 10]. Second, economic vulnerability can intersect with gender-based constraints (e.g. girls’ families may not prioritize spending limited household resources on girls’ education) and perpetuate low expectations for girls to excel in educational or career opportunities. Faced with limited opportunities and low expectations from family or community members, girls may shift their priorities away from pursuing training and education and increase their intentions to find a male partner and start a family – thereby, increasing their risk for relying financially on male partners and risk for IPV [4, 7, 10, 11].

IPV also exacerbates risk for poverty among women and girls by impeding education and employment opportunities; for example, abusive partners often restrict girls’ ability to work outside the home and the effects of IPV can negatively impact girls’ performance in school or work [12]. Also, IPV has been consistently found to be associated with childbearing at younger ages, high parity, and unintended pregnancy across multiple global regions, including sub-Saharan Africa [13–15]. Women and girls who report IPV from male partners are significantly more likely to experience reduced control over reproductive decisions to use contraceptives, including condoms, as well as decisions related to pregnancy timing [2, 16–19]. Research suggests that one-third of adolescent pregnancies could be prevented by eliminating girls’ experiences of violence [20]. Overall, preventing IPV is critical to support economic empowerment as well as the health and well-being of girls and women.

Research on the efficacy of economic interventions (i.e., interventions promoting economic self-sufficiency) to reduce IPV has documented that the most promising approaches are those that include a focus on issues related to gender and social empowerment of women and girls [21–24]. Cash transfer programs are the most common economic intervention among adolescent girls; however, few have included a gender and social empowerment component [21]. In addition, recent research has found that interventions improving financial literacy support young women’s control over financial decision-making, which is primary to women’s empowerment and financial independence from male partners and others [25]. Interventions that address economic vulnerability among young populations in sub-Saharan Africa have improved perceptions of future vocational and educational opportunities available for girls and women, and increased positive expectations for the future, partly by reducing hopelessness about future plans [11, 26–30]. Overall, the most promising economic interventions to reduce IPV among adolescent girls are those that provide concrete economic resources alongside financial literacy training and gender equity empowerment. However, little is known regarding the effectiveness of economic interventions to reduce IPV among adolescent girls in Nigeria.

This study aims to conduct a preliminary efficacy trial to evaluate Girls Invest, an economic empowerment intervention, to reduce economic and social risks associated with IPV among adolescent girls in Ibadan, Nigeria. The study employed a waitlist control design in which participants in the control group received referrals to local support organizations (standard of care) and were offered the Girls Invest intervention after the follow-up period, ensuring ethical access to program benefits. Satisfaction and acceptability as well as intervention impact on social and economic risk factors for IPV were assessed. Girls Invest involves gender equity and financial skills training modules delivered via a mobile “app” and upon completion of the app-based trainings, provides girls with financial resources/incentives to support their education and/or vocational training. Girls Invest focuses on improving: 1) economic factors associated with IPV, including perceptions regarding girls’ economic vulnerability [7, 31], financial literacy, and ability to achieve self-sufficiency, and 2) social factors associated with IPV, including gender inequitable norms that promote male dominance in relationships, acceptance of IPV, which can limit girls’ prioritization of or access to education, employment, or financial endeavors [17, 31]. Notably, these social and economic factors delay identification of IPV, promote financial reliance on male partners, contribute to male partner control over decision-making in relationships, and restrict girls’ ability to leave abusive partnerships [18, 19, 32].

Methods

This study involved a two-arm cluster randomized controlled trial among 258 adolescent females ages 15–19 years in 16 secondary schools within Ibadan South-East and Ibadan North-East Local Government Areas (LGAs) of Ibadan to evaluate preliminary efficacy of Girls Invest on IPV related outcomes. These LGAs were selected as they have the highest number of low-income communities among the five LGAs within the Ibadan metropolis. Low-income communities were of particular focus considering the intersection of economic vulnerability and IPV risk [1, 2]. Sixteen schools within low-income communities were randomly selected from the 33 schools approved by the Ministry of Education; schools were randomized to treatment assignment (Girls Invest intervention, n = 8 or wait-list control group, n = 8) using a randomizer software package after baseline data collection (See Fig. 1). Each school represented a cluster from which participants were selected using simple random sampling where the class register served as the sampling frame. The wait-list control groups were provided with referrals to local support organizations, as the standard of care, and received Girls Invest subsequent to the six-month follow-up data collection. This preliminary efficacy study did not include an attention-matched control condition given that the trial’s purpose was to evaluate feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary efficacy to inform a larger randomized controlled trial. Procedures were standardized across arms: participants in both groups completed identical assessments and received the same number and timing of reminders and compensation for surveys. Research staff contact was limited to scripted administrative messages (e.g., enrollment confirmation and survey reminders) and did not include intervention-related content. Blinding of research staff to treatment groups was not possible with this study design. The study protocol adheres to CONSORT guidelines and was approved by the Human Research Protection Program at San Diego State University (HS-2021–0031) in the US and the University of Ibadan/University College Hospital Joint Institutional Review Board, in Nigeria (UI/EC/21/0183). Intervention description

The Girls Invest intervention involves: 1) app-based trainings on topics related to financial literacy, gender equity, and sexual/reproductive health and 2) financial resources/incentives supporting girls’ education/career.

The Girls Invest intervention promotes financial literacy and aims to raise girls’ critical consciousness related to gender-based economic and social constraints that promote IPV (e.g., economic constraints that promote financial dependence on male partners, social norms that support IPV). Girls Invest app modules encourage girls to reflect on traditional gender norms that disfavor girls’ future trajectories. Trainings support gender equitable beliefs and attitudes that promote girls’ educational/economic endeavors, health and well-being, and power or control within their families and relationships. In order to complete each module and advance to the next level, girls need to perform satisfactorily on topic quizzes. App content is interactive and includes real life scenarios to maximize girls’ engagement. Additionally, to further support engagement and maintain girls’ interest and attention, each training is designed to take up to 60 min for girls to complete.

Participants were provided access to a smartphone to complete the training modules. The smartphones were kept with the research team and girls could use them after school. To reinforce learning, a booklet containing the content of the app training was given to each girl after completion of the mobile app learning. App training module topics included: Gender roles and goal setting: this module discusses how gender roles affect girls’ education, career, body image, power in family and relationship decision-making, property rights, and rights over their bodies, including harmful traditional practices. Financial resources provided to girls included paying for examination fees schools required for girls to continue/further their education or purchasing an item to support apprenticeship such as a sewing machine, baking oven and pans, or hair dryers (equivalent to N80,000, approximately 100USD). These financial resources were identified by girls participating in the intervention and were chosen over cash transfers to better ensure that participants had ownership of the resource/incentive (i.e., rather than cash transfers that could be used by the family and not fully directed at the needs of girls).

Recruitment

The school authorities were visited by trained research staff to inform them about the study process. Each school provided a focal person (class teacher), who worked with the research staff to coordinate recruitment. Girls in senior secondary classes one and two (years 10 and 11) in each of the 16 schools and who were between the ages of 15–19 years were offered to participate in the study. Girls in year 12 were not selected because they had less than six months to complete their education and would not have time to complete the intervention, while girls in the junior secondary schools (years 7—9) are younger than 15 years. All eligible girls (aged 15—19 years and in school years 10 −11) in each school were randomly selected to participate (n = 200 girls per school) and given parental consent forms. Trained research staff, with the assistance of the focal person visited each school (years 10–11) to explain the study, including the random treatment assignment, and information about the Girls Invest intervention (e.g., topics covered) among selected girls. Parents were also invited to the schools and informed about the study process; the consent form (among those under 18) and informational documents were taken home by participants. Girls who did not obtain parental signed consent were not eligible and replaced (n = 151). We obtained parental consent from all parents among girls who were under 18 years of age; consent was obtained from girls 18 years and older and assent from girls younger than 18 years. Surveys were conducted in each school after the recruitment process was completed. Subsequently, schools were randomized to treatment assignment (Girls Invest or a waitlist control condition). While schools were recruited within similar LGAs, none of the selected schools were close to each other geographically to warrant concern for contamination across arms. Data collection procedures

Data collected for this study were drawn from process evaluation program monitoring data, participant surveys, and focus group discussions. All participant focus group and survey data were collected between September 2023 and July 2024.

Process evaluation monitoring data were used to report completion rates of the app-based trainings among participants in the intervention group. During the implementation phase of the study, data were collected via the app to describe whether participants completed all modules, and for those with partial completion, how many of the five modules participants completed. Program monitoring data also included app generated data on how long participants spent on each training. As part of program monitoring, data documenting the provision of economic incentives based on app-training completion was also collected.

Surveys were conducted with all participants at baseline (prior to randomization and treatment group assignment) and at a six-month follow-up timepoint after the baseline survey. The survey collected data on girls’ experiences of IPV, gender-based attitudes, and perceptions of economic vulnerability as well as demographic characteristics. Research staff administered the survey in a one-on-one private setting. The survey took about 45 min to complete; participants received N4,000 (approximately $7) for the baseline survey and N6,000 (approximately $10) for the follow-up survey as compensation for their time. The follow up rate was 69% among intervention and control groups, with no significant difference in follow-up by treatment group (p = 0.72). Notably, in the intervention group, attrition was largely related to participants dropping out (n = 48) prior to participating in the intervention (e.g., moving schools or leaving school), and thus, while they completed the baseline survey, they were not in school during intervention implementation (i.e., when their school was provided with the phones to complete the app-based modules) (Fig. 1).

Focus group participants (n = 62; approximately 12–13 participants in each of 5 focus groups) were conducted among study participants after completion of the Girls Invest intervention. We randomly selected 5 of the 8 intervention schools in which to conduct focus groups. Participation was voluntary and demographics matched those of the larger sample. After completing the six-month follow-up survey, participants from the 5 selected schools who voiced interest in participating in the focus groups were contacted to schedule the focus group. Trained and experienced facilitators conducted informed consent among all focus group participants and subsequently conducted the focus group, which lasted about 60–90 min and took place in a private room. Participant satisfaction and acceptance of the intervention were assessed, as well as how app-based trainings impacted participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs regarding Girls Invest topics. Participants were reimbursed N6,000 (approximately $10) upon completion of the focus group discussions, consistent with ethical and local standards for compensating participants of this age group for their time. Focus group discussions were digitally recorded, transcribed, and de-identified.

Measures

Program monitoring data were automatically downloaded from the app, generating a spreadsheet providing information on module completion, including the time spent on each module. Additional process data were recorded by research staff regarding the provision of the financial incentives upon app-based training completion, including the type of financial incentive received.

Survey measures included sample characteristics as well as gender attitudes and economic factors associated with IPV.

Sample characteristics

Survey measures included the following sample characteristics: age, ethnicity (Yoruba, Ibo, Hausa, Other), religion (Christianity, Islam, Other), relationship status (currently in a relationship, having ever had a relationship but not currently, never had a relationship), employment (yes/no), apprenticeship training (yes/no), and living situation (living with parents, other family, or others).

Attitudes supportive of traditional gender roles were measured by seven items assessing male and female roles in the following: decision-making (e.g., when to have sex, household purchases), household chores, childcare, reproductive decisions (including pregnancy prevention), and in receiving education/training. Responses involved 5-point Likert scales ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree. Summary scores were calculated, with a Cronbach alpha of 0.60, indicating adequate internal consistency.

Attitudes supportive of IPV were measured using 7 validated items adapted from Demographic Health Surveys (DHS) asking whether any of the following scenarios a husband has good reason to hit his wife: (1) She does not complete housework to his satisfaction, (2) She disobeys him, (3) She refuses to have sexual relations with him, (4) She asks him whether he has other girlfriends, (5) He finds out she has been unfaithful, (6) She goes out without telling him, or (7) She does not prepare a meal to his satisfaction. Responses involved 5-point Likert scales ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree. Summary scores were calculated for the three items, with a Cronbach alpha of 0.89, indicating high internal consistency. It should be noted that while these items are the most commonly used items to measure attitudes towards IPV, including among those aged 15 or older, they have not been validated specifically among young populations of adolescents.

Coping with experiences of gender-based discrimination included three items assessing perceived ability to identify instances of “gender-based discrimination against me” and coping with such experiences as being able to “take care of myself,” and recognizing that such experiences are not “about anything I did as an individual”. Responses involved 5-point Likert scales ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree. Summary scores were calculated for the three items, with a Cronbach alpha of 0.65, indicating adequate internal consistency.

Perceptions of economic vulnerability were assessed using eight items asking about perceived household economic situation, including having enough money to purchase basic necessities (e.g., food, clothes, other needs) and money to pay for education/training of the participant. Items also assessed perceived financial stress of the household. Responses involved 5-point Likert scales ranging from strongly agree to strongly disagree. Summary scores were calculated for the three items, with a Cronbach alpha of 0.60, indicating adequate internal consistency.

Focus group guides were developed 1) to provide feedback on the intervention, including the app-based platform and financial incentives provided and 2) to understand the impact of participating in Girls Invest. Specifically, participants were asked to describe their opinions about the use of an app as a model of intervention delivery, as well as their satisfaction with the app training curriculum and financial incentives. The focus groups also addressed how each training topic had impacted the participants’ knowledge, attitudes, and beliefs regarding economic and gender related factors that promote IPV (e.g., economic vulnerability, gender norms that support IPV).

Power and sample-size assumptions

We powered the study for the intermediate social and economic outcomes: 1) attitudes supportive of traditional gender roles, 2) attitudes supportive of IPV, 3) coping with experiences of gender-based discrimination, and 4) perceptions of economic vulnerability.. The unit of randomization was the school (cluster). We assumed a two-sided α = 0.05 and 80% power, with 16 clusters randomized [1:1] to intervention vs. control, an average cluster size of 15 participants at endline, and intracluster correlation (ICC) [ρ = 0.02] based on the literature. We anticipated 30% attrition from baseline to endline. Sensitivity analyses varying ICC (0.01–0.05) and attrition (30%) yielded minimum detectable effects ranging from 8–13 percentage points, respectively. Given the small number of clusters (8 per arm), results should be interpreted as preliminary evidence of feasibility and potential efficacy, and future studies with larger numbers of clusters will be required to detect smaller effects.

Data analysis

Quantitative survey data were used to describe the sample characteristics, and combined with program monitoring data to assess the proportion of participants completing the app-based trainings, how long participants spent on each training, and whether they received financial incentives based on app training completion, as well as what type of financial incentive they received.

A change score (change-from-baseline) approach was used to assess changes between baseline and follow-up for the treatment effect in: 1) attitudes supportive of traditional gender roles, 2) attitudes supportive of IPV, 3) coping with experiences of gender-based discrimination, and 4) perceptions of economic vulnerability. The primary analysis was conducted using a linear mixed model to account for intra-cluster correlation (ICC). Since we used change scores (post-baseline) for the analysis, the subjects with missing post-baseline scores were excluded from the analysis. No significant difference was found between treatment groups regarding sample characteristics (Table 1). Covariates such as demographic variables were evaluated and included in model fittings. Due to non-statistically significant findings, these covariates were removed from the final models. Thus, these findings suggest that missingness is likely random and unlikely to introduce systematic bias. For analyzing the focus group data, an inductive, content analysis approach was used. Content analysis involves generating and applying codes to sections of text, then reviewing the text by various codes and ‘code families’ to identify recurring themes. The coding team created an initial list of codes based on key domains and used these to code discussions. To enhance coding agreement, data were individually coded by two researchers, and coding procedures and definitions were discussed and compared for agreement. Additions of new codes or changes in code definitions were determined via consensus among the research team. Additional codes related to participants sharing information they learned in the app trainings were created to reflect these emerging themes. No new codes emerged after two-thirds of the focus groups were coded, suggesting that content saturation was achieved. For this particular analysis, we retrieved coded text on themes related to acceptability with the program as well as economic and social impacts. Inter-coder reliability was assessed, with 80% coder consistency or higher between coders deemed sufficient for inter-coder reliability.

Results

Overall, findings support high levels of acceptability and satisfaction with the intervention. Survey findings suggest an increase in coping with gender inequitable norms/discrimination and trends suggesting reduced support of gender inequitable norms and a decreased perception of economic vulnerability among Girls Invest participants relative to the waitlist control group. These survey findings were further substantiated by focus group findings with Girls Invest participants, indicating increased awareness of intervention topics related to gender equity and IPV, as well as how access to money can influence relationship decision-making power and girls’ risk for IPV. Participants also reported greater knowledge on financial topics and confidence in their ability to achieve financial independence. Details of these findings from the process evaluation, survey data, and focus group discussions are included below.

Sample characteristics

Participants were 16 years of age on average (SD 0.94). The majority (95–97%) were from the Yoruba tribe of southwest Nigeria and just over half (53–60%) reported Islam as their religion. The majority (72–76%) reported that they had never had a romantic relationship. Just under half (46–47%) reported training in an apprenticeship and most (74–77%) reported living with their parents for more than a year. There were no statistically significant differences between intervention and control groups in sample characteristics (Table 1).

Process evaluation & program monitoring data findings

Among the 142 participants randomized to the intervention arm who had the opportunity to complete the intervention, 98% completed all five levels of the app. Each of the five levels of the app took 45–60 min to complete. Participants reported high levels of satisfaction with the app-based platform (details from focus group findings are provided below). On completion of the five levels, research assistants collected information on the apprenticeship each girl is or will like to undergo. This informed the type of incentives they received such as sewing machines, hair dryer, hair straightener, as well as baking materials which included a gas cylinders, baking oven and pans. Those that were not in any apprenticeship received payments to fund their senior secondary school leaving examination or admission into a tertiary institution.

Participant survey findings

Compared to the control group, participants who received the Girls Invest intervention had greater improvements in being able to identify and cope with gender-based discrimination (e.g., understanding it is not about me as an individual, taking care of myself; p = 0.02); these improvements were statistically significant. While not statistically significant, there was a trend indicating a greater reduction in attitudes supportive of traditional gender roles in the intervention arm compared to the control arm (p = 0.07) between baseline and six months follow-up time points. Although, there was a trend suggesting that participants in the Girls Invest intervention group were less likely at follow-up to report perceptions of economic vulnerability (p = 0.21) compared to the control group, these differences were not statistically significant at p < 0.05. Discussion

This study is among the first to evaluate an economic empowerment intervention using a mobile app to reduce social and economic risk factors for IPV among adolescent girls in Nigeria. Our findings highlight the high levels of satisfaction and acceptability of the intervention, as well as feasibility of the Girls Invest mobile app-based implementation, given the high completion rates among participants. Furthermore, the results of focus group discussions and participant surveys suggest the promise of Girls Invest to address the social and economic risks associated with IPV. We found that at the 6 months follow-up assessment, participants of Girls Invest reported being able to better cope with experiences of gender-based discrimination, and findings suggested some trends related to reduced support of gender inequitable attitudes and improvements in participants’ perceptions of economic vulnerability. Focus group data further documented participants’ reports of greater confidence in achieving self-sufficiency and improved expectations for their futures. While the survey data did not show any significant differences in attitudes towards IPV between treatment groups, the focus group data highlighted that participants felt better able to identify what constitutes healthy versus unhealthy relationship behaviors. Finally, our findings highlight that participants shared what they learned with their friends and family members. Altogether, these findings suggest that the combined app-based modules and financial incentives of Girls Invest provided a scalable platform for accessible and standardized training on financial literacy, health, and empowerment (i.e., supporting gender equity), while the financial incentives provided a tangible means for girls to exercise agency and economic decision-making. Findings suggest that this dual mechanism of social and economic empowerment may be central to improving girls’ confidence, gender-equitable attitudes, and perceptions of economic security. Overall, these preliminary findings suggest that the Girls Invest economic empowerment intervention may hold promise in addressing social and economic risk factors associated with IPV among adolescent girls in urban school-based settings Nigeria.

Our findings on the reduction of gender-inequitable attitudes among participants in the Girls Invest intervention build upon existing literature that underscores the potential effectiveness of economic empowerment programs incorporating a gender equity and rights-based approach. By integrating content designed to enhance critical consciousness in these areas, such interventions may contribute to meaningful shifts in attitudes. Much of this previous work has been conducted among adult women across the globe [24, 32]. Among the economic empowerment interventions evaluated among adolescent girls, many of these have focused on economic outcomes [32, 34, 35] and several studies have assessed sexual and reproductive health outcomes [34–40]. Notably, while some cash transfers evaluated among adolescent girls have been conditional on educational enrollment, most have not provided additional training on gender equity related topics. However, previous economic interventions promoting girls’ vocational training, which have also provided assets similar to that of Girls Invest, such as start-up kits for girls’ vocational activities, have included information on sexual/reproductive health and marriage, with findings supportive of reduced adolescent pregnancy and improved employment related outcomes [40]. While preliminary, our findings from Girls Invest are aligned with this existing previous research highlighting the utility of economic empowerment interventions to include content that addresses gender-based economic and social challenges unique to women and girls [32].

Similar to findings from several economic empowerment interventions, particularly among adult women across the globe, [24, 34, 37] our focus group data highlight participant reports of increased knowledge related to what constitutes healthy versus unhealthy relationship behaviors, and particularly, decreased attitudes accepting of IPV. However, we did not find significant differences in attitudes supportive of IPV between treatment groups using the survey data. It is possible that the focus groups captured more nuanced reflections on relationship behaviors than could be detected through the standardized survey measures or may have provided a space for participants to discuss and apply concepts in their own words, which may have revealed early shifts in awareness and critical thinking not yet reflected in quantitative attitudes scores. Another potential explanation for these conflicting findings is that the survey data used DHS items representing acceptance of scenarios when a husband has good reason to hit his wife (e.g., not completing housework to his satisfaction, disobeying him) that may have been considered extreme or less relevant to this young population; whereas in focus groups, participants brought up more subtle examples of abusive or unhealthy relationship behaviors that they learned in the trainings (e.g., controlling behaviors, sexual coercion). Notably, in surveys, participants reported low acceptance of the DHS items representing IPV overall (mean of about 2, with 1 being the lowest support and 5 being the highest support of these items on average), and thus, there may not have been enough opportunity to find further significant reductions in this outcome over time. Future research is needed that includes survey measures tailored to this age group and that include a more diverse range of IPV related behaviors.

Our preliminary findings suggesting improvements in perceptions of economic vulnerability, financial literacy, and self-sufficiency are aligned with previous evaluations of economic interventions among adolescent girls. While future research with a full-scale trial and longer-term follow-up timepoints are needed, we found trends in decreased reports of perceived economic vulnerability among intervention versus control group participants. Newer to the literature, our focus group findings highlight participant reports of improved financial literacy, which they stated also increased their confidence to independently manage their own finances and achieve self-sufficiency. This finding is aligned with previous intervention studies focusing on improving financial literacy among youth [11, 21, 25–30, 32, 34, 36, 38–40]. Furthermore, within the focus group discussions, participants reported increased expectations for their futures, particularly regarding what is possible for them to achieve for their education/vocation, which may be due partly to increased perceptions of financial literacy and reported confidence to navigate financial decisions independently. Notably, previous economic interventions focusing on the promotion of vocational training among adolescent girls have reported similar findings [40]. Additionally, increased future expectations may also be a result of enhanced critical perspectives related to gender-based inequities, [41] where participants critiqued norms that discouraged girls from achieving their educational and vocational goals. Future full-scale trials will be needed to better understand the mechanisms underlying these findings.

Our study has several limitations. First, study findings are most generalizable to similar samples of adolescent girls recruited from schools within low-income communities in this urban context of Ibadan, Nigeria. We sought to recruit a representative sample of participants for the surveys and focus groups, however, self-selection bias may have skewed findings given that participation was voluntary. Yet, most of the participants invited into the study agreed to participate; for the focus groups, most participants from the randomly selected five schools were interested and participated in the focus group. In addition, recall bias may exist in evaluation of findings considering surveys and focus groups were conducted six months post intervention. There may also be courtesy bias in obtaining information from participants via focus groups, where participants may be less willing to voice concerns or ideas thought to be in opposition to others or to what facilitators want to hear; however, to reduce this bias, we had research staff not associated with the program conduct the focus groups and be clear that anything discussed would be confidential. A key limitation of this study is that the control and intervention groups were not matched for attention or contact time, and the study was not blinded. Therefore, nonspecific factors such as perceived support or participant expectations could have influenced outcomes. Future study is needed including a fully powered trial, which will include an attention-matched comparison group and blinded outcome assessment to better isolate intervention effects. Finally, while one major goal of the Girls Invest intervention was to prevent IPV, the current study was designed to measure more intermediate factors associated with IPV risk and was not statistically powered to measure IPV. With only 16 clusters (8 per arm), the study’s statistical power is limited, particularly for detecting small effects or outcomes with high intracluster correlation. The findings should therefore be interpreted as indicative rather than conclusive, underscoring the need for a larger, adequately powered trial. Furthermore, the IPV attitude items adapted from the DHS may have been too extreme for this adolescent sample, contributing to a low baseline mean and potential floor effects. Future research should include IPV-related measures tailored to adolescents that assess more subtle, developmentally appropriate forms of relationship control, coercion, and abuse to more sensitively capture intervention-related changes. Additionally, the current study follow-up assessment at 6 months was not a long enough time period to expect significant changes in IPV experiences. Thus, future, larger trials will be needed that include longer follow-up assessments with larger samples to detect this outcome.

These limitations notwithstanding, our study findings build upon prior research conducted in other global regions highlighting the potential of economic empowerment interventions to promote girls’ social and economic empowerment, and thereby, reduce risks associated with IPV. Given the focus of Girls Invest on changing gender attitudes that are often strongly influenced by girls’ social environments, approaches to more purposely engage girls’ social networks may enhance intervention effects. Additionally, given our finding that participants often shared information learned from the Girls Invest app with their friends and family members, future work in this area may be useful to understand any added benefits from integrating app-based approaches with strategies to intentionally promote the diffusion of programmatic information within participants’ social networks. Future implementation of Girls Invest could enhance community-level impact by more intentionally engaging girls’ social networks—an approach that may also strengthen the intervention’s effectiveness in shifting individual attitudes among girls directly participating in Girls Invest. Our research findings also demonstrate the potential for mobile app-based economic empowerment interventions. Smartphone possession and usage among this population is high, with about 60% of Nigerian teenagers having internet-enabled phones [42] – with Nigeria having among the highest rates of smartphone possession in Africa [43]. Thus, with the increasing use of smartphones in Nigeria and globally, mobile technology may be a useful, accessible, and acceptable platform to support scalability, particularly among young populations [33], and is lower in cost compared to traditional in-person approaches. Finally, we found improvements with girls’ reported ability to cope with gender-based discrimination, suggesting that efforts to build girls’ critical consciousness on gender-based issues are important components in supporting economic empowerment, however, it is critical that interventions such as Girls Invest be complemented by broader, systemic interventions that address structural inequalities that create barriers to self-sufficiency among women and girls globally. Overall, our results suggest that this combined approach involving a mobile-based digital training platform and financial incentives provided an interactive, gender-transformative learning experience, with the financial incentives creating opportunities for girls to apply new knowledge through real economic decision-making and action. While future research is needed to confirm these findings, this dual approach—building girls’ critical consciousness and strengthening their economic agency—may offer a promising, scalable pathway to reduce IPV risk and enhance girls’ long-term safety, health, and well-being.
