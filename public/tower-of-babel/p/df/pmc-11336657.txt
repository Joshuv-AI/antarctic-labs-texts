Roseburia intestinalis‐derived extracellular vesicles ameliorate colitis by modulating intestinal barrier, microbiome, and inflammatory responses
Authors: Hwa Seung Han, Soonjae Hwang, Seung Young Choi, Emmanuel Hitayezu, Mabwi A Humphrey, Altai Enkhbayar, Dae‐Geun Song, Myungsuk Kim, Jong‐Sung Park, Young‐Tae Park, Jin‐Soo Park, Kwang Hyun Cha, Ki Young Choi
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammatory bowel disease (IBD) is a chronic disorder characterized by recurrent gastrointestinal inflammation, lacking a precise aetiology and definitive cure. The gut microbiome is vital in preventing and treating IBD due to its various physiological functions. In the interplay between the gut microbiome and human health, extracellular vesicles secreted by gut bacteria (BEVs) are key mediators. Herein, we explore the role of Roseburia intestinalis (R)‐derived EVs (R‐EVs) as potent anti‐inflammatory mediators in treating dextran sulfate sodium‐induced colitis. R was selected as an optimal BEV producer for IBD treatment through ANCOM analysis. R‐EVs with a 76 nm diameter were isolated from R using a tangential flow filtration system. Orally administered R‐EVs effectively accumulated in inflamed colonic tissues and increased the abundance of Bifidobacterium on microbial changes, inhibiting colonic inflammation and prompting intestinal recovery. Due to the presence of Ile‐Pro‐Ile in the vesicular structure, R‐EVs reduced the DPP4 activity in inflamed colonic tissue and increased the active GLP‐1, thereby downregulating the NFκB and STAT3 via the PI3K pathway. Our results shed light on the impact of BEVs on intestinal recovery and gut microbiome alteration in treating IBD.Keywords: bacterial extracellular vesicle, Ile‐Pro‐Ile, inflammatory bowel disease, microbiome, R. intestinalis

INTRODUCTION

Inflammatory bowel diseases (IBDs), including Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, are chronic tissue disorders characterized by disrupted intestinal barrier function, gut microbiome imbalances, and dysregulation of the mucosal immune response to commensal gut bacteria (Citi, 2018; Halfvarson et al., 2017; Kostic et al., 2014; Round & Mazmanian, 2009; Turner, 2009). Standard interventions for IBDs have primarily focused on controlling symptoms by suppressing immune responses, overlooking underlying issues such as damage to the gastrointestinal tract mucus layer, loss of intestinal barrier function, and gut microbiome dysbiosis (Al‐Bawardy et al., 2021; Stallmach et al., 2010). Furthermore, conventional immunosuppressive drugs have intrinsic drawbacks, including systemic side effects, opportunistic infections, risk of malignancies, autoimmune reactions, and liver toxicity (Bernstein et al., 2010; Lautenschläger et al., 2014; Wilson et al., 2010; Zhang et al., 2015).

Technological advances in metagenomics and high‐throughput screening have revealed a correlation between microbiome composition, health, and disease (Manor et al., 2020). Gut bacteria were unveiled to play critical roles in maintaining host health and the pathogenesis of many diseases. Alterations in the diversity and abundance of intestinal microbes were identified as etiological factors ascribed to the development, progression, and treatment of IBD (Khan et al., 2019; M. Lee & Chang, 2021). Accordingly, strategies to modulate the gut microbiome have been explored to enhance therapeutic outcomes in preclinical and clinical IBD studies (Fong et al., 2020; McQuade et al., 2019; Oka & Sartor, 2020; Smits et al., 2013). However, mechanisms underlying the interplay between the gut microbiome and human health have yet to be elucidated fully (Tian et al., 2023; Zeng et al., 2017).

Recent microbiology studies have highlighted the crucial roles of bacterial extracellular vesicles (BEVs) secreted by gut bacteria, including intestinal commensal bacteria, probiotics, and pathogenic bacteria, as essential mediators that significantly impact the intestinal microenvironment and human health (Díaz‐Garrido et al., 2021; X. Liang et al., 2022; L. Liu et al., 2021; Nishiyama et al., 2020; X. Wang et al., 2023). Subsequently, the BEVs have been considered potential therapeutics for treating intractable diseases by controlling the diversity and abundance of intestinal microbes and intestinal homeostasis (Huang et al., 2022; H. Liu et al., 2021; McMillan & Kuehn, 2021; Tian et al., 2023). BEVs are proteoliposomes, typically 20−400 nm in size, produced by Gram‐negative and Gram‐positive bacteria. They are considered a promising therapeutic strategy owing to their unique structural and biological characteristics (E. Y. Lee et al., 2009; Shin et al., 2019; Toyofuku et al., 2019). Originating from the surface of bacteria, BEVs share composition and content similarities with the parent bacterial membrane and cytoplasm. They offer protective packaging for nucleic acids, lipids, proteins, metabolites, and toxins in vesicles, which (i) protects the cargo from degradation by other bacteria, host organisms, or environmental factors, (ii) maintains a favourable microenvironment for enzymatic activity, (iii) facilitates long‐distance movement in the body, and (iv) allows penetration of the mucus layer and interaction with the host cell without causing sepsis (Shen et al., 2022). In addition to this, BEVs have emerged as integral mediators in bacterial‐bacterial and bacteria‐host communication, playing a role in quorum‐sensing, biofilm formation, antibiotic resistance, antimicrobial peptide expression and deployment, and nutrient acquisition, ultimately contributing to microbiota‐mediated intestinal homeostasis (Ñahui Palomino et al., 2021; Shen et al., 2022; Sultan et al., 2021).

Given these insights, we hypothesized that BEVs could be a critical mediator for the interplay between the gut microbiome and human health, as well as for IBD treatment by reducing dysbiosis and inflammation and recovering the intestinal barrier (Figure 1a). Based on Human Microbiome Project (HMP) analysis, Roseburia intestinalis (R), known to be deficient in IBD patients compared to normal people, was selected as an optimal BEV producer for IBD treatment. We prepared R‐derived extracellular vesicles (R‐EVs) and evaluated their therapeutic efficacy on dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)‐induced colitis. In particular, we identified one critical tripeptide in R‐EVs that modulates the microbial community, inhibits colonic inflammation, and promotes intestinal recovery in inflamed colonic tissues. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report to describe the anti‐inflammatory properties of R through R‐EV‐based IBD treatment. This therapeutic approach based on R‐EVs has great potential as a safe and effective anti‐inflammatory therapy, facilitating the clinical application of BEV‐based treatments for intractable IBD.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

Cyanine 7 N‐hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) ester (7) was purchased from LumiProbe (MD, USA). Dulbecco's modified Eagle's medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), Dulbecco's phosphate‐buffered saline (DPBS), antibiotic‐antimycotic (AA) solution (100×), and trypsin‐EDTA were obtained from GIBCO Life Technologies (MD, USA). TFF with a 300‐kDa MWCO ultrafiltration membrane filter capsule was purchased from Repligen Korea Co., Ltd. (Seongnam, South Korea). R (KCTC 15746) was purchased from the Korean Collection for Type Cultures (KCTC). All bacteria used in the experiment were obtained from the KCTC and American Type Culture Collection (ATCC). Anti‐Phospho‐PI3 Kinase p85/p55 (#4228), Anti‐STAT3(#9139), Anti‐Phospho‐STAT3(#9145), Anti‐NFκB p65(#8242), Anti‐Phospho‐NFκB p65(#3033), Anti‐β‐actin(#4970), anti‐rabbit IgG antibodies (#7074), and anti‐mouse IgG antibodies (#7076) were purchased from Cell Signalling Technology (MA, USA). Anti‐GLP‐1(ab200474) antibody was purchased from Abcam (Cambridgeshire, UK). IPI was purchased from Cayman Chemical (MI, USA).

16S analysis in stool samples from the HMP2

We analyzed the bacterial communities in stool samples from 130 people who participated in the HMP2 (Lloyd‐Price et al., 2019). Specifically, 65 patients with CD, 38 with UC, and 27 healthy participants were included in the analysis (Table S1). Based on endoscopic and histopathological findings, healthy participants who were not diagnosed with IBD were classified as ‘nonIBD’, and those diagnosed with CD or UC were classified as ‘IBD’. Of the 130 people included in the analysis, 64 were women, and 66 were men; the number of stool samples and participants for each classification is shown in Table S1. Differential abundance of genera and species analyses were performed using ANCOM 2.1 (Kaul et al., 2017) after adjusting for covariates, including age, race, recruitment site, and antibiotic use. Significance was defined as ANCOM W > 0.7.

Isolation of R‐EV

R was cultured in reinforced clostridial broth (MBcell, Seoul, South Korea) for 18 h at 37°C in an anaerobic workstation (Coy Laboratory Products) until the late mid‐log phase was reached. The culture volume was gradually increased in the order of 1, 10, 1000, and 3000 mL through 1–2% inoculation. Culture supernatants (3 L for one preparation) were harvested at an optical density (OD600nm) of 0.7 (colony‐forming units, ≥2.8 × 1010 CFU/mL). The bacterial culture solution was pelleted at 2,000 × g for 20 min, and the resulting supernatant was passed through a 0.22 µm bottle top filter (Corning, NY) to remove any cell debris from the medium. Subsequently, the filtered CM was concentrated via TFF with a 300 kDa MWCO ultrafiltration membrane filter capsule to remove contaminants. Finally, purified R‐EVs were suspended in PBS and stored at −70°C until further use. For in vivo imaging, R‐EVs were labelled with NHS monoester‐functionalized cyanine 7. In brief, R‐EVs were incubated with 5 µM of Cy7 NHS monoester dye at 37°C in the dark for 1 h. The solution was purified using a Sephadex PD‐10 column (GE Healthcare, NJ) and subsequently used for in vivo imaging.

Characterization of R‐EV

The morphology of the R‐EVs was observed using TEM (JEOL‐2100F, Tokyo, Japan) at an accelerating voltage of 200 kV. For TEM sample preparation, R‐EVs were dropped onto a 200‐mesh carbon‐film‐coated grid (Samchang Inc., Seoul, Korea), negatively stained with 1% uranyl acetate for 1 min, and washed with deionized water. The size distribution and number of R‐EVs were measured using a particle size analyzer (Nano ZS90, Malvern Instruments, Worcs, UK) and nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) (NanoSight LM10, Malvern Instruments Ltd., Malvern, UK), respectively. The surface zeta potential of R‐EVs was determined at 25°C using Zetasizer Nano ZS90 (Malvern Instruments, Worcs, UK). R‐EV protein levels were quantitatively measured using a Pierce BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific lnc, MA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions.

For ultrathin‐section‐TEM images, bacterial samples were immediately pre‐fixed in a mixture of freshly prepared 2.5% glutaraldehyde in 0.1 M sodium cacodylate trihydrate buffer (CB) for 12 h at 4°C. Next, the samples were post‐fixed in 2% osmium tetroxide for 1 h and 1% uranyl acetate for 12 h prior to dehydration in graded ethanol and the subsequent infiltration and embedding steps. After polymerization at 60°C for 12 h, ultrathin sections were cut using a Leica ULTRACUTS/FCS (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) and mounted on Formvar carbon‐coated copper grids. The sections were post‐stained with 1% (w/v) aqueous uranyl acetate and lead citrate then examined under a Tecnai F20 field‐emission gun electron microscope operated at 200 kV (FEI, Tecnai G2‐F20, Hillsboro, USA).

For cryo‐TEM observation of isolated R‐EVs without cells, the sample was prepared on lacey carbon film‐supported grids with 200 mesh. A thin aqueous film, blotted with filter paper, was fabricated using a Vitrobot‐type FP 5350/60 (FEI, Hillsboro, USA) and immediately plunged into liquid ethane. The resulting grids were stored in liquid nitrogen and then transferred to a cryotransfer holder. Images were obtained using a CCD camera (2k, Gatan) equipped with a Tecnai F20 field‐emission gun electron microscope operated at 200 kV (FEI, Tecnai G2‐F20, Hillsboro, USA). For the quantitative detection of lipoteichoic acid (LTA) from R‐EVs, a double‐antibody sandwich‐based LTA ELISA kit was used according to the manufacturer's instructions (MyBioSource, CA, USA).

Protein preparation and in‐gel tryptic digestion

Frozen pellets of R (n = 3) were thawed and resuspended in 500 µL cold lysis buffer (50 mM Tris‐Cl pH 8.0, 5 mM EDTA, 0.4% SDS, 50 mM NaCl, and 1 mM PMSF). Bacterial resuspensions were transferred to bead tubes (Garnet 0.70 mm, PowerBead tubes, Qiagen, USA) and then homogenized at 30 Hz for 8 min using TissueLyser II (Qiagen, USA). Homogenized samples were heated up to 60°C for 15 min and disrupted with a sonic probe (VC505, Sonics, Newtown, CT, USA; amplitude 20% and 1 s on/off cycle for 30 s, twice). Supernatants were collected following the centrifugation (> 20,000 g, 15 min, 4°C) and stored at −80°C. After determining the protein concentration of R lysate and EVs (n = 3) using a Rapid Gold BCA protein assay kit (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA), 20 µg protein was precipitated overnight in a five‐fold volume of cold acetone at −20°C. Protein precipitates were pelleted by centrifugation (> 20,000 g, 10 min, 4°C) and briefly dried. The pellets were resuspended in loading buffer (LDS sample buffer, 50 mM DTT) and denatured by heating at 98°C for 5 min. Protein samples were separated on 4−12% NuPAGE Bis‐Tris gel (Life Technologies, Grand Island, NY, USA) and run into a gel of around 1 cm thickness. The gel was stained with 0.1% Coomassie brilliant blue and rinsed with water. Each gel lane was excised and cut into 1 mm3 cubes. The gel pieces were washed twice, de‐stained with 50 mM ABC in 50% acetonitrile (ACN) solution (30 min, 55°C), and then dehydrated in 50%, 70%, and 100% ACN. Subsequently, in‐gel digestion of proteins was performed according to a previously reported protocol (Shevchenko et al., 2006). The gel pieces were reduced and alkylated with 10 mM dithiothreitol and 50 mM iodoacetamide, respectively, then washed and dehydrated again. Next, 25 ng/µL trypsin in reaction buffer (50 mM ABC, 0.1 mM CaCl2, pH 8.0) was added to each sample. Following the addition of an equal volume of reaction buffer, the samples were incubated at 37°C overnight. Trypsin inactivation was done by adding 200 µLof 10% FA in 10% ACN prior to ultrasonication (5510R‐MT, Branson Ultrasonics, Danbury, CT, USA) for 10 min at 30°C. Tryptic peptides were extracted with the sequential addition of 200 µL of 0.5% TFA in 50% ACN and 0.25% TFA in 75% ACN and 100% ACN, each followed by ultrasonication (20 min, 30°C). Every extraction step, except for the last, was performed twice. Pooled peptide extracts were concentrated using SpeedVac (SPD1030, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and then stored at −80°C.

LC‐MS/MS and data processing

After the reconstitution of dried tryptic peptides in 10 µL solvent A (0.1% FA in water), 2 µL of each sample was loaded for LC‐MS/MS analysis equipped with the Easy nLC 1000 system (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA) and Q Exactive Orbitrap high‐resolution mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA). Samples were trapped in an Acclaim™ PepMap™ 100 C18 column (75 µm × 2 cm, Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA) and separated on an EASY‐Spray column (75 µm × 15 cm, particle size ≤3 m, pore size 100 Å, PepMap™ RSLC C18, Thermo Scientific, San Jose, CA) using a 90 min gradient of solvent A and B (0.1% FA in ACN). The gradient included 5 to 40% solvent B at a flow rate of 300 nL/min and a column temperature of 35°C. The eluted peptides were ionized at 2 kV. A full MS scan was performed from m/z 400 to m/z 2000 with a full width a (FWHM) resolution of 70,000, followed by a data‐dependent HCD MS/MS scan of the top 10 ions. Parameters used for MS/MS scan were: resolution = 17500, loop count = 10 (Top 10), isolation window = m/z 2.0, normalized collision energy = 27, peptide match = preferred, exclude isotope = on, dynamic exclusion = 20.0 s.

The raw MS spectra files were searched against the Roseburia genus database comprising 146385 genes (downloaded on 24 July 2020, from UniProtKB: http://www.uniprot.org/proteomes) using Proteome Discoverer (v2.4; Thermo Fisher Scientific). The search settings were as follows: Sequest HT search engine, trypsin (full), maximum two missed cleavage sites, MS tolerance (10 ppm), MS/MS tolerance 0.02 Da, carbamidomethylation (C) for fixed modification, and oxidation (M) for variable modification. Peptide/protein identification and quantitation were conducted on Scaffold (v4.10.0, Proteome Software Inc.) with a threshold of 99.0/99.9%. For protein identification, a minimum of two peptides is required. The total precursor intensity (TPI) values were used for the quantitative analysis. Based on the protein sequences, GO terms were exported from UniProtKB. Each identified protein was aligned against the Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) database (https://ftp.ncbi.nih.gov/pub/COG/COG2020/data/) using DIAMOND (Buchfink et al., 2021)(v2.0.9; default parameters, very‐sensitive mode). Unless otherwise stated, all data were analyzed and visualized using GraphPad Prism (v9.0.1). Each identified protein was required to have valid values in ≥50% of samples in at least one group. The missing values were imputed as min/2 for each sample. Normalized TPI intensities (by the sum of values per sample) were used for further analyses. Statistical analysis was performed using multiple unpaired t‐tests with Benjamini‐Hochberg correction. Significant false discovery rate (FDR)‐adjusted P‐values (q) are indicated by asterisks (*q < 0.05, **q < 0.01).

To analyze the metabolites of R‐EVs, R and R‐EVs were extracted with 50% methanol and filtrated through a syringe filter of 0.45 um pore size. HR‐MS and MS/MS spectra were obtained using a Q Exactive™ Hybrid Quadrupole‐Orbitrap™ mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with Acquity UPLC® BEH C18 (2.1 × 100 mm I.D., 1.7 µm) column (Waters, Milford, MA, USA). The LC‐MS/MS analysis was performed using mobile phases that consisted of 0.05% formic acid in water (A) and ACN (B) and the following gradient elution method: 0−10 min, 10−100% B; 10−12.5 min, 100% B. The flow rate was set to 0.3 mL/min. Full MS spectra were acquired under positive ionization conditions from m/z 100 to m/z 1500 at 70,000 FWHM resolution, with MS/MS fragmentation data obtained in data‐dependent scan mode using 30 V collision energies at 17,500 FWHM resolution.

The LC‐MS/MS data of R and R‐EV extracts were converted to a Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) compatible format (. mzXML), and uploaded to the GNPS server. Molecular networks were generated on the GNPS analysis platform under filtration by removing all MS/MS fragment ions within ± 17 Da of the precursor m/z. The precursor ion mass tolerance and MS/MS fragment ion tolerance were set to 2.0 and 0.5 Da, respectively. The networks were visualized using Cytoscape (v3.8.2).

In vitro cell culture

The human colon cell lines Caco‐2 and Raw264.7 macrophage cells were grown at 37°C in a 75% humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2 in 24‐well plates. Before treatment, Caco‐2 and Raw264.7 cells were washed twice with PBS and kept in serum‐free DMEM and RPMI medium. All cells, related reagents, and supplies were purchased from GIBCO Life Technologies (Rockville, MD, USA).

Western blotting

For analysis in DSS‐induced mice, colon tissues were lysed in 10 mL Tissue Extraction reagent I buffer (Invitrogen, MA, USA) per gram of tissue sample containing a protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktail (Thermo Scientific, MA, USA). The colon tissue lysates were centrifuged at 11,200 × g for 5 min at 24°C, and the supernatants were transferred to a new microfuge tube. The supernatants were frozen immediately at −80°C before analysis. Colon tissue lysates were separated by SDS‐PAGE (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, Hercules, CA, USA) and transferred to a polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membrane (Bio‐Rad Laboratories, CA, USA). The PVDF membranes after SDS‐PAGE were blocked with 5% non‐fat dry milk (Georgiachem, GA, USA) or Bovine Serum Albumin (Georgiachem, GA, USA) in Phosphate‐buffered saline with 0.1% Tween® 20 detergent buffers. For measuring the amount of proteins in colon tissue, their lysates were analyzed by appropriate application of primary antibodies [GLP‐1 (1:2000), Phospho‐PI3 Kinase p85/p55 (1:1000), Phospho‐STAT3 (1:1000), STAT3 (1:1000), Phospho‐NFκB p65 (1:1000), NFκB p65 (1:1000), and β‐actin (1:1000)]. Sections were then incubated with the appropriate horseradish peroxidase‐conjugated secondary antibody [anti‐rabbit IgG antibody (1:5000) or anti‐mouse IgG antibody (1:5000)] for 1 h at room temperature. According to the manufacturer's instructions, immunoreactive proteins were visualized using ECL chemiluminescent reagent (GenDEPOT, TX, USA) and quantified by densitometry.

Quantitative RT‐PCR

Cells/tissue samples were lysed using TRIzol reagent (Life Technologies, Carlsbad, CA, USA) for RT‐PCR. Total RNA was isolated from the samples using the chloroform‐isopropanol method according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was synthesized using a high‐capacity cDNA synthesis kit (Invitrogen). Two replicates were performed for each sample. Mouse samples were amplified using TaqMan gene‐specific probes on a 7500 Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). Commercial TaqMan probes (Thermo Fisher Scientific) were used for the TaqMan assay. The samples were amplified using SYBR Green gene‐specific primers and a 7500 Real‐Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems). A master mix for the pre‐amplification step of the TaqMan or SYBR Green method was prepared using the Toyobo Master Mix Kit (Toyobo, Osaka, Japan). Murine Muc‐2 mRNA expression was normalized using GAPDH mRNA expression. Human Muc‐2 mRNA expression was normalized using β‐actin mRNA expression. Results were expressed as 2∆∆Ct and fold change was determined by comparing with that of the untreated control group.

In vitro TEER measurement and FITC‐Dextran permeability in Caco‐2 cell monolayers

Caco‐2 cells were seeded on collagen‐coated polycarbonate membrane Transwell inserts with a surface area of 0.33 cm2 and 0.4 µm pore size (SPL Life Science, Pocheon, Republic of Korea). The culture medium for Caco‐2 cells was replaced with fresh DMEM containing 10% FBS twice a day. After the TEER exceeded 400 Ohm·cm2, monolayer Caco‐2 layers were exposed to 2.5% DSS (w/v) for 6 h either alone or in co‐treatment with 50 µg (protein level) R‐EVs. TEER of Caco‐2 monolayers was analyzed after DSS and EV incubation for 3 or 6 h with an epithelial volt‐ohm‐metre in each insert and multiplied by the membrane surface area (0.33 cm2). It was corrected by subtracting the background TEER of the blank membrane from that of culture media (without cells). Data were collected from quadruple inserts per treatment in three experiments and expressed as a percentage of basal TEER (i.e., 400 Ohm·cm2) obtained before EV treatment. At the end of TEER measurements, 50 µg/µL of FITC‐labelled 4 kDa dextran (FITC‐D4; Sigma‐Aldrich, MO, USA) was added to the apical side of cells and incubated for 1 h at 37°C. The permeability of monolayer Caco2 layers was measured by sampling the media of the bottom wells, followed by analysis of the fluorescence levels of FITC‐D4 spectrophotometrically using a 96‐well microplate reader at excitation and emission wavelengths of 485 and 540 nm, respectively.

Establishment of DSS‐induced IBD model

All experiments performed with live animals complied with the relevant laws and institutional guidelines of the Korea Institute of Science and Technology (KIST), and the institutional committees approved all experiments (KIST‐2020‐110). Female C57BL/7J mice (7‐8 weeks) were purchased from DBL (Incheon, Republic of Korea) To investigate the preventive effects of R‐EVs, 2.5% DSS with a molecular weight between 36,000 and 50,000 (MP Biomedicals, CA, USA) was administered to mice (5−15 mice/group) via autoclaved drinking water for 7 days, followed by distilled water administration for 1 day. The mice were orally injected with R‐EV (200 µg/mouse/day) or R‐EVlow (20 µg/ mouse/day) on days 1, 3, 5, and 7, then sacrificed at day 10 after DSS treatment.

To test the therapeutic effects of R‐EVs, mice were treated with 1.5% DSS (5−12 mice/group) mixed with drinking water for 7 days. Thereafter, the treatment was continued for the duration of the experiment. The mice were orally injected with R‐EV (200 µg/mouse/day), weighed, and monitored daily for signs of disease. The disease activity index (DAI) was calculated using a previously published scoring system (Chassaing et al., 2014; Wirtz et al., 2017).

ELISA

C57BL/6 mice were euthanized via CO2 and cardiac puncture was performed. After sacrificing the mice, whole blood was collected in a 1 mL syringe (29G; BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and placed in a heparin tube (BD Biosciences) to prevent blood coagulation. After centrifugation (12,000 × g, 20 min, 4°C) of the blood, the serum was transferred to a sterile microfuge tube (SPL Life Science, Pocheon, Republic of Korea). It was then frozen immediately at −80°C and thawed on an ice rack before analysis. Further steps to analyze cytokines were performed using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions (R&D Systems, Minneapolis, MN, USA). For NO assay, mice serum was diluted in PBS and used for NO assay. The NO assay was performed in duplicate. Mouse serums were incubated with Griess reaction reagents (Invitrogen, CA, USA) for 10 min. After the reaction, the absorbance of each well was measured at 550 nm using a microplate reader (TECAN, Mannedorf, Switzerland). Based on the standard curve, the amount of NO in the serum was quantified.

The intestinal permeability of the mice was assessed by measuring endotoxin activity in the serum using a chromogenic endotoxin quantification kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific, CA, USA). Serum was collected from each mouse via heart puncture using a sterile syringe and a heparin tube. Sterile serum was diluted in endotoxin‐free water, and endotoxin activity was measured according to the manufacturer's protocol.

DPP4 activity and plasma active GLP‐1 levels in the serum were evaluated using a DPP4 activity assay kit (Sigma‐Aldrich, MO, USA) and an active GLP‐1 ELISA assay kit (Eagle Biosciences, NH, USA), respectively.

In vivo distribution of R‐EV

The institutional committees of KIST approved all experiments (KIST‐2020‐109). Female athymic nude mice (7‐8 weeks) were purchased from DBL (Incheon, Republic of Korea). To observe the in vivo biodistribution of Cy7‐labelled R‐EVs, DSS‐induced IBD mice were prepared by oral administration of 2.5% DSS in autoclaved drinking water for 7 days, followed by administration of distilled water for 7 days to nude mice (Orientbio Inc., Seongnam, Republic of Korea). Next, a solution containing Cy7‐R‐EV was orally administered to the mice, and their time‐dependent biodistribution was monitored at predetermined intervals (3, 6, 9, and 12 h). Major organs were visualized using an IVIS Lumina III In Vivo Imaging System (Caliper Life Sciences, MA, USA) with a 670‐nm pulsed laser diode. All data were quantitatively calculated using the region of interest function of the software embedded in the IVIS Imaging System, and the values are presented as mean ± standard deviation for groups of at least three animals.

Blood chemistry

To investigate liver function in the blood, DSS‐induced IBD mice were established, as described above. The blood collected from the mice was centrifuged for 10 min at 4,000 rpm at 4°C. The isolated serum was analyzed for aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), and alkaline phosphatase (ALP) levels. The serum parameters were evaluated by Raon‐Bio Inc. (Yongin, Republic of Korea).

Distal colon histology

For histological analysis, faecal material from the large intestine was gently placed in cold PBS after sacrificing the mice. Distal colon tissues (2 cm from the rectum) were fixed in methanol‐Carnoy's fixative solution (60% methanol, 30% chloroform, 10% glacial acetic acid) for 6 h at room temperature. The tissues were treated with 100% methanol 2 × 15 min, 100% ethanol 2 × 15 min, and xylene 2 × 15 min. Next, they were permeabilized with xylene and incubated in molten paraffin (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany) for 30 min. After incubation, the tissues were embedded in paraffin. The paraffin blocks were cut into 7 µm sections using a microtome (Leica, Wetzlar, Germany). The sections were mounted on slides and heated for 30 min to increase their adhesiveness. The dried slides were stored at room temperature until staining.

For hematoxylin and eosin staining (H&E), the slides were dewaxed by heating at 60°C for 1 h, followed by incubation with fresh xylene at 60°C for 30 min, and treatment with absolute ethanol for another 30 min. To analyze the number of goblet cells per crypt, paraffin‐embedded tissue sections were serially dehydrated with xylene and ethanol, followed by AB staining, as previously described (Chassaing et al., 2015). The number of goblet cells per crypt was measured in eight different areas of one section, and in at least 15 sections from each mouse. AB+ cells per crypt were analyzed in five different areas of two sections and in at least eight sections from each mouse. The number of goblet cells (AB+ cells) per crypt was quantified in 15 randomly selected 200 × fields per specimen. AB+ cells per crypt were visualized using an Olympus BX51 microscope. Histological evaluation of colon inflammation was based on epithelial changes, mucosal architecture, and inflammatory cell infiltration scores, as previously described (Erben et al., 2014). The sum of each score represented the histological score. Representative images of AB‐ and H&E‐stained sections were obtained using an optical microscope and rendered using Adobe Photoshop (Adobe, CA, USA).

Synthetic gut microbiome model

Synthetic gut microbial communities were reconstructed using Venturelli's method (Clark et al., 2021) and Humphrey's method (Mabwi, Hitayezu et al., 2021). Briefly, all species were precultured at 37°C in an anaerobic chamber to maintain a stable growing culture. Mucin‐agar gel was prepared to simulate the mucosal environment by adding 50 g/L porcine mucin, 30 g/L agar, and 0.5 g/L L‐cysteine hydrochloride. The pH was adjusted to 6.5 with 1 N HCl before autoclaving. After autoclaving, approximately 2 mL of the mucin‐agar liquid solution was pipetted into 14 mL culture tubes and allowed to solidify in an anaerobic chamber. To create the desired microbial community combination, each preculture solution from 16 anaerobes was diluted with GAM or BB medium so that the final optical density was 0.00125 for Escherichia coli and Enterococcus faecalis, 0.005 for all other bacteria, and 0.01 for Akkermansia muciniphila and Alistipes indistinctus, considering their bacterial growth rates. Then, 15 mL of each microbial diluent was combined (with a total volume of approximately 240 mL), mixed, and then dispensed (3 mL) into 14 mL culture tubes containing mucin‐agar gel. The tubes were incubated at 37°C in an anaerobic chamber for 36 h and the genomic DNA was extracted from the solid mucin‐agar and the upper liquid part. Before genomic DNA extraction, the mucin‐agar solid gel was rinsed thrice with sterile phosphate‐buffered saline to ensure that only bacteria capable of adhering to the gel were retained.

DNA extraction and bacterial 16S amplicon sequencing

The cecum of each mouse and bacterial pellets from the synthetic microbiome model were collected in a sterile tube, immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80°C until further use. Total genomic DNA was extracted using the QIAamp Power Faecal Pro DNA Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). The Illumina‐adapted universal primers 341F/805R, which target the V3‐V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene, were used for DNA amplification. The PCR mixture consisted of 2.5 U of the Ex Taq enzyme, 10× buffer, 2.5 mM dNTP mix (TaKaRa, Tokyo, Japan), 5−50 ng of DNA template, and 12.5 pmol of each primer. After purification using AMPure XP beads (Beckman Coulter, High Wycombe, UK), the PCR amplicons were quantified using a Qubit dsDNA HS Assay Kit (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA). The concentration of each sample was normalized to 4 nM, pooled into a single tube, and sequenced on a MiSeq platform using a paired‐end 2 × 300 bp reagent kit (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA).

Microbiome pipeline analysis steps

The raw sequences were processed using the QIIME2 pipeline (v2019.7) (Bolyen et al., 2019). Low‐quality, duplicated, and chimeric sequences were eliminated using the Divisive Amplicon Denoising Algorithm 2 (DADA2). To classify the filtered reads into taxonomic groups, a Naïve Bayes classifier was trained using the V3‐V4 16S rRNA region, a 341/805R primer set, and the Greengenes 99% reference set (v13.8). This trained feature classifier was then used to assign a taxonomy to each read using the default settings in QIIME. Microbial composition and diversity were analyzed using MicrobiomeAnalyst (Chong et al., 2020). Non‐metric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) plots were generated from a Bray‐Curtis distance matrix to represent microbiota compositional differences among groups visually. To select subsets of taxa at genus level that were differentially abundant between each group, linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe) was applied under the condition α = 0.05 with an LDA score of at least 3.5 (Segata et al., 2011).

Crystal violet biofilm assay

A slightly modified crystal violet biofilm assay (Kelly et al., 2020) was used to determine the biofilm formation ability of Bifidobacterium following R‐EV or IPI supplementation. B. breve and B. longum subsp. infantis were precultured in 10 mL of GAM at 37°C for 24 h. The next day, 1% of each bacterium was inoculated in GAM and BB medium, treated with R‐EV (40 µg protein/mL) and three concentrations of IPI (1, 3, and 10 µM) in 96‐well cell plates, and incubated at 37°C for 36 h. At the end of incubation, cell plates were carefully washed thrice with deionized water and dried for 1 h at 60°C. Later, the biofilms were stained with 100 µL of 1% crystal violet for 1 min, and the excess crystal violet was removed by washing thrice with deionized water. Next, crystal violet‐stained biofilms were solubilized with 100 µL of 5% (v/v) acetic acid, and the absorbance was determined at 570 nm wavelength using the microplate reader (Synergy HT, Biotek).

Statistical analysis

All values are expressed as the mean ± standard error of the mean or mean ± standard deviation. The differences between experimental and control groups were assessed using Student's t‐test or one‐way ANOVA and considered statistically significant (marked with an asterisk [*] or double asterisk [**] in the figures) if p < 0.05 and p < 0.01, respectively. The schematic illustrations were created using www.biorender.com.

RESULTS

Depletion of R. intestinalis abundance in patients with IBD

We analyzed bacterial communities in 1,638 stool samples from 130 participants in the Integrative Human Microbiome Project (HMP2) (Lloyd‐Price et al., 2019) (Table S1). Epidemiological studies of IBD have shown a greater prevalence and severity of Crohn's disease (CD) in women than in men (Bernstein et al., 2006; Cleynen et al., 2016; Cleynen et al., 2013; Subramanian et al., 2017), whereas ulcerative colitis (UC) is more prevalent and severe in men (Lophaven et al., 2017). Female sex hormones affect the immune system and worsen IBD symptoms (Goodman et al., 2020). Therefore, we conducted an ANCOM analysis on metagenomic sequencing data to identify the depleted taxa in male and female patients with IBD.

Our results revealed numerous sex‐specific species with reduced abundance in patients with IBD compared to healthy participants. For example, in women, Ruminococcus bromii, R, and Bacteroides cellulosilyticus were significantly (W‐statistic value≥0.7, log fold change[IBD/nonIBD] < 0.6) depleted in patients with CD and those with combined CD and UC (Figure 1b and Tables S2‐S4). In men, Akkermansia muciniphila, Alistipes putredinis, and Bacteroides massiliensis were significantly (W ≥0.7, FC[IBD/nonIBD] < 0.6) depleted in patients with IBD, CD, and UC (Tables S5‐S7). Notably, short‐chain fatty acid (SCFA)‐producing bacteria were deficient in patients with IBD, with R alone being detected in over half of the female stool samples (Figure 1c). Given the considerable correlation between species differences and their percentage in HMP2 women, we selected R as the model bacterium to investigate whether R‐EVs can alleviate colitis in an IBD model.

Preparation and characterization of R‐EVs

Rapid and efficient large‐scale isolation of BEVs from biological fluids is imperative for clinical applications. For isolation and purification of R‐EVs, we processed the conditioned medium (CM) obtained from R through an automated tangential flow filtration (TFF) system with a 300 kDa MWCO ultrafiltration membrane filter (Figure 2a). Ultrathin‐section transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images confirmed substantial in vitro production of R‐EVs by R. Cryo‐TEM further revealed the presence of a single lipid membrane structure in R‐EVs, which is characteristic of an independent bacterial membrane vesicle secreted by Gram‐positive bacteria (Qing et al., 2019) (Figure 2b). R‐EVs exhibited hydrodynamic diameters of 76 ± 10 nm, a particle concentration of 1.7 × 1012 particles/mL, and a particle purity of 1.4 × 109 particles/µg protein. They displayed a surface charge of −14.4 ± 0.6 mV owing to the negatively charged phospholipid membrane (Figure 2c and Figure S1). Our investigations into the origin of R‐EVs were supported by the identification of substantial amounts of the Gram‐positive marker molecule lipoteichoic acid (LTA) by ELISA, indicating that the R‐EVs originated from Gram‐positive bacteria (Figure 2d). These results validate the TFF‐based production system as a safe and effective strategy for isolating R‐EVs from R.

Proteomic profiling of R‐EVs

For proteomic profiling, protein samples (n = 6) were prepared from (R) (n = 3) and R‐EVs (n = 3), digested in‐gel, and analyzed using LC‐MS/MS. The Sequest HT search engine was used to search raw files against the Roseburia genus database from UniProtKB, and 999 proteins were identified (Figure S2). A notable difference was observed between the proteomic profiles of R and R‐EVs (Figure 2e). A total of 72 proteins (44 being R‐EV‐specific) and 294 proteins (266 being R‐specific) were significantly abundant (log2FC(R‐EV/R) ≥1 or ≤−1, p < 0.01) in R‐EV and R, respectively. To estimate the functional capabilities of the differentially expressed proteins, each was assigned Gene Ontology (GO) and Clusters of Orthologous Groups (COG) annotations (Figure 2f). According to the GO annotations, R‐EV‐enriched proteins were primarily associated with ATP binding (4), involved in cellular amino acid metabolic processes (6), and related to the cytoplasm (4). However, for almost all GO terms, R exhibited more abundant features, except ‘carbohydrate binding’, ‘beta‐galactosidase activity’, and ‘beta‐galactosidase complex’ where R‐EV‐enriched gene counts were greater than those of R (Figure S3A).

Functional composition analysis of COG categories demonstrated that more than 80% of total proteins in R‐EV fell within four functional categories: lipid metabolism (I: 30%), energy production and conversion (C: 21.2%), carbohydrate metabolism and transport (G: 19.9%), and amino acid metabolism and transport (E: 9.2%) (Figure 2f). Compared to R, R‐EVs displayed significantly (q < 0.01) increased function in three COG categories: function unknown (S, 3.1‐fold), lipid metabolism (I, 1.9‐fold), and carbohydrate metabolism and transport (G, 1.4‐fold) (Figure 2g). Particularly, 9 COGs from category ‘G’ and two from ‘I’ were significantly enriched in R‐EV (q < 0.05) (Figure 2h and Figure S3B). Specifically, Most of them were found to be significantly involved in the modulation of inflammation related to lipogenesis, nitric oxide (NO), fatty acid or via JAK2/STAT3 pathway, TGF‐β1 pathway (Behl et al., 2021; Guerra et al., 2022; Jin et al., 2021; Key et al., 2020; Kotlyarov & Kotlyarova, 2021; Rasouli et al., 2017; Regnault et al., 2013; Y. Wang et al., 2022). These results indicate a possible contribution of multiple R‐EV proteins involved in modulating the inflammatory response, thereby alleviating DSS‐induced colitis.

In vivo preventive effect of R‐EVs in the colitis model

To investigate the preventive efficacy of R‐EVs against IBDs, we established colitis models using 2.5% dextran sulfate sodium (DSS) in drinking water for seven days. R‐EVs were administered to female C57BL/6J mice by oral gavage on days 1, 3, 5, and 7 (Figure 3a). For the efficacy comparison, the R‐treated group was chosen as positive control in this experiment. Preventive efficacy was determined by assessing various parameters, including changes in body weight, histological evidence of inflammation, and disease activity in mice (Table S8). Histological analyses using Hematoxyline and Eosin (H&E) and Alcian blue (AB) showed a loss of the epithelial cell barrier with infiltrated immune cells and depleted goblet cells in the DSS group compared to the control group. In contrast, the R‐EV group displayed an inhibitory effect on colonic inflammation, restoring intestinal epithelial integrity and mucosal architecture and exhibiting less infiltration of immune cells along with retention of goblet cells (Figure 3b‐e). Additionally, the DSS+R‐EV group also showed significantly enhanced preventive efficacy compared with the DSS and DSS+R groups, maintaining colon length, reducing the disease activity index, and alleviating body weight loss (Figure 3f‐h). Specifically, the DSS+R‐EV group exhibited 1.41 and 1.08 times longer colon length than the DSS and DSS+R groups. Furthermore, low‐dose R‐EV treatment (DSS+R‐EVlow) also effectively reduced the total histological score and disease activity index and maintained goblet cells compared to the DSS group. In particular, DSS+R‐EVlow groups showed 1.36 and 1.07 times longer colon lengths than the DSS and DSS+Rlow groups (Figure S4). The colon length of the DSS+R‐EV and DSS+R‐EVlow group was 81 and 78% compared with the normal control group (Figure S7).

Endotoxins and pro‐inflammatory cytokine levels in the serum of colitis models further revealed the preventive effects of R‐EVs (Figure 3i‐j). The DSS+R‐EV group significantly suppressed endotoxin, nitric oxide (NO), interleukin‐6 (IL‐6), and TNF‐α in serum. Furthermore, qRT‐PCR showed a remarkable decrease in the expression of IL‐1β and Reg3γ, with increased Muc2 and ZO‐1 expression in colonic tissue. These results indicate the potential preventive effects of R‐EVs in the DSS‐induced colitis model. Additionally, R‐EV treatment in the prevention model did not induce liver toxicity after oral administration in normal mice compared to the control groups (Figure S5A). Thus, R‐EVs are potent preventive and therapeutic strategies that can induce recovery of impaired intestinal permeability and effectively inhibit colitis progression in a safe manner.

R‐EV effects on the intestinal microbiota in the colitis

To explore whether R‐EV administration affects the gut microbial community, we performed 16S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing of the cecum from the colitis model (Figure 4). Compared to the control group, DSS treatment induced marked microbial community changes, increasing the Proteobacteria and Deferribacteres phyla in the prevention model. R‐EVs significantly increased the Actinobacteria phylum, including the genera Bifidobacterium, Collinsella, and Adlercreutzia. Notably, the DSS+R and DSS+R‐EV groups were distinctly clustered (blue colours) from the DSS group (red colour) in β‐diversity analysis (Figure 4a,b). DSS treatment in the therapeutic mice model did not show a clear microbial shift pattern (Figure S6).

We investigated α‐diversity in the prevention model, such as Chao1 richness and the Simpson and Shannon diversity indices, to compare the species richness and evenness within each group (Figure 4c). Although the Chao1 and Simpson diversity index were not significantly different between the groups, the Shannon diversity index was significantly higher in the R‐EV‐treated group. To evaluate the variation in cecal microbiota between groups and identify the genus differentially abundant in each group, we implemented the linear discriminant analysis effect size (LefSe) and found significant bacterial taxa (genus level) for each group (Figure 4d). R‐EV and R treatments significantly decreased the relative abundance of Butyricicoccus, Odoribacter, and Dehalobacterium. In contrast, the relative abundance of Aerococcus, Adlercreutzia, and Sutterella increased in the DSS+R and DSS+R‐EV groups compared to those in the DSS group. Notably, Bifidobacterium, a beneficial probiotic bacterium associated with IBD, was significantly increased in DSS+R and DSS+R+EV groups (Figure 4e). These results suggest that the amelioration of several inflammatory symptoms by R‐EVs in the DSS‐induced IBD mouse model may highly associated with Bifidobacterium.

R‐EV effects on microbial change in synthetic microbiome model

We examined whether an in vitro synthetic microbiome model could reproduce the microbial changes by R‐EVs. The synthetic gut microbial community comprised 16 core gut isolates, similar to the human gut core microbiota, and a mucin‐agar gel mimicking the mucosal environment (Figure 4f). To investigate the effect of R‐EVs on the increase in Bifidobacterium within the gut ecosystem, we administered R‐EVs to an in vitro synthetic microbiome model. Epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG) was used as a positive control for its ability to increase the abundance of Bifidobacterium (Z. Liu et al., 2020; X. Wang et al., 2017).

The microbial profiles of both the mouse and synthetic microbiome models showed similar ratios for the phyla Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes. However, some bacteria, including Enterococcus and Veillonella, were abnormally dominant (Figure 4g). Remarkably, R‐EV treatment significantly increased the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium breve and was more effective than EGCG (Figure 4h). Moreover, this pattern was similarly observed in both Gifu Anaerobic Medium (GAM) broth, which is relatively rich in nutrients, and Bryant and Burkey (BB) broth, which has low biomass owing to its low carbon source components. Altogether, these results demonstrate that R‐EVs could effectively alter the gut microbial community in both a well‐controlled synthetic microbial community model and a mouse model.

Analysis of R‐EV metabolites and their effects on Bifidobacterium biofilm formation

The Metabolites in R‐EVs were explored using high‐resolution MS/MS data and the Global Natural Products Social Molecular Networking (GNPS) platform, in which several molecules were identified (Figure S8). Among them, the molecules corresponding to IPI attracted our attention based on their potential ability as quorum‐sensing signal molecules for the growth of other gut microbiota and DPP4 inhibitors for alleviating gut inflammation. In LC‐MS/MS data, IPI of 0.68 and 1.24 µM were confirmed in R and R‐EVs, respectively (Figure S9).

We further compared the direct effects of R‐EVs and their inherent tripeptide IPI on Bifidobacterium growth. In a biofilm formation experiment, R‐EVs (40 µg protein/mL) with a range of IPI (1−10 uM) significantly enhanced the biofilm formation of B. breve compared to the control group (Figure 4i). Similarly, other Bifidobacterium strains, such as B. longum subsp. infantis, showed notably increased biofilm formation in the presence of R‐EVs and IPI, indicating that R‐EVs can promote the growth of Bifidobacterium in a microbial ecosystem.

Study of in vivo therapeutic efficacy of R‐EVs in the colitis model and their mechanisms

After examining the preventative effects of R‐EVs against IBD and their impact on the diversity and abundance of gut microbes, we eventually sought to confirm their therapeutic efficacy in a DSS‐induced colitis model. R‐EVs were orally administered to DSS‐induced mice daily for 7 days after establishing the colitis model (Figure 5a). Histological data showed a significantly diminished total histological score and loss of goblet cells in the colonic tissue of the DSS+R‐EV group (Figure 5b‐e). In addition, the colon length of the group was recovered close to that of the control group and significantly longer than that of other treatment groups (Figure 5f). The DSS+R‐EV group showed reduced disease activity index values and fully recovered body weight (Figure 5g,h). Consistent with these results, survival rates in the DSS+R‐EV group were higher than those in the DSS group (Figure 5i). In particular, the DSS+R‐EV group showed remarkably reduced endotoxin, NO, and pro‐inflammatory cytokine levels, approaching those of the healthy control group, which indicated the therapeutic effect of R‐EVs in the inflamed colon (Figure 5j). Moreover, in vitro Transwell studies demonstrated that the DSS+R‐EV group had significantly increased trans‐epithelial electrical resistance (TEER) values and decreased both FITC‐dextran and endotoxin permeability compared to the DSS group, respectively (Figure S10A‐10C). R‐EV treatment in the therapy model did not induce liver toxicity after oral administration in normal mice compared with control groups (Figure S5B).

The colon tissue and serum obtained from the IBD therapy mouse model were analyzed to explore further how the metabolites (IPI) in R‐EVs directly alleviate IBD symptoms. The DSS+R‐EV group showed significantly not only reduced dipeptidyl peptidase 4 (DPP4) activity in the colon compared to the DSS group but also an increased plasma concentration of the active form of glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) (Figure 5k‐l). Afterwards, western blot analysis further confirmed GLP‐1 and related pathway biomarkers such as PI3K, NFκB, and STAT3 in the inflamed colonic tissues (Figure 5m). GLP‐1 expression in the DSS+R‐EV group was 2.0 times higher than that of the DSS group, while the expression of the DSS+R group was not significantly increased. The expression levels of PI3K in the DSS+R‐EV group were significantly reduced by 4.2 times compared to the DSS group. Moreover, the DSS+R‐EV group significantly downregulated the expression levels of pNFκB/NFκB (2.0 times) and pSTAT3/STAT3 (7.1 times) compared to the DSS group. On the other hand, the DSS+R group only reduced the expression level of pSTAT3/STAT3 (10.6 times), not NFκB, compared to the DSS group (Figure 5n).

Since the inflamed colonic mucosa can recruit a large number of macrophages, vigorously secreting IL‐6 and TNF‐α as well as producing reactive nitrogen intermediates, we further examined the in vitro anti‐inflammatory studies of R‐EVs using activated macrophages (Figure S10D). Compared to the lipopolysaccharides (LPS) group, the R‐EV group reduced NO, tumour necrosis factor α (TNF‐α), and Interleukin‐6 (IL‐6) levels and promoted Interleukin‐10 (IL‐10). Notably, the R‐EV group showed an overall superior anti‐inflammatory effect in four indicators compared to extracellular vesicles derived from other species of the Roseburia genus (Figure S11). Overall, these data substantiate the ability of R‐EV to alleviate colonic inflammation in DSS‐induced mice by downregulating NFκB and STAT3 via suppression of the PI3K pathway.

In vivo and ex vivo distribution of R‐EVs in DSS‐induced IBD model

Subsequently, we monitored the in vivo and ex vivo tissue distribution of R‐EVs via a near‐infrared bioimaging system. We prepared DSS‐induced animal models by orally administering DSS and water to BALB/c‐nude mice for two weeks. Next, Cy7‐labelled R‐EVs were injected into normal controls and DSS‐induced IBD animal models (Figure 6a). Interestingly, a stronger fluorescence signal was clearly detected 3 h after administration in the inflamed intestinal region of the IBD group compared to the normal group (Figure 6b). At 12 h post‐intragastric administration, the average fluorescence intensity decreased significantly in both the normal and IBD groups. However, the fluorescence signal in the IBD group remained stronger than that in the control group (Figure 6c). These results were further supported by ex vivo organ imaging at 3 and 12 h post‐intragastric administration (Figure 6d). The fluorescence signals of the IBD group intestines were notably higher (1.6 and 1.2 times at 3 and 12 h, respectively) than those of the normal group intestines. Other organs showed negligible fluorescence signals. As shown in Figure 6e, the fluorescence intensity in the serum of the normal group was approximately 2.3 times higher than that of the IBD group. This translocation to inflamed sites after oral administration is presumably due to the in‐situ accumulation of positively charged proteins, such as transferrin, bactericidal/permeability‐increasing proteins, and antimicrobial peptides in IBD mice. By electrostatic interaction with these proteins, negatively charged R‐EVs exhibit preferential adherence to inflammatory areas, possessing efficient target‐specificity at diseased locations compared to the neutral or positively charged nanoparticles (Antoni et al., 2014; Beloqui et al., 2013; Jubeh et al., 2004; Li et al., 2020). Thus, R‐EVs have an excellent affinity for the inflamed large intestine, indicating their significant potential in IBD therapy.

DISCUSSION

Gram‐positive bacteria are distinct from Gram‐negative bacteria for peptidoglycan layer thickness. Since Gram‐positive bacteria's thickness is greater than Gram‐negative bacteria's, the thick peptidoglycan layer may be a physical barrier to the release of BEV. However, growing evidence shows that BEVs can reach the extracellular space through the thick peptidoglycan cell wall (Hong et al., 2011; H. Liu et al., 2021; Prados‐Rosales et al., 2011; Rivera et al., 2010; Toyofuku et al., 2019). Although the mechanism for their biogenesis is still an unsolved question, some hypotheses have been postulated: BEVs are forced through the wall by turgor pressure, enzymes with cell wall‐modifying properties such as endolysin and autolysin are released with BEVs to increase pore size and permeability to facilitate the delivery of BEVs, and use of protein channels or specific structures to guide them to the extracellular environment (Brown et al., 2015; Cuesta et al., 2021).

A few studies demonstrated that Roseburia improves the host's intestinal environment and activates the mucosal immune system (Luo et al., 2019; Quan et al., 2018; Seo et al., 2020; Tan et al., 2019). However, the mechanisms underlying microbe‐host interactions are yet to be elucidated. Previous studies have shown only the anti‐inflammatory effect of R through the reduction of oxidative stress, downregulation of inflammatory cytokines, and increased SCFA content in the gut. As a result, the molecular mechanisms underlying its interaction with the host and the therapeutic effects have not been explored. Therefore, this study sought to scrutinize the mechanism of action of R using R‐EVs in treating intestinal disorders.

The potential role of BEVs was investigated in the treatment of experimental IBD models in which BEVs were involved in inflammatory response‐related pathways such as JAK1/STAT1 and JAK1/STAT6, TLR4/Myd88/NFκB, MAPK/NFκB, Nrf2/HO‐1 pathway, and M2 macrophage polarization (Cao et al., 2019; Hao et al., 2021; L. Liang et al., 2022; L. Ma et al., 2023; Ye et al., 2023). To the best of our knowledge, however, this is the first study to show the therapeutic role of R‐EVs as a critical factor responsible for the therapeutic effects of R as well as the shift in the gut microbial community and host response.

Challenges in clinical EV applications include an effective production system, such as a scalable, reproducible, and sterile isolation process. Conventional techniques for isolating B‐EVs from biological fluids, including ultracentrifugation, density gradients, and size exclusion chromatography (Busatto, Giacomini et al., 2018; Gámez‐Valero et al., 2016; Gardiner et al., 2016), have limitations such as input volume, time‐consuming steps, and EV yield, hindering their clinical potential. TFF is an effective and productive purification technique that addresses these challenges, resulting in higher EV yield and improved batch‐to‐batch consistency (Busatto, Vilanilam et al., 2018). Considering these merits, we introduced the TFF system to obtain R‐EVs from a large volume of the R culture medium.

The microbiome modulatory effects of R‐EVs were evaluated on a synthetic gut microbiome model simulating a well‐controlled intestinal environment with controlled viruses and other pathogens (Mabwi, Kim et al., 2021; Venturelli et al., 2018; Vrancken et al., 2019). R‐EVs displayed a remarkable similarity between the mice and synthetic microbial community models, with a significant increase in Bifidobacterium. The role of Bifidobacterium as a positive modulator for IBD has been well‐established (Chen et al., 2021; X. Ma et al., 2021; N. Wang et al., 2021). It also exhibits butyrogenic effects through cross‐feeding interactions with butyrate producers, such as Faecalibacerium and Roseburia (Belenguer et al., 2006; Falony et al., 2006). Recent studies have shown that γ‐glutamylcysteine or exopolysaccharides produced by Bifidobacterium are primary substances representing its anti‐inflammatory activity (Castro‐Bravo et al., 2018; Engevik et al., 2021; Yu et al., 2019). Therefore, the significant increase in Bifidobacterium under R‐EV treatment shows that the amelioration of DSS‐induced colitis may occur via Bifidobacterium.

Next, we also investigated R‐EV components that enhance the relative abundance of Bifidobacterium in a microbial ecosystem. IPI treatment stimulated biofilm formation in two Bifidobacterium strains, B. breve and B. longum. The tripeptide IPI, also known as diprotin A, is a representative DPP4 inhibitor and regulates glucose by stabilizing GLP‐1 (Rónai et al., 1999). Interestingly, IPI was first isolated from a bacterial culture solution of Bacillus cereus (Umezawa et al., 1984) and has recently been identified as one of the EVs produced by a fungus, Cryptococcus gattii (Reis et al., 2021). A recent transcriptome and metabolome study for the biofilm formation of Bifidobacterium showed that small peptides such as alanyl‐proline, leucylproline, and prolyl‐tyrosine were upregulated when the Bifidobacterium biofilm formation was activated (Z. Liu et al., 2021). This fact suggests that the small vesicular peptides uniquely present in microbial‐derived EVs can act as quorum‐sensing signal molecules that affect the growth of other gut microbiota. Most studies on the interaction of Bifidobacterium and R have focused on the substantial butyrate produced by R through cross‐feeding with Bifidobacterium‐generated materials. Thus, an increase in butyrate producers such as Roseburia, Faecalibacterium, and Eubacterium can be increased using Bifidobacterium (Belenguer et al., 2006; Cha et al., 2018; Falony et al., 2006; Riviere et al., 2016). However, our findings are the first to reveal the impact of R‐EVs produced from R on Bifidobacterium, demonstrating that R‐derived small peptides promote Bifidobacterium biofilm formation.

In the DSS‐induced colitis model, R‐EVs effectively inhibited NFκB and STAT3 via the PI3K pathway. The marked ameliorative effects of R‐EV in the DSS‐induced colitis model might be due to the IPI in the nanostructure. These results align with previous reports, which indicate that DPP4 inhibitors downregulate pro‐inflammatory mediators like IL‐1β, TNF‐α, and IL‐6 in inflammatory disease mouse models and macrophages (He et al., 2020; Hirakawa et al., 2015; Lin & Lin, 2016). Although R‐EVs contain IPI in their nanostructures and display the therapeutic effect in colitis models via PI3K/ NFκB and STAT3 pathway, more in‐depth investigations regarding the content of single peptide depending on bacteria species and production environment are needed to better understand the microbe‐host interaction.

BEVs share many similarities with eukaryotic EVs, including nanosized structure, spherical shape, stability upon freezing and at 37°C, and the ability to transport genetic material. On the other hand, several differences have been found regarding structure, tolerance to high temperatures, composition, and their biogenesis (Cuesta et al., 2021). In addition, BEVs display remarkable advantages, including fast proliferation abilities, mature culture techniques, easy gene editing, and large‐scale cultivation, which have tremendous potential for industrialization owing to cost‐effectiveness and scalability compared with EVs from eukaryotic cells. Although the study of BEV is at an earlier stage than that of eukaryotic EVs, the number of BEV studies has continuously increased in recent years (Huang et al., 2022; H. Liu et al., 2021; Toyofuku et al., 2019). Therefore, these properties of BEVs provide distinct benefits compared with other types of cell‐derived vesicles for a wide range of applications.

BEVs exhibit many desirable features, such as their unique nanosized structure, ease of modification, mass production, and industrialization. However, there are still significant challenges for the clinical translation of BEVs as delivery systems or therapeutics as follows: (i) potential safety issues by the presence of LPS for Gram‐negative bacteria, (ii) lack of standard methods for BEVs’ isolation and purification, (iii) lack of well‐defined identification markers present on BEVs, and (iv) complex contents, and (v) ambiguous internalization mechanisms to host cells.

To address the issues, quick, affordable, standardized methods and innovative BEV modification strategies are imperative. Since BEV biology is highly complicated, vigorous multidisciplinary research would be imperative for a better understanding of the microbiome, BEVs, host, and their interplay for facilitating the clinical application of BEV.

In this report, we investigated the functional mechanism of how R produces therapeutic effects in IBD and demonstrated that R‐EVs have a critical therapeutic role as potent anti‐inflammatory agents for treating IBD. We subsequently identified the single peptide (IPI) derived from R and R‐EVs, which induces the systemic increase of GLP‐1 and regulates dysbiosis. In the DSS‐induced colitis model, orally administered R‐EVs exhibited significant accumulation in inflamed colonic tissue, leading to the recovery of intestinal damage by suppressing inflammatory cytokines and increasing mucin and tight junction without noticeable liver toxicity. Moreover, R‐EVs effectively modulated dysbiosis by increasing the Bifidobacterium genus in the microbial community. Due to the presence of IPI, R‐EVs reduced the DPP4 activity in inflamed colonic tissue and increased the plasma active GLP‐1, thereby downregulating NFκB and STAT3 via the PI3K pathway. Therefore, these results suggest that R‐EVs can restore DSS‐induced colitis and are highly promising as BEVs‐based therapeutics for treating intractable disease.
