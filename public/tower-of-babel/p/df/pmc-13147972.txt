South China Sea “神针(Trident)”: Solution for ocean forecast foundation models
Authors: Wei Zhao, Xiaopei Lin, Yishuai Jin, Weinan Huang, Yun Liang, Shoude Guan
Published: 2026

Main text

Artificial intelligence (AI) has rapidly advanced short-term forecasting in the Earth system, driving progress in the development of both ocean and meteorological foundation models. Meteorological foundation models lead the way and have already been incorporated into global operational weather forecasting systems. The Pangu global model, trained on the ECMWF Reanalysis v.5 (ERA5) dataset at a 25-kilometer (km) resolution, which can resolve major large- to meso- and small-scale weather systems, has demonstrated competitive forecast skill relative to traditional numerical weather prediction systems in selected benchmarks.1 Moreover, the ECMWF integrates its Artificial Intelligence Forecasting System (AIFS) into operational forecasting.2 The development of AI-driven meteorological foundation models provides rapid and accurate weather forecasting, supporting many socio-economic aspects such as energy, insurance, and shipping. Motivated by the success of meteorological foundation models, substantial efforts are now underway to develop global ocean foundation models. Using global ocean reanalysis datasets, nascent ocean foundation models such as Xihe,3 Wenhai,4 and Langya have been developed. These global ocean foundation models, trained at a higher computational resolution (such as GLORYS12 at 1/12° or approximately 10-km resolution) than those for the atmosphere, are reported to well forecast large- to mesoscale ocean dynamic processes. Despite the use of higher-resolution training datasets, it is still challenging to represent and forecast sub-mesoscale to small-scale ocean processes in ocean foundation models. Here, we examine the causes of this limitation and propose a practical pathway for developing AI-driven regional ocean foundation models, using the newly developed South China Sea (SCS) “神针(Trident)” foundation model as an example.

The motivation for developing Trident is seeking a pathway to develop an ocean foundation model distinct from meteorological models, driven by two fundamental considerations: (1) a much higher-resolution training dataset is required to resolve sub-mesoscale to small-scale ocean dynamics and (2) the scarcity of available ocean observations compared with those of the atmosphere. In terms of dynamical scales, the atmospheric Rossby deformation radius spans hundreds to over 1,000 km, whereas the ocean Rossby deformation radius is much smaller, typically only 10–50 km. A 25-km-resolution ERA5 reanalysis dataset is therefore sufficient to resolve large- to small-scale major weather systems reasonably well for the training of meteorology foundational models. However, even at a 10-km resolution, ocean numerical models can resolve large- and mesoscale processes well but struggle with sub-mesoscale and small-scale processes such as sub-mesoscale eddies, internal waves, fronts, and ocean responses to transient weather systems. Resolving these processes requires model resolutions at a km or even sub-km scale, far beyond the ability of available global ocean reanalysis datasets. It should also be noted that such high spatial resolution requires correspondingly high temporal resolution to adequately capture the rapid evolution of mesoscale, sub-mesoscale, and small-scale ocean processes. Many of these processes evolve on timescales of hours to days, and insufficient temporal sampling can lead to phase errors or omission of transient features. In terms of observations, atmospheric observations benefit from a mature, dense, and global three-dimensional network comprising satellites, radar, and ground stations, enabling continuous monitoring of multi-scale weather systems. In contrast, ocean observations are primarily limited to the sea surface from satellites. Subsurface layers depend heavily on limited data collected from moorings and profiling floats, leaving large areas of the ocean interior unsampled. Therefore, the relatively coarse reanalysis data and sparse observations hinder the development, improvement, and operational application of ocean foundation models to a much greater extent than those in meteorology.

To address the increasing demand to resolve sub-mesoscale to small-scale processes in operational forecasting, ocean foundation models and their associated training datasets must achieve spatial resolutions at km and even sub-km scales, as well as temporal resolutions down to hours and even minutes. Achieving this globally, however, remains constrained by both computational and observational limitations. Computationally, increasing the horizontal resolution from 10 km to a km scale, including the accompanying increase in vertical resolution, would result in a hundreds-fold increase in computational cost. From the perspective of ocean observations, achieving global, real-time, three-dimensional ocean observations comparable to those available for the atmosphere is impossible due to the scarcity of high-resolution subsurface data. In summary, given the current computational and observational limits, global ocean foundation models cannot be reliably trained and validated at required resolutions. By contrast, regional ocean foundation models can leverage dense, high-resolution data networks in specific areas, where both observational capabilities and computational requirements are achievable. As a result, focusing on regional models is not only a practical pathway but also a more effective strategy for advancing AI-driven ocean forecasting in a scientifically and operationally viable manner.

Here, we introduce the SCS 神针(Trident) model, a prototype regional ocean foundation framework for regional ocean forecasting (Figure 1). The SCS, a semi-enclosed marginal sea of the western Pacific, is characterized by energetic and complex multi-scale processes, including large-scale circulations, tides, internal waves, mesoscale eddies, sub-mesoscale motions, and motions in response to frequent super typhoons. These processes substantially impact offshore energy development, shipping safety, and disaster preparedness, generating urgent demand for high-resolution observations and accurate and rapid short-term forecasts. The development of the SCS Trident model can be supported by the South China Sea Mooring Array (SCSMA),5 the largest and most comprehensive regional ocean observation system in the world. The Trident model is developed on a 20-year training dataset that integrates high-resolution numerical simulations at km and hourly resolution alongside observations from the SCSMA system (Figure 1). This dataset allows a more detailed representation of sub-mesoscale to small-scale ocean variabilities than the daily-averaged GLORYS12 reanalysis datasets on which emerging global ocean foundation models are trained. Hourly sampling enables the model to learn rapidly evolving sub-mesoscale to small-scale processes. Building on this data advantage, the model incorporates dedicated modules that further enhance the representation of internal waves and typhoon-related processes. To capture multi-scale ocean dynamical processes, the model employs a deep learning architecture that combines depth-wise separable convolutions with Fourier neural operator layers. Operating at km resolution and sub-km resolution in specific areas, with hourly updates across the SCS, the model provides high-resolution forecasts up to 14 days ahead for large- and mesoscale processes as well as sub-mesoscale to small-scale dynamics. The foundation model has already been applied in various operational applications by offering multi-scale ocean information and user-oriented outputs. For instance, in collaboration with China National Offshore Oil Corporation, the Trident model provides timely, high-resolution spatiotemporal forecasts of internal waves and typhoon-induced processes to support offshore platform management and decision-making, such as guarding the safe time window for underwater drilling. These applications highlight the Trident model’s scientific feasibility and practical relevance, serving as a proof of concept for its broader adoption in marine forecasting and related ocean applications.

The Trident model currently depends on numerical simulations to establish its initial conditions. This dependence limits its progress toward becoming a fully end-to-end intelligent forecasting system. The next step is to leverage the SCSMA system to reconstruct initial conditions directly from multi-source observations. Achieving this would represent an important step toward a forecasting framework in which predictions are driven directly by observations. By doing this, the model would go beyond traditional numerical initialization and evolve into an integrated system that seamlessly connects observations with forecasts.

Looking forward, the challenge is not merely to achieve finer resolution or higher accuracy but to rethink the fundamental purpose of ocean foundation models, shifting their role toward interpreting and directly supporting decision-making in marine applications. The next generation should go beyond reproducing environmental fields and instead evolve into general-purpose intelligent systems for the marine environment. Such systems would not only resolve and forecast multi-scale dynamical processes but also translate them into actionable guidance, such as identifying safe windows for offshore operations or favorable conditions for shipping, aquaculture, and recreation, thus undertaking interpretive tasks that were previously reliant on human experts.

A central challenge in this transition is aligning physical environmental fields with event-based and textual information, enabling models to bridge ocean dynamics with the kinds of descriptions and decisions relevant to human activities. Achieving this shift requires a deep integration of observations, simulations, and intelligent models across physical, ecological, economic, and social domains, together with the development of representation and reasoning capabilities that connect physical processes to events, risks, and decisions. With continued advances in architectures, observing systems, and computational capacity, regional demonstrations will gradually scale toward a unified, high-resolution global forecasting framework. This will lay the foundation for a dynamic digital twin of the global ocean, an intelligent representation that not only predicts the ocean states but also serves as a guiding platform for sustainable development, disaster resilience, and a more balanced coexistence between humans and the ocean. We therefore call for coordinated efforts across the ocean science and engineering communities to advance regional ocean foundation models as a critical step toward intelligent and actionable ocean forecasting systems.
