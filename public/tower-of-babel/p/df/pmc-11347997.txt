Long‐term immune changes in patients with relapsed/refractory chronic lymphocytic leukemia following treatment with venetoclax plus rituximab
Authors: Arnon P Kater, Barbara F Eichhorst, Carolyn J Owen, Ulrich Jaeger, Brenda Chyla, Marcus Lefebure, Rosemary Millen, Yanwen Jiang, Maria Thadani‐Mulero, Michelle Boyer, John F Seymour
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune dysregulation is a hallmark of chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL). Anti‐CD20 antibodies (e.g., rituximab [R]) can be combined with venetoclax (Ven) to treat CLL. However, anti‐CD20 antibodies can increase hypogammaglobulinemia risk, while the effects of Ven on immune dysregulation are still uncertain. We report long‐term immune changes in VenR‐ and bendamustine‐R (BR)‐treated patients with relapsed/refractory CLL in the MURANO trial (NCT02005471). Patients were randomized to fixed‐duration VenR (2 years Ven; VenR for the first 6 months) or BR (6 months). Immune cell levels were evaluated at the end of combination treatment (EOCT), end of treatment (EOT; VenR arm only), and 12 and 24 months post‐EOCT. Overall, 130/194 VenR‐ and 134/195 BR‐treated patients completed treatment without progressive disease. In patients who completed VenR combination therapy, median immunoglobulin (Ig)G and IgM levels decreased from baseline to EOT (p ≤ 0.01 and p ≤ 0.0001, respectively); by 24 months, post‐EOT IgG had returned to baseline level and IgM had increased from baseline (p ≤ 0.001). Median IgA levels increased from baseline to 12 (p ≤ 0.0001) and 24 months post‐EOT (p ≤ 0.0001). In BR‐treated patients, changes in IgG, IgA, and IgM levels across the assessed time points were not significant, and by 24 months, post‐EOCT IgG, IgA, and IgM were above baseline levels. Grade ≥3 infection rates on treatment were low. Overall, immune recovery was observed with VenR and BR, with stabilization of Ig levels after treatment. Post‐treatment infection rates were generally low, making these very tolerable therapies for CLL.

INTRODUCTION

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is characterized by acquired and progressive immune dysfunction that often leads to hypogammaglobulinemia and subsequent frequent infections. 1 , 2 In patients with CLL, infections are one of the main causes of morbidity and mortality and are responsible for up to 25%–50% of deaths. 3 Hypogammaglobulinemia is one of the most commonly recognized immune deficiencies associated with CLL and affects up to 85% of patients during the course of their disease. 3 Treatment of CLL with anti‐CD20 antibodies, such as rituximab (R), is a known risk factor for the development of hypogammaglobulinemia and, in turn, increased risk of infections. 4 The exacerbation of CLL‐associated immune dysfunction by anti‐CD20 antibodies is therefore a fundamental limitation of this therapy type, highlighting the need for alternative treatments which avoid further immune dysfunction and/or work to restore immune activity in patients with CLL.

T‐cell dysfunction is associated with progressive disease 5 ; progressive accumulation and exhaustion of T cells are observed in patients with advanced CLL. 6 A preclinical (in vitro and in vivo) study demonstrated that CLL‐induced skewing of T cells toward a dysfunctional short‐lived effector phenotype, resulting in impaired T‐cell memory formation, that could impact responses to viral infections by reducing the proliferative and killing capacity of T cells. 7 Purine analogs, such as fludarabine, interfere with DNA synthesis causing a depletion of T cells (specifically CD4+) and deficiencies in cell‐mediated immunity. 8 The alkylating agent bendamustine in combination with R (BR) is a commonly used treatment for CLL, although little is known about the long‐term impact of BR on the immune profile. A retrospective analysis of immune reconstitution in patients with CLL demonstrated that BR therapy was associated with delayed recovery of CD4+, and late recovery was associated with a higher risk of Grade ≥3 infections. 9

Additional treatment options for CLL include B‐cell lymphoma‐2 (BCL‐2) inhibitors such as venetoclax (Ven), which can induce tumor cell apoptosis; preclinical in vitro and in vivo studies have shown that BCL‐2 inhibition is not detrimental to the survival or activation of T‐cell or natural killer (NK)‐cell subsets. 10 , 11 , 12 Similarly, a clinical study of Ven in women with systemic lupus erythematosus indicated that, while there were dose‐dependent reductions in B cells, there were no marked changes in neutrophils, NK cells, hemoglobin, or platelets. 13 A study that profiled peripheral blood cells from patients with CLL and acute myeloid leukemia found no impact on the concentration of T cells before versus after Ven treatment. 14 However, another study found that Ven‐based treatment led to deep responses in most patients, decreased absolute numbers of B, T, and NK cells, and restored NK‐cell function. 15 Notably, this study assessed expression of PD‐1 by CD8+ T cells and reported significantly higher expression in lymph nodes versus peripheral blood, 15 suggesting that studies assessing peripheral blood alone will likely report lower numbers for T cells. In a study of healthy volunteers, B cells recovered to normal levels within an average of 48 days after treatment with 200 mg of Ven and 59 days after treatment with 400 mg of Ven (following a reduction in B‐cell levels of 24% and 38%, respectively). 16 Taken together, these results indicate that immune recovery can occur following treatment with Ven, suggesting that Ven may not lead to the increased risk of infections associated with other CLL treatments.

The Phase 3 MURANO trial (NCT02005471) evaluated the safety and efficacy of fixed‐duration Ven in combination with R (VenR), compared with BR in patients with relapsed or refractory (R/R) CLL. 17 At the 5‐year update of MURANO, survival outcomes remained superior in VenR‐treated patients versus patients who received BR: median progression‐free survival was 53.6 versus 17.0 months (p < 0.0001), respectively, and 5‐year overall survival rates were 82.1% versus 62.2% (p < 0.0001), respectively. 18 Further, the rate of Grade 3 or 4 infections and infestations was numerically lower with VenR compared with BR (17.5% vs. 21.8%, respectively). 17

Though the results of the MURANO trial resulted in the approval of VenR for fixed‐duration treatment of patients with R/R CLL, little has been reported on the long‐term impact of this regimen on immune recovery. Here, we report, for the first time, long‐term immune changes post‐treatment with VenR in patients with R/R CLL from the MURANO trial. Data from the BR arm were also analyzed as a comparator.

METHODS

Study design and conduct

MURANO is a Phase 3, global, open‐label, randomized trial; the full study design, including eligibility criteria, dosing, prophylactic measures, and monitoring, has been published previously. 17 Briefly, patients with R/R CLL were randomized 1:1 to VenR (Ven 400 mg daily for 2 years plus monthly R for the first 6 months) or 6 months of BR. Treatment with Ven continued until disease progression (PD) or a maximum of 2 years from the start of combination therapy, whichever occurred first. A study schema is available in Supporting Information S1: Figure S1. The clinical cutoff date for the current analysis was May 8, 2020.

The trial was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the International Conference on Harmonization of Good Clinical Practice guidelines; the trial protocol was approved by the ethics committee at each participating institution and written informed consent was received from all patients before any trial‐related procedures had commenced.

Assessments

Peripheral blood minimal residual disease (MRD), immunoglobulin (Ig)G, IgA, and IgM levels, and B‐, T‐, and NK‐cell counts were determined; this occurred at baseline, on Day 1 of Cycle 4, at the end of combination treatment (EOCT) visit, and then every 3 months for 3 years (including at the end of treatment [EOT] with Ven monotherapy), followed by every 6 months until PD. Patients were excluded from the Ig analysis if there was no matching baseline Ig sample available, or if they received Ig replacement therapy (involving re‐infusion of IgG). Given the increased risk of hypogammaglobulinemia associated with R, Ig level assessment was conducted for two subgroups in the VenR arm: (i) all patients who completed VenR combination therapy (to observe the effect of VenR) and (ii) all patients who completed the full 2‐year VenR regimen (to observe the effect of Ven monotherapy, as R washout will have occurred during the monotherapy phase).

MRD was measured centrally and analyzed by allele‐specific oligonucleotide polymerase chain reaction and/or flow cytometry; the threshold for undetectable (u)MRD was <10−4. For the current analyses, patients were classified by their MRD status at EOCT and EOT (for the BR arm, EOCT and EOT are the same time point) so that any difference in immune cell levels or reconstitution based on MRD response could be evaluated. Ig levels were analyzed locally, and B, T, and NK cells were analyzed centrally by flow cytometry with markers of CD3, CD4, CD8, CD16, CD19, and CD56.

Adverse events were reported up to 28 days following the last dose of Ven or up to 90 days following the last dose of R, whichever was longer. However, serious adverse events that were considered related to treatment were reported indefinitely. Further details of disease status assessment and safety monitoring have been described previously. 17 , 18

Only patients who completed their full treatment course without PD were included in the analysis.

Statistical analysis

In this post hoc analysis among evaluable patients, statistics are descriptive only. Changes in Ig levels from baseline to EOCT, EOT, and at 12 months and 24 months post‐EOT were compared using a paired, nonparametric Wilcoxon test; Ig levels and B‐, T‐, and NK‐cell counts between patients with uMRD and MRD were compared using an unpaired, nonparametric Mann–Whitney test. Ig levels between all patients and patients with Grade ≥3 infections were also compared using an unpaired, nonparametric Mann–Whitney test.

RESULTS

Patient population

Of 389 patients enrolled in MURANO, 194 were randomized to the VenR treatment arm and 195 to the BR treatment arm. Of the 194 patients who received VenR, 176 completed the combination therapy without PD, and 130 completed 2 years of VenR without PD. Of the 195 patients who were assigned to receive BR, 154 completed all 6 months of treatment, 134 of whom did not have PD at EOT.

In the VenR arm, 40/176 patients who completed combination therapy and 25/130 patients who completed 2 years of therapy without PD received Ig replacement therapy (involving re‐infusion of IgG; this was neither recommended nor restricted in the protocol) during the study and were excluded from the IgG analysis. Similarly, in the BR arm, 19/134 patients who completed combination therapy had Ig replacement therapy and were excluded from the IgG analysis. Baseline demographics and disease characteristics of the MURANO study population have previously been published. 17

Serum Ig in VenR‐ and BR‐treated patients

Among patients with known MRD status at EOCT in the VenR arm, all Ig levels had recovered to or above baseline levels by 24 months post‐EOT (Figure 1A). The addition of R to Ven for 6 months did not appear to impact IgG and IgA levels (median levels at EOCT vs. baseline: IgG, 6.11 vs. 6.30 g/L; IgA, 0.46 vs. 0.46 g/L) but may have impacted IgM levels (median IgM levels at EOCT vs. baseline: 0.18 vs. 0.25 g/L [p ≤ 0.0001]). The median IgG level initially decreased significantly from 6.30 g/L at baseline to 5.80 g/L at EOT (p ≤ 0.01) but recovered to near baseline level by 24 months post‐EOT (6.20 g/L). There was a significant increase in the median IgA level from 0.46 g/L at baseline to 0.60 g/L at 12 months post‐EOT, and to 0.85 g/L at 24 months post‐EOT (both time points: p ≤ 0.0001 vs. baseline). The median IgM level decreased from baseline to EOT, though had recovered significantly by 24 months post‐EOT (0.41 vs. 0.25 g/L at baseline; p ≤ 0.001). Overall, Ig levels were similar among patients with uMRD and MRD+ at EOCT, although median IgM levels at 24 months post‐EOT were higher in patients with uMRD versus patients with MRD+ (0.47 vs. 0.24 g/L, respectively; p ≤ 0.01; Figure 2A).

In patients who completed the full 2‐year VenR regimen, the median level of IgG decreased from 6.40 g/L at baseline to 6.00 g/L at EOT (p ≤ 0.01; Figure 1B). At 12 and 24 months post‐EOT, IgG was not significantly different from baseline. The median IgA level had significantly increased from 0.45 g/L at baseline to 0.59 g/L at 12 months post‐EOT (p ≤ 0.01), and to 0.77 g/L at 24 months post‐EOT (p ≤ 0.0001). The median IgM level decreased from 0.25 g/L at baseline to 0.18 g/L at EOT (p ≤ 0.0001) but had recovered by 12 months post‐EOT, and by 24 months post‐EOT had significantly increased to 0.38 g/L (p ≤ 0.01 vs. baseline). There was no significant difference in median Ig levels between patients with uMRD versus MRD+ at EOT, with the exception of IgG at 24 months post‐EOT, which was lower in the uMRD group (6.15 vs. 7.91 g/L, respectively; p ≤ 0.05; Figure 2B).

In patients treated with BR, there were no significant changes from baseline in IgG, IgA, and IgM levels across the assessed time points, but all Ig levels were above baseline levels at 12 and 24 months post‐EOCT (though these increases were minimal; Figure 3A). In addition, MRD status at EOCT (uMRD vs. MRD+) did not have any significant impact on IgG, IgA, or IgM levels at any time point (Figure 3B).

Findings from paired data (baseline and EOCT or baseline and EOT) corroborate the findings for VenR from all patients, indicating a significant difference in IgM levels at baseline versus EOCT (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2A) and a significant difference in IgG and IgM levels at baseline versus EOT (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2B). However, the paired tests for BR (Supporting Information S1: Figure S2C) show a significant difference in IgM levels at baseline versus EOCT which is not observed with BR in all patients (Figure 3A).

B‐, T‐, and NK‐cell levels among VenR‐ and BR‐treated patients

B‐, T‐, and NK‐cell levels over time among VenR‐treated patients are presented in Figure 4A. CD19+ B‐cell depletion was observed on VenR treatment, with B‐cell recovery first evident around 1‐year post‐EOCT and stabilization by approximately 30 months post‐EOCT. Of note, B‐cell depletion on treatment was more pronounced in patients with uMRD at EOT, but the observed recovery and stabilization were consistent regardless of EOT MRD status (data not shown). Given the differences in disease burden at EOT, comparable CD19+ B‐cell recovery in patients with uMRD and MRD+ is likely to be representative of a larger relative proportion of normal B cells versus CLL cells; further analyses are required to confirm this supposition.

For VenR‐treated patients, the median (range) CD4+/CD8+ ratio at baseline was 1.10 (0.33–5.77) and was maintained throughout the study. At baseline, median CD3+ T‐cell counts (including CD3+ CD4+ T cells and CD3+ CD8+ T cells) were higher (1991/µL; range: 135–17,334) than reference values for healthy individuals, as previously described (700–2100/µL). 11 However, cell counts decreased to within the normal range during combination treatment (Cycle 4, Day 1), and remained within the reference range post‐EOT; a similar pattern was observed for NK‐cell counts. CD19+ B‐cells were depleted on treatment with BR but had stabilized by 12 months post‐EOCT. The median CD3+ CD4+ T‐cell counts reduced to below normal range on treatment and then returned to normal range after 2 years post‐EOCT (follow‐up month [FUM] 27, Figure 4B). CD3+ CD8+ T‐cell counts and CD56+ CD16+ NK‐cell counts decreased to within the normal range during BR treatment (Cycle 4, Day 1), and remained within the reference range post‐EOCT (Figure 4B). At 48 months post‐EOCT, there was no significant difference in CD19+ B‐cell, CD3+ CD4+ T‐cell, CD3+ CD8+ T‐cell, and CD56+ CD16+ NK‐cell counts between patients with uMRD or MRD at EOCT (Figure 5B).

Among patients with MRD at the end of BR treatment, those with high CD4 counts (>200/mm3, n = 25) had inferior PFS compared with those with low CD4 counts (≤200/mm3, n = 53; HR: 2.01, 95% CI: 1.22–3.31; Supporting Information S1: Figure S3); median PFS post‐EOT was 15.7 months (95% CI: 12.7–21.6) for the CD4 high group and 24.2 months (95% CI: 18.7–29.5) for the CD4 low group. For VenR‐treated patients who were MRD+ at EOT, we did not observe a statistically significant difference in PFS between CD4 high (n = 30) and CD4 low (n = 4) groups (HR: 2.12, 95% CI: 0.50–8.97; Supporting Information S1: Figure S4). For VenR‐ and BR‐treated patients who achieved uMRD at EOT, PFS differences were not observed between patients who were CD4 high and those who were CD4 low (Supporting Information S1: Figure S3 and S4).

Rate of infections among VenR‐ and BR‐treated patients

The overall rate of infections while on treatment was 12.5% (n = 12/96) among patients treated with VenR, with a lower Grade ≥3 infection rate observed in patients who were MRD+ versus those who were uMRD at EOT (6.7% [n = 2/30] vs. 15.2% [n = 10/66], respectively). Grade ≥3 infections were more frequently observed during combination treatment (10.3% [n = 10/97]) than post‐combination treatment (5.2% [n = 5/97]) and only occurred in patients who had uMRD at EOCT. For patients in the BR arm, the rate of Grade ≥3 infections occurring on treatment was 9.7% (n = 9/93) and was lower in those with uMRD (4.3% [n = 1/23]) versus MRD+ (11.4% [n = 8/70]) at EOCT. Following BR treatment, the rate of Grade ≥3 infections was 5.4% (n = 5/93) and was similar in patients with uMRD (4.3% [n = 1/23]) versus MRD+ (5.7% [n = 4/70]) at EOCT; however, it should be noted that follow‐up time for monitoring adverse events was 90 days for BR versus 28 days for patients in the VenR arm who received Ven monotherapy after combination therapy. Across both VenR and BR arms, pneumonia was the most common Grade ≥3 infection. Full details of Grade ≥3 infections among VenR‐ and BR‐treated patients by MRD status at EOCT are available in Supporting Information S1: Table S1.

For patients treated with VenR who experienced Grade ≥3 infections, there were no significant differences in median IgG, IgA, and IgM levels at EOCT compared with patients who did not experience Grade ≥3 infections (Figure 6A). In contrast, there were significant increases in median IgG (p < 0.05) and IgM (p < 0.05) levels at EOCT in patients treated with BR who experienced Grade ≥3 infections, compared with those who did not experience Grade ≥3 infections (Figure 6B).

The overall rate of Grade 1–2 infections while on treatment was 75.0%, (n = 72/96) among patients treated with VenR, with higher rates of Grade 1–2 infections in patients who were MRD+ versus those who were uMRD (90.0%, [n = 27/30] vs. 68.2% [n = 45/66], respectively) at EOT. Grade 1–2 infections were more frequently observed during combination treatment (59.2% [n = 74/125]) than post‐combination treatment (42.4% [n = 53/125]). For patients in the BR arm, the rate of Grade 1–2 infections occurring while on treatment was 40.9% (n = 38/93) and was lower in those with uMRD (34.8%, [n = 8/23] versus MRD+ (42.9%, [n = 30/70]) at EOCT. Full details of Grade 1–2 infections among VenR‐ and BR‐treated patients by MRD status at EOCT are available in Supporting Information S1: Table S2. There were no significant differences in levels of IgG, IgA, and IgM in patients who had a Grade 1–2 infection versus patients who did not have an infection, across both VenR and BR arms (data not shown).

DISCUSSION

In this post hoc exploratory analysis of the Phase 3 MURANO study, immune recovery was observed in patients with R/R CLL following fixed‐duration treatment with VenR or BR. MRD status did not have an impact on Ig recovery in either treatment group. B‐cell depletion was observed with VenR and BR. B‐cell recovery was first evident around 1‐year post‐EOCT for VenR, with stabilization by approximately 30 months post‐EOCT, while BR B‐cell recovery had stabilized by 12 months post‐EOCT. T‐cell and NK‐cell counts, though moderately elevated at baseline, normalized with VenR treatment and remained within normal ranges 19 both during and after treatment. Similarly, CD3+ CD8+ T‐cell and NK‐cell counts decreased to within normal ranges 19 during BR‐treatment and remained within the reference range post‐EOCT. The overall incidence of Grade ≥3 infections was low; however, the rate was numerically higher on VenR treatment in patients who achieved uMRD by EOCT compared with those who were MRD+. The reason for this difference in infection risk according to MRD status is not yet known and requires further investigation. Surprisingly, in the BR arm, median IgG and IgM levels were significantly higher at EOCT in patients with Grade ≥3 infections versus those without (both p < 0.05), though the reason for this is unclear.

Data regarding the long‐term impact of novel treatment regimens on immune recovery in patients with CLL are limited. In the Phase 2 CAPTIVATE trial (NCT02910583), B‐cell counts progressively recovered to normal after completion of Ven, 20 as observed in our study. In patients receiving IbrVen in the Phase 3 GLOW trial (NCT03462719), NK cells increased during ibrutinib lead‐in but decreased following Ven initiation and were maintained within the normal range throughout treatment. 20 This is consistent with the changes in NK cell count during VenR combination treatment and Ven monotherapy in our study.

Similar to the findings in our study, serum IgA levels increased after 6 months of acalabrutinib and ibrutinib treatment in two independent Phase 2 clinical trials and were sustained for more than 2 years with acalabrutinib and for up to 5 years with ibrutinib. 21 During long‐term ibrutinib treatment, serum IgG levels decreased slightly, but the authors concluded that this was unlikely to be of clinical significance. 21 In contrast, we observed that IgG levels recovered to baseline level by 24 months post‐EOT with VenR and BR. In addition, patients who achieved a ≥50% increase in serum IgA levels from baseline after 12 months of acalabrutinib and ibrutinib treatment had a decreased risk of infection compared with those achieving <50% IgA increase (risk ratio 0.67, p = 0.031). 21 The risk of infection was higher in patients with R/R disease receiving acalabrutinib or ibrutinib compared with patients receiving either drug as first‐line therapy (risk ratio 1.49, p = 0.03). 21 Our study was composed of patients with R/R CLL (all of whom received chemoimmunotherapy) and yet the overall rate of Grade ≥3 infections was low, demonstrating the favorable tolerability profile of VenR.

In both acalabrutinib and ibrutinib treatment cohorts, peripheral blood T‐cell counts were elevated above reference ranges at baseline and remained unchanged during the first 3 months of treatment but normalized after 6 months of treatment. 21 This is consistent with the changes in T‐cell count observed with all treatments in the CAPTIVATE trial, 20 IbrVen in the GLOW trial, 16 and with what we observed following VenR treatment. Although it may be suggestive of similar class effects of these drugs, the alternative explanation is that this T‐cell modulation reflects effective control of the CLL itself, but further studies in larger patient populations would be required to confirm this. Therefore, normalization of T‐cell counts observed with VenR in our study may offer an advantage versus BR treatment, as bendamustine is associated with an increased risk of lymphopenia and associated infections. 22 Results of our trial suggest that as patients treated with VenR had normal levels 11 of T and NK cells during and after treatment, these risks are reduced.

A recent study suggested that high CD4 counts (>200/mm3) were associated with inferior PFS in patients who remained MRD+ following FCR treatment, but not in patients who achieved uMRD following FCR treatment. 23 In MURANO, we observed a similar trend in patients treated with BR, but not in patients treated with VenR. However, given the low number of patients treated with VenR who were MRD+ and CD4 low (n = 4), this result would need to be interpreted with caution.

The development of hypogammaglobulinemia in CLL leads to increased susceptibility to infections, 2 with a direct correlation reported between low levels of IgG and IgA and increased morbidity and mortality from common bacterial infections. 6 In a large cohort study of patients with CLL (N = 706), patients with hypogammaglobulinemia and a history of major infections had significantly lower Ig levels than patients without a history of major infections (IgG p < 0.0001, IgA p < 0.0001, and IgM p = 0.0018). 24 For patients with CLL at high risk of major infections, characterized by aggressive disease requiring chemoimmunotherapy and combined antibody deficiency, Ig replacement therapy significantly decreased the incidence of major infections. 24 Thus, the combined use of Ig replacement therapy with alternative treatments such as Ven, which avoid immune dysfunction, may reduce the overall risk of infections and morbidity in patients with CLL.

A decrease in infections over time was seen in the CAPTIVATE trial, 20 similar to the MURANO trial; however, a trend toward lower infection rates in patients who achieved uMRD versus those who did not 20 contrasts with the findings from our study where patients achieving uMRD have a higher rate of Grade ≥3 infections occurring on treatment versus MRD+. A pooled analysis of data from the MURANO phase 3 study and a phase 1b (M13‐365) study that assessed the exposure–response relationships of VenR in R/R patients, indicated that there were no significant associations between steady state levels, drug exposure, and grade ≥3 neutropenia or infection rates. 25 Another study, based on data from the phase 3 CLL14 study and the phase 1b dose‐finding GP28331 study, that investigated the pharmacokinetics and exposure–response parameters of Ven with obinutuzumab for first‐line treatment of CLL, observed no significant correlations between venetoclax exposure and the probability of treatment‐emergent grade ≥3 neutropenia, grade ≥3 thrombocytopenia, grade ≥3 infections. and serious adverse events. 26 Therefore, we can conclude that the higher rate of infections in uMRD patients (versus MRD+ patients) is not linked to venetoclax pharmacokinetics.

There are several limitations of this study. This is a post hoc exploratory analysis of the phase 3 MURANO study; however, the trial was not specifically designed to evaluate the long‐term impact of VenR and BR on immune recovery. As such, data on all potentially relevant cell types were not collected, and some assumptions have been made (e.g., we assume that the CD19+ were polyclonal, since there were no clinical signs of progression, but we cannot confirm whether there was a proportion of clonal CD5+ B cells or not). Furthermore, information on vaccinations (e.g., influenza or pneumococcus) pre‐recruitment is not known and this could have an impact on the rates of certain adverse events.

CONCLUSION

In summary, this is the first report, to our knowledge, of the assessment of long‐term immune recovery following VenR treatment in patients with R/R CLL. We observed stabilization of Ig levels, irrespective of MRD status, and normalization of T cells during and after VenR therapy, which is consistent with the limited published data available for other agents in this setting. The overall rate of Grade ≥3 infections was low with VenR, indicating the potential for better tolerability compared with other CLL therapies, though additional studies are needed to investigate this fully. Analyses are underway to determine the correlation between infection risk and MRD status in CLL.
