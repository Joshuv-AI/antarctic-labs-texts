Prior Dual Antiplatelet Therapy and Mortality After Intracerebral Hemorrhage: A Danish Nationwide Cohort Study
Authors: Christian Ovesen, Luis Alberto García Rodríguez, Jesper Hallas, Camilla Storm, Stine Munk Hald, Sören Möller, Magdy Selim, David Gaist
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited data support the link between dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) and adverse outcome among patients with intracerebral hemorrhage. This study aimed to investigate the association between prior use of DAPT and mortality after intracerebral hemorrhage.MethodsPatients with first‐ever intracerebral hemorrhage on current APT at the time of admission, were identified and followed 180 days after admission through the Danish nationwide registries. Prior exposure to DAPT or single APT (SAPT) was established through the Danish National Prescription Registry. Inverse probability of treatment weighting was used to control for confounding.ResultsA total of 5800 patients were on antiplatelet therapy. 5535 (95.4%) were on SAPT, and 265 (4.6%) were on DAPT. Compared with patients on SAPT, patients on DAPT more commonly had a history of ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (134 [50.6%] versus 2393 [43.2%]), ischemic heart disease (172 [64.9%] versus 1561 [28.2%]), congestive heart failure (57 [21.5%] versus 505 [9.1%]) or chronic kidney disease (25 [9.4%] versus 205 [3.7%]). The 180‐day cumulative mortality rate was 52.6% for patients on DAPT and 45.6% for patients on SAPT (P=0.004). After weighting and compared with SAPT, prior use of DAPT was associated with a 43% higher mortality risk at day 1 (weighted risk ratio 1.43, 95% CI, 1.13–1.82). Higher early mortality among patients on DAPT was found to be the driving force behind the higher day 180 mortality in this group.ConclusionsAmong patients with intracerebral hemorrhage, prior DAPT was found to be associated with a higher mortality compared with SAPT.Keywords: antiplatelet therapy, cerebral hemorrhage, stroke/epidemiologySubject Categories: Intracranial Hemorrhage, Cerebrovascular Disease/Stroke

Clinical Perspective

What Is New?

This nationwide cohort study provides evidence that dual antiplatelet therapy before intracerebral hemorrhage is associated with more severe neurological deficits on admission and increased mortality as compared with prior single antiplatelet therapy.

The association between prior dual antiplatelet therapy and increased mortality among patients with intracerebral hemorrhage was primarily driven by an increased mortality early after admission indicating a potential link to early hematoma expansion.

What Are the Clinical Implications?

Medical professionals providing care for patients with intracerebral hemorrhage should be aware that prior dual antiplatelet therapy is associated with an increased risk of poor outcome exceeding the risk of poor outcome associated with prior single antiplatelet therapy.

Medical professionals providing care for patients with intracerebral hemorrhage should be aware that prior dual antiplatelet therapy is associated with an increased risk of poor outcome exceeding the risk of poor outcome associated with prior single antiplatelet therapy.

Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) is the cornerstone treatment for a wide range of cardio‐ and cerebrovascular conditions. With the publication of several landmark clinical trials demonstrating the superiority of DAPT versus single antiplatelet therapy (SAPT) in preventing recurrent cardiovascular ischemic events, the clinical uptake of DAPT regimens has increased. 1 , 2 , 3 Safety data from clinical trials have, however, shown a greater risk of intracranial hemorrhage among patients treated with DAPT. 4 Consequently, with a higher uptake of DAPT regimens and a higher incidence of intracerebral hemorrhage (ICH) among DAPT users, an increasing number of patients with ICH with prior use of DAPT can be expected.

Current population‐based studies indicate that approximately 30% of patients with ICH are on APT at the time of ICH onset. 5 , 6 It is generally believed that prior use of APT in general and DAPT in particular can lead to worse functional outcome or to death, by increasing the propensity for larger hematoma volumes and expansion. Large cohort studies and meta‐analyses suggest that compared with antithrombotic‐naïve patients, prior use of SAPT is associated with a small‐to‐modest increase in the risk of poor functional outcome and death after ICH. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 Compared with SAPT, data on patients experiencing ICH while on DAPT remain scarce. Although limited data support a higher risk of death and morbidity among patients with ICH who were taking DAPT compared with antithrombotic‐naïve patients, 6 , 11 further data from large unselected populations are necessary to provide additional insight into the prognosis of patients who develop ICH while on DAPT.

The primary objective of this study was to investigate whether prior use of DAPT as compared with SAPT is associated with increased mortality after ICH as well as more severe neurological deficits on admission.

METHODS

Setting and Data Sources

In Denmark (total population 6 million), all residents are entitled to free services from the tax‐funded public health care system. The public health care system provides patients with a suspected stroke easy access to imaging and stroke unit admission. All residents have a unique civil registration number allowing unambiguous linkage of national and medical registries.

In Denmark, it is mandatory for all stroke units to report standardized data for patients diagnosed with ICH to the nationwide Danish Stroke Registry within 7 days of onset. 12 The Danish Stroke Registry contains information on time from symptom onset to admission, smoking status and severity of stroke on admission (assessed using the Scandinavian Stroke Scale [SSS]) from 2003 and onwards. 12 In addition to the Danish Stroke Registry, 3 other sources were used in this study: the Danish National Patient Registry, 13 the Danish National Prescription Registry, 14 and the Civil Registration System. 15 The Patient Registry holds information on all secondary care diagnoses, covering inpatient contacts since 1977 and outpatient contacts since 1994. Diagnoses from primary care (general practitioners) are not covered. The Prescription Registry contains information on all prescriptions redeemed at community pharmacies in Denmark from 1995 and onwards. The Civil Registration System holds data on migration history and vital status (including date of death) of all residents being born or living in Denmark from 1968 and onwards.

In accordance with Danish law regarding register‐based research, the study was approved by the Region of Southern Denmark, and informed consent was waived. 16 This study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guideline. To minimize the possibility of unintentionally sharing information that can be used to reidentify private information, Danish law prohibits individual patient data from registries being made publicly available.

Study Population

The study population constituted all patients ≥50 years of age registered in the Danish Stroke Registry with admissions for first‐ever ICH from January 1, 2005, until August 31, 2022, who were on current antiplatelet medication at the time of admission (index date). The age limit was selected to enhance the likelihood of spontaneous ICH because secondary causes of ICH tend to be more common in younger patients. History of previous ICH was obtained from the Danish Stroke Registry and from the Patient Registry.

Follow‐Up and Outcome

Patients were followed from the date of their index admission until a maximum of 180 days had elapsed or until their death, whichever occurred first. The primary outcome was cumulated all‐cause mortality assessed up to (and including) day 1, day 30 or day 180 after the index date. The date of death was established through the Civil Registration System. In addition, admission SSS score obtained from the Danish Stroke Registry was analyzed as a secondary outcome due to its potential mediating effect between DAPT and mortality.

Exposure to Antiplatelet Therapy

APT use was defined as treatment with aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor, prasugrel, or dipyridamole (including the combination of dipyridamole and aspirin) (Table S1). As in previous work, 5 , 17 exposure status to APT was established based on information from the Prescription Registry. Current use was defined as a prescription supply of an antiplatelet agent covering the index date (full detail in Supplementary Methods 1). Patients were classified as being on DAPT if they were current users of 2 antiplatelets and as being on SAPT if they were current users of 1 antiplatelet. Dual use of combined dipyridamole and aspirin was considered SAPT (in a sensitivity analysis dual use of dipyridamole and aspirin was reclassified as DAPT).

Potential Confounders

Data from the Patient Registry and the Prescription Registry were used to classify each patient’s medical history and use of concomitant medications before the index date (see Supplementary Methods 1 and Table S1).

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive analyses of categorical data are presented using counts and proportions, and continuous/ordinal data using either mean±SD or median and interquartile range as appropriate. The cumulative all‐cause mortality in the first 180 days of follow‐up was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier estimator stratified by prior use of DAPT or SAPT.

The percentage of missing data is displayed in Table S3. Before stabilized inverse probability of treatment weight (IPTW) estimation and implementation of the outcome models (as described subsequently), multiple imputations with chained equations were used to impute missing data under the missing at random assumption (full detail in Supplementary Methods 2). Ten imputed data sets were created.

For each mortality analysis (at day 1, day 30, and day 180 after index date), a generalized linear model with binomial response distribution was used to estimate risk ratio (RR) and absolute risk difference between patients on prior use of DAPT versus SAPT. Unweighted (crude) and weighted analyses (using stabilized IPTW) were conducted for each outcome. Stabilized IPTW was used to control for confounding by creating a weighted pseudo‐population balanced on baseline characteristics. 18 All variables listed in Table 1 were considered as potential confounding covariates and included in the propensity score model except SSS as it was considered a mediating factor in the causal pathway between exposure (antiplatelet therapy) and outcome (mortality). To investigate the mediating effect of SSS score, an exploratory analysis was performed, in which SSS score was included in the propensity score model. To account for the additional uncertainty arising from the estimation of the propensity score, main analyses were repeated using bootstrapping to estimate the SEs. Detailed methodology regarding IPTW estimation and the corresponding SEs is provided in Supplementary Methods 3. Standardized mean difference (for categorical variables as proposed by Austin et al. 18 ) was calculated using the first imputed data set, with a difference > 0.1 indicating covariate imbalance. All main analyses were repeated after restricting the comparator group of SAPT to aspirin alone or clopidogrel alone, respectively.

Potential effect modification by concurrent oral anticoagulant use, age (<75 versus ≥75 years), sex (men versus women), and duration of current use of antiplatelet treatment before date of symptom onset (<6 months versus ≥6 months) was investigated. For each subgroup, the propensity score was accordingly refitted, and the effect estimate calculated within each subset. 19 Evidence of effect modification by subgrouping variables was assessed by comparing effect estimates using a Wald test.

Patients were divided into 3 SSS severity categories based on the severity of their neurological deficits on admission (mild deficits: SSS score, 43–58; moderate deficits: SSS score, 26–42; and severe deficits: SSS score, 0–25). To investigate if the probability of being in the severe deficits category on admission was affected by the use of DAPT, a generalized linear model was fitted with admission deficits (dichotomized to severe versus mild/moderate deficits) as dependent variable and DAPT (versus SAPT) as independent variable. Unweighted (crude) and weighted analyses (using IPTW) were conducted. The distribution of SSS severity categories in the IPTW pseudo‐population was obtained from a multinomial logistic regression model. All analyses were conducted using Stata 18.0 (StataCorp, TX, USA).

RESULTS

A total of 5800 patients were identified as current users of APT upon ICH onset (Figure 1). Of these, 5535 (95.4%) were receiving SAPT, and 265 (4.6%) DAPT. Among patients on SAPT, 3362 (60.7%) were taking aspirin and 1208 (21.8%) clopidogrel (Table S2). Among patients on DAPT, 222 (83.8%) were taking a combination of aspirin and clopidogrel (Table S2). Baseline characteristics and missingness are presented in Table 1 and Table S3. Mean (SD) age was 75.7 (9.3) years among patients on DAPT and 77.3 (9.6) among patients on SAPT. Among patients on DAPT, 164 (61.9%) were men and among patients on SAPT, 2941 (53.1%) were men. Compared with patients on SAPT, patients on DAPT were more likely to have a history of ischemic stroke or transient ischemic attack (134 [50.6%] versus 2393 [43.2%], Table 1), ischemic heart disease (172 [64.9%] versus 1561 [28.2%]), congestive heart failure (57 [21.5%] versus 505 [9.1%]), or chronic kidney disease (25 [9.4%] versus 205 [3.7%]). Compared with SAPT, patients on DAPT were more often on concomitant antihypertensive therapy, statin therapy, proton pump inhibitor (Table 1). Thirty (11.3%) of the patients on DAPT were on concomitant therapy with oral anticoagulants compared with 541 (9.8%) of the patients on SAPT.

Mortality: DAPT Versus SAPT

At day 1, the cumulative mortality was 24.2% for patients on current DAPT and 15.3% for patients on SAPT (Figure 2 and Table 2). After IPTW, most standardized differences of baseline data were below or close to 10% indicating good balance (Table 1). After IPTW and compared with SAPT, prior use of DAPT was associated with 43% higher mortality at day 1 (IPTW RR [wRR], 1.43 [95% CI, 1.13–1.82]) (Table 2). At day 30 and day 180, the cumulative mortality was 47.2% for patients on current DAPT and 37.0% for patients on SAPT, and 52.5% for patients on current DAPT and 45.6% for patients on SAPT, respectively (Figure 2 and Table 2) An increased mortality of 29% was observed at day 30 (wRR, 1.29 [95% CI, 1.13–1.47]), and 14% at day 180 (wRR, 1.14[ 95% CI, 1.01–1.29]) (Table 2). When the additional uncertainty contained in the estimation of the propensity score was considered, evidence was found for a difference in mortality only between patients on SAPT and DAPT at day 1 and day 30 (Table S4). IPTW absolute risk differences are presented in Table S5. If only the incident mortality within each time‐period was considered (rather than the cumulated mortality), strong evidence was found for an association between prior use of DAPT and higher mortality within day 0 to 1 (crude RR, 1.58 [95% CI, 1.27–1.98]). No such evidence was found for the other time periods (day 2 to 30 and day 31 to 180) (Table S6 and Figure S1).

After IPTW, 4 variables (smoking status, previous venous thromboembolism, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and use of nonsteroid anti‐inflammatory drugs) displayed minor imbalances between patients on SAPT and on DAPT (Table 1). These were not regarded as substantial confounders but were adjusted for in a sensitivity analysis by adding these as independent covariates in the generalized linear model applied in the IPTW pseudo‐population. The adjustments did not affect the conclusion for mortality at day 1 and day 30 (Tables S7 and S8). The evidence for a higher mortality at day 180 was weakened slightly by the adjustments. To understand the impact of the baseline SSS score as a mediator of the effect of DAPT on mortality, an exploratory analysis was undertaken including SSS score in the propensity model. After the inclusion of the SSS score in the propensity model, the increased risk of mortality in the DAPT group weakened at all time points, but evidence remained for increased mortality associated with DAPT at day 1 and day 30 (Tables S9 and S10).

For the stratified subgroup analyses of mortality at day 1 and day 180, a similar trend with higher mortality in the DAPT group versus the SAPT group was, generally, observed across all the subgroups (Figures S2 and S3). Evidence of heterogeneity was observed in the association between current DAPT and day 1 mortality when stratified by duration of current use (P for heterogeneity = 0.001, Figure S2). No evidence of heterogeneity was observed in the association between current DAPT and day 1 mortality when stratified by concurrent anticoagulation therapy (P for heterogeneity = 0.17). Among patients without concurrent anticoagulation therapy, current DAPT was associated with a 40% increase in mortality at day 1 (wRR, 1.40 [95% CI, 1.07–1.84]). Among patients with concurrent anticoagulation therapy, there was no association between current DAPT and increased mortality at day 1 (wRR, 0.74 [95% CI, 0.30–1.79]). For mortality at day 180, strong evidence of heterogeneity was observed when stratifying by concurrent anticoagulation therapy (P for heterogeneity = 0.002, Figure S3). Among patients with concurrent anticoagulation use, current DAPT was associated with 58% increase in mortality at day‐180 (wRR, 1.58 [95% CI, 1.30–1.93]), whereas no evidence for an association was found in patients without concurrent anticoagulation therapy (wRR, 1.08 [95% CI, 0.94–1.24]).

Mortality: DAPT Versus Aspirin Alone or Clopidogrel Alone

The cumulative mortality until day 180 for patients on DAPT compared with aspirin alone or clopidogrel alone is displayed in Figure S4. Standardized mean differences before and after IPTW weighting are displayed in Figure S5. After IPTW and compared with aspirin alone, current DAPT was associated with a 60% higher mortality at day 1 (wRR, 1.60 [95% CI, 1.27–2.03]), 43% at day 30 (wRR, 1.43 [95% CI, 1.26–1.63]), and 23% at day 180 (wRR, 1.23 [95% CI, 1.09–1.38]) (Table S11). There was no evidence for higher mortality with the use of DAPT compared with clopidogrel alone after IPTW day 1 (wRR, 1.23 [95% CI, 0.95–1.61]), day 30 (wRR, 1.16 [95% CI, 0.99–1.35]), and day 180 (wRR, 1.10 [95% CI, 0.96–1.26]) (Table S11). IPTW absolute risk differences are presented in Table S12.

Admission Stroke Severity

The median [interquartile range] SSS score on admission was similar between patients on DAPT and SAPT (30.0 [6.0–49.0] versus 29.5 [9.0–47.0]). The distribution of the admission stroke severity before and after IPTW is presented in Figure S6. After IPTW and compared with SAPT, current DAPT was associated with a 15% higher likelihood of presenting with severe neurological deficits (SSS score, 0–25) (wRR, 1.15 [95% CI, 1.00–1.32]).

Sensitivity Analysis: Dual Use of Dipyridamole and Aspirin Reclassified as DAPT

In the following sensitivity analysis, dual use of dipyridamole and aspirin was reclassified as DAPT. After the reclassification 4774 (82.3%) patients were classified as receiving SAPT and 1026 (17.7%) as receiving DAPT. Standardized mean differences before and after IPTW are displayed in Figure S7. After IPTW and compared with SAPT alone, current DAPT was associated with a 20% higher mortality at day 1 (wRR, 1.20 [95% CI, 1.03–1.39]), 17% at day 30 (wRR, 1.17 [95% CI, 1.08–1.27]), and 13% at day 180 (wRR, 1.13 [95% CI, 1.05–1.21]) (Table S13). DISCUSSION

Among patients with ICH, prior exposure to DAPT was associated with a higher mortality when compared with prior exposure to SAPT alone. The elevated mortality was not contingent upon any specific subgroups or whether the combination of aspirin and dipyridamole was classified as DAPT or SAPT. The increased mortality risk associated with DAPT primarily manifested within the acute phase following admission, but the difference was sustained throughout the follow‐up period. Our analyses suggest that some of the effect of DAPT on mortality might be mediated through neurological status upon admission, but this effect could not explain the entire association between prior exposure to DAPT and increased mortality. The mortality associated with exposure to DAPT was especially pronounced when compared with aspirin alone but diminished when compared with clopidogrel alone. Furthermore, patients with prior exposure to DAPT were found to have a higher likelihood of presenting with severe neurological deficits upon admission compared with those with prior exposure to SAPT alone.

Previous studies have investigated the association between DAPT use and outcome after ICH. 6 , 11 , 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 Most studies included only smaller subgroups of patients on prior DAPT, and none of these smaller studies reported statistically significant increased hematoma volume, increased risk of hematoma expansion or higher mortality among patients with exposure to DAPT. 20 , 21 , 22 , 23 In a previous study based on the Danish registries (published in 2018), patients with ICH treated with a combination of aspirin and clopidogrel before admission were compared with patients treated with aspirin as monotherapy. 24 The study failed to establish an association between DAPT treatment and mortality, possibly due to low statistical power. 24 Our results are consistent with the results from the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association GWTG (Get With The Guidelines) registry reporting strong evidence for an association between prior use of DAPT and higher in‐hospital mortality. 6 , 11 The association between DAPT and higher in‐hospital mortality found in the GWTG registry was present when comparing prior DAPT use to antithrombotic‐naïve patients and to patients with SAPT use. 6 , 11 It is, however, noteworthy that data from the GWTG registry did not find evidence of an association between DAPT use and a higher risk of poor functional outcome at discharge. 6 , 11

In comparison to our cohort, a higher proportion of the patients on SAPT in the GWTG registry was on aspirin monotherapy (approximately 90% versus 60% in our study). 6 As prior SAPT was not associated with increased mortality in the GWTG registry (compared with antithrombotic‐naïve patients), it is plausible that the large proportion of patients on aspirin monotherapy could have contributed to an increased contrast between patients on SAPT and DAPT, thereby accentuating the worse prognosis of patients on DAPT. 6 This is in line with our study’s findings that also showed a pronounced difference in the relative mortality risk when DAPT use was compared with aspirin alone versus clopidogrel alone. Yet, it should be noted that this major imbalance between aspirin monotherapy and clopidogrel monotherapy opens the door for a potential treatment selection bias (eg, confounding‐by‐indication). The finding that DAPT results in more severe bleedings and an increased risk of mortality (presumably indicative of a greater bleeding propensity) also concurs with data from randomized clinical trials demonstrating a higher incidence of intracranial hemorrhage among patients on clopidogrel plus aspirin compared with aspirin alone. 4 To the best of our knowledge, there are no similar data (either from randomized or observational designs) comparing the incidence of intracranial hemorrhage of patients on clopidogrel plus aspirin compared with clopidogrel alone.

Our finding that DAPT is associated with a higher risk of severe neurological deficits upon admission could be explained by an association between prior use of DAPT and rapid early hematoma expansion resulting in larger admission hematomas. Previous studies have observed an association between the severity of neurological deficits on admission and the pace of hematoma expansion very early after symptom onset. 25 Larger final hematoma volumes, as a result of a more aggressive hematoma expansion, are likely also an important mediating factor in the association between prior DAPT use and higher mortality. Admission hematoma volumes and postadmission hematoma expansion have previously been shown to be important drivers of poor outcome among patients with ICH. 26 , 27 The pathophysiological link between hematoma expansion and prior exposure to DAPT is also supported by our observation that the increased mortality risk associated with DAPT primarily manifested within the first day following admission. This observation indicates a potential direct relation to the dynamics of the expanding hematoma early after symptom onset (eg, pace of hematoma expansion, intraventricular extension, and final hematoma volume).

The study has several strengths. It is based on nationwide automated administrative registries and a clinical stroke database that rely on mandatory, prospective collection of data. In addition, the Danish health care system provides free access to health care. These factors reduce the risk of selection bias caused by socioeconomic status, eliminate recall bias, and ensure a high degree of completeness in follow‐up. However, the study also has potential limitations. Despite the use of nationwide data, <5% of patients with ICH who received antiplatelets were on DAPT, with the vast majority being on SAPT. The decision of whether to treat a patient with DAPT versus SAPT is likely highly influenced by several factors (eg, severity and subtype of stroke), as exemplified by the marked differences in comorbidity of patients exposed to DAPT versus SAPT in our study. It is therefore likely that key factors in the decision chain leading to choice of DAPT/SAPT are not adequately captured by our adjustment process. Although we sought to mitigate this risk by including underlying conditions generally warranting the use of DAPT treatment in our propensity score models (eg, ischemic heart disease and cerebrovascular diseases), the risk of residual confounding remains a possibility. This may be even more pronounced for our subanalyses comparing DAPT with monotherapy using aspirin and clopidogrel, separately. Consequently, we interpret our findings concerning DAPT/clopidogrel versus DAPT/aspirin comparisons as hypothesis generating. As the antiplatelet exposure is based on prescriptions redeemed at community pharmacies, information on in‐hospital medication use, medication adherence, and persistence was lacking, as was information on prescribed doses. Over‐the‐counter use of low‐dose aspirin is not captured by the Prescription Registry. However, this source of misclassification is estimated to play a minor role, as ∼90% of all low‐dose aspirin use is prescribed in Denmark. 28 Patients suffering in‐hospital ICH related to APT (eg, in relation to invasive cardiovascular procedures) were also likely missed due to the lack of data on in‐hospital medication use. Furthermore, the lack of access to imaging data and data on functional outcomes in the Danish Stroke registry impaired our ability to offer a detailed analysis of which factors are likely mediating the relationship between DAPT use, ICH severity, and mortality. Lack of access to imaging data also impaired our ability to investigate any heterogeneity in the association between DAPT use and mortality by hematoma location or by underlying small vessel pathology (deep perforating vasculopathy or cerebral amyloid angiopathy). Finally, although it is documented that 82% of patients with an ICH diagnosis in the Danish Stroke Registry are spontaneous (ie, no trauma, or underlying structural cause such as neoplasm, or macrovascular abnormality), 29 the lack of radiological data impaired our ability to effectively exclude patients with underlying structural causes of ICH. The results must consequently be interpreted as the marginal risk increase associated with the use of DAPT across the entire population of patients with ICH (>50 years of age) regardless of hematoma location or underlying cause.

This study has potential important implications to consider. Even though the study does not have any bearing on the judicious use of DAPT in secondary prophylaxis after ischemic events, the study highlights the aggravated prognosis associated with DAPT treatment in those patients experiencing an ICH thereafter. Future studies will need to validate these findings and directly elucidate the mechanisms behind the increased mortality (increased hematoma volume or higher likelihood of postadmission hematoma expansion).

CONCLUSIONS

Prior exposure to DAPT was found to be associated with a higher mortality and a higher risk of severe neurological deficits upon admission compared with SAPT in patients experiencing ICH. The higher mortality primarily manifested within the acute phase after admission. Future research is warranted to ascertain the underlying mechanisms driving the association and elucidate whether the risk of mortality in patients receiving prior aspirin monotherapy differs from the mortality in patients receiving clopidogrel monotherapy.
