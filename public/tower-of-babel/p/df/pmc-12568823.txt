Opportunities for physical activity research in Puerto Rico: a review of the literature
Authors: Raúl D Gierbolini-Rivera, Alexandre A de Paula da Silva, Farah A Ramírez-Marrero
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPuerto Rico (PR) is the most physically inactive jurisdiction in the United States, with half of its population being inactive. Understanding the evidence on physical activity (PA) research in PR is crucial to advancing the field. This narrative review aimed to examine existing literature on PA in PR and evaluate the current research capacity in this area. This type of review was necessary due to the limited studies on PA in PR and the broad scope of the research question. Each study was assigned a phase (1 through 5) based on the Behavioral Epidemiology Framework (BEF) and the socio-ecological model for determinants/correlates. Following PRISMA guidelines, seven databases were systematically searched for English and Spanish articles, resulting in 26 eligible studies out of 797 records screened. The included studies focused on the following BEF phases, with the majority (16, 61.6%) concentrating on Phase 3, which involved identifying correlates, determinants, mediators, or moderators. Followed by Phase 4 (intervention evaluation), with seven studies (26.9%). Two studies (7.7%) were addressed in Phase 2 (developing/validating methods), and only one study (3.8%) focused on Phase 1 (establishing links between PA and health). No studies explored Phase 5 (translation into policy/practice). Overall, these patterns highlight a field that has identified who is inactive but has yet to develop mature measurement systems, strong causal evidence, or scalable solutions. We recommend forming transdisciplinary, diverse, and inclusive teams comprising researchers, policymakers, practitioners, and community members to enhance the evidence base and translate it into effective PA policies and practices.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12982-025-01041-3.Keywords: Exercise, Health promotion, Chronic disease, Sedentary behavior

Introduction

Over one-third of adults globally, or approximately 1.8 billion people, are physically inactive [1]. The global pandemic significantly impacts both public health and the global economy, as it contributes to a heightened risk of morbidity and mortality from major non-communicable diseases such as cardiovascular disease, diabetes, obesity, and cancer [2]. Physical inactivity accounts for an estimated 5 million deaths annually and over $53.8 billion in associated costs globally each year [3, 4]. Promoting physically active lifestyles provides significant health, social, and economic benefits for individuals of all ages, ethnicities, or socio-economic backgrounds [4–6].

Physical inactivity in Latin America remains a significant challenge, affecting approximately one-third of the adult population [7]. It contributes to 11.4% of all deaths in the region and places a heavy financial burden on healthcare systems, costing an estimated 1.3 billion dollars annually [4, 8, 9]. Puerto Rico (PR) faces a concerning challenge with physical inactivity, with approximately 49.4% of the population being physically inactive [10]. That makes PR the jurisdiction of the United States (US) with the highest prevalence of physical inactivity, followed by the state of Alabama, with a prevalence of 30.7% [10]. Latinos in the US are the fastest-growing ethnic group; however, they also have the highest prevalence of physical inactivity [10, 11].

PR’s unique position highlights two intertwined realities. On one hand, it is an unincorporated territory of the US, reflecting its political and economic ties. On the other hand, it shares many social and health inequities with Latin American and Caribbean nations, shaped by a shared history of colonialism and its enduring impacts [12–14]. This dual identity underscores the complexities of PR’s socio-political and health landscape [12–14]. Exploring strategies, interventions, policies, and investments to promote physical activity (PA) at a population level in PR can offer valuable insights for improving public health, not only within the island but also among other Latino populations in Latin America and the US [15–17]. Similar to the rest of the world and the Latin American region, being sedentary in PR is a risk factor for a multitude of other chronic diseases and shortened life expectancy [2, 8, 18, 19].

Evidence from the Global Observatory of Physical Activity demonstrates a strong connection between PA research productivity, regular national surveillance systems for PA, and the implementation of national PA promotion plans [20, 21]. Research productivity in PR is crucial in supporting effective PA policy and surveillance efforts aimed at health promotion [20, 21]. While previous efforts by the Global Observatory of Physical Activity to measure PA research in PR have offered valuable insights, there is a need for a more comprehensive understanding of the current evidence-based research on PA in PR [20, 22]. It is crucial to understand the research gaps and opportunities to build local research capacity, which could provide valuable perspectives for the future of the PA field in PR. Therefore, this review has two aims: (1) to examine the existing body of literature on PA research in PR and (2) to describe the current capacity and opportunities for PA research.

Methods

We conducted a narrative review using a systematic process and adhered to the PRISMA guidelines [23] to synthesize the research on PA in PR. There were two justifications for conducting a narrative review: (1) limited literature in PA and PR; and (2) the broad nature of the research questions.

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included in the narrative review according to the following criteria: (1) research on PA as an outcome, (2) either a partial or complete sample of participants from PR, and (3) only original research articles were included.

Databases and search strategy

To identify research on PA in PR, one of the members of the research team (RGR) used the following six electronic databases: PubMed-Medline, Scopus, Global Health (EBSCO), APA PsychInfo (EBSCO), SPORTDiscus (EBSCO), Scielo, and Web of Science. The search strategy for all the electronic databases followed the same structure and were conducted in English, except for SciELO, which was conducted in Spanish. Handsearching, a manual method of scanning and selecting journals for relevant articles that may have been missed during indexing in electronic datasets, was used to ensure that relevant articles were not overlooked [24]. Handsearching occurred in the following journals: “Revista Kairós-Gerontologia,” “Anales en Gerontologia,” “Pensar en Movimiento: Revista de Ciencias del Ejercicio y la Salud,” and “Revista Evaluar.” The following syntax combinations of keywords were always used: (1) Physical Activity AND Puerto Rico, (2) Exercise AND Puerto Rico, (3) Sport AND Puerto Rico, (4) Exercise AND Adolescents AND Puerto Rico, (5) Exercise AND Adults AND Puerto Rico, (6) Exercise AND Adults AND Puerto Rico, and (7) Exercise AND Chronic Disease AND Puerto Rico. The full search strategy was conducted on November 10, 2024 and is available in Appendix 1. One of the team members (RGR) searched for peer-reviewed articles with no time restriction. Article screening and data collection process

To ensure inter-rater reliability, two members of the research team (RGR & AAPS) reviewed each title and abstract to identify if they met the inclusion criteria of the review. Once both abstractors completed their reviews, they compared their entries, discussed any discrepancies, and reached a consensus. If the articles established PA as an outcome and had a sample of participants from PR, they were included for full-text screening. Articles that did not clearly specify their sample were included in the full-text screening to verify if they met the criteria for inclusion. Articles in the full-text screening stage that did not meet the inclusion criteria were excluded from further consideration (Fig. 1).

Behavioral Epidemiology Framework by Sallis et al. [25]

Operationalization of the behavioral epidemiology framework

This narrative review utilized the Behavioral Epidemiology Framework (BEF) as outlined by Sallis et al. (2000) to guide the systematic classification of the PA studies included in this review [25]. The BEF provides a structured, sequential approach for organizing and evaluating the evidence base to guide public health interventions (see Fig. 2), classifying studies into one of five phases: (1) Establish Links between the Behavior and Health; (2) Develop Methods for Measuring the Behavior; (3) Identify Factors that Influence the Behavior; (4) Evaluate Interventions to Change the Behavior; and (5) Translate Research into Practice.

Flowchart of Studies Included in the Review

The BEF offers two key advantages for this narrative review: (1) it enables a nuanced assessment of the evidence base for the research context of PR, and (2) it highlights under-represented research phases, patterns, and gaps, which could inform recommendations for future work [25]. To extend the applicability of the BEF, this narrative review incorporated the socio-ecological framework from Bauman et al. (2012) [26]. This approach synthesized the global evidence on PA correlates at multiple ecological levels (individual, interpersonal, environmental, policy, and global) and across PA domains (leisure, transport, occupation, and household) [26]. They distinguish between correlates (variables with documented associations) and determinants (variables supported by longitudinal or experimental evidence of causation) and emphasize the importance of identifying mediators and moderators [26]. Integrating these concepts into the BEF enabled a more granular classification of the studies in this review.

Each included study was reviewed in full text and coded into one primary BEF phase, based on the study’s aim, design, and measured outcomes. Phase 1 would consist of research linking PA behaviors to health outcomes. Phase 2 would include studies where the primary intent was to develop, validate, or refine tools to measure PA, sedentary behavior, or related concepts (exercise or physical fitness). Phase 3 would include studies where the primary goal was to identify variables associated with PA, sedentary behavior, or related outcomes (including correlates, determinants, moderators, or mediators). Phase 4 studies would consist primarily of interventions designed to evaluate strategies for modifying PA behavior. Finally, Phase 5 studies would include those that translate interventions into policy or large-scale practice contexts. When studies contained elements from multiple phases, classification reflected the primary aim or most important contribution, as justified in the “rationale” column.

To capture the socio-ecological framework, five additional coding dimensions were identified [26]. First, the “Factor Type” involved determining whether a study identified correlates, determinants, mediators, moderators, or none of these. Second, “Ecological Level” aligned with the levels of the socio-ecological model: individual, interpersonal, environmental, policy, and global [26, 27]. Third, the PA Domain was coded according to the following domains: leisure-time, occupational, transport, household, or mixed (if multiple domains were measured) [26]. Fourth, we noted whether the studies included an analysis of moderation or cross-level interactions. Fifth, we indicated whether the factor addressed fell into an under-researched category according to the socio-ecological model (e.g., genetic, evolutionary, climate change, brain mechanisms). The combinations of the two frameworks would allow the review to not only demonstrate the phase distribution of the PA research of PR, but also assess which ecological level, PA domain, and factor types are used, providing targeted insights to determine the PA research landscape [26].

Data extraction and synthesis

To summarize the evidence from the included articles, the following information was extracted: title/author/year of publication, study aim, study design, study population/sample, data collection tools, factor type, key findings, ecological level, PA domain, if an interaction was tested, if a novel factor was presented, if there was measurement development, if an intervention was tested, and if the intervention findings were translated or implemented into real-world practice. Finally, each study was assigned a phase (1 through 5) according to the BEF, along with a rationale. At the end of the data extraction process, the descriptive synthesis was prepared by two members (RGR and AAPS) of the research team, including descriptive characteristics (frequencies/percentages) of the BEF phases and a narrative synthesis of the study characteristics (e.g., PA domains, ecological levels, data collection methods, factor types). Results

Across the 26 studies (see Table 2), their aims ranged around (1) describing PA, sedentary time, and correlates across the life course; (2) testing culturally relevant or home-based interventions in clinical groups; and (3) developing and validating measures or exploring perceptions of PA in different contexts. The studies span two decades, from 2004 to 2024, with the majority published from the 2010s (N = 16) into the early 2020s (N = 7), followed by the 2000s (N = 3). Most of the studies were cross-sectional designs, ranging from different populations (e.g., preschoolers [30] and elementary school children [32]; adolescents [41, 45, 52]; university students/medical residents [34, 43]; End-Stage Renal Disease (ESRD) patients [42]; older adults [49, 51, 53]), with psychometric validation [50], and qualitative phenomenology (cancer survivors’ perspectives [44]; physical therapy students [28]; and older adults [49]).

Interventions included randomized controlled trials (RCTs), such as a Latin dance vs. aerobic exercise trial on participants with cardiovascular disease (CVD) [29]; the feasibility of home exercise vs. gym exercise trial [47], or the effectiveness of the Project Viva intervention on breast cancer survivors [31]; a culturally adapted home exercise intervention vs. a standard exercise intervention [38], a non-randomized HIIT study on Hispanic women living with HIV and Hispanic Women living without HIV [33], and a pre-experimental behavioral activation intervention on breast cancer survivors [37].

Objective PA measures mainly consisted of right hip-worn accelerometers, typically for seven consecutive days, 24 h a day, in preschoolers [30], ≥ 10 h/day in elementary school samples [32], seven-day wear in older adults [51], and among adults living with HIV [46]; Álvarez (2013) used accelerometers for six consecutive days in adolescent girls [52]; and Morales-Cruz (2017) used a seven-day pedometer to track daily step counts [37]. Subjective data-collection tools consisted mainly of the use of the IPAQ (short or long form) for medical residents [34], adolescents/parents [52], older adults [53], ESRD patients [42], HIV [46], and college students [43], the GLTEQ, which captured leisure-time PA in breast-cancer survivors [36, 40], and the V7-DPAR to identify adolescent PA patterns [41, 45]. Additional collection tools consisted of physical fitness tests (6MWT, SF-36, DASI) [29], treadmill test, 12-minute walk test, etc [47]; program login tracking for access to the “Cuidalos” program [35]; and the attitudes scale (AHEFD-11) [50].

Consistent patterns emerged by age: preschoolers accumulated sufficient LVPA/MVPA mainly on weekdays; however, there was substantial sedentary time during preschool hours, and the boys’ MVPA exceeded the girls’ MVPA [30]. In contrast, adolescents exhibited less PA, and few met the ≥ 60 min/day MVPA recommendations, with middle schoolers being more active than high schoolers, and boys were more active compared to girls [41, 45]. Female adolescents whose PA was measured objectively with accelerometers averaged 87 min MVPA per week, below the recommended guidelines [52]. Among elementary school children, neither PE time nor reported facility use was related to MVPA or ST during the school week [32]. University students with higher levels of stress were associated with lower PA levels [43]. Medical residents demonstrated low knowledge of the PA guidelines, and approximately 40% reported no leisure-time PA [34].

Older adults consistently reported low MVPA and high sitting time, with men engaging in more PA and women being more sedentary with higher sitting times [53]; objective monitoring in day centers (HOPE centers) confirmed low steps and MVPA, but older adults in the San Germán center showed better physical fitness compared to the Mayaguez center [51]. Qualitative work emphasized the meanings of PA for health, independence, and social connection, alongside barriers such as illness and fatigue [49]. Among clinical populations, ESRD patients were largely inactive, with household chores being the most common PA, and more years on hemodialysis being associated with lower total PA [42]. Among people with HIV, active individuals had a better body composition and life satisfaction [48]; however, a follow-up study found that self-report measures overestimated PA relative to objective measurements with accelerometers and pedometers [46].

In the parent-adolescent program “Cuidalos”, digital engagement barriers (e.g., limited computer access and difficulty re-accessing the program) surfaced [35]. Physical therapy students reported improvements in perceived health, well-being, and stress reduction during a 6-week PA intervention (EMA) [28]. In a cardiac rehabilitation intervention, tropical Latin dance was found to be comparable to traditional aerobic exercise in terms of improvements in functional capacity and quality of life [29]. Among breast cancer survivors, a pilot intervention of home exercise vs. gym exercise was found to be feasible, safe, and well-tolerated, with high adherence in both gym and home-based groups [47].

Behavioral activation, a structured psychosocial intervention that consisted of engaging in PA through walking (measured with pedometers over seven days), over a 12-week open clinical trial, reduced depression and anxiety symptoms while also increasing daily steps for breast cancer survivors. Interventions informed by the social cognitive theory enhanced exercise self-efficacy and PA levels [38]. Family support was associated with higher vigorous PA, and social modelling was associated with greater MVPA and total PA, as well as reduced sedentary time [38]. Home-based exercise intervention, Project VIVA! improved strength and ROM, even when some fitness outcomes were unchanged over 16 weeks [31]. Cross-group comparisons showed Hispanic breast cancer survivors were less physically active compared to non-Hispanic whites, and self-efficacy, social modeling, and family support were key determinants [36, 40]. In HIV + women, a 6-week LV-HIIT improved peak workload and time to peak; however, CRF gains were limited compared to HIV- women [33].

Across phases, physical inactivity was typically conceptualized as not meeting age-specific guidelines (e.g., < 60 min/day MVPA in youth) or as low device-derived MVPA or steps over 6 to 7-day wear protocols. Sedentary behavior was generally conceptualized as total sitting time or screen viewing time, measured objectively as accelerometer-derived “sedentary time” over 6 to 7-day wear periods (e.g., elementary school-aged children, older adults, and HIV cohorts) and subjectively via standardized questionnaires, primarily the IPAQ sitting-time item [38, 52] and the Past-day Adults’ Sedentary Time Questionnaire [38]. Interaction testing occurred only once [38] in Phase 4, and no novel factors were identified. In Phase 4, most studies targeted the individual level (N = 7), with some focusing on the interpersonal level (N = 3) and others on the environmental level (N = 2).

Discussion

This narrative review synthesized evidence from Puerto Rican research across diverse Puerto Rican populations (e.g., clinical populations [29, 42], university students [34, 43], adolescents [41, 45, 52], children [30, 32], and older adults [49, 51, 53]). Approximately 62% of the studies describe patterns and correlates of PA rather than evaluating interventions or translating evidence into policy or practice [30, 32, 35–37, 40–45, 48, 49, 51–53]. There were consistent gender disparities in PA participation across the studies, with male populations consistently being more physically active across age groups [45, 53]. Measurement was heterogeneous and often more focused on leisure-time PA; domains such as transport, household, and occupational PA were rarely specified or quantified. Sedentary behavior was frequently captured with a single survey item, such as sitting time [49] or screen time [39], rather than being treated as an exposure with its own determinants and health effects [54]. Many of the PA data collection methods consisted of self-report surveys or objectively measured PA with pedometers or accelerometers; however, no studies combined these methods with other approaches (e.g., GPS data) to clarify where and how movement occurs [55]. Collectively, these patterns point to a field that has characterized who is physically inactive, but has yet to build mature measurement systems, strong causal evidence, or scalable solutions.

Under the BEF, most studies aligned with Phase 3, with nearly half of the remaining studies in Phase 4, a few studies in Phases 1 and 2, and no studies aligning with Phase 5. This pattern provides strong evidence for describing who is physically inactive or active and which factors are correlated with PA and sedentary behavior [25]. However, there is limited focus on validating context-appropriate measurements (Phase 2), intervention testing (Phase 4), and implementation/scale-up (Phase 5). We extended the BEF with additional dimensions from Bauman et al. (2012), which provided further context regarding the implications for the current PA research field in PR. First, PA domains are essential for action, but they were underrepresented in this review [26, 56].

Bauman et al. (2012) emphasize that correlates are domain-specific; actionable targets differ for leisure (e.g., PA programming, facilities), compared to transport (e.g., street design, public transport), household (e.g., gardening, chores), and occupational (e.g., agriculture, construction) [26]. Routinely reporting domains, especially adding transport, household, and occupational to leisure, can help avoid labeling people as inactive simply because their PA happens outside traditional exercise [56]. Distinguishing between occupational (necessity-driven) and leisure-time (choice-driven) activities is ethically essential because they carry different autonomy risks and recovery demands [57]. Being specific with reporting and measuring different domains prevents labeling heavy labor as “success” and can guide recommendations toward choice-driven PA [56, 57].

Second, the PA research in PR is skewed toward individual-level correlates (e.g., age, gender, BMI, education) [30, 35], with fewer studies examining interpersonal (e.g., family support, social modeling) [40], environmental (e.g., equipment access, PA facilities) [32], or policy factors, and no multi-level interactions. However, more scalable solutions for PA often sit at the environmental or policy level (e.g., urban design, public/active transport systems, green infrastructure) [58]. Future studies should pair behavioral supports with environmental/policy changes and test interactions (e.g., how built-environment improvements benefit populations differently). A call for moving from cross-sectional correlates toward determinants to identify actionable targets could inform how and for whom interventions work and position effective strategies for Phase 5 implementation and translation to policy or practice [25, 26].

The findings of this review highlight the need for pragmatic trials and/or natural experiments (e.g., new parks, green infrastructure, Safe Routes to School, Open Streets) with mediation analyses [26, 60–63]. Fifth, this review did not contain any studies with a focus on novel areas of research, such as genetic (e.g., brain mechanisms, obesity as a determinant), evolutionary (e.g., early-life exposures), macro-social drivers (e.g., urbanization, industrialization, economic transitions), and environmental interactions (e.g., climate change, extreme weather, pollution exposure) [26]. This could help expand determinants of PA research in PR.

Sedentary behavior has independent health effects and interactions with PA [64]. Framing the day as a 24-hour composition (e.g., sleep, sedentary behavior, PA) could clarify realistic trade-offs (e.g., breaking up sitting vs. adding light PA vs. increasing MVPA) and improve guidance for schools, clinics, and senior programs [65]. Self-report often overestimates PA, pairing accelerometry/pedometry with brief domain questions and GPS data could produce valid, actionable data for targeted, scalable PA programs [46, 55]. The following are current research gaps and priorities for the PA field in PR. First, validated PR-Spanish instruments that capture all PA domains and SB (including PA bout patterns, and standardized device protocols, including the use of 24-hour movement) [65–67]. Second, the development of evidence from longitudinal cohorts and natural experiments (e.g., park installations, street redesigns, school policy changes) using difference-in-differences or interrupted time-series designs [59–61, 68, 69].

Third, developing pragmatic, multilevel trials that pair behavioral supports with environmental/policy changes and report implementation outcomes (acceptability, adoption, fidelity, cost, maintenance) could inform scale-up [70]. Fourth, a better population and setting coverage, for example, girls and adolescent young women, working-age adults, rural communities, and workplace/commute settings, as well as clinical groups, should include explicit quantification of non-leisure domains and sedentary behavior [56, 57]. Fifth, a more nuanced contextualization of movement by linking devices to GPS data and environmental data (e.g., parks, sidewalks, transit, heat), alongside standardized reporting and open science (e.g., device wear criteria, implementation metrics, making data openly available) [71–73]. Sixth, qualitative studies were scarce in this review, with only three studies identified, and no mixed-method studies were found. Expanding qualitative inquiry can capture lived experiences, cultural meanings, and context-specific barriers/facilitators across Puerto Rican communities [74]. Mixed-method designs enable triangulation, pairing device-based PA or survey measures with narratives and observations to help guide interventions [75].

Current ongoing studies in PR employing accelerometry are examining PA through accelerometry in medical residents, patients with inflammatory bowel disease, employees of the University of Puerto Rico-Rio Piedras campus, and restaurant industry workers. A longitudinal study focusing on children’s PA, physical fitness, nutritional, and psychological aspects is currently ongoing. Key limitations in PR when conducting research and disseminating findings are the lack of protected time for researchers to write and prohibitive publication fees, exacerbated by the severe austerity measures of the Financial Oversight and Management Board, which have led to draconian budget cuts for PR [76]. Year by year, hundreds of millions of dollars are cut from the University of Puerto Rico’s budget, impacting both its infrastructure and the creation of new knowledge [77].

Targeted funding is essential to support local research capacity in PR. However, given the recent US federal policies that undermine scientific funding, securing funding for future public health research in PR is becoming increasingly challenging [78–82]. Additionally, any research focused on equity or progressive topics is more prohibitive under the new federal administration [82]. An institutional coalition could help address research gaps, leveraging the strengths of the University of Puerto Rico, improving collaborations with local and international organizations, and sharing resources through the existing Puerto Rico Physical Activity Network [22]. Integrating PA education into medical and public health curricula could further strengthen local research capacity and initiatives, thereby enhancing their broader public health impact [22].

Opportunities for future research

Considering the constraints of conducting research in PR, including resource limitations and structural inequities rooted in its colonial relationship with the US, there are clear opportunities to advance PA research. Embed studies within existing delivery systems, Department of Health programs, schools, municipalities, federally qualified health centers, WIC/Head Start, workplaces, and senior centers to test real-world feasibility. Combine culturally familiar strategies (e.g., family-based social support) with built environment and transport changes (e.g., open-streets (Ciclovia) pilots, safe routes to school, shaded walking, and biking paths). Design interventions that also deliver climate resilience co-benefits, including shade, cooling, green infrastructure, and active transport [83]. Strengthen surveillance by adding domain-specific PA and sedentary behavior, periodically device-validating self-report instruments, and creating a PR movement data repository; build multidisciplinary capacity (e.g., public health, transport, planning, education) [22, 56].

Additionally, engaging in implementation science training and research (e.g., EPIS/CIFR, Re-AIM) could support the equitable scaling up of interventions and policy implementation [85]. Future PA research should broaden coverage and address disparities among children, adolescents, people with chronic conditions, and low-income populations; integrating climate, built, and natural environment factors into PA research will help align efforts with global health priorities and emphasize the critical role of evidence-based findings in shaping public health policies and interventions [86, 87]. Collaborating across sectors and fields such as urban design, urban planning, architecture, engineering, transportation, and health could lead to innovative insights, as seen in other examples in Latin America [57].

Advocating for policies that ensure sustainable funding and improve infrastructure for high-quality PA research is vital to support these efforts [88]. PA is inherently complex, shaped by both necessity and choice, and spans leisure and transportation; this complexity is amplified by the systems that communities and national policy actions create [90]. A systems-thinking lens could reframe PA from an individual behavior to one embedded in historical, social, economic, cultural, and environmental factors, deepening our understanding of PA’s role in addressing PR’s multifaceted challenges [89, 90]. We recommend convening transdisciplinary, diverse, and inclusive teams of researchers, policymakers, practitioners, and community members to strengthen the evidence base and translate it into effective PA policy and practice [90].

Limitations

This narrative review is not without limitations. It only includes studies with partial to complete samples of local Puerto Ricans; thus, future reviews should consider comparing the literature from Puerto Ricans residing on the island with that from those living in the mainland United States [84]. Additionally, although this review followed a structured systematic process, it was not a systematic review, and the quality of the studies was not assessed. While multiple comprehensive databases were utilized, additional databases such as Oviedo, Latindex, and Dialnet could be explored in future reviews to capture more studies relevant to the Puerto Rican population [84].

Conclusion

As research on PA continues to develop in PR, it is essential to emphasize further the importance of recognizing and leveraging the multifaceted benefits of PA beyond its role in preventing chronic diseases, particularly by involving diverse sectors and creating new opportunities for promoting PA. Advancing PA research requires embracing systems thinking, fostering collaboration across sectors, prioritizing equity, investing in advocacy, leveraging the co-benefits, and policymaking to fully harness PA’s wide-ranging health, social, and economic benefits [88, 89, 91].
