The Influence of Gender on Publication Outcomes in Postgraduate Surgical Research: A 12‐Year Cohort Study
Authors: Chan Ho Kim, Nazim Bhimani, Anthony R Glover, May Thwin, Chaiwei Tong, Cindy Xu, USYDSurgEd Collaborative Group, Meet Patel
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundHistorically, systemic barriers have limited women's participation in surgical training and academia. Whilst the representation of women among surgical trainees has increased globally, continued examination of gender inequality within surgical academia is necessary to ensure holistic and sustainable diversity. This study explored the association between student and supervisor gender and research outcomes and assessed temporal trends in gender representation within a postgraduate surgery coursework degree.MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted among students who completed the Master of Surgery dissertation subject at The University of Sydney between 2010 and 2021. Data included dissertation mark, publication rate, time to publication, citation count, journal impact factor and total publications within 2 years. Outcomes were compared by student and supervisor gender, and student–supervisor dyads, using Mann–Whitney U, chi‐squared and Kruskal–Wallis tests. Temporal trends were analysed using regression models.ResultsA total of 424 students were included (305 men, 119 women). Dissertation mark and short‐term research outcomes did not differ by student gender with both having a median of one publication. Students supervised by men had higher dissertation publication rates (56% vs. 30%, p < 0.001) and more publications within 2 years (median 1 vs. 0, p = 0.009) than those supervised by women. Woman–woman student–supervisor dyads were less likely to publish their dissertation (29%) than man–man (57%) and woman–man dyads (53%) (p = 0.006). While the number of women supervisors increased over time, the number of women students and the proportional representation of women students and supervisors remained unchanged.ConclusionAcademic performance and short‐term research outcomes were similar by student gender, suggesting that disparities in surgical training and academia are unlikely to be explained by differences in academic capability or early research productivity. Differences associated with supervisor gender and persistent under‐representation highlight ongoing structural influences on academic productivity, and the need for sustained, system‐level equity initiatives across the surgical training pipeline.Keywords: academic performance, gender equity, postgraduate, research, surgery

Introduction

Historically, systemic barriers have limited opportunities for women aspiring to become surgeons and to develop an academic identity. This has seen a wide range of implications from underrepresentation in academic publishing, lower success rates in grant funding, fewer speaking opportunities at high‐impact conferences, and underrepresentation in editorial teams [1]. These persistent inequalities undermine fairness and the diversity of perspectives and approaches within the field. Moreover, they impair the surgical workforce's ability to reflect the demographics of the patient population. Ultimately, this may reduce the attraction of talent to surgery, limit patients' choice of surgeons and potentially impact patient outcomes. In 2017, the Royal Australiasian College of Surgeons (RACS) established gender equity targets to increase the proportion of women selected into Surgical Education and Training (SET) to 40% by 2021 and women's representation on major college committees and leadership roles to 40% by 2020 [2]. RACS has since set a new 40:40:20 gender equity target for 2027, whereby 40% of trainees selected are women, 40% are men, and 20% are of any gender, and a similar representation of women in college leadership roles [2].

Locally, Sakowska and Toffoletti [3] demonstrated gains in women first authors in the Australian and New Zealand Journal of Surgery between 2002 and 2021, presumably driven by women who were early‐career researchers or early in their surgical careers. However, only 11% of articles published had a woman as the last author, suggesting that women still face inequalities within the academic landscape despite institutional level changes [3]. As research productivity is increasingly used as a marker of career progression, understanding the influence of gender on research output is critical to ensuring equitable access to training and leadership positions within the surgical colleges.

The Master of Surgery (MS) degree at the University of Sydney (USYD) is designed to prepare Australian medical practitioners for careers in surgery. The program integrates surgical curricula with formal education in research methodology, providing the skills required to conduct and publish research. To date, Patel et al. [4] remain the only study investigating academic outcomes within an Australian postgraduate surgical master's degree by coursework. Notably, only 12% of supervisors in the cohort were women, and students with women supervisors were 73% less likely to publish their dissertation, despite women supervisors being associated with higher dissertation marks. These findings were unexpected and raise important questions regarding the interaction between student and supervisor gender and academic outcomes in surgical research training. It was hypothesised that publication outcomes would not differ by student gender, and that the representation of women among students and supervisors would have increased since 2017.

The aim of this study is to: (1) compare the quantity and quality of publications between men and women students within 2 years of completing the dissertation subject (2) assess the influence of supervisor gender and specific student‐supervisor gender combinations on these outcomes (3) evaluate trends in gender representation within the MS dissertation subject following the 2017 RACS Diversity and Inclusion Plan.

Materials and Methods

Ethics and Consent

Ethics approval for this study was obtained from The University of Sydney Human Research Ethics Committee (Approval no: 2023/268; Application version: 2.01). The study was conducted in accordance with the National Statement on Ethical Conduct in Human Research, 2023 and Declaration of Helsinki guidelines, 2024.

Cohort

This study involved a retrospective analysis of an existing database generated from administrative and publicly available data. This cohort study included students enrolled in the MS program at the University of Sydney who completed the Dissertation B subject between 2010 and 2021. This subject requires completion of a research project worth 18 credit points and approximately 300 h of study time. Enrolment in the Master of Surgery requires completion of a primary medical qualification and registration as a medical practitioner in Australia or New Zealand. Completion of the dissertation component is not compulsory, and students can complete a research project with a supervisor of their choice. Supervisors are not required to hold an affiliation with USYD or another university, nor are they required to have completed any specific research training.

The inclusion criteria were students who successfully completed the Dissertation B subject (SURG5051 or SURG5008). Students who withdrew from the subject prior to the census date or failed the subject were excluded. Students with insufficient data on supervisors or the dissertation project were not included in the study.

Students' dissertations, research outcomes and supervisorial details were extracted from a previously established database, detailed by Patel et al. [4]. In summary, the student grades and demographic details were obtained from the central USYD analytics department, and the students' dissertation titles, research area, study type and supervisor details were extracted from the Dissertation final submission. A manual search was undertaken to determine whether the dissertation was published, the number of publications the student had prior to starting the Dissertation B subject, and the number of publications after 2 years of completing the Dissertation B subject, using PubMed and Web of Science. Supervisor demographic details were extracted from publicly available online sources including institutional profiles and professional webpages, using a methodology consistent with previously described literature [5, 6].

Statistical Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise student and supervisor characteristics. Continuous variables were assessed for normality and variables with non‐normal distribution are presented as medians with interquartile ranges. Categorical variables are reported as frequencies and proportions. Comparisons between two independent groups were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test for continuous outcomes and the Pearson chi‐squared test for categorical outcomes. Analyses of months to publication, number of citations and journal impact factor were performed among students with a published dissertation.

To examine the combined effect of student and supervisor gender, participants were stratified into four student‐supervisor gender dyads. Continuous outcomes were compared using the Kruskal–Wallis test, while categorical outcomes were analysed using the Pearson chi‐squared test. Where overall tests were significant, post hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test.

Temporal trends in student enrolment by gender were examined using negative binomial regression to account for overdispersion, with year of dissertation completion modelled as a continuous predictor and annual enrolment counts as the dependent variable. Temporal changes in the proportion of women students were assessed using a binomial generalised linear model with an events/trials specification. For supervisor analyses, negative binomial models demonstrated numerical instability with a dispersion parameter approaching zero. Hence, Poisson regression with robust (sandwich) standard errors was used to evaluate temporal changes in the number of women supervisors. Changes in the proportion of women supervisors were assessed using a binomial generalised linear model with an events/trials specification. Effect estimates from negative binomial and Poisson regression models are presented as incidence rate ratios (IRRs) and estimates from binomial generalised linear models are presented as odds ratios (ORs), each with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

All statistical tests were two‐sided, and statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac, version 31 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 585 students were identified. Of these, 151 were excluded because they withdrew from or failed the subject, and a further four were excluded due to missing data (Figure 1). Therefore, 424 students (305 men; 119 women) were included in the study. Two supervisors had missing data, resulting in a final cohort of 422 supervisors (372 men; 50 women). Supervisors were not necessarily unique, as individuals could supervise multiple students either concurrently or across different years.

Outcomes Based on Students' Supervisor Gender

In addition, Table 1 stratifies research outcomes by supervisor gender. Students with men supervisors were more likely to publish their dissertation (56%) compared to those with women supervisors (30%) (p < 0.001) and had a greater number of publications within 2 years of unit completion (median 1 vs. 0, respectively; p = 0.009). There was a trend towards students with women supervisors having a higher median dissertation mark (median = 80) compared to men supervisors (median = 76); however, this was not significant (p = 0.052). There was no difference between time to publication (p = 0.512), journal impact factor (p = 0.522), or number of citations within 2 years of dissertation publication (p = 0.714).

As shown in Table 2, there were 274 man student–man supervisor dyads, 29 man student–woman supervisor dyads, 98 woman student–man supervisor dyads and 21 woman student–woman supervisor dyads. A difference in dissertation publication rates across dyads was observed (p = 0.006). Man student–man supervisor dyads demonstrated a higher proportion of published dissertations than expected (57%), whereas man student–woman supervisor dyads demonstrated a lower‐than‐expected publication rate (31%). Woman student–man supervisor dyads published at rates consistent with expected values, while woman student–woman supervisor dyads demonstrated fewer published dissertations than expected (29%). There was a difference in the number of publications within 2 years across student–supervisor gender combinations (man–man median = 1; man–woman median = 1; woman–man median = 1; woman–woman median = 0; p = 0.032). However, post hoc pairwise comparisons were not statistically significant.

Trends in Gender Participation in the Dissertation Subject

The proportion of women students enrolled for the Dissertation B subject varied from 6.3% to 44.2% between 2010 and 2021 (Figure 2a). Negative binomial regression demonstrated no significant temporal trend in the number of women students enrolled over time (IRR per year 1.05, 95% CI 0.98–1.14, p = 0.178) (Table 3). A binomial generalised linear model demonstrated there was neither a significant linear change in the proportion of women students over time (OR per year 1.02, 95% CI 0.96–1.08; p = 0.510) (Table 3).

Comparing the pre‐ and post‐2017 periods, there was no significant change in the number of women students enrolled (IRR 1.34, 95% CI 0.76–2.33; p = 0.317) or in the proportion of women students (OR 1.14, 95% CI 0.76–1.35; p = 0.557).

Meanwhile, women supervisors accounted for 3% to 19% of the supervisor cohort between 2010 and 2021 (Figure 2b). The number of women supervisors increased by approximately 9% per year between 2010 and 2021 (Poisson regression with robust standard errors: IRR 1.09 per year, 95% CI 1.03–1.16; p = 0.003), but the year of dissertation completion was not associated with the odds of having a woman supervisor (OR per year 1.06, 95% CI 0.97–1.15; p = 0.212) (Table 3). There was no significant change in the number of women supervisors (IRR 1.64, 95% CI 1.00–2.71; p = 0.051) or the proportion of women supervisors (OR 1.42, 95% CI 0.79–2.58; p = 0.241) between the pre‐ and post‐2017 cohorts.

Discussion

The presented study demonstrates no difference in dissertation grades or short‐term research outcomes between men and women students. These findings suggest that, within this academic training environment supported by structured faculty input and assessment processes, early research performance does not appear to disadvantage women students. This is consistent with existing literature from America, where both women and men surgical residents have similar research productivity after completing a research elective [7]. Therefore, structured research programmes provide a valuable platform for equitable research opportunities across genders. Importantly, the absence of performance differences contrasts with the previously demonstrated persistent under‐representation of women in surgical training and senior academic roles, indicating that disparities are unlikely to be explained by differences in academic capability or early research productivity. Instead, these findings support the view that gender inequities in surgery arise from structural, cultural and progression‐related factors rather than individual‐level performance. The results reinforce local and international efforts to improve women's representation in surgery through structural equity initiatives. These include the development of flexible training pathways to support the family and work‐life balance needs of all surgical trainees, expansion of leadership and mentorship opportunities for women, and the promotion of respectful and inclusive workplace cultures, as opposed to women‐specific remedial programmes [8, 9, 10, 11].

Despite equivalent research outcomes, women students remained underrepresented within the MS programme. This mirrors international trends in surgery and academia, where men were more likely to be enrolled in MD‐PhD programmes, despite similar levels of interest in research‐intensive careers across genders [12]. Previous studies have identified barriers including limited access to mentorship, funding opportunities, flexible training pathways and competing caregiving responsibilities [8, 12]. Women supervisors were also substantially underrepresented in the MS programme, with six women supervisors comprising 14% of total supervisors in 2021. This aligns with national and international literature showing slower growth in women's representation as last authors—a surrogate marker of senior academic leadership—compared with first authorship [3, 13]. Women remain underrepresented globally in surgical authorship, as clinical trial principal investigators, and among recipients of major research funding, despite comparable or superior academic performance relative to men [14, 15, 16]. In Australia, longstanding workforce disparities persist, with women comprising only 17.2% of the active surgeons [17]. However, senior academic representation cannot be explained by workforce ratios alone. Women surgical faculty experience slower academic promotion, even after adjusting for prior demographic differences in medical residents and students [18, 19, 20]. This disparity is commonly attributed to the ‘leaky pipeline’ phenomenon, whereby attrition occurs disproportionately among women academics [3]. Gender diversity within surgical academia is associated with broader research perspectives and improved innovation; therefore, downstream strategies are equally vital [21].

These systemic disparities in academic seniority and resource allocation may partially contextualise the observed differences in student publication outcomes according to supervisor gender. Consistent with Patel et al., students supervised by men were more likely to publish their dissertation and demonstrated higher short‐term publication output [4]. However, our analysis did not demonstrate any difference in journal impact factor or citation count, suggesting no measurable difference in research quality. Given the historical underrepresentation of women in surgery, it is plausible that women supervisors within our cohort were, on average, earlier in their academic careers than their male counterparts. Hence, differences in academic seniority, research and supervisory experience, research group size and volume of funded projects may have contributed to the observed differences in dissertation publication and short‐term research outcomes, independent of gender itself. Currently, the dissertation programme provides limited formal supervisorial training, with students instead receiving direct support in research skills, such as statistical analysis or literature searching, from university staff. With the introduction of dedicated and structured supervisorial training, it is possible that the differences identified in the present study may diminish over time, particularly as greater parity is achieved across the academic and surgical pipeline.

When stratified by student‐supervisor gender pairing, men students with men supervisors published more dissertations than expected, while women students with men supervisors published at the expected rate. In contrast, both men and women students supervised by women supervisors had lower‐than‐expected dissertation publication rates. These findings suggest that student gender alone is unlikely to account for the observed disparity in research outcomes between supervisor genders and same‐gender pairings may not exert a consistent or synergistic influence on dissertation publication outcomes. However, longitudinal mentorship outcomes and potential benefits of gender concordance—including career development, professional identity formation and long‐term academic progression—remain unexplored.

A prospective multicentre study would provide a more robust framework for evaluating factors associated with research participation and productivity within surgical training pathways. Such a study should systemically collect data on supervisor seniority, academic rank, funding support, research group size, protected research time, mentorship practices, familial and social responsibilities, employment status and student career intentions. In addition, surveys exploring reasons for undertaking or not undertaking dissertation electives could help identify barriers to participation. This would allow assessment of whether the associations observed in the present study persist after adjustment for structural and career‐stage factors, and would provide a stronger evidence base for interventions aimed at improving equity within surgical academia.

This study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting its findings. Although it incorporated student and supervisor gender, it was unable to account for additional potentially relevant characteristics such as supervisor seniority, academic rank, grant funding or research group size, and supervisory approach, which may confound the observed associations between supervisor gender and publication outcomes. Second, the analysis focused only on research outcomes that were within 2 years of unit completion, though the impact of the MS on these outcomes is likely to diminish as students' progress in their careers. Nonetheless, an area of future research could include the progression towards higher degrees by research or attainment of academic positions. Long‐term follow‐up to ascertain the proportion of MS graduates who secure an advanced training position would also provide valuable context regarding the role of the programme within the broader surgical training pathway. Finally, this study was conducted within a single institution and programme, which may limit generalisability to other postgraduate surgical research programmes.

Conclusion

In summary, men and women students of the MS programme exhibited comparable dissertation grades and short‐term publication outcomes. Therefore, structured research programmes provide an equitable platform for developing an academic career through formal assessment and structured teaching in research methods. A combination of historical disadvantages and ongoing academic inequities may hinder the capacity of senior women supervisors to engage students in the academic pipeline and sustain research productivity. Continued advancement towards gender equality in the field of surgery will require ongoing efforts to address both upstream and downstream sources of inequity in training and academic progression.
