Mechanical complications of transcatheter aortic valve replacement: a review article
Authors: Rudaina Ismail Osman Ahmed, Mazin Salah, Hasan Abua’Isha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Aortic stenosis (AS) represents a prevalent valvular heart disease, particularly affecting the elderly population, with substantial implications for morbidity and mortality. This review examines the epidemiology, pathophysiology, management strategies, complications, and future implications of AS and its treatment modalities, focusing primarily on transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR). The prevalence of AS is rising as the population ages, with notable differences observed across various demographic groups and regions. Additionally, AS can also occur in younger adults, typically due to a bicuspid aortic valve, the most common congenital heart defect, which further contributes to the condition’s growing clinical significance. Surgical aortic valve replacement (SAVR) has historically been the standard treatment for severe AS, but TAVR methods have emerged as a less invasive alternative, particularly for high-risk surgical candidates. SAVR has historically been the standard treatment for severe AS, but TAVR has undergone rapid evolution and widespread acceptance, with over 200 000 procedures performed globally. Various delivery routes for TAVR exist, each with its advantages and limitations, including transfemoral, subclavian, direct aortic, transapical, and transcarotid approaches. Complications associated with TAVR include vascular complications, stroke, conduction disturbances, coronary artery obstruction, acute kidney injury, infections, and others. Strategies for complication prevention and management are actively researched and implemented. The review also discusses future implications of TAVR, highlighting advancements in device technology, procedural techniques, patient selection criteria, and the expanding patient population eligible for TAVR. Despite challenges and complications, TAVR has revolutionized the treatment landscape for AS and offers hope for patients previously deemed unsuitable for surgery.

As a sign of the aging process, this issue is becoming increasingly prevalent as the average age of the population rises[2]. AS affects 2–9% of individuals who are above 65 years old, while aortic sclerosis is observed in 25% of adults in the same age group, with a noticeable rise linked to the aging process[3]. According to one study, approximately one in eight individuals over 75 years old are diagnosed with moderate or severe VHD[4]. Furthermore, younger adults also develop AS, often due to a bicuspid aortic valve, which remains the most frequent congenital heart anomaly contributing to the condition[5].

Epidemiological data on aortic valve disease shows differences between high-income and low-income nations. Rheumatic valvulopathy is more commonly seen in low-income countries. Even within the same country, prevalence rates differ widely (for instance, 374 cases per 100 000 in Maori/Pacific, native Australian, and New Zealand communities versus 17.2 per 100 000 in the general New Zealand population). The prevalence varies greatly, from 46 per 100 000 in northern India to 2400 per 100 000 in the Solomon Islands. Infective endocarditis is most frequently seen in low-income nations and across Africa[6].

In Africa, the incidence of clinical rheumatic heart disease (RHD), detected through auscultation, was reported as 100 cases per 100 000 annually in Sudan. The prevalence in Sudan was 10.2 per 1000, when compared to 2.3 per 1000 in Saudi Arabia and 5.1 per 1000 in Egypt[7].

Each year, around 300 000 surgical aortic valve replacements (SAVRs) are performed worldwide, and with the aging population, this number is expected to double by 2050[8]. While symptomatic severe AS is fatal if left untreated, patients’ life expectancy returns to normal if timely mechanical relief of stenosis is provided. The management of mild, asymptomatic, advanced, and severe cases, along with the effect of new percutaneous therapies, remains a topic of significant debate in the treatment of AS[2].

Although the clinical and pathological description of AS dates back to the 18th century, Ross and Braunwald’s 1968 study provided a clearer definition of the disease’s natural history and prognostic importance. It is now commonly accepted that the onset of symptoms like syncope, angina, and exertional dyspnea is a poor prognostic indicator of LV decompensation. The only available treatment is valve replacement therapy, which has been shown to increase patient longevity and enhance results[9].

For patients who do not show symptoms, aortic valve replacement is generally delayed until symptoms appear or if abnormal results are found during an exercise test[9]. Since the advent of cardiopulmonary bypass, SAVR has become the standard treatment for severe symptomatic AS, showing a noticeable improvement in symptoms and quality of life compared to medical management[10]. Additionally, SAVR is associated with low morbidity and mortality rates[11].

However, it is important to mention that SAVR is not suitable for all patients, especially those deemed unfit for surgery due to a higher risk of complications. Some are considered high-risk when all factors for surgery eligibility are taken into account[11]. Fortunately, patients who are not suitable for surgery can opt for TAVR, a promising alternative for patients at high risk for SAVR. A transcatheter aortic valve (TAV) is designed to be compressed into a small catheter, which is then inserted into the patient’s damaged aortic valve under fluoroscopic guidance to restore its function.

Some TAVs, like the Edwards SAPIEN family, are balloon-expandable, while others, such as Medtronic’s CoreValve, are self-expandable due to their shape-memory nitinol stents. In both cases, the TAVs are placed within a calcified native valve that remains permanently open, providing the surface against which the stent is held in place by resistance. Recently, TAVs have been used to replace malfunctioning bioprosthetic or previous transcatheter valves in a procedure known as valve-in-valve (ViV)[12].

Transcatheter aortic valve implantation (TAVI) has emerged as a safe and effective option for patients with symptomatic severe AS who are at high or moderate risk for SAVR[13].

This groundbreaking innovation, though initially met with skepticism, is now widely accepted, thanks to numerous clinical trials and evidence-based studies[14]. To date, more than 200 000 TAVI procedures have been performed in over 65 countries, with 25 000 TAVIs being performed annually in the United States alone[15,16]. TAVI is now officially recognized as a class I indication for individuals with high surgical risk and severe AS[17].

The search included two main databases: PubMed and Google Scholar, which were chosen for their breadth of peer-reviewed biomedical and clinical literature.

Searches were limited to English-language publications between 2001 and 2024. Studies were selected based on their relevance, impact (as indicated by citations and journal reputation), and methodological quality.

Study selection and screening process

Titles and abstracts were screened independently by the first author. Full texts were reviewed when relevance was unclear. Final inclusion was based on consensus regarding clinical significance and contribution to the understanding of TAVI/TAVR. Preference was given to highly cited or guideline-influencing papers.

History

The advancement of TAVR was a result of a requirement for an elective treatment method for high-risk surgical individuals with aortic valve infection. It is assessed that 33% of patients who meet signs for SAVR are not offered surgical treatment inferable from end-stage infection, old age, or numerous comorbidities[18–20].

In the early 21st years, the procedure was performed on humans after multiple effective animal TAVRs were made. This procedure was performed by Alan Cribier in the year 2002, on a patient aged 57 years with extreme AS, cardiogenic shock, and sub-acute limb ischemia that made him grouped as a high risk and unsuitable for surgery. After balloon aortic valvuloplasty did not manage to deliver sustained results, Cribier embedded a percutaneous valve that had already been utilized before during his animal studies.

Generally, this procedure was performed under conscious sedation utilizing femoral vein access as well as trans-septal ways. The patient showed hemodynamic improvement and his valve was in a pronounced condition for 4 months, but sadly passed away due to complications arising from his leg ischemia. Nonetheless, this case exhibited that catheter- based aortic valve substitution could steadily be acted in people. Following this underlying report, the first retrograde TAVR through femoral supply route access was acted in 2005[10].

Notwithstanding progresses, existing TAVR gadgets do have restrictions, counting the powerlessness to embed without being capable to recover or reposition later after being fully extended, hemodynamic accommodation during implantation, and the prerequisite for fast pacing during deployment.

Valve malposition necessitating a second TAVR valve has been related with an expanded procedural risk[21,22]. The requirement for permanent pacemaker implantation is one more significant restriction of TAVR. Paravalvular leak (PVL) can happen. Contrasted with patients without leaks, there is a higher mortality related with moderate or serious PVL[23].

The core plan is analogous for each of the three valves, involving a tri-leaflet pericardial bioprosthesis that is attached on a metal stent and borne inside the local aortic valve annulus. That being said, huge dissimilarities in both layout and utilization do not cease to exist[10].

The Edwards SAPIEN valve is made from bovine pericardium, which is fixed onto a cobalt- chromium stent and encased in an external securing edge. The latest version of this valve, the SAPIEN 3, is available in sizes 20, 23, 26, and 29 mm, and is deployed using a 14-Fr or 16-Fr sheath (for the 29 mm valve). Before delivery, the valve is tightly compressed onto a balloon catheter, which is inflated and deflated to position the valve within the aortic valve annulus. Once the SAPIEN valve is placed, no further adjustments to its position are possible[10].

Medtronic has introduced its new valve generation, the Evolut PRO, which builds on the Evolut R platform. This valve features an external porcine pericardial covering at the lower two rows of the stent frame, designed to reduce PVL. The ability to reposition and re-sheath the prosthesis is maintained. The available sizes for the Evolut PRO are 23, 26, and 29 mm. Compared to the previous generation, the Evolut PRO requires a larger introducer sheath (16-F compared to 14-F for the Evolut R) and a minimum vessel diameter of 5.5 mm, whereas the Evolut R required 5 mm[24].

The Lotus valve system, developed by Boston Scientific, features a woven Nitinol frame with three bovine pericardial leaflets. The valve is delivered through a controlled mechanical system, allowing for repositioning and retrieval before final deployment[25].

Delivery routes

There are various means in which the aortic valve can be implanted. The route that is more commonly used is the retrograde transfemoral arterial access (in Fig. 1) due to it being a less invasive site, and it is also the only site in which the implantation can be done percutaneously, and can sometimes be made without general anesthesia[26]. Schematic figure for delivery routes[30].

In this procedure, both femoral arteries are accessed. This is because one of the femoral arteries will undergo the placement of the 18-Fr sheath, while the second femoral artery will be used for the 5-Fr pigtail via a 6-Fr sheath into the noncoronary sinus to basically act as a pointer for valve placement. In addition, sometimes the arm is used in permitting arteriography throughout placement[27].

The transfemoral approach is currently used with a percentage of 0.5–87.8%, and was first done in the year 2005[28].

A study has even concluded that the transfemoral approach is considered more favorable[29].

The second route is the subclavian access, and this is a modern site. In the subclavian access, the subclavian artery is wide open in the deltopectoral region of the frontal chest wall. It is very important to be careful as the brachial plexus lies above the subclavian artery[27].

The most common artery is usually the left subclavian artery, due to the right subclavian being a bothersome implantation angle, with the CoreValve and Evolut R being the often used valves. In order to create a hole site a surgical cut-down is required, followed by settlement of the sutures on the vascular wall, penetration of the artery, and followed by the placement of a rigid guide wire to the left ventricle above the pig-tail diagnostic catheter and 8 French sheath. Afterward, the placement of an 18 French sheath via the subclavian artery into the ascending aorta takes place. The placement of the valve itself is similar to the femoral approach[28].

It is worth mentioning that a study improvised that the subclavian access is not recommended in individuals that have a subclavian artery with a diameter less than 7 mm, substantial tortuosity, a history of coronary artery bypass grafting (CABG), and patent in situ internal mammary artery grafts[26].

Another arterial technique is the direct aortic access, which is used in people who are not fit for the two routes mentioned earlier or are at a very high risk of vascular complications. It is contraindicated in two conditions: porcelain aorta and the presence of calcified atherosclerotic plaques on the anterior wall of the ascending aorta.

Once the left ventricle has the access site of the stiff wire, and the ascending aorta has the 18 F inserted, the valves can be implanted using one of the two surgical approaches: the right anterior mini-thoracotomy or upper mini-sternotomy[28].

Another route is the transapical access, in which the Edwards SAPIEN valve is used; this is used for individuals who do not have suitable iliofemoral vessels[27].

The least used approach is the transcarotid access; it is used when the other routes are not suitable for the individual. It carries disadvantages like increased risk of stroke and proximity of the sheath and the delivery system with the carotid artery wall, but has the benefit of being a facile vascular entry[28].

The concept between the conduction system and conduction disturbances post-TAVR

Peri-procedural conduction abnormalities following transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) can be adequately accounted for based on the anatomy where the conduction system lies in close association with the area of the aortic valve.

The triangle of Koch, which includes the ligament of Todaro, coronary sinus ostium, and area of atrioventricular (AV) node involving the septal leaflet of tricuspid valve, contains the AV node in the right atrium[31–33]. The coronary sinus ostium serves as its base, which is formed by ligament of Todaro and tricuspid valve attachment to the septum on the AV portion of the membranous septum. The AV node lies just below its apex and continues on to Bundle of His, passing through a specific elongated structure on one side and through the membranous septum. There lies the conduction system on the left side, continuing to left bundle branch, where it emerges below the membranous septum and runs along with the crest of interventricular septum. The left bundle branch, continuing to provide conduction to interleaflet triangle, passes under the membranous septum and along with interventricular crest and provides innervations to interleaflet triangles through its attachment to its base where the noncoronary and right coronary leaflets of the aortic valve attach to left bundle branch. This anatomy holds pivotal qualities toward understanding conduction abnormalities post TAVR. Post-mortem anatomy has suggested that conduction abnormalities following TAVR occur due to direct physical damage toward conduction system along with varying levels of secondary damage due to edema, hematoma, and ischemia[34].

The part of the His bundle proximal to it as it passes through the membranous septum, as well as the great variability from individual to individual in the antero-posterior position of the AV node relative to the apex of the triangle of Koch, acquires a certain significance. Kawashima and Sato[35] recorded such variations anatomically in a post-mortem examination of 115 elderly persons. There were three important observations: about 50% had the usual right-sided AV bundle, about 30% had left-sided AV bundles, while the remaining 20% had the AV bundle lying below the membranous septum, barely under the endocardium. Those persons having a short membranous septum are likely prone to conduction abnormalities secondary to TAVR, owing to the above two factors[34].

There is evidence about a correlation between AS and conduction abnormalities, apart from the interaction between the transcatheter valve and the conduction system. Several theories try to explain this correlation, such as the evolution of LV remodeling, which has been associated with the appearance of left bundle branch block (LBBB) and successive AV block (AVB) in AS[34], and the deposition of calcification on the conduction system due to its association with the aortic valve complex[24]. Urena et al analyzing 435 candidates for TAVR in a prospective manner, found a 3.5% rate of episodes of progressive AV block or LBBB in 24 h ECG recordings performed the day before the procedure[36].

A meta-analysis comparing clinical outcomes in patients with symptomatic severe AS who underwent either TAVI or SAVR found that the 2-year mortality rate for TAVI patients was reduced to 12%, compared to SAVR, with the transfemoral approach yielding an even lower rate of 17%. This reduction in mortality was consistent across all levels of standard surgical risk, regardless of the FDA-approved THV systems used. Additionally, TAVI was associated with a lower risk of new-onset atrial fibrillation, acute kidney injury (AKI), and major bleeding. However, it was linked to an increased likelihood of permanent pacemaker implantation[39].

Permanent pacemaker implantation typically occurred due to conduction disturbances following TAVR, such as new-onset left bundle branch block (LBBB) and advanced AV block, which are common complications[34]. The relationship between the conduction system and LBBB was previously explained.

An incidence of new-onset LBBB between 4% and 65% has been observed among patients who have undergone TAVR using first generation valves. However, there appears to be a relatively higher incidence of de novo LBBB with the self-expanding CoreValve prosthesis (Medtronic, Minneapolis, Minnesota) with a reported incidence of 18–65%, compared with 4 and 30% with the balloon-expandable Edwards SAPIEN/SAPIEN XT valves. There is a lack of experience with newer generations of TAVR technology, although there are reports of an incidence of 12 and 22% with the SAPIEN 3 valve. New-onset LBBB and the need for a permanent pacemaker have also been observed with the self-expanding Portico TAVR system, with no vented inflow, and with potentially beneficial characteristics of partial re-sheathability and repositionability. However, new-onset LBBB and the need for a permanent pacemaker implantation may have adverse effects on long-term patient outcomes[34].

The current techniques for prevention and improvement were made by designing newer models of delivery systems and bioprosthesis, change in layout by reducing the dimension of the sheath, and having enhanced covers for the annulus by additional valve sizes, as well as improved placement of the bioprosthesis and improved fastening[14].

Coronary artery obstruction is believed to be one of the complications of valve-in-valve TAVR, but fortunately one of the uncommon ones[40–42].

Coronary obstruction has also been reported more in females, and it most commonly affects the left carotid artery[43], as well as patients that have had a history of surgical bioprosthesis[44].

The pathophysiology of this condition takes place when the transcatheter heart valve dislocates the underlying surgical or native aortic valve leaflet in a forward direction and obstructs the coronary artery ostia, by either one of the two maneuvers, directly, or either by requisitioning the sinus of Valsalva at the sinotubular junction[45].

A new approach aimed at reducing iatrogenic coronary occlusion during the TAVR procedure has been named bioprosthetic or native aortic scallop intentional laceration, or the BASILICA approach, which has been named from the LAMPOON procedure and involves the intentional laceration of the anterior mitral leaflet.

However, mitral valve intervention is used to prevent LV outflow tract obstruction. Electrosurgical incision crossing valve leaflets is needed in both BASILICA and other techniques to prevent obstruction of crucial structures during procedures of transcatheter valve insertion[45].

At present, BASILICA is still being assessed with existing commercial instruments. There is initial evidence on its efficacy in the prevention of coronary obstruction in patients at risk of either bioprosthetic or native aortic valve lesions[45].

A classical preventive strategy for coronary blockage due to TAVR includes proper patient selection based upon CT scan evaluation[45].

An additional preventive method would be SAVR. Nevertheless, in high-risk patients, it may not be an ideal method. For patients considered inoperable, additional options are limited. Palliative care is believed to be a joint process. The use of guide wires, catheters, or stents for intubation of high-risk coronary arteries to provide early intervention or prevent obstruction of the coronary arteries due to TAVR has also been proposed[41,42,46].

These catheter instruments are inevitably positioned between the aortic root and the TAVR implant. Unfortunately, the pre-positioned stents are only trapped and often need to be placed even when they are not otherwise required.

Rescue percutaneous coronary intervention does not have a high success rate due to the fact that the pinned valve leaflets have the likelihood of not being traversable. Inferior, cyclic compression and other local vascular perturbations that are available including (“snorkel,” “chimney,” or “periscope”) stents unfortunately directed to numerous stent deformations and thrombosis and serious ischemic complications[45].

In cases of coronary obstruction, a doable and effective treatment in most cases is percutaneous coronary intervention[43].

As mentioned earlier, stroke is one of the complications of TAVR. A study stated that the occurrence of post-TAVR stroke during 30 days was 5.4%, which was equivalent to their control group of 5.2%. It has also been linked with poor prognosis and higher rate of mortality[47]. Stroke following TAVR has been classified into early, delayed, or late depending on the duration post-TAVR of up to 2 days, 2–30 days, or 30 days post TAVR, respectively[17,47].

It has also been stated that “early” stroke which is within the first 30 days after TAVR is a critical defacement for 30-day survival. As well as identifying preliminary involvement as a major and self-determining risk factor for the existence of stroke post TAVR[47].

It is important to highlight AKI, as one study reported an AKI incidence of 18%, with 2% of patients requiring hemodialysis, which is consistent with previous studies that show rates ranging from 12 to 28%[7,22]. Both studies identified periprocedural red blood cell (RBC) transfusion as a significant predictor of AKI, and according to Bagur et al, this was independently linked to 30-day mortality. While the exact pathophysiological relationship between periprocedural RBC transfusions and AKI following TAVI is not yet clear, patients with these risk factors should be closely monitored during the early postprocedural period and possibly beyond[48].

In terms of infections, urinary tract infections (UTIs) were the most common, occurring in 10% of patients, followed by pulmonary and access site infections, which affected 5 and 3% of patients, respectively[48], with similar findings reported by Webb et al[37]. Early removal of urinary catheters has been shown to reduce UTI rates, although this is challenging since many patients require catheters until they are able to move independently[48]. Infectious endocarditis (IE) is an additional consequence that has been noted in patients after both TAVR and surgical valve replacement.

TAVR patients are thought to be at higher risk for IE due to their more advanced comorbidities compared to those undergoing surgical valve replacement[13].

After TAVI, Staphylococcus (30.4%), Streptococcus (29.9%), and Enterococcus (20.5%) were the most frequent causal species of IE. An increased risk of developing IE was independently linked to HF, substantial bleeding, sepsis during TAVI hospitalization, younger age, a history of congestive HF, the requirement for a permanent pacemaker, and HF. When IE patients were readmitted to the hospital, the in-hospital death rate was 15.6%. The incidence of early IE was 1.7% per person annually in a sizable cohort of TAVI patients in the United States.

Factors such as age, comorbidities, invasive procedures, and complications during hospitalization were all linked to the occurrence of IE post-TAVI[13].

Other complications include access site dissection, occlusion, or thrombosis, which typically become apparent when the initiating sheath is removed. These can often be managed with balloon angioplasty or stent implantation, and a crossover technique can significantly facilitate the management of vascular complications[38]. Thrombosis is thought to have multifactorial causes, with certain factors increasing the likelihood of its development, such as the placement of a prothrombotic metallic frame, circulation disturbances during valve placement, older age, comorbidities, and a prothrombotic predisposition[49].

Cerebral embolic events have been reported in up to 10% of TAVI cases, though most are considered clinically insignificant microemboli when observed on MRI. Nevertheless, it is important to reduce manipulation of the aortic arch and native valve (e.g., by minimizing the number of balloon inflations) to lower the risk of neurological events[38].

Future implications

The TAVR field is advancing rapidly, with notable improvements in technology, procedural techniques, patient selection, and biomedical engineering. With the development of more advanced devices, novel techniques, and improved implantation systems, TAVI has become significantly safer and more accessible. Initially, the focus was on older patients with AS and multiple co-morbidities. However, the indications are now expanding to include a broader range of patients, such as those with lower surgical risks, declined surgical bioprostheses, and even individuals with other valvular diseases, including isolated aortic or mitral insufficiency. Few medical fields demonstrate the rapid and meticulous progression seen in TAVI[14].

Limitations

One limitation of the study is the variability in prevalence rates due to factors like geography, healthcare access, and socioeconomic status. Most research tends to focus on older populations, which may overlook younger adults, especially those with congenital conditions like a bicuspid aortic valve. Additionally, the reliance on retrospective data and limited long- term follow-up can make it difficult to fully understand disease progression and treatment outcomes. Finally, regional differences in access to treatments like TAVR could affect the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

TAVR has managed to save lives, despite the complications discussed in this paper. It has opened a door of hope for the candidates who were not suitable for surgery.

In addition to that, most of the complications have been identified and means of managing these complications are constantly worked on. Enhancements in the device and knowledge about the complications will certainly decrease the risk.

The question here is what will be the future of TAVR in the next centuries?

Will it be suitable for young adults? Will the new enhancements drastically reduce the complications? Will it even replace surgery?

These are all questions that only the future will tell.
