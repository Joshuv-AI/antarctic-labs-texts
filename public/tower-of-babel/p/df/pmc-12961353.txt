Total Bolus Transit Time Predicts Cardiac Dysfunction in Acute Ischemic Stroke and Transient Ischemic Attack Patients: A Retrospective Double Center Study
Authors: Simone Bellavia, Pier Andrea Rizzo, Irene Scala, Jacopo Di Giovanni, Agostino Meduri, Aldobrando Broccolini, Giacomo Della Marca, Simona Scalise, Adriana Paladini, Francesca Colò, Simona Gaudino, Paolo Calabresi, Marcella Caggiula, Giovanni Frisullo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDiagnosing cardioembolic stroke/transient ischemic attack (TIA) rapidly remains a major challenge. Current emergency cardiac assessments often provide insufficient data. This study tests whether bolus tracking, a readily available technique during brain CTA, can be leveraged to quickly identify reduced ejection fraction (EF) and potential cardioembolic etiology in acute stroke patients.Patients and MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed CTA and echocardiography data from acute ischemic stroke/TIA patients across two comprehensive stroke centers. We measured total transit time (TTT) based on aortic attenuation values of 40 Hounsfield Units (T40HU) and 100 HU (T100HU) obtained during routine CTA. ΔT100–T40HU, representing the time difference required for aortic contrast enhancement to increase from 40 to 100 HU, was also calculated as a key parameter. EF was measured by transthoracic echocardiography using the Simpson biplane method.ResultsIn a cohort of 443 patients, we found a statistically significant inverse correlation between EF and T40HU, T100HU, and ΔT100–T40HU. Notably, ΔT100–T40HU demonstrated good discriminatory ability for identifying heart failure (AUROC = 0.88) and poor discriminatory ability for cardioembolic stroke (AUROC = 0.66). Multivariate analysis further confirmed that T40HU, T100HU, and ΔT100–T40HU were independent predictors of both heart failure and cardioembolic stroke based on the TOAST classification criteria.ConclusionBolus tracking during CTA provides a reliable and easily accessible radiological tool for the identification of heart failure and cardioembolic stroke, enabling a faster diagnostic workup and facilitating appropriate selection of further diagnostic investigations within the acute emergency setting.

Introduction

Cardioembolic strokes account for approximately one‐quarter of all ischemic strokes, with atrial fibrillation (AF) being the most frequent cause. However, other cardiac conditions, such as valvular heart disease, infective endocarditis, and heart failure (HF), are also well‐established etiologies [1, 2]. Heart failure is the second most common cardiac disorder associated with cardioembolic stroke. Although it frequently coexists with AF, it is also an independent risk factor, responsible for approximately 9% of all ischemic strokes and associated with a 9%–10% annual risk of recurrence [3, 4]. The combination of ischemic stroke and HF is further linked to increased stroke severity, early mortality, and persistent disability [5]. Consequently, early recognition of HF and related cardioembolic sources is crucial to initiate appropriate treatment and improve long‐term outcomes. Although the diagnosis of HF and AF is often relatively straightforward in outpatient settings [6], these conditions are frequently underdiagnosed during the acute evaluation of stroke patients in the emergency department (ED). This is mainly due to the paroxysmal nature of AF [7] and the limited use of comprehensive echocardiographic assessments in all patients presenting with suspected stroke or transient ischemic attack (TIA). In contrast, atherothrombotic stroke and TIA—arising from extracranial or intracranial atherosclerotic plaques—are typically more readily identified in the ED. This is largely due to the routine use of neck and brain computed tomography angiography (CTA) for the detection of large vessel occlusions and underlying plaques. During CTA, intravenous iodinated contrast media travels through the left ventricle into the ascending aorta and cerebral circulation. The timing of image acquisition following contrast administration is crucial for optimal visualization of vascular anatomy. As such, bolus‐tracking protocols have become standard practice, providing more consistent image quality compared to fixed‐delay techniques [8]. Total bolus Transit Time (TTT), defined as the interval from contrast injection to its appearance in the ascending aorta, is routinely recorded during bolus‐tracking CTA protocols. Prior studies have suggested that TTT may be prolonged in patients with AF associated with diastolic dysfunction [9]. However, to date, no studies have examined the relationship between Left Ventricular Ejection Fraction (LVEF) and TTT, particularly in patients with acute ischemic stroke. In this study, we aimed to investigate the correlation between echocardiography‐derived LVEF and TTT measured during emergency CTA in patients presenting with acute ischemic stroke or TIA. Our objective was to explore the potential of TTT as an indirect marker of cardiac pump dysfunction, potentially enhancing diagnostic accuracy in identifying cardioembolic sources of stroke and TIA.

Methods

The dataset used and/or analyzed during the current study is available from the corresponding author upon reasonable request.

Study Design and Setting

This was a retrospective, double center observational study conducted at two comprehensive stroke centers. All consecutive patients discharged with a diagnosis of ischemic stroke over a 12‐month period were considered for inclusion; the enrollment period was between January 1, 2024, and December 31, 2024. The study was approved by the local Institutional Review Board (Protocol No. 0007643/23‐ID5531) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Given the retrospective and observational nature of the study, the requirement for informed consent was waived by the local ethics committee.

Participants

Patients were eligible if they met the following inclusion criteria: (1) Diagnosis of ischemic stroke or TIA, (2) Assessment with brain and neck CTA in the emergency department within 24 h of symptom onset, (3) Received an in‐hospital transthoracic echocardiographic (TTE) examination, (4) Underwent at least 48 h of continuous electrocardiographic (ECG) monitoring. Patients were excluded if they had: (1) Intracranial hemorrhage, (2) Stroke mimics, (3) Missing CTA parameters or missing LVEF measurement, (4) No echocardiographic follow‐up in cases of in‐hospital diagnosis of reduced LVEF.

Data Sources and Measurement

Data were collected from clinical records and included: demographics, medical history, vascular risk factors, echocardiographic findings, 48‐h ECG monitoring results, and NIH Stroke Scale (NIHSS) scores at admission and discharge. Stroke etiology was classified according to the Trial of Org 10,172 in Acute Stroke Treatment (TOAST) criteria [10], based on clinical, radiological, and laboratory data obtained during hospitalization and at a three‐month outpatient follow‐up.

Imaging Protocol

Brain and neck CTAs were performed in the ED using 64‐slice CT scanners, operated by an experienced technician in the presence of neuroradiologists, according to a standardized protocol. Detailed CT scan parameters are described in the Supporting Informations. Bolus‐tracking was employed to optimize arterial‐phase imaging. TTT was assessed using repeated low‐dose single‐slice acquisitions at the carina level and measured as the time required for the contrast‐enhanced region of interest (ROI) in the ascending aorta (2 cm from its origin) to reach 40 and 100 Hounsfield Units (HU) and defined as T40HU and T100HU, respectively. Given that both T40HU and T100HU can be affected by technical factors, we also calculated the ΔT100–T40HU parameter, defined as the time interval required to reach a contrast enhancement of 100 HU in the region of interest (ROI), starting from 40 HU, and recorded in seconds.

Echocardiography

All patients underwent TTE examination during hospitalization. Echocardiographic studies were performed using clinically approved equipment equipped with a 5‐MHz phased‐array probe. LVEF was measured using the B‐mode Simpson biplane method. Additional data collected included assessment of right heart function, valvular abnormalities (stenosis or insufficiency of the mitral and aortic valves), diastolic dysfunction parameters, and pulmonary systolic pressure. Based on the 2022 heart failure guidelines [11], patients were categorized into two groups: “normal or mildly reduced LVEF” (NoMrEF, > 40%) and “reduced LVEF” (rEF, ≤ 40%). To minimize the risk of misclassifying transient LVEF reductions secondary to neuro‐cardiogenic responses to stroke [12], patients initially identified with rEF during hospitalization underwent follow‐up echocardiography at least 3 months after the index event. Only patients with persistently reduced LVEF (≤ 40%) at follow‐up were retained in the rEF group. Patients whose LVEF improved to > 40% were reclassified into the NoMrEF group.

Statistical Analysis

For comparisons among groups, numerical variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median and interquartile range (IQR), as appropriate. Categorical variables were summarized as absolute numbers (n) and percentages (%). The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to assess the normality of quantitative data. Group comparisons for non‐normally distributed variables were conducted using the Mann–Whitney U test. For categorical variables, the chi‐square test or the Fisher–Freeman–Halton test was applied, as appropriate. For each variable both the p‐value and the standardized mean difference were reported. Correlation analyses were performed using the Spearman rank correlation test. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was conducted to evaluate the diagnostic performance of T40HU, T100HU, and ΔT100–40HU in discriminating patients with HF or cardioembolic stroke from those without. The diagnostic accuracy of each metric was analyzed through the area under the ROC curve (AUROC).

To improve the clinical applicability of ΔT100–40HU, we computed the percentage of confirmed HF diagnoses at each time value and grouped similar values into three discrete groups (Δ‐groups) with an increasing risk of a rEF diagnosis. For each Δ‐group, the average proportion of patients diagnosed with reduced ejection fraction (rEF) was calculated. The AUROC of the Δ‐groups was then compared to that of the continuous ΔT100–40HU variable.

To find independent predictors of HF and cardioembolic etiology, we performed a backward stepwise binary logistic regression analysis, adjusting for possible confounders selected based on statistical significance in univariate analysis (p < 0.05). Additionally, all regression models were adjusted for age and sex, both of which are associated with the occurrence of HF and cardioembolic stroke. The goodness‐of‐fit of the logistic regression model was assessed using the Hosmer–Lemeshow test. A two‐sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. An additional post hoc sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the association between TTT metrics (T40HU, T100HU and ΔT100–40HU) with the occurrence of atrial fibrillation detected after stroke (AFDAS), after removing patients with a diagnosis of AF known before hospital admission. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Graphical visualizations were generated using the ggplot2 package in R version 4.3.1 and SPSS Statistics [13, 14].

Patients with rEF were more frequently male (78.0% vs. 58.9%; p = 0.02) and had greater stroke severity (median NIHSS: 8 vs. 5; p < 0.01). They also had higher rates of mitral valve failure (41.5% vs. 12.9%; p < 0.01) and aortic valve disease (19.5% vs. 8.9%; p = 0.05).

Cardioembolic stroke occurred in 128 patients (28.9%). These patients were older (77 ± 12 vs. 70 ± 14 years; p < 0.01), had more severe stroke (median NIHSS: 7 vs. 4; p < 0.01), and were more likely to have atrial fibrillation (85.9% vs. 3.2%; p < 0.01), mitral valve failure (25.0% vs. 11.7%; p < 0.01), and right heart valve disease (17.2% vs. 6.0%; p < 0.01). Hypercholesterolemia was more common in non‐cardioembolic strokes (42.5% vs. 28.9%; p < 0.01).

Mechanical thrombectomy was performed more frequently in patients with cardioembolic stroke (52.3% vs. 23.8%; p < 0.01), while thrombolysis rates were similar across groups. As expected, LVEF was significantly lower in patients with cardioembolic stroke (52 ± 12 vs. 59 ± 9; p < 0.01). Finally, CTA bolus‐tracking parameters (T40HU, T100HU, ΔT100–T40HU) were significantly delayed in both rEF and cardioembolic subgroups (all p < 0.01) (Table 1).

ROC analysis demonstrated good diagnostic accuracy in identifying rEF, with AUROCs of 0.77 for T40HU and 0.85 for T100HU (Figure 1C). Although predictive value was lower for cardioembolic stroke (TOAST‐2), T40HU and T100HU still showed a significant association (AUROC = 0.69 and 0.71, respectively; p < 0.01) (Figure S2).

The ΔT100–T40HU parameter showed the strongest inverse correlation with LVEF (ρ = −0.54; p < 0.01; Figure 2A) and yielded good diagnostic performance for rEF (AUROC = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.81–0.95; p < 0.01) (Figure 2B). This grouped model maintained a good diagnostic accuracy (AUROC = 0.88; 95% CI: 0.83–0.95; p < 0.01) (Figure 2b).

ΔT100–T40HU as an Independent Predictor of rEF and Cardioembolic Stroke

To confirm the association between ΔT100–T40HU and cardiac dysfunction, we performed a binary logistic regression analysis. Independent predictors of rEF included male sex (aOR 3.31; 95% CI 1.22–8.93; p = 0.02), smoke (aOR 2.62; 95% CI 1.05–6.50; p = 0.04), mitral valve failure (aOR 4.62; 95% CI 2.06–10.34; p < 0.01), and ΔT100–T40HU (aOR 1.60; 95% CI: 1.36–1.88; p < 0.01) (Figure 3A).

In a separate model, age (aOR 1.03; 95% CI 1.01–1.05; p < 0.01), mitral valve failure (aOR 1.99; 95% CI 1.10–3.59; p = 0.02), previous heart failure diagnosis (aOR 2.75; 95% CI 1.37–5.51; p < 0.01), and ΔT100–T40HU (aOR 1.22; 95% CI 1.08–1.39; p < 0.01) were identified as independent predictors of cardioembolic stroke (TOAST‐2) (Figure 3B). Additional models confirmed that T40HU and T100HU were also independently associated with both rEF and cardioembolic stroke (Tables S2, S3).

Finally, we performed a sensitivity analysis to assess the association of bolus tracking metrics with the diagnosis of AFDAS. The results of the univariate analysis are presented in Table S4. In the binary logistic regression model, ΔT100–T40HU (aOR 1.26; 95% CI 1.09–1.45; p < 0.01) and T100HU (aOR 1.07; 95% CI 1.01–1.13; p = 0.04), but not the T40HU, acted as independent predictors of AFDAS (Table S5).

Discussion

In this study we found an inverse correlation between heart ejection fraction and the Total Transit Time, measured through surrogate parameters—the T100HU, T40HU, and ΔT100–T40HU—which appears to be independent predictors of HF and cardioembolic cerebrovascular events. Our findings suggest the implementation of bolus tracking techniques during the emergency CTA, which could provide both morphological information on neck and brain vessels and cardiac output. Although the T40HU and T100HU were already predictors of HF, to minimize possible biases related to technical and procedural variability among exams (needle gauge, arm position, vein caliber), we also considered the ΔT100–T40HU, defined as the time required for enhancement in the ROI to increase from 40 HU to 100 HU. In this regard, concerning the potential influence of right heart disease, we did not observe statistically significant differences in the prevalence of right‐sided valvular disease between the NoMrEF and rEF groups. Additionally, to minimize the risk that the observed reduction in ejection fraction was attributable to stroke‐related transient cardiac dysfunction [12], such as Takotsubo disease, we included in the rEF cohort only patients with a confirmed EF ≤ 40%, as assessed by transthoracic echocardiography at the three‐month follow‐up. Among patients initially classified as rEF, only two showed a slight improvement in left ventricular function at the 3‐month follow‐up and were therefore reclassified as NoMrEF. This finding is consistent with previous reports, where transient cardiac dysfunction has been reported in 0.5%–1.2% of stroke cases [12]. In a previously published study prolonged TTT, as derived from bolus tracking, has been shown to be associated with a significantly higher risk of atrial fibrillation and elevated left atrial pressure [9]. Our study supports this finding and confirms CTA as a useful tool in the identification of possible cardioembolic etiology in patients affected by acute ischemic stroke. While routine TTE in all stroke patients remains controversial, due to cost‐effectiveness concerns and variable diagnostic yield [15], our findings suggest that ΔT100–T40HU may serve as a rapid, non‐invasive marker of reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and cardioembolic stroke available during standard‐of‐care emergency CTA. This may allow clinicians to prioritize cardiologic work‐up—including echocardiography and prolonged cardiac monitoring—supporting a more personalized diagnostic algorithm. Advanced cardiological imaging techniques, such as cardiac CT and cardiac MRI, are increasingly used to search for embolic sources in stroke patients; however, these instruments are unavailable during emergency management in most hospitals. In contrast, CTA with an extended window can detect intracardiac thrombi in the emergency department but does not assess heart ejection fraction [16]. Combining bolus tracking with extended‐window CTA could provide a more comprehensive evaluation of both thrombus presence and cardiac function, enhancing the diagnostic utility of CTA. However, our study has several limitations: first, the small sample size and the retrospective design may limit the statistical power and robustness of the conclusions. Second, the heterogeneity of CTA protocols and potential procedural discrepancies between centers could affect the consistency of imaging data, limiting external validity and generalizability. Third, not all patients admitted to our centers underwent CTA or transthoracic echocardiography, which may have introduced a selection bias into the analyzed cohort. Furthermore, despite our effort to minimize technical and procedural biases, variables such as vein caliber, catheter gauge, and infusion pressure can affect the arrival time of contrast media, necessitating strict protocols standardization to achieve reproducible results. Therefore, a prospective, multicenter study with standardized imaging protocols and complete cardiac assessments would be essential to validate and expand our results.

In conclusion, bolus tracking techniques, including the ΔT100–T40HU parameter, may provide additional radiological information associated with HF and cardioembolic stroke. Although their discriminative performance is limited, these measures could serve as adjunctive markers in the early stratification of patients with acute ischemic stroke, potentially supporting the identification of individuals who may benefit from further cardiological assessment. Such evaluation may include transthoracic and transesophageal echocardiography, prolonged cardiac monitoring, and in selected cases, loop recorder implantation. In this context, CTA—routinely employed in the emergency setting to assess vascular pathology—may also provide valuable supplementary insights when a cardioembolic etiology is suspected, possibly facilitating a more streamlined diagnostic workflow and more efficient use of healthcare resources.
