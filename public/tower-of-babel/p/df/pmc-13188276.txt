Efficacy of video laryngoscopy versus direct laryngoscopy for critically Ill adults undergoing tracheal intubation: a trial sequential analysis and meta-analysis
Authors: Zhen Wang, Zhu Luo, Mingkun Yan, Jingyuan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPrevious meta-analyses have yielded conflicting conclusions about video laryngoscopy (VL) efficacy in critically ill adults, often including heterogeneous populations (e.g., trainees) or non-emergency settings. This study addresses a critical knowledge gap by focusing exclusively on experienced operators in emergency intubations.MethodsWe updated our systematic review to include 18 RCTs (5,097 patients) published up to January 2026, exceeding prior meta-analyses (e.g., 12 RCTs in 2025). Novel subgroup analyses explored settings (ICU/ED/prehospital) and neuromuscular blockade (NMBAs) use. Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) was incorporated to assess evidence certainty.ResultsVideo laryngoscopy (VL) was associated with a significantly higher first-attempt success rate compared to direct laryngoscopy (DL) (risk ratio [RR] = 1.09; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.01–1.17; p < 0.0001), with substantial heterogeneity (I² = 88%; 18 trials). The benefit appeared more pronounced in the ICU and ED settings. No significant differences were observed in intubation time (MD=-2.38, 95% CI [-5.86, 1.11]), mortality (RR=0.97, 95% CI [0.84, 1.11]), or hospital stay (MD=-0.17, 95% CI [-2.04, 1.71]). Subgroup analyses revealed greater VL efficacy with routine neuromuscular blockade use (RR = 1.11, P = 0.03) The trial sequential analysis indicated insufficient sample size for definitive conclusions.ConclusionVL enhances first-pass success in experienced hands, especially in ICUs. Larger trials are needed to confirm broader applicability. This study advances prior work by focusing on high-acuity settings and rigorous methodologic standards.Trial registrationThis review is registered with PROSPERO (http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO, CRD42023458066).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12871-026-03788-x.Keywords: Airway management, Video laryngoscopy, Tracheal intubation, Ccritical care, ICU, ED

Introduction

Video laryngoscopy (VL) has become a recommended technique for tracheal intubation due to its demonstrated advantages over direct laryngoscopy (DL), including superior glottic visualization, higher first-attempt success rates, and a reduced complication profile [1]. A 2022 meta-analysis published in the British Journal of Anaesthesia reinforced this view, concluding that VL reduces failed intubation attempts and the incidence of hypoxemia in adults [2]. However, a 2023 Bayesian network meta-analysis reported no significant difference in overall success rates between VL and DL specifically in critically ill patients, highlighting an area of ongoing debate [3]. These conflicting conclusions highlight the importance of identifying the specific conditions under which VL provides the greatest benefit. For instance, a 2023 Bayesian network meta-analysis found that channeled VL devices (e.g., King Vision) underperformed compared to both non-channeled VL and DL, indicating that the efficacy of VL may be context-dependent and potentially less pronounced outside controlled settings like operating rooms [3]. In line with a comprehensive appraisal of the evidence, the 2025 DAS guidelines provide unified recommendations, advocating for VL as the primary tool for managing unanticipated difficult intubation across all clinical environments to reduce practice variability [4].

The urgency and high risks inherent in managing critically ill patients requiring emergency intubation (in the ED, ICU, or prehospital settings)—such as hypoxia and cardiac arrest [5–7]—make the first intubation attempt critical. Multiple attempts are strongly associated with an increased risk of adverse events [8–10], even if a direct causal link to mortality has not been conclusively established [11]. Consequently, first-pass success remains a cornerstone quality metric in airway management [12, 13]. If VL can consistently demonstrate superiority in this high-stakes population, its adoption could significantly improve patient outcomes and survival rates.

While prior meta-analyses (e.g., Hansel et al., [2]; Aziz et al., [14]) have explored VL in mixed populations, none have exclusively focused on experienced operators in emergency intubations—a critical gap highlighted by the DAS 2025 guidelines. Our study addresses this on restricting inclusion to RCTs with experienced operators (> 50 intubations/year), conducting subgroup analyses by clinical setting (ICU/ED/prehospital) and integrating TSA to contextualize evidence certainty.This article aims to synthesize current evidence and provide clear guidance on the use of video laryngoscopy for tracheal intubation in critically ill patients requiring emergency airway management.

Materials and methods

Our review is registered with PROSPERO (http://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO, CRD42023458066).

Search strategy

This review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines [15]. Two investigators (Z.W. and Z.L.) independently conducted a systematic literature search referring to MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science and CENTRAL in 2026/01/05. The primary outcome was the first-attempt success rate of tracheal intubation, and the secondary outcomes were the time needed for successful intubation, in-hospital mortality and length of hospital stay. These outcomes align with the DAS 2025 guidelines, which prioritize first-attempt success and continuous monitoring as key indicators for safe airway management.

A systematic search was conducted across four electronic databases (MEDLINE, EMBASE, Web of Science, and CENTRAL) from inception to January 5, 2026. The search strategy was designed to encompass three core concepts: (1) the intubation devices (Video Laryngoscopy​ and Direct Laryngoscopy), (2) the clinical setting (Critical Care, Emergency Medicine, Prehospital Care), and (3) the patient population (Critically Ill​ adults). These concepts were combined using the Boolean operator “AND”. Within each concept, relevant terms and their synonyms were combined using “OR”. Search terms included controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms in MEDLINE) and free-text words.

Risk of bias

We assessed the methodological quality of the included randomized controlled trials using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool, RoB 2 (revised 2019) [16],. We adapted the Cochrane RoB 2 tool to include emergency-specific criteria (e.g., blinding feasibility in critical care settings). Inter-rater agreement (κ = 0.82) exceeded prior reviews.The overall risk of bias was classified as “Low” only if all domains were rated as low risk; “Some concerns” if one or more domains raised some concerns but none were high risk; and “High” if any domain was judged as high risk. In practice, two reviewers independently performed the assessment using the official RoB 2 Excel tool, which automates the judgment process and generates summary graphs and tables. Disagreements were resolved through discussion or by a third researcher, and the results are presented in the “Results” section.

Data collection and analysis

We considered patients with operation set in ED, ICU or prehospital as critically ill patients who needed emergent intubation. The primary outcome is the first-attempt success rate. The secondary outcomes are time to intubation, in-hospital death and length of hospital stay. We also notice that different definitions of time to intubation have been given by different articles. After discussion, we defined the duration of intubation by the process from the opening of the mouth to the occurrence of the first CO2 measurement. Two researchers extracted data including year of publication, country, sample size, first-attempt success rate, duration of intubation, length of hospital stay and in-hospital mortality, level of experience of the operator, type of VL, setting of intubations, and presence or absence of neuromuscular blocking medication administered (NMBAs).

For dichotomous outcomes (e.g., first-attempt success, mortality), we calculated the Risk Ratio (RR)​ with 95% confidence interval (CI). For continuous outcomes (e.g., time to intubation, length of stay), we calculated the Mean Difference (MD)​ with 95% CI. When studies reported continuous outcomes as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) or ranges, we estimated the mean and standard deviation using the methods described by Wan et al. [17] and Luo et al. [18]. Specifically, we applied the Luo method when median, minimum, and maximum values were available, and the Wan method when median and IQR were reported, as this conversion was required for 5 of the 9 included studies. These methods provide optimal estimates of the sample mean and standard deviation from commonly reported quantiles in clinical studies.

All meta-analyses were performed using the random-effects model​ to account for clinical and methodological heterogeneity among studies. The generic inverse variance method was used for pooling. For dichotomous outcomes, the random-effects model was implemented using the Mantel-Haenszel method​ under the DerSimonian and Laird estimator for the between-study variance (τ²). Given the substantial heterogeneity anticipated and observed, we also applied the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) adjustment​ to the 95% CIs for the primary outcome as a sensitivity analysis to provide more conservative and robust interval estimates.

Heterogeneity was assessed using the Cochrane’s Q statistic​ (with a significance level of p < 0.10) and quantified using the I² statistic. I² values of 25%, 50%, and 75% were interpreted as indicating low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. We also calculated the prediction interval to estimate the distribution of true effects in a new study.

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we performed several sensitivity analyses: (1) leave-one-out analysis​ to assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled estimate; (2) analysis restricted to studies with low risk of bias; and (3) comparison of results using HKSJ-adjusted CIs​ versus standard DerSimonian-Laird estimates. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on clinical setting (ICU vs. ED), neuromuscular blocking agent use, and VL blade type. These subgroup analyses were pre-specified in our PROSPERO protocol.

Publication bias was assessed using funnel plots​ and Egger’s test​ when at least 10 studies were available for a given outcome. Asymmetry in the funnel plot or a significant Egger’s test (p < 0.10) was considered indicative of potential publication bias.

We performed Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA)​ to adjust for random errors due to repeated significance testing and to estimate the required information size (RIS). TSA was conducted using the TSA software (version 0.9.5.10 Beta) from the Copenhagen Trial Unit. We calculated the RIS based on a type I error of 5%, type II error of 20% (power = 80%), and the observed heterogeneity (I²) from our meta-analysis. The monitoring boundaries were constructed using the O’Brien-Fleming alpha-spending function.

Statistical analyses were performed using Review Manager (RevMan) version 5.4.1​ (The Cochrane Collaboration, 2020) and R software (version 4.3.1)​ with the ‘meta’ package for HKSJ adjustment. Trial sequential analysis was performed using Trial Sequential Analysis software (version 0.9.5.10 Beta). The level of certainty of evidence is assessed by GRADEpro GDT according to the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) method [19]. The GRADE evidence profile (Summary of Findings table) is presented in Supplement 2. All statistical tests were two-sided, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant, except for heterogeneity tests where p < 0.10 was used.

Three subgroup analyses are organized. The criteria for grouping are the setting of intubation, types of VL device and whether the use of neuromuscular blocking medication is administered (NMBAs). There were a total of three groups in the setting, which included ED, ICU and prehospital. Five groups, also shown as McGrath MAC, C-MAC, Glidescope, King Vision and mixed types, were included in the second subgroup analysis. The NMBAs group is divided into all and most, selective and none. These subgroup analyses were pre-specified in our PROSPERO protocol​ to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. No formal adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied, as these analyses were exploratory in nature and aimed at generating hypotheses rather than testing definitive conclusions.

Results

Study selection

The procedure of study selection is shown in a PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). A total of 165 records from MEDLINE, 70 from Web of Science, 338 from EMBASE, and 155 from CENTRAL were included. The total number of first searches is 728. We used Endnote to exclude duplicate articles by the number of 286. After that, we gave a quick screen into titles and abstracts, which resulted in the exclusion of 375 articles for reasons like stimulated studies, irrelevant studies, pediatric studies and studies aimed at training. A total of 67 articles were reviewed for eligibility by full text and we excluded 49 of them. 11 of them were excluded because their type was not an RCT, 15 because there was no comparison of VL and DL, 17 because the settings were not in ED, ICU or prehospital, 5 because there was no outcome of interest and 1 because the operators were all novices.

Risk of bias within studies

The risk of bias will be presented in Figs. 2. Based on the RoB2 risk of bias assessment of the included randomized controlled trials, the overall risk of bias for most studies was rated as “high risk” or “some concerns.” This is primarily due to the challenges of blinding operators and outcome assessors in emergency tracheal intubation settings within emergency and critical care, which introduces performance and detection bias. Additionally, some studies showed deficiencies in the prospective registration and adherence to pre‑specified outcome reporting protocols. These limitations may influence the accuracy of the effect estimates and, to some extent, reduce the overall certainty of the evidence in this systematic review.

Outcome

First-attempt success rates

By the use of a random effect model, a total of 5097 intubations (2572 VL and 2525 DL) in 18 studies were included in the comparison of first-attempt success rates (Fig. 3). The pooled analysis revealed a point estimate favoring video laryngoscopy (VL) for first-attempt success (RR = 1.09, 95% CI: 1.01–1.17; p < 0.0001; I² = 88%; 18 trials). However, interpretation of this finding requires caution. When adjusted for substantial heterogeneity using the Hartung-Knapp-Sidik-Jonkman (HKSJ) method, the confidence interval widened to 0.99–1.21, encompassing the null effect. Furthermore, Trial Sequential Analysis (TSA) indicated that the cumulative evidence has not yet reached the required information size (estimated required sample: 9,782 vs. current sample: 5,097), suggesting that the available data are insufficient to draw a firm conclusion. Therefore, these results may suggest a potential benefit of VL, but do not constitute conclusive evidence that VL certainly improves first-attempt success rates in this population.

Subgroup analysis of first-attempt success rate

Subgroup by settings

One study is dismissed for the setting of both ED and ICU without giving individual data on two different settings. In the ED setting, the difference was of borderline statistical significance (RR = 1.08, 95% CI: 1.00–1.16; p = 0.05; I² = 72%; 8 trials). The results show difference in the ICU group (RR = 1.19, 95% Cl [1.03, 1.38], P = 0.02). No statistically significant difference was observed in prehospital emergency care (Supplement 4). Statistical heterogeneity remained substantial across all study groups, with particularly high inconsistency observed in the prehospital cohort (I²=96.0%). The emergency department (ED) and intensive care unit (ICU) groups also demonstrated considerable heterogeneity (I²=72.0% and 76.0%, respectively). Notably, the between-group heterogeneity (I²=54.0%) suggests moderate variability in treatment effects across different clinical settings.

Subgroup by NMBAs

One study was excluded due to lack of reporting on pharmacological agent administration. The analysis revealed a statistically significant improvement in outcomes when neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) were administered to all or most patients (RR = 1.11, 95% CI [1.01, 1.22], P = 0.03; Supplement 5). Substantial heterogeneity was observed across NMBA administration strategies: I²=88.0% in the “all/most” group, I²=96.0% in the “selective” group, and I²=76.0% in the “none” group. Notably, between-subgroup heterogeneity was relatively low (I²=16.3%), suggesting that while variability within each administration strategy was considerable, the differences between strategies were more consistent.

Subgroup by VL types

The ‘free-choice VL’ subgroup analysis should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating​ rather than definitive evidence, as it may be confounded by operator preference and experience rather than reflecting true device-specific effects. Analysis of treatment groups revealed that significant improvement was observed exclusively in the free-choice cohort (RR = 1.17, 95% CI [1.06, 1.30]). In contrast, all other intervention groups failed to demonstrate statistically significant treatment effects (Supplement 6). Notably, between-group heterogeneity was negligible (I²=0), indicating consistent relative treatment effects across different intervention approaches.

Time to intubation

15 studies with 4604 intubations (2311 VL and 2293 DL) were included in the comparison of time to intubation. A method offered by Wan and Luo is used to convert median into mean. The pooled mean difference (MD) was − 2.38 s (95% CI: -4.12 to -0.64; p = 0.007; I² = 94%; 9 trials), indicating that video laryngoscopy reduced intubation time by approximately 2.4 s compared to direct laryngoscopy (Supplement 7). Heterogeneity is tested and the results show that I² = 94%. The level of certainty of the evidence is low. The grade evidence profile is attached in Supplement 2.

In-hospital mortality and length of hospital stay

4 studies of 567 patients were included in the analysis of in-hospital mortality and 3 studies of 515 patients revealed length of hospital stay. There was no significant difference in in-hospital mortality between the VL and DL groups (RR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.84–1.11; p = 0.65; I² = 0%; 4 trials). No publication bias was found. The level of certainty of the evidence is moderate.

The MD of the length of hospital stay was − 0.17 [-2.04, 1.71] (Supplement 9) in a randomized model, showing no significant differences. The I² reached 79%, which means that the heterogeneity was high. No publication bias was found. The level of certainty of the evidence is moderate. Discussion

This meta-analysis of 18 randomized trials found that video laryngoscopy (VL) may be associated with a higher first-attempt intubation success rate compared to direct laryngoscopy (DL) (RR = 1.09), although the certainty of evidence was low. The potential benefit must be interpreted in the context of an inconclusive Trial Sequential Analysis and the loss of statistical significance when applying more conservative statistical models (HKSJ adjustment) to account for high heterogeneity. Consequently, while a benefit is plausible, it cannot be stated with certainty based on the current evidence.

The key methodological contribution of this study lies in the systematic exploration and control of the high heterogeneity present in previous research, rather than merely reporting its existence. Anticipating the inherent variability across studies conducted in diverse critical care settings (ED, ICU, and pre-hospital) regarding patient populations, intervention details, and methodological designs, we pre-specified a multi-layered strategy to investigate heterogeneity. First, we performed rigorous subgroup analyses, stratified by clinical setting (ED vs. ICU vs. Pre-hospital), type of VL device, and the routine use of neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs). These analyses were not post hoc but were pre-specified in the study protocol (PROSPERO), aiming to identify specific sources of heterogeneity. For instance, we found that the advantage of VL was more pronounced in the ICU setting (RR = 1.19), suggesting that the severity of patients’ underlying physiological derangements or the experience of the airway management team might be key effect modifiers. Furthermore, we conducted extensive sensitivity analyses, including leave-one-out analysis, an analysis restricted to studies with a low risk of bias, and a comparison of results from random-effects versus fixed-effects models. These analyses consistently demonstrated the robustness of the pooled estimate for our primary outcome (first-attempt success rate), thereby strengthening the reliability of the core conclusions. This meta-analysis incorporates 18 RCTs involving 5,097 patients [38–40]. Compared with recent meta-analyses, our study excluded training-oriented studies or those involving inexperienced operators to better reflect real clinical practice. Elimination of studies using specialized intubation devices (e.g., airway scopes or bougies) is also conducted to enable a more direct comparison between VL and DL.

We conducted trial sequential analysis (TSA) for first-attempt success rates, using incidence data from Hansel’s meta-analysis on general patient intubation as reference. While the Z-curve crossed conventional significance boundaries, it failed to reach the TSA boundary, indicating that the current sample size remains insufficient for definitive conclusions. This suggests the need for additional studies to establish more robust evidence.

However, the lack of VL benefit in prehospital settings contrasts with data from a multicenter trial in German [41], possibly due to environmental variability (e.g., poor lighting, limited space) or operator experience differences [26, 31]. A 2023 Cochrane review [42] similarly noted that VL’s efficacy is context-dependent, emphasizing the need for setting-specific protocols.

Non-channeled VLs (e.g., C-MAC) performed comparably to channeled devices, contrasting with meta-analyses by Aziz et al. [14], who reported higher success with hyperangulated blades (a blade design category characterized by a steep curvature to facilitate viewing around anatomical obstacles, not a manufacturer-specific feature). Our subgroup analysis found no significant difference between non-channeled (e.g., McGrath MAC, C-MAC, GlideScope) and channeled VLs (e.g., King Vision). Notably, highest success rates occurred when operators self-selected devices, potentially reflecting either operator expertise or better matching of devices to individual airway anatomies - though limited sample sizes warrant further investigation.

The superior first-attempt success of VL is consistent with global evidence, including a 2023 American multicenter trial by Prekker et al. [32], which reported a 14.3% absolute increase in success rates with VL in trauma patients. The enhanced glottic visualization afforded by VL likely mitigates challenges posed by physiological derangements (e.g., hypoxia, obesity) common in critically ill patients [43, 44]. Notably, our ICU subgroup results (RR = 1.19) mirror those of the INTUBE study [45], where VL reduced hypoxemia rates during intubation (P < 0.01).

The substantial heterogeneity (I² = 88.4%) observed for the primary outcome of first-attempt success is an important limitation. We acknowledge that this likely reflects, in part, the clinical heterogeneity in illness severity and indication for intubation​ across the included studies. The DAS 2025 guidelines used a Delphi process to achieve consensus despite heterogeneity, suggesting that clinical guidelines can provide pragmatic recommendations even when evidence is variable. As noted by the reviewer, patients intubated in these settings present with a wide spectrum of conditions, from respiratory failure in medical patients to major trauma or cardiac arrest. These differing physiological states (e.g., varying degrees of hypoxia, hypotension, or altered mental status) can significantly impact intubation difficulty and success rates, contributing to the variability in observed effects between trials. Although we attempted to explore this through subgroup analyses by clinical setting, the residual heterogeneity remained high, suggesting that unmeasured patient-level factors are a major driver of the differences.

VL demonstrated greater advantages when neuromuscular blocking agents (NMBAs) were routinely administered (RR = 1.11, 95% CI [1.01, 1.22], P = 0.03). This may relate to NMBA-induced ventilation challenges that complicate DL intubation [46, 47]. However, no significant difference emerged with as-needed NMBA use, and while within-group heterogeneity was high (I²=88.0–96.0%), between-group heterogeneity remained low (I²=16.3%), suggesting no definitive causal relationship between NMBA use and first-attempt success improvement. The DAS 2025 guidelines strongly recommend routine use of NMBAs to facilitate tracheal intubation, as they improve conditions for VL and reduce complications, supporting the subgroup findings here.

The nonsignificant difference in intubation time (MD = − 2.38 s, 95% CI [− 5.86, 1.11]) parallels findings from the DEVICE trial [32] and a Brazil meta-analysis [48], where VL added minimal time despite improved success. This suggests that VL’s procedural benefits (e.g., fewer attempts) may offset its technical complexity [24, 43]. Nevertheless, prolonged intubation attempts (> 30 s) remain a risk factor for adverse events [8], warranting individualized device selection. Although the pooled analysis showed a statistically significant reduction in intubation time with video laryngoscopy (MD -2.38 s, p = 0.007), the clinical relevance of this small difference is uncertain. The wide confidence interval (-4.12 to -0.64 s) and substantial heterogeneity (I² = 94%) suggest that the true effect may vary considerably across different clinical contexts and patient populations. In critically ill patients requiring emergent intubation, a time saving of 2–4 s may not translate into meaningful clinical benefits, particularly when balanced against other factors such as device availability, operator experience, and patient-specific airway characteristics. Future studies should focus on patient-centered outcomes (e.g., hypoxemia, hemodynamic instability) rather than procedural time alone.

The neutral mortality outcomes (RR = 0.97) align with Jiang et al. [40] but contradict a 2024 Lancet Respiratory Medicine meta-analysis [27] linking VL to lower 28-day mortality in septic patients (P = 0.03). This discrepancy may reflect differences in patient populations or post-intubation care [49]. For example, a JAMA study [50] found that VL reduced aspiration rates, yet our analysis lacked data on such secondary outcomes.

Despite the clinical interest in comparing hyperangulated versus conventional angle blades, we did not perform this subgroup analysis due to several methodological constraints. First, only 6 of the 18 included studies (33%) explicitly specified the blade angle used, with the majority either not reporting this information or using a combination of blade types. Second, the small number of studies with clear blade angle classification (n = 6) would result in severely underpowered subgroup comparisons, increasing the risk of type II errors and potentially misleading conclusions. Third, substantial clinical heterogeneity existed across studies in terms of operator experience, patient characteristics, and clinical settings, which would confound any attempt to isolate the independent effect of blade angle. These limitations highlight the need for future trials to prospectively report detailed device specifications, including blade angle, to enable more robust comparisons between different VL configurations.

The new contributions of this study lie in: theoretically reconciling the conflicting conclusions of previous research on “whether video laryngoscopy (VL) is effective,” clarifying that “experienced operators + ICU” is the key to VL’s benefit (first-attempt success rate RR = 1.19, p = 0.02), and revealing that prehospital scenarios may see the visual advantage offset by physiological stress (RR = 0.98); methodologically establishing a gold standard for critical care VL research (Trial Sequential Analysis [TSA] + bias framework + standardized data conversion), paving the way for subsequent randomized controlled trial (RCT) designs; and practically providing a “context-specific decision-making basis” for the DAS 2025 guidelines—prioritizing the use of VL for emergency intubation in ICUs while cautioning its application in prehospital settings pending a balance of equipment and operator proficiency, centering on “first-pass success” as the core safety metric (RR = 1.09) to correct the past overemphasis on “time savings (-2.4 seconds).” In short, this study is not merely an accumulation of data volume but rather a targeted innovation addressing the core problem, building a bridge for the precise application of VL in critical care from “controversy” to “consensus.”

Our meta-analysis suggests that video laryngoscopy may modestly improve first-attempt success rates in critically ill patients requiring emergent intubation (RR 1.09, 95% CI 1.01–1.17). However, this finding should be interpreted with caution due to substantial heterogeneity (I²=88.4%) and the fact that the accrued sample size (n = 5,097) did not reach the required information size of approximately 9,782 patients as determined by trial sequential analysis. The pooled estimate represents an average effect across diverse clinical settings and patient populations, and the true effect may vary considerably depending on operator experience, device type, and patient characteristics.

Limitations

The selection of the blade is actually an important part of the evaluation of various indicators of endotracheal intubation, but this study did not focus on this aspect, and the subsequent study can be carried out again according to the comparison of the blade model to make the results more precise.

Furthermore, while we classified King Vision as a channeled VL device by default, it should be noted that non-channeled blade types do exist within the King Vision product line. This classification ambiguity warrants careful consideration in future analyses, as the inclusion of different blade types without proper differentiation may contribute to heterogeneity in study outcomes. Future research should implement more precise device categorization to account for these technical variations.

While we excluded novice operators, an Anesthesia editorial [51] argues that VL’s learning curve is steeper than often assumed, necessitating simulation-based training. Our analysis lacked standardized criteria for defining and measuring operator proficiency. The absence of objective metrics (e.g., minimum number of successful intubations performed, formal competency assessments) to evaluate operator skill levels represents a significant limitation. This methodological gap may have introduced variability in procedural outcomes, as studies likely employed different thresholds for determining operator competency.

In addition, we can know from the risk of bias that all the articles did not double-blind the participants, which can interfere with the results of the study to some extent.

Fourth, our analysis was limited by the incomplete reporting of blade angle specifications in the included studies.​ As noted in the discussion, only a minority of studies explicitly reported whether hyperangulated or conventional angle blades were used, precluding meaningful subgroup analysis. We strongly recommend that future trials prospectively report detailed device specifications, including blade angle, manufacturer, and model, to enable more robust comparisons between different VL configurations.​ Standardized reporting of airway device characteristics would facilitate future meta-analyses to determine whether specific blade designs confer differential benefits in critically ill patients.

Fifth, the clinical definition of ‘critically ill’ and the specific indications for intubation varied across the included trials. This spectrum of underlying illness severity (e.g., from severe sepsis to cardiac arrest) is a likely contributor to the substantial heterogeneity observed and limits the precision of our pooled estimates.

Finally, the conversion of median to mean values for 5 studies reporting intubation time, while methodologically sound, may introduce some imprecision and is a potential contributor to the high heterogeneity observed for this outcome.

Conclusion

In critically ill adults undergoing emergent tracheal intubation, the pooled data from this meta-analysis are consistent with a potential increase in first-attempt success rate when using video laryngoscopy compared to direct laryngoscopy. However, the definitive superiority of VL remains unproven due to the fragility of the result under robust statistical adjustments and the insufficient sample size according to TSA.​ Future large-scale, definitive trials are needed to confirm or refute this potential benefit.

These findings support the preferential use of video laryngoscopy by trained clinicians​ in acute care settings to maximize the likelihood of first-pass success, consistent with recent international guidelines. Future research should prioritize large-scale, randomized trials that are adequately powered to confirm these findings and to detect differences in patient-centered outcomes such as mortality. Additionally, studies should employ standardized protocols and explicitly report operator experience levels and specific device types to reduce heterogeneity and facilitate clearer interpretations.
