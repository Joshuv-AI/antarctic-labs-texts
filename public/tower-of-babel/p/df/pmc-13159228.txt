Efficacy of aerobic exercise as a combination therapy for depression compared to standardized therapy: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Chengcheng Zhu, Karen M von Deneen, Halijah Binti Ibrahim, Puyan Chi, Mingrui Shao, Weiping Du, Xueying Zhu, Xin Wei, Zeyu Wu, Aiping Chi, Ting Peng, Linfeng Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundUtilizing aerobic exercise prevails as a popular choice for physical activity, serving as the predominant exercise intervention for addressing depression. It is also used as supplementary therapy to standardized treatments, including pharmacotherapy, psychotherapy, and treatment as usual (TAU). Despite this, the therapeutic mechanism of aerobic exercise as an adjunctive therapy, as well as its interaction with standardized treatments, has not been fully elucidated. The primary goal of the current study was to evaluate the effectiveness of aerobic exercise as an adjunct to these standardized treatments in enhancing the antidepressant effect, compared with standardized treatment alone.MethodWe systematically searched randomized controlled trials that compared the efficacy of combining aerobic exercise with standardized therapy against standardized therapy alone in treating depression. The final analysis incorporated 20 trials, with a total of 914 participants. Calculation of the pooled standardized mean difference between aerobic exercise combined therapy and standardized therapy was conducted using a random-effects model.ResultsCompared with standardized therapy alone, aerobic exercise combined therapy demonstrated a significant moderate effect (SMD = -0.72 [-0.96, -0.47], p < 0.00001, I² = 66%). In subgroup analyses, older patients showed a numerically larger effect, but the age subgroup difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.06). In analyses stratified by standardized treatment modality, aerobic exercise combined with pharmacotherapy showed the largest effect (SMD = -0.97 [-1.41, -0.54], p < 0.0001, I² = 78%), and the between-subgroup difference was significant (p = 0.02). In addition to antidepressant effects, aerobic exercise combined therapy was also associated with improvements in other health-related outcomes. Subgroup findings further suggested that both low- and moderate-intensity aerobic exercise were associated with favorable treatment outcomes, with low-intensity exercise showing potential in patients with lower baseline physical fitness or more severe depressive symptoms. Moreover, a higher total exercise dose was generally associated with greater therapeutic benefit.ConclusionsAerobic exercise may be a useful adjunct to standardized treatment for depression and may help improve overall treatment outcomes. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the substantial heterogeneity across studies, the conceptual breadth of “standardized therapy,” and the limited evidence available for several secondary outcomes. Future studies should further clarify the optimal exercise prescription, underlying mechanisms, and long-term effectiveness across different patient subgroups.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12991-026-00650-9.Keywords: Aerobic exercise, Combination therapy, Depression, Systematic review, Meta-analysis

Introduction

Depression and standardized treatments

Depression is a prevalent mental health concern impacting a significant portion of the global population. According to the World Health Organization, [1] around 350 million individuals worldwide are afflicted with depression, accounting for about 4.4% of the total population. Depression has a broad and profound impact on individuals’ health and lives, which not only includes challenges to physical well-being, [2] but also poses a serious threat to other aspects such as quality of life; [3] additionally, research has shown that depression severely impacts the social engagements of many individuals [4]. According to the NICE Guideline (2010), [5] and the APA Practice Guideline (2010), [6] the standardized treatment of depression primarily includes four intervention approaches: pharmacological therapy, psychological therapy, physical interventions, and social support, which are applicable to individuals with varying severity levels and personal characteristics. Among these, pharmacological therapy, psychological therapy, and physical interventions are widely applied in the standardized treatment of depression due to their established implementation protocols and broad applicability. Studies have reported that their therapeutic effects are generally comparable [7–10]. However, data suggest that just 55% of patients opt for standardized treatment, [11] with only 30% of individuals attaining complete remission within a 12-week period when utilizing initial antidepressant medications.[12]. Despite there being several possible explanations for this limited therapeutic effect, maladaptive neuroplasticity emerges as a plausible candidate. Cognitive symptoms and cognitive dysfunction are common features of depression and contribute substantially to its treatment difficulty and chronicity. Rather than necessarily reflecting overt neurological disease, these impairments may be associated with altered brain function and maladaptive neuroplastic processes [13]. Neuroplasticity may therefore represent one of the physiological mechanisms underlying depression treatment. The concept of neuroplasticity pertains to the brain’s ability to undergo structural and functional alterations in order to adapt to internal and external stimuli [14]. It is noteworthy that neuroplasticity exhibits different characteristics, contingent upon the environmental factors that have shaped its development. In favorable conditions, it shows adaptability, whereas in adverse environments, it tends to display maladaptive traits. In other words, the restoration of adaptive brain function may require not only pharmacological treatment or other forms of stimulation, but also supportive environmental conditions that facilitate beneficial neuroplastic change. Antidepressant medications, for example, have been proven to elevate neuroplasticity substrates, including neurotransmitters and neurotrophic factors, thereby supporting the reorganization of neuronal links [14]. Nevertheless, this process in itself does not generate antidepressant effects; instead, it heightens the capacity of environmental factors to instigate modifications in neuronal links [14, 15]. Therefore, effective antidepressant effects may require appropriate environmental conditions to induce better neural plasticity.

Aerobic exercise as a treatment modality

Exercise has been recommended by most depression treatment guidelines as a complementary or alternative approach to standardized treatment, [16, 17] with aerobic exercise being the most common form, such as running, walking, and cycling [5, 18]. It is important to note that this refers to planned, structured, repetitive, and purposeful physical activity [19]. A meta-analysis indicated that the antidepressant effect of exercise surpasses that of the control group receiving no treatment [20]. Moreover, this effect is comparable to that of pharmacotherapy, [21] and similar findings have been observed in comparisons with psychotherapy [22]. There are also multiple studies indicating that aerobic exercise training (AET) can improve symptoms of depression [23–27]. Nevertheless, the study by Cooney et al. [20] linked notable exercise benefits to the incorporation of trials with elevated bias risks, a viewpoint also reflected in the research by Krogh et al. [18]. Nevertheless, some treatment guidelines recognize the significant antidepressant effects of exercise (CANMAT) and provide specific recommendations regarding exercise dosage [28]. It is worth noting that multiple studies have shown that aerobic exercise can increase serum brain-derived neurotrophic factor (sBDNF), plasma brain-derived neurotrophic factor (pBDNF), and platelet brain-derived neurotrophic factor (platelet BDNF) levels, [29, 30, 31] thereby providing a potential physiological explanation for the antidepressant effects of aerobic exercise. Preliminary research findings suggest that combining exercise with standardized treatment can provide additional therapeutic benefits. To date, three meta-analyses investigated the effects of exercise and pharmacotherapy on individuals suffering from depression [18, 32, 33]. Kvam et al. [32] investigated the effectiveness of exercise as a supplementary treatment to pharmacotherapy using sensitivity analyses (4 trials, 188 participants), where the findings demonstrated a moderate impact of exercise as an adjunctive therapy (g = −0.50), although the statistical analysis was not significant. Lee et al. [33] conducted a subgroup analysis on standardized therapies (12 trials, 595 participants) and found that combining exercise with pharmacotherapy also confirmed a moderate effect (SMD = −0.68) with statistical significance (p < 0.0001). Additionally, Krogh et al. [18] also showed that combining exercise with pharmacotherapy can enhance treatment outcomes; their study conducted a subgroup analysis, contrasting trials that combined exercise with pharmacotherapy to those that used exercise therapy alone, and they found large effect sizes in both subgroups (effect size = −0.92). Currently, there is only one review [33] providing systematic evidence for the combination of exercise and psychotherapy, while other studies have primarily focused on non-clinical depression samples, as seen in the works of Bernard et al. [34] and Bourbeau et al. [35]. The study conducted by Lee et al. [33] demonstrated a moderate effect (SMD = −0.49) through subgroup analysis when exercise was paired with psychotherapy; however, this outcome did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.19). Conversely, the subgroup analysis by Bourbeau et al. [35] revealed that for patients with elevated levels of depression, exercise combined with behavioral therapy exhibited a moderate antidepressant effect compared to behavioral therapy alone (g = −0.47). In the meta-analysis by Lee et al. [33] the combination of exercise with “treatment as usual,” representing the integration of multiple standardized treatment approaches, demonstrated a moderate effect size (SMD = −0.60). Overall, there is a need for sufficient systematic evidence to prove the efficacy of combining exercise with standard therapies, particularly in the context of exercise combined with psychotherapy and “treatment as usual.” It is also important to note that the trials included in the aforementioned meta-analyses primarily utilized aerobic exercise interventions (such as walking, running, and elliptical training), with some employing resistance training or mixed exercise [36, 37]. These were collectively categorized as “exercise” without further differentiation. Only one study discussed the type of exercise in a subgroup analysis, [33] which found no significant differences between aerobic exercise and other types of exercise (strength/mixed) (p = 0.76), and similar to the overall analysis, exhibited high heterogeneity (I2 = 70%). Given that different forms of exercise induce distinct metabolic changes and have varying impacts on health, [38, 39] along with evidence from independent studies suggesting additional antidepressant effects of aerobic exercise, [40, 41, 42] and the prominent role of aerobic exercise in depression treatment guidelines, it may be necessary to distinguish between types of exercise in systematic research. This would help avoid bias resulting from the conflation of different exercise types, which may influence outcomes.

Potential mechanisms and treatment interactions

Even though there are mechanisms that explain the efficacy of both exercise therapy alone and standardized therapy alone, the combined therapeutic effects of the two have not yet been thoroughly studied, particularly in relation to the combined effects of aerobic exercise. Nevertheless, preliminary evidence suggests that exercise may bolster the efficacy of standardized treatments through mechanisms that are both complementary and overlapping. Given the numerous health benefits of exercise itself, these advantages may complement the effects of standardized therapies. Additionally, there is substantial evidence supporting the positive impact of exercise on physical health and other health indicators in patients with depression, particularly in the following areas: risk factors for cardiovascular and metabolic health, [43, 44] psychosocial well-being indicators like quality of life and daily functioning [45, 46] and various cognitive functions such as attention and working memory [47]. The positive results of cognitive enhancement are seen as a result of exercise-stimulated neural plasticity, as evidenced by Kandola et al. [48] thereby establishing a critical connection between exercise mechanisms and traditional approaches to depression treatment. Furthermore, in animal studies on depression, research found that combining aerobic exercise with pharmacotherapy, compared to pharmacotherapy alone, can increase hippocampal BDNF levels and reduce depression-like behaviors.[49, 50] Similar findings have been observed in human studies; for instance, aerobic exercise has been demonstrated to trigger the secretion of proteins, such as BDNF [51]. Furthermore, aerobic exercise has been observed to facilitate the secretion of BDNF in individuals with depression [29–31]. These findings may suggest a relationship between aerobic exercise and neuroplasticity mechanisms. Based on this, we have reason to believe that combining aerobic exercise with standardized therapies could result in greater antidepressant efficacy. As noted by the study of Guerrera et al. [52] this combined effect may represent a synergistic interaction, wherein the collective impact surpasses the cumulative effects of individual components. Bernard and Carayol [53] also expressed the same view in their study.

Purpose

Initial meta-analysis data indicate that augmenting standardized therapies with exercise can yield additional antidepressant benefits. Specifically, combining exercise with pharmacotherapy, [18, 32, 33] psychotherapy, [54] or “treatment as usual” [33] has shown promise. In this study, “treatment as usual” refers to treatment regimens in which patients, at the time of enrollment, were not receiving a single standardized therapy, but rather a combination of two or more therapeutic approaches drawn from the standard treatment categories, and continued treatment according to this regimen [33]. Building on previous research, the present meta-analysis aimed primarily to evaluate the antidepressant efficacy of aerobic exercise combined with standardized treatment, compared with standardized treatment alone, and to explore whether trial, patient, or treatment characteristics moderated these effects. In addition, the study examined secondary outcomes related to intervention acceptability, including completion and compliance rates, as well as other clinically relevant outcomes, such as quality of life, cognitive function, and functional ability, in order to provide further insight into the potential therapeutic interactions and mechanisms underlying aerobic exercise as an adjunct to standardized treatment.

Methods

The present systematic review was registered in advance with the PROSPERO database (ID: CRD42024554881) on July 4th, 2024, and complied with the PRISMA standard [55].

Participation criteria

Participants

For this study, we included individuals aged 18 years and over with depression or elevated depressive symptoms of any severity. The inclusion criteria encompassed both formally diagnosed patients who met the criteria of the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM-IV), Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM-5), or the International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision (ICD-10), and participants without a formal diagnosis who had elevated depressive symptoms according to validated depression rating scales. This included individuals whose scores reached or exceeded the study-defined cutoff on instruments such as the Beck Depression Inventory (BDI) or the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D). These measures were considered indicators of depressive symptom severity rather than formal diagnostic tools. In addition, patients with comorbid mental health conditions were eligible if depression was the primary treatment focus.

Types of studies

We included published randomized controlled trials (RCTs) reported in English in which participants were randomly assigned to treatment groups. Eligible studies compared aerobic exercise plus standardized treatment (Aex + CT) with standardized treatment alone (CT), thereby allowing evaluation of the adjunctive effect of aerobic exercise.

Interventions

(i)This study followed the NICE Guideline, [5] and the APA Practice Guideline (2010), [6] and classified standardized monotherapy for depression into three categories: pharmacological treatment, psychological therapy, and physical therapy. Pharmacological treatments for depression may include selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) and serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (SNRIs), as well as other antidepressant classes such as tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs), with commonly prescribed medications including sertraline, paroxetine, venlafaxine, duloxetine, and amitriptyline [5, 6]. Psychological interventions mainly involve cognitive behavioral therapy (CBT), interpersonal therapy (IPT), and mindfulness-based cognitive therapy (MBCT), which have been shown to be effective across various patient populations [10]. Physical therapies include repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), which are recommended in treatment-resistant or severe cases [56, 57]. As social support interventions are difficult to quantify and have limited clinical standardization in current guidelines, they are not included in the present study. In the present review, these interventions were grouped under the overarching label of “standardized therapy” because each represented an established non-exercise treatment context for depression against which the adjunctive effect of aerobic exercise could be evaluated. However, these modalities differ substantially in mechanism, intensity, and clinical context; therefore, they were not assumed to be equivalent, and pooled findings across them were interpreted cautiously and further examined in subgroup analyses by treatment modality.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of this study was depressive symptoms, assessed as a continuous post-intervention variable. Because observer-rated measures of depression generally provide greater objectivity and more standardized scoring procedures than self-rated measures, they were considered more appropriate for reflecting depression severity. Therefore, when a trial used multiple assessment methods, including observer-rated scales such as the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAM-D) and self-rated scales such as the Beck Depression Inventory-II (BDI-II), priority was given to the observer-rated outcome.

Secondary outcomes

The secondary objectives of this study were to evaluate the acceptability and clinically relevant outcomes of the interventions. Acceptability outcomes included completion rate (the proportion of participants who completed the intervention), dropout rate (the proportion of participants who withdrew prematurely), treatment adherence (the extent to which participants followed the treatment protocol), and side effects or adverse events (any negative health effects related to the intervention).

In addition, this study assessed other clinically relevant outcomes associated with the intervention, including remission and response rates, physical health (e.g., cardiovascular endurance and muscular strength), cognitive function (e.g., memory, attention, and other cognitive abilities), quality of life (participants’ well-being and satisfaction in daily life), and follow-up outcomes evaluating the sustainability of treatment effects after the intervention.

Search strategy

Two independent authors (ZC, CA) conducted searches on Pubmed, Embase, Web of Science, and the Cochrane Library from database inception to July 4th, 2024 to identify randomized controlled trials. An additional search was performed on August 1 st, 2025 to encompass relevant studies published between the two search dates. To maximize the inclusion of potential studies, a broad range of relevant terms was employed in the search to capture all related articles. Two authors (ZC, CA) independently assessed the titles and abstracts of the remaining articles after removing duplicates. The full texts of the remaining studies were independently reviewed by two authors (ZC, CA) to determine the final inclusion. All disagreements between the two authors were resolved with the involvement of a third author (KMD).

Data extraction

Two independent reviews (ZC & CA) utilized an extraction template to gather data on participant characteristics (inclusive of subjects, age, sex, and severity), study design details (such as intervention duration, treatment arms, depression measurement tools, and control conditions), as well as attributes of both standardized treatment (including type, dosage, and duration) and aerobic exercise therapy (including intensity, format, and duration). All disagreements between the two authors were resolved with the involvement of a third author (KMD).

Risk of bias assessment

The studies ultimately included were assessed for overall risk of bias (i.e., low risk, unclear risk, or high risk) using the Cochrane Collaboration’s tool. The assessment covered the following items: sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessors, incomplete outcome data, selective outcome reporting, funding, and other biases [59]. Due to the inherent challenges of blinding participants in exercise interventions, as acknowledged in a previous Cochrane review, [20] because of the inherent characteristics of exercise interventions, it is difficult for participants to be unaware of their group assignment, making it challenging for most trials to achieve participant blinding. Therefore, following previous research, [33] this study assessed overall risk based on three key criteria: allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessment, and incomplete outcome data. If all three criteria were judged as low risk, the overall risk was classified as low. If any particular criterion was judged as high risk, the overall risk was classified as high. Similarly, if any one criterion was unclear, the overall risk was classified as unclear. If both were unclear and high risk was present, the overall risk was classified as high.

Data merging and analyses

Meta-analyses

A random-effects meta-analysis was performed because substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity was anticipated across studies. Standardized mean differences (SMDs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were used as the effect size measures for comparisons between the intervention and control groups. Risk of bias was assessed using Review Manager 5.3, and the meta-analysis was conducted using Stata SE 11.0.

Because all included studies were randomized controlled trials (RCTs), baseline characteristics were assumed to be broadly balanced between groups. In addition, most studies reported no significant baseline differences in depression scores between the intervention and control groups, although some did not provide complete pre-intervention data. Therefore, this meta-analysis was based on post-intervention mean scores (M ± SD) and sample sizes from the aerobic exercise combined therapy group and the standardized therapy group, from which SMDs and heterogeneity estimates were calculated. The interpretation of SMDs followed Cohen’s (1988) guidelines [60], with values of 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 representing small, medium, and large effects, respectively.

When studies lacked data required for this review, the following approaches were used to obtain the necessary information: (1) contacting the authors, (2) extracting data from published meta-analyses, and (3) calculating values from relevant data reported in the original text. In trials including multiple exercise or control groups, eligible data were extracted in accordance with the guidance of Higgins and Green in the 2011 Cochrane Handbook [61] to construct a single pairwise comparison.

Sensitivity and subgroup analysis

To assess the stability of the study’s results across different types of trials, we conducted a sensitivity analysis. Sensitivity analysis was conducted by sequentially excluding each included study. Publication bias was first assessed using a funnel plot, [62] followed by Egger’s test. When p < 0.05, the trim-and-fill method was employed to further evaluate the impact of publication bias on the pooled results. Subgroup analysis was conducted to evaluate the impact of various factors on treatment outcomes, including study design elements such as the therapeutic environment and the form of standardized therapy, as well as participants’ baseline characteristics (mild-moderate or severe, average age) and the design of the exercise intervention (form, supervised or not). We also conducted subgroup analyses based on the intensity of aerobic exercise (light, moderate, or vigorous) and the total exercise dose (whether it met the recommended exercise dosage). Given the variation in metrics used across included trials to assess exercise intensity (e.g., %VO₂max, %HRmax, METs), intensity classification in this study was based on the guidelines of the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) [63]. For the classification of total exercise dose and whether it met the recommended therapeutic level, calculations were made according to the CANMAT guidelines [28]. Chi-squared tests were utilized to determine subgroup effects, where the significance level of the difference between groups was established at 0.05, consistent with the threshold applied in the primary analyses. No formal correction for multiple testing was applied to the subgroup analyses. Therefore, these analyses should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory.

Heterogeneity analyses

This study assessed statistical heterogeneity using Higgin’s I2 statistic, where guidelines for interpreting heterogeneity were based on specific thresholds: with considerable heterogeneity indicated when I² > 75%, with substantial heterogeneity indicated when 50% < I² ≤ 75%, and with limited heterogeneity indicated when I² < 40%.

Supplementary analysis

In the analysis of this portion of the data, the primary focus was on the secondary outcomes of this study, such as completion rates across different groups, adherence to exercise therapy and traditional therapy, as well as improvements in various psychological and physiological indicators of the patients. We provided a descriptive summary of these data and performed statistical analyses using SPSS software (version 28.0).

Search results

The initial search identified 9981 studies, with this number decreasing to 5614 after duplicates were eliminated. After reading and screening the titles and abstracts, a total of 41 articles remained. After a thorough review of the full texts, we excluded articles that did not meet the inclusion criteria, ultimately retaining 20 articles for formal inclusion in the study. Study design

We included a total of 20 studies, the majority of which employed a 2-arm design (n = 13, 65%), while the remaining studies used a 3-arm design (n = 7, 35%). Over half of the studies implemented interventions lasting at least 9 weeks (55%), with an average intervention duration of 9.89 ± 5.92 weeks. The intervention duration was fixed in all studies except for two studies, [45, 64] where the duration was based on the patients’ length of hospital stay. Half of the trials were executed in inpatient settings (n = 10, 50%), while 9 trials took place in outpatient settings (45%). Only one trial [65] was conducted in a mixed setting, with 55% of patients being outpatients and 45% were inpatients. Three studies (15%) conducted follow-up evaluations to examine the sustainability of treatment outcomes from 1 to 6 months after intervention [65–67].

Subjects

The meta-analyses comprised 914 subjects in total. The aerobic exercise combined therapy group (Aex + CT) included 477 participants, while the standardized therapy group (CT) included 437 participants. The mean age of the patients was 46.88 ± 16.29 years, with 85% of the patients being female. Nearly half of the patients exhibited severe symptoms at baseline, according to scale test results (n = 456, 49.89%). All participants exhibited varying levels of clinical depressive symptoms at the time of inclusion.

CT treatment-arms

Among the included trials, half employed pharmacotherapy as the standardized treatment (n = 10, 50%). Four studies utilized specific antidepressants [40, 68, 69,70] whereas the other six studies allowed the utilization of diverse antidepressants. Studies generally lacked detailed descriptions of medication dosing regimens, with the majority indicating medication usage at ‘therapeutic/adequate doses’ as per established guidelines. [42, 68, 69, 71, 72] Due to strict privacy legislation, Legrand and Neff [73] were unable to access medical records and could only speculate about adequate medication dosages. One study [72] employed combination pharmacotherapy, with medications administered 9 to 15 months prior to the study’s start, dose requirements were specified for tricyclic antidepressants, SSRIs, and SNRIs. In the study by Danielsson et al. [54] to compare different antidepressants, the recommended dosage of imipramine (150 mg/day) was standardized, and corresponding imipramine-equivalent doses were calculated using equivalent factors for other medications. Belvederi Murri et al. [69] reported that all patients initially received 50 mg of sertraline, with 43% of patients in the control group and 17% in the exercise group having their dosage increased by the end of the study. In the investigation conducted by Siqueira et al. [70] the sertraline dosage was capped at 50–100 mg/day. At the trial’s conclusion, the exercise group was administered a reduced sertraline dose in comparison to the control group, albeit without a significant association with HAM-D scores.

Two studies employed psychotherapy as a form of standardized treatment [67, 74]. One study employed cognitive behavioral therapy, [74] and the other one implemented behavioral activation therapy [67]. In the trial by Euteneuer et al. [66] the exercise group and control group each received one of two cognitive-behavioral therapy treatments (CBT-E and CBT-C). CBT interventions were both grounded in a standardized CBT manual and structured according to the typical phases utilized in CBT. CBT-E incorporated physical activity into the CBT framework, while CBT-C focused on behavioral activation, emphasizing pleasurable experiences without significantly increasing physical activity. In the trial by Szuhany and Otto, [67] behavioral activation (BA) combined with aerobic exercise and BA combined with light stretching were used as interventions. The behavioral activation was derived from the approach outlined by Lejuez et al. [75] The two trials included participants who had stable medication doses for a minimum of 2 and 8 weeks, respectively, before being enrolled in the study, and both attended a minimum of 8 sessions, meeting the therapeutic effectiveness criteria outlined in the CANMAT guidelines [76].

Eight trials were categorized as ‘treatment as usual,’ encompassing two or more treatment modalities. Four trials combined pharmacotherapy with psychotherapy, [65, 66, 77, 78] three trials combined pharmacotherapy with electroconvulsive therapy (ECT), [45, 67, 79] and one trial combined pharmacotherapy with sleep deprivation therapy [80]. Of the eight trials conducted under ‘treatment as usual,’ seven were carried out in an inpatient setting, [45, 64, 66, 77, 78, 79, 80] with the exception of a single trial, which comprised of 45% inpatients and 55% outpatients [65]. Three studies did not specify the pharmacotherapy regimen [65, 77, 78]. Schuch et al. [64] reported no significant group or time effects in the comparison of imipramine-equivalent doses, with the mean doses at discharge being 114.40 ± 25.1 mg in the exercise group and 195.32 ± 27.7 mg in the control group. Among the three trials combining pharmacotherapy with ECT, only one [79] described the medication dose (40 mg/day of citalopram) in conjunction with ECT sessions scheduled thrice weekly, while the other two [45, 64] did not provide detailed information on the medication dosage and ECT treatment.

In summary, more than half of the CT treatments combined standardized therapy with additional interventions (55%), including light stretching or mental health education. Some CT treatments used pharmacotherapy alone (30%), while a minority combined pharmacotherapy with ECT (15%).

Aex + CT treatment-arms

Across the included studies, the Aex + CT arms combined standardized therapy with aerobic exercise, with the CT component corresponding to the treatment regimen described in each trial. Some trials used a 3-arm design (n = 6, 30%), of which three trials included a stand-alone exercise group without any treatment intervention [68, 69, 79]. One trial had a waitlist control group, [74] another trial included a strength training combined with standardized therapy group, [42] and one trial combined basic body awareness therapy (BBAT) with standardized therapy [54]. Since the data required for this study focuses on groups combining aerobic exercise with standardized therapy, data from the above six trials were partially excluded and integrated into the meta-analyses. The majority of studies (n = 15, 75%) conducted physical activity sessions under full supervision and engaged in physical activity at moderate or higher intensity levels (n = 17, 85%), with a minimum of 90 min per week (n = 14, 70%). Of all the studies reviewed, more than half (n = 11, 55%) achieved the prescribed therapeutic exercise dosage for treating depression, which involved engaging in moderate to high-intensity exercise for a minimum of 90 min per week for at least 9 weeks.

Depression outcome measures

The majority of trials assessing depression used clinical rating scales (n = 15, 75%). Among these trials, some employed a combination of the HAM-D clinical rating scale and BDI self-rating scale (n = 6, 30%). Five trials used the HAM-D clinical rating scale alone, [45, 64, 65, 69, 71] while two trials combined the Montgomery–Asberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS) and BDI scale [67, 78]. .Additionally, two trials used the MADRS and Bech–Rafaelsen Melancholia Scale (BRMS) individually [54, 80]. Five trials relied solely on the BDI self-rating scale for depression assessment [40, 41, 73, 74, 77]. Overall, among the studies included in this review, the HAM-D was predominantly utilized among clinical rating scales (n = 11, 55%), whereas the BDI stood out as the predominant self-rating scale (n = 13, 65%).

Risk of biased results

Among the bias risk evaluations, 10 studies were rated as low risk, 5 as high risk, and 5 as unclear risk. Some trials that used pharmacotherapy as the standardized treatment were considered to have an overall high risk of bias (3 out of 10), while the majority of “treatment as usual” trials (5 out of 8) and 40% of the pharmacotherapy trials (4 out of 10) were considered to have an overall low risk of bias. Meta-analysis results

Out of the 20 studies, 12 provided sufficient information to extract post-treatment depression scores (M, SD) and sample sizes (N), allowing for the calculation of SMD and CI. In two studies, [42, 73] the post-treatment data were separated for the control and exercise groups to obtain the post-treatment scores. In three studies, [54, 71, 72] post-treatment scores were derived by subtracting the mean difference score from the baseline score. In one study, [77] only the standard error (SE) was provided, which we chose to convert to standard deviation (SD) to obtain the post-treatment scores. Finally, the post-treatment scores for two additional studies [64, 68] were obtained from previously published meta-analyses. All details concerning the post-treatment values and strategies employed for addressing missing data can be found in Supplementary Table 1.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis was performed by sequentially excluding each included study. The results showed that the overall findings remained statistically significant regardless of which study was excluded, indicating the robustness of the meta-analysis results (Fig. 4). A funnel plot was constructed using the standard error of the standardized mean difference (SE [SMD]) as the vertical axis and SMD as the horizontal axis (Fig. 5). Begg’s test indicated significant publication bias among the included studies (p = 0.003). However, the trim-and-fill method did not identify any missing studies to be imputed (Fig. 6), and the pooled meta-analytic results remained unchanged, suggesting that publication bias did not materially alter the main findings. At the same time, its potential influence should not be ignored. Small-study effects, selective reporting, or unpublished negative studies may still have affected the pooled estimate, and the overall effect size should therefore be interpreted with caution.

Subgroup analysis

In the subgroup analysis, a total of nine subgroups were examined. Most pooled effects showed no influence from subgroup conditions, such as the therapeutic environment (p = 0.76), baseline severity (p = 0.28), or supervised of training (p = 1.00). Nevertheless, there was a significant subgroup effect identified concerning the type of standardized therapy utilized (p = 0.02). In this subgroup, trials using pharmacotherapy (n = 10 trials, 476 patients) exhibited a larger effect size with high heterogeneity (SMD = −0.97, I2 = 78%, p < 0.0001); with psychotherapy (n = 2 trials, 89 patients), the analysis revealed a smaller effect size and zero heterogeneity (SMD = −0.13, I2 = 0%, p = 0.56); it is worth noting that “treatment as usual” exhibited relatively low heterogeneity (I² = 25%) and a moderate effect size (SMD = −0.60, p < 0.00001). Because no formal correction for multiple testing was applied and several subgroup categories included relatively small numbers of trials, these subgroup findings should be interpreted cautiously as exploratory rather than confirmatory, and they should not be used to support causal inferences. Further details of the analysis are presented in Table 3.

The analysis of subgroup effects based on the average age of patients was not significant (p = 0.06). In trials involving adults under 60 years (n = 17 trials, 794 participants), there was a significant moderate effect size (SMD = −0.61, I² = 61%, p < 0.00001), while the subgroup of adults aged 60 and over showed a significantly larger pooled effect (SMD = −1.63, I² = 72%, p = 0.002). No subgroup effect was found based on active exercise control (p = 0.87). For exercise form (group vs. individual; p = 0.61), the subgroup effect was also not significant.

Aerobic exercise served as the primary intervention in this study. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on exercise intensity and total exercise dose. The subgroup analysis based on exercise intensity revealed no statistically significant interaction effect (p = 0.75). More than half of the trials employed moderate-intensity exercise (n = 11 trials, 406 patients), which demonstrated a significant moderate effect size (SMD = −0.68, I² = 60%, p < 0.0001). Fewer trials adopted vigorous-intensity exercise (n = 6 trials, 200 patients), which also yielded a moderate effect size (SMD = −0.73, I² = 78%, p = 0.008). The fewest trials implemented light-intensity exercise (n = 3 trials); however, the number of participants (208 patients) was comparable to that of the vigorous-intensity group, and the results showed a larger effect size with lower heterogeneity (SMD = −0.87, I² = 36%, p < 0.00001).

For subgroups based on the total exercise dose, the criterion for classification was whether the prescribed dose met the recommended threshold for treating depression. More than half of the trials met the recommended dose (n = 11 trials, 471 patients), showing a larger effect size (SMD = −0.85, I² = 71%, p < 0.0001), whereas those that did not meet the recommended dose exhibited a moderate effect size (SMD = −0.61, I² = 63%, p = 0.0002).

Results of secondary outcomes

Acceptability

We first examined the completion rate, and the statistics showed that the average completion rate for the overall sample was 84.04% ± 13.86%. The average completion rate for the combined group Aex + CT-arm was 84.18% ± 14.21%, while the control group CT-arm had a completion rate of 83.83% ± 14.57%. Only one trial [79] did not report a completion rate. A comparison of the overall completion rates between the combined treatment and control groups revealed no significant difference, t (19) =−0.36, p = 0.72. The majority of studies provided reasons for dropout (n = 14 trials, 70%), with four trials reporting no dropouts throughout the study, [40, 41, 45, 78] and only two trials not reporting reasons for dropout [54, 79]. Most trials reported exercise compliance and either directly or indirectly provided compliance rates (n = 15 trials, 75%). However, the criteria for assessing compliance were inconsistent. Two trials only mentioned low compliance without providing data, [65, 77] and three trials did not report exercise compliance at all [40, 74, 80]. In contrast, only three trials reported compliance of standardized treatment, [68, 69, 79] while most did not provide this information. Five studies reported the occurrence of side effects or adverse events (25%), including minor injuries caused by exercise, [54, 68, 69, 73, 74] and minor side effects from antidepressants [68, 69]. Additionally, two studies reported no side effects or adverse events, [64, 80] while the remaining studies did not report such information. Supplementary Table 2 provides additional information on acceptability.

Rates and trajectories of remission/response

Fourteen studies (70%) reported and compared remission rates. Of these, five studies [40, 45, 69, 71, 79] found significant improvement in the aerobic exercise combined treatment group (Aex + CT), (p ≤ 0.001), while two studies reported near-significant improvements (p = 0.058 [42], p = 0.061 [72]), whereas another seven studies reported no significant difference between the two groups (p > 0.05). [54, 64, 66–68, 77, 78] Ten studies (50%) reported and compared response rates. Four of these [42,73,78,80] reported significant improvement in the aerobic exercise combined treatment group (Aex + CT) (p ≤ 0.044), while the other six reported no significant difference between groups (p > 0.05). [54, 64,66,70,72,77]. Three trials reported on the trajectory of symptom reduction: two found that the combined treatment group (Aex + CT) had faster remission times (p = 0.002 [69], p = 0.005 [74]), while one study [68] reported faster response times in the control group (CT-arm) (p = 0.02). However, this study also noted that in the combined treatment group, patients experiencing mild depression demonstrated a tendency towards quicker response rates when a combination of exercise and antidepressants was administered. Supplementary Table 2 provides additional information on Remission and Response.

Physical activity, fitness, and health

Twelve studies (60%) recorded changes in physical activity-related indicators and fitness status before and after intervention. Most of these studies (n = 7, 58%) showed that, compared with the control group (CT), the Aex + CT group demonstrated significantly greater improvements in physical activity-related indicators and fitness status (p ≤ 0.049). Four studies [54, 66, 69, 77] measured changes in physical activity using VO2 max as an indicator. Three studies [65, 70, 78] used VO2 max, Max power output, and VO2-VT2 as indicators, and two studies [67, 74] reported changes using METS. These indicators all reflected varying degrees of improvement in physical activity levels in the combined treatment group. Five studies reported changes in health-related indicators, with three studies [41, 65, 78] finding greater improvements in the combined treatment group (Aex + CT); these included improvements in fat mass, heart rate, HDL, and BMI (p ≤ 0.022). While another two studies [66, 72] also demonstrated improvements in health-related indicators in the combined treatment group, such as BMI, heart rate, blood pressure, HDL, and LDL; nevertheless, the differences between the two groups did not reach statistical significance (p ≥ 0.206). Supplementary Table 3 provides additional information on Physical Activity and Health.

Functioning/quality of life

Four trials used different scales to compare functional changes before and after intervention (Global Assessment of Functioning [GAF], Clinical Global Impression Scale [CGI-S], Work and Social Adjustment Scale [WSAS]). Out of these, two trials [41, 72] reported significantly greater effects in the Aex + CT group (p ≤ 0.033), while the other two trials [54, 67] reported no significant differences between groups (p > 0.05). Additionally, four trials used various measures to assess changes in quality of life before and after intervention (Life Satisfaction Index, World Health Organization Quality of Life Assessment Instrument - Brief version [WHOQOL-BREF], Quality of Life Enjoyment and Satisfaction Questionnaire [Q-LES-Q]). Out of these, two trials [45, 64] found significantly greater effects in the Aex + CT group (p ≤ 0.004), but these effects were only observed in the physical and psychological domains. We did not observe any significant effects in other domains, such as social relationship or environment domains. The other two trials [67, 68] reported improvements in quality of life across all groups, but statistical analysis showed no significant differences between groups (p > 0.05). Additional details are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Affective/cognitive measures

Six studies used different scales to evaluate changes in affective outcomes before and after intervention, assessing indicators such as anxiety, self-esteem, stress, and mental toughness. Four studies [41, 66, 67, 77] reported significantly greater effects in the Aex + CT-arm (p ≤ 0.049), while two studies [54, 68] found no significant differences (p ≥ 0.35). Only one study [66] evaluated changes in cognitive function before and after intervention. Results showed greater benefits for the Aex + CT treatment compared to CT alone, but this was only reflected in working memory (p < 0.001), with no differences observed in other cognitive function measures (alertness, flexibility, verbal change). Additional details are provided in Supplementary Table 3.

Biological markers

Before and after intervention, three studies [71, 74, 79] investigated changes in biomarker levels among patients with depression, all of which reported more significant treatment effects in the Aex + CT group. In the study by Euteneuer et al. [74] compared to the control group (CT), the combined treatment group (Aex + CT) showed significantly higher levels of the anti-inflammatory marker at week 8 (p = 0.005) and week 16 (p = 0.018), and significantly lower levels of C-reactive protein at week 16 (p = 0.007). Salehi et al. [79] reported that plasma brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) levels increased significantly in both the Aex + CT-arm and CT-arm after intervention (p < 0.001). Although the Aex + CT arm showed the greatest increase, the group main effect was not significant (F (2,57) = 1.57, p > 0.05). Over time, plasma BDNF levels increased across all groups, with the Aex + CT arm showing the greatest increase. Silveira et al. [71] reported changes in electroencephalographic mean frequency (EEG MF) after intervention., with the Aex + CT-arm showing a greater increase in total mean frequency (TMF) in the posterior region (p = 0.034) and higher theta mean frequency (θMF) in the posterior region as well (p = 0.051). In contrast, the CT-arm showed higher θMF in the anterior region.

Follow-up data

Three studies reported follow-up information [65–67]. Imboden et al. [66] followed patients for six months after discharge and reassessed all study variables. From baseline to follow-up, there was no significant time-by-group interaction for any of the studied variables, and no between-group differences at follow-up were found for HAM-D, BDI, response, or remission rates. However, the initial short-term improvements were largely maintained in both groups, including depressive symptom severity and several other clinically relevant outcomes. Kruisdijk et al. [65] planned assessments up to 12 months, but the dropout rate had reached 55% by month 6. Because of this substantial attrition, the authors restricted the statistical analysis to the first 3 months of the intervention; therefore, no interpretable follow-up comparison between the Aex + CT and CT arms was available. Szuhany and Otto [67] conducted a 1-month follow-up after treatment. Across the total sample, depressive symptoms, quality of life, functioning, and exercise levels improved significantly over time; however, there was no significant between-group effect or condition-by-time interaction for depression outcomes (MADRS: condition p = 0.36, interaction p = 0.44) or for related clinical measures. At follow-up, 39% of the total sample met criteria for recovery from depressive symptoms, and rates of recovery did not differ significantly between conditions (p = 0.71). Thus, in this study, follow-up findings supported maintenance over time rather than a clear superiority of the Aex + CT arm over the CT arm.

Discussion

Previous meta-analyses [18, 32] have shown that combining exercise with antidepressant medication provides additional benefits for treating depression. We extended these earlier studies by broadening the scope of standalone antidepressant treatment to include standardized therapies, such as medication, “treatment as usual,” and psychotherapy. Furthermore, this study refined the intervention strategy by focusing specifically on aerobic exercise and expanding the number of included trials. Our findings confirm that aerobic exercise combined with standardized therapies produces medium-to-large antidepressant effects compared to standardized therapies alone.

At the same time, these findings should be interpreted in light of substantial between-study heterogeneity (I² = 66%), as well as marked variation in participant characteristics, treatment settings, comparator content, and outcome measurement. These sources of heterogeneity limit both the generalizability of the pooled estimate and the precision of its clinical interpretation. In particular, the category of “standardized therapy” in this review encompassed pharmacotherapy, psychotherapy, and broader treatment-as-usual regimens, which differ considerably in mechanism and clinical context. Accordingly, the pooled overall estimate should be understood as reflecting the average adjunctive effect of aerobic exercise across diverse treatment contexts, rather than a uniform effect within a single, conceptually homogeneous treatment category.

Our findings indicate that aerobic exercise combined with “treatment as usual” produces a significant moderate effect (SMD = −0.60) compared to “treatment as usual” alone, with low heterogeneity (I2 = 25%). Additionally, our study shows that among comparisons between aerobic exercise combined with different standardized therapies versus standardized therapies alone, aerobic exercise combined with medication yields a significant large effect (SMD = −0.97), whereas Lee et al. [33] reported a moderate effect (SMD = −0.68). This discrepancy can be explained by our restriction to aerobic exercise, suggesting that pure aerobic exercise as an adjunct to medication has a larger effect compared to mixed exercise (aerobic, resistance, and others) as an adjunct to medication. Moreover, the combination of aerobic exercise with psychotherapy showed a smaller and non-significant effect compared to psychotherapy alone (SMD = −0.13, p = 0.56). This may be due to the relatively small total sample size for psychotherapy (n = 89, 9.74%). The non-significant difference might also be explained by the fact that some participants were taking antidepressants during psychotherapy. It is recommended that forthcoming studies control for the use of antidepressants to better distinguish the effects of combining aerobic exercise with different types of treatments.

It is worth noting that the consistent antidepressant effect of augmenting aerobic exercise was observed across various treatment settings and baseline severity levels, including patients formally diagnosed with major depressive disorder. Additionally, although older adults (aged 60 and above) showed a numerically larger effect size than adults aged < 60 years, the between-subgroup difference was not statistically significant (p = 0.06). Therefore, this finding should be interpreted cautiously as a non-significant trend rather than firm evidence of greater benefit in older adults. Regarding treatment modalities, aerobic exercise showed additive effects across various standardized treatments, where the effects of different standardized treatment modalities are inconsistent. Notably, when combined with pharmacotherapy, the pooled effect was larger than when combined with psychotherapy or “treatment as usual,” and the subgroup comparison was statistically significant (p = 0.02). This finding suggests that aerobic exercise may have particular adjunctive value in pharmacotherapy-based treatment contexts. However, because this subgroup analysis was exploratory and based on broad treatment categories rather than specific antidepressant molecules or dosing strategies, the result should be interpreted cautiously and viewed as hypothesis-generating rather than mechanistically conclusive.

The format of exercise delivery also played a role. Group-based exercise demonstrated greater antidepressant effects than individual exercise, possibly due to enhanced social interaction and support. Furthermore, partially supervised or unsupervised programs were found to be as effective as fully supervised ones, suggesting flexibility in implementation. Similarly, active control conditions such as stretching or relaxation produced comparable therapeutic effects, supporting the notion that structured physical activity—regardless of intensity or format—can be a valuable component in depression management.

It is notable that the antidepressant effect of aerobic exercise was evident across different intensity levels. While no significant subgroup interaction was observed (p = 0.75), moderate- and high-intensity exercise both produced moderate effect sizes. Interestingly, low-intensity exercise, despite being assessed in fewer trials, showed the largest effect with relatively low heterogeneity, suggesting that even mild physical activity may offer meaningful benefits for depressive symptoms. This finding may be particularly relevant for populations with limited physical capacity, such as older adults or those with comorbid conditions. Subgroup analysis by total exercise dose further revealed a potential dose–response relationship. Trials that met the recommended exercise threshold demonstrated greater antidepressant effects than those that did not, indicating that adequate cumulative exercise volume may be critical for achieving optimal outcomes. However, the high heterogeneity observed in the high-dose group suggests that additional factors—such as program adherence, supervision, or delivery format—may influence treatment effects.

Concerns about combined treatments may arise from the perception that additional therapies could further burden patients, making them less acceptable, particularly in the case of combining exercise therapy with standardized treatments. The preliminary findings from our study suggest that including aerobic exercise alongside standardized treatment has no impact on the acceptability of either intervention. Our primary basis for this view stems from our comparative analysis of the average completion rates between the Aex + CT-arm and CT-arm, which revealed that both were comparable. Due to inconsistencies in data and measurement standards, we did not conduct a formal comparison of exercise compliance and side effects. However, the vast majority of trials reported high levels of exercise compliance, further indicating that aerobic exercise, as an adjunct therapy, has a high level of acceptability. Research has shown that incorporating aerobic exercise therapy into standardized treatment can enhance patients’ adherence to standardized therapies. For instance, the study by Belvederi Murri et al. [69] found that patients in the Aex + CT-arm (93%) exhibited higher adherence rates to the antidepressant medication (sertraline) compared to those in the CT-arm (74%). This finding aligns with previous meta-analysis evidence, [33, 81] indicating a positive correlation between adherence to standardized treatment and completion rates in exercise trials among patients with depression. Future research should focus on adherence to standardized therapies and adverse events as outcomes, further analyzing the impact of combined exercise therapy on the overall acceptability of the interventions.

This study also identified several treatment-related outcomes. Benefiting from the combined effects of aerobic exercise, some studies demonstrated that the Aex + CT-arm showed better health and health-related improvements compared to the CT-arm. Since exercise can also promote physical health and improve related parameters, these improvements in health-related indicators further support the fidelity of aerobic exercise treatment in this study, as well as its ancillary value as an adjunct therapy. Some studies offer initial evidence supporting the advantages of aerobic exercise as a combined therapy in terms of response rates, remission rates, and symptom reduction trajectories. However, it is worth noting that a considerable number of studies found no benefits, attributing this to the absence of statistically significant differences between groups. Overall, the combined treatment group exhibited higher remission and response rates than the standardized treatment group. Only a few studies (n = 2) provided evidence for functional improvements, with one study offering very limited information. Additionally, only two studies provided evidence for improvements in quality of life; however, these studies were authored by the same researchers and utilized the same measurement methods.

Evidence for secondary outcomes should be regarded as preliminary. Although some studies suggested benefits of combined aerobic exercise therapy for remission and response, functional outcomes, quality of life, affective measures, cognition, and biological markers [54, 68], these findings were often based on small numbers of trials, diverse measurement tools, and modest sample sizes. As a result, the comparability of these outcomes across studies was limited, and the corresponding conclusions remain less robust than those for the primary depression outcome.

This caution is particularly relevant for remission, quality of life, cognition, and biomarker outcomes. Remission and response findings were mixed across studies; quality-of-life benefits were supported by only a small number of trials and were not consistent across domains; cognitive evidence was derived from very few studies and was limited to selected domains such as working memory; and biomarker findings were highly heterogeneous in both the biological systems assessed and the methods used. Therefore, these findings should be interpreted as preliminary signals rather than definitive evidence of broad secondary benefits.

Potential therapeutic mechanisms

This work builds on previous research, updating the evidence and perspectives, suggesting that incorporating aerobic exercise can enhance antidepressant effects and improve various patient outcomes. The results align with the concept that engaging in physical activity, and consequently maintaining physical fitness, initiates a series of physiological mechanisms that promote beneficial antidepressant effects [82]. In light of the beneficial effects of aerobic exercise on neurobiological indicators, there is reason to believe that the combined impact of aerobic exercise and standardized therapies could exert overlapping influences on neuroplasticity, a perspective also noted in previous research [33]. Presently, only Salehi et al. [79] examined this correlation. The research findings indicated that while the combination therapy group exhibited a more pronounced antidepressant response and elevated peripheral BDNF levels, no correlation was observed between symptom amelioration and peripheral BDNF levels. As there is limited evidence available, it is unclear if these proposed mechanisms are responsible for the combined therapeutic effect. Another very important factor is that the measurement of neurobiological effects in humans poses challenges, often requiring indirect assessment through peripheral indicators; therefore, we can utilize the results from animal models and perform reverse translation, which may contribute to the study of human depression.

Our meta-analysis results suggest that aerobic exercise combined with standardized therapy (where pharmacotherapy has shown the best results) can improve the treatment outcomes for depression. However, evidence from this study is insufficient to support the existence of a synergistic effect between these two treatments. A synergistic effect refers to a combined effect of multiple individual interventions surpassing the sum effects of individual components [83]. Based on previous research, [84] we need a 2 × 2 factorial design to explore whether a synergistic effect exists. In such a design, participants would be randomly assigned to one of four groups: (1) exercise-only group, (2) standardized therapy-only group, (3) combined exercise and standardized therapy group, and (4) control group. This experimental design would allow us to examine the treatment effects across different groups, especially the comparison between the combined exercise and standardized therapy group and other groups. We could then analyze whether the efficacy of the combined treatment is antagonistic (1 + 1 < 2), additive (1 + 1 = 2), or synergistic (1 + 1 > 2). However, most of the trials included in our study do not have such a design, making it difficult to provide evidence for the presence of a synergistic effect with the combined treatment.

Evidence from this meta-analysis suggests that aerobic exercise, when combined with standardized treatments, particularly pharmacotherapy, offers additional therapeutic benefits. For example, two studies [64, 69] demonstrated that patients in the combined treatment group reduced their use of antidepressants after intervention. This indicates that lower doses of medication may achieve the same therapeutic effects. This finding suggests that when exploring the interaction between exercise therapy and standardized treatments, adjusting the dosage of interventions could help in observing changes in the outcomes. This applies not only to adjustments in the dosage of pharmacotherapy, but also in the dosage of aerobic exercise interventions. In simple terms, we can refer to the study by Danielsson et al. [54] and use the strategy described by Bollini et al. [85] to standardize medication dosage, adjusting drug intake in terms of imipramine-equivalent units. On the other hand, we can refer to Schuch et al. [64] for the exercise intervention design, adjusting the aerobic exercise dosage by total weekly caloric expenditure, accompanied by intensity monitoring. Overall, in combination with a 2 × 2 factorial trial design, this approach would require more complex and rigorous trials to provide stronger evidence for the interaction between these two therapies.

Limitations and methodological considerations

So far, the majority of studies and meta-analytic evidence have primarily focused on comparing the effectiveness of exercise with standardized treatment for depressive disorder. Extending prior work, this systematic review quantified the augmented antidepressant effects of aerobic exercise when utilized alongside standardized therapy. The overall quality of evidence, especially in the psychological therapy trials, was lower because this line of research remains preliminary. Compared with the overall effects reported in earlier meta-analyses on exercise therapy for depressive disorder (e.g., I2 = 81%, Krogh et al. 18; I2 = 70%, Lee et al. [33]), heterogeneity in the present study was somewhat lower but still substantial (I² = 66%). The subgroup analyses suggested that variation in treatment settings and exercise formats may have contributed to this heterogeneity, while standardized treatment modality and aerobic exercise intensity appeared to be important explanatory factors. However, these potential explanatory factors should be interpreted cautiously because subgroup analyses were exploratory.

When interpreting the results of this study, several methodological limitations should be considered. First, most trials did not clearly report patients’ treatment status in sufficient detail during the intervention period, which limited subgroup analyses based on treatment characteristics. Second, medication regimens were often insufficiently described, and dosage information was incomplete or, in some cases, had to be inferred. Therefore, although subgroup analyses showed a larger pooled effect when aerobic exercise was combined with pharmacotherapy than with psychotherapy or treatment as usual, the present meta-analysis could not detect whether augmentation effects differed according to specific antidepressant molecules, drug classes, dosage strategies, or other pharmacological characteristics. Thus, the larger pooled effect observed in the pharmacotherapy subgroup should be interpreted at the level of broad treatment modality rather than as evidence of molecule-specific effects. More broadly, the overarching category of “standardized therapy” combined conceptually distinct interventions that differ substantially in mechanism and clinical context. This choice was appropriate for evaluating the adjunctive role of aerobic exercise across real-world treatment settings, but it also limits the extent to which pooled comparisons can be interpreted as mechanistically uniform.

A further limitation is that not all included participants were formally diagnosed with depression using standardized diagnostic criteria. In some trials, eligibility was based on elevated scores on validated depression rating scales (e.g., BDI or HAM-D), which should be interpreted as proxies for depressive symptom severity rather than as diagnostic confirmation. This may have introduced clinical heterogeneity and may limit the precision and generalizability of the pooled findings. In addition, women accounted for approximately 85% of the pooled sample, which may restrict the generalizability of these findings to male patients and to more sex-balanced clinical populations. Finally, the pooled antidepressant effect should also be interpreted in light of possible publication bias. Although the trim-and-fill procedure did not materially alter the pooled estimate, Begg’s test was significant (p = 0.003), suggesting that small-study effects or selective reporting cannot be excluded.

Evidence from this study is also insufficient to support the notion that combined aerobic exercise therapy has significant long-term effects. Among the included trials, only three provided follow-up information, and the available data mainly supported maintenance of initial improvements over time rather than a clear long-term superiority of combined aerobic exercise therapy over standardized treatment alone. Therefore, the limited follow-up evidence does not allow firm conclusions regarding the durability of the antidepressant effects of combined therapy. In addition, conclusions regarding remission, quality of life, cognition, and biomarkers remain preliminary because these outcomes were reported in relatively few studies and were assessed using heterogeneous measures.

Lastly, in trials where psychotherapy was used as the standard treatment, the combined treatment group did not show significant statistical differences compared with the standardized treatment group. This may be due, first, to the limited number of psychotherapy trials included in this review (n = 2), and second, to potential methodological limitations in these studies. For example, the exercise interventions in both trials were unsupervised and partly based on participant self-report, while symptom severity was assessed using self-reported scales such as the BDI-II. These features may have reduced measurement precision and intervention fidelity, thereby weakening confidence in the corresponding subgroup estimates.

Clinical implications

Current research suggests that patients are more likely to achieve higher levels of symptom remission through the combination of aerobic exercise and standardized treatments, as opposed to standardized treatments alone. Our findings demonstrated large and statistically significant effects when aerobic exercise was combined with pharmacotherapy, and moderate effects when paired with treatment as usual. Although the combination with psychotherapy did not reach statistical significance, it was associated with faster symptom relief and improvements in certain secondary outcomes.

Subgroup analyses based on aerobic exercise parameters revealed additional clinical considerations. While no overall statistically significant differences were observed across intensity levels (light, moderate, or vigorous), distinct patterns emerged. Notably, low-intensity aerobic exercise was associated with relatively greater antidepressant effects compared to higher intensities, despite the limited number of studies in this subgroup. This finding suggests that even light-intensity activity—being more feasible and tolerable for patients with low physical fitness or more severe depressive symptoms—may provide meaningful clinical benefits.

Moderate-intensity aerobic exercise, which was adopted in over half of the included trials, consistently produced stable and favorable outcomes across different treatment settings. Although not showing the highest magnitude of effect, moderate intensity appeared to offer a balance between efficacy, safety, and patient adherence, and may represent a practical and scalable option in routine clinical care. In contrast, vigorous-intensity interventions did not demonstrate superior benefits and were less frequently implemented, possibly due to adherence challenges in clinical populations. In addition to intensity, the total dose of aerobic exercise emerged as a potentially critical factor. Trials with higher cumulative exercise doses—regardless of intensity—tended to report more favorable outcomes, suggesting a possible dose–response relationship. This implies that ensuring sufficient frequency and duration of exercise over time may be more influential than prescribing high-intensity protocols alone.

Considering these findings—alongside the broader physical and psychological benefits of regular aerobic activity and its low risk of adverse effects—it is strongly recommended that clinicians and patients consider integrating aerobic exercise into standardized depression treatments. Exercise intensity and dosage should be guided by established recommendations or standards, and tailored to the individual needs of each patient, taking into account symptom severity, physical fitness, and personal preferences to optimize treatment efficacy and adherence.

Conclusions

This meta-analysis suggests that combining aerobic exercise with standardized treatments may improve depressive outcomes compared with standardized treatments alone, particularly when added to pharmacotherapy or treatment as usual. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because of substantial between-study heterogeneity, the conceptual breadth of the “standardized therapy” category, and the limited evidence available for several secondary outcomes. While the combination with psychotherapy showed limited evidence of added benefit, it may still support faster symptom relief in some cases. Both low- and moderate-intensity aerobic exercise were associated with favorable treatment patterns, but these subgroup findings were exploratory and should not be overinterpreted. Likewise, the apparent association between higher total exercise dose and greater therapeutic benefit should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive evidence of a dose–response effect.

Overall, the present findings support aerobic exercise as a potentially useful adjunctive strategy in depression care, but stronger conclusions regarding mechanisms, optimal prescriptions, and effects on remission, quality of life, cognition, and biomarkers will require larger and more methodologically rigorous trials. Future research should further investigate these mechanisms and the interaction between exercise and standardized therapies, employing 2 × 2 factorial designs and quantifying and adjusting the dosage of both interventions accordingly.
