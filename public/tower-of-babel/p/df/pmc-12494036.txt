Water-flow electric-gating effect on a van der Waals surface
Authors: Hua Kang, Yang Yue, Jiayu Liang, Xuejun Wang, Dingding Jiang, Hangyan Chen, Yunqi Liu, Qinghong Yuan, Dacheng Wei
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Since the early 19th century, electric effects at fluid-solid interfaces have been widely studied, which primarily originate from migration of ions or their interactions with functional groups on solid surfaces. However, the intrinsic electric effect of moving water molecules on a pristine van der Waals surface has seldom been explored. Here, we find a water-flow electric-gating effect on graphene, WSe2, and MoS2 without dangling bonds or functional groups. It is an intrinsic electric effect, different from a traditional ion-reliant or functional group–dependent fluid electrokinetic effect. On the basis of this finding, we develop a water-flow gated transistor (WGT) capable of transducing flow signals down to 600 nanometers per second into electrical signals, achieving a voltage responsivity up to 1.53 × 104 volts per meter-second, about two orders of magnitude higher than existing hydroelectronic devices. The WGTs can serve as a basic unit of hydroelectronics, enabling efficient signal transduction and logical calculation.

INTRODUCTION

Electrokinetics in fluids, as a cutting-edge interdisciplinary field bridging fluid mechanics and electronics, has long been a hot topic within scientific research. Since Quincke’s initial discovery of the streaming potential in 1859 (1), the Helmholtz double-layer model (2, 3), along with the theoretical work of Guggenheim (4) and von Smoluchowski (5), has substantially advanced the understanding of charge distribution at fluid-solid interfaces, fostering the development of electrophoresis (6) and electroosmosis (7). In recent years, various interfacial electric effects associated with water have been explored, including falling droplets (8, 9), waves (10, 11), water evaporation (12, 13), moisture adsorption (14, 15), and continuous water flow (16). The rapid development of water electrokinetic technology has largely extended the capability of energy harvesting as well as information transduction based on electric effects at the flowing water-solid interfaces.

Several mechanisms have been proposed to elucidate the electrokinetic phenomena, including the Coulombic field theory (17), electron drag (18), and pulsating asymmetric ratcheting (19). For instance, when solid materials are immersed in an aqueous electrolyte solution, counterions in the diffusion layer of the electric double layer are transported downstream and form a gradient distribution because of evaporation, resulting in an electric potential difference between the upstream and downstream regions (12). In addition, when a droplet of aqueous solution moves on a solid surface, the migration of the air-water-solid interface triggers the charging and discharging of pseudocapacitance at the front and back ends of the droplet, generating an electric potential difference along the path of the droplet motion (8, 20). Similarly, when water flows over a solid surface rich in oxygen-containing functional groups, charge redistribution at the interface also generates a potential difference along the flow direction (10, 16). Despite remarkable progress, the occurrence of the electrokinetic phenomena relies on the differential ion migration within water or special interactions between flowing water molecules and functional groups on the surface, generating streaming potentials along the flow direction (text S3) (21, 22). However, a clear understanding of the intrinsic electric effects of flowing water on solid surfaces is still absent.

A two-dimensional (2D) material, characterized by atomic-scale thickness, large specific surface area, and high carrier mobility (23), has emerged as an ideal platform for investigating the interfacial electric effects (24). It has an ideal surface without dangling bonds or functional groups, and there is only a van der Waals interaction between water molecules and the solid surface. Here, we demonstrate an intrinsic water-flow electric effect on the 2D material, such as graphene, WSe2, and MoS2. The effect originates from the redistribution of flowing water molecules at the interface, disrupting the charge transfer equilibrium and triggering an additional doping effect. On the basis of this finding, we develop a water-flow gated transistor (WGT) capable of transducing water flow signals into electrical signals, with a flow velocity down to 600 nm s−1 and a voltage responsivity up to 1.53 × 104 V m−1 s, among the highest values reported so far. WGTs can function as a basic unit of hydroelectronics at the interface between the water circuit and the electrical circuit. We build logic circuits by WGTs, achieving efficient signal transduction and calculation.

RESULTS

Intrinsic water-flow electric effect

A microfluidic chip, integrating a microfluidic channel on a field-effect transistor (FET), is shown in Fig. 1A and fig. S1, and the water flow direction is perpendicular to the conducting channel of the FET. The conducting channel consists of 2D materials, such as graphene, MoS2, or WSe2, and a Ag/AgCl reference electrode is embedded within water as a gate electrode. The water flow exhibits laminar characteristics (fig. S2 and text S1). As water flows across the conducting channel of the graphene FET (g-FET), the source-drain current (IDS) can be modulated by adjusting the water flow velocity (v). The variation of IDS can be estimated by ΔIDS/I0, where ΔIDS is defined as IDS minus the initial value I0 (measured when the water remains stationary). With an increase in the water flow velocity, ΔIDS/I0 gradually increases. As the velocity exceeds 32.8 μm s−1, ΔIDS/I0 reaches a stable value of about 16.7% (Fig. 1B and fig. S3). Under continuous water flow on the graphene surface for >25 min, the ΔIDS response remains nearly constant (fig. S4B). As shown in fig. S4C, the g-FET demonstrates a highly reproducible ΔIDS response under identical water flow velocities.

To elucidate the variation mechanism of ΔIDS/I0, transfer curves of g-FET are measured under exposure to water flow with velocities increasing from 0 to 41.1 μm s−1. The Dirac point (VDirac) corresponds to VGS when IDS reaches its minimum. It appears at 100 mV for stationary water (Fig. 1C), which is defined as VDirac,0. As the water flow velocity increases, a consecutive rightward shift of VDirac is observed. The variation of VDirac, denoted as ΔVDirac, is determined by the formula ΔVDirac = VDirac − VDirac,0. Appreciable ΔVDirac responses of about 10 mV is observed upon the velocity of 0.6 μm s−1 and overall 85 mV for 41.1 μm s−1 in Fig. 1E. A charge transfer ΔQ of ~5.2 pC occurs at 0.6 μm s−1 and increases to 73.2 pC at 41.1 μm s−1 (fig. S5 and text S4). These results show that the Fermi level of graphene shifts toward the valence band when water flows (inset in Fig. 1E), indicating a p-type doping effect (text S5) (25).

To explore the universality of the water-flow electric effect, WSe2 and MoS2 featuring van der Waals surfaces are used as channel materials in FETs, and the transfer characteristic curves are illustrated in Fig. 1D and fig. S7. The threshold voltage (Vth) denotes the minimum gate-to-source voltage (VGS) required to form a conducting channel between the source and drain electrodes of a FET. The shift of Vth (ΔVth) derives from the equation ΔVth = Vth − Vth,0, where Vth,0 represents the value when water remains stationary. With the increase in flow velocity, ΔVth gradually rises for both WSe2-FET and MoS2-FET, reaching 111 and 58 mV at 41.1 μm s−1, respectively (Fig. 1E). The positive shift of Vth further validates the consistent p-type doping effect on van der Waals surfaces triggered by water flow (26, 27).

Water-flow doping mechanisms

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulation is used to investigate the optimized distribution and orientation of water molecules within a theoretical model where water molecules are confined by a monolayer graphene (fig. S8). Once flowing, the density of water molecules nearest the graphene surface reaches 1.41 g cm−3, surpassing the 1.32 g cm−3 noted for static water (Fig. 2, A to C; fig. S9; and text S2). Simultaneously, the distance between the graphene surface and oxygen atoms in water molecules nearest to the surface decreases from 5.49 to 5.39 Å, indicating that water molecules are drawn closer to the graphene surface as demonstrated in Fig. 2 (A, B, and D). In addition, the orientation of water molecules nearest to the graphene surface in flowing water tends to be parallel, as evidenced by the increased percentage of H─O bonds parallel to the graphene surface, with about 50% in flowing water compared to only 45% under static conditions (Fig. 2, A, B, and E).

Leveraging the structure derived from the MD simulation, density functional theory (DFT) calculations are conducted to elucidate the impact of interfacial water molecule distribution and orientation on the electronic behavior of graphene. Water molecule models are constructed with H─O bonds oriented either vertically or parallel to the graphene surface to simulate the interactions of static or flowing water with graphene (Fig. 2F). The optimized distances between the oxygen atom and graphene are 3.40 Å as the H─O bond is oriented vertically to the graphene surface and 3.25 Å for the parallel, aligning with the results from the MD simulation (fig. S10). Vertical H─O bonds facilitate electron transfer from water molecules to graphene, whereas the parallels direct the transfer in the opposite direction, as revealed by charge density difference (CDD) calculations. The Fermi level of graphene in flowing water shifts downward to −4.08 eV from −4.01 eV in static water, as evidenced by the band structure and density of states (DOS) plots in Fig. 2G, aligning with the trend of the Dirac point shift within the transfer characteristic curves (Fig. 1, C and E) and confirming the additional p-type doping effect induced by water flow (28). When water is stationary on the graphene surface, a charge transfer equilibrium at the water-graphene interface remains. As water flows, the p-type doping effect disrupts the equilibrium and forms a new equilibrium state, leading to a decrease in the Fermi level.

We also explore the doping effect on other 2D materials with van der Waals surfaces. Water molecules are modeled with H─O bonds oriented either vertically or parallel to the WSe2 surface. In the most stable configuration, water molecules with H─O bonds oriented parallel exhibit a shorter oxygen-to-WSe2 surface distance of 2.98 Å versus 3.29 Å for the vertical orientation, indicating a preference for closer proximity to the surface (Fig. 2H and fig. S11). Electron transfer from water molecules to WSe2 occurs as H─O bonds are oriented vertically to the surface, while parallel H─O bonds prompt electron transfer in the reverse direction. In addition, water molecules are prone to approaching the MoS2 surface when the H─O bonds are oriented parallel to it (Fig. 2I and fig. S12). The charge transfer between water molecules and MoS2 is similar to that observed with WSe2 and graphene, demonstrating that water flow can lead to p-type doping in universal 2D materials featuring van der Waals surfaces.

High-efficiency signal transduction

A two-electrode device is designed to measure the electrode potential of graphene induced by water flow, with one end equipped with a Ag/AgCl reference electrode immersed in water and the other end featuring a Cr/Au layer deposited on the graphene surface as a current collector. The open-circuit voltage between the Ag/AgCl reference electrode and graphene across a range of water flow velocities represents the electrode potential of graphene, denoted as VFS in Fig. 3A. To confirm that the electrical signals originate exclusively from the two-electrode device, a widely recognized switching-polarity test is implemented (29, 30). A positive ΔVFS is generated with forward connection upon water flow, while a reverse connection yields a negative ΔVFS output (Fig. 3B), revealing that the measured ΔVFS signals are the responses generated by the two-electrode device to the water flow. As shown in Fig. 3C, a pronounced ΔVFS response of 1.3 mV is observed at a water flow velocity of 0.6 μm s−1, which increases to 135 mV as the velocity rises to 20.5 μm s−1. Notably, the ΔVFS signal reaches a plateau at 20.5 μm s−1 (fig. S13), with no appreciable response increase even when the flow velocity further increases to 41.1 μm s−1. ΔVFS can persist for over 25 min (Fig. S4A). The signal-to-noise ratio of ΔVFS at the lowest flow velocity of 600 nm s−1 is ~6.5, indicating high measurement precision. The average ΔVFS estimated from 11 devices is 142.7 mV with a standard deviation of 16.5 mV (fig. S14), demonstrating device-to-device consistency. The variation trend of ΔVFS aligns with that of ΔIDS/I0, suggesting that the alteration of graphene electrode potential upon water flow directly influences the ΔIDS/I0 fluctuations. The relative direction of water flow at a constant velocity exerts a negligible effect on the electrode potential of graphene (fig. S15).

To evaluate the transduction efficiency, we calculate the ΔVFS responsivity, defined as the ratio of the generated ΔVFS signal to the water flow velocity. It reaches 1.53 × 104 V m−1 s, exceeding the state-of-the-art value of 280 V m−1 s by two orders of magnitude (Fig. 3D) (31). Besides, the water flow signal down to 0.6 μm s−1 (equivalent to 600 nm s−1) can be efficiently transduced into electrical signals, lower than the previously reported (32) minimum value of 10 μm s−1, showcasing the exceptional signal transduction capability within the field of hydroelectronics (8, 16, 31–40). Two critical factors are primarily responsible for the outstanding signal transduction performance. 2D materials, with all of the atoms and charge carriers directly exposed to the surface, exhibit ultrahigh sensitivity to subtle water flow disturbances (41). There is a prominent charge transfer between flowing water molecules and the van der Waals surface; as such, even a minimal flow velocity can induce a substantial alteration of the electrode potential.

Water-flow gated transistor for hydroelectronics

Leveraging the water-flow electric-gating effect, a type of hydroelectronic device (WGT) is constructed, where water flow modulates the channel current IDS (Fig. 4A), equivalent to applying an electrical gate to traditional FETs. The output characteristics of |IDS|-|VDS| for graphene-WGT (g-WGT), WSe2-WGT, and MoS2-WGT are depicted in Fig. 4B and fig. S16 with water flow velocity ranging from 0 to 41.1 μm s−1. With a constant |VDS|, altering the water flow velocity equates to tuning the gate voltage VFS of the WGT, consequently yielding varied |IDS| outputs. Figure 4C presents the |IDS|-VFS transfer curves across a range of water flow velocities, revealing the distinct current on/off ratios of 1.17 for g-WGT, 96.09 for WSe2-WGT, and 3.56 for MoS2-WGT, underscoring the capability of water flow to regulate the conduction state of WGTs. Thus, the WGT can serve as a signal transduction device at the interface between the water circuit and the electrical circuit.

Transistor-based logic circuits constitute the essential building blocks of large-scale integrated circuits and form the cornerstone of modern information technology. To evaluate the feasibility of WGTs in logic circuit construction and gauge the suitability for commercial deployment, WGT units are used to construct an AND gate (Fig. 4D). Figure 4E illustrates the circuit diagram of the AND gate device with water flow serving as the input terminals (Vin) for logical operations. The WGT alternates between high and low levels, controlled by the water flow switch. Flow velocities of 0 and 200 μm s−1 correspond to the low and high levels of the WGT (Fig. 4F), symbolizing the logical outputs of “0” and “1,” respectively. The logic gate circuit outputs “1” only when both water flow inputs are 200 μm s−1; if either input is “0,” the circuit switches to output “0.” Therefore, the WGT is capable of functioning as a basic unit for logical operations and is expected to extend the applications in integrated circuits. In addition, water flow, as an essential cooling technique, can effectively dissipate heat from the WGT. Large-scale integrated circuits based on WGTs promise to substantially reduce heat generation and concurrently boost the computational performance.

The charge transfer equilibrium between water molecules and the van der Waals surface can be switched reversibly between static and flowing water states. As water transitions from a static to flowing state and back to static, the IDS of the WGT fluctuates correspondingly and ultimately reverts to its initial value. The ΔIDS response of the g-WGT manifests key features of synaptic plasticity in human neural networks, with the intensity of the postsynaptic output signal weighted by the frequency of presynaptic inputs (42, 43). With infrequent water flow inputs at a velocity of 23.3 μm s−1 (e.g., two flow pulses with about 23-min intervals in Fig. 4G), the ΔIDS of the g-WGT exhibits a swift decay following the cessation of water flow, aligning with the characteristics of short-term plasticity (STP). Conversely, under frequent water flow inputs at 23.3 μm s−1 (e.g., 25 flow pulses with about 1-min intervals in Fig. 4G), ΔIDS accumulates gradually, exhibiting characteristics of long-term potentiation (LTP). The ΔIDS of the g-WGT also demonstrates a cumulative effect in response to frequent water flow inputs at velocities of 6.7 and 33.0 μm s−1, as shown in fig. S17. Nevertheless, ΔIDS decays rapidly after the water flow reverts to a quiescent state from 33.0 μm s−1. The ΔIDS of the g-WGT, following 48 water flow pulses at 6.7 μm s−1 (about 0.8-min intervals), is less than half the value of ΔIDS recorded after 25 pulses at 23.3 μm s−1 (~1-min intervals). Traditional FETs with electrical gates exhibit an instantaneous change of ΔIDS upon alterations of the gate voltage, while the hysteresis effect of ΔIDS response of WGTs with water-flow gates emerges as the water flow initiates and terminates, showcasing prominent memory effects and holding great promise for the application in neuromorphic hydroelectronic chips.

DISCUSSION

We find an intrinsic electric effect that results from the rearrangement of flowing water molecules on van der Waals surface, providing a deeper understanding of the fluid electrokinetics. It is a universal effect in nature. Clarifying its underlying mechanisms holds profound implications for the advancement of physiology, neuroscience, ecology, earth science, etc. Hydroelectronics rely on the transduction between water flow signals and electrical signals. Leveraging this effect, we develop WGT, an elemental device for hydroelectronic signal transduction between the water circuit and the electrical circuit. It responds to water flow with a flow velocity of 600 nm s−1 and a voltage responsivity of 1.53 × 104 V m−1 s. Notably, the voltage responsivity is higher than the values reported previously by two orders of magnitude, underscoring the exceptional transduction efficiency. Combined with microfluidic technology, the WGT microfluidic chip systems based on bionic microstructures and biomaterials are expected to mimic the physiological functions of tissues and organs in vitro, thus facilitating the development of organ-on-chip devices. The efficient signal transduction of WGTs enables the in situ analysis of microscopic physiological and pathological processes, opening opportunities for drug development and biomedical research. Blood flow is an essential physiological activity within the living body. Implantable intelligent hydroelectronic chips based on WGTs will enable concurrent monitoring and sophisticated logical analysis of blood flow status, paving a way for precise diagnosis of cardiovascular diseases. WGT chips have synaptic plasticity, laying a robust foundation for the advancement of brain-like computing, neuroelectronics, and intelligent bionic sensing systems. This work opens up promising horizons for extensive applications across health care, artificial intelligence, and hydroelectronic systems.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Materials

MoS2, prepared by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) on a 300-nm SiO2/Si substrate, was obtained from Shenzhen Six Carbon Technology Co., Ltd. WSe2 was grown at 820°C in Ar/H2 (80/5 standard cubic centimeter per minute) for 3 min. NaCl was loaded on the SiO2/Si substrate. WO3 and Se powders were used as the sources of WSe2 (44). Polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS) was obtained from Shenzhen Ausbond Co., Ltd. To minimize ion interference, all experiments were performed using deionized water with a resistivity higher than 1.5 × 107 ohms·cm. The water was purified using a Milli-Q Elix system to remove ion compositions.

Characterization

Atomic force microscopy (AFM; XE7, Parks System) was used to measure the surface topography of graphene, WSe2, and MoS2. Raman spectra for graphene, WSe2, and MoS2 were measured by a HORIBA XploRA with a 532-nm laser. The micrograph of the g-WGT conducting channel was characterized by an optical microscope (Nikon).

Fabrication of the FETs and WGTs

For g-FETs and g-WGTs, large-area monolayer graphene was synthesized on a copper foil using the CVD method (45), with methane serving as the carbon source. The Raman spectrum of graphene (fig. S18A), along with the AFM image (fig. S19A), confirmed that the graphene film was a monolayer. The channel length for both the g-FET and g-WGT between the drain and source electrodes was 3 mm. Polymethyl methacrylate (PMMA) was spin-coated on a graphene/copper foil to facilitate the transfer of graphene onto a SiO2/Si substrate using the electrochemical delamination method (46, 47). An aqueous solution of NaOH (0.5 M) was used as the electrolyte. Upon applying a voltage of 2.3 V, microbubbles formed at the copper/graphene interface, leading to the exfoliation of the graphene/PMMA film. After thorough cleaning with deionized water, the graphene/PMMA film was transferred onto a SiO2/Si substrate (20 mm by 20 mm), then baked for 2 hours at 180°C, and lastly immersed in acetone for 2 hours to dissolve the PMMA. To clean the graphene surface, final annealing was conducted at 400°C for 30 min in a high vacuum of 10−5 torr. The drain and source electrodes (Cr/Au, 5/38 nm) were deposited on top of the graphene film through thermal evaporation. The dimensions of both the source and drain electrodes were 7 mm by 18 mm (fig. S1). The conductive channel of the g-FET device between the source and drain electrodes was designed with a length of 3 mm and a width of 10 mm, and the g-WGT featured the same channel dimensions.

WSe2 was transferred onto a clean, 300-nm SiO2/Si substrate through a 10% HF etching process. Fabrication of the WSe2-FET, WSe2-WGT, MoS2-FET, and MoS2-WGT devices was achieved through the photolithography technique. The Raman spectra for both WSe2 and MoS2 are depicted in fig. S18, and the AFM images are shown in fig. S19.

The PDMS block is fabricated with dimensions of 8 mm by 10 mm by 16 mm (fig. S1). The PDMS incorporates Φ 1-mm water inlet/outlet ports and a Φ 1.5-mm port for Ag/AgCl reference electrode integration. The microfluidic channel, with a length of 9 mm and a regular hexagonal cross section of 0.87 mm in side length, was fabricated using a homemade mold, as demonstrated in fig. S20. The microfluidic channel was fixed onto the FETs or WGTs using a homemade clamp. The contact area between the water flow in the microfluidic channel and the graphene surface spanned a rectangle of 0.87 mm by 9 mm, as illustrated in fig. S21. During testing, the water flow exclusively contacted the graphene surface without touching the Cr/Au electrode; therefore, the observed signals were not influenced by electrode-water interactions.

For the test of synaptic plasticity, graphene was transferred from the copper foil onto a 300-nm SiO2/Si substrate, on which Cr/Au (5/38 nm) electrodes (30 μm by 460 μm) were processed using photolithography and liftoff techniques beforehand. To ensure device consistency, the graphene sheet was patterned into rectangular shapes using photolithography and oxygen plasma etching techniques. As water flowed only across the Cr/Au electrode, the ΔIDS/I0 response exhibited negligible variation, indicating minimal interaction between the water flow and the electrode (fig. S22).

Device measurement

The electrical performance measurement was conducted using the semiconductor parameter analyzer (Keysight, B1500A). Deionized water was obtained by introducing tap water into the water purification system (Milli-Q Elix) to remove ionic contaminants. A syringe pump (SPLab02, Baoding Shenchen Pump Industry Co., Ltd.) was used to precisely control the flow velocity. The syringe pump switched between perfusion and extraction modes to regulate the forward and reverse flow of water in the pipe (fig. S23). Before the test, the g-FET was immersed in water for 10 min. The time-resolved IDS was measured at VDS = 100 mV and VGS = 0. The |IDS|-VGS curves for the g-FET device were measured by varying the water flow velocity from 0 to 41.1 μm s−1 at VDS = 100 mV.

|IDS|-VGS curves for the WSe2-FET device were measured at VDS = 300 mV. |IDS|-VGS curves for the MoS2-FET device were measured at VDS = 50 mV. |IDS|-|VDS| curves for the WSe2-FET device were measured at VGS = −800 mV. |IDS|-|VDS| curves for the MoS2-FET device were measured at VGS = 300 mV.

The logic gate tests were conducted using the source meter (Keithley 2636B). The AND gates (TC7SH08F) were purchased from Toshiba in Japan. The WGTs were interfaced with the signal amplifiers (AD620, Keruitai Electronic Technology Co., Ltd.), which were subsequently connected to the input terminals of the AND gate. The magnification was set to 15 times. The Vcc was set to 2 V. The water flow velocity at the low-level setting of the WGT was 0 μm s−1, and at the high-level setting, it was 200 μm s−1.

DFT computational details

The calculations of electrical properties were performed within the DFT framework as put into practice in the Vienna ab initio simulation package code (48). The projector augmented wave method was applied to the core region, and the generalized gradient approximation with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof functional was used for the exchange-correlation potential (49), setting the energy cutoff to 530 eV. Brillouin zone integration was sampled by a 2 by 3 by 1 k-mesh. The system was simulated with a periodic boundary condition by placing two layers of water molecules on the surface of a graphene sheet with a length of 19.68 Å and a width of 12.78 Å, spaced by 20 Å along the normal direction. The systems were fully relaxed without any constraint.

MD simulation method

All MD simulations were carried out using the LAMMPS package (50). The simulation model consisted of a graphene-water-graphene (BGW) system with a water reservoir sandwiched between two parallel (19.7 Å by 12.8 Å) graphene sheets separated by a distance of 65.1 Å, as depicted in fig. S8.

The Nose-Hoover thermostat maintained the system temperature at 300 K, and the BGW system was first equilibrated in the NVT ensemble for 3 ns with a 1-fs time step. The equations of motion were integrated using the Leapfrog method, with periodic boundary conditions and a total of 2418 atoms.

Parameters for carbon atoms in graphene sheets were taken as the parameters for aromatic carbon “CA” in the CHARMM27 force field, with the atoms fixed as rigid during simulations. The rigid SPC/E model was used for water. Water molecules were simulated using the SPC/E water mode. Nonbonding interactions were modeled by the Lennard-Jones potential with a cutoff of 12 Å. The global cutoff of electrostatic interactions was also set as 12 Å. Lennard-Jones parameters for the energy interaction between two unlike nonbonded atoms were obtained by applying Lorentz-Berthelot mixing rules. Corrections to long-range electrostatic interactions were calculated using the particle-particle particle-mesh technique. Nonbonding interactions were described by the Lennard-Jones potential.

The BGW system with periodic boundary conditions along three dimensions was first minimized by cg style in the LAMMPS package. The stopping tolerance for force was set up as 0.00001 within the 1 × 106 max number of force evaluation checking steps. In the flowing calculations, a constant force was applied along the x dimension to each water molecule in the reservoir. The initial minimum force was set as 8.9 × 10−4 eV Å−1 with subsequent force values doubled or tripled as needed.
