DFT and experimental evaluation of NiSnO₃ nanocomposites in photocatalytic applications
Authors: Hocine SadamNesrat, Abdelatif Aouadi, Djamel Bezzerga, El-Abed Haidar, Sara Aouadi, Salah Eddine Laouini, Mohammed Messaoudi, Ayomide Victor Atoki, DalilaSaidane Mosbahi, Nader Shehata
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe development of multifunctional nanomaterials capable of addressing energy and environmental challenges remains an important goal in materials research. Herein, NiSnO₃ ternary oxide was synthesized via a hydrothermal route and systematically investigated for its structural, optical, and functional properties. Structural and optical characterizations confirm the formation of a phase-pure, nanocrystalline material with a bandgap of approximately 1.5 eV and enhanced visible-light absorption. Nitrogen adsorption–desorption measurements reveal a mesoporous structure with a BET surface area of 88.14 m2·g⁻1, providing abundant accessible active sites. The multifunctional performance of NiSnO₃ was evaluated in photocatalytic, photoelectrochemical, and environmental remediation applications. In photocatalytic hydrogen evolution, NiSnO₃ achieved an H₂ yield of 805 µmol·g⁻1 after 4 h under simulated sunlight, exceeding the activity of NiO and SnO₂ reference materials. During CO₂photoreduction, methane production reached 566 µmol·g⁻1, accompanied by 241 µmol·g⁻1 of H₂, with stable performance over five reaction cycles. In water treatment applications, NiSnO₃ exhibited high adsorption capacities toward Pb2⁺ (609 mg·g⁻1) and Cu2⁺ (779 mg·g⁻1), and enabled efficient degradation (> 93%) of chlorpyrifos and organic dyes under natural sunlight. Photoelectrochemical measurements further revealed a photocurrent density of 12.5 µA·cm⁻2, indicating improved charge separation and interfacial charge transfer. These results demonstrate that NiSnO₃ is a promising multifunctional oxide for photocatalytic energy conversion and environmental remediation, highlighting the potential of compositional tuning and electronic structure engineering in the design of advanced functional materials.Keywords: Photoelectrochemical chemistry, Energy efficiency, Hydrogen production, Hydrothermal method, Dye removal, CO2 ReductionSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Environmental sciences, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

The development of sustainable materials for energy conversion and environmental remediation has attracted increasing attention in recent years1. In this context, nanostructured metal oxides have emerged as promising candidates due to their chemical stability, tunable electronic properties, and high surface reactivity, making them suitable for applications in photocatalysis and pollutant removal21. Among the available synthesis approaches, hydrothermal methods offer an effective route to fabricate complex oxide nanostructures with controlled morphology, phase purity, and crystallinity, which are essential for optimizing light-induced redox processes2,3. Combining p-type and n-type metal oxides represents a widely adopted strategy to improve charge separation and suppress recombination in photocatalytic systems. For example, NiO is a p-type semiconductor with good chemical stability but limited electrical conductivity, while SnO₂ is an n-type oxide with high electron mobility but weak visible-light absorption3–7. Their combination can enhance charge carrier dynamics and surface reactivity, leading to improved photocatalytic performance4,8.

Extending this concept, ternary Ni–Sn–O oxide systems provide additional opportunities to tailor electronic structure and multifunctional behavior through cationic interactions between Ni2⁺ and Sn4⁺ species9. However, the synthesis of phase-pure and compositionally uniform Ni–Sn–O nanomaterials remains challenging, particularly at the nanoscale. A deeper understanding of structure–property relationships in such systems is therefore required, especially for applications involving water purification and solar energy conversion. Environmental pollutants such as heavy metals, organic dyes, and pesticides pose serious risks to aquatic ecosystems and are often resistant to conventional treatment technologies5,10,11. In parallel, solar-driven hydrogen production and CO₂photoreduction are considered promising routes toward carbon–neutral energy systems, requiring stable semiconductor materials with suitable band structures and efficient charge transport properties12,13.Recent studies have highlighted the advantages of metal oxide–based hybrids in photocatalysis. Li et al.6 reviewed the progress in CO₂ reduction using oxide hybrids, demonstrating enhanced charge separation and solar fuel generation.Zhenget al7 reported efficient degradation of organic pollutants with hybrid oxides under visible light. More recently, Prakash et al.8 emphasized the role of oxide semiconductor hybrids in photoelectrochemical CO₂ conversion, confirming that heterojunction engineering significantly improves catalytic efficiency.

Furthermore, integrating nanostructured oxides into photoelectrochemical platforms enables the evaluation of interfacial charge transfer and device-level performance under realistic operating conditions14.

Despite the increasing interest in metal oxide nanostructures for environmental and energy applications, the Ni–Sn–O system remains largely unexplored. Most studies have focused on individual oxides like NiO or SnO₂, while their combination into a single ternary nanostructure with enhanced properties is still rare. There is a clear lack of research on phase-pure NiSnₓOᵧ materials that can simultaneously perform multiple tasks such as heavy metal adsorption, dye and pesticide degradation, hydrogen generation, CO₂ reduction, and photoelectrochemical energy conversion under solar light. This gap highlights the need to develop multifunctional Ni–Sn–O nanomaterials with controlled structure and improved efficiency.

Motivated by these considerations, we herein report the hydrothermal synthesis of a ternary NiSnO3 nanomaterial and investigate its multifunctional performance across a range of energy and environmental applications. Through comprehensive characterization, we explore its efficiency in adsorbing and degrading waterborne pollutants, generating hydrogen via photocatalysis, converting CO₂ to methane, and producing photocurrent in PEC configurations, demonstrating its potential as a versatile and scalable material for future clean technologies.

Materials and methods

Chemicals

Nickel(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O, ≥ 99%), tin(IV) chloride pentahydrate (SnCl₄·5H₂O, ≥ 98%), andurea (CO(NH₂)₂, ≥ 99%) were used as precursors for NiSnO₃ synthesis. Ethanol (C₂H₅OH, ≥ 99.9%) and deionized water served as solvents, while triethanolamine (C₆H₁₅NO₃, ≥ 99%) was employed as a sacrificial electron donor. Chlorpyrifos pesticide (C₉H₁₁Cl₃NO₃PS, ≥ 98%) and organic dyes—Rose Bengal (C₂₀H₂Cl₄I₄Na₂O₅, ≥ 95%), Methylene Blue (C₁₆H₁₈ClN₃S, ≥ 95%), Brilliant Crystal Blue (C₃₇H₃₄N₂Na₂O₉S₃, ≥ 90%), and Bromophenol Blue (C₁₉H₁₀Br₄O₅S, ≥ 95%)—were used as model pollutants. For adsorption studies, Pb2⁺ and Cu2⁺ ions were supplied from lead nitrate (Pb(NO₃)₂, ≥ 99%) and copper sulfatepentahydrate (CuSO₄·5H₂O, ≥ 99%).

Hydrothermal synthesis of NiSnO3 nanoparticle

The synthesis of NiSnO3 nanoparticles was adapted from a previously reported procedure15, with slight modifications to optimize phase formation and crystallinity. Specifically, 0.01 mol of nickel(II) nitrate hexahydrate (Ni(NO₃)₂·6H₂O), 0.03 mol of tin(IV) chloride pentahydrate (SnCl₄·5H₂O), and 0.10 mol of urea (CO(NH₂)₂) were dissolved in 80 mL of deionized water under continuous stirring for 30 min to ensure complete homogenization. Urea was employed as a homogeneous precipitation agent and pH regulator, as its gradual thermal decomposition releases hydroxyl ions, promoting controlled nucleation and uniform growth of the Ni–Sn precursor. No external pH adjustment was applied, and the solution pH was governed by the decomposition of urea during the hydrothermal process.

The resulting clear solution was transferred into a 100 mL Teflon-lined stainless steel autoclave and subjected to hydrothermal treatment at 180 °C with a heating rate of approximately 5 °C·min⁻1 for 12 h. After completion of the reaction, the autoclave was allowed to cool naturally to room temperature, the precipitate was collected by centrifugation, washed several times with deionized water and absolute ethanol, and then dried at 60 °C overnight. The as-obtained powder was subsequently calcined in air at 500 °C for 3 h to achieve the desired crystalline NiSnO3 phase.

Characterization of NiSnO3 nanoparticles

The synthesized NiSnO3 nanoparticles were comprehensively characterized using a suite of complementary techniques to elucidate their structural, morphological, vibrational, and optical properties. UV–Vis absorption spectroscopy (SECOMAM 9600 UV, France) was employed to probe the optical response and estimate the bandgap energy via Tauc analysis. Fourier-transform infrared (FTIR) spectroscopy (Nicolet iS50, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) in the range of 400–4000 cm⁻1 was utilized to identify functional groups and assess the chemical bonding environment. The morphological features and particle size distribution were examined through field emission scanning electron microscopy (FESEM, TESCAN VEGA3, USA). Crystalline structure, phase composition, and purity were investigated by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a benchtopdiffractometer (Proto Manufacturing, USA). Raman spectroscopy (OceanHDX, Ocean Insight, USA) provided additional insights into lattice vibrations and molecular structure, while photoluminescence (PL) spectra were recorded using a fluorescence spectrophotometer (FS5, Edinburgh Instruments, UK) to evaluate charge recombination behavior and electronic transitions. Collectively, these analyses confirmed the successful formation of phase-pure NiSnO3 nanostructures with properties tailored for photocatalytic and environmental applications.

Photocatalytic hydrogen production

Photocatalytic hydrogen production experiments were performed at room temperature using NiO, SnO₂, and NiSnO3 nanoparticles. A 100 mL quartz photoreactor was employed, hermetically sealed with a silicone rubber septum. A total of 5 mg of the photocatalyst was dispersed in 20 mL of an aqueous solution consisting of deionized water and 5% triethanolamine (TEOA), which served as a sacrificial electron donor. The suspension was homogenized by continuous stirring. UV–visible irradiation was provided by a 300 W xenon lamp (Oriel), covering the 300–800 nm spectral range, and the light intensity at the reactor surface was calibrated to 100 mW·cm⁻2. A Xenon lamp was used as the light source, positioned 10 cm from the reactor, following the configuration reported in previous studies18. Prior to light irradiation, nitrogen gas was purged through the suspension for 30 min to eliminate dissolved oxygen. The reactor temperature was maintained at 10 °C using a water-cooling system to ensure stable reaction conditions during illumination.Hydrogen evolution was monitored using an Agilent gas chromatograph (model 8890) equipped with a 5 Å molecular sieve column and a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). High-purity nitrogen was used as the carrier gas at a controlled flow rate of 22.5 mL·min⁻1, and the GC oven temperature was maintained at 50 °C. Quantitative analysis of hydrogen was performed using external calibration with standard gas mixtures. Gas samples (300 µL) were collected from the headspace after 1 h of illumination. This procedure was repeated three times, and the average peak area was calculated to determine the hydrogen production rate.Calibration curves for H₂ were constructed using standard gas mixtures in the concentration range of 50–1000 ppm. The detection limit was determined based on a signal-to-noise ratio of 3, yielding a value of approximately 10 ppm for H₂.These calibration details confirm the accuracy and reproducibility of the quantitative analysis of H₂ evolution during photocatalytic experiments.

Photocatalytic CO₂ Reduction

Photocatalytic CO₂ reduction experiments were carried out using NiO, SnO₂, and NiSnO3 nanoparticles under simulated solar irradiation (AM 1.5)19. A 20 mL aqueous solution containing 5% triethanolamine (TEOA) was prepared by mixing TEOA with deionized water, serving as a sacrificial electron donor. Subsequently, 5 mg of the photocatalyst (NiO, SnO₂, or NiSnO3) was dispersed into the solution using ultrasonication for 20 min to obtain a homogeneous suspension.The resulting mixture was transferred into a quartz photoreactor and hermetically sealed with a silicone septum. To remove dissolved oxygen, the system was purged with high-purity nitrogen gas for 30 min. Afterwards, high-purity CO₂ gas was bubbled through the suspension for 1 h to ensure complete saturation.Thephotoreactor was then placed under a solar simulator at a fixed distance of 15 cm. The reaction temperature was maintained at 80 °C throughout the experiment. Gas samples were collected at regular 1-h intervals from the reactor headspace using a gas-tight syringe. The collected samples were analyzed using gas chromatography (GC) to quantify the generated hydrogen (H₂) and methane (CH₄).The photocatalytic performance was evaluated by expressing the evolved gases in micromoles per gram of photocatalyst (μmol·g⁻1) as a function of reaction time.Calibration curves for CH₄ were constructed using standard gas mixtures in the concentration range of 50–1000 ppm. The detection limit was determined based on a signal-to-noise ratio of 3, yielding a value of approximately 15 ppm for CH₄.These calibration details ensure reliable quantification of CH₄ as a reduction product of CO₂, thereby confirming the photocatalytic efficiency of the NiSnO₃ nanostructures.

Electrochemical studies

Photoelectrochemical measurements were conducted using a conventional three-electrode configuration housed in a rectangular quartz cell. The working electrode consisted of fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass (1 cm2) modified with a thin film of NiO, SnO₂, or NiSnO3 semiconductor layers. A platinum wire and an Ag/AgCl (saturated KCl) electrode were employed as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. The electrolyte solution was a 0.5 M aqueous Na₂SO₄ solution, used without any pH adjustment to ensure neutrality and avoid perturbing interfacial charge transfer dynamics.

To fabricate the photoanode, 10 mg of NiO, SnO₂, or NiSnO3 nanoparticles were ultrasonically dispersed in 10 mL of 2-propanol to form a homogeneous suspension. An aliquot of the resulting ink was drop-cast onto a pre-cleaned 1 cm2 FTO substrate and left to dry under ambient conditions overnight to ensure uniform adhesion and solvent evaporation. Illumination was provided using a 150 W xenon arc lamp equipped with an AM 1.5G filter to simulate sunlight, delivering a light intensity closely matching terrestrial solar irradiance (100 mW.cm⁻2). The photoresponse was recorded under chopped illumination to evaluate the stability and reversibility of photocurrent generation20.

Band gap validation through density functional theory calculations

Using the VASP code9, we first performed full structural relaxation within the rhombohedral R symmetry, employing a plane-wave energy cutoff of 600 eV and a force convergence criterion of 0.01 eV/Å. Subsequently, spin-polarized calculations were carried out using the PBE + U approach10, with an effective U value of 6 eV applied to the 3d orbitals of Ni atoms11. To determine the magnetic ground state of NiSnO₃, spin-polarized DFT calculations were performed by considering different magnetic configurations, including non-magnetic (NM), ferromagnetic (FM), and antiferromagnetic (AFM) orderings. The total energies of these configurations were compared, and the AFM configuration was found to be the most energetically stable. This behavior is consistent with antiferromagnetic superexchange interactions between neighboring Ni2⁺ centers mediated by oxygen atoms. Accordingly, the AFM configuration was adopted for all subsequent electronic structure calculations. For the self-consistent field (SCF) calculations, a Monkhorst–Pack k-point mesh of 11 × 11 × 11 was used, while a denser grid of 25 × 25 × 25 was adopted for the density of states (DOS) calculations to accurately determine the electronic band gap. The calculated band gap of 1.22 eV is in excellent agreement with the experimentally measured value, as shown in Fig. 2.

Fourier-transformation infrared spectroscopy (FTIR)

Figure 3 presents the FTIR transmission spectrum of the synthesized NiSnO₃ nanoparticles. The absence of a broad absorption band around 3400 cm⁻1 and the lack of a distinct peak near 1630 cm⁻1 suggest minimal presence of surface-adsorbed or physisorbed water. This indicates effective drying or calcination of the sample27,28. Several moderate-to-strong absorption bands are observed at 1753, 1542, 1341, and 1213 cm⁻1. These may correspond to residual nitrate or carbonate species originating from precursor salts. Their presence, while detectable, appears limited, suggesting low levels of contamination29. The prominent and sharp absorption peak at 792 cm⁻1 is characteristic of metal–oxygen (M–O) stretching vibrations, likely associated with Ni–O and Sn–O bonds. This confirms the formation of the mixed metal oxide framework of NiSnO₃. Additionally, the band at 420 cm⁻1 further supports the presence of lattice vibrations typical of crystalline metal oxides30. The absence of peaks related to organic residues or unreacted precursors confirms the chemical purity of the final product, which is consistent with the high-temperature calcination step employed post-synthesis.

Raman spectroscopic analysis

Raman spectroscopy was used to study the structural properties and phases of the synthesized NiSnO3 nanoparticles. The spectrum was recorded in the wavenumber range of 190 cm⁻1 to 3500 cm⁻1, as shown in the Fig. 4. The Raman spectra show a significant peak at around 2000 cm⁻1, which is a characteristic feature of transition metal oxides. This peak is usually linked to the stretching of the metal–oxygen bonds in the material’s crystal structure, indicating specific vibrational modes involving interactions between the metal and oxygen in the nanoparticles. The peak at 2000 cm⁻1 corresponds to a phonon mode associated with metal–oxygen bond stretching in transition metal oxides. This type of peak is often seen in nanoparticles of transition metal oxides with similar crystal structures and can provide useful information about the crystallinity and integrity of the material. The shift in the peak position compared to bulk NiSnO3 is likely due to the nanoscale effects, such as surface strain or surface reconstruction, which affect the phonon modes and their frequencies31,32.

These peaks are generally related to crystal defects or surface interactions between the nanoparticles and their environment. The secondary peaks, appearing at positions like 1070 cm⁻1 and 1380 cm⁻1, are usually associated with surface interactions or the formation of secondary phases on the nanoparticle surfaces. In some cases, these peaks may indicate the formation of additional oxides such as NiO or SnO₂ on the surface due to contamination or interactions with CO₂ or water during preparation33,34.

The spectrum may also show unexpected peaks due to the interaction of NiSnO3 with environmental gases such as CO₂ or O₂, leading to the formation of additional oxides on the surface of the nanoparticles. Through preparation methods (like sol–gel or mechanical processes), there could be interactions between the metal oxides and CO₂ in the environment, which might lead to the formation of CO₃2−, which would appear in the Raman spectrum as an additional peak at 1070 cm⁻136,37.Overall, the Raman analysis provides comprehensive insights into the crystal structure, surface effects, and phase transitions of NiSnO3 nanoparticles. The prominent peaks reflect the formation of metal–oxygen bonds, while the secondary peaks give information about surface interactions and secondary phases. These findings contribute valuable information about the properties of the nanoparticles, supporting their potential applications in fields such as catalysis and sensing.

The Raman spectrum exhibits a broad and intense feature centered around 2000 cm⁻1. This high-wavenumber response is higher than the typical fundamental lattice vibrations of metal–oxygen bonds and is therefore not assigned to a single metal–oxygen phonon mode. Similar features have been reported in nanostructured transition metal oxides and are commonly attributed to defect-related luminescence, second-order Raman scattering, or surface-related vibrational overtones.35.

The mesoporous architecture and relatively high surface area are expected to provide a large number of accessible active sites and promote efficient diffusion of reactant molecules, which are beneficial for both adsorption and photocatalytic/photoelectrochemical reactions12. Moreover, the nanoscale particle size estimated from BET analysis (16.47 nm) is consistent with the morphological observations, confirming the nanostructured nature of NiSnO₃. These textural features, combined with the favorable electronic structure, contribute synergistically to the enhanced multifunctional performance of the material13.

The Langmuir surface area was observed to be significantly higher than the BET value. This discrepancy arises from the fundamental assumptions of the Langmuir model, which considers monolayer adsorption on homogeneous sites and neglects interactions between adsorbed molecules. Such an idealized approach often leads to overestimation of the accessible surface area, particularly in mesoporous and heterogeneous nanomaterials14. In contrast, the BET method extends the adsorption model to multilayer coverage and accounts for adsorbate–adsorbate interactions, providing a more realistic description of gas adsorption in porous solids15,16. Therefore, the BET surface area is generally considered more reliable for NiSnO₃ nanostructures and similar photocatalytic materials.

Dye degradation and photocatalytic mechanism

The photocatalytic degradation behavior of four synthetic dyes-Rose Bengal (RB), Methylene Blue (MB), Brilliant Crystal Blue (BCB), and Bromophenol Blue (BPH) using NiSnO3, as a photocatalyst under natural sunlight, are presented in both Figs. 7 and 8. Both figures illustrate the UV–Vis absorption spectra of each dye over time, highlighting the progressive decrease in their characteristic absorption bands: RB at around 546 nm, MB at 664 nm, and both BCB and BPH near 590 nm. The gradual drop in absorbance intensity in each spectrum indicates the effective breakdown of the dye molecules through photocatalytic oxidation, and this is particularly evident in the cases of RB and MB, where the peaks nearly vanish after 120 min of irradiation. In contrast, BCB and BPH showed less pronounced reductions, reflecting moderate photocatalytic degradation. Figure 9 summarizes the overall degradation kinetics of the dyes, showing the percentage removal versus time and clearly distinguishing the superior degradation of RB and MB (97% and 94%, respectively) compared to BCB (78%) and BPH (65%). This variation in photocatalytic response is closely related to the dyes’ molecular structures, light absorption characteristics, and surface affinity toward the NiSnO3 photocatalyst. RB and MB, being planar and cationic, have higher adsorption tendencies on the catalyst surface and interact more effectively with photogenerated reactive species. On the other hand, BPH contains bulky bromine substituents that introduce steric hindrance and electronic effects that may limit radical attack, while BCB may have weaker interactions with the catalyst surface.

The outstanding photocatalytic performance of NiSnO3 can be attributed to multiple interrelated structural and electronic factors, primarily its engineered nanostructured morphology, which plays a pivotal role in maximizing the catalyst’s surface-related reactivity. The nanoscale dimensions of the NiSnO3 particles significantly increase the specific surface area, thereby offering a larger number of accessible active sites for the adsorption of dye molecules42. This enhanced adsorption facilitates intimate contact between the pollutants and the photocatalyst surface, which is essential for initiating the photocatalytic reactions efficiently. Moreover, the reduced diffusion length for photogenerated charge carriers within the nanostructured matrix promotes faster migration of electrons and holes to the surface, thus enhancing the likelihood of interfacial redox reactions before recombination occurs43.

Beyond the geometric advantages, the intrinsic electronic structure of NiSnO3 is fundamentally tailored by the coexistence of Ni2⁺, Ni3⁺, and Sn4⁺ cations within its crystalline lattice. The presence of Ni in mixed valence states (Ni2⁺/Ni3⁺) not only facilitates internal electron transfer processes—acting as a redox shuttle—but also introduces energy levels within the bandgap that serve to trap or separate charge carriers44. These redox centers can temporarily capture photogenerated electrons or holes, thereby prolonging the carrier lifetimes and reducing the rate of direct recombination. In parallel, the incorporation of Sn4⁺ into the spinel-like or ternary oxide framework modulates the conduction band edge, effectively lowering the bandgap and enabling absorption in the visible region of the solar spectrum45.

Previous studies have reported that the ionic radius mismatch and electronic differences between Ni and Sn species can induce local lattice distortions, which in turn generate oxygen vacancies and surface defects. These defects are known to act as auxiliary active sites for O₂ adsorption and activation, thereby accelerating the formation of superoxide (•O₂⁻) and hydroxyl (•OH) radicals—key oxidative agents in photocatalytic degradation46. In line with these reports, the synergy between morphological, electronic, and defect-related attributes is expected to contribute to efficient charge carrier separation, enhanced ROS generation, and a robust degradation pathway for organic pollutants. In our study, this discussion is literature-based, as scavenger experiments to confirm the dominant species were not performed due to current laboratory limitations. We acknowledge the importance of such experiments and plan to include them in future work to provide direct experimental evidence.

Increasing the catalyst concentration to 6 mg/mL enhanced adsorption across all samples. For NiSnO3, Qₑ rose from 179 to 586 mg/g for Cu2⁺ and from 147 to 538 mg/g for Pb2⁺ between 1 and 4 h. Photocatalytic hydrogen production

Figure 13a presents the photocatalytic hydrogen production performance (expressed in µmol/g) over time for SnO₂, NiO, and NiSnO3 nanoparticles under identical experimental conditions. After one hour of illumination using anXe lamp and an aqueous solution containing 5% triethanolamine (TEOA), the recorded hydrogen yields were 97 µmol/g for SnO₂, 114 µmol/g for NiO, and a significantly higher 201 µmol/g for NiSnO3. This indicates an initial enhancement of approximately 107% compared to SnO₂ and 76% relative to NiO. After two hours, the respective hydrogen amounts increased to 186, 218, and 394 µmol/g, while after three hours, the values reached 290, 335, and 589 µmol/g, respectively. At the end of the fourth hour, NiSnO3 achieved a final yield of 805 µmol/g, compared to 461 µmol/g for NiO and 388 µmol/g for SnO₂. These data demonstrate a markedly higher photocatalytic activity and faster hydrogen generation kinetics for NiSnO3. The average hydrogen evolution rates over four hours were 201.25 µmol/g/h for NiSnO3, versus 115.25 and 97 µmol/g/h for NiO and SnO₂, respectively. The growth profile for NiSnO3 is quasi-exponential, contrasting with the near-linear behavior observed for the reference materials, indicating superior charge separation and sustained reaction efficiency over time.

The superior performance of NiSnO3 can be attributed to several synergistic factors. Firstly, the multi-element composition induces a cooperative interaction between Ni2⁺/Ni3⁺ and Sn4⁺ within the crystal lattice, resulting in a modified electronic structure that enhances the separation of photogenerated charge carriers72. Rather than forming a classical p–n junction, the coexistence of Ni- and Sn-centered electronic states induces a heterojunction-like internal charge separation and local band bending within the single-phase material, which facilitates the generation of an internal electric field and suppresses charge recombination73. Secondly, the nanostructured nature of NiSnO3 is expected to provide a high surface area, increasing the density of active sites for photoelectrochemical reactions, and ensuring uniform particle distribution over the FTO surface, which enhances light absorption and interfacial charge transfer74.

Although NiO displays a relatively moderate and stable photocurrent, its performance is limited due to intrinsic challenges, including sluggish electron transport and significant charge recombination75. SnO₂, despite its favorable electronic conductivity and wide band gap, exhibits poor visible light absorption, which accounts for its low photocurrent density. Furthermore, the lack of abundant surface active sites and restricted surface kinetics further reduce its PEC activity76.The enhanced photocurrent response of NiSnO3 is closely linked to its efficient photogenerated charge carrier dynamics. Upon light absorption, electrons are excited from the valence band to the conduction band, leaving behind holes, as described by the reaction77:

The excited electrons migrate to the FTO substrate and flow through the external circuit, while the holes participate in the water oxidation reaction at the photoanode surface78:

Oxygen evolution at the surface confirms the material’s capability for photogenerated hole-driven oxidation. In less efficient materials like NiO and SnO₂, rapid charge recombination undermines this process. In contrast, the internal electric field and optimized energy level alignment in NiSnO3 substantially retard recombination and enhance the lifetime of charge carriers79. Moreover, the improved light harvesting and electronic transport properties, stemming from the material’s complex composition and structural uniformity, further augment its PEC efficiency. These features render NiSnO3 a highly promising candidate for solar-driven water splitting, wastewater treatment, and sustainable photoelectrochemical applications80.

The enhanced photocatalytic and photoelectrochemical performance of NiSnO₃ can be rationalized by a proposed synergistic interaction between the redox-active Ni2⁺/Ni3⁺ centers and the Sn4⁺ framework13,24. Although direct experimental evidence for this interaction is not provided in the present study, the synergistic effect is indirectly supported by the significantly improved activity of NiSnO₃ compared to the individual NiO and SnO₂ counterparts, together with the reduced photoluminescence intensity and the enhanced photocurrent response25. Furthermore, density functional theory (DFT) calculations indicate a modified electronic structure and favorable charge redistribution upon Ni incorporation, which is consistent with improved charge separation and transport26.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we have successfully synthesized a novel ternary oxide, NiSnO₃, through a simple hydrothermal process and have exhibited its exceptional multifunctionality in the energy and environmental domains. The synergistic interaction between Ni and Sn species within a unified nanostructure results in the engineered material exhibiting a rare combination of robust redox activity, enhanced charge separation, and narrow bandgap.Under moderate, solar-driven conditions, NiSnO₃ outperforms its binary counterparts in photocatalytic hydrogen generation, CO₂ photoreduction, adsorptive and photodegradative pollutant removal, and photoelectrochemical energy conversion. Its potential for integration into practical clean technology platforms is emphasized by its high stability, reusability, and scalable synthesis.This work not only broadens the compositional topography of ternary metal oxides but also underscores the potential of structurally engineered Ni–Sn–O systems as adaptable candidates for decentralized solar energy and water remediation applications.
