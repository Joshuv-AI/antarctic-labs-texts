Adipose Tissue‐Targeted Delivery of Rosiglitazone With Iron Oxide Nanoparticles Ameliorates Insulin Resistance in Male Mice
Authors: Jakob E. Ostberg, Zeeshan Ahmed, Katayoun Saatchi, Parleen K. Pandher, Yamna Rahim, Urs O. Häfeli, Sarah L. Gray
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectiveThis study aimed to assess iron oxide magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) for adipose tissue‐targeted therapeutics to restore adipose tissue function and insulin sensitivity in obesity.MethodsThe insulin‐sensitizing effects of rosiglitazone and known adverse effects were leveraged for this proof‐of‐concept assessment of adipose tissue drug targeting. Rosiglitazone was adsorbed to alendronic acid‐coated MNPs (rosiMNPs) and a biocompatible magnet implanted in the right inguinal white adipose tissue (ingWAT) of obese, insulin resistant male mice. Following rosiMNP treatment (1.5 mg/kg/day, 18 days), insulin sensitivity and adipose tissue health were assessed.ResultsRosiMNPs restored insulin sensitivity as well as systemic rosiglitazone (1.5 mg/kg/day, 18 days), with a shift in adipocyte size compatible with PPARγ‐induced adipocyte hyperplasia and no increase in circulating adiponectin. PPARγ target genes were induced in ingWAT with rosiMNPs or systemic rosiglitazone, but only in liver and gonadal WAT of systemically treated mice. Additionally, iron accumulation was shown in the right, targeted ingWAT depot, but not in the left, untargeted ingWAT, kidney, or liver, suggesting targeting was achieved. Unfortunately, the dose and duration of systemic treatment were ineffective at inducing changes in kidney.ConclusionsResults demonstrate the potential benefits of adipose tissue‐targeted therapeutics to improve adipose tissue function to restore insulin sensitivity to prevent the metabolic complications of obesity.

Introduction

Current therapeutics that directly and safely treat insulin resistance in chronic metabolic disease such as obesity and type 2 diabetes are limited [1, 2]. Given that insulin resistance precedes impairments in insulin secretion in many cases of type 2 diabetes, early treatment of insulin resistance could delay or prevent progression to type 2 diabetes and insulin replacement therapy [3, 4]. While many oral antidiabetic therapeutics lower circulating blood glucose (BG), these drugs do not specifically improve insulin sensitivity [3]. A historical class of antidiabetic agents, the thiazolidinediones (TZDs), has potent insulin‐sensitizing effects by enhancing adipose tissue function and expansion capacity, particularly in subcutaneous white adipose tissue (scWAT) [5]. While TZDs are effective at improving insulin resistance, off‐target effects on cardiovascular, kidney, and bone health have reduced their clinical use [1, 6]. Therefore, novel insulin sensitizers are needed. Recent advancements in nanotherapeutics, a pharmacological approach to bind or encapsulate drugs to/in nanoparticles to increase drug solubility, half‐life, bioavailability, or tissue specificity, may provide such novel approaches [7].

A variety of nanoparticles are being explored for therapeutics [8]. Among these, FDA‐approved iron oxide magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs) show promise for tissue or cell‐targeted pharmacology due to their strong magnetic properties, straightforward synthesis, biocompatibility, and minimal cytotoxicity in mammalian tissues [9, 10]. Applications of MNPs are broad, supporting diagnostic imaging, magnetic hyperthermia, and tissue‐targeted drug delivery when used in combination with external or implanted magnetic fields [9, 11]. While much focus in nanomedicine has been directed to tumor‐targeted cancer therapies, targeted drug delivery to metabolically active tissues, such as adipose tissue, could enhance the specificity of oral antidiabetic therapeutics. Here, we assess the use of MNPs to enhance the specificity of a well‐characterized TZD, rosiglitazone, as a proof‐of‐concept study for adipose tissue targeting.

As a key metabolic organ, adipose tissue becomes a driver of metabolic impairment when it is dysfunctional, such as in obesity or lipodystrophies [12, 13, 14]. Importantly, different adipose tissue depots uniquely influence metabolic health. While visceral obesity is associated with negative metabolic health, preserving expansion of scWAT provides a “safe storage” depot for excess nutrients [15, 16, 17, 18, 19]. Therefore, preserving and/or restoring adipose tissue function in obesity by promoting the health and deposition of lipids in scWAT is a potential therapeutic strategy to prevent metabolic disease in obesity.

TZDs are synthetic ligands for peroxisome proliferator‐activated receptor gamma (PPARγ), a ligand‐inducible transcription factor highly expressed in adipose tissue [5, 20]. Ligand activation of the PPARγ‐retinoid X receptor alpha (RXRα) heterodimer induces the expression of target genes that drive adipogenesis, lipid uptake and transport, adiponectin production and secretion, and anti‐inflammatory cytokine production, thereby restoring adipose tissue health in obesity [5, 6, 20]. TZD action is especially potent in scWAT, generating new insulin‐sensitive adipocytes to store excess lipids and sparing insulin‐responsive tissues such as the liver and pancreas from the metabolically toxic effects of ectopic lipid deposition [1, 5, 20, 21]. The restorative effects of TZDs on adipose tissue function and insulin sensitivity, alongside their adverse side effects already mentioned, make TZDs suitable for proof‐of‐concept studies assessing strategies for adipose tissue‐targeted therapies to treat insulin resistance in obesity. Previously, we demonstrated that rosiglitazone can be adsorbed to alendronic acid‐coated MNPs (rosiMNPs) and targeted to the subcutaneous inguinal WAT (ingWAT) depot using an implanted magnetic field, where rosiglitazone retains its biological activity in vivo [11]. Building on this prior work that demonstrated drug loading, magnetic targeting, and preserved activity of rosiglitazone when bound to MNPs, here we evaluate the therapeutic efficacy and physiological effects of adipose tissue‐targeted rosiMNPs in a rodent model of diet‐induced obesity and insulin resistance.

Here, we assessed the potential of an 18 days course of rosiMNPs targeted specifically to ingWAT to improve insulin resistance in obese, insulin resistant mice. We also report on the benefits and drawbacks of using MNPs to target rosiglitazone to adipose tissue and discuss the limitations of drug adsorption to MNPs. Our results demonstrate the potential of adipose tissue for targeted insulin‐sensitizing therapeutics.

Methods

Adsorption and Release Profiles of Rosiglitazone to/From Magnetic Nanoparticles (rosiMNPs)

Iron oxide MNPs coated with alendronic acid (Al) and adsorbed to rosiglitazone (rosiMNPs) were prepared as previously described [11]. As the dynamic laser light scattering (Malvern Zetasizer, Westborough, MA) showed a main peak around 50 nm and a second one around 150 nm, as before, we did not analyze the magnetic core size by transmission electron microscopy. The zeta potential was identical to the previously measured −42 mV. Al‐coated MNPs (200 μg) were washed (2× MilliQ H2O), suspended in 0.9mM rosiglitazone‐HCl in H2O (Ontario Chemicals Inc., Guelph, ON), sonicated (40 kHz, 10 s), and shaken (750 rpm, 24 h RT). Adsorption reactions took place in individual glass vials with 200 μg MNPs in 1 mL of 0.9mM rosiglitazone, as large‐scale reactions were inconsistent. Adsorption curves were generated by calculating the concentration of rosiglitazone (abs 318 nm) in the supernatant at 0, 2, 3, 4, and 24 h. A nickel‐plated magnet (9.525 mm diameter × 9.525 mm thick, Grade N38, pull strength: 160 oz.; Gaussian surface: 4500; Gauss Boys Super Magnets, Portland OR) was used to accumulate rosiMNPs against the side of the vial for clean supernatant collection. Following the 24 h adsorption, rosiMNPs were resuspended in 1× phosphate buffered saline (PBS) and shaken at 750 rpm for 24 h at 37°C. The supernatant was sampled hourly to generate cumulative release profiles (% of bound rosiglitazone released from MNPs over 24 h).

Animals and Housing

Animal use protocols were approved by the Animal Care and Use Committee, University of Northern British Columbia (UNBC), in accordance with the Canadian Council of Animal Care. Prior to experiments, male C57BL/6 mice (Charles River Laboratories, Montreal, QC) were housed at 21°C on a 12 h light/dark cycle (7:00–19:00) in groups of five, with sterile corncob bedding, two bedding squares, ad libitum access to water, and a standard rodent chow diet containing 13.5% of kilocalories as fat (5001, LabDiet Inc., Leduc, AB).

Model Generation: Diet‐Induced Obesity and Insulin Resistance

At 10 weeks of age, mice were either fed the standard chow diet (n = 10) or a high‐fat diet (HFD) containing 45% of kilocalories as fat (D12451, Research Diets, New Brunswick, NJ, n = 40) ad libitum for 12 weeks to induce obesity and insulin resistance. Body weight (BW) was measured weekly. Random‐fed BG (mmol/L) was measured every 4 weeks and fasted BG (mmol/L, 16 h fast) was measured after 12 weeks of HFD using a handheld glucometer (OneTouch Ultra 2 Meter, LifeScan Inc., Milpitas, CA) by sampling from the saphenous vein between 9:00 and 11:00. After 12 weeks of HFD, random‐fed plasma insulin (Ultrasensitive Insulin ELISA kit [Lot#09858], ALPCO, Salem, NH), insulin sensitivity (insulin tolerance test [ITT]), and oral glucose tolerance (oral glucose tolerance test [OGTT]) were measured. For the ITT, BG was measured at 0, 10, 20, 30, and 60 min following a 4 h fast and an intraperitoneal (ip) injection of insulin (1.5 U/kg Humalog, Eli Lilly, Indianapolis, IN). The OGTT measured BG at 0, 10, 20, 30, 60, and 120 min after a 16 h fast and a bolus of 40% D‐glucose by oral gavage (2 g/kg, Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO).

Surgical Implantation of Neodymium Magnet in scWAT

Following assessment of carbohydrate metabolism, mice were weight‐matched, grouped (n = 10/group, five groups, Table 1), and retained on their respective diet for the remainder of the experiment. Sample size was determined using the resource equation method, accounting for potential animal loss to ensure sufficient statistical power in this long‐term model of obesity. Mice in the HFDmag + rosiMNP group were given prophylactic analgesia and anesthetized using isoflurane (4% induction, 2%–3% maintenance). Prior to surgery, tools were sterilized using a bead sterilizer (Fine Science Tools, Foster City, CA). The flank was shaved, skin sterilized 3× with alternating chlorohexidine gluconate and 70% isopropanol, and an incision (3 cm) was made to expose the subcutaneous ingWAT depot on the right of the mouse in the prone position (Figure S1). An incision (1 cm) was made in the ingWAT, and a small, gold‐plated neodymium magnet (4.0 mm diameter × 1.0 mm thick, 0.19 g, Grade N52, pull strength: 14 oz; flux density at surface 3309 gauss: Gaussian surface: 2100; Gauss Boys Super Magnets) was placed vertically into the pocket (approximately 1 cm deep) using titanium forceps (Fine Science Tools) and held in place with a single 3‐0 undyed, braided polyglactin 910 (Vicryl suture, Ethicon, Somerville, NJ) (Figure S1). The skin was closed with two to three sutures and tissue glue (Vetbond Tissue Adhesive, Associated Veterinary Purchasing, Langley, CA). Animals were placed in a heated recovery cage for monitoring. All other HFD groups received a sham surgery including all steps except placement of the magnet. The lean control group received no surgery.

Treatment

Topical analgesia was provided over the wound for 3 days post surgery, and once fully recovered (11 days), animals were dosed daily with their respective treatments for 18 days (Table 1). RosiMNPs (1.5 mg rosi/kg/day bound to 151.40 ± 63.67 μg MNPs [mean ± SD]) were prepared daily (see Section 2.1) for subcutaneous (sc) injection, where they were vortexed and injected (75.97 ± 31.72 μL) directly above the implanted magnet or sham surgery site. BW was measured every 4 days to assess overall health of the animals (predetermined endpoint > 20% BW = removal from study, not implemented). At the end of the treatment period, insulin sensitivity was assessed by ITT (1.5 U/kg insulin), circulating insulin (ng/mL), and glucose (mmol/L). One week post ITT, plasma and tissues were collected by dissection (interscapular brown adipose tissue [iBAT], ingWAT [right and left depots], gonadal WAT [gWAT], liver, pancreas, kidney). Adipose tissue, liver, and pancreas were weighed with half flash frozen for RNA extraction and half fixed in 10% formalin. Plasma adiponectin (mg/mL, Mouse Adiponectin ELISA Kit [Lot#1116858‐7], Abcam, Cambridge, UK) and TNFα (pg/mL, Mouse TNFα ELISA Kit [Lot#2101080199], Abcam) were measured. Many samples fell below the lowest point of the standard curve for TNFα and were artificially assigned the value of the limit of detection (LOD) of the kit (9.1 pg/mL). The number of samples assigned the LOD value from each group are as follows: chow: five, HFD + vehicle: seven, HFDmag + rosiMNP: three, HFD + rosiMNP: three, and HFD + rosi: eight.

RNA Extraction and cDNA Synthesis

RNA was isolated (TRIzol, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) as per the manufacturer's protocol. Genomic DNA was removed (Turbo DNA‐free Kit, Thermo Fisher Scientific), RNA concentration and purity measured, and integrity assessed on a 1% bleach, 1.5% agarose gel with 0.1 μL/mL 10,000× GelRed Nucleic Acid Stain (VWR International, Radnor, PA). RNA (500 ng) was reverse transcribed to cDNA (iScript Reverse Transcriptase Supermix, BioRad Laboratories, Hercules, CA).

Measurement of Reference and PPARγ Target Gene Expression With RT‐qPCR

Reference genes with GeNorm M values < 1 (Maestro, BioRad Laboratories) and no significant differences in expression between groups were deemed suitable for normalization. Expression of PPARγ target genes (adipocyte protein 2 [aP2], cluster of differentiation 36 [CD36], lipoprotein lipase [LPL], tumor necrosis factor alpha [TNFα], uncoupling protein 1 [UCP1], aquaporin 2 [AQP2], Klotho, sodium channel epithelial 1 subunit gamma [SCNN1G], serum/glucocorticoid‐regulated kinase 1 [SGK1], transforming growth factor‐beta [TGFβ]) was measured in ingWAT, gWAT, liver, and kidney by RT‐qPCR using SYBR Green I detection chemistry (BioRad Laboratories) with the following reaction conditions: 95°C/3 min, 95°C/10 s, and (56.5°C–60°C)/10 s (40 times), and melt curve assessment for target specificity: 65°C/5 s + 0.5°C/cycle to 95°C. Primers and probe were designed in accordance with the MIQE guidelines using the NCBI PrimerBLAST and IDT PrimerQuest tools (Integrated DNA Technologies, Newark, NJ) (Table S1), respectively [22]. Relative mRNA expression was determined using the ΔΔCq method (Maestro Software, BioRad Laboratories).

Measurement of Adipocyte Area in Right ingWAT Depots and Assessment of Iron Deposition in ingWAT (Right and Left), Liver, and Kidney

Tissues were fixed, paraffin embedded, sectioned (5 μm), and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H + E, Wax‐It Histology Services or University of British Columbia Animal Care Services Research Histology Lab, Vancouver, BC) or Perls' Prussian Blue for ferric iron and counterstained with Nuclear Fast Red (n = 3/group). In H + E‐stained sections, eight images per sample (n = 4/group) were captured (Olympus Life Science, Tokyo, Japan), binarized, thresholded, and area measured (ImageJ, National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD). Frequency distributions were calculated and compared between groups (Prism, GraphPad, La Jolla, CA).

Statistical Analysis

Differences in BW, BG, plasma insulin, plasma adiponectin, plasma TNFα, insulin sensitivity, glucose tolerance, and relative gene expression between the five groups were determined by one‐ or two‐way ANOVA with Holm‐Šidák's correction for multiple comparisons or mixed effects analysis. For comparison of relative gene expression between two groups, two‐tailed, two‐sample equal variance Student's t‐tests were employed. Frequency distributions of adipocyte area were analyzed using Fisher's exact test. Data are presented as mean ± SD and significance declared at p < 0.05 (Prism). Samples outside of mean ± 2SD were deemed outliers and removed.

Results

Rosiglitazone Was Adsorbed to and Released From Alendronic Acid‐Coated MNPs

Optimal time for rosiglitazone (350 μg) adsorption to 200 μg MNPs in vitro was achieved following a 48 h incubation (Figure 1A). However, the percentage of rosiglitazone released from MNPs following a 24 h adsorption period was significantly higher (Figure 1B). To maximize rosiglitazone release, we utilized a 24 h incubation for adsorption. In vitro characterization of rosiglitazone adsorption and release profiles concluded that each 200 μg aliquot of MNPs adsorbed 34.35% ± 0.82% of 350 μg rosiglitazone following a 24 h adsorption incubation, generating rosiMNPs containing 0.60 μg rosi/μg MNP. Release of rosiglitazone plateaued after 11 h, resulting in 62.77% ± 0.94% release following hourly washes for 12 h. This allowed us to achieve a daily dose of 1.5 mg rosi/kg with variable amounts of MNPs (Table 1).

A Model of Diet‐Induced Obesity and Insulin Resistance Was Induced With 12 Weeks of HFD Feeding

As expected, obesity and insulin resistance were induced with 12 weeks of HFD feeding (Figure 2). BW increased in all groups fed HFD compared to chow‐fed mice after 1 week and for all weeks thereafter (Figure 2A). Significantly higher BG concentrations were observed following 4 and 8 weeks of HFD feeding when compared to chow‐fed feeding (Figure 2B). Plasma insulin concentrations (ng/mL, fed) were significantly elevated after 12 weeks of HFD feeding compared to chow feeding (Figure 2C). Mice fed HFD for 12 weeks were glucose intolerant (Figure 2D,E) and had significantly impaired glucose clearance 10 min post insulin administration (Figure 2F,G), compared to mice fed chow diet.

Treatment of Obese Mice With rosiMNPs and an Implanted Magnet Improved Insulin Sensitivity Similar to Systemically Administered Rosiglitazone Without Changes in BW

BW was not changed in obese mice treated with rosiglitazone (systemic or targeted) compared to vehicle (Figure 3A). Obese mice treated systemically with rosiglitazone (HFD + rosi) had significantly increased glucose clearance in response to an insulin challenge (1.5 U/kg) at all time points compared to obese control mice (HFD + vehicle) (Figure 3C). In fact, we observed no significant differences in insulin‐induced glucose clearance between HFD + rosi mice and age‐matched chow‐fed mice (Figure 3C). Obese mice treated with rosiMNPs (HFD + rosiMNP and HFDmag + rosiMNP) also demonstrated significant improvements in insulin‐induced glucose clearance, with improved glucose clearance at 10 and 20 min or 10, 20, and 30 min, respectively, when compared to obese control mice (HFD + vehicle) (Figure 3C,D).

RosiMNP Treatment Induced the Expression of PPARγ Target Genes in the Targeted ingWAT, With No Changes in Circulating Adiponectin or TNFα

Following dosing, mRNA for two PPARγ target genes, aP2 and LPL, was significantly upregulated in the right ingWAT lobe of obese mice treated systemically with rosiglitazone (HFD + rosi) and obese mice treated with rosiMNPs with an implanted magnet (HFDmag + rosiMNP), compared to obese controls (HFD + vehicle) (Figure 4A). Based on the known effects of PPARγ agonists on TNFα expression, significant upregulations in TNFα mRNA expression in obese mice treated with rosiMNPs were unexpected (Figure 4A). Comparison of PPARγ target gene expression in targeted vs. untargeted ingWAT lobes in HFDmag + rosiMNP mice revealed significant changes in CD36 and UCP1 expression in the targeted lobe (Figure 4B). While plasma adiponectin concentrations were significantly elevated in mice treated systemically with rosiglitazone (HFD + rosi) when compared to obese controls (HFD + vehicle), no significant upregulation of circulating adiponectin was observed in HFDmag + rosiMNP mice compared to obese controls (Figure 3D). No significant differences in circulating TNFα were observed between groups (Figure 3D).

RosiMNP Treatment in Obese Mice Promotes Changes in Adipocyte Morphology

Gross iBAT weight did not differ post treatment. ingWAT weight significantly increased with obesity and in mice receiving rosiMNPs without an implanted magnet compared to those with an implanted magnet, while gWAT weight increased only in mice treated with systemic rosiglitazone when compared to lean controls (Figure 3B). Significant differences in the distribution of adipocytes by size in the targeted ingWAT lobe were observed with obesity and treatment (Figure 5). Interestingly, mice receiving rosiMNPs with an implanted magnet had a similar distribution of adipocyte size to chow‐fed mice, which was significantly different from obese mice treated with vehicle (Figure 5A–C).

Systemic Rosiglitazone Treatment but Not rosiMNP Treatment Induced PPARγ Target Gene Expression in Liver and gWAT

As expected, liver weight (Figure 6C) was increased and genes (aP2, CD36) involved in lipid storage and metabolism were upregulated in liver of obese mice compared to lean controls (Figure 6B). Systemic rosiglitazone treatment increased the relative expression of aP2, CD36, and LPL, an effect not observed in mice with an implanted magnet treated with rosiMNPs (HFDmag + rosiMNP) (Figure 6B). In gWAT, only UCP1 mRNA expression was induced with systemic rosiglitazone treatment compared to obese controls, but not in mice receiving rosiMNPs (Figure 6A). We observed no significant differences in relative gene expression of select PPARγ target genes in kidney between the five groups (Figure 6D).

Discussion

Short‐term (18 days), low‐dose (1.5 mg rosi/kg/day) rosiglitazone treatment targeted to one ingWAT depot improved insulin sensitivity similar to systemic rosiglitazone treatment (1.5 mg/kg/day, 18 days) in obese, insulin resistant male mice. Rosiglitazone is a potent whole‐body insulin sensitizer via actions on the nuclear receptor PPARγ [6]. The comparable improvements in insulin sensitivity observed with systemic rosiglitazone and ingWAT‐targeted rosiglitazone delivery (HFDmag + rosiMNP) reinforce adipose tissue as a key site mediating rosiglitazone's effects and maintaining peripheral insulin sensitivity [5, 23].

While weight gain is a known effect of TZD treatment in clinical and preclinical rodent models, the low dose and short duration of treatment in this study did not induce significant effects on BW which are observed with higher doses (8–10 mg/kg) and longer duration (4–6 weeks) of TZDs [24, 25]. Despite this, changes to adipose tissue morphology were observed with a significant shift to smaller adipocytes in the targeted ingWAT depot from mice receiving adipose tissue‐targeted rosiglitazone (HFDmag + rosiMNP) compared to ingWAT from obese controls (HFD + vehicle), resulting in a profile similar to ingWAT from lean mice. This is compatible with rosiglitazone's effects to promote adipocyte hyperplasia through increased adipogenesis [6, 26, 27]. These morphological signs of adipose tissue health align with the measured improvements in whole‐animal insulin sensitivity in these mice and rosiglitazone's ability to promote the generation of small, insulin‐sensitive adipocytes and enhance whole‐body insulin sensitivity [26, 28, 29].

Circulating adiponectin was increased in mice receiving systemic rosiglitazone, but not in mice receiving rosiMNPs compared to obese controls (Figure 3D). The adiponectin gene (ADIPOQ) is under the transcriptional control of PPARγ and rosiglitazone therapy is associated with elevated circulating adiponectin [29, 30]. Given adiponectin is secreted from peripheral white adipose tissue throughout the body, it is likely that localized rosiglitazone delivery to a single ingWAT depot did not stimulate enough adiponectin secretion to increase circulating levels and was not a key mechanism for the observed whole‐body improvement in insulin sensitivity in HFDmag + rosiMNP mice.

The physiological and morphological observations of improved adipose tissue health aligned with PPARγ target gene expression in ingWAT. Genes involved in lipid uptake and intracellular fatty acid transport (aP2, LPL) and thermogenesis (UCP1) were upregulated similarly in mice treated with rosiglitazone systemically (HFD + rosi) or targeted to ingWAT (HFDmag + rosiMNP) compared to obese controls (HFD + vehicle) [31, 32]. While TNFα mRNA expression was significantly elevated in obese compared to lean mice, a known effect of HFD feeding, the expected downregulation of TNFα mRNA with systemic rosiglitazone treatment was absent; instead, TNFα mRNA was elevated in HFDmag + rosiMNP mice [33, 34]. Plasma levels of TNFα were low in all groups. No significant differences in circulating TNFα were seen between groups, suggesting that the elevated TNFα mRNA expression seen in the targeted ingWAT depot did not result in a systemic effect. We speculate that the increased TNFα mRNA in ingWAT of HFDmag + rosiMNP mice is a local inflammatory response to the implantation of the magnet [35]. When comparing gene expression in the right, targeted to the left, untargeted ingWAT depots within HFDmag + rosiMNP mice, we observed a downregulation in CD36 and an upregulation in UCP1 mRNA expression in the targeted depot. Induction of UCP1 mRNA expression in rosiglitazone treated groups (HFD + rosi and HFDmag + rosiMNP) is a well‐known effect of PPARγ agonism that contributes to restoring energy balance in obesity [36]. The increase in expression of UCP1 in the targeted depot when compared to the untargeted depot supports PPARγ activity in the targeted ingWAT depot of HFDmag + rosiMNP mice.

To assess specificity of rosiMNPs, we measured select PPARγ target gene expression in visceral gWAT, liver, and kidney. TNFα mRNA was increased in gWAT of all HFD groups compared to lean controls, compatible with the known induction of hypoxia and a proinflammatory gene profile with diet‐induced obesity [17]. UCP1 was the only PPARγ target gene induced in gWAT with systemic rosiglitazone treatment compared to obese vehicle controls. While not entirely understood, the diminished response of visceral WAT to rosiglitazone action compared to scWAT is thought to be attributed to lower basal activity of the peroxisome proliferator response element (PPRE) in visceral WAT even with high concentrations of rosiglitazone [37]. This phenomenon is validated by rosiglitazone's known promotion of adipose tissue expansion subcutaneously and not viscerally [20]. Given the low levels of UCP1 expression in gWAT, any increase in UCP1 with rosiglitazone treatment would be detected [38]. Interestingly, the induction of UCP1 mRNA in gWAT of mice treated with systemic rosiglitazone (HFD + rosi) was not observed in the group receiving adipose tissue‐targeted treatment (HFDmag + rosiMNP), supporting tissue‐specific targeting to the injected ingWAT depot. Elevated liver weight in mice treated with systemic rosiglitazone and rosiMNPs compared to obese controls prompted analysis of PPARγ target gene expression in this tissue. In obesity, PPARγ expression is induced in hepatocytes and TZDs promote lipid uptake by hepatocytes reducing circulating fatty acids [39]. Compatible with this, we observed significantly induced PPARγ target gene expression (aP2, CD36, LPL) with systemic rosiglitazone treatment compared to obese controls (HFD + vehicle). PPARγ target gene activation was not observed in liver of HFDmag + rosiMNP mice compared to obese controls (HFD + vehicle). Taken together, expression profiling of PPARγ target genes in gWAT and liver supports the conclusion that rosiMNPs in the presence of an implanted magnet achieved retention of rosiglitazone in the targeted ingWAT depot.

Specificity of rosiMNP delivery to targeted ingWAT was further supported by histological evidence using Prussian Blue iron stain, where iron deposition was only detected in the right, targeted ingWAT depots of mice treated with rosiMNPs, with no iron being detected in the contralateral ingWAT depot, kidney, or liver of these mice or controls. Interestingly, iron deposition was greatest around the site of the implanted magnet in HFDmag + rosiMNP mice. These results are consistent with existing knowledge on iron oxide MNPs, where implanted magnetic fields result in enhanced MNP localization in target tissues and improve treatment specificity [11].

A known side effect of rosiglitazone treatment is fluid retention and subsequent edema‐induced congestive heart failure. These symptoms are ascribed to PPARγ agonism in kidney, including regulation of genes encoding subunits or kinases that impact ion/water channel availability or activity and cytokines or endocrine factors that regulate fluid and water balance [6, 40]. After 18 days of rosiglitazone treatment, we observed no changes in PPARγ target gene expression in kidney between groups. Induction of PPARγ activity in kidney and subsequent fluid retention are reported with higher doses of rosiglitazone than used here (> 10 mg rosi/kg) [41, 42]. We conclude that the relatively low dose (1.5 mg rosi/kg) and short duration (18 days) of treatment used in our study, while appropriate for the induction of insulin sensitivity, were not sufficient to induce gene expression changes in the kidney [41, 42]. Therefore, to fully assess the specificity of adipose tissue‐targeted rosiglitazone treatment and the avoidance of nonadipose tissue side effects, a higher dose and longer duration of rosiglitazone are required. Unfortunately, several characteristics of rosiMNPs made this unattainable. While synthesis of MNPs and rosiglitazone adsorption to the MNPs were reproducible for small quantities of particles, when scaled for larger batches we noted low adsorption efficiency, limiting our dose to 1.5 mg rosi/kg/day [11]. To achieve consistent dosing, particles were generated daily, in small batches, a cumbersome process not compatible with long‐term dosing needed to assess the adverse effects of rosiglitazone on kidney and bone. Several other nanoparticle systems are being explored for drug delivery, including protein‐based, polymeric, polysaccharide‐derived, and lipid nanoparticles (LNPs) [43]. Ongoing experiments are exploring the potential of LNPs, whose clinical safety is exemplified by their use in the first mRNA vaccine during the COVID‐19 pandemic [44, 45]. While current clinical LNP formulations are limited in their ability to target specific tissues, novel LNP formulations with high affinity for adipose tissue are being engineered to overcome the dosing limitations of MNPs for chronic treatment. In addition, antibody conjugation to LNPs has been suggested as a way to guide LNPs to target tissues and limit accumulation in tissues such as liver and spleen [45]. Exploring LNPs for adipose tissue drug targeting could allow for the delivery of higher drug dosages while avoiding the need for an implanted magnet, therefore enhancing clinical translatability. The results presented herein demonstrate the benefits of targeting superficially located subcutaneous adipose tissue to ameliorate adipose dysfunction in obesity to restore whole‐body insulin sensitivity and improve metabolic health.
