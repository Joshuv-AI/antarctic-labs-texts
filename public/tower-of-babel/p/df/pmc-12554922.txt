Hypercalcemia of Down Syndrome
Authors: Seth Lamb, Melissa Litao, Nasreen Talib, Jacob Redel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe present a case of hypercalcemia in a 12-month-old female with Down syndrome. The patient presented with significant weight loss (810 g since her 6-month visit) and severe growth failure (weight 5.8 kg, −4.5 SDs). Laboratory evaluation revealed severe hypercalcemia with appropriately suppressed PTH and 1,25-hydroxyvitamin D levels. Renal ultrasound confirmed medullary nephrocalcinosis. After excluding common etiologies, the patient was diagnosed with hypercalcemia of Down syndrome, also termed Abnormal Calcium, Calcinosis, Creatinine in Down syndrome. Treatment with intravenous fluids, calcitonin, and transition to a low-calcium formula (Calcilo XD) normalized serum calcium levels. The patient maintained normal calcium levels on restricted dietary calcium intake for 7 months after discharge. This case adds to the limited literature on this rare but significant association, highlighting the importance of considering hypercalcemia in patients with Down syndrome presenting with growth failure. Although the pathophysiology remains unclear, a hyperabsorptive intestinal mechanism has been proposed. Early recognition and dietary calcium restriction are essential to prevent significant morbidity in affected children.

Introduction

Down syndrome is the most common chromosomal condition diagnosed around the world. In the United States, almost 5000 babies with Down syndrome are born annually [1]. Down syndrome is associated with numerous medical conditions due to the extra genetic material from the 21st chromosome. The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends screening for medical conditions seen in children with Down syndrome, include hearing loss, obstructive sleep apnea, congenital heart defects, thyroid problems, autoimmune disorders, visual impairments, immune problems, and joint problems, among others [2]. Hypercalcemia has not traditionally been associated with Down syndrome.

Hypercalcemia in infants and young children can stem from a variety of causes, including hyperparathyroidism, vitamin D intoxication, subcutaneous fat necrosis, medications, and malignancy, among others. Clinical manifestations include vomiting, polyuria, kidney injury, nephrocalcinosis, nephrolithiasis, and failure to thrive. Hypercalcemia of Down syndrome can lead to severe growth failure and renal impairment in affected infants and young children. Although it is thought to be rare and its underlying mechanism remains unclear, delayed diagnosis may lead to significant morbidity, as well as expensive and time-consuming hospitalization and work up. Here, we present a case of hypercalcemia of Down syndrome, a condition sometimes referred to as ABCD syndrome (abnormal calcium, calcinosis, creatinine in Down syndrome) to contribute to the limited literature and raise awareness of this condition.

Case Presentation

A 12-month-old female with a medical history of Down syndrome, congenital hypothyroidism, and dysphagia with G-tube dependence presented at a well-child visit with an 810-g weight loss from her 6-month visit. There was no personal or family history of autoimmune disorders.

Diagnostic Assessment

Initial laboratory values revealed a calcium level of 17.1 mg/dL (SI: 4.27 mmol/L) (reference range, 8.6-10.5 mg/dL [SI: 2.1-2.6 mmol/L]), phosphorus 5.2 mg/dL (SI: 1.7 mmol/L) (reference range, 4.2-7.0 mg/dL [SI: 1.4-2.3 mmol/L]), albumin 4.9 g/dL (SI: 49 g/L) (reference range, 2.9-5.1 g/dL [SI: 29-51 g/L]), 25-hydroxyvitamin D 50 ng/mL (SI: 125 nmol/L) (reference range, 30-80 ng/mL [SI: 74.9-199.7 nmol/L]), and alkaline phosphatase 103 IU/L (SI: 103 IU/L) (reference range, 140-335 IU/L [SI: 140-335 IU/L]). Weight was 5.8 kg (−4.5 SDs). She was admitted to an outside pediatric intensive care unit 2 days after presentation with failure to thrive and severe hypercalcemia. Fluids were initiated at 1.25 times the maintenance rate, and home feeds of Similac Advanced (24 kcal/oz) via G-tube were continued. Her calcium levels remained elevated (Fig. 1). After 3 days, calcitonin was administered, and her formula was transitioned to Calcilo XD XR (Abbott Nutrition). Additional laboratory tests showed appropriately low 1,25-hydroxyvitamin D of 13.4 pg/mL (SI: 33.4 pmol/L) (reference range, 24-86 pg/mL [SI: 59.9-214.7 pmol/L]) and low PTH at 8.9 pg/mL (SI: 0.94 pmol/L) (reference range, 10-89 pg/mL [SI: 1.1-9.4 pmol/L]), consistent with a non-PTH-mediated etiology. Liver enzymes were within normal limits. The urine calcium-to-creatinine ratio was elevated at 1.7 mg/mg (SI: 4.79 mmol/mmol) (reference range, 0.02-0.50 mg/mg [SI: 0.07-1.5 mmol/mmol]), an expected finding representing an appropriate renal response to significant hypercalcemia as the kidneys attempt to excrete the excess calcium load. PTH-related peptide was undetectable (≤4.2 pmol/L) (SI: ≤4.2 pmol/L) (reference range, <2.0 pmol/L [SI: <2.0 pmol/L]).

Serum calcium levels following hospital admission. Patient was admitted on day 2. Intravenous fluids were initiated on day 2. A dose of calcitonin was administered on day 5 and Calcilo XD formula was started on day 6.

A renal ultrasound confirmed medullary nephrocalcinosis. With no other identifiable cause for hypercalcemia, the patient was diagnosed with ABCD syndrome.

Treatment

She continued taking Calcilo XD formula, and serum calcium levels remained normal. Hypophosphatemia, thought to be due to refeeding syndrome, was treated with PHOS-NaK 0.5 packets twice daily. Her serum calcium normalized 6 days after admission. She was discharged on 960 mL of Calcilo XD 20 kcal/oz formula daily (165 mL/kg/d).

Outcome and Follow-up

As an outpatient, she continued Calcilo XD formula and maintained normal calcium 7 months after discharge.

In an early case reported by Proesmans et al, a toddler with Down syndrome was found to have hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, medullary nephrocalcinosis, and renal failure [3]. The cause of these abnormalities was speculated to be related to dietary factors, particularly high calcium intake. In another case from Andreoli et al, a child with Down syndrome also presented with hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, and nephrocalcinosis, despite no overt intake of excess calcium or vitamin D [4]. Both cases showed improvement with dietary calcium restriction, pointing toward a potential hyperabsorptive mechanism in the intestines. Because of this connection, it was hypothesized that hyperabsorption of calcium could be linked to genetic overexpression related to chromosome 21 [5].

A third report from Filler et al describes a male with Down syndrome presenting with hypercalcemia and nephrocalcinosis, who was managed successfully with a low-calcium diet [6]. This case, like the previous ones, ruled out common causes of hypercalcemia such as hyperparathyroidism and vitamin D intoxication. Notably, the patient was exclusively formula-fed, with no exposure to cow's milk, supporting the hypothesis of excessive intestinal calcium absorption rather than overconsumption.

Five newer case reports have been published, similar to the previous 3. These cases emphasize the importance of dietary calcium restriction as the primary intervention. Ramage et al (2002) detailed a 4-year-old female with incidental hypercalcemia and renal impairment, which improved with dietary adjustments [6]. Tran et al (2009) reported a 24-month-old male whose calcium levels normalized with a low-calcium formula, showing significant weight improvement by 40 months [7]. Nguyen et al (2021) described an 11-month-old male with intractable vomiting and failure to thrive, resolving hypercalcemia with dietary modification, although weight gain remained a concern [8]. Feller et al (2024) presented 2 cases: a 4-year-old with recurrent hospitalizations requiring specialized formulas and a 17-month-old with poor weight gain, both responding well to calcium restriction [9].

It is worth noting that 1 previously reported case presented with a similar constellation of hypercalcemia, hypercalciuria, and nephrocalcinosis in the setting of Down syndrome, but was ultimately managed as a case of incomplete distal renal tubular acidosis with alkali therapy [10]. Although we have excluded this case from our literature comparison because of its distinct etiology, it serves as an important reminder of the need for a thorough diagnostic evaluation.

A consistent and concerning feature across all reported cases of this syndrome, including our own, is the presence of medullary nephrocalcinosis. While the exact timeline for its development is not defined in this condition, evidence from other causes of infantile hypercalcemia suggests its onset can be remarkably rapid. For instance, a report on subcutaneous fat necrosis found that infants as young as aged 21 to 38 days old had developed nephrocalcinosis secondary to severe hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria [11]. This finding underscores that the renal damage in ABCD syndrome may occur rapidly upon development of severe hypercalcemia.

Our case adds to the existing literature, demonstrating that ABCD syndrome should be considered in a child with Down syndrome and poor weight gain or delayed growth. The underlying pathophysiology remains unclear. Although a state of intestinal hyperabsorption is the most likely source of the profound calcium load because of increased wasting of calcium from the kidneys, the mechanism appears to be vitamin D-independent. The appropriately suppressed 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D levels seen in our case and others effectively rule out classic vitamin D-mediated hyperabsorption. This points toward a more unique pathology, possibly involving a primary upregulation of intestinal calcium channels (eg, TRPV6) or another, unknown genetic influence from trisomy 21. Furthermore, given the increased prevalence of autoimmune conditions in individuals with Down syndrome, an underlying inflammatory or autoimmune-mediated mechanism for altered calcium homeostasis cannot be definitively excluded. Further investigation into the genetic and molecular mechanisms of this syndrome is warranted to enhance understanding of its etiology and inform management strategies.
