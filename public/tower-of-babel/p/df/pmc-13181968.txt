Public-private healthcare utilisation and satisfaction among older adults with palliative care needs in India: a study based on Longitudinal Ageing Study in India
Authors: Terrymize Immanuel, Benson Thomas M., Naveen Salins, Jenifer Jeba Sundararaj, Roop Gursahani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundStrengthening health systems to accommodate the growing need for palliative care is a global priority. Currently, one in eight older adults in India requires palliative care with complex healthcare needs. The interaction of older adults with these needs with public and private healthcare providers is unknown. This study aims to determine the patterns of public and private healthcare utilisation and their disparities among older adults with palliative care needs.MethodologyA cross-sectional study design was employed using the Wave-1 data of the LASI. Using the SPICT-LIS, 2903 older adults with palliative care needs were identified for the analysis. The primary outcome variable, i.e. the utilisation of the type of health facilities for outpatient visits and hospitalisation, was summarised as descriptive statistics. The Chi-square and Kruskal-Wallis tests were employed to identify the association between background characteristics and the utilisation of different facilities. Additionally, multivariate logistic regression (Odds Ratio) was performed to determine the factors associated with utilisation of private facilities.ResultsThe utilisation of private facilities (60.3%) was higher than that of public facilities (23%) and other facilities (16.6%) for outpatient care. Similarly, hospitalisation in private health facilities was the highest (58.3%), followed by public (39.7%) and other facilities (1.9%). Disparities in the utilisation of private facilities based on wealth status, caste, food insecurities and geographical regions were observed for both outpatient care and hospitalisation. A higher level of satisfaction with private facilities compared to public health facilities was observed in terms of waiting time, being treated respectfully, providing clear explanations, maintaining privacy during conversations, and cleanliness.ConclusionOlder adults with palliative care needs used private healthcare facilities significantly more than public ones for outpatient care and hospitalisation. Their use of private facilities was also linked to disparities based on socio-economic status. Addressing gaps in public health service provisions is crucial to achieving universal health coverage for older adults with palliative care needs. Public-private partnerships, social welfare schemes, and expanded health insurance could help reduce these disparities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14470-w.

Introduction

Strengthening health systems to accommodate the growing needs for palliative care is a global priority [1]. This is especially relevant to Low- and Middle-Income Countries (LMICs), as they account for 80% of global palliative care needs, and their health systems are often underprepared to meet this significant demand [2]. In India, one in eight older adults requires palliative care [3], and this is likely to increase as the population of those over 60 years is projected to reach 315 million by 2050, up from 103 million recorded in the 2011 census [4]. The healthcare consumption of older adults requiring palliative care is expected to be high due to their greater healthcare needs [5, 6]. However, access to palliative care is severely limited in India [7], and the provision of health services to older adults through existing systems has several gaps [8].

In India, both public and private healthcare facilities are the leading providers of healthcare, offering a wide range of services. However, the palliative care provisions are fragmented within the health system and across the country. Within this fragmented health system, palliative care services remain severely limited. The Lancet Commission on Palliative Care and Pain Relief estimated that approximately 7 million Indians require palliative care, yet only 1–2% of those in need have access to such services [9, 10]. The implementation of the National Programme for Palliative Care (NPPC) under the National Health Mission, by the Government of India, has been slow and uneven due to limited budget allocation and workforce shortages [11, 12]. Hence, the services for palliative care in India are predominantly concentrated in urban areas and tertiary facilities. The state of Kerala is the only exception with near universal access through a community-based model that is built on local self-government institutions and volunteer networks [7, 13, 14].

Public health facilities play an important role in reducing out-of-pocket expenditure and universal health coverage, but the services are constrained by delays, staffing shortages, infrastructure limitation and gaps in quality standards [15, 16]. On the contrary, private health facilities are able to provide timely and personalised services; however, the out-of-pocket expenditures and economic hardships are a significant concern [15, 17]. Several studies conducted in India have shown that the use of private healthcare among older adults is substantially higher than that of public healthcare [18, 19]. However, how older adults with palliative care needs interact with public and private healthcare providers remains largely unknown. This information is essential for strengthening public health infrastructure and establishing mechanisms for financial protection, as this vulnerable population will require long-term, multi-disciplinary care that can impoverish them. Additionally, such information can inform policy decisions on resource allocation and service provision. Therefore, this study examined patterns and disparities in the utilisation of public and private healthcare among older adults with palliative care needs in India.

Methods

Conceptual framework

This study is grounded in Andersen’s Behavioural Model of Health Services Use [20], integrated with the WHO Health System Responsiveness Framework [21] (Table 1). Andersen’s model is one of the most widely used theoretical frameworks in health services research. The model posits that the healthcare utilisation of an individual is determined by community-level factors (contextual characteristics) and individual characteristics.

In this study, contextual characteristics were represented by geographic region. This is brought about by differences in healthcare infrastructure, the number of public and private facilities, and socioeconomic development in different parts of India. Individual characteristics were classified into three domains as delineated in the model: (i) predisposing factors, encompassing demographic attributes (age, sex) and social structure variables (education, caste, religion); (ii) enabling factors, comprising economic status (wealth quintiles based on monthly per capita expenditure, health insurance coverage), local access (place of residence), and social support (living status, social activity, food insecurity); and (iii) need factors, including limitations in activities of daily living, body mass index, and diagnosed morbidity.

Health behaviours were operationalised as healthcare utilisation patterns. This included outpatient visits, hospitalisation, the type of facility used (public, private, other), and specific services utilised. Additionally, the service quality was assessed using the WHO Health System Responsiveness Framework. The WHO defines health system responsiveness as the aspects of care that reflect how well the health system meets patients’ legitimate expectations. While Andersen’s model traditionally categorises satisfaction under ‘outcomes,’ the six domains assessed in this study such as prompt attention (waiting time), dignity (being treated respectfully), communication (clear explanations), confidentiality (privacy during conversations), autonomy (choice of health provider), and quality of basic amenities (cleanliness) reflects quality of the services than health outcomes. In this study, the two frameworks serve a complementary analytical functions. The Andersen’s Behavioural Model provides the structure for identifying determinants of healthcare utilisation. The WHO Health System Responsiveness Framework, on the other hand, is used to assess the manner in which care is delivered across facility types through descriptive comparison of patient-reported experience scores. Responsiveness indicators are thus conceptualised as process quality measures associated with the type of facility utilised, rather than as direct outcomes of the determinants modelled in the regression analysis. This integrated approach enables the study to examine both the structural drivers of facility choice and the experiential quality of care received, providing a more comprehensive understanding of public-private disparities. Design

A cross-sectional study design was used, employing the Wave-1 data from the Longitudinal Ageing Study in India (LASI) [19]. LASI is a unique, nationally representative survey of 73,396 older adults and their spouses, selected from all Indian states and union territories through a multi-stage stratified area probability cluster sampling method. The survey was conducted between 2017 and 2019. Its primary aim is to examine the effects of ageing on health and disease, work and retirement, social relationships, economic status, family life, healthcare utilisation, the availability and use of social support schemes, and biomarker profiles. The survey is intended to be conducted every five years over the next 25 years, beginning with the baseline (Wave 1). Data collection involved individual questionnaires, household questionnaires, and community questionnaires. Data were collected using Computer Assisted Personal Interview (CAPI) technology, which included built-in checks to minimise errors. The survey is overseen by the International Institute of Population Sciences (IIPS), Mumbai, under the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare (MoHFW), Government of India, New Delhi.

Data

From the LASI Wave-1 dataset, 31,902 older adults aged 60 years and older were selected. Among them, 2,903 older adults with palliative care needs were identified using the general indicators of poor and deteriorating health from the Supportive and Palliative Care Indicator Tool for Low-Income Settings (SPICT-LIS). SPICT-LIS is an adapted version of the original SPICT, developed specifically for resource-limited settings to help identify individuals who may benefit from palliative care assessment and planning. The tool has been validated in low-income clinical settings and comprises two components: general indicators of poor or deteriorating health and clinical indicators related to life-limiting conditions.

In this study, we operationalised the general indicators of SPICT-LIS using comparable variables available in the LASI dataset. The general indicators include: poor performance status or declining performance, significant weight loss, dependence on care needs, persistent symptoms despite treatment, and unplanned hospital admissions/increased visits to hospitals or clinics. The detailed methodology for mapping these indicators to LASI variables has been described in our previous publication [3]. Briefly, poor performance status was identified as being confined to bed or chair for more than 14 days in the last 30 days, aside from any hospital or nursing home stays. Persistent symptoms were captured through questions on ongoing health complaints such as pain, breathlessness, fatigue, cough, etc., despite treatment. Dependence for care needs was measured as experiencing difficulties with everyday activities and requiring help from others to perform them. Increased healthcare use was measured as having to use hospitalisation more than three times a year with complications of chronic life-limiting illnesses. Using these operationalised indicators, 2,903 older adults meeting the criteria for palliative care needs were identified for analysis. While SPICT-LIS was originally designed for clinical use, its application to population-based survey data through proxy indicators represents a novel methodological approach to estimate palliative care needs at the population level. This operationalisation, detailed in our previous publication [3], enables estimation of palliative care burden using nationally representative data in settings where clinical records are unavailable. However, there are limitations to this approach. The use of indicators from population survey data to operationalise a clinical screening tool introduces the possibility of misclassification bias. Issues such as normalisation of symptoms, recall limitations, or cultural factors that discourage reporting of health complaints observed in survey settings can result in under identification of the cases. This potential under-identification suggests that the true population with palliative care needs may be larger than identified in this study, and the utilisation patterns observed here may represent a conservative estimate. Outcome variable

The main outcome variable of this study is the type of facility utilised for healthcare among older adults with palliative care needs. This variable was generated separately for the utilisation of outpatient visits and hospitalisation. The utilisation of healthcare facilities was elicited in the LASI survey through the following questions for outpatient visits: “Which type of facility did you last visit to see the healthcare provider?” and, similarly, for hospitalisation, “Which type of facility did you visit during your last hospitalisation?” The response included public facilities, private facilities, other facilities (NGO/Charity/Trust/Church-run hospitals, semi-public/private facilities, Health Camps, etc.). The reference period considered for both outpatient visits and hospitalisation was the past 12 months from the date of the survey.

The other outcome variables include the reason for outpatient visit and hospitalisation, services utilised during the visit, and process quality assessed through patient-reported experience. The reason for outpatient visits and hospitalisation was elicited with the following questions: “What is the main reason for your recent outpatient visit/last hospitalisation?” The response was categorised into acute conditions, chronic life-limiting conditions, and accidents and injuries. The services utilised were elicited using the question “What kind of treatment and/or diagnostics did you receive?” The response included medical consultations, medications (and injections), surgery, laboratory tests, imaging services (X-ray, CT scan, ultrasonogram, MRI), etc.

Patient-reported experience was assessed using six domains aligned with the WHO Health System Responsiveness Framework: prompt attention (waiting time), dignity (being treated respectfully), communication (providing clear explanations), confidentiality (privacy during conversations), autonomy (choice of health provider), and quality of basic amenities (cleanliness). While Andersen’s model broadly categorises satisfaction under outcomes, these domains more precisely capture process quality—how care is delivered rather than the health results of care. Responses were collected using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from very bad (1) to very good (5).

Exposure variables

The exposure variables were organised according to Andersen’s Behavioural Model of Health Services Use, as described in the Conceptual Framework section (Table 2).

Predisposing factors included demographic characteristics such as age (60–75 years, above 75 years) and sex (male, female), followed by social structure variables such as educational status (primary and below, middle and above), caste (Scheduled Caste and Scheduled Tribes, Other Backward Caste, Others), and religion (Hindu, Muslim, Christian, Others).

Enabling factors encompassed three sub-categories: (i) economic status based on monthly per-capita expenditure (MPCE) quintiles (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, richest) and health insurance coverage (yes, no); (ii) geographic access based on place of residence (rural, urban); and (iii) social support based on living status (living with spouse, living alone or with others), social activity (low/nil, moderate/high), and food insecurity (no, at least one indicator). Geographic region (north, central, east, northeast, west, south) was included as a contextual enabling factor reflecting variations in healthcare infrastructure and provider availability across India. Food insecurity was identified when the older adult reported any one of the following indicators, namely, being hungry due to unavailability of food at the house, not eaten for the whole day due to unavailability of food, not eating enough food of choice (excluding fasting/food related restrictions due to religious or health related reason) and having lost weight due to food shortage at the household. Social activity was computed using a composite score of 12 variables ranging from engagement with friends, attending social, religious and political events, engagement in entertainment activities, and outings. Those scoring between 0 and 2 were classified to have nil or low social activity, while those scoring between 3 and 12 were classified to have moderate to high social activity.

Need factors included both perceived and evaluated components. Presence of impairments in Activities of Daily Living (ADL) was classified as no impairment, one impairment, two or more impairments. This categorisation included six ADL, namely dressing (including putting on chappals and shoes), walking across the room, bathing, eating, getting in and out of bed and using the toilet. Additionally, body mass index (BMI) categories (normal, underweight, overweight/obese) and morbidity profile classified as no morbidity, one morbidity, two morbidities, or three or more morbidities, based on eleven chronic conditions (hypertension, diabetes, chronic heart disease, chronic lung disease, stroke, chronic neurological diseases, chronic arthritis and other bone diseases, dyslipidaemia, depression, and chronic renal diseases).

Statistical analysis

Both descriptive and inferential statistics were utilised to summarise the results. The primary outcome variable, i.e., the type of health facility utilised for outpatient visits and hospitalisation, was summarised as frequencies and percentages. The chi-square test was used to examine the association between the choice of health facility and subgroups of exposure variables.

Multivariate logistic regression was conducted to estimate the likelihood of utilising a private facility for outpatient visits and hospitalisation, as private facilities emerged as the most commonly utilised in the descriptive analysis. The selection of covariates for the multivariate models was guided by Andersen’s Behavioural Model of Health Services Use, as described in the Conceptual Framework section. This theoretically-driven approach ensured comprehensive coverage of factors known to influence healthcare utilisation—predisposing, enabling, and need factors—while maintaining interpretability of results.

Prior to model estimation, multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). All VIF values were below 5, indicating no problematic multicollinearity in the models. Model fit was assessed using the Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test.

Results

From the LASI data, 2903 older adults were identified to have palliative care needs. Among them, 2,127 (75.1%) reported at least one outpatient visit in the last 12 months preceding the survey and were included in the outpatient utilisation analysis (Table 3). Similarly, 457 (14.2%) reported at least one hospitalisation in the last 12 months preceding the survey and were included in the hospitalisation analysis (Table 4). The percentages for facility type utilisation (public, private, other) reported in Tables 3 and 4 are calculated based on these respective analytical sub-samples rather than the total sample of 2,903. Table 3 shows the pattern of outpatient healthcare utilisation among them and the association with background characteristics. The majority (69.2%) were between 60 and 75 years old, and 56.4% were female; most of them (89.3%) had only primary education or less. Approximately 42.9% of them belonged to the OBC (Other Backwards Caste) category, and 80.4% identified as Hindus. The majority of older adults (80.7%) with palliative care needs lived in rural areas, and 52.8% of them lived with their spouses. Health insurance coverage was present in only 16.3% of these older adults, and only 8.1% of them engaged in moderate to high social activity. The presence of two or more restrictions in ADL (activities of daily living) was noted in 30.5% of participants, and 65.4% belonged to the underweight category. The presence of multimorbidity was observed in 35.5%. Among the various regions in India, 30.9% of older adults were from the eastern region, while 26.6% were from the central region.

Preference for health facilities for outpatient healthcare utilisation

The utilisation of private outpatient facilities (60.3%) was higher than that of public facilities (23%) and other outpatient facilities (16.6%), as shown in Table 3. However, large disparities in the utilisation of outpatient care, based on the socio-demographic characteristics of older adults, were evident for both public and private facilities. Older adults between 60 and 75 years (23.9%) and males (25.7%) were relatively more dependent on public facilities than their counterparts. Similarly, disadvantaged populations such as underweight (25%), primary education and less (23.5%), poverty (25.9%), those who are living alone (25.6%), those experiencing food insecurity (27%) and having multiple morbidities (25.7%) also relied more on the public healthcare system than their counterparts. On the other hand, individuals with affluence (71.6%), those from the OBC category (66.5%), those residing in urban areas (64.2%), and those engaging in more social activities (70.5%) utilised private facilities more frequently for outpatient care.

In the multivariate analysis for outpatient care, enabling factors emerged as key determinants of private facility utilisation. Geographic region showed the strongest association, with older adults with palliative care needs from the western region having more than twice the odds of utilising private facilities compared to those from the northern region (AOR = 2.20, 95% CI: 1.44–3.36), while those from the northeastern region had substantially lower odds (AOR = 0.41, 95% CI: 0.24–0.69). Wealth status showed a significant gradient, with those in the richest quintile having higher odds of private outpatient utilisation (AOR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.18–2.52). Food insecurity was associated with significantly lower private facility utilisation (AOR = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.42–0.66), underscoring the role of broader socioeconomic vulnerability in shaping healthcare-seeking behaviour. Among predisposing factors, social position as indicated by caste influenced utilisation patterns, with OBC category individuals having higher odds of private facility use compared to SC/ST populations (AOR = 1.71, 95% CI: 1.30–2.26). Preference for health facilities for inpatient healthcare utilisation

Similar to outpatient care, Table 4 shows that the utilisation of private health facilities for hospitalisation was higher (58.3%) than public (39.7%) and other facilities (1.9%). However, disparities in the utilisation of private facilities for hospitalisation emerged during the subgroup analysis. Though overall private utilisation was higher, among the underweight older adults with palliative care needs (45.8%), poorest wealth quintile (59.3%), SC/ST category (53%), urban residents (56.4%), and those from eastern (59.1%) and northeastern (55.2%) region the public facility utilisation for hospitalisation was relatively higher than their counterparts. Contrastingly, higher utilisation of private hospitalisation was noticed among older adults with palliative care needs, who were in the overweight/obese category (74.3%), the affluent wealth quintile (79.3%), rural residents (63.5%) and those from western regions of India (82.3%) than their counterparts.

In the multivariate analysis for hospitalisation, the enabling factors of the Andersen Behaviour Model emerged as the strongest determinants of private facility utilisation. Wealth status demonstrated the most pronounced association, with older adults with palliative care needs in the richest quintile having nearly five-fold higher odds of utilising private facilities compared to those in the poorest quintile (AOR = 4.76, 95% CI: 2.47–9.16). Geographic region was also strongly associated with significantly higher private hospitalisation in the western region (AOR = 3.07, 95% CI: 1.62–5.80) and lower utilisation in the eastern region (AOR = 0.45, 95% CI: 0.24–0.83). Among the need factors of the model, multimorbidity showed a clear dose-response relationship, with those having three or more morbidities having nearly four times higher odds of private hospitalisation (AOR = 3.95, 95% CI: 2.12–7.34). The complete regression results are presented in Table 4.

The duration of hospitalisation in terms of average number of days was also analysed for both public and private facilities across all sub-groups. The mean duration of hospitalisation was 6.2 days and 5.3 days in public and private facility respectively. A statistically significant difference in the mean duration of hospitalisation in the public facilities was observed across the wealth quintiles, with 3.9 days in the poorest quintile and 10.4 days in the richest quintile. A similar pattern was observed for hospitalisations in the private facilities, with 3.26 days among the poorest quintile and 6.13 days among the richest quintile.

Figure 2 shows the utilisation for health facilities based on the nature of illness during outpatient visits and hospitalisation. Utilisation of private facilities for outpatient care for the treatment of chronic life-limiting conditions (57%), acute illnesses (54.4%) and accidents and injuries (52.9%) was more than public and other facilities. Utilisation for public facilities for hospitalisation was slightly higher (49.1%) than for private facilities for the treatment of acute illnesses. However, the utilisation of private facilities was significantly higher for the treatment of chronic life-limiting conditions (53.7%) and accidents and injuries (72.2%) during hospitalisation.

Table 5 shows the proportion of various healthcare services used from public, private, and other facilities during outpatient visits and hospitalisations. Overall, healthcare services provided by private facilities were used more than those from public and other facilities. Services such as laboratory investigations (71.3%), endoscopy or colonoscopy (77.1%), and surgery (76.2%) were used significantly more from private facilities than from public and other facilities during outpatient visits. Similarly, during hospitalisation, services such as laboratory investigations (63.1%), imaging investigations (64.6%), surgery (65.6%), and Ayurvedic and Herbal medications (70.5%) were used significantly more from private facilities than from public and other facilities.

As shown in Table 6 of self-rated experience during outpatient visits and hospitalisation, both private and other health facilities provided older adults with a better experience during outpatient care and hospitalisation than public facilities. During an outpatient visit, higher self-rated experience scores were observed for private facilities in the domains of providing a clear explanation (3.99), being treated by a health provider of choice (3.97), and cleanliness (4.02). Similarly, during hospitalisation, the experience scores in the domains such as waiting time (3.94), providing clear explanations (4.03) and cleanliness (4.09) were higher for private facilities.

Discussion

The analysis of patterns of healthcare utilisation showed that older adults with palliative care needs utilise the private facilities significantly more than the public facilities for outpatient care and hospitalisation. Older adults with palliative care needs also reported a higher level of satisfaction with the services provided by private facilities and other facilities than with those provided by public facilities. This higher utilisation of private facilities among older adults with palliative care needs was also accompanied by disparities that are strongly influenced by their social position. Though no studies have explored public-private utilisation among older adults with palliative care needs, studies conducted among older adults in general have shown a similar preference for private facilities [18, 19, 22]. One of the consistent themes in the choice of private facilities is the offer of personalised care in these facilities, which is crucial for those with palliative care needs [23–26].

Additionally, the preference for private facilities can also be attributed to the better experience offered by these facilities when compared with public facilities [24, 25, 27]. These studies report higher waiting times [24], timings not suitable for healthcare visits [27], poor quality of services [24] and non-availability of medical personnel during visits as reasons for not preferring public facilities [27, 28]. In the analysis of the self-rated user experience during healthcare utilisation in the current study, private facilities scored higher than public facilities in all domains, including cleanliness, waiting time, being treated respectfully, seeing the health provider of choice, and being able to talk privately. However, these satisfaction differences should be interpreted cautiously, as they may be influenced by expectation bias among out-of-pocket paying patients and self-selection of wealthier individuals into private care. Additionally, public facilities disproportionately serve more disadvantaged individuals whose perceptions and experiences influence these ratings. Moreover, higher satisfaction scores should not be equated with superior health outcomes. These findings highlight the need to improve the responsiveness of public facilities rather than indicating overall quality differences between facility types. Addressing these lacunae will be a crucial public health priority as public health facilities are crucial for universal health coverage and in reducing out-of-pocket health expenditures [17, 29–31].

Although the utilisation of private facilities was higher in the present analysis, this higher utilisation without adequate health insurance coverage and financial security can increase out-of-pocket health expenditure and financial toxicity [17, 32, 33]. The subgroup analysis also revealed significant disparities that particularly affected vulnerable social groups. Older adults with palliative care needs from the poorest wealth quintile, experiencing food insecurity, from the lower social position, from the northeastern and eastern states in India had significantly lower utilisation of services from the private facilities. These states face persistent challenges in healthcare infrastructure, limited connectivity, geographic isolation, and a greater reliance on public sector provision [34, 35]. Northeastern states in particular face additional obstacles due to rugged terrain and limited transportation networks, which restrict both the availability of private facilities and patients’ ability to reach them [36]. These supply-side constraints mean that facility choice in these regions is determined less by individual preference and more by what is geographically and financially accessible. Furthermore, the WHO study on global ageing and adult health, along with several other studies conducted in India, also shows that healthcare access and health outcomes are strongly influenced by financial status, social position, and geographic location [16, 17, 32, 37].

Although health insurance coverage has been shown to improve healthcare access and utilisation [16, 30, 31], in our analysis among those with palliative care needs, it did not significantly enhance healthcare utilisation in the private sector. One of the potential reasons for this may be the inadequate healthcare coverage for the complex and long-term needs of older adults with palliative care needs [38]. The identification of vulnerable groups among those requiring palliative care is a crucial step towards policy action [39]. Addressing social determinants such as improving financial security for older adults, improving access to social welfare schemes to reduce food insecurities, and ensuring equitable access by strengthening healthcare infrastructure in eastern and northeastern regions are essential to reduce the social and regional disparities identified in this study.

The preference for private hospitalisation among multimorbid older adults may reflect a perception that private facilities offer more comprehensive specialist services, shorter waiting times, and better coordination across multiple disease management needs [40, 41]. Public facilities, particularly at the primary and secondary levels, are often not equipped to manage the complex, multidisciplinary care requirements of patients with multiple co-existing chronic conditions [42]. This is particularly concerning in the context of palliative care, where multimorbidity necessitates integrated and long-term care approaches. The finding that clinical complexity drives patients toward the private sector has important implications for health system design. Strengthening public hospital capacity for multimorbid patients through multidisciplinary teams and integrated care could reduce financial burden on vulnerable populations [33, 43].

These findings have important implications for health system reform in India. The high utilisation of private facilities, coupled with low health insurance coverage (16.3%) among older adults with palliative care needs, raises concerns about financial protection. The government health insurance scheme known as Ayushman Bharat Pradhan Mantri Jan Arogya Yojana (AB-PMJAY) provides hospitalisation coverage up to INR 5 lakhs for economically vulnerable families [44]. However, the coverage does not adequately address the long-term, recurring healthcare needs of those requiring palliative care, and home-based care [30, 38]. State-level insurance schemes and social welfare programmes similarly tend to focus on acute episodic care rather than the continuous care model required for those with palliative care needs. Strengthening public palliative care services through the existing National Program for Health Care of Elderly (NPHCE) and NPPC frameworks appears to be the most scalable approach [45, 46], given that the public health infrastructure already extends to the sub-district level through Health and Wellness Centres. However, the issues such as workforce shortages, inadequate training, and competing programme priorities are the limitation to overcome. Additionally, public-private partnerships may improve access to specialist services, and quality improvement [47]. The Kerala community-based palliative care model provides a compelling system-level example. The integration of palliative care into local self-government institutions and leveraging trained volunteer networks has enabled Kerala to achieve near-universal palliative care access through community-based infrastructure [7, 13, 14]. This model demonstrates that reducing dependence on private facilities for palliative care is feasible when public systems are strengthened with strong community participation, local governance support, and dedicated funding.

Strengths and limitations

This is one of the first studies exploring the preference and utilisation of health facilities among older people with palliative care needs in India. The analysis of preferred healthcare facilities, along with self-rated experiences across six domains, offers contextual clarity on the factors influencing preference. The study results, based on a nationally representative sample, provide specific recommendations for policy action. Since the study is cross-sectional, it cannot examine the factors influencing the choice of facilities during follow-up, outpatient visits, and hospitalisation. The cross-sectional nature of the study design also limits causal inference; hence, the results of self-rated satisfaction and utilisation should be interpreted with caution. The operationalisation of SPICT-LIS using indicators from survey data may introduce misclassification bias. This method may have lower sensitivity than clinical assessment, potentially under-identifying individuals with palliative care needs. This means that the observed utilisation patterns may not fully capture the heterogeneity of healthcare-seeking behaviour among all older adults with palliative care needs. The availability of palliative care services in public and private facilities could not be considered in the preference analysis because this information was not collected in the LASI survey. The limitations of large-scale nationally representative surveys, such as language differences and interviewer fatigue, also apply to this study.

Conclusion

Older adults with palliative care needs preferred private healthcare facilities significantly more than public ones for outpatient care and hospitalisation. This preference for private facilities during healthcare utilisation is linked to disparities based on social determinants, with individuals from the poorest wealth quintile, those experiencing food insecurity, those belonging to lower social positions, and residents of eastern and northeastern states facing inequities in care. Strengthening public health facilities, especially in the eastern and northeastern regions, along with public-private partnerships and social welfare schemes that address income inequalities, food insecurity, and social disparities, can help reduce these inequities.
