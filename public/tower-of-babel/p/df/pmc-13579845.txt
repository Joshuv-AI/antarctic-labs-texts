Exploring the nexus between perceived organizational support and professional identity among nursing professionals: a mediation-moderation mechanism
Authors: Shen Chunhong, Sha Yang, Shuna Zhai, Ruowei Wang, Abid Hussain, Junyan Zhao, Zhenya Zou, Pei Zhang, Ping Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundProfessional identity (PI) is essential for nurses’ commitment, job performance, and retention. There is, however, limited evidence on the psychological and contextual pathways through which perceived organizational support (POS) influences PI. This study analyzed the associations between POS and PI among Chinese nurses in public hospitals, and studied the mediating role of self-efficacy, which also included the moderating role of organizational culture, based on the Social Exchange Theory and Social Cognitive Theory.MethodsA study was conducted among 624 nurses in public hospitals in Shandong Province, China. Data were gathered from validated instruments on POS, PI, SE, and OC. The proposed model of mediation and moderation was tested using structural equation modeling (SEM) and bootstrapping analysis.ResultsPOS was positively associated with both PI (β = 0.410, p < 0.001) and self-efficacy (β = 0.596, p < 0.001). Self-efficacy was also positively related to PI (β = 0.553, p < 0.001). The mediation analysis showed a partial mediation effect of self-efficacy between POS and PI (β = 0.330, p < 0.001).ConclusionsThere are two ways in which nurses’ PI is influenced by POS, one direct and the other indirect. The results indicate that supportive organizational practices and psychological empowerment are important factors to enhance the nurse’s PI. Healthcare managers should establish a positive working culture and interventions that strengthen the self-efficacy of nurses to ensure the sustainability of the nursing workforce and nursing profession.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-04962-9.Keywords: Professional identity, Perceived organizational support, Organizational culture assessment, Self-efficacy, Social exchange theory, Social cognitive theory

Introduction

Professional identity, based on Erikson’s ego identity theory, is acknowledged as a fundamental psychological construct that affects employees’ work attitudes, commitment, and long-term career progression. Early scholars defined professional identity as an emotional bond to one’s chosen career, evidenced by the commitment to persist in the profession and the level of fondness for it [1–3]. The concept of professional identity has evolved and been applied across multiple sectors, such as education, finance, and healthcare, where it has been examined as both an outcome variable and a mediator connecting organizational and individual factors [4, 5]. In the healthcare field, professional identity has become a crucial element for improving motivation, resilience, and patient-centered care among medical personnel. Nonetheless, despite its significance, research investigating the organizational precursors and contextual factors of professional identity is limited, particularly in nursing in China.

Nurse professional identity is a multidimensional concept that reflects an individual’s cognition, emotions, and behavioral compatibility with the profession. This research is conceptualized based on the Professional Identity Scale of Nurses developed Liu, Lam [6] which used six dimensions of conceptualizing professional identity that include but are not limited to self-efficacy (confidence in performing nursing duties), self-determinacy (autonomy in professional practice), professional influence (perceived impact on patients and the healthcare system), professional self-concept (internalization of nursing as part of his or her identity), professional belonging (sense of acceptance within the professional community) and professional coping (ability to cope with work related issues). These dimensions combined form a picture of internalization of professional values and active involvement in professional roles, and allow understanding the process of formation of the professional identity through perceived organizational support in a comprehensive manner [7, 8].

The original Professional Identity scale consists of items measuring elements of the professional identity that are distinct from self-efficacy, but it is crucial to make the distinction between elements of the professional identity and self-efficacy [7]. While the original Professional Identity scale includes dimensions related to self-efficacy, it is important to distinguish between identity-related beliefs and competence-based perceptions [9]. In the present study, Professional Identity is theoretically conceptualized as a multidimensional construct; however, to maintain conceptual clarity in the structural model, elements directly reflecting perceived competence are treated separately under self-efficacy. This distinction allows for a clearer examination of the psychological mechanism linking organizational support to identity formation [10].

A growing body of studies has recognized perceived organizational support as a crucial factor influencing favorable employee outcomes. Perceived organizational support indicates employees’ convictions that their organization appreciates their contributions and prioritizes their welfare. Empirical research shows that perceived organizational support not only cultivates more employee engagement but also augments psychological resources, including self-efficacy [11, 12].

In healthcare settings, perceived organizational support has been associated with career self-management, resilience, and work performance [13, 14]. Although these findings highlight the significance of perceived organizational support in influencing professional outcomes, there is a lack of Understanding of how perceived organizational support translates into a professional identity. This concept encompasses employees’ recognition, sense of belonging, and emotional commitment to their job. Acknowledging this gap is especially critical in nursing, where elevated turnover, stress, and burnout jeopardize staff stability and the quality of treatment. A separate line of research emphasizes the essential function of self-efficacy, characterized as people’s belief in their capacity to execute tasks and surmount obstacles proficiently. It is important to clarify that self-efficacy refers to perceived ability rather than objective competence. According to Social Cognitive Theory, self-efficacy involves an individual’s belief in their capacity to successfully perform tasks, independent of their actual skills or the outcomes of their performance. Therefore, the current research focuses on nurses’ subjective confidence in their professional skills rather than their objective clinical abilities or performance metrics in healthcare. This distinction is crucial for maintaining conceptual clarity regarding how organizational factors influence professional identity, as examined through psychological mechanisms [15, 16]. In academic and medical contexts, self-efficacy is recognized as a crucial determinant of professional identity, enabling individuals to enhance their sense of competence and belonging within their respective domains [9, 17].

Furthermore, self-efficacy is progressively acknowledged as a mediating mechanism that elucidates how organizational resources, such as perceived organizational support, are converted into profound psychological results. Nevertheless, empirical research specifically examining the mediation pathway from perceived organizational support to professional identity via self-efficacy is limited in healthcare, which constrains our Understanding of the psychological mechanisms by which organizational support may contribute to professional identity. Simultaneously, experts have emphasized the importance of organizational culture as a key contextual factor influencing employee attitudes and identities. Culture encompasses the collective norms, values, and practices that define the organizational environment [18].

Supportive and participant cultures provide acknowledgment, collaboration, and psychological safety, facilitating the efficient translation of self-efficacy into professional identity [19, 20]. In contrast, hierarchical or inflexible cultures may inhibit the manifestation of self-efficacy, thereby diminishing its impact on professional identity. Nevertheless, despite theoretical claims, there is a paucity of empirical research investigating the moderating influence of organizational culture on the link between self-efficacy and professional identity, especially within healthcare environments. Collectively, these gaps highlight the need for a comprehensive paradigm that examines the direct and indirect effects of perceived organizational support on professional identity through self-efficacy, as well as the role of organizational culture in shaping these interactions. This study offers three contributions by concentrating on nursing workers in Shandong Province.

Initially, it may contribute to the literature on professional identity by identifying perceived organizational support as a crucial organizational precursor within the Chinese healthcare field. Secondly, it improves theory by recognizing self-efficacy as a mediating factor connecting perceived organizational support to professional identity. Third, it presents organizational culture as a moderating factor, emphasizing the contextual limitations within which self-efficacy may contribute to professional identity. The study provides new insights into the interaction between organizational support and culture, and individual psychological resources, thereby enhancing professional identity and presenting both theoretical developments and practical implications for healthcare administration.

Previous literature consistently indicates a positive correlation between perceived organizational support and professional identity) among nursing professionals. Surveys by Sabanciogullari and Dogan (2015) and Fitzgerald (2020) demonstrate that nurses who perceive higher levels of organizational support tend to have a stronger emotional attachment to their profession, lower turnover intentions, and greater work engagement. In the context of the Chinese healthcare system, studies by Peng et al. (2022) and Zhang et al. (2021) highlight that POS significantly influences nurses’ professional commitment and identity development. Additionally, Wang et al. (2017) found that POS acts as a vital organizational asset, mitigating job stress and enhancing professional identification among nurses. While the direct relationship between POS and PI has been established, existing literature primarily focuses on descriptive analyses and bivariate correlations, rather than exploring the psychological processes that convert organizational support into long-term professional identity. A critical gap in the literature is the lack of exploration into the mediating mechanisms that explain how perceived organizational support (POS) affects professional identity (PI). While researchers acknowledge that organizational support influences employee performance, the psychological processes that facilitate this translation remain unclear. This gap is particularly significant in nursing, where professional identity is not merely an attitudinal construct but an active psychological resource that fosters commitment during chronic stressors. A key theoretical mediator in this context is self-efficacy, which refers to an individual’s belief in their ability to perform tasks and manage challenges. Bandura [15] concept of self-efficacy explains how environmental resources, such as organizational support, are internalized into self-referential beliefs that ultimately shape behavior and identity. In nursing, POS can enhance self-efficacy through affirmation, resource provision, and developmental opportunities, enabling nurses to integrate their professional roles into their self-concept. However, there is limited empirical research examining this mediation pathway. Without identifying self-efficacy as a mediator, we cannot fully understand whether organizational support may contribute to professional identity through affective commitment or indirectly via competence beliefs. This distinction carries significant implications for the design of interventions.

Theories

Social Exchange Theory

Blau [21] and interactions based on mutual benefit expectations [22] develop SET relationships on mutually reciprocated exchanges. Cropanzano, Anthony [23] used the SET to mean (i) the initiator acting on the target, (ii) an attitudinal or behavioral reaction of the target in the reciprocity, and (iii) the relationship that results. The organizational support theory is founded on social exchange, where employees will deliver a high level of performance at the workplace in case, they consider themselves to have been appreciated and supported by the organization [24]. Certain studies have established that POS, being a source of social support, can enhance the perception of benefit on the part of nurses as professionals [25]. According to SET, perceived organizational support [26] is taken as the starting point of the exchange cycle in this model, Kilroy, Dundon [27] established that perceived organizational support could generate work enthusiasm, belonging to the organization, a feeling of being cared about and assisted by the organization, active re-entry to work by the nurses to the organization, boost professional identity, better nursing services by nurses, and patient satisfaction. The mediation of self-efficacy in SET is also informed [28]. Positive working conditions are an indicator of trust and the availability of resources, contributing to the self-efficacy of nurses by increasing their confidence to confront professional issues [22, 29].

Social Cognitive Theory (SCT)

The social cognitive theory (SCT), which is formally presented by Bandura [30], is put forward as the result of an interactive and dynamic interplay between personal factors (such as beliefs and self-efficacy), behavior, and the environment of an individual [31]. Under this model, self-efficacy, observational learning, and environmental support are the key concepts that take center stage in explaining how perceived organizational support is related to professional identity among nursing employees [32, 33]. Several aspects of perceived organizational support include making available the relevant training materials, creating a favorable working environment, adequate physical and emotional support, and reward/punishment systems [29]. Self-efficacy as a mediating variable is the one that mediates the effects of POS on professional identity [34].

Workers who feel that the organization is supportive would become more likely to form greater faith in their ability to undertake their professional duties, which may indicate improved self-efficacy as compared to a more objectively assessed competence [35]. As an example, the nursing staff can be trained and continue learning in the department regularly to gain the latest medical knowledge and practices, and thus improve their clinical skills [36]. SCT anticipates that positive environments make people have confidence in their capacity to execute their functions well. SCT puts self-efficacy as one of the major motivators and identity shapers [37]. The presence of POS as the environmental stimulus is related to an increase in the self-efficacy (cognitive factor), which in turn facilitates the formation of professional identity (behavioral outcome), and organizational culture influences this process [38, 39].

Perceived organizational support can be seen as a multi-level explanation for the professional identity of nurses using the theories of Social Exchange Theory and Social Cognitive Theory. In terms of SET [21, 23], perceived organizational support takes the form of an organizational-level relational resource that gives nurses the sense of being recognized, cared for, and invested in by the institution. These positive interactions engender positive attitudes, feelings of attachment, and increased professional commitment. The focus of organizational support within the framework of SET, however, does not completely account for internalization of organizational support to more stable self-perceptions. This explanation complements the research of SCT by highlighting the importance of self-efficacy as a cognitive mechanism at the individual level that is a mechanism through which environmental support influences identity formation [31]. A positive organizational attitude is linked to the nurses’ belief in their competence, thus increasing their self-efficacy beliefs. These efficacy beliefs then lead to an internalization of the values of the profession, role identification, and professional belonging, which further enhances professional identity. Thus, SCT provides some insight into how the external support is cognitively transformed into professional identity through self-efficacy, while organizational support begins the relational exchange process, as explained by SET [28]. The findings of these theories integrate well with the POS → self-efficacy → professional identity pathway and shed light on the relationship between organizational-level and individual-level mechanisms in the process of professional identity formation.

Additionally, several alternative explanations for the POS-PI relationship warrant consideration. This relationship may be mediated by other psychological processes, such as psychological capital or emotional labor strategies. Social identity theory suggests that professional identity may also arise from enhanced group identification. Furthermore, reverse causality could be at play, where nurses with a stronger professional identity perceive greater organizational support. The observed relationship might also result from common method variance or unmeasured characteristics, such as proactive personality or positive affectivity.

This paper discusses these possibilities through various methodological approaches. Self-efficacy is directly tested as a mediating factor while controlling for demographic and organizational variables. Competing model analyses were conducted to compare the hypothesized mediation model against alternative causal directions (e.g., PI → self-efficacy), with results supporting the proposed model. Common method bias was assessed using the Harman single-factor test and confirmatory factor analysis, which indicated that it did not account for the observed relationships. Finally, organizational culture was examined as a moderator to evaluate the impact of contextual factors on these relationships. These strategies enhance both the theoretical and empirical validity of the proposed model.

Literature review and hypotheses development

Perceived organizational support and professional identity

According to Erikson’s ego identity theory, early researchers across diverse fields have articulated their perspectives on professional identity [2]. Certain researchers propose that professional identity is primarily an emotional construct, characterized by a commitment to remain in one’s current work and a degree of affection for that profession [5, 8]. With the supplementing and improvement of the concept of professional identity by other scholars, researchers gradually started investigating professional identity in each industry. Some researchers have also begun conducting in-depth studies on professional identity in various industries. The professional identity of medical sector professionals was incorporated into a model to examine the connection between professional identity and other factors, or to serve as a bridge [7, 40, 41]. Previous research suggests that professional identity is influenced by perceived organizational support. Therefore, if nursing staff in Shandong Province receive increased organizational support, they are likely to feel a stronger connection to their work and dedicate more effort to patient care. Based on this, we propose the following hypothesis:

Hypothesis 1

Perceived organizational support has a positive impact on the professional identity of nursing staff.

Perceived organizational support and self-efficacy

Perceived organizational support (POS) refers to employees’ “global beliefs concerning the extent to which the organization values their contributions and cares about their well-being” [67], which is regarded as a psychological agreement that the employee makes with the enterprise [68]. Perceived Organizational Support (POS) denotes employees’ perception that their organization appreciates their contributions and prioritizes their welfare. A recent study suggests that POS has a significant impact on enhancing self-efficacy in many work environments. Liu, Yang [42] discovered that among nursing assistants in China, elevated perceived organizational support was substantially correlated with enhanced self-efficacy, which subsequently moderated the relationship between Perceived Organizational Support and core competencies. Similarly, Zheng, Feng [43] demonstrated that perceived organizational support and leader–member exchange (LMX) strongly predicted self-efficacy among employees in Indonesian Islamic financial institutions, with self-efficacy mediating the association between perceived organizational support and job embeddedness.

In an academic context, Li, Kanchanapoom [44] established that for university educators in China, perceived organizational support not only directly may contribute to self-efficacy but also indirectly affects job performance via self-efficacy and job satisfaction. These studies collectively indicate that perceived organizational support is a vital organizational predictor of self-efficacy, which improves outcomes such as competence, engagement, and performance. Building on these findings, Peng, Chen [13] discovered that in a cohort of Chinese nurses, perceived organizational support significantly forecasted career self-management, with general self-efficacy serving as a mediating factor.

To and Yu [45] similarly illustrated that for emergency nurses subjected to workplace violence, perceived organizational support indirectly bolstered resilience via self-efficacy and cognitive reappraisal strategies, highlighting the psychological resources activated by perceived organizational support. Oubibi, Fute [46] emphasized in their research that perceived organizational support enhanced university educators’ job performance both directly and indirectly through personality qualities, self-efficacy, and job satisfaction. These studies collectively demonstrate that POS is an essential organizational resource that improves self-efficacy, hence promoting competence, resilience, and performance in challenging professional settings. Thus, we can hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2

Perceived organizational support has a positive impact on self-efficacy.

Self-efficacy and professional identity

Self-efficacy denotes an individual’s belief in their capacity to accomplish tasks or surmount problems within a particular setting effectively [47]. This assumption not only affects behavioral decisions but also dictates the degree of effort exerted and emotional reactions when encountering challenges [48]. Self-efficacy, a fundamental psychological resource, denotes an individual’s evaluation of their prospective performance, emphasizing perceived capability over actual skill levels. Research indicates that individuals with elevated self-efficacy exhibit enhanced perseverance and optimism in confronting challenges. In contrast, those with diminished self-efficacy are prone to avoidance or capitulation due to adverse emotions, including anxiety and fear [49].

Furthermore, self-efficacy is dynamic, evolving as experiences accrue and settings transform [50]. Self-efficacy plays a pivotal part in the development of professional identity. A study on inclusive education instructors revealed that those with elevated self-efficacy exhibited more involvement and proactive problem-solving in their teaching, subsequently strengthening their professional identity [51]. A separate study revealed that the teaching self-efficacy of rural physical education instructors, encompassing aspects such as classroom management, student engagement, and pedagogical techniques, significantly forecasted their professional identity [52]. These findings suggest that self-efficacy has a positive impact on professional identity, inspiring educators and enhancing their performance. Self-Determination Theory asserts that confidence in task execution is a fundamental element of motivation [53]. Motivation is not only a vital catalyst for teacher conduct but also an essential component in the development of professional identity. Watt and Richardson highlighted the strong correlation between self-efficacy and professional conduct Chen, Ping [54], whilst Sun, Zheng [32] established that self-efficacy is a crucial prerequisite for the formation of professional identity.

Professional identity is a dynamic psychological characteristic that signifies an individual’s continuous acknowledgment and placement within their career [55]. Studies demonstrate that individuals possessing high self-efficacy generally display enhanced professional dedication, increased career value, and elevated job happiness [56]. A study on Iranian foreign language educators revealed a substantial positive association between self-efficacy and professional identity [57]. Additionally, Li et al. validated a reciprocal association between professional identity and self-efficacy among high school students [10]. It is crucial to note that professional identity does not evolve swiftly; instead, it emerges via the continuous interaction between the individual and the social milieu. Ibarra posited that individuals generally undergo a phase of provisional identity before the complete formation of their professional identity [28] longitudinal research conducted by Wang, Yang [58] indicate that the clarity of professional identity may contribute over time. In this process, self-efficacy, as a vital psychological resource, actively contributes to the development and reinforcement of professional identity.

Hypothesis 3

Self-efficacy has a positive impact on professional identity.

Mediating role of self-efficacy

Perceived Organizational Support (POS) denotes employees’ perception that their organization appreciates their contributions and is concerned about their welfare. Evidence clearly associates perceived organizational support with self-efficacy, a fundamental psychological resource. Zhao, Guo [59] indicated that within the cohort of Chinese nursing assistants, perceived organizational support was a predictor of enhanced self-efficacy, which subsequently mediated its influence on core competencies. Rozikan, Zakiy [60] similarly demonstrated that in Indonesian financial institutions, perceived organizational support and leader-member exchange improved work embeddedness through self-efficacy. Ni, Wu [61] discovered that perceived organizational support enhanced instructors’ work performance both directly and indirectly via self-efficacy and job satisfaction in higher education. Healthcare research corroborates these findings.

Ren, Li [62] indicated that perceived organizational support facilitated nurses’ career self-management through general self-efficacy. In contrast, Tang, Wang [63] illustrated that perceived organizational support indirectly bolstered resilience among emergency nurses via self-efficacy and cognitive reappraisal. This body of study collectively recognizes self-efficacy as a crucial mechanism by which organizational support may contribute to competence, engagement, and resilience. Recent evidence suggests a connection between self-efficacy and professional identity, which is characterized by individuals’ acknowledgment of and dedication to their profession. Sun, Chen [64] discovered that self-efficacy among pre-service physical education teachers reinforced professional identity via purpose in life and self-esteem.

Lin, Wang [65] likewise indicated that reflective ability indirectly influenced junior nurses’ professional identity through self-directed learning and self-efficacy. Haque and Kour [66] corroborated a favorable link between self-efficacy and professional identity in nursing students. Collectively, this research suggests that perceived organizational support may contribute to self-efficacy, thereby reinforcing professional identity in various occupational contexts.

Hypothesis 4

Self-efficacy mediates the relationship between perceived organizational support and professional identity.

Moderating role of organizational culture

There is growing evidence that the context of an organization contributes to understanding the relationship between individual psychological resources and professional outcomes [67, 68]. According to the Social Cognitive Theory, the environment influences the magnitude of the transformation of personal beliefs into identity-related outcomes. Self-efficacy is the level of confidence that nurses have in their own professional skills, but organizational culture can either strengthen, confirm, and support these beliefs or undermine them within the work context. Through a supportive and participatory culture, where psychological safety, collaboration, recognition, and shared professional values are enhanced, the process by which self-efficacy translates into professional identity may be strengthened [69]. If this is the case, the beliefs of competence are socially affirmed and supported by professional institutions, which increases the likelihood of internalizing their professional roles. Rigid or highly bureaucratic cultures, on the other hand, can inhibit autonomy, restrict professional involvement, and reduce the likelihood of the development of self-efficacy into identity [70]. Previous studies also suggest that cultures that focus on trust and innovation, as well as on interaction support, positively influence the extent to which employees incorporate efficacy beliefs into their professional self-concept and working identity [25]. So in this study, organizational culture was treated as a contextual boundary condition that could positively or negatively affect the relationship between self-efficacy and professional identity. Thus, the following hypothesis is formulated:

Hypothesis 5

Organizational culture moderates the relationship between self-efficacy and professional identity.

Study conceptual framework

Methodology

Context selection

The current study employed a two-step sampling method to gather data from nurses in public hospitals in Shandong Province, China. In the first phase, participating hospitals were selected using convenience sampling, based on their availability and administrative approval to conduct the survey. During the second phase, simple random sampling was used to select individual nurses from the participating hospitals. The nursing administration departments provided lists of qualified registered nurses, and random invitations to complete the questionnaire were sent to these participants. Data collection took place between September and November 2025. This stratified sampling approach allowed the researchers to include various hospital environments while maintaining randomness in the selection of specific respondents [71]. This methodology, commonly used in the social sciences, involves survey research to systematically gather quantitative data. It aims to test a hypothesis and evaluate a theory.

The questionnaire is divided into two parts. The first section collects demographic details, and the second part assesses constructs such as perceived organizational support, professional identity, general self-efficacy, and organizational culture assessment instruments. The questionnaires were distributed both as hard copies and electronically by the hospitals’ nursing departments. Before collecting data, the research team informed nursing supervisors about the study’s objectives and obtained consent from hospital administrators. Participation was voluntary, and only nurses with at least six months of experience at the hospital were considered eligible. Respondents were assured of the confidentiality and anonymity of their responses. Written informed consent was obtained before participation, and respondents were free to withdraw from the survey at any time without any repercussions. Adhering to the recommended sample size recommendations established by Saunders, Lewis [72] and [73], it was decided that 729 individuals should be sampled. Overall, 624 proper answers were obtained, so the effective response rate was equal to 85.6%.

Measures

Perceived organizational support was examined through 13 items from the studies of [74] and [75]. POS in the nursing profession is very important since it is used to determine how well nurses feel appreciated and taken care of by their organizations, which directly relates to the psychological well-being and professional commitment of the workers. In the context of nursing, which is a highly stressful job, the knowledge about POS can enable the identification of organizational factors that alleviate burnout, promote self-efficacy, and provide a stable and engaged workforce required to deliver quality patient care [29]. Professional identity was measured through 30 items adapted from [76]. PI Scale, which was designed by Liu, Lam [6] and tested on the Chinese nurses comprises six dimensions: self-efficacy (confidence in clinical competencies), self-determination (autonomy in professional roles), professional influence (impact on patients and practice), professional self-concept (internalization of nursing as personal identity), professional belonging (acceptance within the nursing community), and professional coping (ability to deal with occupational stressors). The given approach is multidimensional in that it acknowledges professional identity as a combination of interrelated mental conditions that might vary in their reaction to organizational variables. Our measure allows us to understand specifically how organizational support manifests in particular elements of identity formation, with informed interventions then applied to the nursing workforce development process [77].

General self-efficacy was assessed by 10 items adapted from the study of [78]. It must be noted that the self-efficacy construct used herein in the study entails perceived ability as opposed to professional ability. The scale gauges the beliefs of the respondents about their capacity to arouse motivation, cognitive ability, and behavior strategies to address the demands of the profession. Thus, the scores are associated with psychological evaluations of competence, as opposed to clinical skill or performance measures that are externally validated [79]. The organizational culture assessment instruments were examined through 24 items adapted from [80]. The importance of measuring organizational culture in the nursing sector lies in the fact that it involves the values, beliefs, and norms that are shared and which determine the professional environment, and this is how nurses view their positions, how they relate with one another, and respond to the expectations of the institution. Knowledge of organizational culture assists in determining contextual elements that both contribute to and inhibit the transmission of individual psychological resources, including self-efficacy into professional identity, job satisfaction, and retention, which is more critical to healthcare environments characterized by high stress rates and cultures that directly influence patient care and workforce stability [81]. Pilot Study

During the measurement validation process, the scale items were carefully reviewed to identify potential conceptual overlap between the PI and SE constructs. Several PI items reflected self-evaluative statements related to problem-solving ability and personal competence, which closely resembled items from the general self-efficacy scale. To ensure clearer construct distinction, overlapping items were removed before confirmatory factor analysis. Specifically, three items from the POS scale (POS11–POS13), three items from the SE scale (SE1, SE4, and SE9), and eight items from the PI scale (PI9, PI16, PI19, PI21, PI24, PI25, PI27, and PI28) were removed. Following the removal of these items, the measurement model was re-estimated using the remaining indicators, and all subsequent analyses were conducted using this refined measurement model to improve discriminant validity among constructs. Now, POS was examined with 10 items, PI with 22 items, and SE with 7 items.

It should be noted that there was no redefinition of the construct of Professional Identity when items were removed because they overlapped. Rather, it has evolved its operationalization for the purpose of conceptualizing it more clearly in relation to self-efficacy. The retained items maintained the essential nature of the professional identity constructs professional belonging, self-concept, values internalization, and role commitment, the latter of which is not theoretically related to the self-efficacy construct. This method can help achieve discriminant validity and also allows for the mediation analysis to account for relations between theoretically distinct constructs.

Pilot study

A pilot study, involving 97 (13.30% of the total target population) responses, was carried out to examine questionnaire reliability. SPSS was used to calculate the value of Cronbach’s alpha (CA) with an acceptable level of 0.70 in a social study [82]. Table 1 presents the CA coefficient for each construct, which suggests that respondents fully understand the questionnaire item and the internal reliability of measurement [83]. None of the items needed to be changed according to the pilot analysis.

Data analysis

Missing data, outliers, and normality in the data were screened. Cases where missing items were greater than 20% were eliminated. The values of the skewness and kurtosis were in the range of ± 2, which favored the use of maximum likelihood estimation. No evidence of multicollinearity problems was found. The data were processed with the help of the Social Science Statistical Package (SPSS) and structural equation modeling (SEM) in AMOS v.24 by Kanwel, Lingqiang [84]. We used a three-stage process to use the SEM method. Also, the moderating influence of OC was investigated using Hayes ' PROCESS macro, 4.1 [85].

The first one was the model validation with the help of confirmatory factor analysis (CFA). The final process was to analyze the proposed structural model to find out the correlation between each of the variables. The CFA used various measures of fit, including 2/df, comparative fit index (CFI), normalized root mean square residual (SRMR), Tucker-Lewis’s index (TLI), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA) [86]. We also applied the process used by Andrew Hayes to examine the moderating direct effects of other associations, and this aligns with our conceptual model [87].

Common method bias

Since the research relied on self-reported information collected at a single point in time, the risk of common method bias (CMB) was evaluated using both procedural and statistical methods. To reduce evaluation apprehension, anonymity and confidentiality were assured to respondents, and the questionnaire items were presented in a mixed order to minimize bias in the response pattern. Statistically, a single-factor test of Harman was conducted, revealing that the proportion of variance explained by the first factor was 47%, which is below the recommended threshold of 50%. This suggests that common method bias is likely not a significant concern [83]. Additionally, the variance inflation factor (VIF) values for all constructs did not exceed 3.3, indicating that multicollinearity and potential common method variance were not issues. Furthermore, a confirmatory factor analysis comparing the hypothesized multi-factor measurement model to a single-factor model demonstrated that the single-factor model had a significantly poorer fit, providing further evidence that common method variance does not account for most of the covariance among the study variables [88].

Results

We distributed 729 questionnaires and received 624 valid responses, resulting in a response rate of 85.6%. Among the participants, there were 106 men (17%) and 518 women (83%), with additional details provided in Table 2. This strong response rate not only may contribute to the reliability and representativeness of the data but also contributes valuable empirical insights into the relationship between perceived organizational support and professional identity among nursing professionals in Shandong Province [88]. It is important to interpret these findings in light of the professional composition of the sample. Table 2 indicates that the majority of respondents were Nurse Practitioners (18.4%), followed by supervisory nurses or higher (60.0%), while general nurses made up a smaller percentage (21.6%). Advanced or supervisory nurses typically possess more clinical experience, professional autonomy, and interaction with the organization, which can influence their perceptions of organizational support and their level of professional identity. Therefore, the observed relationships between perceived organizational support, self-efficacy, and professional identity may reflect the perspectives of more experienced or specialized nursing staff. This consideration is crucial when interpreting the findings and comparing them with studies focused primarily on staff nurses or early-career practitioners.

The Professional Identity and self-efficacy correlation was relatively high (r = 0.821), though it was theoretically consistent with the nursing literature and Social Cognitive Theory. Competence perceptions are closely related to identity formation in healthcare contexts, such as nursing, as confidence related to the capability of the profession influences professional self-concept and occupational commitment. Discussion

The gained insights aid in determining the main factors affecting the pathway of career development among nurses and emphasize organizational mechanisms that can be used to enhance their sense of professional belonging. Also, the implications of the findings are practical both in the overall quality of healthcare services and patient satisfaction, as the nursing workforce is more motivated and professionally engaged. The current research examined the process by which perceived organizational support can contribute to professional identity in nurses working in public hospitals in Shandong Province and identified the moderating effect of self-efficacy and the hypothesized but not substantiated intermediary position of organizational culture. The empirical evidence indicates that POS is a strong predictor of PI (β = 0.410, p < 0.001), which supports Hypothesis 1 and corresponds with previous literature that identifies professional identity as an outcome of organizational recognition, trust, and support. The fact that POS and PI are positively related can be related to the previous studies that show that contextual structures and organizational affirmation affect professional identity, which is associated with emotional attachment and allegiance of individuals toward their profession [4, 93]. In addition, potential measurement overlap between the Professional Identity and Self-efficacy constructs was addressed by removing conceptually similar items during the measurement validation process. The revised model demonstrated improved discriminant validity, ensuring that both constructs represent distinct theoretical concepts.

In the context of Social Exchange Theory, nurses have the opportunity to mutualize the care of the organization with more affinity and commitment to their professional activities, which is associated with identity formation as an element of a relationship process of exchange [22, 94]. Hypothesis 2 is also substantially supported by the results, that is, POS has a positive impact on self-efficacy (β = 0.596, p < 0.001). This correlation is in line with the postulates of SCT, which states that when people are in conducive surroundings, they will be able to mobilize psychological resources that will reinforce their belief in their ability to succeed [31]. According to the previous research, it was also observed that POS has been associated with higher levels of self-efficacy among teachers, bank workers, and medical employees, indicating that the support provokes intrinsic motivation and performance confidence [95]. The existing results can be applied to the nursing field of China, which focuses on institutional appreciation, developmental opportunities, and emotional reassurance to make nurses see themselves as capable and professionally competent. Moreover, findings support Hypothesis 3, where self-efficacy does predict professional identity significantly (β = 0.553, p < 0.001). This result aligns with the research that demonstrates self-efficacy as a variable that is related to professional engagements, resilience, and commitment to a workplace [96, 97].

Self-efficacy within SCT is a cognitive process that supports professional identity, since when individuals believe that they are highly competent, they internalize the role that they have at work as a significant component of themselves and their sense of self [98]. There is also a relatively high correlation between self-efficacy and professional identity, which calls for theoretical discussion. Efficacy beliefs are closely linked to internalization of professional identity in nursing because clinical confidence, problem-solving skills, and perceptions of competence are directly related to occupational self-definition [99]. Self-efficacy must not be equated with professional identity, however. Self-efficacy is specifically about perceived capability, and professional identity has more general aspects like professional belonging, value internalization, emotional commitment, and role-based self-concept [100]. Thus, the maintenance of a high correlation across items following the item refinement did not indicate that competence beliefs and identity formation were not distinct.

The mediation analysis gives good substantiation to Hypothesis 4, where POS and PI are partially mediated by self-efficacy (β = 0.330, p < 0.001). According to this mediation, organizational support is not only directly associated with the professional identity of nurses but also indirectly, via strengthening the perceived competence of nurses. This result is supported by previous research that revealed that self-efficacy mediates the outcomes of organizational support on outcomes, including emotional resilience, competence, and professional engagement [101]. This confirms the point that professional identity is created not only under the influence of structural conditions but also under the influence of subject psychological empowerment [102]. This study focuses on the self-efficacy construct as it relates to nurses’ perceived abilities, rather than their objectively measured professional competence. Consequently, the results should not be interpreted as evidence that organizational support directly contributes to clinical skills. Instead, the findings indicate that when organizational conditions are favorable, nurses’ confidence in their professional abilities increases and is positively linked with professional identification. The result again confirmed the integrated theoretical perspective of SET and SCT, which suggests that organizational support acts as a relational exchange process first, and is then internalized as self-efficacy beliefs, which in turn enhance professional identity among nurses [103].

Surprisingly, Hypothesis 5 was rejected: the organizational culture did not mediate the influence of self-efficacy on professional identity (β = −0.016, t = − 0.694, p = 0.487). This result is contrary to theoretical assumptions and previous empirical research that indicate that collaborative and supportive cultures reinforce the process of identity formation [104, 105]. Nevertheless, this immoderation implies that there could be a cultural contextual meaning. Although the moderating effect of organizational culture on the relationship between self-efficacy and professional identity was not statistically significant, this finding remains theoretically informative. One possible explanation is the relatively limited variation in perceived organizational culture across public hospitals, which may reduce the likelihood of detecting interaction effects. Another explanation may relate to the level of measurement, as organizational culture was assessed at the individual perception level rather than aggregated at the organizational level. It is also possible that the relationship between self-efficacy and professional identity is relatively robust across different cultural contexts. Future studies could further explore this relationship using multi-level designs or samples with greater cultural variability.

The assumption that identity formation is not a purely internal psychological process, but that contextual reinforcement mechanisms are also involved in shaping identity formation, was the basis for proposing organizational culture as a moderator. The supportive environments are expected to support the efficacy beliefs to translate into professional identity, based on Social Cognitive Theory. Supportive environments consist of social validation, psychological safety, and opportunity for professional participation. Despite the lack of any significant moderation effect in this study, the results indicate that the effect of self-efficacy on professional identity may be consistent across various organizational contexts in Chinese public hospitals. The result may be because the institutions in the Chinese healthcare system are relatively standard, and nurses’ sense of professional authority helps to define their professional identity, despite some slight cultural differences between hospitals.

The institutional culture in the Chinese public hospitals can be standardized and regulated more than being differentiated on a per-unit or microclimate basis within an organization. Alternatively, it is also possible that the strength of the direct connection between self-efficacy and PI is so strong that any further cultural amplification becomes insignificant. The findings suggest that, irrespective of the type of culture, be it hierarchical, clan, or market-oriented, the personal belief of competence is always a factor in identity formation [106]. Also, based on demographic analysis, most of the respondents were young-to-middle-aged female nurses and had a bachelor’s degree, serving mostly at the internal medicine and surgical departments. This population trend coincides with the national trends in the nursing workforce and puts this study in a representative population.

Another factor of importance when interpreting the findings is the professional makeup of the sample. A significant number of the respondents occupied advanced or supervisory nursing roles, with 60.0% of respondents being supervisory nurses or above. Higher-level professional nurses typically have more clinical experience, organizational exposure, decision-making authority, and professional autonomy, which can promote feelings of organizational support and bolster professional identity. These nurses are also more likely to be involved in leadership activities, mentoring, and institutional communication processes, which could boost their confidence and professional belonging. Therefore, the positive constructs of perceived organizational support, self-efficacy, and professional identity, which were found in the relationship between the three, may be more reflective of the experiences of experienced or senior nursing staff than of newly employed or junior nurses. This sample composition also accounts for the lack of significant interaction between self-efficacy and professional identity in the organizational context, since more highly experienced nurses may have less need for the context of the organization to influence their professional identity or competence than less experienced ones. Thus, the results need to be interpreted with caution when applied to early career nurses or less experienced clinical staff. Future research should evaluate the relationships for various career phases and between various professional ranks and nursing levels to see if they differ between these groups.

It also supports the usefulness of identity-building processes to retention and well-being among younger nurses who desire to have a long career. Collectively, the conclusions give a subtle insight into the identity development in nursing [107]. The direct impact of POS and self-efficacy on PI is high and significant, which underlines the role of organizational care and personal psychological empowerment. The lack of cultural moderation begs the question of the conceptualization and measurement of the cultural influences in large-scale epidemiological institutions [108, 109]. In general, the findings contribute to the theoretical understanding of the multifaceted and multi-layered nature of the development of professional identification among nurses in the Chinese health care environment.

Theoretical contribution

The study has three different theoretical implications. The first thing is that it increases the scope of the Social Exchange Theory by demonstrating that POS starts a relational exchange process that results in the reinforcement of professional identity among nurses. Instead of the usual tendency to concentrate only on general workplace attitudes (e.g., job satisfaction or retention), this research takes SET to the area of identity construction as psychological feedback to the organizational investment. The findings confirm the idea that professional identity is a socio-relational construct that is informed by organizational-employee exchanges of care, recognition, and respect.

Second, the research adds to the Social Cognitive Theory by showing that self-efficacy is an important cognitive mediator in the POS, PI association. Although literature has been able to draw the relationship between POS and self-efficacy independently and between self-efficacy and work-related outcome, this study establishes the relationships in a linear identity formation process. This gives empirical support to the statement in SCT that efficacy beliefs make environmental support into internalized identity structures. Third, the research questions the hypotheses of the mediating role of organizational culture in the cognitive-identity process. The insignificant moderating outcome indicates that at least in the Chinese hospital contexts, self-efficacy operates without being influenced by the cultural differences in professional identity formation. This calls for the editing of the existing theoretical models, which have considered the organizational culture as a universal contextual amplifier.

Rather, the theory of the future should take into account the national cultural norms, institutional standardization, and professional hierarchies that could counterbalance the intervening effects. Altogether, combining SET and SCT, proving psychological mediation, and challenging the assumptions of cultural contingency through this study contribute to the theoretical level of understanding how an organizational support system influences the formation of professional identity among nurses.

Practical contribution

The implications of the findings have a high level of practical importance in healthcare administration, policymakers, and the human resource departments of the hospital. To begin with, the close positive correlation between POS and PI indicates that the institutional policies need to focus on obvious organizational care, i.e., recognition programs, workload accommodation, open communication, and meaningful career development support to help nursing personnel identify with their profession. Enhancement of organizational support may help reduce turnover intention and improve work motivation. Second, due to the mediation of self-efficacy, hospitals ought to invest in interventions that can boost the beliefs of competence among nurses. Coach-oriented leadership behaviors should be embraced by supervisors who stimulate autonomy, constructive feedback, and learning. Building the confidence of nurses will not only increase the performance of nurses but also establish better intra-professional feelings in them.

In particular, hospitals may enhance P.O.S by implementing formal mentoring programs where experienced nurses serve as mentors for younger nurses regarding professional adjustment, emotional coping, and career advancement. Further reinforcement of the value and belonging of nurses to an organization could come from recognition mechanisms like employee appreciation programs, performance-based recognition, and public recognition of clinical achievements. Furthermore, empowering leadership behaviours such as frequent communication with staff, staff participation in decision making, and provision of psychological support services can contribute to the development of an empowering work culture. Healthcare facilities can provide self-efficacy-building tools such as simulation-based clinical training, competency development workshops, peer-assisted learning, and structured feedback systems, which enable nurses to build confidence in handling complex clinical scenarios. The reflective practice sessions and ongoing professional development opportunities may bolster nurses’ confidence in their professional practice, which can foster a more positive professional image and contribute to the sustainability of the workforce in the long term.

Third, in spite of the fact that no moderating effect of organizational culture was observed in the case of the association between SE and PI, the significant positive associations between OCA and both SE and PI indicate that cultural environments, even though interactive, still have an underlying role. Therefore, the promotion of inclusive and conducive departmental cultures is significant.

The leaders in the organization ought to promote teamwork, values, integrity, respectful interaction, and safety. Lastly, since the demographic statistics indicate the presence of large numbers of young nurses in the initial career progression, the organizations ought to establish identity-affirming career advancement opportunities, present explicit promotion indicators, role specialization routes, and length of employment guarantees. In general, the results are empirical reasons supporting the strategic investment into the organizational support systems and psychological empowerment as the principal factors contributing to the committed and stable nursing workforce.

Contextualization of the Chinese public hospital system

The importance of our findings must be understood within the context of the Chinese public hospital system, characterized by a hierarchical structure, a focus on standardized clinical practices, and workforce challenges. Nurses face issues such as high patient-to-nurse ratios, demanding workloads, and bureaucratic processes, which can lead to role strain and professional burnout [110, 111]. This is particularly relevant to our finding that POS is one of the strongest predictors of PI and self-efficacy. Even minor demonstrations of organizational concern, such as fair workload distribution, open communication between supervisors and staff, and recognition of effort, can serve as powerful countermeasures against the depersonalizing effects of a rigid system. Additionally, the non-significant moderating role of organizational culture suggests that in a highly standardized public sector, the direct and empowering influence of self-efficacy on professional identity is a significant psychological phenomenon that operates independently of minor cultural differences between hospitals. This indicates that positive outcomes related to nurses’ identities can be universally achieved by investing in their sense of competence and confidence.

Quality of workforce and patient care

The mediating effect of perceived organizational support on professional identity through self-efficacy provides a research-based framework for enhancing workforce sustainability. The opposite of a strong professional identity often manifests as turnover intention and burnout, two widespread challenges in nursing. Our model suggests that hospitals can address these issues not only by increasing salaries but also by investing in the psychological empowerment of their employees. When nurses operate in a supportive environment characterized by high POS, their self-efficacy improves, allowing them to believe they can achieve their goals. This newfound confidence subsequently is associated with their professional identity, making them more resilient to stress and more committed to their roles. Nurses with a fortified professional identity are more likely to view their challenging jobs as a vocation rather than a source of exhaustion.

While patient outcomes were not directly measured in this study, we can infer a strong connection to the quality of care provided. Nurses with high self-efficacy and a strong professional identity are more likely to demonstrate engaged, compassionate, and safe clinical behaviors. They are better equipped to advocate for patients, navigate complex situations, and adhere to best practices. Therefore, organizational support not only fosters a stable and satisfied workforce but also lays the psychological foundation for delivering high-quality, patient-centered care. Health care administrators should view interventions aimed at enhancing perceived organizational support (POS) and self-efficacy as integral components of a comprehensive patient safety and quality improvement strategy, rather than as mere fringe benefits.

Ethics of identity-concentrated interventions

While developing professional identity is a noble pursuit, the ethical implications of such psychological interventions must be considered. Our study highlights a potential ethical conflict: the fine line between organizational support and exploitation. Firstly, interventions aimed at strengthening professional identity should respect nurses’ autonomy. These initiatives should foster a personal sense of calling and commitment rather than manipulate emotions to extract more work from nurses. If nurses perceive organizational support as merely a tactic to increase their workload without addressing systemic issues like understaffing, it can lead to cynicism and resentment, ultimately undermining the trust that perceived organizational support (POS) is meant to build. Ethical integrity requires transparent communication and a genuine commitment to improving working conditions.

Secondly, there is a risk of unintentional consequences, such as over-commitment leading to burnout. A strong professional identity can be a double-edged sword. Dedicated nurses may develop unrealistically high expectations of themselves and others, blame themselves when systems fail, and sacrifice their well-being for the sake of their patients and profession. Emotional exhaustion can intensify in resource-constrained environments, particularly if a sense of duty is not accompanied by adequate organizational support. Therefore, efforts to build professional identity must be complemented by robust structural and psychological safeguards. Organizations have a moral obligation to ensure that when they encourage nurses to foster a sense of belonging, they also provide the necessary support for manageable workloads, mental health resources, and ethical leadership to prevent this commitment from becoming detrimental. The positive POS-SE-PI loop we identified should be sustained through ongoing organizational reciprocity, rather than as a one-time intervention aimed solely at maximizing productivity.

Conclusion

This research has given empirical evidence that perceived organizational support is significantly positively associated with professional identity in nurses, and the association is partially mediated by self-efficacy. Using the premises of both the Social Exchange Theory and the Social Cognitive Theory, the study proves that nurses empowered by their organizations develop self-confidence in their professional skills, which in effect reinforces their role as esteemed care providers. The results are relevant to contemporary healthcare management research to determine the factors that support career commitment, resilience, and professional satisfaction among nursing professionals. Furthermore, the fact that organizational culture has no moderating impact undermines the existing ideas of contextual amplification and poses a question that personal psychological resources can have a strong identity-creating impact regardless of the state of culture.

The result makes one re-examine the operationalization and interpretation of cultural variables in a healthcare organization, especially in highly standardized systems like the Chinese public hospitals. The overall implication of these findings is that organizational practices, psychological empowerment, and professional identity are intertwined among the nursing employees. The research indicates the need to make strategic investments in enabling environments and competence-building systems to maintain a robust and dedicated nursing workforce. The reinforcement of professional identity in both organizational and psychological pathways have significant outcomes not only on the welfare of the employees but also on the quality of patient care, staff retention, and stability of the healthcare system. This study eventually adds up to a detailed and combined insight into the processes that constitute professional identity in nursing and provides evidence-based guidance on future organizational and managerial actions.

Limitations and future directions

Due to the cross-sectional research design employed in this study, causal relationships among perceived organizational support, self-efficacy, and professional identity cannot be definitively established. Consequently, the findings should be viewed as statistical associations rather than causal effects. Future longitudinal or experimental studies are essential to determine the temporal ordering and causal mechanisms linking these constructs.

First, the cross-sectional design limited the ability to draw causal implications from the recognized pathways. Longitudinal studies could track identity development over time, providing a more accurate reflection of evolving psychological and organizational processes. Second, the use of self-report measures may have introduced social desirability bias, particularly in hierarchical healthcare settings where respondents might fear negative impressions. Incorporating mixed methods, such as interviews and behavioral observations, would enhance the validity of the measurements.

Third, this research was conducted in a single province in China among public hospitals, which may limit cultural and institutional generalizability. Future studies should replicate this research in private hospitals across different regions of China and in various global contexts to examine potential variations.

Additionally, the lack of a moderation effect from organizational culture suggests that the cultural variable may have been measured too broadly or that the cultural homogeneity in the sampled hospitals reduced variation. Future research could explore microcultures within departments or assess cultural influences at the leadership level. Finally, incorporating additional mediators, such as psychological capital, emotional labor, or job crafting, could provide a more comprehensive model of identity formation. With theoretical and methodological advancements, future studies will be better equipped to analyze the complex interactions between organizational structures, individual psychology, and identity-related occupational commitment.
