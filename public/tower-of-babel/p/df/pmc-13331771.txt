When MRI and CSF are inconclusive: A case report of neurobrucellosis presenting with dysarthria in an adolescent
Authors: Yara Ghanem, Sameh Issa, Rami Shrouf, Ahmad Khleif, Talal Asafrah, Mahmoud Abdelrazzaq Abu Mayaleh, Safwat Zidat, Ayham RA Zidat
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionNeurobrucellosis is a serious complication of brucellosis, a common zoonotic infection. Its clinical diagnosis is often challenging due to the wide spectrum of clinical manifestations and the limited sensitivity of conventional diagnostic tools, including neuroimaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis, which may be normal or non-diagnostic.Case presentationA 17-year-old male was recently diagnosed with brucellosis and was on standard triple antibiotic therapy. He presented with worsening systemic symptoms and new dysarthria. Contrast-enhanced brain magnetic resonance imaging was normal despite clear neurologic involvement. Introduction

Brucellosis stands as one of the most prevalent zoonotic diseases globally, with an estimated 500,000 new human cases reported annually [1]. Caused by Gram-negative coccobacilli of the genus Brucella, the infection is primarily transmitted to humans through the consumption of unpasteurized dairy products or direct contact with infected animals such as sheep, cattle, and goats. While its incidence has declined in many industrialized nations, it remains endemic and a significant public health concern in regions including the Middle East, the Mediterranean basin, parts of Asia, Africa, and South America [2]. The disease's impact is not only on human health but also on the economic and agricultural sectors of these endemic areas [1].

While systemic brucellosis can affect nearly any organ system, involvement of the nervous system, termed neurobrucellosis (NB), is a relatively uncommon but formidable complication [3]. The reported incidence of NB varies widely in the literature, ranging from 0.5% to as high as 25% of all brucellosis cases, and it is considered a sign of severe disease [4]. Neurological complications are particularly infrequent in the pediatric and adolescent populations, where they may affect less than 1% of children with systemic infection [2]. The clinical manifestations of NB are notoriously protean, capable of mimicking a vast array of other neurological disorders, including meningitis, meningoencephalitis, myelitis, cranial neuropathies, cerebrovascular events, and psychiatric syndromes [5]. This clinical heterogeneity often leads to significant diagnostic delays, with the diagnosis typically being made 2–12 months after the onset of symptoms, thereby increasing the risk of long-term morbidity and poor outcomes [2].

The diagnosis of neurobrucellosis is particularly challenging due to the lack of a single definitive clinical, radiological, or laboratory finding. Instead, it relies on a combination of clinical suspicion and supportive investigations, all of which may be non-specific. Conventional diagnostic tools, while essential, have important limitations. A substantial proportion of patients with clinically confirmed NB, in some series approaching 50%, may have unremarkable or "normal" neuroimaging studies [1].Similarly, CSF analysis may not always exhibit the classic inflammatory changes expected in a central nervous system (CNS) infection, and microbiological confirmation through culture is often difficult to achieve [6]. This diagnostic uncertainty places greater emphasis on clinical judgment and the use of adjunctive diagnostic methods such as serology and molecular testing (e.g., PCR), particularly in endemic regions [7]. Consequently, reliance on a single negative investigation may be misleading and may delay appropriate therapy.

This report presents the case of a 17-year-old male with confirmed neurobrucellosis whose clinical picture was characterized by the subacute onset of dysarthria (slurred speech) in the setting of a normal contrast-enhanced brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and minimally abnormal CSF findings. The case is presented to highlight the diagnostic challenge of neurobrucellosis when routine neuroimaging and CSF analysis are non-diagnostic, and to emphasize the importance of integrating clinical findings with serological and molecular evidence in establishing the diagnosis.

Case presentation

The patient is a 17-year-old male with no significant past medical history, who was in his usual state of good health prior to the current illness. Approximately three weeks before hospital admission, the patient was diagnosed with brucellosis at an outpatient clinic. He commenced on a treatment regimen of oral doxycycline (100 mg twice daily) and rifampicin (600 mg once daily). One week prior to admission, intravenous gentamicin was added to his regimen. Despite this triple-antibiotic therapy, the patient’s condition failed to improve. He experienced persistent constitutional symptoms, including general fatigue, fever, loss of appetite, and vomiting. A few days before presenting to the hospital, he developed a new and concerning neurological symptom: slurred speech (dysarthria).

On Day 1, the patient was admitted to the medical ward. On the same day, he developed symptomatic sinus bradycardia with heart rates in the 50 s, prompting an immediate transfer to the Medical Intensive Care Unit (MICU) for close monitoring. The initial working diagnoses upon admission were brucellosis with suspected CNS involvement (neurobrucellosis) and a need to rule out infective endocarditis (IE) given the fever and new cardiac finding.

Upon admission, laboratory investigations revealed leukopenia with a white blood cell (WBC) count of 3.1 × 10⁹/L (normal range: 4.0–10.0 ×10⁹/L), accompanied by a relative lymphocytosis of 53.4% (normal range: 25–45%) and monocytosis of 15.1% (normal range: 0–6%). Liver function tests were significantly elevated, with an alanine aminotransferase (ALT) of 168 U/L (normal range: 0–45 U/L) and an aspartate aminotransferase (AST) of 128 U/L (normal range: 0–37 U/L). The C-reactive protein (CRP) level was low at 1.9 mg/L (normal range: up to 6 mg/L). Three sets of blood cultures were drawn from different sites, all of which ultimately showed no growth after five days of incubation.

A comprehensive diagnostic workup was initiated. On Day 2, a contrast-enhanced brain MRI was performed and reported as normal, with no evidence of meningitis, encephalitis, abscess, or vascular abnormalities. A chest X-ray was also unremarkable. A transthoracic echocardiogram (ECHO) showed suspected vegetations, raising concern for IE. However, a subsequent transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) performed the same day definitively ruled out vegetations, revealing only a mild posterior pericardial effusion.

Neurologically, the patient remained conscious, alert, and oriented throughout his admission, but the dysarthria persisted. To further investigate for neurobrucellosis, a lumbar puncture was performed on Day 4. The CSF analysis revealed a WBC count of 10 cells/mm³ (reference range: 0–5 cells/mm³), a normal protein level of 27.7 mg/dl (normal range: 15–45 mg/dl), and a normal glucose level of 63 mg/dl (normal range: 50–80 mg/dl). The CSF appeared clear. CSF Gram stain and bacterial cultures were negative. Brucella PCR on CSF returned positive, confirming central nervous system involvement. Serological testing for brucellosis performed earlier showed strongly positive results, with a Brucella IgG index of 3.2 and an IgM index of 3.1 (positive result: >1.2 index).

Following an infectious disease consultation, the patient's antibiotic regimen was modified for presumed Neurobrucellosis. The previous regimen was discontinued, and he started on intravenous ceftriaxone 2 g every 12 h, oral doxycycline 100 mg every 12 h, and oral rifampicin 600 mg once daily.

Over the course of his 8-day hospital stay, the patient demonstrated significant clinical improvement. The symptomatic bradycardia resolved completely within 48 h. His dysarthria showed progressive improvement, and his vomiting subsided. Serial monitoring of liver enzymes showed a downward trend. By Day 8, he was afebrile and hemodynamically stable.

The patient was discharged on Day 8 in an improving and stable condition. The final discharge diagnoses were neurobrucellosis and resolved sinus bradycardia. The discharge plan included continuation of intravenous ceftriaxone for a total of 22 days, followed by a prolonged course of oral doxycycline and rifampicin for a total of four months. He was scheduled for outpatient follow-up to monitor his clinical progress and liver function status.

Discussion

This report describes a diagnostically challenging case of adolescent neurobrucellosis in which the diagnosis was established through a combination of clinical suspicion, supportive serology, and molecular confirmation, despite non-diagnostic conventional investigations, including neuroimaging and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis. The case illustrates a key clinical question: how should neurobrucellosis be diagnosed when routine investigations such as MRI and CSF are inconclusive or minimally abnormal?

Rather than relying on a single definitive test, the diagnosis of neurobrucellosis requires a multimodal and clinicopathological approach, integrating epidemiological exposure, systemic infection, neurological manifestations, and adjunctive laboratory testing. In this case, the presentation with isolated dysarthria, together with a normal brain MRI, minimally abnormal CSF, and negative cultures, demonstrates how reliance on conventional diagnostic pathways alone may result in missed or delayed diagnosis.

A central issue in neurobrucellosis is the limited sensitivity and variability of cornerstone diagnostic modalities. Neuroimaging, although essential for excluding alternative etiologies, is frequently non-revealing. Published literature suggests that a substantial proportion of patients with confirmed neurobrucellosis—up to 40–50% in some series—may demonstrate normal or non-specific MRI findings [8]. This highlights that a normal MRI cannot exclude CNS involvement, as the underlying pathology may reflect microvascular inflammation, small-vessel vasculitis, or functional impairment below the resolution of conventional imaging techniques [9].

Similarly, CSF findings in neurobrucellosis exhibit a broad spectrum of abnormalities. While the classic pattern includes lymphocytic pleocytosis, elevated protein, and low glucose, many patients present with minimal or even normal CSF parameters, particularly in early or partially treated disease [10], [11]. In the present case, only mild pleocytosis was observed, underscoring that CSF analysis alone lacks sufficient sensitivity to rule out the diagnosis.

Microbiological cultures from both blood and CSF were negative. This is a common challenge in diagnosing brucellosis, as Brucella species are fastidious, slow-growing, intracellular organisms that are difficult to isolate, particularly after the initiation of antibiotic therapy [6]. In this patient, the prior three-week course of multiple antibiotics almost certainly rendered the cultures sterile, removing a potential "gold standard" for diagnosis.

In the setting of non-diagnostic neuroimaging, minimally abnormal CSF findings, and negative culture results, the diagnosis of neurobrucellosis in this case relied on a multidimensional clinical reasoning approach rather than a single confirmatory test. The presence of a previously established diagnosis of brucellosis, together with clinical deterioration despite appropriate antimicrobial therapy and the development of a new focal neurological deficit (dysarthria), significantly increased the suspicion for central nervous system involvement. In this setting, serological evidence played a key supportive role, as markedly positive Brucella IgM and IgG titers reinforced the likelihood of active infection in the appropriate clinical context [5]. However, definitive diagnosis was ultimately established through CSF Brucella PCR, which provided direct microbiological confirmation of CNS involvement. This case therefore underscores that in neurobrucellosis, diagnostic certainty is often achieved through the integration of clinical assessment with serological and molecular tools rather than conventional investigations alone.

From a clinical perspective, the patient’s primary manifestation was dysarthria, a motor speech disorder that typically points to dysfunction in the cerebellum, brainstem, or associated motor pathways [12]. The absence of any corresponding lesion on MRI necessitates a consideration of pathophysiological mechanisms that can cause focal neurological deficits without producing overt structural changes. The most plausible explanation in this context is a localized inflammatory or ischemic process, likely driven by brucellar vasculitis [3].

Cerebrovascular involvement is a recognized, albeit uncommon, manifestation of neurobrucellosis. It is thought to result from an inflammatory vasculitic process affecting both large and small cerebral vessels, leading to a wide spectrum of neurological presentations, including ischemic stroke, transient ischemic attacks, intracranial hemorrhage, and venous sinus thrombosis [3]. Several reports have described cerebrovascular presentations as the primary manifestation of neurobrucellosis, including focal neurological deficits such as aphasia or hemiparesis [13]. In this context, the patient’s dysarthria may be explained by a transient or subtle microvascular inflammatory process involving the brainstem or cerebellar pathways, which may not be detectable on conventional neuroimaging. This interpretation is consistent with the recognized limitation of MRI in excluding small-vessel or functional inflammatory pathology in neurobrucellosis.

The presence of systemic manifestations in this patient, including symptomatic bradycardia and a mild pericardial effusion, further supports the multisystem inflammatory nature of brucellosis, which may involve multiple organ systems concurrently. Although cardiac involvement is less frequent than osteoarticular or neurological disease, it has been described and reflects systemic disease activity [3]. Similarly, transient elevation of liver enzymes is a well-recognized feature of systemic brucellosis and typically resolves with appropriate antimicrobial therapy.

A key aspect of this case is the clinical deterioration despite standard therapy for uncomplicated brucellosis (doxycycline, rifampicin, and gentamicin), which raised concern for complicated disease with possible central nervous system involvement. This progression, particularly the emergence of new neurological symptoms during treatment, should prompt consideration of inadequate CNS penetration of antimicrobial therapy rather than treatment failure due to resistance alone.

The central nervous system represents a pharmacological sanctuary site due to the restrictive nature of the blood–brain barrier (BBB), which limits the penetration of several antimicrobial agents [14]. Although doxycycline and rifampicin demonstrate moderate CNS penetration, their concentrations may be insufficient in established CNS infection, particularly in the absence of optimized CNS-targeted therapy [7]. The subsequent clinical improvement following the addition of high-dose intravenous ceftriaxone, an agent with enhanced CNS penetration especially in the setting of inflammation, provides supportive therapeutic evidence for CNS involvement, rather than serving as a standalone confirmatory diagnostic criterion.

This clinical response underscores a key therapeutic principle in neurobrucellosis: successful management requires prolonged combination therapy with agents capable of achieving adequate CNS penetration. Recommended regimens typically include doxycycline, rifampicin, ceftriaxone, and/or trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole for extended durations (at least 4–6 months) to ensure eradication of intracellular organisms and reduce relapse risk. Relapse remains a recognized complication of brucellosis, occurring in a subset of patients, particularly when treatment duration is inadequate or CNS involvement is unrecognized. In this case, the clinical course is more consistent with initially insufficient CNS-targeted therapy rather than true relapse following completed treatment, highlighting the importance of early recognition of neuroinvasive disease.

Conclusion

This case highlights the diagnostic complexity of neurobrucellosis and emphasizes that its recognition cannot rely solely on conventional neuroimaging or routine cerebrospinal fluid analysis. Normal or minimally abnormal MRI and CSF findings do not exclude central nervous system involvement, particularly in endemic regions where clinical suspicion should remain high. Instead, the diagnosis of neurobrucellosis should be approached as a clinicopathological correlation, integrating exposure history, systemic brucellosis, neurological manifestations, and supportive laboratory evidence. In this context, serological testing and molecular methods such as CSF PCR play a critical role in confirming the diagnosis when conventional investigations are inconclusive. Early consideration of neurobrucellosis in patients with known brucellosis who develop new neurological symptoms is essential to avoid diagnostic delay and prevent irreversible neurological complications. Prompt initiation of appropriate CNS-penetrating antimicrobial therapy remains the cornerstone of management and is associated with favorable outcomes.
