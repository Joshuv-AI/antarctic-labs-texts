Crop yield and water productivity modeling using nonlinear growth functions
Authors: Iman Hajirad, Khaled Ahmadaali, Abdolmajid Liaghat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Growth curve modeling plays a crucial role in precision agriculture by enabling rapid analysis of plant growth dynamics. Understanding the complex mechanisms of crop growth is essential for optimizing agricultural productivity. In this study, nonlinear Logistic and Gompertz models were employed to predict biological yield and water productivity of silage maize in arid and semi-arid regions, using growing degree days (GDD) as a key predictor. The experiment included two primary irrigation regimes: deficit irrigation (W2 and W3, providing 60% and 80% of crop water requirements, respectively) and full irrigation (W1, providing 100%). A sigmoid model was also introduced for its ease of biological interpretation. To evaluate model performance, coefficient of determination (R²), normalized root mean square error (NRMSE), and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) were used. Results indicated that Logistic and Gompertz models achieved high accuracy, with R² exceeding 99% under pulse irrigation and 80% under continuous irrigation. These models revealed that the maximum biological yield rate occurred at GDD equal 1014 °C (50 days after planting). Furthermore, the absolute growth rate followed a bell-shaped pattern in the Logistic model and a right-skewed distribution in the Gompertz model. The findings confirm that Logistic and Gompertz models effectively simulate the dynamic growth of silage maize under varying irrigation and temperature conditions. These models not only facilitate quantitative crop growth predictions but also provide a decision-support tool for irrigation planning and precision crop management in arid and semi-arid regions. The integration of such models into smart agricultural systems can significantly enhance resource optimization and sustainable farming practices.

Introduction

Water scarcity is becoming a critical global issue, particularly in arid and semi-arid regions7. Agriculture, as the largest consumer of freshwater, uses approximately 70% of harvested water25,53, yet less than 60% of irrigation water is used efficiently8. With the growing global population, the demand for food increases, intensifying pressure on water resources13. To address this challenge, it is essential to enhance water conservation and efficiency through advanced irrigation methods, such as drip irrigation. Therefore, research should focus not only on increasing agricultural productivity but also on optimizing water use45.

Crop modeling has gained increasing importance over the past decade due to the need for sustainable agricultural practices in response to environmental degradation and population growth9,19,50,51,55. These models help optimize agro-environmental management, from fertilizer and water application to maximizing farm profitability. However, crop growth is a dynamic process influenced by environmental factors, management practices, and genetic traits, leading to complex interactions that affect yield outcomes10. When irrigation water is insufficient, evapotranspiration losses increase, and yields decline, making precise crop yield estimation essential for efficient irrigation system design61. Accurate yield predictions enable farmers to optimize irrigation strategies, ensuring higher productivity while supporting food security and sustainability11. Furthermore, early yield forecasting allows policymakers to manage agricultural risks effectively73.

Various approaches have been used to predict crop yields, including field surveys, statistical analyses, and crop growth models. While field surveys provide direct measurements, they are labor-intensive and time-consuming. Statistical methods, though effective in identifying correlations between crop performance and environmental factors, often struggle with generalizability4,49. In contrast, crop growth models offer a process-based approach to understanding plant development. Biomass, a key biophysical indicator of harvest efficiency, serves as a foundation for many yield prediction models27. However, biophysical models require extensive data inputs, limiting their applicability in data-scarce regions. Empirical regression models, such as Richards, Gompertz, and Weibull equations, offer simpler alternatives but may lack generalizability across diverse geographic areas5,46,67.

Plant growth modeling, which dates back to De Wit15, has evolved significantly over time, becoming an essential tool for evaluating crop productivity and predicting the effects of climate change2,26. A variety of well-established models, such as DSSAT-Maize, EPIC64, and CERES-Maize32, have been developed to simulate maize growth. Additionally, hybrid maize models like APSIM35, CropSyst58, and CERES-Maize33 have become widely utilized in maize research69. These models provide valuable insights into maize growth under different climatic conditions, irrigation practices, and fertilization strategies40. However, successful application of these models depends on proper parameter calibration, as different environmental conditions require model adjustments for accurate predictions. In water-limited regions, where maize productivity is heavily dependent on irrigation, optimizing water management becomes a priority41. Recent advancements in sensor technology have improved irrigation precision by enabling real-time monitoring of soil moisture levels6,48.

Water productivity (WP), defined as the ratio of crop yield to water use, is a key metric in evaluating irrigation efficiency. Enhancing WP is particularly critical in arid regions, where implementing efficient irrigation strategies, selecting high-WP maize varieties, and optimizing water distribution systems can significantly improve resource use efficiency28,29,34. Regression models offer valuable tools for assessing the effects of water deficits on plant growth, helping optimize irrigation strategies to minimize yield losses under limited water availability18. However, irrigation deficits must be carefully managed, considering critical growth stages and irrigation timing to minimize adverse effects on yield44. Although reduced irrigation generally leads to some yield loss, the relationship between irrigation levels and yield is not always linear. While many studies rely on linear regression models, Garcia et al.20 and Trout & DeJonge60 demonstrated that irrigation water use efficiency (IWUE) does not always decline proportionally with increasing water deficits. The choice of statistical models significantly affects the interpretation of results, and nonlinear regression models, particularly sigmoidal models, may better capture maize growth responses to water stress71. Sigmoidal models, such as Gompertz and Logistic equations, offer biologically meaningful parameters that enhance the understanding of crop-water interactions and improve yield optimization1,68. In order to simulate biological yield and water productivity for silage maize, our study used Logistic and Gompertz nonlinear regression models. This decision was supported by the fact that these models can faithfully capture the sigmoidal pattern of normal crop development dynamics. These nonlinear sigmoidal models are ideal for describing the biological yield characteristics and maize’s reaction to water stress, in contrast to simpler linear models that frequently miss growth plateauing. The ability to provide physiologically relevant parameters that improve yield optimization and our understanding of crop-water interactions is a major benefit of the logistic and Gompertz functions. Even while more intricate biophysical models provide in-depth mechanistic insights, they sometimes require large data inputs and are difficult to calibrate, particularly in areas with little data. Our strategy provides a workable substitute by utilizing the ease of use and potency of these regression-based methods for irrigation scheduling and yield forecasting.

In this study, we employ Gompertz and Logistic models to simulate the weekly biological yield (Biological yield refers to the amount of dry matter weight of the plant’s aerial organs (including leaves, stems, wood, husks, and ears)) and water productivity of ZP 606 maize under different irrigation levels. By integrating weekly WP estimations, key growth phases, and model-derived parameters, we aim to provide a comprehensive assessment of how irrigation deficits affect maize growth. The strength of this approach lies in its simplicity, making it suitable for broader applications, especially in data-scarce regions. However, this simplicity also introduces limitations, as these models do not capture detailed physiological processes in the way that biophysical models do. While regression-based models effectively describe growth dynamics and yield trends, they may lack the predictive power needed for precise process-based simulations under varying environmental conditions. Thus, in scenarios where detailed mechanistic insights are required, biophysical models remain advantageous. Nonetheless, our approach offers an alternative to traditional biophysical model calibration, leveraging regression-based techniques to enhance yield prediction accuracy while maintaining practical applicability in diverse agricultural settings.

Materials and methods

Field experiment

The study was conducted during the summer of 2018 at a 1300 m² field plot in the Pishvay Varamin region in central Iran (51°41’42.8"E, 35°19’51.9"N, 973 m, Fig. 1). The experiment employed two main irrigation regimes: full irrigation (W1, supplying 100% crop water requirement) and deficit irrigation (W2 and W3, supplying 60% and 80% crop water requirement, respectively). Additionally, two secondary factors were implemented: pulsed and continuous irrigation management.

Water and plant management

The study utilized a drip irrigation system (Tape) featuring local emitters discharging 0.7 L/h at 20 cm intervals, positioned midway between crop rows (Fig 3). The irrigation interval for all treatments was constant and two days. Irrigation remained continuous for all treatments until the six-leaf stage, transitioning to either continuous or pulsed management thereafter (Fig. 4). Water was supplied from nearby farm wells and passed through a filtration system before entering the farm’s irrigation network. Nitrogen fertilizer was applied through fertigation at a rate of 184 kg per hectare, evenly distributed over four stages throughout the growing season for all experimental treatments. To ensure proper germination, the first irrigation was carried out immediately after planting. Also, In order to regulate and quantify the amount of irrigation water volume, calibrated volume meters were also positioned in the flow path to each treatment.

Soil moisture measurement

Soil moisture monitoring was conducted using a PR2/6 Profile Probe (Fig. 5), calibrated for field conditions at various depths (0–10, 10–20, 20–30, 30–40, 50–60, and 90–100 cm). Access tubes, consisting of 120 cm long insulated PVC pipes (i.e., access pipe) with blocked ends, were installed in the center of each plot. The calibration equations for each depth were derived, and the Profile Probe data were used to determine the appropriate timing and depth of irrigation.

Pulsed and continuous irrigation treatment

Pulse irrigation consists of a series of irrigation cycles, where each cycle includes an irrigation phase (on) and a rest phase (off). The irrigation-rest cycle is repeated until the required amount of water is fully applied to the field. The irrigation depth remained identical for both pulse and continuous management regimes, and no rainfall was recorded throughout the experiment. Under continuous irrigation, water was applied uninterruptedly45. In contrast, for pulse irrigation, the calculated water requirement for each treatment was supplied in three pulses with equal on and off durations. Specifically, if the required irrigation time for a treatment was three hours, the irrigation was scheduled as three one-hour pulses with one-hour rest intervals between them, achieved by turning the irrigation valves on and off. Variation of reference evapotranspiration and cumulative irrigation depth over the growth period was presented in Fig. 6.

Model description

Logistic model

The logistic model depicts a sigmoidal curve that initially increases, accelerates in the middle, and then slowly reaches its maximum value at the end before leveling off. The crop growth curve follows this trend17,66. The logistics growth equation is defined as follows:2\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Y=\frac{A}{1+{e}^{B+Ct}}$$\end{document}

Where: Y represents the biological performance of the plant (biological performance represents the weight of the dry matter of the aerial parts of the plant (including leaves, stems, wood, pods and cobs) in this study, sampling was done from all treatments and repetitions, C is the upper limit of biological yield, t is the number of days after planting (DAP), A and B are the coefficients of the equation. Considering the effect of different weather conditions and planting dates, some reports have shown that it is possible to use GDD instead of DAP in logistics performance. On the other hand, GDD is an important factor for plant growth, which affects the formation of dry matter and plant performance. Therefore, the logistics model can be expressed as follows:3\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Y=\frac{A}{1+{e}^{B+C\times\:GDD}}$$\end{document}

Each product has upper and lower biological temperature limits, above and below which the growth of the product stops. The upper and lower biological temperature limits for fodder corn are 40 °C and 7 °C, respectively23. The logistic function meets the requirements of irreversible growth, substrate is proportional to the rate of the biochemical reactions, and the dry weight (amount of biomass) is proportional to the enzyme quantity involved in the biochemical reactions. It is one level of complexity over the simple exponential growth and monomolecular functions where the biochemical reactions occur at a constant rate and substrate is unlimited respectively59. GDD is the difference between the average daily temperature and the minimum temperature required for plant activity42:

4\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:GDD=\sum\:\left({T}_{avg}-{T}_{base}\right)$$\end{document}

Where: Tavg is the average daily temperature (in degrees Celsius) and Tbase is the minimum temperature required for plant activity (°C). McMaster and Wilhelm42 proposed the following method to calculate Tavg:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:\left\{\begin{array}{c}{T}_{avg}=\frac{\left({T}_{x}+{T}_{n}\right)}{2}\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\\\:{T}_{avg}={T}_{base}\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:if\:\:\:{T}_{avg}\le\:{T}_{base}\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\\\:{T}_{avg}={T}_{upper}\:\:\:\:\:\:\:if\:\:{T}_{avg}\ge\:{T}_{upper}\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\:\end{array}\right.$$\end{document}

Where: Tupper is the maximum tolerable temperature for plant activity (in degrees Celsius), Tx and Tn represent the maximum daily temperature and minimum daily temperature in degrees Celsius, respectively24.

Gompertz model

This model has been used for many years to investigate the death rate in human societies. After that, different forms of this relationship have been used in a variety of agricultural uses, such as checking the rate of seed germination and the speed of plant growth and development57. This model is presented as follows:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Y=A{\times\:e}^{\left(-{e}^{-B(t-C)}\right)}$$\end{document}

Where: A is the maximum biological yield index, B is the coefficient indicating the speed of yield increase, C is the time indicating the time when the growth rate of the product reaches its maximum value. According to the Gompertz model, at time C, the value of the product growth rate is equal to the value of the coefficient B22,43,70. The Gompertz model based on GDD is as follows:7\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:Y\left(GDD\right)=A{\times\:e}^{\left(-{e}^{-B(GDD-C)}\right)}$$\end{document}

Figure 7 shows a schematic view of the logistic and Gompertz functions and their parameters for a better understanding of the models. The Gompertz is similar to the logistic function in that it produces an s-shaped curve. However, substrate is assumed to be unlimited and biochemical reaction rates are reduced as GDD accumulate59. This results in the possibility of an asymmetric curve and increased flexibility over the logistic, but with a fixed inflection point. There are three parameters including the upper asymptote (A), the relative growth rate at M (B), and the GDD at which the growth rate is maximized (C)70. The coefficients of the sigmoid equations for both the Logistic and Gompertz models were determined using MATLAB 2019a and NCSS 2023 software.

Crop water productivity (CWP)

To evaluate water efficiency in the study area, the water productivity index was calculated. Water productivity can be estimated using various methods and equations. In general, it is defined as the ratio of the produced yield to the amount of water consumed. The yield can be expressed as total biomass or dry matter production, while water consumption may refer to transpiration, evapotranspiration, irrigation water, or evapotranspiration plus deep percolation. Crop water productivity is the ratio of yield to water consumption, which is calculated from the following equation:8\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:CWP=\frac{{Y}_{Biological}}{I}$$\end{document}

Where: YBiological represents biological yield (in tons per hectare) and I represents the depth of irrigation water (mm)25.

The simulation is considered excellent with a NRMSE less than 0.1, good if the NRMSE is greater than 0.1 and less than 0.2, acceptable if the NRMSE is greater than 0.2 and less than 0.3, and poor if the NRMSE is greater than 0.330,39.

In addition to NRMSE, the MAPE (Mean Absolute Percentage Error) index was also employed to assess model performance. The MAPE index was interpreted based on thresholds commonly adopted in agricultural modeling literature, where MAPE values below 10% are considered highly accurate, values between 10% and 20% acceptable, and values above 20% indicative of lower model performance30.

Result

The logistic equation based on Growing Degree Days (GDD) (Eq. (3)) was applied to measure the biological yield across different irrigation levels under pulse and continuous management strategies during the growing season. The comparison between the measured and predicted biological yields, shown in Fig. 11, confirmed that the logistic model based on GDD accurately predicted the biological yield. The relationship between predicted and measured yields across all treatments and measurement times is shown in Figs. 8 and 9. According to Table 2 and the statistical indicators, the model performed well, yielding high accuracy with R², NRMSE, and MAPE values ranging from 0.9930 to 0.9982, 0.0249 to 0.0514, and 0.0317 to 0.1113, respectively, for the logistic model, and from 0.9934 to 0.9991, 0.0173 to 0.0502, and 0.0552 to 0.1823, respectively, for the Gompertz model. Overall, both models successfully simulated maize’s biological performance under various irrigation conditions. Slight differences were observed between pulse and continuous irrigation management. Additionally, results showed that in deficit irrigation treatments, continuous irrigation management yielded better results compared to pulse irrigation. The values of parameters A, B, and C of Eq. (3) for different irrigation levels are shown in Table (2). These parameters were used to predict the biological yield at harvest for both models and compared to the measured yields (Figs. 8 and 9). This index was interpreted based on thresholds commonly adopted in agricultural modeling literature. The MAPE index, interpreted based on commonly used thresholds in agricultural modeling literature, indicated that most values (Tables 2 and 3) fell within the acceptable (10–20%) or highly accurate (< 10%) ranges, confirming the models’ reliability under different irrigation regimes and management strategies.

The results from the logistic model based on Growing Degree Days (GDD) demonstrated its capability to simulate maize’s biological performance effectively under varying irrigation conditions and management strategies. A comparison of predicted and measured yields showed that the logistic model provided accurate predictions. Statistical indicators further confirmed its high accuracy. The Gompertz model also performed well, though slight differences between treatments were observed. In terms of irrigation management, continuous irrigation was more effective in water deficit conditions than pulse irrigation. These findings highlight the potential of both models for optimizing water management, particularly in water-scarce regions. The models also proved valuable for predicting biological performance, making them useful tools for future irrigation planning and agricultural management.

Discussion

Due to its high input/high output nature, maize needs a steady and abundant supply of water in order to yield large amounts. Our findings supported previous observations in this area and shown how sensitive maize is to irrigation deficits. Even in the absence of future climate change estimates showing a severe water shortage, optimizing water management to fulfill crop demand is already a priority, particularly in dry and semi-arid regions26. This necessity underscores the importance of precise modeling tools that can guide irrigation practices under varying conditions. To further explore this relationship, the current study implemented advanced modeling techniques to quantify the effects of irrigation levels on maize performance. A detailed statistical comparison between the Logistic and Gompertz models was conducted using R², NRMSE, and MAPE across all treatments. The results (Tables 2 and 3) showed that both models achieved high predictive accuracy, particularly for biological yield (R² > 0.99). For water productivity, although the Logistic model slightly outperformed Gompertz under continuous irrigation, both models demonstrated acceptable error levels. This performance consistency indicates that both models are reliable tools for analyzing maize responses across different irrigation strategies. These comparisons support the robustness of both sigmoidal models under varying water management strategies. These findings underline the importance of selecting appropriate mathematical models to reflect maize’s physiological response to irrigation. The study employed logistic and Gompertz model analysis to quantify the impact of irrigation shortfall on maize biological yield and water productivity. In doing so, it highlighted the value of using biologically meaningful models over purely statistical or empirical alternatives. While these models were effective, it is worth noting that the response of maize to different inputs, including nitrogen and water, is nearly always assessed using a quadratic model, a linear model, or both14,16,26,47. The Gompertz and Logistic models have a horizontal asymptote that extrapolates the growth rate to decrease after its maximum, in contrast to the linear regression model’s assumption that the growth rate will increase continuously. As a result, they are better suited to the characteristics of the relationship between biological yield and water input. This distinction becomes especially relevant when interpreting crop responses in non-linear, stress-affected environments. An additional advantage of these sigmoidal models lies in the interpretability of their parameters. Another, possibly even more significant benefit of using this model rather than a linear fit is that the biological significance of its curve parameters are well-established26. The parameters A, B, and C of both Logistic and Gompertz models carry important biological significance. Parameter A represents the asymptotic maximum biological yield (ton/ha), B indicates the relative growth rate at the inflection point, and C is the GDD at which the growth rate is at its maximum. Such biologically meaningful parameters enable not only more accurate model fitting but also provide valuable insights into the growth dynamics of maize under different irrigation conditions.

Such clarity in parameter interpretation enhances the model’s utility in practical agricultural planning and policy-making. When maize experiences water stress, it produces a variety of response mechanisms. Water stress causes dry matter accumulation to move to the roots, which is why it is anticipated that above-ground biomass growth will be restricted in both quantity and rate63. Maize responds to water scarcity by reducing transpiration to maximize water uptake from the soil. While this adaptation increases water productivity (WP), it also limits growth. This trade-off illustrates the complexity of balancing water conservation with optimal biomass accumulation. At the lowest irrigation levels, although WP may be high, it reflects a trade-off between water savings and reduced productivity, as severe water stress leads to significant yield loss and increased production costs. On the other hand, moderate WP levels strike a balance by optimizing irrigation input, where mild water stress has minimal impact on yield, thereby maintaining productivity while conserving water and controlling production costs74.Understanding this balance is essential for formulating efficient irrigation regimes. To better understand the physiological and agronomic consequences of these stress adaptations, it is important to examine maize growth under different irrigation regimes.

Our results indicate that maize harvested for silage purposes follows a distinct growth pattern, with biomass accumulation reaching its peak before physiological maturity. The data analysis confirms that silage maize is typically harvested at the early dent stage when moisture content is suitable for ensiling. This early harvest timing significantly influences how growth models should be interpreted and applied. Given this harvest timing, model-based analysis becomes essential to interpret pre-maturity growth patterns and irrigation effects. From a biological perspective, the Logistic and Gompertz models provide insights into the plant’s adaptive responses to water stress. The observed plateau in biological yield under full irrigation corresponds to the crop’s physiological maturity phase, while delayed saturation under deficit treatments reflects prolonged stress-induced growth adjustment. Additionally, the model parameters reflect the shifts in growth dynamics and help identify critical periods for silage maize biomass accumulation, which is crucial for harvest timing and irrigation planning in arid and semi-arid regions. These findings demonstrate the value of integrating physiological principles into statistical modeling frameworks. However, despite the strong overall fit, some deviations from the modeled trend were observed. The model captured the overall growth dynamics well; however, a slight deviation in the final growth phase suggests that a polynomial or double sigmoidal function might better represent the growth trajectory at the end of the season, particularly under water-limited conditions. Such refinements could enhance accuracy, especially for late-stage predictions.

Similarly, in Fig. 12, while the overall trend of water productivity (WP) largely follows a sigmoidal pattern, a slight decline is observed after the peak in some treatments. This phenomenon, where WP does not strictly plateau but shows a minor decrease, is consistent with real-world agricultural scenarios, particularly under certain irrigation management strategies21,74. This underscores the necessity of aligning model expectations with observed field behavior. This subtle post-peak decline reflects the complexity of field conditions rather than a failure of the model. For silage maize, harvest occurs at the early dent stage to optimize moisture content, not necessarily at the absolute maximum dry matter accumulation or water use efficiency36,45. This early harvest stage ensures high ensiling quality but limits post-peak data availability in biomass accumulation. Consequently, model calibration must consider the biologically relevant harvest stages for silage.

If water continues to be applied beyond the period of maximal return in biomass accumulation, or if the plant begins early senescence under stress conditions, the ratio of biological yield to cumulative water input (WP) can exhibit a slight reduction after its peak, even if the absolute yield remains high37,56. Such dynamics underscore the importance of identifying the optimal irrigation window to maximize WP without incurring diminishing returns. Therefore, WP should be interpreted in relation to both physiological stage and water application patterns. This does not invalidate the use of sigmoidal models, as they effectively capture the most critical phases of WP development, including the rapid increase and the point of diminishing returns, which are crucial for irrigation scheduling1,54. While ideal sigmoidal curves approach a true asymptote, the observed post-peak fluctuations in field data represent complex biological responses to varying water availability and plant physiological stages, rather than a fundamental flaw in the model’s applicability for describing the general trend. This distinction reaffirms the practical value of these models despite minor end-phase deviations. The high statistical performance of the models (R², NRMSE, and MAPE) further supports their utility in predicting and managing WP under these conditions, focusing on the most agronomically relevant growth phases1,37. Nonetheless, some technical limitations in field sampling must also be acknowledged. It should be noted that inadequate sampling at the conclusion of the growing season is probably the cause of the minor upward tendency at the end of the Gompertz curve in some treatments (as seen in Fig. 9). Such sampling limitations are common in silage studies due to the early harvest schedule. The field data may not represent maximal biological maturity since silage corn is collected when still green, frequently in the early dent stage. The end result is a slight overestimation as the model fitting depends on a small amount of late-season data. Similar limitations have been documented in the literature, and this problem is typical in empirical models3,37. Therefore, future work should aim for more representative end-of-season data collection, where possible.

Even though the models performed well (R2 was consistently above 0.99 for biological yield) during the crucial stages of fast biomass accumulation, the very late growth phase showed minor deviations that are normal for empirical sigmoidal functions like the Logistic and Gompertz models. Instead of capturing the intricate nutrient redistribution or senescence that become more noticeable as the crop nears full physiological maturity or encounters extreme water stress at the very end of its cycle, these models are made to capture the general S-shaped growth trend that leads to an asymptote. This limitation is especially acceptable in the context of silage maize, which is usually harvested at the early dent stage to maximize moisture content for ensiling. Thus, the high accuracy during peak growth is far more critical than late-stage precision in this context. The models’ appropriateness for the study’s goals is therefore confirmed by the excellent accuracy attained during the primary biomass accumulation phase, which directly influences the best time to water and harvest silage. Therefore, despite minor late-stage deviations, the Gompertz and Logistic models remain valid and effective tools for evaluating silage maize yield and irrigation strategies. The models’ overall usefulness as straightforward yet reliable instruments for irrigation planning and yield estimate in water-limited contexts is unaffected by this little drawback at the extreme end of the growth curve. This highlights the practicality of using these models for real-world decision-making where timely interventions are critica. To ensure consistency and clarity in the mathematical formulation, the Logistic and Gompertz equations were standardized using GDD as the independent variable (Eqs. 3 and 7). All parameters were recalculated accordingly. The variables and parameters used in each model were explicitly defined to avoid ambiguity and to allow better reproducibility and interpretation. Such methodological rigor strengthens the model’s scientific credibility and aligns with prior research. Standardization also facilitates model comparison across studies and agro-ecological zones. This observation is in agreement with previous studies, which suggest that models incorporating additional flexibility in growth representation improve predictive accuracy in the late-season phase20,37.

Our findings showed that under pulse and continuous management, CWP significantly increased on the lowest irrigation regimes. This is a symptom of severe water stress and is consistent with study findings by Garcia et al.20 and Zwart & Bastiaanssen74. Therefore, decreasing irrigation water results in yield losses and increases water productivity in terms of both biological yield and CWP. This trade-off between yield reduction and water efficiency emphasizes the need for optimizing irrigation inputs, especially under resource-limited conditions. Biological yield is a key indicator in the development of silage maize yield. In particular, for arid regions such as Varamin, water scarcity and low soil nutrients were the two primary factors limiting silage maize yield production12,31,45. While the current model does not explicitly incorporate water-use parameters, our results confirm that water availability significantly influences biomass accumulation and final yield. Hence, even without direct integration of irrigation variables, the models reflect water-driven physiological responses through yield outcomes. In light of this, it becomes evident that future work must move beyond single-season calibration. Future research should focus on multi-season validation to ensure the model’s applicability under diverse irrigation regimes, especially in deficit-irrigated treatments where water stress plays a dominant role in determining yield outcomes. Despite the fact that the model was first validated using a single-season dataset, additional analysis is required to confirm its robustness across multiple growing seasons. Such an approach will enable more resilient and generalizable irrigation planning strategies. The transferability of the model to different irrigation scenarios requires validation under varying climatic conditions, as maize yield is highly dependent on water availability. These assessments are crucial for assessing the model’s resilience in wider applications and quantifying the potential effects of changing input parameters on model predictions. However, the main goal of this work was to empirically show that the Logistic and Gompertz models are applicable to field-based data under controlled settings. The model’s dependability is shown by its outstanding statistical performance (R2 > 0.99, low NRMSE, and MAPE). This statistical reliability strengthens the model’s foundation for future predictive tools. The system’s behavior under various irrigation regimes may already be inferred from the physiologically interpretable parameters (A, B, and C). Nonetheless, further research should expand the modeling scope to enhance decision support under real-world variability. Future studies will use multi-season and multi-location datasets to enable thorough sensitivity and uncertainty evaluations, significantly enhancing the model’s ability to assist decisions. Incorporating broader datasets will also allow more adaptive modeling tailored to diverse agro-climatic conditions.

In some areas, it may be agronomically and financially advantageous to assess alternative crops with higher intrinsic water productivity, especially in situations where water scarcity persists, since severe deficit irrigation drastically lowers maize yield despite slight increases in water productivity. The overarching goal of optimizing system-level production per unit of water intake is in line with this suggestion.

This aligns with broader system-level goals of improving output per unit of water use. In order to maximize water management and guarantee a good maize yield, simulating the biological characteristics of silage maize under various irrigation regimes, pulses, and ongoing management is crucial. When simulating the biological yield process of a crop, Gompertz and logistic models are a straightforward and useful tool37,72. Their simplicity allows integration into decision support systems accessible to both researchers and practitioners. Therefore, in order to simulate biological yield and water productivity under different irrigation levels, it is crucial to comprehend the effects of water and two irrigation management strategies on the parameters of the Logistic and Gompertz models.

The term “GDD” describes the effective temperature that crops acquire during a specific development stage. This temperature indicates the quantity of heat that crops need during this growth phase. It is more accurate to use GDD to anticipate crop growth since, in general, the amount of GDD needed by the same crop to complete its growth phase is rather steady23,37. This consistency in thermal time enhances the predictability of crop growth across environments. This stability makes GDD a valuable independent variable for modeling growth dynamics across variable climates. Recently, there has been an increase in interest in creating broad logistic models appropriate for many locations utilizing GDD. For instance, a universal logistic model was created by Liu et al.37 and Wang et al.63 to forecast the growth indices of cotton and winter wheat in various regions using the GDD, respectively. When compared to the DAP as an independent variable, the GDD-based logistic model can mitigate the effects of regional climatic variations on crop growth characteristics. This makes GDD-based modeling a robust choice for cross-regional agricultural forecasting. The primary causes of the variations in irrigation water management and temperature in the experimental locations were the biological yield and yield formation of silage maize. Thus, using GDD not only standardizes time-based comparisons but also reflects actual physiological development more accurately. As a result, we developed regression equations based on GDD that took into account how management style affected the logistic and Gompertz model’s parameters (A, B, and C). According to the variation trend of the Gompertz and Logistic model parameters, A, B, and C first increased at a given irrigation level before decreasing when a management type was used. This pattern reflects a non-linear response of maize to irrigation intensity, which is critical for fine-tuning irrigation schedules.

The biological yield can be predicted with accuracy by using these proven regression equations. Additionally, Ma et al.38 discovered that when irrigation volume rose, the variation trend of spring wheat’s logistic model parameters A (maximum yield) and B first increased and subsequently declined. However, parameter C did not manifestly alter. Similar variations in the logistic parameters (A, B, and C) of winter wheat under varying irrigation levels in China’s Loess Plateau were also found by Wang et al.62. These comparative insights reinforce the consistency and interpretability of the sigmoidal models across crops and regions. These comparative results support the broader applicability of the model structure across crop types and regions. The study’s suggested models are easy to compute and practical for farmers to apply. The results indicated above demonstrated how effectively the constructed prediction model functioned while simulating the biological yield of maize silage in the Varamin region. This practicality supports the use of these models in agricultural extension programs and local decision-making. However, as model performance slightly declined toward the end of the season, alternative functional forms, such as polynomial or double sigmoidal models, may better capture growth trends at the later stages. In future studies, refining model structure and expanding the dataset will be essential for improving predictability under water-deficit conditions. Such refinements will enhance the precision and adaptability of growth models in climate-sensitive farming systems.

Conclusions

Plant growth is complex, and involves many processes and its understanding is essential to maximize the crop’s potential. The use of modeling is an essential tool to characterize the plant’s growth and development, in addition, generates strategies for future plantings, adapting the management. Therefore, this study was conducted with the aim of using logistic and Gompertz functions to simulate biological performance and water productivity through many experimental crop growth models based on GDD. However, these models can only be applied for the same weather conditions under which models were developed. In this investigation, biological yield and water productivity of silage maize were simulated by Logistic and Gampertz growth model based on GDD under different water deficit levels under pulse and continuous management. Results indicated that Logistic and Gompertz function based on GDD predicted biological yield and water productivity during growing season with higher accuracy. Therefore, Logistic and Gompertz functions based on GDD that is based on the cumulative heat units can be used for different weather conditions and planting dates. Our results suggest that implementing Logistic and Gompertz regression would allow for a good evaluation of the data in addition to providing useful curve parameters. Using biological yield in this analysis also revealed critical periods in silage maize growth while as allowing us to estimate how and when biological yield growth is affected by the water scarcity. Considering our findings about the effect of irrigation deficit on maize, we suggest that substituting maize with a crop with higher water productivity in severe water scarcity could also be a valid solution. Also, in this study, we developed an empirical prediction model of biological yield and water productivity based on logistic and gompertz models under different water irrigation levels and pulse and continuous management in Varamin region. The model was established using data of field. Results showed that the model are acceptable to model biological yield and water productivity. Therefore, the prediction model can be recommended for of silage maize. It is also recommended to validate the model using data from different seasons and irrigation conditions, as water availability can greatly impact crop growth, particularly under deficit irrigation. This will ensure the model’s accuracy and applicability in various environmental and water management scenarios.
