Risk factor analysis and surgical outcomes in emergency gastrectomy for gastric perforation: a single-center retrospective cohort study
Authors: Min Hong Lee, Yoon Mo Kim, Mi Kyung Oh, Hyun Il Kim, Min Gyu Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeGastric perforation remains a life-threatening condition despite advances in medical therapies and surgical techniques. In clinical emergencies involving gastric perforation, distinguishing between benign and malignant etiologies remains uncertain until histological confirmation is obtained.MethodsThis study analyzed 79 patients who underwent emergency gastrectomy for gastric perforation between 2010 and 2024. Patients were categorized into ulcer and cancer groups based on pathological findings. Clinical data, including preoperative conditions and postoperative outcomes, were analyzed using univariate and multivariate analyses.ResultsElderly (aged ≥75 years), symptom duration ≥48 hours, and American Society of Anesthesiologists physical status classification IV-E were significant risk factors for postoperative mortality. The cancer group had longer operation times, while ventilator use was more common in the ulcer group. No significant differences were observed in overall mortality rates.ConclusionEmergency gastrectomy can be a viable treatment for gastric perforation in regions with a high incidence of gastric cancer. However, patients with advanced age, delayed symptom onset, or poor physical status require careful surgical planning. Tailored strategies may improve outcomes, particularly in high-incidence regions for gastric cancer.Keywords: Gastrectomy, Mortality, Stomach neoplasms, Stomach perforation, Stomach ulcer

INTRODUCTION

Peptic ulcer disease has become significantly less common due to advancements in medical treatment, such as the eradication of Helicobacter pylori and widespread use of proton pump inhibitors. However, when perforation occurs, it still represents a surgical emergency with high mortality, often ranging between 4% and 25% depending on clinical presentation and timing of intervention [1,2,3,4,5]. While gastric ulcer perforations are less common than duodenal ulcer perforations, they present distinct challenges, particularly due to their potential association with gastric malignancies. Despite advancements in both surgical and non-surgical treatment modalities, the mortality rate for gastric perforations remains notably high (20.8%–25.0%) [1,2,3]. Systematic and standardized treatment protocols for gastric ulcer perforations are also less well-established, reflecting the complexity and variability in their clinical management.

Both benign ulcers and gastric malignancies have been implicated as underlying causes, with cancer reportedly accounting for approximately 6%–16% of cases [6,7]. Prior investigations, including our study, have emphasized that clinical features and imaging results often fail to clearly differentiate ulcerative from cancer-related perforations [8,9]. As such, surgeons frequently face intraoperative uncertainty regarding both diagnosis and the extent of resection required.

In our previous study [8], we proposed that in regions with high gastric cancer prevalence, emergency gastrectomy performed by experienced gastric cancer surgeons may be a viable and effective treatment option. This study expands upon prior observations by identifying risk factors associated with postoperative outcomes following gastrectomy for gastric perforation. A detailed evaluation of clinical, surgical, and pathological data is conducted to support individualized surgical planning in emergency settings.

METHODS

Ethics statement

This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board of Hanyang University Guri Hospital, affiliated with Hanyang University College of Medicine (IRB No. 2025-02-012). It was performed in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and written informed consent was obtained from all subjects at the time of enrollment.

Patient selection and study design

This retrospective study included a total of 79 patients who underwent emergency gastrectomy for gastric perforation at Hanyang University Guri Hospital between June 2010 and June 2024. All procedures were carried out by a single surgeon with extensive experience in managing gastric cancer and ulcer-related conditions. Prior to surgery, all patients received contrast-enhanced abdominal CT scans, which were interpreted by board-certified radiologists to assess the presence and characteristics of gastric perforation.

At our institution, primary repair was not employed as a treatment option in cases of gastric perforation due to the risk of an underlying malignancy. Patients who had undergone primary repair at an outside hospital and were referred for further surgical intervention after histologic diagnosis of gastric cancer were excluded from the study. Only patients who underwent curative-intent gastrectomy at our center were included. Cases involving perforation from trauma, metastatic lesions, or hematologic malignancies such as lymphoma were also excluded (Fig. 1).

Definitions of clinical variables

Shock was defined as hypotension at the time of emergency room presentation (systolic blood pressure <90 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure <60 mmHg) requiring sustained vasopressor support.

Acute kidney injury (AKI) was defined as either a rise in serum creatinine by ≥0.3 mg/dL or ≥50% within 48 hours, or urine output <0.5 mL/kg/hour for more than 6 hours [10]. Patients with preexisting chronic kidney disease were not included in the AKI group.

Admission to the intensive care unit (ICU) postoperatively was determined collaboratively by the attending surgeon and anesthesiologist, with all ICU care provided by board-certified intensivists.

Laparoscopic surgical approach

The surgeon performing these procedures possessed substantial expertise in laparoscopic management of generalized peritonitis, including emergent operations for perforations and necrotic conditions involving the stomach, small bowel, colon, and rectum [8,11,12,13].

Laparoscopic gastrectomy was attempted only when hemodynamic stability could be confirmed, typically after initial resuscitation. In such cases, intraoperative anesthesiology consultation was required before proceeding.

Patients with persistent hemodynamic instability were not considered candidates for laparoscopy and instead underwent open gastrectomy.

Clinical data and parameters

Clinical variables collected from medical records included age, sex, body mass index, the use of NSAIDs, history of prior major abdominal surgery, American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) physical status (PS) classification, duration from symptom onset to surgical intervention, and mental status at presentation.

Preoperative laboratory findings such as serum albumin and hemoglobin levels were recorded; hypoalbuminemia was defined as an albumin concentration below 3.5 g/dL. The ASA PS classification was determined by the anesthesiologist immediately prior to surgery.

Surgical outcomes

Primary surgical outcomes assessed included operative time, presence of postoperative complications, mortality within 30 days of surgery, and length of hospital stay. Postoperative complications were classified according to the Clavien-Dindo grading system [14]. Multi-organ failure resulting from septic shock was considered a major postoperative complication. Mortality was defined as any death occurring within 30 days following the surgical procedure, regardless of etiology.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 21.0 (IBM Corp.). Results are presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test, while the Student t-test was applied for continuous data. Multivariate analysis was performed using logistic regression to identify independent predictors of adverse outcomes. Variables with a P-value <0.1 in univariate analysis were included in the multivariate model. Relative risks (RR) and 95% confidence intervals were reported for all significant variables. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

RESULTS

Preoperative characteristics

Analysis of patient characteristics revealed a statistically significant difference in the use of NSAIDs, with higher usage in the ulcer group compared to the cancer group (34.9% vs. 8.3%, P = 0.004). Surgical outcomes according to pathology

A comparison of intraoperative and early postoperative outcomes showed that patients diagnosed with gastric cancer had significantly longer mean operative times than those with ulcer-related perforations (150.8 minutes vs. 125.3 minutes, P = 0.025). In contrast, the need for postoperative ventilator support was notably higher in the ulcer group (37.2% vs. 11.1%, P = 0.006). Additionally, patients with cancer experienced longer hospital stays following surgery (18.8 days vs. 12.8 days, P = 0.012) (Table 2).

Univariate analyses of prognostic factors

Univariate analysis identified several variables associated with increased rates of postoperative complications. These included advanced age (≥75 years, P = 0.016), female sex (P = 0.008), delayed presentation (symptoms >48 hours before admission, P = 0.001), presence of septic shock (P = 0.004), ASA PS classification of IV-E or higher (P = 0.010), and presence of preoperative AKI (P = 0.023).

When examining mortality, similar factors showed statistical significance. Mortality was higher among older patients (≥75 years, P < 0.001), females (P = 0.015), those presenting more than 48 hours after symptom onset (P = 0.005), patients in septic shock (P < 0.001), patients with ASA PS classification ≥ IV-E (P < 0.010), and individuals with hypoalbuminemia (albumin <3.5 g/dL, P = 0.001) (Table 3).

Multivariate analysis of risk factors

In the multivariate model, three factors were independently associated with increased risk for both postoperative complications and mortality. Patients aged 75 years or older had an RR of 9.924 for complications (P = 0.013). Symptom onset exceeding 48 hours was associated with RRs of 5.834 for complications and 4.583 for mortality (both P < 0.05). Similarly, an ASA PS classification of IV-E showed strong predictive value, with RRs of 6.036 for complications and 12.987 for mortality (both P < 0.05) (Table 4).

Mortality case characteristics

Among the 14 patients who died following gastrectomy for gastric perforation, most were elderly (aged ranging from 64 to 92 years) and had significant comorbid conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, dementia, or lung cancer. The majority had experienced symptom onset more than two days prior to admission and presented with ASA PS classifications of III-E or IV-E. Many also exhibited signs of septic shock at the time of hospital arrival. Surgical procedures included both laparoscopic and open distal or total gastrectomies, with final pathological staging ranging from IIIA to IV. The most common causes of death were complications arising from preoperative shock, respiratory failure, cardiac arrest, and multi-organ dysfunction (Table 5).

DISCUSSION

Gastric perforation represents a life-threatening emergency that demands immediate surgical intervention. Although surgical techniques and perioperative care have significantly improved, there remains no universally accepted guideline for optimal surgical management [1,8,15,16,17,18,19,20,21,22]. One of the principal challenges in managing this condition is the difficulty in distinguishing malignant from benign causes preoperatively—an issue particularly relevant in regions with high gastric cancer prevalence [8,9]. Moreover, while perforated duodenal ulcers have been extensively studied, research on gastric perforation remains limited, and much of the existing literature is outdated. This highlights the necessity for more contemporary data to guide clinical decision-making and refine treatment protocols.

The choice of surgical techniques in gastric perforation must be guided by the patient’s clinical condition and the underlying etiology. Surgical strategies can vary from simple suture repair to oncologically appropriate gastrectomy with lymph node dissection. Among these, determining the cause of perforation—benign or malignant—remains a key factor in deciding the extent and nature of surgery.

Epidemiological trends in gastric cancer differ widely across the globe and are shaped by factors such as geography, genetics, environmental exposure, and socioeconomic status [23,24,25]. As a result, the proportion of gastric cancer-related perforations also varies regionally [6,7,8,21]. These epidemiological differences must be taken into account when tailoring surgical strategies for gastric perforation.

Primary repair is commonly favored in patients with poor overall health or diffuse peritonitis, as it imposes a lower physiological burden. However, failure of initial repair can lead to worsening of intra-abdominal infection, deterioration of systemic status, and increased surgical complexity upon reoperation. Thus, the trade-off between operative risk and long-term oncologic safety must be carefully weighed.

Our previous work and current findings both confirm the ongoing difficulty in differentiating ulcer perforation from cancer, even with advanced imaging and intraoperative assessment [8]. In areas with high gastric cancer incidence, curative gastrectomy should be considered early when feasible. We encountered several patients initially treated with primary repair who were later diagnosed with gastric cancer. In these cases, delayed radical gastrectomy proved to be surgically demanding due to adhesions from prior inflammation and peritonitis, and was often complicated by technical challenges such as difficult lymph node dissection and duodenal stump management. Notably, there is a lack of research examining outcomes in patients who underwent secondary curative surgery after initial simple repair for malignant perforation.

The present study provides valuable clinical insights regarding emergency gastrectomy for gastric perforation. Our analysis showed that operative time was significantly shorter in the ulcer group compared to the cancer group, likely due to the easier processes of lymphadenectomy in the former. However, postoperative ventilator dependence was more common among patients with benign perforations, potentially reflecting more severe systemic inflammation. Importantly, there were no significant differences in rates of postoperative complications or mortality between the two groups, supporting the feasibility of gastrectomy in selected cancer patients presenting with perforation.

Furthermore, our findings identified advanced age (≥75 years), delayed presentation (>48 hours), and an ASA classification of IV-E as independent predictors of poor postoperative outcomes. These high-risk patients may benefit from expedited surgery and intensified perioperative monitoring and support. Many non-survivors had experienced preoperative shock, which often progressed to multi-organ failure despite prompt surgical treatment—underscoring the importance of aggressive early management in this subgroup.

This study’s retrospective design limits causal inference, and selection bias may exist as all surgeries were performed by a single surgeon. Multicenter prospective studies are needed to validate and generalize the findings. Another limitation is the lack of a comparison group with primary repair or conservative treatment, as our institution does not perform these procedures. Future multicenter studies are needed to address this gap. The absence of long-term follow-up, particularly in the gastric ulcer group, limits the ability to assess long-term outcomes such as survival. Future studies with structured follow-up are needed to address this gap. Sex and comorbidities were significant in univariate analysis but not in multivariate analysis, likely due to confounding. Larger future studies may enable meaningful subgroup analysis. Nevertheless, we believe the results offer meaningful guidance in managing this complex surgical emergency.

Based on the findings of our study, emergency gastrectomy with curative intent may be considered a primary surgical option for gastric perforation in regions with a high incidence of gastric cancer. However, our multivariate analysis identified several significant risk factors—such as advanced age, delayed presentation, and high ASA PS classification—that were closely associated with adverse short-term surgical outcomes.

Accordingly, in patients exhibiting these high-risk characteristics, surgical approaches should be selected with greater caution. In such cases, primary repair may serve as a safer alternative by reducing the physiological burden and facilitating improved short-term postoperative recovery. Ultimately, individualized surgical planning based on each patient’s clinical status may contribute to better outcomes in this complex and heterogeneous patient population.
