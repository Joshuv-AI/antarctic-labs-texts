Cardiovascular risk factors and serious health events in individuals with eating disorders: a systematic review
Authors: Kate E Murphy, Morgan J Sidari, Thomas A Skerlj, Kirsten L McMahon, Stephen Parker, Chloe Pearson, Jana Y Waldmann, Elizabeth Eggins
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis systematic review aimed to determine how cardiovascular risk factors are associated with serious adverse health events in individuals with eating disorders, aiming to inform clinical decision-making about hospital admission and monitoring.Main bodyWe searched 15 multidisciplinary databases from inception to October 2025 following PRISMA guidelines. Eligible studies needed to compare outcomes between individuals with eating disorders who exhibited specific cardiovascular parameters (e.g., resting heart rate, blood pressure, QTc prolongation) and those who did not. Eligible outcomes were serious adverse health events, including death, cardiac arrest, malignant arrhythmias and/or cardiac failure. Of 14,034 de-duplicated records, six studies met inclusion criteria, all focusing exclusively on anorexia nervosa (AN). Only two provided high-confidence evidence. Hypotension at admission was associated with 8.65-fold increased likelihood of in-hospital mortality (95% CI 5.45, 13.7). Composite cardiac complications predicted 3.29-fold increased odds of mortality (95% CI 1.27, 8.55). The evidence for QTc prolongation was insufficient and for the presence of bradycardia was low confidence. Postural hypotension, postural tachycardia, resting tachycardia, and heart rate variability were not examined in any eligible study.ConclusionLimited evidence exists for the predictive validity of most cardiovascular parameters for serious adverse health events in eating disorders. Hypotension emerged as the clearest predictor; however, the absence of evidence should not be interpreted as evidence of absence. Current clinical guidelines appropriately adopt a cautious approach given this evidence gap. Large-scale, multi-site cohort studies are needed to establish which cardiovascular parameters predict imminent risk and to examine eating disorder diagnoses beyond AN.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40337-026-01691-x.Keywords: Eating disorders, Anorexia nervosa, Cardiovascular risk factors, Mortality, Cardiac arrest, Systematic review, Predictive validity, Hypotension, Bradycardia, Hospital admission criteria

Background

Eating disorders (EDs) are serious, complex mental illnesses characterised by persistent disturbances in eating behaviour, cognition, and related behaviours that substantially impair physical health, psychosocial functioning, and quality of life [1]. Mortality risk is markedly elevated across ED diagnoses, with a recent meta-analysis reporting the highest standardised mortality ratio (SMR) in anorexia nervosa (AN) (SMR = 5.21), followed by eating disorder not otherwise specified (SMR = 2.51), bulimia nervosa (SMR = 2.20), and binge eating disorder (SMR = 1.46) [2]. Standardised mortality ratio (SMR) refers to the ratio of observed deaths compared with expected deaths within the general population. Suicide represents a major contributor to premature mortality in EDs accounting for 20% of deaths in those diagnosed with AN [3]. While suicide remains a serious cause of mortality risk in these individuals, physical health complications significantly contribute to the multifactorial mortality burden associated with eating disorders.

The impact on physical health sequelae and death in individuals with EDs often is multi-system and may include pulmonary, endocrine, hepatic, gastrointestinal, and cardiovascular disease [1, 3]. Cardiovascular complications are frequently suspected in cases of sudden clinical deterioration or death,however, objective data clarifying the underlying cardiac mechanisms are often limited [4]. This uncertainty is further compounded by gaps in clinician confidence regarding the identification and management of associated medical complications, as well as the absence of international consensus regarding thresholds for escalation to inpatient medical or psychiatric care [4–6].

The broad range of cardiovascular complications is a consequence of the heterogeneous nature of EDs particularly with respect to dietary intake, nutritional status, and compensatory behaviours. Cardiovascular impairment is thought to develop from a complex interplay of malnutrition, electrolyte imbalance, structural cardiac alterations, and autonomic nervous system dysfunction [7]. Cardiovascular abnormalities in individuals with AN is well-described which include sinus bradycardia, hypotension or hypertension, conduction disturbances, dysregulation of peripheral vascular tone, and structural cardiac changes. Pericardial effusions have also been well documented with positive findings in approximately 20–25% of individuals undergoing cardiac imaging and are frequently clinically silent, often resolving with nutritional rehabilitation [7, 8]. In contrast, individuals with bulimia nervosa are more likely to experience electrolyte disturbances—particularly hypokalaemia secondary to purging behaviours such as vomiting, laxative or diuretic misuse. Electrolyte disturbances increase the risk of cardiac arrhythmias, QT interval prolongation, and conduction abnormalities, and may precipitate acute cardiac events [7, 9].

Longitudinal cohort studies have suggested increased long-term risk of ischemic heart disease and conduction disorders in individuals with bulimia nervosa compared with population controls [10]. Chronic metabolic processes contributing to long-term cardiovascular risk, morbidity and mortality have been linked to both bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorders, with binge eating disorder strongly associated with dyslipidaemia, hypertension, insulin resistance, and other features of metabolic syndrome [11]. Cardiovascular abnormalities may be structural or functional and can be either symptomatic or asymptomatic, however, the challenge for clinicians is that these do not necessarily correlate with eating disorder severity or duration, further contributing to the complexity of assessing cardiovascular risk in these individuals [12].

The sensitivity and reliability of clinical history and examination to inform a cardiovascular risk assessment in individuals with EDs is difficult for both junior and experienced clinicians. Clinical examination frequently identifies bradycardia, hypotension, prolonged QTc interval, postural instability in heart rate and blood pressure, as well as altered heart rate variability, often accompanied by reported symptoms including dizziness, presyncope, chest pain, dyspnoea, and palpitations [7, 13]. However, the examination can vary markedly from one person to the next due to the well-recognised phenomenon that those individuals with EDs may appear medically stable despite prolonged periods of malnutrition, may under-report symptoms or have difficulty recognising symptoms related to cardiovascular risk [14]. As a result, reliance on clinical history and examination alone may be insufficient for accurate risk stratification.

Despite the established association between EDs and cardiovascular change, the prognostic significance of these abnormalities remains uncertain. Although rates of serious adverse health events and mortality are clearly elevated in individuals with EDs, objective evidence identifying reliable cardiovascular predictors of rare but severe health outcomes, such as death, cardiac arrest, myocardial infarction, or malignant arrhythmias, remains limited and is largely confined to case reports and small observational studies [15, 16]. To our knowledge, no systematic review has specifically examined cardiovascular parameters as predictors of these outcomes. Consequently, although international clinical guidelines frequently recommend hospital admission for individuals with EDs who exhibit cardiovascular abnormalities to facilitate monitoring and assessment, the predictive value of such abnormalities for mortality or other serious adverse health events remains unclear [17].

Predicting rare but severe cardiovascular events in individuals with EDs presents with other unique clinical and non-clinical challenges. Although such events occur infrequently, they often affect relatively young individuals, for whom sudden death or cardiac arrest is unexpected, with longitudinal data identifying that a substantial proportion of death occurs within the first decade of diagnosis, with elevated risk across young and mid-adult years [18]. The uncertainty of cardiovascular predictors that can assist with prognostication, as well as limited available literature to guide clinical decision making is likely to further contribute to the burden of clinicians, affected individuals and their family or support persons. Premature deaths often attract increased clinical and medico-legal scrutiny, including coronial investigation as well as the broader and profound impacts on families and support networks of the specific affected individuals.

In parallel, lived experience research indicates that individuals with EDs often describe encounters with acute medical services, including emergency department presentations and hospital admissions, as highly stressful and difficult to navigate [19]. Qualitative syntheses highlight challenges related to uncertainty, inadequate communication, and fragmented care pathways, particularly at points of escalation or transition between services [20]. From this perspective, clearer and more consistent clinical guidance may not only support clinician decision-making but also assist individuals with EDs and their primary care providers to better anticipate, understand, and navigate medical care. As such it is expected that improved guidance would assist clinicians to undertake transparent discussions regarding individualised cardiovascular risk, their anticipated monitoring, and the rationale for admission or discharge decisions.

Given that Global Burden of Disease (GBD) data found that prevalence rates of eating disorders increased worldwide between 1990 and 2021 [21] clinicians working in emergency, medical, and psychiatric settings are increasingly required to balance concerns about potential cardiovascular risk against finite inpatient resources. This systematic review therefore aims to determine the extent to which cardiovascular risk factors are associated with serious adverse health outcomes in individuals with eating disorders, with the intention of informing emerging clinical guidance and supporting clinicians to provide safer, more consistent decision-making in acute medical settings and during transitions of care.

Method

The protocol for this review was prospectively registered in PROSPERO (Registration Number: CRD42024583403) and was reported in line with PRISMA guidelines [22] and conducted in line with other relevant guidelines produced by the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI [23, 24] and Cochrane Collaboration [25], and Campbell Collaboration [26]).

Criteria for considering studies for the review

To be eligible for inclusion in the review, a study needed to empirically examine the association between cardiovascular risk factors and serious adverse health events in participants aged ≥ 16 years who have either a diagnosis of an eating disorder or elevated symptoms of an eating disorder via a validated measure of disordered eating. The age threshold of ≥ 16 years was selected because the review aimed to inform adult inpatient guideline development, and notes that cardiovascular physiology and treatment pathways differ substantially in younger paediatric populations. Eligible eating disorders or symptoms were those that are covered under the Feeding and Eating Disorders category within the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM-5-TR) [27], excluding Pica. Cardiovascular parameters included resting heart rate, resting blood pressure and heart rate variability, postural heart rate changes, postural blood pressure changes and any arrhythmia as detected by an electrocardiograph (e.g. QTc prolongation). Serious adverse health events included any of the following: death, cardiac arrest, and cardiac or heart failure. Outcomes could be in the form of official data from health records (in-hospital or post-discharge) or in the form of self-report data (e.g., surveys, interviews).

Eligible study designs needed to be cross-sectional, preferably longitudinal, whereby individuals with an eating disorder and/or elevated eating disorder symptomatology with the specified cardiovascular parameters were compared to individuals with an eating disorder and/or elevated eating disorder symptomatology who did not have the cardiovascular parameters or who have different levels of exposure. For longitudinal studies, the risk factor must precede the outcome. Both bivariate and multiple variable studies were included to explore direct and unique associations whilst controlling for covariates. We purposely used broad inclusion criteria to produce a comprehensive field wide review that synthesises the state of the evidence regarding how cardiac risk factors relate to adverse outcomes in this population.

Search methods for identification of studies

The systematic search covered 15 multidisciplinary databases and was initially run from inception until September 2024 (see Supplementary 1 for the complete search strategy) and then updated in September–October 2025. Databases captured both published peer-reviewed literature and unpublished grey literature. In addition, the reference lists of eligible studies were harvested to identify any studies that may not have been indexed in academic databases or identified in the search. This additional search step was not prespecified in the protocol and did not identify any eligible studies beyond what had already been captured by the systematic search.

Data collection and analysis

The de-duplicated results of the search (JW, EE) were imported into AI-enhanced review software—DistillerSR [28]—for study selection. At the title and abstract screening stage, review authors (KM, MS, EE, TS, KMc) assessed whether the citation and associated abstract related to cardiac issues in the context of consumers with eating disorders/disordered eating (broadly defined). Two screeners were required to exclude a record, whereas only one screener was required to include a record for full-text screening. Any discrepancies were mediated by a third review author or via discussion among the author team. This mode of screening was used to inform the Large Language Model (LLM) AI-enhanced features of DistillerSR, which provides a prediction of the likelihood that a citation would be included for closer consideration at the next screening stage (i.e., full-text screening). Drawing on other high-quality systematic reviews (e.g., [29, 30]), we ceased human screening ceased when DistillerSR identified that 95% of potential studies had been screened, or where the likelihood of inclusion was ≤ 5%. A single review author (EE) then screened batches of 50 AI-ranked and randomly drawn records until no new citations were considered worthy of closer examination on full-text.

Records not excluded at the title and abstract screening stage progressed to full-text screening. All efforts were made to source a full-text version of each potentially eligible study, including ordering through institutional libraries. If the full-text document could not be sourced (e.g., conference abstracts), the title and abstract were screened using the full-text eligibility criteria. Records that could not be unequivocally excluded are listed in the ‘Studies awaiting classification’ reference list (see Supplementary 2). Studies awaiting classification refers to records for which insufficient information was available to conclusively determine eligibility despite attempts to source full-text data and as such these studies were not included in synthesis or effect size calculations. All documents were screened by two authors (KM, MS, TS, KMc, SP), with disagreements mediated by a third author (EE) or by discussion among the author team. Documents were screened to verify that they met all inclusion criteria (i.e., participants, exposures/risk factors, outcomes, design). Multiple or secondary reports of the same study were joined in DistillerSR and represented a single study in any subsequent syntheses. All study reports were used for data extraction.

Included studies were independently coded by two review authors (EE, SP) to extract study characteristics, outcome data, and complete risk of bias assessments. Study characteristics included: document type, geographical location of the study, sample size, sociodemographic and psychiatric characteristics of study participants, characteristics and measurement of cardiac predictors, and outcome data (including type and measurement). Any disagreements were mediated via discussion among the author team. The Risk of Bias in Non-Randomized Studies of Exposure (ROBINS-E), was used to rate the risk of bias for included studies. As per the tool guidance, full risk of bias ratings were conducted only if studies made attempts to control for a list of prespecified confounding factors. For the purposes of this review, we specified these confounding factors to be: Age, sex, family history of cardiac disease, premorbid history of cardiac disease or malformations, substance misuse, and prescribed medications. We calculated odds ratios (and associated 95% Confidence Intervals (CI)) to represent the size of the association between cardiovascular risk factors and serious adverse health events. Where data from an included study were not in a dichotomous format, we calculated the appropriate effect size for the data (e.g., SMD) and then converted the effect size so that all effect sizes used the same metric [31]. We encountered no dependency or unit-of-analysis issues that would warrant additional methodological considerations for synthesis (e.g., multiple conceptually similar outcomes for one study, multiple follow-ups, clustering). If data required for effect size calculation were not reported in an included study, the corresponding author of the study was contacted for the required data. Where the data could not be provided, the study was excluded from effect size calculation and/or planned meta-analyses but still included in the review. The registered review protocol specified the planned meta-analytic approach and associated analyses (e.g., assessing and exploring heterogeneity, subgroup analyses, assessing reporting bias),however, the nature of the included studies precluded this approach. Rather, each study is represented with a single standardised effect size and its 95% CIs, data permitting. Overall, the results are predominantly synthesised using the SWiM guidelines [32].

Use of artificial intelligence

Aside from the AI-assisted screening software as described above, LLMs were used for rewording, assessing and improving clarity for multidisciplinary readership, and other simple tasks (e.g., producing the abbreviation list) for the manuscript. All manuscript content produced with LLM assistance was verified by the authors.

Results

The systematic search identified 21,015 records which included a mixture of retrospective analyses, observational cohort studies and longitudinal follow up studies. The de-duplicated results (n = 14,034) were imported into AI-enhanced review software—DistillerSR—for screening. At title and abstract screening stage, 12,637 citation records were excluded because they did not relate to cardiac issues in the context of eating disordered consumers (broadly defined). A total of 1,397 citation records were considered potentially eligible and progressed to full-text screening (see Fig. 1).1 While there were many studies that established the heightened risk of death and presence of cardiac complications in individuals with eating disorders compared to the general population, only 6 studies directly compared the presence versus absence (or varied degree of risk factors) in individuals with eating disorders and then compared the rates of adverse events. These studies could not be combined with meta-analysis due to differing cardiac predictors and outcomes. The full list of excluded studies can be provided on request.

Study 1 by Frederiksen et al. [33] examined the impact of borderline prolonged QTc intervals (> 440 ms) versus prolonged QTc intervals (> 460 ms) in Danish individuals with AN, using occurrence of ventricular tachycardia, aborted cardiac arrest, cardiac arrest, and/or all-cause mortality as an outcome. Of the 430 individuals with AN at baseline, 5 had prolonged QTc intervals (1%). At follow-up (M = 10.1 years later, n = 560), there were 13 deaths in the sample, though none were related to cardiac or organ failure (e.g., they died from cancer, poisoning, overdose, traffic accident).2 Of the 430 individuals with AN and then had ECG data, there were 29 with a borderline prolonged QTc interval, with no adverse events in any of these cases. A total of 4 individuals had an adverse event (cardiac (incl. aborted) or ventricular tachycardia). Two of these individuals had normal QTc intervals, and two had missing ECG data. This means that this study does not provide sufficient data to calculate an effect size. In other words, there is insufficient data to calculate whether there is heightened risk of death or cardiac arrest when individuals with AN have prolonged QTc intervals.

Study 2 by Facchini et al. [34] similarly examined whether the risk of ventricular arrhythmia was higher for individuals with AN (n = 3) if they had prolonged QT intervals. One of the three individuals had a ventricular arrhythmia, and this individual had prolonged QTc intervals. Combining the data into a standardized effect size indicates that the odds of a ventricular arrhythmia for individuals with prolonged QTc intervals is no greater than without prolonged QTc intervals (OR = 3; 95% CI 0.0595, 151.1904). However, this effect size is based on a very small sample size and should be interpreted with caution.

Study 3 by Edakubo et al. [35] examined whether the risk of in-hospital mortality was higher for individuals with AN (n = 6937) if they had indicators of hypotension or bradycardia at admission. Bradycardia measured by use of Atropine in first 48 h of admission. Hypotension measured by use of Catecholamine in the first 48 h of admission. There were 195 in-hospital deaths in the sample (2.8%). The authors found that individuals with hypotension were 8.65 times more likely to die in hospital compared to those without hypotension (95% CI 5.45, 13.7), based on a multivariate logistic regression model. However, individuals with bradycardia were not more likely to die in hospital compared to those without bradycardia (OR = 0.27, 95% CI 0.03–2.12).

Study 4 by Yahalom et al. [36] examined whether the risk of ventricular arrhythmia was higher for individuals with EDs (n = 23; 12 eligible based on age for this review) if they had bradycardia (HR < 50 bpm) or prolonged QTc intervals. Three individuals had ventricular arrhythmic activity (ranging from 1 to “few”, not otherwise specified). QTc intervals were within the normal range for all individuals, so an effect size could not be calculated. However, the odds of experiencing ventricular arrhythmic activity were no different for those with or without bradycardia (OR = 0.25; 95% CI 0.02, 4.0). Again, this effect size is based on a very small sample size and should be interpreted with caution.

Study 5 by Guinhut et al. [37] examined whether the risk of mortality in individuals with AN (n = 384) differed depending on the presence of ‘cardiac complications’. The authors defined this as “cardiac rhythm disorders (bradycardia < 40/min, tachycardia, cardiac repolarization abnormalities), cardiac failure (acute pulmonary oedema and/or left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) ≤ 50%), elevated troponin without myocardial infarction, pericardial effusion)”. Based on a multivariate cox regression modelling, the authors found that the risk of mortality was 3 times higher in the presence of cardiac complications (HR = 3.29; 95% CI 1.27, 8.55). Greater confidence can be placed in this study given the large sample size and the robust analysis used by the authors to statistically control for possible confounding factors.

Study 6 by Okabe et al. [38] examined whether the risk of death was higher in individuals with AN (n = 20) depending on the presence of bradycardia or prolonged QTc intervals. One individual died in their sample. The odds of death were no different within individuals with AN whether or not they presented with bradycardia (OR = 6.2308, 95% CI 0.222, 174.8768) or prolonged QTc intervals (OR = 7.4, 95% CI 0.1183, 463.0854). Again, this effect size is based on a very small sample size and should be interpreted with caution.

Discussion

The aim of this systematic review was to identify cardiac predictors and risk factors associated with serious health adverse outcomes and mortality in individuals diagnosed with eating disorders. Following a comprehensive systematic review only six studies met the inclusion criteria. The included studies evaluated only a subset of the cardiovascular parameters of interest, namely prolonged QTc intervals, hypotension, and bradycardia, despite the broad range of cardiovascular abnormalities described in eating disorders. Significant heterogeneity was present across the six studies regarding both the types of cardiovascular risk factors assessed and the measurement and definition of parameters. As a result, the methodological and definitional variability across studies precluded meaningful meta-analysis. The risk of bias was considered high for all studies, which reduces the confidence in the findings. A particular limitation was small sample sizes and lack of control for important confounding factors.

One rigorous study examined in-hospital mortality among individuals with AN and identified hypotension as being significantly associated with mortality [35]. However, there are limitations in the reporting of objective data, including no defined value ranges or analysis of hypotension severity and mortality risk, which may have implications for clinical applicability. The presence of bradycardia was also assessed in this study but was not associated with increased risk of all-cause inpatient mortality. Additionally, the study reported use of catecholamines within the first 48 h, which was linked with increased mortality. While this is not a direct cardiac predictor, it may suggest severity of cardiovascular instability which may be clinically relevant [35]. Due to the absence of detailed clinical data, there is insufficient evidence available to consider whether these results were secondary to psychiatric illness, acute insult, or pre-existing medical comorbidities.

The second more rigorous study examined longer-term mortality in a prospective cohort of individuals with AN and identified a composite measure of cardiac complications as being significantly associated with mortality (Guinhut et al. [37]). The composite measure included bradycardia below 40 beats per minute, tachycardia, cardiac repolarization abnormalities, cardiac failure, elevated troponin without myocardial infarction, and pericardial effusion. The study found that individuals with composite cardiac complications had a 3.29-fold increased risk of mortality; however, the use of a composite measure presents limitations for clinical applicability, as it is not possible to determine which specific cardiac parameters within the composite are independently predictive of mortality, or whether certain combinations of abnormalities carry particularly high risk.

The remaining four studies provided low-confidence evidence due to very small sample sizes and availability of effect size data. Three of the studies examined QTc interval prolongation in relation to serious adverse events. Frederiksen et al. [33] followed 430 individuals with AN over a mean of 10.1 years and identified 13 deaths, though none were cardiac-related, and insufficient data were available to calculate the predictive value of QTc prolongation. Facchini et al. [34] and Okabe et al. [38] examined only 3 and 20 individuals respectively, yielding odds ratios with extremely wide confidence intervals that preclude meaningful interpretation. Yahalom et al. [36] examined bradycardia in 23 individuals with AN and found no significant association with ventricular arrhythmic activity.

No applicable studies examined the remaining cardiac risk factors of interest, namely postural hypotension, postural tachycardia, resting tachycardia, or heart rate variability (HRV). Taken together, results suggest there is very limited evidence to support (or, importantly, to dispute) the assertion that cardiovascular risk factors indicate imminent risk of cardiac arrest or mortality, cardiovascular or otherwise.

Limitations of evidence included in the review

The criteria for inclusion in this systematic review were strict, in line with its goals. The evidence for elevated cardiovascular risk factors in people with eating disorders is well-established, and the evidence for elevated risk of death is also well-established; however, the validity of cardiovascular risk factors as predictors of increased mortality cannot be assessed through studies that address either topic in isolation. To address this, studies comparing the outcomes of individuals with eating disorders with and without the cardiovascular risk factors of interest are required.

In line with the research goal, strict inclusion criteria were applied. This likely limited the number of studies included and the range of potential cardiovascular risks considered. The inclusion of only six studies significantly limits the ability to comprehensively describe and capture the varied nature of eating disorder symptomatology in relation to rare and severe cardiac events. Additionally, all included studies focused exclusively on AN populations, which limits the generalisability of the findings to other eating disorder diagnoses. The finite range of cardiac predictors assessed and analysed describes only a narrow subset of the well-documented medical complications in this group of eating disorder conditions.

Outcome measurement varied across studies, with some examining in-hospital mortality, others examining long-term all-cause mortality, and others examining intermediate outcomes such as ventricular arrhythmia. This heterogeneity prevented meta-analysis and limits the ability to synthesise findings across studies. Additionally, most studies assessed cardiovascular parameters at a single timepoint, typically at admission or baseline evaluation, and could not address whether changes over time or persistence of abnormalities carries different prognostic significance than transient abnormalities.

As all included studies focused on AN and hospital-based samples, there is limited generalisability to other eating disorder diagnoses, outpatient settings, and community populations. Although the review aimed to examine cardiovascular predictors across eating disorder populations broadly, all eligible studies focused exclusively on AN. This finding highlights a substantial evidence gap regarding prognostic cardiovascular risk in other eating disorder diagnoses, including bulimia nervosa, binge eating disorder, ARFID, and other specified feeding and eating disorders. Also, the differences in how cardiovascular measurements were defined and recorded (e.g., hypotension and bradycardia inferred via medication proxies; differing QTc correction formulae and thresholds) add further uncertainty. Furthermore, the small number of events in both studies prevents robust adjustment for confounders such as severity of malnutrition, comorbid medical illness, electrolyte disturbances, and medication use. These factors mean that the strength of inferences that can be drawn about causality or independent prognostic value is low.

Limitations of review processes

Several methodological safeguards were employed to minimise bias in the review process. The protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO prior to conducting searches or screening studies. The review followed PRISMA guidelines and additional guidance from the Joanna Briggs Institute, Cochrane Collaboration, and Campbell Collaboration. Dual independent screening was conducted at both the title and abstract stage and the full-text screening stage, with disagreements resolved through mediation by a third reviewer or discussion among the team.

As discussed, the purpose of conducting this systematic review was to inform the guidelines for inpatient admission criteria for eating disorders for adult hospitals in Queensland, Australia [39]. Three of the authors (KM, KMc, MS) work for Queensland Eating Disorders Service (QuEDS) and were involved in the process of updating these guidelines, in collaboration with other internal and external committee members. As team members of the statewide eating disorder service, these authors could be perceived to have a vested interest in securing hospital admissions for the population that their team assess and treat. To address this potential source of bias, these team members could not independently make inclusion/exclusion decisions. It could now be argued that finding insufficient evidence for most cardiovascular parameters does not serve the interests of securing hospital admissions.

Finally, this review used AI-assisted screening in DistillerSR. The AI-assisted screening was halted when the software indicated that 95% of eligible studies had been identified and the likelihood of the next highest-ranking record being eligible was less than 5%. This means that approximately 5% of potentially eligible studies may not have been screened by human reviewers. However, given that extensive human screening of over 1,300 full-text articles identified only six eligible studies, it is unlikely that the unscreened 5% would substantially alter the conclusions of the review. While the use of artificial intelligence (AI) is not uncommon in contemporary reviews, it has the potential to introduce errors in specific settings and uses. A recent study comparing conventional screening with AI-based methods found that both AI-only and traditional-only approaches have the potential for error, and that AI used as a complementary tool may benefit systematic review processes [40]. For this review, a hybrid traditional-AI model was used, with the intention of drawing on the benefits of both approaches and reducing possible error. This approach may be a relevant area for further research, with particular focus on its impact on sensitivity and specificity.

Implications for practice, policy, and future research

To the best of our knowledge, this review is the first to directly evaluate the evidence for cardiovascular risk factors’ predictive validity for cardiac arrest and/or mortality in eating disorder populations. The review supports the well-established consensus that individuals with EDs have an increased risk of mortality compared to the general population, as demonstrated in both earlier and more recent meta-analyses.

Existing literature confirms that cardiac complications are common in EDs, and narrative reviews highlight high rates of bradycardia, hypotension, QTc prolongation, and other abnormalities. However, the findings of this review suggest that, within the small number of prognostic studies available, hypotension and markers of cardiovascular instability have clearer associations with mortality than do bradycardia or QTc prolongation alone. This review found no evidence that QTc prolongation, in the contexts studied, predicts serious cardiac events or cardiac-specific mortality, even though QTc is widely used as a marker of arrhythmic risk in other medical populations [41, 42]. Our findings partly diverge from the assumptions underlying some clinical guidelines, which often treat prolonged QTc and marked bradycardia as key markers of imminent cardiac risk in ED populations. The results should not be interpreted as evidence that these markers are benign, but rather as an indication that current prognostic evidence is sparse and that serious outcomes may depend on broader clinical context (e.g., degree of malnutrition, electrolyte disturbances, and acute medical stressors). The absence of a clear association between bradycardia or QTc prolongation and serious cardiac events in the included studies suggests that these parameters, in isolation, may have limited prognostic value for predicting imminent mortality or cardiac arrest. Nonetheless, bradycardia and QTc prolongation remain important markers of physiological stress and illness severity and should be interpreted alongside nutritional status, electrolyte balance, medication use, and other clinical features.

The association between hypotension (and related proxies such as catecholamine use) and in-hospital mortality draws attention to the importance of recognising and promptly managing circulatory compromise in individuals with eating disorders. The results support existing guidance that persistent hypotension and overt cardiovascular instability should trigger urgent medical assessment and intervention.

Without an adequate understanding of which cardiovascular markers truly predict serious events, there is a risk of both over- and under-treatment of individuals with eating disorders. Over-treatment (e.g., unnecessary medical admissions based on minor or isolated abnormalities) may expose patients to iatrogenic harms and strain health-care systems. Under-treatment (e.g., failure to admit or monitor patients with significant medical instability) risks preventable morbidity and mortality. This review highlights the necessity to recognise and address several factors that are likely to benefit clinicians in the management of eating disorders. First, the importance of established and updated guideline documents to acknowledge the limited ED-specific prognostic evidence underpinning current cardiovascular thresholds. Second, to emphasise contextual, holistic risk assessment rather than reliance on single numerical cut-offs that also consider local health service culture, resources and expertise. Finally, to prioritise research to validate cardiovascular criteria for admission and monitoring.

These findings can be viewed alongside the context of the recent World Federation of Societies of Biological Psychiatry (WFSBP) consensus statement in recognising AN as involving significant biological metabolic and cardiovascular disturbance, including cardiac and neurophysiological parameters relevant to physical health risk [43]. However, our review extends this position by examining prognostic validity rather than biological association. In keeping with the WFSBP statement’s conclusion that evidence regarding sensitivity, specificity and prognostic value of biomarkers remains limited, we found very few studies directly assessing whether cardiovascular parameters predict serious health outcomes.

This review highlights an evidence gap for cardiac predictors and risk factors for severe cardiac adverse events in individuals with eating disorders. The paucity of evidence may reflect constraints on methodology and resources, ethical factors stemming from the sensitivity and nature of eating disorders, and our limited understanding of underlying pathophysiology. The rarity of significant cardiac outcomes is a major challenge for future research.

Large-scale, multi-centre, longitudinal and prospective studies are needed to obtain the objective data necessary to answer prognostic questions in this area. Future studies should systematically measure cardiovascular parameters (heart rate, blood pressure, orthostatic changes, QTc, arrhythmias) using standardised definitions and correction methods. They should collect detailed data on nutritional status, electrolyte disturbances, medications, and comorbidities. Studies should distinguish cardiac from non-cardiac causes of death and capture non-fatal serious cardiac events. Future research should cover different ED diagnoses, genders, age groups, and treatment settings. Development and validation of multivariable prognostic models that comprise cardiovascular parameters alongside other clinical and psychosocial risk factors could be a sensible next step to take.

Conclusions

Prior work has established that eating disorders are associated with high mortality and frequent cardiovascular abnormalities, but the prognostic significance of specific cardiovascular signs has remained unclear. This review identified only two high confidence studies directly examining the association between cardiovascular parameters and serious adverse events in eating disorder populations. Within this limited evidence base, hypotension and indicators of cardiovascular instability were associated with increased in-hospital mortality, whereas bradycardia and QTc prolongation alone were not clearly associated with cardiac arrest or mortality. Cardiovascular monitoring is still vital in the care of individuals with eating disorders, however, current cardiovascular thresholds for medical admission and monitoring are based on very limited eating disorder-specific prognostic evidence. There is an urgent call for rigorous, large-scale research to deepen our understanding of cardiovascular risk in eating disorders so that clinicians can better balance care based on safety, resource use, and consumer experience.
