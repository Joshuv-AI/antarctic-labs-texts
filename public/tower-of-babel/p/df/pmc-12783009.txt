Increased liver graft weight is associated with a high incidence of postreperfusion syndrome in living donor liver transplantation: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Sushmitha Dongari, Gaurav Sindwani, Udit Dhingra, Anil Yadav, Deepak Tempe, Viniyendra Pamecha, Nihar Mohapatra, Nilesh Sadashiv Patil
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe postreperfusion phase during liver transplantation is among the most challenging periods for anesthesiologists. In living donor liver transplantation (LDLT), graft size varies considerably and may influence postreperfusion hemodynamics. This study aimed to evaluate the impact of absolute liver graft weight on the incidence of postreperfusion syndrome (PRS) and subsequent hemodynamic recovery following graft reperfusion.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed data from 100 adult patients who underwent LDLT. The incidence of PRS and hemodynamic variables were compared in relation to graft weight.ResultsPRS occurred in 38% of patients. Multivariate analysis identified graft weight and Model for End-Stage Liver Disease–Na scores as significant predictors of PRS. A graft weight cutoff of 651 g best predicted PRS (area under the curve, 82.1%; 95% confidence interval [CI], 73.7%–90.5%). Patients were stratified into group A (≥651 g) and group B (<651 g). Surprisingly, group A had a significantly higher incidence of PRS but required less noradrenaline at intensive care unit transfer (0.20±0.07 vs. 0.28±0.17 µg/kg/min, P=0.003) and had a shorter duration of mechanical ventilation (748.15±322.37 vs. 1,156±1,020 minutes, P=0.007). Phenylephrine requirements during reperfusion were higher in group A (400 vs. 300 µg, P=0.01).ConclusionsAn absolute graft weight ≥651 g was associated with a higher incidence of PRS, but counterintuitively this was linked to faster hemodynamic stabilization and reduced mechanical ventilation time. These findings underscore the complex interplay between graft size and postreperfusion physiology in LDLT.Keywords: Graft, Hemodynamics, Living donors, Liver transplantation, Reperfusion injury

INTRODUCTION

Liver transplantation (LT) has become the gold standard treatment for patients with end-stage liver disease. However, LT is accompanied by significant hemodynamic instability, especially during the dissection and reperfusion phases. Postreperfusion syndrome (PRS) is a feared complication occurring during the transition from the anhepatic to the neohepatic phase, marked by the unclamping of the portal vein [1]. Aggarwal et al. [2] defined PRS as a decrease in mean arterial pressure (MAP) greater than 30% from baseline, lasting at least 1 minute within 5 minutes following graft reperfusion. Conversely, Hilmi et al. [3] broadened this definition to include even milder presentations of PRS. The reported incidence of PRS varies widely from 8% to 80%, with higher rates noted in cadaveric LT [4].

Multiple factors contribute to PRS occurrence. Donor-related factors such as advanced age and macrovesicular steatosis, as well as recipient-related factors including elevated Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) scores, hyperkalemia, hypocalcemia, prolonged warm and cold ischemia times, use of venovenous bypass, and classical surgical techniques, have all been associated with increased risk [5]. Notably, higher graft weights have been associated with more severe hemodynamic instability in deceased donor LT (DDLT), likely due to a greater release of inflammatory mediators from the larger graft volume [5].

In living donor LT (LDLT), graft weight is typically determined by the graft-to-recipient weight ratio (GRWR) and the donor’s hepatic anatomy. Generally, the goal is to achieve a GRWR around 0.8, resulting in graft weights ranging from 200 to 1,000 g in adult recipients. However, the specific impact of absolute graft weight on intraoperative hemodynamics and PRS in LDLT remains underexplored [6].

This study was inspired by clinical observations in our practice, where larger grafts often appeared to correlate with more significant hemodynamic disturbances immediately following reperfusion. These observations led us to hypothesize that absolute graft weight might influence PRS incidence and subsequent hemodynamic recovery. Therefore, the primary objective of this study was to investigate the association between absolute graft weight and the incidence of PRS in LDLT. Secondary objectives included: (1) evaluating differences in vasopressor requirements during reperfusion between high and low graft weight groups, and (2) comparing the duration of mechanical ventilation, intensive care unit (ICU) stay, hospital stay, and mortality between these groups.

METHODS

After obtaining approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee Board of the Institute of Liver and Biliary Sciences (IEC/2023/102/MA08) and the Clinical Trials Registry (CTRI/2023/10/058569) for retrospective record review and waiver of informed consent, medical records of patients who underwent elective LDLT between January 2022 and August 2023 were reviewed. Laboratory data from the perioperative period were retrieved electronically from the hospital’s information system, and perireperfusion hemodynamic data were documented.

The inclusion criteria were: (1) all adult patients (≥18 years) undergoing elective LDLT for chronic liver disease between January 2022 and August 2023; and (2) complete intraoperative hemodynamic and graft data available. Patients with incomplete data in medical records and those who underwent DDLT, simultaneous liver and kidney transplantations, or surgery for indications other than chronic liver disease were excluded.

In the operating room, standard monitors recommended by the American Society of Anesthesiologists, including pulse oximetry, electrocardiogram, and noninvasive blood pressure, were applied. Anesthesia induction involved fentanyl (2 µg/kg), propofol (1–2 mg/kg), and atracurium (0.5 mg/kg) as a muscle relaxant. Following muscle relaxation, the trachea was intubated using an appropriate-sized endotracheal tube, and volume-controlled mechanical ventilation was initiated. Anesthesia maintenance consisted of isoflurane (1%–2%), fentanyl infusion (1 µg/kg/hr), and atracurium infusion (0.5 mg/kg/hr). A triple-lumen 7F central venous catheter and a single-lumen 9F sheath were placed in the right internal jugular vein using ultrasound guidance. Additionally, 20G left radial and 18G right femoral arterial cannulas were inserted. Central venous pressure was monitored through the triple-lumen catheter, and cardiac output (CO) was continuously assessed using a fourth-generation FloTrac/Vigileo (Edwards Lifesciences) monitor attached to the femoral arterial catheter. Parameters such as stroke volume (SV), CO, systemic vascular resistance (SVR), and SV variation (SVV) were recorded. All procedures within the study period were conducted by a consistent surgical and anesthesiology team. Histidine-tryptophan-ketoglutarate solution was used for graft preservation, and all grafts were weighed before flushing with preservative fluid.

A 20% decrease in MAP immediately after reperfusion triggered intermittent boluses of adrenaline (10–20 µg) or phenylephrine (200–400 µg) per institutional protocol to prevent profound hypotension and ensure patient safety. However, for this study, PRS was defined specifically as a decrease in MAP ≥30% from the immediate prereperfusion value (T1), lasting ≥1 minute, irrespective of vasopressor administration. Patients were transferred to the transplant ICU, electively ventilated with propofol sedation for 8 to 10 hours postoperatively, after which sedation was discontinued, and weaning commenced according to standard weaning criteria.

Data Collection

Preoperative period

Recipient and donor demographics, surgery indications, presence of decompensation, MELD-Na scores, and preoperative laboratory results closest to the surgery date were collected.

Intraoperative period

Graft weight, GRWR (based on total body weight), recipient liver weight, cold ischemia time, warm ischemia time, duration of the anhepatic phase, administered colloids, crystalloids, blood products, urine output, and estimated blood loss were recorded. Lactate levels (mmol/L) and vasopressor requirements prereperfusion, peak postreperfusion, and at ICU transfer were also documented.

Reperfusion time and hemodynamic variables such as heart rate, MAP, CO, SV, and SVR were measured at the following time points: (1) immediately before reperfusion (T1), which was used as the baseline MAP for PRS diagnosis; (2) immediately after reperfusion (T2); and (3) every 30 seconds thereafter for 5 minutes (T3–T12).

Detailed hemodynamic data were captured at 30-second intervals using IntelliVue MX550 (Philips) monitors, which automatically stored numerical data. Video recordings of the monitor screen during the 5-minute perireperfusion period ensured accuracy and completeness. Two blinded observers independently reviewed these data, allowing anesthesiologists to concentrate on intraoperative patient management during critical events, thus maintaining data integrity. PRS was categorized based on the definition of Aggarwal et al. [2] as a MAP decrease ≥30% from baseline (T1), lasting more than 1 minute within 5 minutes postreperfusion, regardless of vasopressor administration.

Postoperative Period: Extubation Criteria

(1) Improving graft function (decreasing serum lactate levels <5 mmol/L); (2) hemodynamic stabilization (MAP >65 mm g) with minimal vasopressor support (noradrenaline <0.1 µg/kg/min); (3) good urine output (≥ 0.5 mL/kg/hr); (4) minimal or no acidosis (pH >7.25); (5) Improving coagulopathy; (6) normal hepatic artery flow (resistive index 0.6–0.9) and portal vein flow (100–150 mL/100 g graft on Doppler ultrasonography); (7) adequate recovery from muscle relaxation and anesthesia; (8) obeying commands and oriented to time, place, and person.

Hemodynamic Stabilization

The time taken for hemodynamic stabilization was calculated as the time from reperfusion of the graft to the discontinuation of all vasopressors to maintain a MAP >65 mmHg.

Early Allograft Dysfunction

According to Olthoff et al. [7], early allograft dysfunction was defined as the presence of at least one of the following: (1) bilirubin ≥10 mg/dL on postoperative day 7; (2) international normalized ratio (INR) ≥1.6 on postoperative day 7; (3) alanine transaminase or aspartate transaminase levels >2,000 IU/L within the first postoperative 7 days.

Postoperative Acute Kidney Injury

Acute injury was defined according to the Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes (KDIGO) criteria [8]: increase in serum creatinine by ≥0.3 mg/dL within 48 hours; or increase to ≥1.5 times the baseline value within 7 days. The baseline creatinine level was taken as the value closest to the date of surgery.

Consolidation

Consolidation was defined as the presence of a radio-opaque patch in a chest X-ray during ICU stay.

Pleural Effusion

Pleural effusion referred to fluid in the pleural cavity requiring drainage by aspiration or intercostal drain insertion.

Reintubation

Reintubation was defined as intubation within 24 hours after successful tracheal extubation in a patient initially intubated under general anesthesia [9].

Intensive Care Unit Discharge Criteria

(1) No requirement for organ support or continuous monitoring; (2) ambulatory status with or without support; (3) improving liver function tests; (4) normal hepatic Doppler ultrasonography (hepatic artery, vein, and portal vein); (5) return of bowel function; (6) tolerating oral intake.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges or mean±standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Comparisons between categorical variables were performed using the chi-square or Fisher exact tests, and continuous variables were compared using the Student t-test or Mann-Whitney U-test. Perireperfusion hemodynamic parameters were compared using repeated-measures ANOVA.

Variables with a P-value <0.05 in univariate analysis were included in a multivariate logistic regression (forward LR method) to identify predictors of PRS and calculate the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Significance was set at P<0.05. Effect sizes were reported as odds ratios for categorical data and mean differences for continuous data. Data were compiled using Microsoft Excel and statistically analyzed with SPSS ver. 28 (IBM Corp.) and MedCalc Statistical Software ver. 14.8.1 (MedCalc Software Ltd).

RESULTS

A total of 150 LDLTs were performed between January 2022 and August 2023. Of these, 100 adult patients with chronic liver disease met the inclusion criteria and were enrolled in the study. The CONSORT (Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials) flow diagram is presented in Fig 1. The etiologies of liver disease were alcoholic liver disease (48%), nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (18%), viral hepatitis (15%), autoimmune hepatitis (6%), hepatocellular carcinoma (2%), cryptogenic, and others (7%). Multivariate analysis showed that absolute graft weight was significantly associated with the occurrence of PRS (Table 1). The AUC for graft weight in predicting PRS was 82.1% (95% confidence interval [CI], 73.7%–90.5%), with an optimal cutoff identified at 651 g, yielding a sensitivity of 74% and specificity of 71% (Fig. 2A). For every 10-g increment in graft weight beyond 651 g, the odds of developing PRS increased by 11%. Based on the graft weight cutoff, patients were categorized into two groups: group A (graft weight ≥651 g, n=46) and group B (graft weight <651 g, n=54). Demographic data, laboratory values, and donor characteristics were comparable between the two groups, as summarized in Table 2. However, the incidence of PRS was significantly higher in group A than in group B (52.1% vs. 25.9%, P=0.007) (Table 3). Group A also required a higher median dose of phenylephrine during reperfusion (400 vs. 300 µg, P=0.01) (Table 3).

MAP and SVR were significantly lower in group A than in group B at time points T4 to T8 (Fig. 3). Despite greater early hemodynamic fluctuations observed in group A, group B required higher peak and ICU-transfer doses of noradrenaline and had elevated lactate levels during the immediate postoperative period (Table 3). Additionally, group B experienced longer durations of noradrenaline support (1,445±938.63 vs. 1,037±477.30 minutes; P=0.007), vasopressin support (826.76±535.94 vs. 608.80±340.77 minutes; P=0.016), and mechanical ventilation (1,156±1,020 vs. 748.15±322.37 minutes; P=0.007) (Table 4). Moreover, the ICU and hospital stays were longer in group B (Table 4). However, in-hospital mortality did not significantly differ between the groups. Multivariate analysis additionally indicated that the MELD-Na score was significantly associated with PRS occurrence. The AUC for the MELD-Na score was 75.8% (95% CI, 65.6%–85.9%). A MELD-Na threshold score of 22 demonstrated a sensitivity of 71% and specificity of 58% for predicting PRS (Fig. 2B).

DISCUSSION

The present study demonstrated that larger liver graft weight (≥651 g, group A) is associated with an increased risk of PRS, lower MAP immediately after reperfusion, and a higher requirement for phenylephrine during reperfusion. Conversely, patients who received smaller grafts (<651 g, group B) had a lower incidence of PRS but required prolonged vasopressor support and extended ICU stays.

PRS is a cardiovascular and metabolic complication arising immediately after portal vein unclamping. Known risk factors in LDLT include macrosteatotic grafts, higher recipient MELD scores, elevated preoperative heart rates, lower preoperative hemoglobin levels, increased intraoperative blood loss, and higher transfusion requirements [10]. Several strategies can mitigate PRS, such as optimizing preoperative hemodynamic parameters and electrolytes, minimizing ischemia times, and utilizing controlled reperfusion techniques. Many of these variables were standardized in our institutional protocol, including strict ischemia time control, exclusion of steatotic grafts, and proactive electrolyte correction. Vasopressors and inotropes were administered when necessary during surgery, along with vigilant monitoring of acid-base status and lactate levels. These interventions likely contributed to the reduced severity of PRS observed.

The current PRS definition by Aggarwal et al. [2] emphasizes immediate hemodynamic changes within the first 5 minutes postreperfusion. However, this definition inadequately captures the full clinical spectrum of PRS in LDLT settings. Our study found patients receiving larger grafts experienced significant initial hemodynamic disturbances typical of PRS but demonstrated faster recovery and more favorable outcomes. In contrast, recipients of smaller grafts exhibited slower recovery, despite less severe initial hemodynamic disruption.

It is important to recognize that the classical Aggarwal definition, originally developed from DDLT cohorts, might not fully apply to LDLT due to fundamental differences in donor profiles, ischemia duration, and recipient optimization. LDLT recipients often experience better preoperative optimization, and shorter ischemia periods, and receive tailored grafts, making PRS pathophysiology potentially distinct. Moreover, delayed or prolonged hemodynamic instability beyond the immediate 5-minute reperfusion period is common, particularly with smaller grafts exhibiting slower metabolic recovery. This highlights the need for an LDLT-specific PRS definition incorporating immediate and subsequent hemodynamic instability and underlying metabolic factors.

Few studies have evaluated absolute graft weight in relation to PRS. Umehara et al. [11] reported higher graft weights in PRS cases (635 vs. 484 g), although no specific cutoff was proposed. In our study, a threshold of 651 g provided 74% sensitivity and 71% specificity for PRS prediction. This association might reflect higher cytokine release from larger grafts, consistent with the correlation of tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-α levels reported by Bezinover et al. [12] and vasopressor requirements postreperfusion. Cytokine surges cause vasodilation, reduced SVR, and hypotension [13]. Additionally, larger grafts may abruptly enhance venous return and cardiac preload, further challenging cardiovascular stability.

Nonetheless, our study did not measure cytokines directly, rendering the hypothesis linking graft size and increased cytokine release speculative. This absence represents a key study limitation. Prospective studies incorporating intraoperative cytokine profiling (e.g., TNF-α, interleukin [IL]-6, IL-8) at defined reperfusion intervals are needed to validate this proposed relationship and provide deeper insight into the inflammatory and hemodynamic effects of graft size in LDLT.

Immediate hemodynamic changes postreperfusion are predominantly driven by cytokine release, with an estimated half-life of 25 minutes [14]. Thus, hemodynamic recovery approximately 75 minutes after reperfusion predominantly depends on graft metabolic capacity and regenerative potential, both associated with graft size. In our study, patients with smaller grafts (group B) experienced delayed MAP recovery and required higher peak noradrenaline doses intraoperatively and during ICU transfer, indicating slower metabolic recovery. Group B also had elevated peak and ICU lactate levels. Consistent with this, Orii et al. [15] reported a correlation between graft weight and lactate clearance, while Chen et al. [16] demonstrated slower regeneration in smaller (left lobe) grafts. The slower metabolic and hemodynamic recovery in group B translated into prolonged mechanical ventilation and increased ICU and hospital stays, reinforcing the clinical importance of graft size.

Our findings also revealed absolute graft weight as a superior PRS predictor compared to GRWR. In end-stage liver disease patients, volume overload and sarcopenia often skew body-weight-based calculations, making GRWR unreliable. Consequently, graft weight estimation solely based on body weight may yield inaccuracies [17]. The recipient MELD-Na score was also a significant PRS predictor. We identified a MELD-Na threshold score of ≥22, providing 71% sensitivity and 58% specificity. A 1-point increase in MELD-Na score correlated with a 15% increased PRS incidence. Tsai et al. [18] similarly linked higher MELD scores with increased oxidative stress markers, suggesting heightened susceptibility to reperfusion injury in advanced cirrhosis patients.

Our multivariate analysis confirmed that both higher MELD-Na scores and larger graft weights independently predict PRS. These findings emphasize PRS as a multifactorial phenomenon influenced by recipient condition and graft characteristics. Although larger grafts may support metabolic demands in high-MELD patients, they can independently induce PRS through heightened cytokine release and hemodynamic stress.

At our center, phenylephrine and adrenaline are routinely administered as boluses to manage hemodynamic instability during graft reperfusion. LDLT patients often present baseline tachycardia due to compensatory responses from cirrhosis-related vasodilation. Adrenaline can exacerbate tachycardia; therefore, concurrent phenylephrine administration provides α-agonist-induced vasoconstriction and reflex heart rate reduction. This approach helps prevent excessive tachycardia, potentially mitigating hepatic venous congestion. Thus, strategic combined use of phenylephrine and adrenaline aims to optimize hemodynamics while reducing graft-related complications.

Given postreperfusion hemodynamic challenges, anesthesiologists must prepare for sudden instability. Preventive measures—including correcting acidosis, maintaining normothermia, and prophylactic vasopressor administration—are critical. While adrenaline and phenylephrine have shown effectiveness in reducing PRS incidence [19,20], excessive administration can lead to excessive MAP elevation and bleeding, whereas insufficient dosing may precipitate hemodynamic collapse.

Accurate PRS prediction, utilizing parameters such as graft weight, can optimize perioperative management and potentially reduce PRS incidence in LDLT. Larger grafts particularly predispose patients to significant hemodynamic shifts and PRS, warranting heightened intraoperative vigilance.

To mitigate the severity of PRS in recipients of larger grafts, anesthesiologists should ensure ready access to vasopressors and inotropes, consider prophylactic phenylephrine or norepinephrine administration just before portal vein unclamping, and proactively elevate MAP above baseline levels. Techniques such as slow or staged reperfusion may facilitate controlled blood flow restoration, reducing acute inflammatory and cardiovascular responses.

The retrospective design and lack of cytokine measurement constitute significant limitations of this study. Future prospective studies examining cytokine profiles and their association with graft weight and PRS severity are warranted.

In conclusion, LDLT recipients with absolute graft weights >651 g are more prone to PRS but recover hemodynamically more rapidly, potentially resulting in shorter mechanical ventilation durations and fewer complications.
