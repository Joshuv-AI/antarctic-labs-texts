Epithelial‐Mesenchymal Transition States and Metabolic Reprogramming Related Signatures Predict Prognosis and Therapeutic Responses in HER2‐Positive Breast Cancer
Authors: Yuhui Zheng, Yinduo Zeng, Mengping Xi, Guoqiang Zhu, Jinyu Yang, Peng Cui, Guoqiang Wang, Yuzi Zhang, Chunwei Xu, Wenxian Wang, Shangli Cai, Li Wang, Xiaowen Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundEpithelial‐mesenchymal transition (EMT) and metabolic reprogramming have been shown to regulate HER2 targeted therapy resistance and tumor metastasis. We aimed to establish an EMT‐associated and metabolic‐related prognostic model in HER2‐positive breast cancer.MethodsmRNA expression and clinical information for HER2‐positive breast cancer are downloaded from the TCGA database. The single sample gene set enrichment analysis score was used to generate EMT subtypes and identify EMT‐related metabolic pathways. A prognostic risk score was developed based on the differentially expressed genes (DEGs) of the EMT‐associated metabolic pathways using least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) Cox regression and then validated in an external cohort (GSE96058).ResultsThe EMT enriched scores differentiated the OS in the HER2‐positive breast cancer TCGA cohort (p = 0.023; HR, 0.38; 95% CI, 0.16–0.9), which was also associated with the carbohydrate, amino acid, nucleotide, and tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle pathways (p < 0.05). A total of 10 genes based on the DEGs between the metabolic groups were used to construct the prognosis model. Patients with low‐risk metabolic scores showed longer OS compared to those with high‐risk metabolic scores (p < 0.001; HR, 0.09; 95% CI, 0.03–0.31). The association between OS and the metabolism score remained significant in the multivariable Cox regression (p < 0.001; HR, 0.08; 95% CI, 0.03–0.22). Similar results were observed in the validation cohort (p = 0.002; HR, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.16–0.69).ConclusionEMT and metabolic reprogramming‐related features are specific prognostic and potential therapeutic biomarkers for HER2‐positive breast cancer patients, which warrant further studies.Keywords: HER2‐positive breast cancer, immune, metabolic reprogramming, prognosis, treatment

Introduction

According to the latest global cancer burden data released by the International Agency for Research on Cancer (IARC) of the World Health Organization, the number of new breast cancer cases worldwide reached 2.26 million in 2020, surpassing lung cancer (2.21 million cases) to become the world's largest cancer for the first time [1]. Breast cancer is the most common type of cancer in women, accounting for about 30% of female tumors, and the ratio of mortality to morbidity is 15% [2]. The heterogeneity of breast cancer is very high. According to the clinical immunohistochemical markers of hormone receptors (HR) including estrogen receptor (ER) and progesterone receptor (PR), HER2 (ERBB2) and proliferation marker protein Ki‐67 (MKI67), breast cancer is classified into Luminal A (HR positive and HER2 negative), Luminal B (HR positive and HER2 positive, with high Ki‐67 (> 14%)), HER2‐enriched subtype (HR negative and HER2 positive), and triple‐negative breast cancer (HR negative and HER2 negative) [3]. The 5‐year survival rate among women with HR+/HER2− is estimated to be 94.3%, followed by HR+/HER2+ at 90.5%, HR−/HER2+ at 84.0%, and HR−/HER2− at 76.9% [4].

About 20%–25% of breast cancer patients have HER2 overexpression due to HER2 gene amplification [5]. HER2‐targeted therapy is the linchpin of managing HER2‐overexpressing breast cancer. Currently, there are nine HER2 antibodies or inhibitors available for the treatment of HER2‐positive breast cancer in clinical, including trastuzumab, pertuzumab, trastuzumab emtansine (T‐DM1), trastuzumab deruxican (DS‐8201a), margetuximab, inetetamab, lapatinib, neratinib and tacatinib, pyrotinib [6, 7, 8]. Compared with chemotherapy alone, trastuzumab combined with chemotherapy has been shown to reduce the mortality of patients with HER2‐positive breast cancer by 20% [9]. Although the application of HER2 targeted therapy improves the survival of HER2‐positive breast cancer, drug resistance in HER2‐positive breast cancer is a very serious challenge.

Breast cancer stem cells (BCSCs) play an important role in the progression, recurrence, and metastasis of breast cancer [10]. Breast cancer stem cells are a small subset of breast cancer cells that resist chemotherapy and radiotherapy treatments [11]. Many studies have shown that the surface molecules of breast cancer stem cells are characterized by high expression of CD44 and low or missing expression of CD24 [12, 13]. Breast cancer stem cells have the potential to promote tumor metastasis by enhancing the expression of epithelial cell adhesion molecules and exhibit the characteristics of epithelial‐mesenchymal transformation (EMT) [10]. EMT is a complex cellular and molecular process in which epithelial cells lose cell–cell contact and apical–basal polarity, thus acquiring mesenchymal cell characteristics [14]. EMT is an important cause of tumor progression, recurrence, metastasis, and drug resistance [15]. Cancer cells acquire cancer‐like stem cell properties through metabolic reprogramming, which leads to EMT progression and the development of aggressive tumor phenotypes [16]. Metabolic reprogramming in breast cancer involves glycolysis, the tricarboxylic acid cycle, the pentose phosphate pathway, and lipid metabolism pathways, which affect genetic and epigenetic processes [17]. During EMT, pathways such as glycolysis, fatty acid synthesis, and glutaminolysis are upregulated to fuel tumor progression, while oxidative phosphorylation and fatty acid oxidation are downregulated. This metabolic reprogramming supports the energy needs and biosynthetic requirements of cancer cells as they become more aggressive and invasive. Metabolic reprogramming in cancer cells induces the utilization of cellular energy sources and regulates the expression of EMT‐related genes. Studies have shown that treatment resistance caused by EMT progression may be co‐regulated by transcription factors (such as Snail, Zbe1, Zbe2, Twist, etc.) and post‐transcription factors (miRNA‐200, Haiki, etc.) [18, 19, 20, 21]. In breast cancer, overexpression of Twist or suppression of E‐cadherin expression can develop resistance to paclitaxel and adriamycin [22]. In addition, overexpression of Snail inhibited docetaxel and gemcitabine in the breast cancer cell line [23]. It has been reported that the EMT driver gene SNAIL2 was upregulated in trastuzumab‐refractory basal HER2 breast cancer cells [24]. Therefore, it is of great significance to understand the mechanism of metabolic reprogramming in the epithelial‐mesenchymal transition of HER2‐positive breast cancer.

In this work, we investigated the EMT‐associated metabolic gene changes and constructed a 10‐gene prognostic signature based on metabolic clusters. These results can help us further understand the mechanism of metabolic reprogramming in different epithelial–mesenchymal transition activity states and provide a prognostic tool in HER2‐positive breast cancer.

Methods

Data Source

The RNA‐seq of 160 HER2‐positive breast cancer samples with corresponding clinical data such as stage, menopause status, ER and PR status, and survival (Table S1) was obtained from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA; https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/). We obtained the breast cancer mutation annotation file (MAF) from the GDC database (https://portal.gdc.cancer.gov/files/6c93f518‐1956‐4435‐9806‐37185266d248), and used the R package TCGAbiolinks (v 2.16.4) to obtain sample treatment data from the GDC database. The EMT‐related genes were acquired from the dbEMT database and MSigDB database, and a total of 1625 genes were obtained by integrating the two databases. We extracted the HER2‐positive breast cancer samples from the GEO database as a validation cohort (GSE96058; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/query/acc.cgi). Epithelial‐Mesenchymal Transition Enrichment Analysis

Based on the above‐mentioned EMT‐related genes, we used the R package GSVA (v1.34.0) to calculate the single sample gene set enrichment analysis (ssGSEA) [25] enrichment score of EMT for each sample from the TCGA database, and then used the surv_cutpoint of the R package survminer (v 0.4.8) to find the most suitable cutoff. Patients with an EMT score above the cutoff were defined as EMT‐H, and the rest were defined as EMT‐L. Kaplan–Meier curves between EMT‐H and EMT‐L were compared by the R package survival (v 3.2‐7).

Metabolic Pathway Enrichment Analysis

We obtained the metabolic signature gene sets that were reported in a previous study [26], which included 348 genes for amino acid metabolism, 766 genes for lipid metabolism, 286 genes for carbohydrate metabolism, 110 genes for the integration of energy, 148 genes for the tricarboxylic acid cycle (TCA cycle), 168 genes for vitamin cofactor metabolism, and 90 genes for nucleotide metabolism. We used ssGSEA to quantify the activity of enrichment levels of the metabolic signatures. The enrichment scores were compared between the EMT subgroups, and key metabolic pathways were identified.

Identification of Metabolic‐Related Differentially Expressed Genes (DEGs)

An unsupervised clustering analysis was performed in the genes of above selected key metabolic pathways in the TCGA database using the PAM algorithm of the R package, ConsensusClusterPlus (v1.50.0). The optimal number of clusters was identified by the “proportion of ambiguous clustering” (PAC). The clusters that were generated by genes of selected metabolic pathways were called metabolic groups. We performed DEGs analysis of the metabolic groups by using the R package “edgeR”. Genes with the absolute log2 (fold change, FC) > 1 and false discovery rate (FDR) < 0.05 were defined as DEGs that were associated with different metabolic groups. We calculated the corresponding log2 (FPKM +1) for further expression analysis. Then we conducted the gene ontology (GO) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analyses for the above DEGs.

Immune Infiltration Analysis

Cell‐type Identification by Estimating Relative Subsets of RNA Transcripts (CIBERSORT) can accurately estimate the immune composition of tumor gene expression data [27, 28]. CIBERSORT gene signature matrix distinguishes 22 human hematopoietic cell phenotypes, including seven T cell types, naive and memory B cells, plasma cells, NK cells, and myeloid subsets. We used the R package CIBERSORT (v1.03) to compare the infiltration level and immune scores between the high‐ and low‐risk groups.

Prediction of Drug Response in Different Risk Groups

The R package pRRophetic [29] (v 0.5) was used to predict the sample's response to 138 drugs. The half maximal inhibitory concentrations (IC50) of every sample in response to 138 drugs were calculated through the R package “pRRophetic”. The IC50 values were compared between the high‐ and low‐risk groups. We only showed the clinical drugs commonly used in breast cancer and drugs that have been reported to have antitumor effects in breast cancer, including lapatinib, docetaxel, doxorubicin, temsirolimus, gefitinib, JNK.9 L, JNK.Inhibitor.VIII, lenalidomide, bortezomib, MG132, and vorinostat.

Cell Lines, Quantitative PCR, Plasmids, CCK‐8 and Transwell Assays

The human breast cancer cell lines BT‐474 (HTB‐20) and HCC1954 (CRL‐2338), and human breast epithelial cell line MCF‐10A (CRL‐10317) were purchased from the ATCC (American Type Culture Collection). BT‐474 was cultured in Hybri‐Care Medium (ATCC, Catalog No. 46‐X) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. HCC1954 and MCF‐10A were cultured in RPMI‐1640 Medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (Gibco), 100 U/mL penicillin, and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. All cell lines were incubated at 37°C and in a 5% CO2 incubator.

RNA was extracted from cell lines with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and treated with DNase I. The RNA was stored at 80°C, and its concentration was measured by NanoDrop2000 spectrophotometry. RT‐PCR was performed on aliquots with 1 μg total RNA to generate 20 μL cDNA by using the HiScript II Reverse Transcriptase (cat#R201‐01, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Semi‐quantitative PCR was performed using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Cat# Q711‐02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) and the LightCycler 480 Real‐Time PCR system (Roche, Rotkreuz, Switzerland). Gene expression level was calculated by the 2−ΔΔCt method. ACTIN was acted as a gene expression control. The primers were designed by primerbank. The related primer sequences were in Table S2.

BCL11B and ADAMTS8 genes were amplified from MCF10A mRNA and cloned into pCMV‐3 × flag. BCL11B and ADAMTS8 overexpressed plasmids and the control vector were transfected into indicated cells using Lipo3000 following the manufacturer's instructions (Thermo Fisher Scientific). After 72 h, RNA was prepared from transfected cells. Overexpression of ADAMTS8 was validated by q‐PCR.

For the cell proliferation assay, cells were seeded into 96‐well plates (at a density of 2000 cells/well). Cell proliferation was analyzed for the following 4 days after planting using Cell Counting Kit‐8 (Selleck, Cat#:B34302) following the manufacturer's instructions. Cell viability was assessed by measuring OD values. Absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a Multiskan Skyhigh photometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA).

Transwell chambers were used to detect cell migration and invasion ability. The cells were collected (1 × 105 HCC1954 cells and 1 × 105 BT474 cells), washed in serum‐free medium once, and seeded onto the upper chamber. The lower chambers included 700 μL medium with 20% fetal bovine serum (FBS). After cultured in an incubator at 37°C for 16 h, the cells were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 30 min. The number of invaded cells was calculated using Image J software.

Statistical Analysis

Data analysis was performed by R software, version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria https://www.r‐project.org/). We used the Wilcox test to compare continuous variables between two groups and the Kruskal‐Wallis test to compare continuous variables between three or more groups. We calculated the Pearson coefficient to measure the correlation between two continuous variables. We used the Kaplan–Meier (KM) survival curves to estimate the survival. Survival between groups was compared with the log‐rank test. The survival analysis was performed by the R package survival (version 3.2–7) and survminer (version 0.4.8). We evaluated the accuracy of the model in predicting survival according to the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC) analysis by the R package timeROC. Unless otherwise stated, p value < 0.05 was considered to be statistically significant.

Results

Metabolic Reprogramming of Different EMT Groups in HER2‐Positive Breast Cancer

We first explored the association between the EMT process and metabolic reprogramming in HER2‐positive breast cancer. Based on the ssGSEA score of EMT, patients with higher EMT scores had better OS than those with lower EMT scores (p = 0.023, HR = 0.38; 95% CI, 0.16–0.9, Figure 1B). The GO analysis of the differentially expressed genes between the two EMT groups indicated that the metabolic process was significantly different between the two groups, especially the collagen metabolism process (Figure 1C).

Then, we compared the ssGSEA scores of the seven metabolic signatures between EMT‐H and EMT‐L groups. The results showed that carbohydrate metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, TCA cycle, and amino acid metabolism were significantly different between EMT‐H and EMT‐L groups (p < 0.05, Figure 1D). It was worth noting that metabolic reprogramming, glycolysis, glutamine metabolism, and lipid metabolism pathways have also been reported to be involved in the development and progression of breast cancer [30]. In particular, breast cancer metabolism reprograms the glutamine metabolic pathway and participates in tumor cell migration and invasion and EMT [31]. We further explored the correlation between immune cell infiltration and the seven metabolic pathways. Consistent with the above results, the amino acid, nucleotide, TCA, and carbohydrate pathways were significantly correlated with immune cell sub‐populations (p < 0.05, Figure 1E). Taken together, we found that carbohydrate metabolism, nucleotide metabolism, TCA cycle, and amino acid metabolism reprogramming might be involved in EMT, which were further studied.

Identification of the EMT‐Related Metabolic Reprogramming Groups

In order to further demonstrate the prognostic value of the EMT‐related metabolic pathways, we then performed non‐supervised clustering in the 810 genes in the above carbohydrate, nucleotide, TCA, and amino acid metabolic pathways. Three groups were obtained (Figure 2A), and the prognosis of metabolic group 1–3 was significantly different (p = 0.009, Figure 2B). The heat map reflects the enrichment of these four important metabolic pathways in different metabolic groups (Figure 2C). The metabolic pathways of nucleotide, TCA, and amino acid in group 3 were down‐regulated, but their expression in the EMT‐L subgroup of group 1 was up‐regulated. Also, the carbohydrate metabolic pathway seemed to be down‐regulated in the EMT‐L subgroup of group 3 (Figure 2C).

Through gene set variation analysis (GSVA) of three metabolic subtypes, we found that there were 24 pathways that have significant differences between the 3 subtypes (Figure S1). We noticed significant abnormalities in the PI3K‐AKT–MTOR signaling pathway, MTORC1 signaling pathway, glycolysis, and P53 signaling pathway (p < 0.001). Interestingly, the PI3K‐AKT–MTOR signaling pathway can induce the expression of glycolytic enzymes and promote glucose transporter (GLUT), and p53 can regulate glycolysis and GLUT through mTOR and AMP‐activated protein kinase (AMPK) [32, 33]. mTOR can be activated in breast cancer and participate in glycolysis metabolic reprogramming through abnormal regulation of the PI3K signaling pathway, mTOR mutation, and HER‐2 overexpression pathway [34].

We then performed DEGs analysis between the three metabolic groups. In total, there were 590 DEGs between group 1 and group 2, 454 DEGs between group 1 and group 3, and 423 DEGs between group 2 and group 3. Functional enrichment analysis was then performed on different metabolic groups. The 590 DEGs between group 1 and group 2 were enriched in regulation of ion transmembrane transport, regulation of cellular divalent inorganic cation homeostasis, regulation of membrane potential, calcium ion homeostasis, etc. by biological process (Figure 2D), collagen‐containing extracellular matrix, cell–cell junction and external side of plasma membrane by cell component (Figure 2D), and cytokine activity, receptor ligand activity and receptor activator activity, etc. by molecular function (Figure 2D). The pathways enriched by KEGG were mainly Viral protein interaction with cytokine and cytokine receptor, IL‐17 signaling pathway, PPAR signaling pathway, Cytokine‐cytokine receptor interaction and Basal cell carcinoma (Figure 2E). The 454 DEGs between group 1 and group 3 were enriched in regulation of cellular divalent inorganic cation homeostasis, regulation of membrane potential, etc. by biological process (Figure 2F), collagen‐containing extracellular matrix, presynapse, external side of plasma membrane by cell component (Figure 2F), and channel activity, and passive transmembrane transporter, etc. by molecular function (Figure 2F). The pathways enriched by KEGG were mainly neuroactive ligand–receptor interaction, IL‐17 signaling pathway, estrogen signaling pathway and dopaminergic synapse (Figure 2G). The 423 DEGs between group 2 and group 3 were enriched in regulation of epithelial cell proliferation, regulation of membrane potential, etc. by biological process (Figure 2H), collagen‐containing extracellular matrix and apical part of cell by cell component (Figure 2H), receptor ligand activity and receptor activator activity by molecular function (Figure 2H). The PI3K‐AKT signaling pathway was identified again in DEGs between group 2 and group 3 (Figure 2I), which indicated that this signaling pathway had important significance in metabolic reprogramming. The identified DEGs between three metabolic groups had potential correlation to both metabolic reprogramming and EMT process.

Construction and Validation of a Prognostic Model Based on DEGs Related to Metabolic Reprogramming

To construct a prognostic model, we first narrowed the gene list by performing univariable Cox regression analysis to select the DEGs between metabolic groups with prognostic value. In total, 88 genes were identified with prognostic value (p < 0.05; the top 20 genes were illustrated in Figure S2), and the KM curves for the top 6 genes were demonstrated in Figure S3. For example, patients with high B3GAT1 expression had prolonged OS compared to patients with low B3GAT1 expression (p < 0.001; HR, 0.2; 95% CI, 0.07–0.55; Figure S3).

We further performed LASSO regression based on the results of univariable Cox regression, and finally constructed a risk score model based on 10 genes: Risk Score = TNFRSF13B*0.1003 + BCL11B*0.0985 + BEND5*0.0825 + TPSAB1*0.0556 + B3GAT1*0.0501 + UGT3A2*0.0315 + ADAMTS8*0.0231 + CD79A*0.0151 + CHAD*0.0121 + CNNM1*0.000189. We calculated the risk score of each sample according to the formula and divided patients into the high‐risk and low‐risk groups with the median of the risk score as the cutoff. Patients in the high‐risk group had better OS than patients in the low‐risk group (p < 0.001, HR, 0.09; 95% CI, 0.03–0.31, Figure 3A). The AUC for 3‐year prediction was 0.86 and the AUC for 5‐year prediction was 0.91 (Figure 3B). The expression of the 10 genes between high‐risk and low‐risk groups is depicted in Figure 3C. In addition, multivariable Cox regression analysis showed the risk model was an independent prognostic factor for HER2‐positive breast cancer patients (p < 0.001; HR, 0.08; 95% CI, 0.03–0.22; Table 1).

In order to further validate the effectiveness of the model, we obtained the validation data set GSE96058 from the GEO database, from which 438 HER2‐positive breast cancer samples were extracted. Patients in the high‐risk group also had worse OS than patients in the low‐risk group (p = 0.002; HR, 0.34; 95% CI, 0.16–0.69, Figure 3D). Differences in Immune Infiltration and Drug Response Predictions Related to Prognostic Model Subgroups

In order to explore the mechanism underlying the prognostic model, we calculated the difference in immune cell infiltration between the high‐risk and low‐risk groups. The results showed that the Macrophages M0 and Macrophages M2 of the samples in the high‐risk group were significantly higher than those in the low‐risk group, while the CD8+ T cells, T cells CD4 memory resting, T cells regulatory (Tregs), and Macrophages M1 in the low‐risk group were significantly higher than the high‐risk group (p < 0.05, Figure 4A). Further statistics of ER, menopause status, PR status, and stage factors in different risk groups showed that the ER was significantly different between the two groups (p = 0.007, Figure 4B).

We then predicted the response of samples from high‐risk and low‐risk groups to drugs based on Cancer Drug Sensitivity Genomics (GDSC). Interestingly, the low‐risk group was more sensitive to the HER2 inhibitor lapatinib, EGFR targeting inhibitor gefitinib, HDAC inhibitor vorinostat, and proteasome inhibitors (bortezomib, lenalidomide, and MG132), JNK inhibitors (JNK.9 L and JNK.Inhibitor.VIII), and chemotherapy drugs (docetaxel and temsirolimus) than the high‐risk group, while the high‐risk group was more sensitive to doxorubicin than the low‐risk group (p < 0.001, Figure 4C). Our results might suggest that an inhibitory immune environment may exist in the high‐risk group and the prognostic signature may potentially guide treatment decisions.

Validation of the Genes in the Prognostic Signature

To validate the function of the prognostic genes identified in our study, two key experiments were conducted. Firstly, we investigated the mRNA levels of the 10 prognostic signature genes in HER2‐positive breast cancer cell lines and normal breast cells. Specifically, MCF10A represented normal breast epithelial cells, while HCC1954 (HER2‐positive, ER/PR‐negative) and BT‐474 (HER2‐positive, ER/PR‐positive) served as HER2‐positive breast cancer cell lines. Consistent with our findings that higher expression correlates with a better prognosis, the mRNA levels of BCL11B and ADAMTS8 were significantly lower in HER2‐positive breast cancer cell lines compared to normal breast epithelial cells (Figure 5A; ADAMTS8: p = 0.0159, MCF10A vs. HCC1954; BCL11B: p < 0.0001, MCF10A vs. BT‐474, one‐way ANOVA with Dunnett's multiple comparisons test). While previous studies have demonstrated the tumor‐suppressive roles of BCL11B and ADAMTS8 in liver cancer and triple‐negative breast cancer, respectively [35, 36], their function in HER2‐positive breast cancer has not been reported. This led us to hypothesize that BCL11B and ADAMTS8 exert anti‐cancer effects in HER2‐positive breast cancer, where their overexpression could inhibit tumor proliferation or migration.

To test this hypothesis, we constructed overexpression plasmids and confirmed successful overexpression via qPCR (Figure 5B,C, ***p < 0.001, **p < 0.01, paired student t‐test). Subsequently, we performed CCK‐8 and transwell assays. As anticipated, overexpression of BCL11B and ADAMTS8 significantly inhibited tumor cell proliferation (Figure 5D–G, ***p < 0.001, two‐way ANOVA) and migration (Figure 5H,I, ***p < 0.001, **p < 0.01, paired student t‐test). These results thus validated the tumor‐suppressive roles of BCL11B and ADAMTS8 in HER2‐positive breast cancer cells, providing a partial biological explanation for our prognostic signature. Further investigations are warranted to elucidate the underlying signaling pathways of BCL11B and ADAMTS8.

Discussion

HER2‐positive breast cancer is an extremely complex and highly heterogeneous disease [37]. Although HER2‐targeted therapy improves OS, the treatment of drug resistance and recurrence and metastasis is a huge challenge [38]. Epithelial‐mesenchymal transition and metabolic reprogramming are jointly involved in the resistance and recurrence of HER2‐positive breast cancer. Our study found that in HER2‐positive breast cancer, four key metabolic pathway genes (carbohydrate, amino acid, nucleotide, and TCA cycle) were associated with EMT subgroups. Subsequently, a risk model was constructed and validated using the DEGs of the metabolic groups generated by the four key metabolic pathways.

In the present study, we first identified carbohydrate, amino acid, nucleotide, and TCA cycle pathways to be EMT‐related out of the seven metabolic super‐pathways. The key transcription factors that induce EMT were related to glucose, fatty acid, and amino acid metabolism. Transcription factor Snail was reported to suppress the expression of fructose‐1,6‐bisphosphatase (FBP1), leading to increased glycolysis and reactive oxygen species production [39]. Nuclear translocation of pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) could bind to transcription factors and inhibit the expression of epithelial cadherin during the EMT process [40]. In previous studies, there was no significant difference in EMT status among breast cancer samples [26], but we extracted HER2‐positive breast cancer samples based on the characteristics of breast cancer heterogeneity and found that the EMT‐L group had a worse prognosis than the EMT‐H group. This indicated that there were significant differences in EMT and metabolic reprogramming between different breast cancer subtypes. The key factor of tumor progression is that tumor cells have strong environmental adaptability. The dynamic regulation of metabolism (also known as metabolic plasticity) allows different subtypes of breast cancer or different cancer cell subgroups in breast cancer to adapt to changes in the microenvironment during tumor growth, epigenetic reprogramming, and treatment [41]. In fact, different subtypes of breast cancer have specific metabolic characteristics and metabolic dependence. Metabolic reprogramming in HER2‐positive breast cancer is closely associated with glycolysis and is consistent with the upregulation of PI3K‐AKT‐MTORC1 signaling [42], which is consistent with our findings.

Additionally, a 10‐gene prognostic signature was constructed through univariable Cox regression and LASSO regression analysis of DEGs. These 10 genes included TNFRSF13B, BCL11B, BEND5, TPSAB1, B3GAT1, UGT3A2, ADAMTS8, CD79A, CHAD, and CNNM1. In this signature, our results indicated that HER2‐positive breast cancer patients with high expression of the B3GAT1 gene had a better prognosis than those with low expression. Furthermore, cell experiments validated two tumor suppressors in our study. The overexpression of BCL11B and ADAMTS8 inhibited proliferation, migration, and invasion in two HER2‐positive breast cancer cell lines (HCC1954 and BT474), providing functional links between prognosis and gene expression. Several genes had revealed functional implications in cancer that were consistent with our results. It was reported that high expression of B3GAT1 correlated with increased overall survival for breast cancer patients [43]. Previous studies demonstrated that BEND5 suppressed breast cancer cell proliferation and metastasis through Notch signaling [44]. In addition, BEND5 could increase the expression of the EMT‐related protein E‐cadherin and reduce that of the EMT‐related proteins N‐cadherin and Vimentin [44]. ADAMTS8, a member of a disintegrin and metalloproteinase with thrombospondin (−like) motifs, has been found downregulated in lung cancer, gastric cancer, and triple‐negative breast cancer [45]. ADAMTS8 could inhibit the EMT‐related pathway, including the Wnt signaling pathway and EGFR/MAPK/ERK pathway [45]. Our prognostic signature was identified from DEGs of three metabolic clusters that were related to EMT, which need further study to demonstrate their metabolic and EMT correlations.

In terms of prognostic models for breast cancer, Erik Knutsen et al. analyzed RNA‐sequencing data from breast cancer cell lines and identified an EMT signature that separates cancer into two subgroups, partial‐EMT and post‐EMT [46]. However, in the basal‐like breast cancer, no evident difference in prognosis between the two EMT subgroups [46]. Similarly, EMT status identified by a 130‐gene signature was not correlated to the overall survival in breast cancer [47]. A 5‐gene metabolic signature predicted overall survival in early‐stage breast cancer, with relatively lower performance in the HER2 positive subgroup (p = 0.0364) [48]. Recently, Li et al. utilized machine learning algorithms and genes related to glutamate metabolism to establish a model for breast cancer prognosis. The AUC values in the training dataset were lower compared to our model (3‐year AUC 0.707 vs. 0.86, 5‐year AUC 0.688 vs. 0.91) [49]. A 12‐gene model that combined diverse cell death patterns predicted prognosis and drug sensitivity of triple‐negative breast cancer [50]. These results from other studies emphasize a great heterogeneity among breast cancer subtypes and the necessity of integrating different cancer features for prognosis modeling.

As immune dysfunction had a direct impact on breast cancer progression and outcome [51], we further explored the relationship between the risk score and infiltration pattern of immune cells. According to the risk score value, TCGA HER2‐positive breast patients were classified into high‐risk and low‐risk score groups. The predicted lower infiltration of CD8+ T cells, CD4 memory resting T cells, and regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the high‐risk group may correlate with impaired immune surveillance. The competitive consumption of glucose and other metabolites by tumor cells may interfere with T cell proliferation and differentiation [52]. Although the treatment landscape for HER2‐positive breast cancer primarily revolves around targeted therapies, hormonal treatments, and chemotherapy, with no routine recommendations for immunotherapy, emerging research indicates that a subset of HER2‐positive breast cancers represents an immune‐modulatory subtype [53], characterized by an activated immune microenvironment. This suggests that such patients might benefit from de‐escalation therapy and immunotherapy. Our results demonstrated higher infiltration of CD8+ T cells and regulatory T cells (Tregs) in the low‐risk group. This observation was consistent with an activated immune microenvironment, thereby suggesting that these patients might derive benefit from immunotherapy. In the low‐risk group, the drug reactions of the HER2 inhibitor lapatinib, EGFR targeting inhibitor gefitinib, HDAC inhibitor vorinostat, proteasome inhibitors (bortezomib, lenalidomide, MG132), and JNK inhibition were significantly better than those of the high‐risk group. The first‐line treatment recommendation for advanced HER2‐positive breast cancer typically involves a combination of dual HER2‐targeted therapy with trastuzumab and pertuzumab, given alongside chemotherapy such as docetaxel. The tyrosine kinase inhibitor lapatinib is used in combination with trastuzumab when prior HER2‐targeted therapy fails. Our analysis revealed that the low‐risk group exhibited increased sensitivity to both docetaxel and lapatinib. This finding aligns with the observed superior prognosis in the low‐risk group. It has been reported that the HDAC inhibitor panobinostat inhibits EMT and tumor metastasis in triple‐negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells by inducing the expression of CDH1 [54]. JNK inhibitor SP600125 has shown anti‐tumor effects in HER2 inhibitor‐resistant breast cancer mouse models, and the JNK inhibitor combined with protein kinase HUNK can inhibit tumor growth in drug‐resistant HER2‐positive breast cancer mouse models [55]. Our findings provided insights into potential drugs for low‐risk HER2‐positive breast cancer patients based on the risk assessment model in this study. However, patients in the high‐risk group were insensitive to common chemotherapeutics except doxorubicin, which needed more research to find effective drugs in the future.

However, this work still had some limitations. First, our study was a retrospective study, and prospective studies were needed to confirm the reliability of the risk‐predicted model. Then, the transcriptomic and the corresponding clinical data of patients with HER2‐positive breast cancer were obtained from a public database, which may result in potential selection bias. Finally, although we identified two tumor suppressive genes in vitro, BCL11B and ADAMTS8, the biological functions and molecular mechanisms of the 10 identified genes should be further explored, especially in terms of EMT and metabolism reprogramming.

The epithelial‐mesenchymal transition and metabolic heterogeneity of breast cancer are complex, and metabolic flexibility greatly promotes the heterogeneity between breast cancer subtypes [56]. Therefore, the use of combination therapies (including chemotherapy, small molecule targeted therapy, epigenetic drugs, or metabolic targeted drugs) to target tumor metabolism is a potential way to block cancer cell growth and tumor progression in breast cancer patients [57, 58]. We have investigated the characteristics of metabolic reprogramming in HER2‐positive breast cancer, analyzed effective drugs that target metabolic reprogramming, identified the tumor suppressive role of two prognostic genes (BCL11B and ADAMTS8) in vitro, which provides an important reference for the pharmacological targeted therapy of metabolism in breast cancer patients.

Conclusions

Our results suggest that EMT and metabolic reprogramming‐related features are specific prognostic and potential therapeutic biomarkers for HER2‐positive patients, which warrant further studies.
