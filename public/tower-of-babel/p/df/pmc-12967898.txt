An Infant With Phakomatosis Pigmentovascularis Complicated by Multisystem Damage: Elaborate Care Leads to a Positive Outcome
Authors: Kaimin Chen, Xiaoxia Huang, Xin Jin, Lilan He, Mingzhu He, Wenli Shen, Yuling Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis report highlights the value of multidisciplinary collaboration, personalized care, dynamic monitoring of seizure activity, and innovative skin management in a 2‐month‐old with Phakomatosis Pigmentovascularis. In parallel, the nursing team implemented a multidimensional “medical–social–psychological” support model, providing psychological guidance and economic assistance to the family to help the infant achieve a favorable outcome.Keywords: elaborate care, epilepsy, infant, phakomatosis pigmentovascularis

Introduction

Phakomatosis pigmentovascularis (PPV) is a rare congenital cutaneous syndrome characterized by capillary malformations and extensive dermal melanosis [1]. Its incidence is higher among Argentinian and Japanese populations and lower in Caucasians [2]. Vidaurri‐de la Cruz et al. [3] reported an incidence of 5.8/100,000 in pediatric patients and 0.634/100,000 in dermatologic patients. Approximately 50% of individuals with PPV exhibit one or more comorbidities [4], including ocular, neurological, vascular, musculoskeletal, or renal involvement [5]. Neurological abnormalities, such as seizures, psychomotor retardation, and cerebral atrophy, often manifest within the first few months of life [1]. For patients with systemic involvement, frequent follow‐up and multidisciplinary management involving ophthalmologists, pediatricians, and neurologists is essential [6].

In addition to typical cutaneous lesions, PPV is also associated with conditions such as anemia nevus, café‐au‐lait macules, vitiligo, and congenital alopecia. Central nervous system abnormalities, including epilepsy, cerebral cortical atrophy, hydrocephalus, and urolithiasis are less common but clinically important. Ocular findings, such as ophthalmic nigrostenosis, iris papillary protrusion, glaucoma, and retinopigmentary alterations, as well as musculoskeletal findings including limb‐length discrepancies and scoliosis, have also been documented [7, 8].

We report a rare case of a two‐month‐old infant with PPV complicated by epilepsy and glaucoma. Globally, research on PPV care remains limited, particularly in pediatric cases that present with multisystem involvement. This report describes the nursing management provided for this infant and aims to offer useful insights and references for similar clinical scenarios.

Case History and Examination

A two‐month‐old male infant was admitted to the hospital for multiple malformations and poor responsiveness since birth, and foaming at the mouth for 2 days. On admission, he exhibited diffuse erythema of the face that blanched on pressure (Figure 1). Multiple patchy blue marks were present on the trunk and left leg (Figures 2, and 3), with several confluent areas. Both eyes showed discharge, and the irises appeared black to the naked eye. Ocular ultrasound revealed vitreous clouding, diffuse thickening of the ocular wall, and irregular optic papillae bilaterally. The genetic testing did not identify any specific pathogenic variations related to the disease phenotype. According to family members, the infant had been persistently drowsy and minimally responsive since birth. He also had urethral hypospadias, foaming at the mouth, prominent phlegm sounds, and mild perioral cyanosis of the lips. Nebulization, sputum suction, cardiac monitoring, and other symptomatic treatments were initiated after admission.

Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment

Although the infant had facial hemangioma and glaucoma, the absence of unilateral meningeal hemangioma allowed exclusion of Sturge–Weber syndrome. Despite blue irises and hearing impairment, the presence of blue nevi—rather than leukoderma—.made Waardenburg syndrome unlikely. After multidisciplinary discussion, the child was diagnosed with Phakomatosis Pigmentovascularis, Epilepsy, Acute Bronchitis, Bilateral Glaucoma, and Hypospadias.

On the 7th day of hospitalization, the child developed spasms characterized by double‐eyed staring, limb stiffening, salivation, and oxygen desaturation during each episode. Seizures lasted 1–2 min and occurred approximately 5–6 times daily. Oral luminal antispasmodic treatment was initiated, and he was transferred from the general ward to the intensive care unit for close monitoring. On the 9th day, he experienced frequent nighttime convulsions, again presenting with staring eyes, straightening of the extremities, and frothing at the mouth. After excluding organic acid metabolic disorders, oral sodium valproate solution, and intravenous midazolam for anticonvulsant treatment were initiated.

On the 11th day of hospitalization, several additional convulsive episodes occurred, featuring gaze fixation, frothing, oxygen desaturation, limb twitching, and increased muscle tone, each lasting approximately one minute. Oxygen was administered immediately, and antispasmodic dosages were adjusted. On the 12th day, his blood ammonia levels were elevated (158 N‐μg/dL), and oral levetiracetam was added.

Conclusion and Results

On the 19th day, his condition had stabilized, allowing transfer back to the general ward. On the 21st day, both frequency and duration of seizures had markedly decreased, and he was discharged in a stable condition.

Discussion

First Aid and Supportive Care for Seizures

Meticulous management and observation are crucial in the care of children with epilepsy [9]. The care team maintained detailed seizure records, including timing, duration, type, and the child's status before and after each episode. Seizures began on the 7th day of admission, occurring 5–6 times daily and presenting as gaze fixation, increased limb tonus, salivation, and oxygen desaturation, lasting 1–2 min. To monitor progression more precisely, the nursing team used video electroencephalogram (VEEG) data, which revealed several convulsive seizures characterized by asymmetric limb rigidity, abduction of the left upper limb, flexion of the right upper limb, and deviation of the head and eyes to the right, followed by mild clonus of the left limb, lasting for 2–9.5 min. VEEG is a monitoring tool that integrates image, sound, and electroencephalogram (EEG), and plays an important role in epilepsy characterization, localization, stereotyping, and causation [10]. By synchronously recording clinical manifestation and EEG activity, and enabling repeated playback, VEEG facilitates comprehensive assessment of the seizure behavior and electrophysiology [11]. In this case, a neurophysiology technician performed neurophysiology diagnosis and operation, reading EEG activity combined with synchronized video recording to document the presence or absence of epileptic seizures, the beginning and end of each episode, and seizure control. Nurses communicated with the technician and the doctor at all times to dynamically adjust the dosage of antiepileptic drugs according to the evolution of the EEG, which substantially improved the therapeutic effect.

During active seizures, immediate nursing interventions involved keeping the child's airway open with the head tilted to one side, removing oral and nasal secretions to prevent asphyxia and aspiration, providing rapid oxygen inhalation to maintain airway patency and effective oxygenation, and closely monitoring changes in vital signs [12]. Rapid termination of epileptiform discharges with first‐line antiepileptic seizure medications was essential, followed by continued seizure medication after stabilizing vital signs. Epilepsy is one of the common chronic disorders of the central nervous system, and antiepileptic seizure medications are the most important and fundamental treatment and the first choice for epilepsy management [13]. The treatment course is long, and most patients require combinations of drugs. Drug dependence, drug resistance, and dose accumulation directly affect organ function and quality of life [14]. Therefore, during medication administration, therapeutic effects and drug side effects were strictly observed, especially liver and kidney function, respiration, skin, and sequelae.

In this case, the child was treated with midazolam pumping to control early convulsive seizures and then switched to oral anticonvulsant therapy with sodium valproate after the number of seizures decreased. However, the child's blood ammonia gradually increased during sodium valproate use. After specialist consultation, oral levetiracetam was initiated and the sodium valproate dosage was tapered. Several days later, the child's blood ammonia gradually returned to normal, showing a better therapeutic effect, and the number of convulsive seizures gradually decreased.

Complications affecting the prognosis of children with epilepsy can occur during treatment due to bed rest, heavy use of multiple antiepileptic and aesthetic drugs, and emergency interventions. Therefore, prevention and control of complications and adverse events are necessary in nursing care. According to consensus, respiratory depression, or hemodynamic instability can occur in 5.5%–42.2% of patients during antiepileptic drug administration, requiring intensive monitoring of respiratory rate, respiratory rhythm, oxygen saturation, arterial blood gases, blood pressure, hemodynamic, and other relevant indicators [15]. Patients with repeated convulsions are also prone to hypoxemia, hyperthermia, and cerebral oedema, which may increase intracranial pressure. Therefore, the head of the bed was elevated by 20°–30° during sedation, and the patient's pupil size, light reflex, and muscle tone were assessed every hour during periods of frequent convulsions.

Maintaining acid–base balance is also important. During a tonic–clonic seizure, the body secretes large amounts of catecholamines, causing continuous muscle contraction and apnoea, which produce large amounts of lactic acid and lead to lactic acidosis. Muscle contraction simultaneously increases body temperature and serum potassium levels and may interfere with respiratory muscle function, resulting in hypoxia and respiratory acidosis. In addition, elevated catecholamines raise blood glucose levels, and hyperglycaemia aggravates metabolic acidosis [16]. The child in this case also had acute bronchitis and was administered budesonide suspension via nebulization. Thirty minutes before breastfeeding, back percussion was performed to expel sputum, and suction was provided when phlegm sounds were detected, ensuring airway patency at all times to prevent asphyxiation during a seizure.

Skin Care

Mild baby shower gel was used to clean the child's skin once a day. To ensure optimal water temperature, two thermometers were used to verify accuracy, maintaining the temperature between 38°C and 42°C. Pigmented areas were gently pressed with a soaked cotton towel to avoid friction. Vascular lesion areas, such as nevus erythematosus, were cleaned by lightly circling with the fingertips to prevent rupture of fragile blood vessels. After cleansing, a 100% cotton gauze towel was used to pat the skin dry, rubbing was prohibited, and the skin was kept dry. Nurses selected soft cotton clothing for the child, changed the bedsheets and clothing daily, inspected and cleaned the bed unit during handovers, maintained skin cleanliness and dryness, and prevented contact with hard objects. To avoid injury, the infant's nails were trimmed weekly into a rounded contour, and cotton anti‐scratch gloves were provided.

The skin at the lesion sites was observed daily to promptly detect blisters, erosions, or color changes (e.g., purple, white, or darkening of pigmented spots) and to monitor for vascular malformations with bulging surfaces or elevated temperature. In addition, Zafar et al. [17] reported that patients undergoing VEEG may experience moderate to severe skin irritation, with an electrode‐related discharge bleeding rate of 19.1%. This may be associated with prolonged VEEG monitoring or irritation from skin preparation media or electrolyte paste. Therefore, we checked the skin integrity and pressure at the electrode contact sites every shift, and any skin damage was treated promptly.

Nutritional Management

Nutritional support was essential, particularly during antiepileptic drug therapy. The nursing team conducted a comprehensive nutritional assessment, reviewing growth trends based on medical history and regularly monitoring weight and height. Nutrition‐related biochemical indices were evaluated to identify potential deficiencies, and the amount of milk and feeding intervals were continuously adjusted according to the child's gastric residuals.

The child's feeding and excretory status were carefully observed and documented to ensure adequate nutritional intake. A marked reduction in milk consumption occurred on the day antiepileptic medications were administered. Assessment using the Neonatal Oral Motor Function Score Scale (NOMAS) [18] revealed sucking disorders, characterized by no response to lip stimulation with the pacifier and only transient response to shaking feeding–bottle stimulation. Consequently, a nasogastric tube was inserted to a depth of 22 cm, and 100 mL of milk was administered every 3 h to meet daily caloric requirements. As the child's condition stabilized, antiepileptic drugs were gradually reduced. After discontinuation of midazolam, nonnutritive sucking was provided 30 min before each feeding to train oral motor function. Once normal sucking returned, the nasogastric tube was removed and oral feeding was resumed. Intake and output were recorded accurately to maintain electrolyte balance and internal homeostasis. After transfer from the intensive care unit to the general ward, feeding instructions and techniques were taught to the family, and the importance of nutrition was explained to enhance their knowledge and caregiving ability.

During the 21st day of hospitalization, the infant's weight increased steadily, with a discharge weight 0.55 kg higher than at admission.

Intraocular Pressure Management

The child received regular pilocarpine and brinzolamine eye drops to reduce intraocular pressure. After instillation, pressure was applied to the lacrimal sac for 2 min to enhance ocular absorption and minimize systemic adverse effects. Nursing interventions focused on reducing triggers of intraocular pressure elevation, including violent crying, coughing, and forceful defecation. Intraocular pressure and visual acuity were monitored regularly. On days 16th and 20th of hospitalization, the infant developed decreased stool frequency and abdominal distension, which were managed with glycerol enemas to prevent straining during defecation. We also monitored for symptoms of acute intraocular pressure elevation, such as photophobia, frequent tearing, and corneal clouding.

Family and Social Support

The nursing team assessed the needs of the family to understand their emotional state, cognitive level, and understanding of the disease. Initially, the family overlooked the infant's epileptic symptoms, interpreting tetany and cyanosis as normal. To address this, we provided detailed explanations regarding the etiology, pathological mechanisms, treatment procedures, and expected outcomes of the disease. Medication principles and potential side effects were discussed, emphasizing the importance of early recognition of disease progression and adverse drug reactions.

A comprehensive assessment of family support was conducted, during which we listened to their concerns, identified sources of stress, and evaluated their coping capacity. The family faced financial strain and was unable to afford living expenses in the city. The charge nurse collaborated with a medical social worker to conduct a needs assessment and connect the family with a charitable foundation that provided temporary housing and special medical assistance. This support significantly reduced their economic and psychological burden. A trusting nurse–family relationship was established, encouraging questions, and providing guidance in managing negative emotions through relaxation techniques, positive thinking, and deep breathing exercises.

A contingency response plan was developed to help the family respond promptly to fluctuations in the child's condition. To ensure ongoing support, a structured post discharge follow‐up plan was created to evaluate treatment outcomes, while connections to additional support resources were provided. During follow‐up, the family reported substantial improvement in their caregiving ability. Through these comprehensive measures, the nursing team successfully supported both the child and family in coping with the illness, enhancing quality of life, and promoting effective treatment.

Conclusions

This study established a multidisciplinary, dynamic consultation mechanism involving pediatrics, neurology, ophthalmology, and dermatology; implemented individualized precision medical management; and successfully improved the prognosis of a child with PPV complicated by multisystem involvement. Based on dynamic assessment of the condition, the nursing team used VEEG monitoring to track seizure frequency and manifestations in real time; implemented innovative strategies to protect skin lesion areas and monitor electrode‐related skin injury; and built a nasogastric‐to‐oral nutritional support system supplemented with oral motor training to facilitate steady weight gain. In parallel, integrated “medical–social–psychological” care was provided, including economic support through charitable funding, embodying patient‐centered humanistic care. This case report provides a replicable practical model for the elaborate management of children with complex PPV.
