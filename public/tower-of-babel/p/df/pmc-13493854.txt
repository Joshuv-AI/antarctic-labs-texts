Aging and aggregation enhance antibiotics persistence and sequestration on polyethylene microplastics
Authors: K. Shashikala, V. Sudheesh, J. Aswathi, J. Sharanya, Deepa Janardanan, Suja P. Devipriya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Microplastics (MPs) are increasingly recognised as chemically active interfaces that influence the environmental fate, transport, and persistence of coexisting contaminants. However, the molecular mechanisms governing antibiotic adsorption on environmentally transformed microplastics remain poorly understood. In this study, molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were employed to investigate the adsorption behaviour of the third-generation cephalosporin antibiotic cefixime on polyethylene (PE) microplastics, explicitly considering the effect of polymer aggregation and environmental aging. Pristine single-chain PE exhibited weak and reversible cefixime adsorption dominated by van der Waals interactions, resulting in high molecular mobility and frequent desorption events. In contrast, polymer aggregation substantially enhanced antibiotic retention by creating confined inter-chain domains that amplify cooperative dispersion interactions and reduced solvent competition at the polymer-water surface. Environmental aging further strengthened adsorption through the introduction of oxygen-containing functional groups capable of electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding with polar moieties of cefixime. These structural and chemical modifications collectively reduced molecular fluctuations, promoted the formation of stable binding sites, suppressed molecular diffusion, and significantly increased interaction energies. The findings demonstrate that environmental transformation of polyethylene microplastics enhance molecular adsorption of cefixime on polyethylene, with potential implications for contaminant transport, persistence, and ecological exposure in aquatic systems. The results provide mechanistic insight into the role of polymer ageing and aggregation in antibiotic adsorption and contribute to the understanding of antibiotic microplastic interactions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-62174-2.

Introduction

Plastic materials have become indispensable in modern society owing to their low cost, durability, chemical resistance, and versatile industrial applications1. However, the extensive production and inadequate disposal of plastics have resulted in the continuous accumulation of plastic debris across terrestrial and aquatic environments2. Environmental weathering processes, including photooxidation, hydrolysis, thermal degradation, and mechanical abrasion, progressively fragment larger plastics into microplastics (MPs), typically defined as particles smaller than 5 mm3,4. Due to their persistence, mobility, and resistance to complete degradation, MPs are now recognised as globally pervasive pollutants distributed throughout marine and freshwater systems, sediments, soils, wastewater, atmospheric compartments, and even food products5,6.

The ecological significance of MPs extends beyond their physical presence in the environment6. Owing to their high surface-area-to-volume ratio, hydrophobicity, and heterogeneous surface chemistry, MPs can act as chemically active interfaces capable of interacting with dissolved contaminants7,8. These interactions may alter contaminant transport, partitioning, persistence, and bioavailability within aquatic systems. Consequently, MPs are increasingly considered not only as particulate pollutants but also as vectors that facilitate the enrichment and transfer of hazardous chemicals through aquatic food webs9,10. Such interactions become particularly important in environments where multiple anthropogenic stressors coexist11,12.

Among emerging aquatic contaminants, antibiotics have received increasing attention due to their extensive usage in human medicine, veterinary applications, and aquaculture13. A substantial fraction of administered antibiotics is excreted either unmetabolised or as biologically active metabolites, ultimately entering aquatic environments through wastewater effluents, hospital discharges, agricultural runoff, and aquaculture activities14,15. Persistent low-level antibiotic contamination can disrupt microbial community structure and accelerate the development and dissemination of antimicrobial resistance (AMR), posing significant environmental and public-health concerns. The co-occurrence of MPs and antibiotics in aquatic systems therefore represents a compounded environmental risk with potentially important implications for contaminant persistence and microbial exposure11,16.

Recent studies suggest that MPs can serve as adsorption platforms for antibiotics, thereby modifying their environmental fate and transport behaviour17–19. Adsorption onto MP surfaces may reduce contaminant degradation, prolong environmental residence times, and create localised hotspots of contaminant accumulation19. In addition, antibiotic enrichment within microplastic-associated biofilms (the plastisphere) may facilitate prolonged microbial exposure and potentially intensify selective pressure for antibiotic resistance20,21. Understanding the molecular-scale interactions governing antibiotic adsorption on MPs is therefore critical for predicting contaminant behaviour and associated ecological risks in aquatic ecosystems.

Polyethylene (PE) is among the most abundant and environmentally prevalent plastic polymers and constitutes a major fraction of global microplastic pollution22. Environmental aging processes substantially modify the physicochemical properties of PE microplastics through oxidation, surface roughening, and the introduction of oxygen-containing functional groups. These transformations alter surface polarity, hydrophobicity, and adsorption behaviour toward organic contaminants18,23. Despite growing interest in MP-contaminant interactions, many previous experimental and computational investigations have relied on structurally simplified models that treat MPs as chemically pristine and structurally homogeneous, thereby overlooking environmentally realistic features such as polymer aggregation and aging-induced surface heterogeneity18,24,25.

Molecular dynamics (MD) simulations have emerged as powerful tools for resolving contaminant-microplastic interactions at atomistic resolution26,27. Previous MD studies have demonstrated that adsorption behaviour depends strongly on polymer chemistry, antibiotic speciation, environmental conditions, and intermolecular interactions including hydrophobic forces, van der Waals interactions, hydrogen bonding, and electrostatic effects28,29. For example, aromatic polymers such as polystyrene exhibit strong affinity toward antibiotics through π–π interactions, whereas aged polymers containing oxygenated functional groups often display enhanced adsorption of polar contaminants through hydrogen bonding and electrostatic attraction29. Nevertheless, the combined effects of polymer aggregation and environmental aging on antibiotic adsorption dynamics remain insufficiently understood, particularly for polyethylene microplastics.

Cefixime, a third-generation30–32 cephalosporin antibiotic widely used in human and veterinary medicine, represents an environmentally relevant emerging contaminant frequently detected in wastewater and aquaculture systems32. The molecule contains multiple polar and ionizable functional groups that can interact strongly with oxidised polymer surfaces, making it an ideal model compound for investigating adsorption processes on environmentally transformed MPs31. Understanding the adsorption behaviour of cefixime on PE microplastics is therefore important for assessing the role of MPs in antibiotic persistence and transport within aquatic environments.

In this study, molecular dynamics simulations were employed to systematically investigate the adsorption behaviour of cefixime on polyethylene microplastics by explicitly incorporating environmentally relevant processes of polymer aggregation and surface aging. Pristine and aged single- and dual-chain PE models were constructed to represent structural and chemical heterogeneity commonly observed in environmental MPs. Adsorption stability and molecular persistence were evaluated using root mean square deviation (RMSD), radial distribution functions (RDF), mean squared displacement (MSD), hydrogen-bond analysis, and interaction energy analysis.

By integrating the effects of polymer aggregation, aging induced surface oxidation, and molecular scale interactions, this study elucidates the fundamental mechanisms governing antibiotic adsorption on environmentally transformed polyethylene microplastics. The results identify the roles of polymer aggregation and oxidation in antibiotic polymer interactions and provide a molecular basis for understanding contaminant adsorption at polyethylene surfaces. The study is intended to identify fundamental interaction mechanisms rather than directly predict contaminant behaviour in complex environmental systems.

Materials and methods

Molecular models and system setup

Polyethylene (PE) microplastics were employed as model polymeric carriers due to their environmental prevalence and documented role in contaminant transport. The present atomistic models represent simplified molecular-scale analogues of PE microplastics rather than experimentally resolved micron-sized particles. Both pristine PE and aged PE models were constructed as representative molecular models. Each PE chain consisted of 50 repeating units, ensuring a balance between realistic polymer representation and computational efficiency. To investigate the influence of environmental weathering, both pristine and aged PE systems were considered. The use of 10 cefixime molecules, exceeding concentrations typically encountered in natural aquatic systems, was necessary to ensure adequate sampling of adsorption events within the computationally accessible molecular dynamics timescale. The primary objective of this study was to elucidate the fundamental molecular mechanisms governing cefixime adsorption on polyethylene rather than to reproduce environmental exposure conditions. Likewise, the use of a single 50 unit polyethylene chain represents a computationally efficient model that captures the essential features of the adsorption process while maintaining tractable simulation costs. Aging was simulated through the incorporation of oxygen-containing functional groups into the polymer framework in order to mimic oxidative transformation processes occurring under environmental exposure. Specifically, two terminal carboxylic acid (–COOH) groups, two carbonyl (C=O) groups, and one hydroxyl (–OH) group were introduced into the polymer structure, corresponding to an oxidation level of approximately 10%. The functional groups were distributed along the polymer chain to generate heterogeneous polar domains characteristic of oxidatively aged polyethylene surfaces29.

To simulate environmentally relevant antibiotic loading, ten cefixime (CEF) molecules were included in each system. Cefixime (CEF) was employed as a representative β-lactam antibiotic because of its widespread environmental occurrence and persistence in aquatic systems. Ten CEF molecules were introduced into each simulation box to model concentrated local exposure conditions relevant to contaminated aquatic environments. Under neutral aqueous conditions, cefixime was simulated in its predominant zwitterionic form, where the carboxyl group was treated in its partially deprotonated form, while the amino functionality is partially protonated. As a result, the molecule possesses both positive and negative charges but maintains an overall electrically neutrality.31,33.

All systems were assembled in cubic simulation boxes (12 × 12 × 12 nm3) under periodic boundary conditions and solvated with explicit SPC/E water. Initial configurations positioned the PE centrally within the simulation box, while CEF molecules were randomly distributed in the aqueous phase to avoid artificial bias towards pre-adsorbed configurations. This setup represents plausible encounters between microplastics and antibiotics in aquatic environments.

Molecular dynamics simulations

All molecular dynamics simulations were performed using GROMACS version 2023.334 with the OPLS-AA force field35. Long-range electrostatic interactions were treated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, while van der Waals interactions were described using a cutoff based scheme36. All bonds involving hydrogen atoms were constrained using the LINCS algorithm37. Energy minimisation was carried out using the steepest-descent algorithm to remove unfavourable contacts.

Following minimisation, systems were equilibrated under constant volume and temperature (NVT) conditions for 1 ns at 300 K using a 2 fs timestep and the velocity-rescale thermostat38. During NVT equilibration. Subsequently, NPT equilibration under constant temperature and pressure (NPT) conditions was performed for 8 ns using the Parrinello–Rahman barostat at 1 bar39. Production simulations were then conducted for 75 ns under NPT conditions.

Marine environments were simulated explicitly through the introduction of Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions at a concentration of 0.6 mol L⁻1, corresponding to the average ionic strength associated with seawater salinity (~ 3.5%). The ions were distributed uniformly throughout the simulation box to evaluate ion-mediated interactions, and salinity-dependent adsorption behaviour40.

A cutoff distance of 1.2 nm was applied for both Coulombic and van der Waals interactions, with long-range dispersion corrections applied to energy and pressure. Trajectory data were collected at regular intervals for post-processing analysis. Root mean square deviation (RMSD), radial distribution functions (RDF), mean squared displacement (MSD), hydrogen-bond dynamics, and nonbonded interaction energies were evaluated over equilibrated trajectories to quantify adsorption stability and interaction mechanisms41. These metrics directly find the role of pristine and aged microplastics as persistent antibiotic reservoirs, with implications for prolonged environmental exposure and AMR risk amplification. Trajectory visualization inspection was performed using VMD42.

Terminology

In this study, PE-1 denotes the single-chain pristine polyethylene-cefixime complex simulated under freshwater conditions, while PE-1 M represents the corresponding single-chain pristine system under marine conditions with explicit ions. PE-2 refers to the double-chain pristine PE-cefixime complex in freshwater, capturing the effects of polymer aggregation, whereas PE-2 M denotes the same aggregated pristine system under marine conditions. PE-A1 represents the single-chain aged polyethylene-cefixime complex in freshwater, in which aging introduces surface polarity and heterogeneity, while PE-A1M corresponds to this aged single-chain system under marine conditions. Finally, PE-A2 denotes the double-chain aged PE-cefixime complex in freshwater, and PE-A2M represents the aged double-chain system under marine conditions, incorporating the combined effects of polymer aging, aggregation, and salinity.

Quantum-chemical calculations

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations and reduced density gradient (RDG) analyses were performed on representative reduced models to complement the molecular dynamics simulations. While molecular dynamics captures collective and aggregation-dependent adsorption behaviour, the DFT approach elucidates the underlying local noncovalent interaction mechanisms. The results of the DFT and RDG analyses are provided in the Supporting Information.

Results and discussion

Polyethylene (PE) microplastics act as vectors for cefixime and therefore represents a primary control on antibiotic enrichment and persistence in aquatic environments. Molecular simulations were used to quantify cefixime adsorption on pristine and aged PE (PE-A) microplastics under freshwater and marine conditions, while resolving the mechanistic roles of polymer structural configuration, surface chemistry, and aqueous composition. Influence of polymer structural configuration on cefixime adsorption

The number of cefixime molecules adsorbed on PE microplastics shows a pronounced dependence on polymer aging, chain aggregation, and aqueous environment, reflecting the way in which structural and environmental factors jointly regulate sorption capacity. The number of adsorbed cefixime molecules was determined through trajectory visualisation and interaction analysis using VMD. A cefixime molecule was considered adsorbed when it remained consistently associated with the polyethylene surface throughout a significant portion of the simulation trajectory and exhibited persistent intermolecular interactions with the polymer surface, rather than undergoing short-lived or random collision events in the aqueous phase. In single-chain systems, pristine PE-1 adsorbs up to five cefixime molecules under freshwater conditions, whereas adsorption decreases under marine conditions, indicating that dissolved ions partially screen van der Waals interactions and limit close contact between cefixime and the hydrophobic polymer surface. In contrast, aged single-chain PE-A1 exhibits higher adsorption, reaching up to seven cefixime molecules in freshwater, despite showing some reduction in marine water. Whereas pristine double-chain PE-2 adsorbs seven cefixime molecules in freshwater and six in marine conditions, aged double-chain PE-A2 displays the highest sorption capacity overall, accommodating up to eight cefixime molecules. Notably, unlike in single-chain systems, marine conditions enhance adsorption in double-chain models for both pristine and aged MPs. This behaviour suggests that polymer-polymer proximity generates confined hydrophobic regions that stabilise cefixime through cumulative dispersion interactions, even in the presence of salts.

Enhancement of adsorption by polymer aging

Polymer aging exerts the strongest enhancement effect on cefixime adsorption by simultaneously increasing adsorption capacity and altering the dominant retention mechanism. Under both freshwater and marine conditions, aged PE systems consistently accommodate more cefixime molecules than their pristine counterparts. Oxidative aging introduces oxygen-containing functional groups, including hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl moieties, which enable hydrogen bonding and dipole-dipole interactions with amide, carboxylate, and heterocyclic nitrogen functionalities of cefixime. These interactions provide stable interaction points that substantially suppress desorption, while hydrophobic regions of the polymer continue to contribute dispersion stabilisation, yielding a mixed and highly persistent binding regime. Representative snapshots for the penetration of antibiotics during the adsorption of cefixime on aged polyethylene single-chain MP system are depicted in Fig. 3.Fig. 3Representative snapshots during the adsorption of pristine and aged polyethylene single-chain MP system with cefixime.

Representative snapshots during the adsorption of pristine and aged polyethylene single-chain MP system with cefixime.

Beyond chemical affinity, aging induces surface roughness, micro-void formation, and pore-like features that allow cefixime molecules to access confined interfacial regions and partially embedded adsorption domains rather than remaining confined to the external surface. This internalisation leads to physical confinement, reduced solvent exposure, and restricted molecular mobility, further stabilising the adsorbed antibiotic molecule. The combined effects of polymer aggregation and surface oxidation produces a synergistic enhancement of adsorption capacity, with aged double chain PE systems exhibiting the highest cefixime adsorption by coupling chemical interactions with nanoscale confinement. The observed increase in adsorption stability suggests that oxidation induced surface functionalisation can substantially modify antibiotic-microplastics interactions at the molecular scale. The results demonstrate that oxygen-containing functional groups create additional interaction sites that promote stronger association between cefixime and polyethylene relative to pristine polymer surfaces.

Enhancement of adsorption by MP aggregation

The enhanced adsorption behaviour observed in the double-chain polyethene (PE) system relative to the single-chain configuration can be attributed to the increased hydrophobic interaction environment provided by the presence of adjacent polymer chains under aqueous conditions. In the single-chain system, the isolated PE chain offers a comparatively limited contact surface for interaction with cefixime molecules. In contrast, the double chain configuration provides a larger effective interaction region and promotes stronger van der Waals interactions between the polymer surface and cefixime molecules. The proximity of neighbouring PE chains also creates more favourable local environments that can stabilise cefixime molecules near the polymer surface. In addition, the reduced exposure of the interfacial region to surrounding water molecules further favours hydrophobic association between cefixime and the PE surface. Collectively, these factors contribute to the enhanced adsorption affinity observed in the double-chain PE system under aqueous conditions.

Structural stability and adsorption persistence: RMSD analysis

Root Mean Square Deviation (RMSD) analysis was used to evaluate the structural stability of cefixime associated with PE microplastics and to quantify adsorption persistence under different polymer configurations and surface conditions (Fig. 4).Fig. 4RMSD profiles illustrating the stability of cefixime adsorbed PE under different conditions.

RMSD profiles illustrating the stability of cefixime adsorbed PE under different conditions.

In pristine single-chain PE systems, cefixime exhibits large and highly fluctuating RMSD values throughout the simulation. The absence of polar functional groups on pristine PE prevents directional binding, allowing the dominance of solvent competition. Under marine conditions, RMSD amplitudes further increase due to ionic screening and enhanced hydration of cefixime, which stabilise the antibiotic in solution and promote detachment from the polymer surface. These observations confirm that pristine, isolated PE chains provide minimal stabilisation towards sustained antibiotic adsorption.

In contrast, double chain PE systems displayed comparatively lower RMSD values and narrower fluctuation ranges than single chain systems, indicating reduced structural deviations during the simulation period. The proximity between adjacent polymer chains may create confined local environments that limit cefixime mobility near the polymer surface and promote cooperative nonbonded interactions. Reduced solvent accessibility around the interfacial region may also contribute to the observed decrease in structural fluctuations.

Aged PE systems exhibited comparatively lower RMSD values among the investigated configurations, suggesting reduced molecular motion relative to pristine PE systems. The introduction of oxygen-containing functional groups on the aged PE surface provides additional interaction sites that can participate in hydrogen bonding and dipole-mediated interactions with cefixime. These additional interactions may contribute to the observed reduction in structural fluctuations. In aged double-chain PE models, the combined influence of surface oxidation and interchain proximity resulted in the lowest RMSD variations observed during the simulations.

Overall, the RMSD results suggest that polymer aging and double chain PE configurations influence the structural behaviour of cefixime at polyethylene surfaces by reducing molecular fluctuations and deviations relative to pristine single chain systems.

Spatial organisation and binding-site specificity: radial distribution function (RDF) analysis

Radial distribution function (RDF) analysis was performed to resolve the spatial relationship between cefixime and PE- MPs at atomic resolution and to identify preferential binding sites governing adsorption stability (Fig. 5). RDF profiles provide quantitative insight into interaction distance, coordination strength, and the nature of antibiotic-polymer association beyond bulk adsorption metrics.Fig. 5RDF profiles characterising cefixime-polyethylene interactions in the studied systems.

RDF profiles characterising cefixime-polyethylene interactions in the studied systems.

In pristine single-chain PE systems, RDF profiles display weak, broad peaks at distances greater than 0.45 nm, indicative of non-specific, dispersion-dominated contacts. The absence of sharp first-shell maxima suggests that cefixime does not occupy well-defined adsorption sites but instead samples a diffuse interfacial region governed primarily by transient van der Waals interactions. This spatially diffuse association, consistent with the large RMSD fluctuations observed, confirms that pristine isolated PE chains provide limited structural guidance for antibiotic retention. RDF profiles characterising cefixime-PE interactions are depicted in Fig. 5.

Double-chain PE configurations exhibit pronounced first RDF peaks centered at shorter distances, reflecting enhanced spatial confinement and cooperative interactions from adjacent polymer chains. These peaks indicate preferential localisation of cefixime, where simultaneous dispersion interactions from multiple polymer segments stabilise the antibiotic. The narrowing of the first coordination shell implies reduced configurational entropy and restricted molecular mobility, explaining the lower RMSD values and increased adsorption capacity observed in aggregated systems.

Aged PE systems show the most distinct and chemically meaningful RDF features. They exhibit sharp, high-intensity peaks between cefixime heteroatoms (O and N) and oxygenated surface groups on the aged polymer surface. These distances are characteristic of hydrogen bonding and strong dipole–dipole interactions, confirming the formation of specific binding sites rather than nonspecific surface association. RDF peaks of double chains further indicate structured interfacial organisations, suggesting that aged surfaces promote ordered antibiotic-water-polymer interactions.

The RDF analysis reveals a clear transition from weak, distance-limited dispersion interactions in pristine MPs to chemically enhanced adsorption on aged surfaces. The coexistence of sharp RDF peaks and reduced RMSD in aged double-chain systems demonstrates that antibiotic adsorption arises from a synergy between surface chemistry and polymer aggregation. Moreover, the aged single-chain system displays pronounced RDF sharpening in freshwater, whereas this enhanced short-range ordering shifts to marine conditions upon chain duplication, suggesting that polymer aggregation alters the balance between hydration and ionic screening effects. Overall, these results demonstrate that aging-induced surface functionalization and polymer aggregation promote stronger and more ordered cefixime-polyethylene interactions, providing molecular level insight into the mechanisms governing antibiotic adsorption on polyethylene microplastics. Whether these enhanced molecular interactions translate into prolonged environmental residence times, altered contaminant transport, or influence antimicrobial resistance development in natural systems remains to be established through experimental investigations and multiscale modelling.

Impact of solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) on cefixime adsorption

The temporal evolution of solvent-accessible surface area (SASA) for cefixime in interaction with pristine and aged PE microplastics under freshwater and marine conditions is presented in Fig. 6. Variations in SASA directly reflect changes in solvent exposure associated with polymer-antibiotic association, surface coverage, and confinement effects.Fig. 6SASA profiles of cefixime in the studied systems.

SASA profiles of cefixime in the studied systems.

In pristine single-chain PE systems, cefixime maintains relatively high SASA values throughout the simulation, indicating persistent solvent exposure and weak, transient surface association. Minor SASA fluctuations correspond to short-lived adsorption events that are readily reversed by solvent competition, particularly under marine conditions where enhanced hydration and ionic effects further stabilise cefixime in solution. These trends are consistent with surface-limited adsorption dominated by dispersion interactions.

In contrast, double-chain and aged PE systems show a sustained reduction in cefixime SASA (Fig. 6), indicating progressive surface shielding and reduced solvent accessibility. In double-chain configurations, aggregation creates inter-chain domains that partially isolate cefixime from bulk water, leading to lower SASA values and increased residence times. Aged PE systems exhibited the most pronounced reduction in SASA values, indicating decreased solvent exposure of cefixime in the presence of oxidised polymer surfaces. This observation may arise from the combined influence of surface oxidation and enhanced intermolecular interactions between cefixime and oxygen-containing functional groups introduced during the aging process. The reduced SASA values suggest a closer association of cefixime with the polymer interface under aqueous conditions. However, SASA analysis primarily reflects variations in solvent accessibility and does not directly demonstrate penetration of cefixime into the polymer matrix.

Molecular mobility and adsorption dynamics: mean squared displacement (MSD) analysis

Mean squared displacement (MSD) analysis was performed to quantify the translational mobility of cefixime in the presence of PE microplastics and to assess polymer configuration and surface aging regulation on antibiotic diffusion and adsorption (Fig. 7). MSD provides a direct measure of molecular freedom and complements RMSD and RDF by linking spatial confinement to dynamic behaviour.Fig. 7MSD profiles of cefixime in studied systems.

MSD profiles of cefixime in studied systems.

In pristine single-chain PE systems, cefixime exhibits near-linear MSD growth with time, indicative of quasi-free diffusion in the aqueous phase. Although transient surface contacts occur, the absence of well-defined RDF peaks and specific binding sites allows cefixime to rapidly escape the polymer interface. This high mobility confirms that adsorption in pristine isolated PE systems is weak and reversible, consistent with diffuse RDF profiles and large RMSD fluctuations.

Double-chain PE systems display a marked reduction in MSD slope, reflecting constrained diffusion upon adsorption. Once cefixime localises within the inter-chain regions identified by sharp RDF first-shell peaks, its translational motion becomes sterically restricted. Cooperative confinement imposed by adjacent polymer chains limits lateral diffusion and solvent-mediated escape pathways, translating spatial confinement into reduced molecular mobility.

Aged PE systems exhibit the most pronounced diffusion suppression, with MSD curves approaching plateaus over extended simulation times. This behaviour indicates long-lived immobilisation of cefixime on the polymer surface. The near-zero MSD slopes coincide with sharp RDF peaks, confirming that chemical interactions such as hydrogen bonding govern adsorption. Hydrogen bonding and dipole-dipole interactions introduce energetic barriers to diffusion, effectively trapping cefixime at specific adsorption sites.

The systematic coupling between RDF peak intensity and MSD suppression reveals that stronger, shorter-distance interactions produce lower diffusion coefficients and longer residence times. In aged double-chain systems, the combined effects of chemical interaction and geometric confinement yield the most pronounced antibiotic immobilisation.

Reduced diffusion coefficients observed in aged and aggregated systems indicate greater adsorption stability and lower molecular mobility at the polyethylene interface. These results further support that polymer oxidation enhances antibiotic adsorption behaviour at the molecular scale.

Hydrogen bonding interactions between cefixime and polyethylene microplastics

Hydrogen bond analysis was performed to further elucidate the role of specific directional interactions in stabilising cefixime adsorption on microplastics (Fig. 8). Hydrogen bonding is particularly relevant for understanding adsorption persistence on aged polymer surfaces, where chemical functionality is introduced through environmental weathering.Fig. 8Time evolution of hydrogen bonds between cefixime and aged polyethylene microplastics.

Time evolution of hydrogen bonds between cefixime and aged polyethylene microplastics.

In pristine polyethylene systems, hydrogen bond formation between cefixime and the polymer surface is negligible. Pristine PE lacks heteroatoms and functional groups capable of acting as hydrogen bond donors or acceptors, and therefore cannot directly participate in hydrogen bonding with the antibiotic. Any transient hydrogen bonds observed arise from water-mediated interactions rather than direct polymer-antibiotic contacts. As a result, the number of hydrogen bonds remains close to zero throughout the simulation, consistent with weak adsorption, high molecular mobility, and rapid desorption observed in RMSD and MSD analyses.

In contrast, aged polyethylene systems show a pronounced increase in hydrogen bond formation with cefixime. Environmental aging introduces oxygen-containing functional groups such as hydroxyl, carbonyl, and carboxyl moieties, which serve as effective hydrogen bond donors and acceptors. Cefixime contains multiple polar functional groups, including amide, carboxylate, and heterocyclic nitrogen atoms, enabling the formation of stable hydrogen bonds with oxidised polymer surfaces. The number of hydrogen bonds increases markedly in aged systems and remains persistent over time, indicating long-lived and directional interactions.

Double-chain configurations further enhance hydrogen bonding stability. Polymer aggregation creates confined inter-chain regions that restrict solvent access and orient functional groups favourably toward cefixime, increasing both hydrogen bond frequency and lifetime. Reduced competition from surrounding water molecules promotes repeated and sustained hydrogen bond formation, contributing to prolonged antibiotic adsorption.

The presence of stable hydrogen bonding correlates directly with sharper RDF peaks at short distances, reduced RMSD fluctuations, and suppressed diffusion observed in MSD analysis. Hydrogen bonds, therefore act as stabilising interactions that complement dispersion and electrostatic forces, transforming adsorption from a weak and transient process on pristine PE into a chemically stabilised association on aged and aggregated microplastics.

The interaction energy reveals that cefixime adsorption at polyethylene microplastic interfaces is governed predominantly by van der Waals interactions, while the aged systems additionally exhibit appreciable attractive electrostatic contributions that further stabilise the adsorption complexes. For pristine PE-1, the strongly negative total interaction energy (− 213.9 kJ mol⁻1) closely follows the vdW component (− 221.1 kJ mol⁻1), while the electrostatic term is small and electrostatic contributions were comparatively weak. This indicates that adsorption is driven primarily by non-specific dispersion interactions associated with hydrophobic contact between cefixime and the polymer surface. Upon transition to marine conditions (PE–M), the marked reduction in both total and vdW interaction energies reflects ionic screening and enhanced hydration, which collectively weaken close-range polymer-antibiotic interactions and diminish adsorption strength.

Aggregation of polymer chains substantially enhances cefixime binding. In pristine PE, the total interaction energy nearly doubles relative to the single-chain system (− 397.7 kJ mol⁻1), with vdW interactions remaining the dominant stabilising force. This strengthening arises from increased contact area and cooperative dispersion interactions. Notably, marine conditions further intensify binding in the double-chain system (PE-2 M), indicating that interchain confinement stabilises cefixime through cumulative hydrophobic interactions that are less susceptible to salt-induced disruption than surface-exposed binding in isolated chains.

Polymer aging introduces a pronounced qualitative shift in interaction mechanisms. In aged single-chain PE-A1, adsorption remains strongly favorable (− 293.7 kJ mol⁻1), but the electrostatic contribution becomes significantly attractive, reflecting the presence of oxidised functional groups capable of engaging in hydrogen bonding and dipole-mediated interactions. However, under marine conditions (PE-A1M), both total interaction energy and electrostatic stabilization are substantially reduced, indicating effective screening of polar interactions by dissolved ions and competition from hydration shells.

The strongest overall stabilization is observed in the aged double-chain system under marine conditions (PE-A2M), which exhibits the most negative interaction energy (− 436.0 kJ mol⁻1). In this case, vdW interactions are complemented by substantial electrostatic contributions, highlighting a synergistic effect of aging-induced polarity and polymer aggregation. The confined interchain environment in aged aggregates appears to shield both dispersion and electrostatic interactions from ionic interference, thereby promoting exceptionally stable adsorption. Collectively, these findings establish that while vdW forces dominate cefixime adsorption across all systems, polymer aging and aggregation introduce additional electrostatic stabilization that markedly enhances retention, particularly in saline environments.

In aged double-chain polyethylene systems, van der Waals and electrostatic interactions act synergistically. Polymer aggregation amplifies dispersion forces, while aging-induced functional groups introduce strong electrostatic stabilization. This combined effect produces the energy minima observed across all systems, corresponding to highly stable and long-lived adsorption complexes. The energetic landscape in these systems strongly predicts adsorption trends and explains the suppressed diffusion and structural stabilization observed in RMSD, RDF, and MSD analyses. Overall, interaction energy reveals a clear mechanistic transition from weak, dispersion-dominated adsorption on pristine isolated polymers to chemically stabilised, high-affinity adsorption on aged and aggregated polyethylene microplastics.

Environmental implications of cefixime on polyethylene microplastics

The molecular dynamics simulations performed in this study indicate that aged polyethylene (PE) microplastics exhibit a greater affinity for cefixime than pristine polyethylene under the simplified atomistic models considered. Enhanced interaction energies and reduced molecular mobility suggest that aged polyethylene surfaces provide more stable adsorption environments.

Surface oxidation increased the adsorption affinity of polyethylene through the introduction of oxygen containing functional groups capable of hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions. In addition, the double chain polymer system generated confined interchain regions that promoted cooperative nonbonded interactions and reduced solvent accessibility at the adsorption interface, resulting in enhanced stabilization of cefixime at the polymer surface. These observations indicate that surface oxidation influences antibiotic adsorption at the molecular level.

The enhanced adsorption observed for aged polyethylene suggests that weathering may modify antibiotic microplastic interactions in aquatic environments. Such changes may influence the local distribution and availability of antibiotics at microplastic surfaces, with potential implications for contaminant behaviour in aquatic systems.

Overall, the findings provide mechanistic insight into cefixime adsorption on polyethylene microplastics. Although the study is based on simplified atomistic models and does not capture the full complexity of natural systems, it contributes to the molecular level understanding of antibiotic polymer interactions and provides a basis for future experimental and computational investigations.

Conclusions

In this study, molecular dynamics simulations were employed to investigate the adsorption behaviour of cefixime on pristine and aged polyethylene (PE) microplastics under freshwater and marine conditions. The results demonstrate that pristine PE exhibits weak and reversible interactions with cefixime, primarily governed by van der Waals forces, resulting in limited adsorption stability and high molecular mobility. In contrast, polymer aggregation significantly enhances cefixime retention by creating confined inter-chain environments that strengthen cooperative dispersion interactions and reduce solvent competition at the polymer surface. Environmental aging further promotes adsorption through the introduction of oxygen-containing functional groups, facilitating electrostatic interactions and hydrogen bonding with cefixime molecules.

Although the present study is based on simplified atomistic models, it provides valuable molecular level insights into the fundamental mechanisms governing cefixime adsorption on polyethylene microplastics. The computational framework enables the systematic investigation of the individual effects of polymer oxidation and particle aggregation, thereby revealing structure property relationships that are challenging to isolate through experimental approaches alone. While the models capture the essential physicochemical features underlying antibiotic polymer interactions, they do not fully represent the complexity of naturally weathered microplastics, including heterogeneous oxidation patterns, semicrystalline morphology, particle scale heterogeneity, biofilm development, and environmentally realistic contaminant concentrations and exposure conditions.

Accordingly, the quantitative adsorption behaviour reported here should not be directly extrapolated to predict contaminant persistence, transport, ecological exposure, or antimicrobial resistance development in natural environments. Rather, this study establishes a robust molecular level mechanistic framework for understanding how polymer oxidation and aggregation govern antibiotic adsorption. These findings provide a sound theoretical basis for interpreting experimental observations, guiding the development of environmentally representative microplastic models, and advancing multiscale investigations of contaminant microplastic interactions. Future studies integrating laboratory adsorption experiments, structurally realistic weathered microplastic models, and extended timescale simulations will be essential to validate the proposed mechanisms and assess their environmental relevance.
