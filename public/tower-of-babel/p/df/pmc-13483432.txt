Energy intensity, offshoring and the illusion of decarbonization
Authors: Claudia Amadei, Cesare Dosi, Francesco Jacopo Pintus
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Declines in the energy intensity of national gross domestic product cannot be simply taken as evidence of a country’s contribution to global decarbonization, notably when they come from structural changes that relocate energy-intensive production abroad. Here we analyze the role of offshoring in shaping energy intensity trends in a panel of 15 countries of the Organization for Economic Co-operation and Development between 1970 and 2021. Using both a decomposition analysis and a structural econometric model, we show that shifts in the composition of national output not mirrored by equivalent changes in domestic consumption patterns significantly and persistently reduce national energy intensity. These findings support the need to move beyond production-based climate metrics and to incorporate global supply chains for a more reliable assessment of national decarbonization pathways.

Introduction

Over the past decades, world energy-related carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions have steadily increased, albeit at a lower rate than gross domestic product (GDP)1,2. A key indicator of the decoupling between emissions and economic growth is the carbon intensity of GDP (i.e., the amount of emissions per unit of economic output). Globally, its decline has been primarily driven by reduced energy intensity of GDP, while the carbon intensity of energy—a measure of how carbon intensive the fuel mix is—has only played a marginal role (Fig. 1). However, while at the global level it is quite straightforward that the declines in the carbon intensity of GDP and in the energy intensity are closely tied, such a relationship cannot be readily inferred across individual countries. Generally speaking, energy intensity reductions can be achieved through structural shifts towards less energy-intensive activities (structural effect) or through technological advances driving down the amount of energy used per unit of economic output (sectoral effect)3–5. Although both processes bring down the national energy intensity, for the former, the consequences in terms of global carbon mitigation vary, depending on whether structural changes are mirrored by equivalent changes in domestic consumption patterns. If that does not happen, a reduction in the energy intensity of GDP could simply hide an increased import of energy-intensive products from countries that may rely on relatively more carbon-intensive technologies6,7. For instance, taking as an example one of the main net CO2 exporters, despite notable improvements, China’s carbon intensity remains about double that of the U.S. and Europe as a whole8. Indeed, while interactions between developed and emerging economies can spur environmental9 and renewable energy innovation10, the latter still exhibit greater carbon intensities. The IDA results are essentially in line with other works pointing out the role of carbon leakage in shaping emissions trends across countries12–14. However, while being a useful descriptive method for analysing how each factor has contributed to overall emissions trends, decomposition analyses based on the Kaya identity primarily show correlations rather than causal relationships. Thus, they cannot be directly used to test hypotheses about the relative contribution of driving forces15. This limitation may be somehow overcome by combining IDA with carefully identified structural econometric estimations16.

Here, we investigate through a Bayesian Panel Vector Autoregressive (VAR) model the macroeconomic mechanisms behind the reduction of national energy intensity of developed economies, with the primary aim to understand whether and to what extent these reductions have been triggered by the phasing-out of energy-intensive production.

Specifically, we investigate the effects of demand-invariant structural changes (i.e., changes in the production landscape that are not mirrored by equivalent changes in the sectoral composition of final demand) on the evolution of energy intensity in a sample of representative OECD economies. In this sense, we intend to shed some light on the actual contribution of energy intensity changes exhibited by developed countries to global decarbonization.

Previous works have disentangled the role of structural and sectoral effects on the evolution of energy intensity through IDAs3,4. Relatedly, other studies have examined the role of international trade and environmental agreements by analysing the divergence between production-based emissions, which occur within a country’s territory, and consumption-based emissions, which are embodied in the goods and services consumed domestically irrespective of where they are produced, under different settings7,12–14,17. We complement this literature by examining how changes in production structures, uncorrelated to changes in domestic demand, have shaped energy intensity, in so doing, highlighting the potential role of offshoring in these patterns. To do so, we use established time-series approaches commonly applied in macroeconomic analysis (i.e., panel VAR). Other works have also employed time series methodologies to investigate co-movements between energy, climate and macroeconomic variables6,18–22. Indeed, many factors that affect the environment vary over time and are bidirectionally interconnected (e.g., changing economic conditions, geopolitical pressures, legislation, natural disasters, and technologies), and this is particularly relevant when evaluating the consequences of CO2 emissions in the medium term.

Existing studies on the relationship between offshoring and energy or carbon intensity have mainly focused on single-country analyses3,5,16,23,24 or on sector-level data25,26 and theoretical models calibration27. In contrast, we provide a macro-level, dynamic evidence using a panel of OECD countries, complemented by an analysis including emerging economies. Our main contribution is therefore methodological, along three key dimensions. First, we provide cross-country evidence on the role of offshoring in shaping energy intensity changes, moving beyond the predominantly country-specific or sector-specific focus of existing studies. Second, by exploiting long-run data on production-based and consumption-based emissions, we propose an empirical strategy that captures offshoring-related structural changes over time, despite the lack of direct country-level measures. Third, such a dynamic framework allows us to assess not only the direction but also the persistence of these effects, going beyond standard decomposition approaches and offering insights relevant for both interpretation and policy.

In this work, we isolate offshoring-related structural changes by using an instrument given by the divergence between consumption-based and production-based emissions over time. Through a statistical index tracking the time-varying divergence between these two variables, we can assess how emissions associated with a country’s final demand deviate from those linked to its domestic production and how this gap evolves over time. The underlying rationale is that the carbon effects of a structural variation in the economy are likely characterized by a change in the discrepancy between consumption-based and production-based emissions, offering a proxy for the potential role of offshoring. This approach is particularly informative when structural changes in production are not accompanied by proportional shifts in domestic consumption patterns. In such cases, the divergence captures the relocation of emissions beyond national borders. By contrast, structural transformations that are perfectly mirrored in final demand remain undetected by this metric, but these are clearly not characterized by offshoring (e.g., a change in the production structure of the economy is implemented because some goods or services are not demanded anymore). Our primary focus, therefore, is on structural changes that are invariant with respect to domestic demand. This empirical strategy offers an efficient alternative solution to capture offshoring, given the absence of long-run, country-level data that directly measure it28.

Results

Methodology and data

To compute the dynamic response of energy intensity to the shock of interest, we estimate a Bayesian Panel VAR model controlling for proxies of sectoral effects (i.e., energy intensity of the industry sector) and structural effects (i.e., the relative shares over national total value added of the industry and the service sectors, respectively). The strength of this approach lies in its ability to capture dynamic interdependencies among variables and to assess causal relationships through impulse response functions. In addition, the Bayesian formulation provides regularization that improves estimation in high-dimensional settings with relatively short time spans and supports a structural identification of shocks. For details on the Bayesian Panel VAR estimation and identification, see the Methods.

The main rationale is that changes in the production structure are captured by tracking the relative contributions of comparatively more and less the most and least energy-intensive sectors to total value added. The analysis spans the years from 1970–2021 and exploits data from the Eora World multi-regional input-output database to construct the index of divergence between consumption- and production-based emissions29.

Given the scope of our analysis, we only include in our panel OECD countries that experienced energy intensity declines over the past decades. Moreover, to avoid confounding effects, we also considered only countries whose consumption-based emissions have increased at a faster rate than production-based ones. In particular, the 15 OECD countries included in the Panel VAR estimation exactly match the sample for which the IDA is displayed in Fig. 2.

Dynamic effects of demand-invariant structural changes on energy intensity

Our estimates provide several insightful results. A shock in the emission divergence measure efficiently captures structural shifts in the economies. Specifically, we define a positive shock as a 10% relative increase in consumption-based emissions compared to production-based emissions. Average response results across countries (Fig. 3) show that such a shock leads to a significant and persistent reallocation of economic activity away from the industrial sector. In particular, the share of GDP value added attributable to industry declines, while the contribution of the service sector increases. This shift is consistent with the notion that rising divergence between consumption-based and production-based emissions reflects the offshoring of energy-intensive activities to countries outside the sample, where emissions are accounted for elsewhere. All results should be interpreted under the assumption that domestic demand remains broadly stable over time, as shown in Fig. 3a. This stability reflects a deliberate modeling choice rather than an empirical finding. Indeed, by holding demand constant, we are able to focus on changes occurring on the production side of the economy, independent of shifts in consumption. This approach allows us to isolate structural transformations consistent with offshoring dynamics. Regarding our main objective, results show that these demand-invariant structural shocks lead to a statistically significant and long-lasting decline in national energy intensity. On impact, energy intensity falls by nearly 2%, and this effect persists over a horizon of up to eight years. In other words, structural changes in the production side of the economy, when not accompanied by corresponding adjustments in domestic demand, can account for a substantial portion of the long-run decline in national energy intensity observed across developed countries. This finding suggests that observed reductions in energy intensity may partly reflect geographic shifts in emissions, rather than genuine improvements in domestic energy use, with important implications for how decarbonization efforts are assessed in global climate policy.

Results are valid under a battery of robustness checks detailed in the Methods.

In particular, the higher a country’s CCPI ranking, the relatively better is its mitigation performance. The effect of demand-invariant structural changes on energy intensity persists only in countries with higher CCPI rankings for emissions, and disappears in those with lower rankings. This is consistent with the fact that the CCPI does not account for emissions embedded in trade, and can inadvertently reward countries that have outsourced carbon-intensive production.

Taken together, these sub-sample analyses indicate that national-level reductions in energy intensity may not always reflect meaningful contributions to global decarbonization. In particular, countries that appear to perform well under production-based metrics tend to show a greater responsiveness of energy intensity to demand-invariant structural shocks, suggesting that part of their apparent progress stems from offshoring rather than genuine improvements in domestic energy use.

Additional evidence from emerging economies

From an international trade perspective, one would expect that if our divergence measure effectively captures offshoring-related structural changes, countries that are net exporters of energy-intensive goods should not exhibit the same response patterns in terms of energy intensity reductions. To test this, we replicate our analysis on a panel of emerging economies (China, India, Indonesia, Morocco, South Africa, and Thailand) characterized by a slower growth in consumption-based emissions relative to production-based emissions over recent decades. This group can therefore be considered as a sample of net emissions exporters. The results (Fig. 5) indicate that, on average, national energy intensities in these countries do not respond significantly in the short term to a shock in the divergence measure and actually tend to increase in the medium term. In contrast to the baseline sample, where such shocks lead to persistent reductions in energy intensity, no systematic response is observed here. This divergence in outcomes suggests that the mechanism captured by our proxy, namely structural shifts in production decoupled from domestic demand, is not active in economies where emissions that are generated to meet external demand are significant. Discussion

By coupling a Kaya decomposition analysis with a multivariate econometric model, we found that the impact of offshoring-related structural changes on national energy trends is generally strong and long-lasting in developed economies. In other words, a non-negligible amount of energy intensity reductions is attributable to increased imports of energy-intensive products, rather than to substantial changes in domestic consumption patterns. Interestingly, this appears to be especially true for OECD countries showing better carbon mitigation performance according to production-based metrics. A similar analysis carried out on a panel of net CO2 exporters has shown that structural shifts in production decoupled from domestic demand have not played a significant role in driving energy intensity trends in emerging economies.

These findings support the view that caution should be taken when using production-based metrics to assess a country’s contribution to global carbon mitigation. Indeed, while genuine improvements in energy use can be certainly observed, measures based solely on domestic production provide an incomplete picture, as they do not account for emissions embodied in imports and may therefore overstate nationwide progress7,12–14.

Clearly, an empirical measure for structural changes that are not matched by equivalent changes in final demand does not exist nowadays28. Still, the divergence between production and consumption-based CO2 emissions used in our econometric analysis may help to dissect any departure in the trends of national consumption- and production-based emissions, in a way that is purged by overall shifts in domestic demand.

Our results also support the rationale for coupling domestic policies incentivizing energy efficiency innovations and decarbonization of national energy systems with initiatives, such as the EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism30, aimed at mitigating the risk of carbon leakage and at incentivizing decarbonization in third countries, thus addressing the global nature of climate change.

This work is not without limitations. First, to identify offshoring, we rely on structural shifts that assume relatively stable domestic demand over time. However, in practice, various hybrid scenarios can arise in which changes in domestic demand occur alongside structural transformations, though these dynamics may not mirror each other precisely. Such scenarios depend heavily on the extent and variation of sectoral consumption patterns, which are inherently complex and difficult to capture within a linear econometric framework. Second, while our measure of emission divergence serves as an effective proxy for detecting shifts in production, it remains, due to data unavailability, an indirect indicator of offshoring, and it is therefore subject to a degree of uncertainty.

Possible avenues for future research lie in the possibility of disentangling the “pure” final demand vis-à-vis domestic production shifts from the carbon-intensity effect. Part of the divergence, indeed, could be attributed to the fact that, even with final demand remaining fixed, energy-intensive imported goods can still bring about a greater emissions’ content, due to more energy-intensive production technologies or more carbon-tilted fuel mixes of exporting countries.

The offshoring factor is the ratio of domestic production of widgets over the domestic demand for widgets sourced from worldwide production. If the factor is less than 1, country i consumes more widgets than it produces domestically, that is, it offshores widgets. The sectoral demand factor measures the share of domestic demand that is attributable to widgets. Hence, this factor is always less than 1. Finally, the demand to output factor compares the overall demand across all sectors of country i to its overall domestic production across all sectors. If this factor is greater than 1, country i demands more from the global economy than it can produce internally.

To assess the role played by the above-mentioned driving factors, one could either rely on index decomposition analysis (IDA) or on structural decomposition analysis (SDA)16,31,32. While both make use of historical data to describe the changes in a given variable of interest (e.g., emissions) and of its main drivers, IDAs rely on sectoral information, whereas SDAs rely on an input-output-based reasoning. Given our scope, centered around the possible mismatch between production and consumption, we rely on an IDA, which is supported by several contributions in the literature3,4,17,23–25,33–35. In particular, we use the Fisher Ideal index methodology36,37, which renders perfect decomposition (i.e., without residuals) and is suited to analyse the evolution of energy intensity and its drivers with respect to a base year.

We use data from the IEA’s World Energy Balances1 and IEA’s Greenhouse Gas Emissions from Energy38 to define the total final energy consumption and energy-related emissions, respectively, by country and by sector. Sectoral and national output are based on United Nations (UN) Statistics Division measures of GDP and total value added computed at constant 2015 prices in US Dollars2, while we use data from the OECD Inter-Country Input-Output (ICIO) Tables39 for the definition of the global sectoral output embodied in domestic demand. The final demand by sectoral aggregates is obtained from the final demands of households, non-profit institutions and governments, gross fixed capital formation, changes in inventories and valuables and direct purchases abroad from residents. The sectors used for the decomposition are: “Agriculture, hunting, forestry, fishing”, “Industry” (i.e., IEA’s Mining and Manufacturing, UN Stats’ Mining, manufacturing and utilities and OECD’s Mining and manufacturing sectors), “Construction”, “Services” (i.e., IEA’s Commercial and public services and Final consumption not elsewhere specified, UN Stats’ Wholesale, retail trade, restaurants and hotels and Other activities and OECD’s sectors corresponding to Wholesale and retail trade, Accommodation and food service activities, and to commercial and public services and other activities). The industry energy intensity is based on a partial numerator-denominator mismatch, as utilities are included only in the denominator. This may lead to a slight underestimation of industry energy intensity, which we interpret as a conservative feature of our approach. The time window for this analysis is 1995 to 2020, with 1995 as the base year. The economies in our sample are: Belgium, Canada, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Spain, Sweden, the United Kingdom and the United States. This window is motivated both by data availability and by the fact that most of the impacts that are attributable to trade globalization are likely to be recorded after the beginning of the new millennium, with China entering the WTO in 2001.

Specification and estimation of the Bayesian Panel VAR

In the econometric analysis, we estimate a Structural Bayesian Panel Vector Auto-regression Model (VAR). VAR models are standard econometric models often exploited in macroeconomic analysis for their ability to capture multiple relationships between time series variables. In essence, a VAR simultaneously estimates multiple regressions in which each endogenous variable is explained by its own lags and the lags of the other variables. Intuitively, the VAR framework treats all variables symmetrically: each variable is explained by its own past values as well as by the past values of all other variables in the system. This structure allows the model to capture dynamic interdependencies and feedback effects among variables without imposing strong assumptions on causal ordering.

In a panel setting such as ours, the VAR model is estimated jointly across multiple observational units (i.e., countries), exploiting both the time-series and cross-sectional dimensions of the data. A Bayesian approach further regularizes estimation by incorporating prior information, which is particularly useful in systems with many parameters and relatively short time spans. Structural identification then allows the innovations εt to be interpreted as economically meaningful shocks, enabling the analysis of how disturbances propagate through the system over time.

The panel is balanced for the period 1970-2021 and quite homogeneous in terms of economic development and political institutions. We use annual data due to the unavailability of CO2 emissions and energy data at higher frequencies. However, as argued, the panel structure allows us to exploit the cross-sectional dimension of data, thereby reaching a consistent number of observations.

Equation (5) describes how the joint dynamics of the variables of interest evolve over time and across countries. In this framework, each variable is allowed to respond not only to its own past realizations, but also to the past behavior of all other variables in the system. The country fixed effects αi capture time-invariant differences across countries, such as structural characteristics or institutional features, while the time fixed effects θt absorb common shocks affecting all countries simultaneously, such as global economic cycles or worldwide energy market developments. This structure allows us to disentangle country-specific dynamics from common global forces, while exploiting the panel dimension to improve statistical power despite the use of annual data.

The correct identification of the shock can only be reached by controlling for other factors that are likely key drivers of the energy intensity. Specifically, together with the energy intensity (ei,t), defined as the ratio between total energy supply and GDP, and the emission divergence measure (γi,t), that we use as a proxy for offshoring in this part of the analysis, we include in the vector of endogenous variables of the model proxies for the other determinants of energy intensity according to the baseline decomposition of equation (2), namely sectoral and structural effects. Note that the two offshoring proxies we use in the IDA and the VAR differ in nature and rely on different data sources. The sectoral effect, sei,t, which captures the within-sector energy intensity variations, is proxied by the energy intensity of the industry sector. To account for the structural effect, we exploit the relative shares over total value added of the industry sector and the service sector (respectively indi,t and seri,t), which represent, respectively, more and less energy-intensive sectors. The use of these proxies, rather than a fully exhaustive sectoral representation, is dictated by our empirical setting and data, and can be viewed as a way to capture broad patterns of within-sector and structural changes. Accordingly, these variables should be interpreted as stylized proxies for sectoral and structural effects, rather than as a comprehensive ranking of energy intensity across the whole economy. This implies that our measure does not necessarily identify the most energy-intensive sector in the full economy, but rather the most energy-intensive sector among those consistently observable in our dataset. Taken together, the set of endogenous variables is chosen to capture both demand-side conditions and structural determinants of energy intensity, allowing us to isolate the role of offshoring-related structural change from other confounding mechanisms.

The estimation strategy is designed to balance model flexibility with the constraints imposed by data availability. Given the relatively long time span but limited time-series frequency of the data, the Panel VAR framework allows us to model rich dynamic interactions while pooling information across countries. The Bayesian approach further disciplines estimation by shrinking coefficients toward economically plausible values, thereby reducing overfitting and enhancing the stability of impulse response analysis in a multivariate setting.

We need careful assumptions on the reduced form equation. First, we assume both dynamic and static homogeneity. This implies that the matrix of parameters is the same across all units (countries), i.e., Ai,l = Al ∀ i, and that the variance-covariance matrix of the reduced-form disturbances is also country-independent, i.e., Σi = Σ ∀ i, with the country-fixed effect smoothing this homogeneity for not explicit country’s characteristics. Second, we assume that the reduced-form shocks ϵi,t are serially and cross-sectionally uncorrelated.

Under these assumptions, a pooled fixed-effects estimation of the matrices of parameter Al is the best approach to estimate the Panel VAR40. However, while assuming no serial correlation is common practice in multivariate estimations, both the no cross-sectional correlation and the dynamic homogeneity assumption can pose challenges. For the former, it is known that spillovers are generally not negligible, especially in homogeneous panels of developed countries. Thus, assuming that in Eq. (5) the residuals are not cross-sectionally correlated may produce a biased estimation of the parameters. Secondly, if countries were characterized by different dynamics, which is reasonable in macroeconomic frameworks, assuming a common slope produces a biased and inconsistent fixed-effect estimator, even when the cross-sectional dimensions and the time span are sizable41.

We address the two problems separately. First, to reliably assume no cross-sectional correlation of the reduced-form residuals, in the spirit of Global Vector AutoRegression models42 we include in the reduced form equation a global exogenous regressor accounting for any spillovers between countries. A similar empirical strategy to overcome the downturn of assuming no cross-sectional correlation between residuals is exploited by Ciccarelli and Marotta43. Specifically, we exploit the Monthly World Industrial Production (WIP) index developed by Baumeister and Hamilton44, who constructed this measure as an update of the original—and discontinued—OECD monthly index of industrial production. The index covers OECD countries and six emerging economies (i.e., Brazil, China, India, Indonesia, the Russian Federation and South Africa). We aggregate WIP data at an annual frequency to match the rest of our endogenous variables’ frequency.

Secondly, the dynamic homogeneity assumption is forced by data availability. Indeed, since only annual data are available for the variables of interest, the number of observations in each country is too small to perform different types of estimation that involve dynamic heterogeneity (e.g., the mean-group estimator).

The best we can do is to partially relax this assumption by performing sub-sample estimations according to specific countries’ characteristics. In doing so, we avoid the imposition of a unique macroeconomic dynamic structure across the whole sample a priori, allowing the matrices of parameters to vary at least for different groups of countries and gaining information about the potential heterogeneity of our results.

The Panel VAR model is estimated using Bayesian techniques, which impose prior beliefs on the parameters to overcome the curse of dimensionality. In particular, we rely on the modified version of the Minnesota prior proposed by Bańbura et al. (2010) to deal with large-scale VARs45. We approximate the posterior distribution by exploiting a Gibbs sampling algorithm. The prior is implemented using dummy variables by following the procedure outlined by Barbùra et al.45, who introduced a modified version of the Minnesota Prior specifically designed for large-scale VARs to address the curse of dimensionality. This prior is tailored for macroeconomic variables. The standard Minnesota prior assumes that all VAR equations are centered around a random walk with drift, which implies that the diagonal elements of the parameter matrix tend to one, while the off-diagonal coefficients approach zero. Furthermore, the prior incorporates the belief that more recent lags provide more information than distant ones, and that for each variable, its own lags explain most of the observed variation, relative to lagged interdependencies. Heterogeneity of the baseline results according to country characteristics

In order to strengthen the results and to comply with the strict identification assumption for dynamic homogeneity, we perform two sub-sample estimations of the Panel VAR. Specifically, the Panel VAR model is estimated for smaller groups of countries according to some specific characteristics, allowing therefore the matrices of parameters Al to vary at least for two different pools of units. Besides relaxing the cross-sectional homogeneity assumption, this empirical exercise also allows us to test the heterogeneity of our main results to the peculiar features of the sample economies.

In particular, with respect to Fig. 4a, countries for which the average contribution of the IDA offshoring factor to energy intensity over the sample period was positive are: Finland, France, Germany and Japan; while countries for which the average contribution of the IDA offshoring factor to energy intensity was negative are: Belgium, Canada, Denmark, Italy, Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Sweden, Spain, United Kingdom and United States. In Fig. 4b, instead, countries with relatively higher (i.e., above median) GHG emission factor for the Climate Change Performance Index are: Germany, Denmark, Spain, France, United Kingdom, Netherlands, Norway and Sweden; while countries with lower ranking are: Belgium, Canada, Finland, Italy, Japan, New Zealand and the United States.
