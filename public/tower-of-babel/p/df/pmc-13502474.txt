Medial entorhinal-hippocampal desynchronization parallels the emergence of memory impairment in a mouse model of Alzheimer’s disease pathology
Authors: Lauren M. Vetere, Angelina M. Galas, Nick Vaughan, Cassidy Kohler, Yu Feng, Zoé Christenson Wick, Paul A. Philipsberg, Olga Liobimova, Kathryn E. Gordon, Antonio Fernandez-Ruiz, Denise J. Cai, Tristan Shuman
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SUMMARYAlzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disease characterized by progressive impairments in episodic and spatial memory, as well as circuit and network-level dysfunction. While functional impairments in medial entorhinal cortex (MEC) and hippocampus (HPC) have been observed in patients and rodent models of AD, it remains unclear how communication between these regions breaks down in disease, and what specific physiological changes are associated with the onset of memory impairment. Here, we use silicon probes to simultaneously record neural activity in MEC and HPC before or after the onset of spatial memory impairment in the 3xTg mouse model of AD pathology. We find that reduced hippocampal theta power, reduced MEC-CA1 theta coherence, and altered phase locking of MEC and hippocampal neurons all coincide with the emergence of spatial memory impairment in 3xTg mice. Together, these findings suggest that disrupted temporal coordination of neural activity in the MEC-hippocampal system parallels the emergence of memory impairment in a model of AD pathology.

INTRODUCTION

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a neurodegenerative disease characterized by progressive age-related cognitive impairments and circuit dysfunction. Recent efforts to find new treatments have largely focused on removing pathological proteins, such as amyloid-beta (Aβ), from the brain, but such therapies have shown limited ability to slow cognitive decline.1,2 An alternative approach that addresses underlying changes in the neural circuits that support memory may be necessary to preserve or restore brain function. In order to design effective interventions, we must first identify specific alterations in circuit function that coincide with the onset of memory impairment and are thus potential drivers of cognitive decline.

Deficits in spatial memory and navigation are among the earliest and most common manifestations of cognitive impairment in AD.3,4 This suggests that the neural circuits controlling spatial memory are particularly vulnerable to the pathological processes driving this disease. Indeed, the hippocampus (HPC) and medial entorhinal cortex (MEC) are both critical for spatial memory5–7 and exhibit extensive dysfunction in patients with AD and in rodent models. There is substantial work demonstrating that AD pathology disrupts hippocampal excitability, plasticity, synaptic function, and place cell coding in rodents.8–15 In addition, upstream inputs to HPC from the entorhinal cortex are similarly vulnerable to hyperexcitability, early accumulation of pathology, and cell loss.16–23 In MEC in particular, recent work in animal models has found early deficits in spatial coding of grid cells.14,24 Together, this work suggests that disrupted function within the HPC-MEC circuit is likely to contribute to the spatial memory impairments observed in patients with AD and animal models of AD pathology.

Successful spatial memory and spatial coding depend upon precise timing and synchronization of neural activity within and across the HPC and MEC.7,25,26 Synchrony of neural oscillations and precise timing of neuronal firing support plasticity and facilitate information flow within this circuitry.27–32 In patients with AD and animal models, network changes disrupt synchrony within these brain regions, manifesting as decreased oscillatory power, epileptiform activity, and even seizures.14,33–41 While network dysfunction is a critical component of this disease,42 it remains unclear when these changes develop across disease progression, how synchrony is disrupted across regions, and how these changes relate to cognitive decline.

Although dysfunction in the entorhinal cortex and HPC is well established in AD, the precise cell types and subcircuits that are most impaired within the MEC and HPC are not fully understood. Inputs from MEC to HPC are made up of two primary projections43,44: the ‘‘direct pathway’’ from MEC layer 3 (MEC3) principal cells to CA1,45 and the ‘‘indirect pathway,’’ which consists of MEC layer 2 (MEC2) stellate cells that project to the dentate gyrus (DG).46 These projections provide the major source of spatially tuned input to the HPC, including grid, border, head direction, and speed cells.47–51 Determining which specific circuits are altered across disease progression is key to understanding how memory deficits arise in AD.

To assess the impact of AD pathology on hippocampal-entorhinal circuits, we used 3xTg mice, which express APPSWE, PS1M146V, and TauP301L mutations. Together, these mutations drive the accumulation of Aβ and hyperphosphorylated tau throughout the brain, as well as progressive memory impairments with age.52 To examine whether network synchrony and spike timing in the HPC-MEC circuit are disrupted in these animals, we performed simultaneous in vivo electrophysiology recordings in MEC and HPC at time points immediately before or after the onset of spatial memory impairments. This approach allowed us to capture network activity and single-unit firing in MEC and HPC at the onset of spatial memory deficits. We found several critical disruptions in MEC-hippocampal synchronization that emerged alongside memory impairments, including reduced hippocampal theta power, reduced theta coherence between MEC and HPC, hyperactivity in MEC2 single units, and disorganized single unit spike timing in MEC and HPC. This loss of synchrony and coordinated neuronal firing in MEC and HPC is likely to impair spatial memory functions that rely on precise timing in these circuits.7,25,26 Together, these findings demonstrate impaired MEC-hippocampal communication in 3xTg mice with a time course that parallels the onset of spatial memory impairments.

RESULTS

Spatial memory impairments emerge between 6 and 8 months of age in 3xTg mice

We first set out to characterize the time course of spatial memory deficits in 3xTg mice. Recent work established that spatial memory was intact at 3 months of age, but impaired by 10 months of age in 3xTg mice,22 so we first sought to define a more narrow time window for the emergence of memory impairment. We therefore investigated spatial memory performance at 6 and 8 months of age using the Novel Object Location (NOL) task, which has been found to be dependent on both the HPC and MEC.6,53–55 Both male and female mice were first exposed to an arena with two identical items. Then, following a delay, the subjects were placed back in the arena with one of the objects moved to a novel location (Figure 1A). Animals with intact spatial memory will naturally explore the displaced object, so we measured how much time each animal spent exploring the moved object as an index of spatial memory. At 6 months of age, we found robust learning in both the WT and 3xTg mice, and no difference between groups in task performance (Figure 1B). However, at 8 months old, 3xTg mice performed significantly worse than their age-matched WT counterparts, with WT mice showing robust learning, while 3xTg mice exhibited chance levels of performance (Figure 1C). This deficit in NOL performance in 8-month-old 3xTg mice was not driven by the sex of the subjects (Two-way Genotype × Sex ANOVA in 8-month-old animals, no main effect of sex: p = 0.796, and no sex × genotype interaction: p = 0.284). In a separate cohort, we also assessed locomotion in an open field and found that 3xTg mice at both time points had reduced distance traveled, velocity, and proportion of time moving compared to WT mice (Figures S1A–S1C). This difference emerged by 6 months of age, and there were no interactions between Group and Age, suggesting that this difference in locomotion does not drive the differences in memory performance we found only at 8 months of age in 3xTg mice. Notably, we found that mice at 6 and 8 months of age required distinct training protocols to produce robust learning in WT mice (Figure 1D), suggesting early age-related changes in cognitive processes56–59 that impact performance across different training protocols. Thus, to assess the effect of the 3xTg mutations on spatial memory within the context of early aging, we utilized slightly different protocols for 6-month-old (Protocol 1) and 8-month-old (Protocol 2) animals that each produced robust learning in the WT group, and thus could be used to assess memory impairments in 3xT mice at each time point (Figure 1, see Methods and Discussion for more information). Despite age-related changes in WT learning between 6 and 8 months of age (Figure 1D), 3xTg mice consistently showed deficits in learning that emerged by the 8-month time point (Figure 1E). Details of all statistics and comparisons across groups and protocols are reported in Table S1, and all raw behavior data are included in Table S2. Together, these data reinforce the conclusion that memory impairment emerges between 6 and 8 months of age in 3xTg mice. However, see the Limitations section for the discussion of challenges in design and interpretation of behavioral experiments in the context of aging and neurodegeneration.

Both the 6 and 8-month time points are prior to the emergence of aggregated Aβ pathology. Little to no amyloid plaques were detected in 6- or 8-month-old animals, but plaques developed in HPC and subiculum by 15 months of age (Figures 1D and 1E), primarily driven by an increase in female 3xTg animals (Three-way ANOVA, Age × Genotype × Sex interaction, p = 0.013). This timing aligns with recent phenotypic characterization of this mouse line, which showed plaque formation in the subiculum/CA1 at 12 months, though soluble APP and Aβ have been detected by 6 month of age, prior to plaque accumulation.60–63 Similarly, no neurofibrillary tangles are present at these time points, though phosphorylated tau in subiculum and CA1 has been detected by 6 month of age.60,64–66 Despite a lack of aggregated plaques or tangles, we did observe cell death and interneuron loss in MEC (see Figure S9 for full immunohistochemistry characterization), which may contribute to behavioral changes in 3xTg mice. Neuronal loss has been reported in a variety of mouse models of AD pathology, though the relative timing between pathology, neuronal loss, and cognitive impairment varies across models and studies.67 Moreover, soluble Aβ21,68–70 and tau71–74 have been linked to early synapse loss, abnormal network activity, cell loss, and changes in neuronal activity and excitability in AD mouse models. Overall, we found that spatial memory impairments emerged between 6 and 8 months of age in 3xTg mice, prior to Aβ plaque or neurofibrillary tangle formation. Therefore, we utilized these time points to examine how spatial memory circuits are disrupted across the development of memory impairments.

Reduced hippocampal theta power emerges between 6 and 8 months of age in 3xTg mice

To determine the specific changes in MEC-hippocampal circuit physiology that emerge across the progression of memory deficits, we performed in vivo silicon probe recordings in 3xTg and WT mice at 6 or 8 months of age (Figure 2). We first trained head-fixed mice to run on a virtual reality (VR) linear track to receive water rewards and then performed simultaneous acute electrophysiology in MEC and HPC using two 256-channel silicon probes (see Figure S2 for the histological verification of probe locations).

We first sought to quantify changes in hippocampal network activity by examining theta oscillations, which are prominent in the temporal lobe and have been associated with successful learning and memory.75–80 From our silicon probe recordings, we first isolated local field potentials (LFPs), rhythmic extracellular voltage fluctuations that reflect the summation of synaptic activity near the recording electrode. We then filtered for activity within the theta frequency band (5-12Hz). The power, or amplitude, of hippocampal theta oscillations depends largely on currents elicited by long-range inputs, in particular those from the entorhinal cortex,76,81 although local inputs and other long-range inputs from medial septum also contribute. Notably, theta power is influenced by running speed and thus can be confounded by potential differences in running behavior between groups. We found no differences in locomotion between WT and 3xTg mice on the virtual linear track, but we did find that older mice of both genotypes ran slower on this head-fixed task (Figure S3A). These data contrast with open field locomotion (Figures S1A–S1C), likely due to the repeated nature of the head-fixed training resulting in extensive habituation, but both results highlight a need to control for differences in locomotion. Thus, to allow comparisons across both genotype and age, we subsampled the electrophysiology data for periods where running speeds were comparable across groups and used this subsampled data for all analyses (Figure S3C).

At 6 months of age, prior to the onset of spatial memory impairments, we found no significant differences in hippocampal theta power or amplitude between 3xTg and WT mice (Figures 3A–3C). However, at 8 months of age, 3xTg mice had reduced theta power across all CA1 and DG layers (Figure 3D). Reduced hippocampal theta power is consistent with a loss of the entorhinal inputs that target distal dendrites in CA1 and DG and elicit local theta-modulated currents there.76,81 However, changes in theta power may also reflect changes in volume conducted theta from nearby brain regions. To more clearly define the source of reduced theta power, we performed current source density analysis (CSD) to isolate local current sources and sinks generated along the hippocampal laminar profile.82,83 Again, at 6 months of age we found no changes in CSD magnitude between genotypes (Figures 3B and 3C). In 8-month-old 3xTg mice, however, CSD magnitude was reduced, most prominently in layers that receive input from the entorhinal cortex (Figures 3E and 3F). This reduction in theta power and CSD magnitude in 8-month-old 3xTg mice was not driven by a particular sex (no effect of sex and no interaction with sex in Three-way Genotype x Layer x Sex ANOVA, see Table S1 for all statistical details and p values). Meanwhile, in MEC, we found no significant differences in theta power across genotypes at either time point (Figures S4C and S4D). Together, these findings suggest that reduced hippocampal theta power, likely driven by disrupted entorhinal input into HPC, emerges between 6 and 8 months of age in 3xTg mice, coinciding with the onset of spatial memory impairment.

Oscillations at other frequencies are also relevant for cognition and have been implicated in various mouse models of AD pathology.37,84–86 For this reason, we also examined hippocampal fast gamma (90–130 Hz) and slow gamma (30–50 Hz). Fast gamma is associated with MEC-HPC communication, while slow gamma has been linked to CA3→CA1 and lateral entorhinal cortex→DG projections.7,87–90 We found decreased fast gamma power in 8-month-old 3xTg mice, with the most pronounced differences localized in the granule cell layer and hilus (Figure S5C), suggesting that MEC inputs to DG may be altered in these mice. Oscillations in the slow gamma band were relatively unaffected (Figures S5B and S5D). Together, these findings are consistent with a disruption of MEC inputs to HPC across the development of memory impairments in 3xTg mice.

Reduced MEC-CA1 theta coherence emerges between 6 and 8 months of age in 3xTg mice

We next sought to assess whether theta oscillations between MEC and HPC become desynchronized across the development of memory impairments in 3xTg mice. Theta synchrony between HPC and cortical regions is thought to support learning and memory by organizing spiking across connected brain regions and promoting plasticity.30 To assess synchrony, we calculated the phase coherence (how consistent the phase lag is between two recording sites) of theta oscillations across HPC and MEC. In 6-month-old animals, we found no changes in theta coherence between MEC and HPC or within HPC in 3xTg mice (Figures 3G and 3H). However, at 8 months of age, 3xTg mice had reduced theta coherence between MEC and CA1 compared to their age-matched WT controls (Figure 3J). In particular, theta coherence between MEC and the stratum oriens, pyramidale, and radiatum layers of CA1 was decreased, while coherence between MEC and DG was unaltered. Meanwhile, theta coherence within the HPC itself was relatively spared (Figures 3G and 3I), as was coherence within MEC (Figure S4B). As with our theta power and CSD results, we found no effect of sex on MEC-HPC theta coherence (see Table S1). Finally, in order to test for potential effects on theta rhythms in healthy aging, we also compared 6 and 8-month-old WT mice. We found no changes in hippocampal theta power, CSD magnitude, or MEC-CA1 coherence across normal aging (all statistics reported in Table S1). Taken together, these findings suggest a specific deficit in synchrony between MEC and CA1 in 8-month-old 3xTg mice that aligns with the onset of memory impairment.

To investigate whether changes in theta frequency could contribute to deficits in coherence, we calculated the power spectrum density (PSD) within the theta range (5-12Hz) for each animal in MEC and each CA1 sublayer and found the frequency with the highest power within that range. We found a decrease in peak theta frequency with age in MEC and CA1 radiatum (Figure S6). Slowing of the theta rhythm in MEC could contribute to reduced entorhinal-hippocampal synchrony, but given that coherence changes were sublayer specific and altered by age rather than genotype (Figure 3J), this slowing is unlikely to drive theta coherence changes in this circuit.

Overall, our analysis of theta oscillations found that reduced hippocampal theta power, CSD magnitude, and MEC-CA1 coherence all emerged in 3xTg mice at 8 months of age, coincident with the onset of spatial memory impairments on the novel object location task. Importantly, though 6-month-old WT animals tended to spend more time running at faster speeds (Figure S3D), the differences we observed in theta power, CSD magnitude, and coherence persisted regardless of running speed (Figures S3E–S3G). Together, these results suggest a progressive breakdown in coordination between MEC and HPC in 3xTg mice that may contribute to spatial memory impairments, but it remains unclear which neuronal populations could contribute to these effects.

Loss of MEC3 spike timing precision in 8-month-old 3xTg mice

Reduced hippocampal theta power and loss of coherence between MEC and CA1 may reflect a loss of properly timed inputs to HPC, as rhythmic inputs from MEC are critical for hippocampal theta oscillations.76,81 Thus, we hypothesized that firing patterns of neurons in upstream MEC might be disrupted in 8-month-old 3xTg mice. To assess this, we isolated single units recorded in MEC and separated them into putative excitatory and inhibitory units based on their firing properties and waveform shapes (see Figure S7 and Methods for further details).91 We examined the synchrony of individual neurons relative to ongoing network activity by assessing theta phase locking. Specific cell types within the HPC and entorhinal cortex tend to fire at particular theta phases, and this coordination is thought to promote plasticity and information flow within this circuitry.91,92 Here, we quantified the strength of phase locking by measuring the mean resultant vector, or R value, for each unit. Cells with lower R values have a weaker preference for their preferred firing phase (see additional visualizations illustrating this in Figure S8). Furthermore, by examining phase locking relative to both local (MEC) or long-range (HPC) theta oscillations, we can gain insight into whether desynchronization emerges locally or specifically between MEC and HPC.

Given the specific deficit we found in coherence between MEC and CA1, we first investigated the firing properties of MEC3 excitatory neurons, which are known to directly project to CA1 (Figures 4A–4D). We first asked whether overall activity levels were altered and found no significant differences in firing rates across groups (Figure 4B). We then examined phase locking of individual MEC neuron firing to local MEC theta oscillations as a metric of local synchrony and function within MEC (Figure 4C). Reduced phase locking of MEC3 neurons with MEC theta would indicate reduced coordination of MEC3 spiking with upstream inputs to MEC and with overall local MEC activity. We found no differences in phase locking of MEC neurons to local MEC theta between 3xTg and WT groups at 6 months of age. However, excitatory MEC3 neurons in 3xTg mice had reduced phase locking strength between 6 and 8 months of age, while WT neurons did not change across these time points. A direct comparison in phase locking of excitatory MEC3 neurons between 3xTg and WT neurons at 8 months of age approached significance but did not reach criterion after adjusting for multiple comparisons (Table S1). Thus, MEC3 phase locking to local theta weakened with age in 3xTg mice, but not in WT mice.

We next examined phase locking of MEC3 excitatory neurons to downstream CA1 theta, in order to assess synchrony between the spiking activity of MEC neurons and neural activity in HPC (Figure 4D). We found no differences in phase locking at 6 months of age, but significant deficits by 8 months of age. Excitatory MEC3 phase locking in 8-month-old 3xTg animals was reduced compared to both age-matched WT controls and younger 6-month-old 3xTg mice. In addition, we examined the preferred phases (mean firing phase, or mu-value) of MEC3 excitatory neurons and found an altered mean firing phase in 3xTg mice only at the 8-month-old time point (Figure S8D), although this effect could be influenced by differences in the distribution of recorded cell types in this region across groups.93,94 Together, these data demonstrate that MEC3 excitatory neurons have reduced theta phase locking to both local and downstream theta that emerges between 6 and 8 months of age in 3xTg mice (Figures 4C and 4D). This suggests a loss of synchronization within MEC as well as between MEC3 neurons and their downstream targets in HPC.

To gain a more complete picture of MEC3 microcircuitry, we also examined the firing properties of MEC3 interneurons, which provide inhibition to local excitatory populations. In inhibitory MEC3 neurons, we again found no changes in firing rates (Figure 4E), and a similar pattern of decreased theta phase locking in 3xTg neurons at 8 months of age when compared to neurons from 6-month-old 3xTg mice or neurons from age-matched WT mice (Figures 4F and 4G). We did not find a shift in the mean firing phases of individual MEC3 interneurons at either time point (Figure S8E), suggesting that interneurons maintained their preferred phase but were less precisely phase locked. These interneuron phase locking deficits further highlight a progressive vulnerability of MEC3 neurons with age in 3xTg mice. Overall, these results point to a desynchronization between MEC3 spike timing and both local and hippocampal theta oscillations.

Hyperexcitability and disrupted theta phase locking in MEC2 of 3xTg mice

MEC2 also sends critical projections to HPC and contains neuronal populations that have been implicated in AD.16,18–20,22,46,95 Thus, we next focused our analysis on MEC2 excitatory neurons (Figures 5A–5D), which include a mix of both stellate cells and pyramidal cells with distinct molecular and anatomical features (however, these cell types cannot be distinguished within our recordings). MEC2 stellate cells make up the major projection to DG46 and have been shown to be vulnerable to pathology in early AD.16,19,20,96 We have previously found that MEC2 stellate cells are uniquely hyperexcitable in 10-month-old 3xTg mice in vitro,22 but their in vivo firing patterns have not been investigated. To address this, we first asked whether MEC2 excitatory neurons had increased activity during spatial exploration in vivo. We found increased firing rates in excitatory MEC2 cells in 8-month-old 3xTg mice (Figure 5B), but no changes in firing rate at 6 month of age, indicating that MEC2 excitatory neuron hyperactivity emerged across disease progression in 3xTg mice.

We next examined the strength of phase locking of excitatory MEC2 neurons to their local (MEC) theta oscillations, in order to assess within-MEC synchronization. MEC2 excitatory units in 3xTg and WT mice had similar phase locking to MEC theta at both time points, suggesting that synchrony within MEC2 is intact in 3xTg mice (Figure 5C). Within the WT group, there was an increase in phase locking strength from 6 to 8-month of age, suggesting changes related to normal aging, but this increase was not detected in 3xTg mice (Figure 5C). Though synchrony of MEC2 spike timing with local MEC activity was mostly preserved, when we examined the synchronization of MEC2 excitatory neurons with downstream CA1 theta oscillations, we found decreased phase locking in 8-month-old 3xTg animals compared to age-matched WT mice. Thus, excitatory 3xTg MEC2 neurons had reduced synchrony with downstream hippocampal theta compared to age-matched WT neurons only at our later time point (Figure 5D). Interestingly, we again observed increased phase locking across age only in the WT group, suggesting that this deficit in 3xTg mice may emerge in part due to a failure to increase phase locking with age, rather than simply a reduction in phase locking strength with age (Figures 5C and 5D).

We next extended our phase locking analysis to examine interneurons in MEC2. These interneurons provide inhibition to the surrounding excitatory cells and are also known to receive long-range input from the medial septum97–99 that modulates theta oscillations. We found no firing rate changes in MEC2 interneurons (Figure 5E), and no differences in interneuron phase locking strength to MEC or CA1 theta at either time point (Figures 5F and 5G). We did, however, find a small but significant shift in the preferred phase in MEC2 inhibitory neurons to downstream CA1 theta in 6-month-old 3xTg mice (Figure S8G). Overall, these MEC2 phase locking results suggest a deficit in synchronization primarily between MEC2 excitatory spiking and downstream CA1 theta in 3xTg mice that emerges across disease progression.

Given the altered MEC3 interneuron firing patterns and increased MEC2 excitatory firing rates we found in 3xTg mice, we next used immunohistochemistry to further examine MEC interneurons for signs of inhibitory dysfunction. Recent studies have highlighted the vulnerability of fast-spiking interneurons across AD mouse models.23,84,100–102 Parvalbumin-expressing (PV+) inhibitory neurons provide inhibition to MEC excitatory neurons, as well as long-range inhibition to HPC.97–99,103–106 These findings led us to predict that PV+ interneurons might be impacted in 3xTg mice. We found that PV+ cell counts in MEC2 and MEC3 were reduced in 3xTg mice compared to WT regardless of age (Figures S9A–S9C). However, no changes were detectable in the HPC at 6 or 8 months of age (Figures S9G–S9J). To examine overall neuronal loss, we also performed staining for NeuN to label all neurons. We found a reduced number of NeuN-expressing neurons in both MEC2 and MEC3 of 3xTg mice (Figures S9D–S9F), but with distinct time courses. In MEC2, NeuN was reduced only in the 8-month-old 3xTg mice, coinciding with electrophysiological changes and memory deficits. In MEC3, the number of NeuN-expressing neurons was reduced earlier, by the 6-month time point. Together, these results demonstrate that 3xTg mice have hyperactivity and reduced phase locking in MEC2 excitatory neurons at 8 months of age, along with early interneuron vulnerability and neuronal loss in MEC.

Progressive disruptions in hippocampal phase locking in 3xTg mice

Given the progressive changes in MEC-HPC synchronization between 6 and 8 months of age, we hypothesized that the timing of hippocampal neurons’ firing would also be disrupted in 8-month-old 3xTg mice. To investigate hippocampal spike timing, we examined single-unit activity in CA1 and DG. We again identified putative excitatory and inhibitory units based on their waveforms, firing rates, and firing properties (Figure S7, see Methods). The majority of CA1 units were located in and around the pyramidal layer, while the majority of DG units were located in the hilus. We first examined firing rates and found no changes between 3xTg and WT neurons in either hippocampal subregion or cell type (Figures S10A–S10D). However, when we examined phase locking of CA1 neurons, we found that phase locking to CA1 theta was reduced in both excitatory and inhibitory neurons of 3xTg mice relative to neurons from WT controls, with this deficit emerging between 6 and 8 months of age (Figures 6A–6C). These results suggest that spike timing in CA1 becomes disorganized with age in 3xTg mice. The timing of this dysfunction aligns with the onset of disrupted spike timing upstream in MEC and with broader deficits in theta synchrony and spatial memory.

We also examined firing patterns in DG, which receives projections from MEC2 and sends input to CA1 via CA3. Here, our recorded population of excitatory DG neurons likely consists primarily of mossy cells due to their location in the hilus and relatively high firing rates.107 Dentate granule cells, which are located in the granule cell layer and have sparse activity and low firing rates, are difficult to detect with silicon probes in awake, head-fixed animals and were rarely seen in our recordings. Though granule cells are the main recipients of input from EC2, all DG excitatory neurons reflect the timing of ongoing processing within the DG and are useful to examine the relationship between oscillatory activity and local firing in DG. We did not see significant changes in phase locking of DG excitatory neurons (Figure 6D). Conversely, phase locking was reduced in DG interneurons recorded from 6-month-old 3xTg mice, and this decrease persisted at 8 months of age relative to neurons from age-matched WT mice (Figure 6E). Interestingly, phase locking was also reduced in WT DG interneurons between 6 and 8 months of age, suggesting that DG interneurons are also impacted by normal early aging processes between these time points. Taken together, these findings point to both early and late-onset synchronization deficits within the HPC of 3xTg mice, with DG interneuron dysfunction emerging early in disease progression. Conversely, CA1 phase locking deficits emerged with a similar time frame to the onset of memory impairment, abnormal network activity, and upstream deficits in MEC circuits.

In addition to reductions in the strength of phase locking, alterations in single-unit firing patterns can also manifest as a shift in the preferred firing phase (mean firing phase, or mu-value). In WT mice, DG excitatory units tend to fire, on average, at the descending phase of CA1 theta oscillations, while CA1 excitatory units tend to fire slightly later, close to the trough (Figure 6F and 6H).81,89,91,92,108 This relationship, where firing in DG is followed by firing in CA1 after a short delay, may facilitate and reflect information flow through the hippocampal tri-synaptic loop. Thus, a shift in spike timing in these two regions is likely to impact intrahippocampal communication. Indeed, we found a disrupted phase relationship in the timing of excitatory neurons in DG and CA1 that emerged in 8-month-old 3xTg mice. Excitatory CA1 units in 8-month-old 3xTg mice tended to fire at an earlier phase of theta relative to age-matched WT controls (Figure 6F), while excitatory units in DG tended to fire at a later phase of theta compared to WT controls (Figure 6H). Together, these changes both contribute to an abnormal temporal relationship between DG and CA1 excitatory neural activity and suggest that information flow through the HPC is disrupted in 8-month-old 3xTg mice.

In hippocampal interneurons, we found that mu values in CA1 were unaffected in 3xTg mice at either time point (Figure 6G), while DG interneurons were shifted toward later phases of theta at 6-month in 3xTg mice (Figure 6I). This early-onset shift in DG interneurons further suggests that 3xTg mice have early DG interneuron dysfunction, although this change was normalized by 8-month of age, perhaps due to some compensatory response to early dysfunction. Notably, none of the changes we observed in phase locking strength or preferred phase can be explained by changes in hippocampal firing rates, as there were no differences in firing rates of either CA1 or DG excitatory or inhibitory neurons in 3xTg mice at either time point (Figures S10A–S10D). Altogether, our analysis of phase locking in HPC suggests that DG interneurons may be vulnerable to early dysfunction prior to the onset of memory impairments, while CA1 dysfunction occurs later and aligns more closely with the onset of cognitive deficits.

Although we found no changes in overall firing rates, our phase locking results led us to ask whether other metrics of spike timing might be disrupted. Phase precession is a well-established feature of CA1 excitatory neurons,109,110 in which a neuron fires at progressively earlier phases of theta as an animal moves through that neuron’s place field. This is thought to support the formation of sequential firing patterns in order to encode trajectories through space. Phase precession relies on entorhinal inputs to CA1,26,89,111 and thus we predicted that dysfunction in MEC timing might disrupt phase precession in CA1 of 8-month-old 3xTg mice. Although our head-fixed VR recordings were not optimized for detection and analysis of place cells, phase precession can be assessed by comparing the frequency of individual unit spiking to the theta frequency in the CA1 LFP.91,112 Cells showing phase precession should exhibit spike trains with frequencies slightly faster than the theta frequency. Indeed, in 6-month WT, 6-month 3xTg, and 8-month WT mice, we found that most CA1 excitatory neurons fired ~1-3Hz faster than the theta frequency of the corresponding LFP, consistent with phase precession in these neurons. However, in 8-month-old 3xTg mice, the difference between the frequency of CA1 spike trains and frequency of theta oscillations was increased when compared to age-matched WT neurons (Figure S10E), suggesting a loss of precession-like firing patterns. Many of these neurons had large differences between unit and LFP frequency, suggesting that this is not simply increased phase precession, but represents a substantially disorganized spiking pattern. This seems to be driven at least in part by increased spiking frequency of units within a spike train (Figure S10F). Overall, these results provide further evidence of disrupted hippocampal spike timing in 8-month-old 3xTg mice.

DISCUSSION

By recording entorhinal-hippocampal activity at time points before or after the emergence of spatial memory impairments in 3xTg mice, we found deficits in local and long-range synchronization that coincided with the onset of spatial memory deficits at 8 months of age (Figures 1B and 1C). We observed several network-level changes in LFPs including reduced hippocampal theta power (Figures 3D and F) and reduced theta coherence between MEC and CA1 in 8-month-old 3xTg mice (Figure 3J). We also found desynchronization at the level of single units, including reduced theta phase locking of MEC3 neurons (Figure 4), hyperactivity and impaired timing of MEC2 excitatory neuron firing relative to hippocampal theta (Figure 5), and disrupted phase locking of downstream CA1 and DG neurons (Figure 6). Together, these deficits in synchronization within and between MEC and HPC represent a disrupted circuit that is lacking the precise timing and organization that supports information processing and spatial memory in healthy animals.81,113,114 These deficits all emerged along a similar time course to memory impairments, suggesting that they may be potential contributors to the development of memory deficits in 3xTg mice.

Disrupted entorhinal-hippocampal circuit processing aligns with the time course of memory impairment in 3xTg mice

By recording neural activity at time points immediately prior to or after the onset of spatial memory impairments, we were able to find circuit deficits that paralleled memory impairment in 3xTg mice. Many studies have examined circuit properties in mouse models of AD pathology, but most have relied solely on time points after the onset of memory deficits, making it difficult to determine which deficits might underlie memory impairments. Importantly, the circuit deficits that directly drive memory impairment should emerge with a similar time course to memory deficits. Conversely, early physiological changes that emerge prior to memory impairment may represent early biomarkers of disease, but are not sufficient to drive memory impairment on their own. Here, we identified several important circuit mechanisms that emerged along the time course of memory impairment, including reduced hippocampal theta power, reduced MEC-CA1 theta coherence, and disrupted spike timing of MEC and HPC neurons. While the causal role of these synchronization processes still needs further investigation, these deficits are likely to contribute to the memory impairments observed in these animals. On the other hand, we also observed early deficits in MEC PV+ interneuron number (Figures S9A–S9C) and inhibitory spike timing in HPC (Figure 6E) that emerged prior to memory impairments. These inhibitory deficits highlight interneuron vulnerability early in the disease process. While these changes are not sufficient to drive memory deficits alone, they could contribute to a cascade of changes that promote cognitive deficits later in disease progression.

Notably, the time course of memory impairment preceded the emergence of aggregated amyloid plaques and neurofibrillary tau. While memory impairments and entorhinal-hippocampal desynchronization emerged between 6 and 8 months of age, we found little to no aggregated pathology at these time points (Figures 1D and 1E). Indeed, recent work extensively characterizing pathology in 3xTg mice showed an increase in insoluble Aβ in HPC and an onset of plaque formation in subiculum and CA1 around 12 months of age, which is in line with our immunohistochemistry results.63 This data suggests that non-aggregated (i.e., soluble) forms of pathology are likely to drive the circuit and memory deficits we observed. Buildup of soluble APP and Aβ has been shown prior to overt plaque formation in these animals, and soluble oligomeric forms of Aβ have been shown to be particularly toxic to neurons.60,62,65,66,68,115–117 Intracellular Aβ has also been found to accumulate in subiculum/CA1 prior to plaque deposition in 3xTg mice.60 While there are no neurofibrillary tangles in 3xTg mice at the ages examined here, minimal hyperphosphorylated tau has been reported in subiculum by 6 months old, spreading into CA1 with age.60,64 Therefore, the presence of increased APP, Aβ, and phosphorylated tau that have not yet formed plaques or tangles could contribute to the physiological changes and memory impairment we report here.

Future studies will need to examine in vivo physiology across mouse models with distinct mutations (i.e., only amyloid or tau-related mutations) to establish which circuit changes converge or diverge across models. It is important to note, however, that the precise relationship between the accumulation of pathological proteins and cognitive impairment is unclear, while changes in network activity may be more predictive of cognitive decline and more responsive to noninvasive therapies.38,118,119 Thus, it is critical to elucidate what changes in brain activity and circuit function are most closely associated with memory impairments to design interventions that can ameliorate cognitive decline.

Reductions in theta power and synchrony suggest impaired communication between MEC and hippocampus in 3xTg mice

We observed major deficits in the synchronization of HPC and MEC in 3xTg mice. Because the entorhinal cortex is a major driver of hippocampal theta,81,113 reduced hippocampal theta power and CSD magnitude in 8-month-old 3xTg mice (Figure 3) are consistent with a reduction of properly timed input from the entorhinal cortex to HPC. This could be driven by synaptic loss or weakened synaptic transmission between the entorhinal cortex and HPC,120–122 and/or by disruptions in spike timing and coordination between regions, an interpretation supported by the intact MEC theta power in these mice. Indeed, poor coordination of spiking between the entorhinal cortex and HPC may impair plasticity between these regions, which could also contribute to synaptic loss and reduced hippocampal theta power.

While reduced theta power was present throughout the HPC (Figure 3A), synchrony was reduced specifically between MEC and CA1, with no changes in theta synchrony between CA1 and DG (Figures 3G–3J). Notably, we found no changes in hippocampal theta power, CSD magnitude, or MEC-CA1 coherence across normal aging in WT mice (all statistics reported in Table S1). Prior studies have suggested a loss of synchrony between EC and HPC in other rodent models of AD pathology, but these findings were based on tetrode recordings from EC and CA1 pyramidal layer only, leaving uncertainty regarding when and where this desynchrony emerges and how it might relate to other neural activity patterns.14,39 Here, the use of silicon probes allowed us to perform laminar recordings across MEC, DG, and CA1 at multiple time points, revealing a reduction of theta coherence specifically between MEC and CA1 oriens, pyramidal layer, and radiatum in 8-month-old 3xTg mice (Figure 3J). Reduced theta synchrony between MEC and CA1 could reflect a loss of synchronized inputs to these regions, such as those from the medial septum, which projects to both MEC and HPC and modulates theta frequency.123–125 Changes in local hippocampal interneurons or inputs from CA3 to CA1 could also impact CA1 theta oscillations, but are unlikely to fully explain the magnitude of these changes in power and coherence.81,108,126–129 In addition, recent work has identified additional projections of MEC2 pyramidal cells to CA1122,130 and long-range inhibitory projections from MEC to HPC103,104,131 that could also mediate MEC-hippocampal synchronization. Further work is needed to explore how these other projections within this circuitry may contribute to desynchrony in AD.

Progressive disruptions of MEC spike timing in 3xTg mice

We also found significant disruptions in the timing of single-unit activity in MEC and HPC. Past studies of entorhinal physiology in rodent models of AD pathology have primarily examined MEC or the entorhinal cortex as a whole, without layer specificity. Here, the enhanced spatial resolution granted by silicon probes allowed us to differentiate between populations of cells in MEC2 and MEC3, which have unique projections to HPC. We examined theta phase locking of MEC and hippocampal units as a metric for synchrony at the single-cell level. Precise spike timing is necessary for plasticity and the encoding of spatial representations, while improper timing may contribute to impaired plasticity and disrupted information coding.25–27 In particular, precise timing of entorhinal inputs into HPC supports coordinated sequential firing patterns in HPC that have been linked to successful spatial memory encoding.7,26,88,89

In MEC3 neurons, we found reduced phase locking to both MEC and hippocampal theta in 8-month-old 3xTg mice, coincident with the emergence of memory impairments (Figure 4). Our data indicate that MEC3 neurons are particularly vulnerable to progressive dysfunction in 3xTg mice. The loss of phase locking to both MEC and CA1 theta suggests that these changes are not driven solely by a loss of coherence between MEC and CA1, but rather by a change in the firing patterns of MEC neurons. Meanwhile, excitatory MEC2 neurons in 8-month-old 3xTg mice exhibited reduced phase locking only to CA1 theta compared to neurons from age-matched WT mice, consistent with a loss of coherence between MEC and CA1 (Figure 5). Overall, the timing of MEC spiking relative to hippocampal theta was disrupted, suggesting that inputs to HPC are not arriving at the optimal time, which could drive impaired plasticity, spatial processing, and spatial memory. Impaired timing of MEC units may also contribute to the progressive changes in phase locking we found in CA1, where projections from MEC terminate.

An important consideration when examining phase locking is to ensure that our results are not driven by changes in the reference signal. Here, we have used the same reference points (middle of CA1 pyramidal layer or MEC) across measurements and found selective impairments in specific subcircuits and populations of neurons. For example, when using CA1 theta as a reference, we found reduced phase locking of MEC2 (Figure 5D) and MEC3 (Figure 4D) excitatory neurons, but intact (and strong) phase locking of MEC2 inhibitory neurons (Figure 5G). Similarly, both MEC2 excitatory (Figure 5C) and inhibitory (Figure 5F) neurons are strongly phase locked to MEC2 theta. The presence of intact phase locking relative to these reference signals would only be possible if theta was sufficiently intact at these locations. If our results showing reduced theta coherence or phase locking were simply due to reduced theta power, we would expect to see changes across all analyses that utilize CA1 theta. Thus, any deficits that we observe can be attributed to the altered timing of the cell population and do not simply reflect absent theta oscillations.

Hyperexcitability, interneuron vulnerability, and cell loss in MEC of 3xTg mice

In addition to poor timing, inputs from MEC to HPC could also be disrupted by a loss of excitatory-inhibitory (E/I) balance in MEC. MEC hyperexcitability in 3xTg mice has been previously characterized in vitro by our lab and others.22,95 We recently showed increased firing and E/I imbalance specific to MEC2 stellate cells (but not other excitatory MEC2/3 cell types) in 10-month-old 3xTg mice in vitro.22 Here, we found an increase in firing rates of 3xTg excitatory MEC2 units at 8 months relative to age-matched WT units (Figure 5B). Though we are unable to differentiate between stellate and pyramidal neurons in MEC2 in vivo, our past work in vitro suggests that this is driven primarily by stellate cells, which project to the DG. Broadly, Aβ has been shown to promote increased excitability and/or hyperactivity in neurons by various mechanisms, including disrupting glutamate reuptake, even without plaque formation.68,115,116,132 Thus, it is possible that these changes are due to the presence of increased Aβ. However, other work has suggested that impaired inhibition may also contribute to or exacerbate increased firing in excitatory cells.22,23,133 While we see a numerical increase in MEC3 excitatory firing rates as well, this change did not reach statistical significance (adjusted p value = 0.094). It is possible that differences in interneuron connectivity and/or in intrinsic excitability across layers and cell types could contribute to increased vulnerability of MEC2 neurons in particular. Notably, we did not see changes in average firing rate in any hippocampal cell types (Figure S10), suggesting a specific vulnerability to hyperactivity in MEC.

Local PV+ interneurons provide a major source of inhibition to excitatory cells in MEC and HPC.98,99,108,134,135 Hippocampal interneuron dysfunction has been widely characterized in rodent models of AD pathology,8,84,100,102,136–139 while entorhinal interneuron vulnerability has gained interest more recently.23 Here, we found reduced PV+ interneuron labeling in MEC of 3xTg mice regardless of age (Figures S9A–S9C). This likely reflects cell death of PV+ interneurons, but could also be due to dysfunction of these cells that leads to reduced parvalbumin protein expression. Overall, impaired inhibition and increased excitatory firing rates could promote excitotoxicity, potentially contributing to the neuronal loss we observed in MEC. Cell death in 3xTg MEC2 could also lead to a loss of inputs from MEC2 into HPC by 8 months of age (Figure S9E). The loss of MEC neurons may also contribute to the observed deficits in theta power and coherence, since entorhinal inputs are a primary driver of theta in HPC.81 However, the emergence of MEC3 neuronal loss (Figure S9F) prior to changes in theta power and coherence suggests that spike timing changes that emerge at 8 months may play a critical role as well. PV+ inhibitory neurons in MEC also receive input from the medial septum, which is critical for theta generation, and send long-range inputs to CA1, making them a point of interest for future work investigating the mechanisms underlying synchrony in these circuits.

Progressive CA1 phase locking deficits and early DG interneuron dysfunction in 3xTg mice

CA1 serves as an integration site for information received directly from EC3 and indirectly from EC2 via DG/CA3. We found reductions in phase locking strength of CA1 excitatory and inhibitory units in 3xTg mice that primarily emerged at 8 months of age (Figures 6B and 6C). These changes could be linked to the upstream changes we see in MEC firing patterns or to the dysfunction of local interneurons, which are known to gate entorhinal inputs and modulate spiking of excitatory cells.108,140,141 Altered firing patterns in CA1 could disrupt the formation and maintenance of spatial maps, thereby contributing to spatial memory impairments. Indeed, previous work has shown worse spatial coding in CA1 place cells in 3xTg mice.13 We also examined phase locking earlier in the hippocampal loop in DG, which receives input from MEC2. We have previously shown that theta phase locking of DG interneurons is disrupted in a mouse model of chronic temporal lobe epilepsy with significant spatial memory deficits and disrupted hippocampal spatial coding.142,143 However, further studies are needed to causally link these changes (see limitations of the study for further discussion). Here, we find early deficits in DG interneuron phase locking strength in a mouse model of AD pathology (Figure 6E), as well as a reduction in phase locking across normal aging. Thus, changes in DG interneuron phase locking may be a common mechanism of hippocampal circuit dysfunction across neurological disorders and aging.

Lastly, we examined the preferred firing phase of hippocampal units (Figure 6). In WT mice, DG excitatory units tended to fire at the descending phase of theta oscillations, while CA1 units fired slightly later, at the trough.81,89,91,92,108 This time lag likely reflects synaptic transmission through the tri-synaptic loop, from DG to CA1 via CA3. In 3xTg mice, however, CA1 units shifted their firing earlier, while DG units shifted their firing later, thereby bringing the average firing phase of these populations closer together (Figures 6F and 6H). Previous work has shown that the theta phase preference of CA1 and DG excitatory units depends upon the relative strength and timing of entorhinal and intrahippocampal inputs.7,89 Based on this evidence, the observed shifts in spike timing may reflect weakening or impaired timing of MEC inputs to CA1 and DG. This could result in an increased influence of other inputs (e.g., inputs from CA3 to CA1 and from commissural pathways to DG). These shifts toward earlier CA1 and later DG spike timing could also reflect subtle hyper- and hypo-excitability in these two regions, respectively. While we did not see any changes in overall average firing rates, when we examined the relative frequency of spike trains compared to theta LFP frequency in CA1 units (a proxy for phase precession), we found that WT and 6-month 3xTg neurons fired just slightly faster than theta frequency, consistent with precession-like behavior (Figures S10E and S10F). This relationship was disrupted in 8-month-old 3xTg mice, providing further evidence of disrupted hippocampal spike timing. Overall, abnormal hippocampal firing patterns may reflect altered strength and/or timing of inputs as well as subtle changes in excitability.

Limitations of the study

Here, we identified several circuit deficits that parallel the emergence of memory impairments in 3xTg mice. While alignment between the emergence of memory and circuit deficits suggests that the disrupted timing of entorhinal-hippocampal neural activity may directly contribute to memory impairment, it is important to acknowledge that these findings are correlational and not sufficient to establish a causal link. In addition, our electrophysiology recordings were not performed during our memory task, which could have provided additional insight into how neurophysiological changes impact behavior. Instead, we chose to record neural activity using multiple high-density silicon probes while animals ran on a linear track, an approach that had important strengths and limitations. First, using a head-fixed recording setup allowed us to perform a large number of recordings (34 total across groups), which was necessary to examine two genotypes at two different time points. In addition, our silicon probes allowed us to precisely target individual sublayers of MEC, DG, and CA1 to examine the coordination throughout the entorhinal-hippocampal circuit. Alternative approaches, such as tetrode recordings (which typically focus on a single cell layer)13,39 or freely behaving dual-region silicon probe recordings (which are technically challenging and typically have very low sample sizes),26,144 would have prevented us from collecting key aspects of this dataset. Of course, our approach also had significant limitations. For instance, the use of acute silicon probe recordings in head-fixed mice did not yield enough spatially tuned units to reliably examine spatial coding in our animals. However, it is important to note that disrupted synchronization could mediate spatial memory deficits independent of changes in spatial coding.26 While deficits in place cells are well-established in 3xTg mice (and other models of AD pathology), spatial coding metrics do not always coincide with changes in spatial memory.14,145 Even if place or grid cells are coding for a stable location, disrupted synchronization would still impair their ability to create a coherent spatial representation necessary for behavior. Future studies will be needed to directly manipulate neural synchrony during a memory task to establish the causal relationship between entorhinal-hippocampal synchronization, spatial coding, and memory performance. Fortunately, new technologies are being developed to enable precise manipulations of neural synchrony between brain regions as well as precise manipulations of the phase locking of neurons to endogenous theta oscillations.126,127

The NOL task is a well-validated spatial memory task that is dependent on both the HPC and MEC,6,53–55 but has important limitations. Indeed, there is substantial variability across protocols that can affect performance, including the behavioral apparatus (e.g., lighting conditions, choice of objects), amount of training, whether the amount of time (e.g., Protocol 1) or amount of exploration (e.g., Protocol 2) is controlled for, the amount of habituation, the delay between training and test, and even housing conditions.146 In our data, we found that WT mice had differences in learning across protocols, which may reflect early age-dependent changes that impact performance. Indeed, even WT mice can begin to show deficits in NOL or novel object recognition performance by 8 months of age.56–59 Differences in protocols may reveal underlying changes in anxiety, exploration, or locomotion that emerge in early aging. Importantly, the NOL task is just one measure of spatial memory, and there is variability in the onset of memory impairments across different spatial learning tasks in 3xTg mice.147 While the primary purpose of our study was to determine how MEC-hippocampal circuits are impacted in 3xTg mice, it is critical to study AD-related pathology in the context of normal aging. Indeed, aging is the top risk factor for AD, and interacts with aging mechanisms as the disease progresses. Thus, to adapt to changes across early aging in our WT mice, we used two slightly modified protocols that controlled for the amount of time in the box (Protocol 1) or the amount of interaction with the objects (Protocol 2) and produced robust performance in 6- and 8-month-old WT mice, respectively (Figure 1D). Importantly, our 3xTg mice clearly demonstrated intact NOL performance in 6-month-old 3xTg mice and impaired performance in 8-month-old 3xTg mice across protocols (Figure 1E). Future experiments will be needed to determine how individual features of the training protocol, experimental setup, or housing conditions may impact performance in WT mice at these early aging time points.

Finally, while medial entorhinal inputs to HPC are critical for spatial memory and hippocampal oscillatory dynamics, other inputs and circuits not examined here may also play important roles. For example, the medial septum also projects to MEC and HPC and may be vulnerable in the context of AD. Future work should examine the role of additional brain regions in entorhinal-hippocampal dynamics.

EXPERIMENTAL MODEL AND STUDY PARTICIPANT DETAILS

All experiments were approved by the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai’s Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) in compliance with the US National Institutes of Health (NIH) guidelines and protocols. Male and female 3xTg (MMRRC 034830-JAX)52 and wild-type (WT; B6129SF2/J, strain #101045, The Jackson Laboratory) mice were used in all experiments. Breeders were purchased from the Mutant Mouse Resource Center (MMRRC) and Jackson Laboratories, and all experimental animals were bred in breeding facilities at Mount Sinai. Mice received ad libitum food and water unless water restricted for virtual reality training. All animals were kept on a 12-h light/dark cycle (lights on at 7:00a.m.) and housed with at least one cage-mate. If a socially compatible littermate was not available, animals were housed with an ovariectomized female mouse (129X1/SvJ, Jackson Laboratory). Experiments took place during the light phase.

METHOD DETAILS

Novel object location behavior

Novel object location behavior was performed in 6 and 8-month-old WT and 3xTg mice. Average age for each group (mean and sem): 6.05 ± 0.03 (WT 6 mo), 6.03 ± 0.04 (3xTg 6 mo), 8.05 +−/0.11 (WT 8 mo), 8.10 ± 0.14 (3xTg 8 mo). We utilized two NOL protocols in this study. All data from both protocols in all four groups can be found in Figures 1D and 1E and in Table S2. Mice underwent one of two NOL protocols.

Protocol 1 (6-month animals in Figure 1B): Animals were handled 1x daily in the experimental room for 5 days, followed the next day by one 30 min habituation to the behavioral chamber with no objects present. Each chamber consisted of a white 1 ft × 1ft x 1ft white plastic box with spatial cues on each interior wall. On the following day, mice were placed in the empty chamber for six minutes (pre-exposure) and removed. The experimenter then placed two identical objects (plastic toys taped to the floor at two corners of the box) in the chamber and returned the mouse to the chamber for three 6-min training sessions. 2 h later, mice were returned to the chamber with one object moved, and allowed to explore for 15 min. We have previously found this protocol to drive robust learning in young mice.93

Protocol 2 (8-month animals in Figure 1C): Animals were handled 1x daily in the experimental room for 5 days, followed by two 5-minute-long sessions of habituation to the behavioral chambers across two days with no objects present. Each chamber consisted of a white 1 ft × 1ft x 1ft white plastic box with spatial cues on each interior wall. On the following day, mice were placed in the chamber with two identical objects (plastic toys taped to the floor at two corners of the box) and allowed to explore until they reached a criterion of 30s of total exploration time, as measured by an experimenter using a stopwatch. This criterion was used to ensure that all groups explored the objects equally and thus had the opportunity to learn about their locations, despite potential differences in activity levels between groups.148 After reaching this criterion, mice were removed from the chamber and returned to their home cage. Four hours later, animals were placed back in the same chamber with one object moved to a new location and allowed to explore until they reached a 20s exploration criterion, for a maximum of 20 min. Animals that did not reach criterion, that met criterion during the training phase in under 5 min, or that had a bias during training such that they spent 70% or more of their exploration time with one of the two objects were excluded from the experiment.

In both protocols, exploration was defined as time the mouse spent within 2 cm of the object, with its nose facing the object, and actively sniffing the object. Videos were scored using Chronotate,149 by an experimenter blinded to experimental group. Performance was calculated by taking time spent exploring the novel object divided by the total time exploring both objects. It is also important to note that data from Protocol 1 and Protocol 2 were collected by different experimenters and included different lighting conditions and objects, which likely impacted the raw exploration values. Thus, the two protocols are not directly comparable. Locomotion metrics during habituation in a separate cohort of animals in Figures S1A–S1C were quantified using ezTrack.150

Immunohistochemistry

Mice were perfused with phosphate buffered saline (PBS, Fisher BP399), followed by 4% paraformaldehyde (Wako). Brains were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, followed by 30% sucrose (Fisher). After saturation (~2 days), they were frozen at −80°C until sectioning. Brains were sectioned sagittally on a cryostat at 40 μm thickness, and sections were stored in 1x PBS for up to 1 day prior to staining. Sections were washed 3x for 10 min in TBS. This was followed by two hours of blocking at room temperature with 0.3% Triton X-100 and 3% Normal Goat Serum in TBS. Next, sections were incubated in 3% normal goat serum with 0.3% Triton X-100 in TBS and primary antibody (NeuN, anti-rabbit Millipore Sigma ABN78, 1:2000, or PV anti-rabbit, ThermoFisher PA-1933, 1:1000) overnight on a shaker at 4°C. On the following day, sections were washed 4x for 15 min in 1x TBS, before being incubated with secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature (Goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488, 1:500). Finally, sections were washed again 4x for 15-min in PBS.

For Aβ immunohistochemistry, an additional step was added at the start of the protocol to improve antigen retrieval.151 Plates were incubated on a heating block at 80°C for 45 min in sodium citrate buffer. Tissue was then incubated with the primary antibody (Aβ Cell Signaling #2454, 1:400) for 48 h.

Following staining, tissue was mounted on microscope slides with DAPI mounting media (SouthernBiotech DAPI Fluoromount-Gm, 0100-20). Slices were imaged with a 20x objective using a Leica DM6B fluorescence microscope with a Lumencor Light Engine and Leica DFC9000 GT camera. PV and NeuN Cell counting were performed using a cell counting algorithm in Python (https://github.com/ZachPenn/CellCounting) by an experimenter blinded to experimental group. For all quantitative experiments, imaging parameters were maintained across subjects. Regions of interest (ROIs) were drawn manually around each layer or subregion by an experimenter blinded to group and counts for a given slice and region were divided by the total area of the corresponding ROI to generate counts per square mm.

Headbar surgery

All animals used in electrophysiology experiments underwent stereotaxic surgery to attach a stainless steel headbar to the skull at approximately 4.5 or 6.5 months old. Mice were anesthetized with 1–3% isoflurane and fixed in a stereotaxic surgical apparatus. Lidocaine (2%, ~0.05mL, Fresenius Kabi) was administered subcutanerously around the epaxial muscles. Hair over the surgical site was removed and skin was cleaned with 70% ethanol (Fisher) and betadine (Purdue Frederick). The skull was exposed and the top layer of epaxial muscles were detached from the skull using fine forceps. Hydrogen peroxide (3%, MEDLINE) was used to remove the fascia and allow clearer identification of bregma and lambda. The exposed skull was scored with a scalpel. The skull was aligned stereotaxically prior to attaching the headbar to the skull using cyanoacrylate glue and dental cement (LANG Dental). A barrier around the exposed skull was created by building up a well of dental cement. The well was filled with Kwik-Sil (World Precision Instruments, Cat# 600022) and covered with a final thin layer of dental cement. Subcutaneous carprofen (5 mg/kg, Zoetis) was delivered subcutaneously during and for two days following surgery. Ampicillin (20 mg/kg, Sigma Aldritch) was delivered during and for six days following surgery. Animals recovered on a heating pad before being returned to their housing facility.

Virtual reality training

After one week of recovery from surgery, animals were handled for 4 min per day for 3–4 days, then habituated to head-fixation on a flat surface for 3–4 days or until they were able to walk calmly while head-fixed. Beginning during the handling phase, mice were water-restricted and weighed daily to ensure that they maintained ~82% of their starting body weight. Additional water was provided if animals showed signs of dehydration or weight dropped below 78%. Next, animals were head-fixed and secured on top of a Styrofoam ball and allowed to habituate to the ball for ~30 min per day for 3–4 days. Once animals were able to walk comfortably on the ball, they were trained to lick a water port to receive 4 μL drops of water while headfixed. Our lickport uses an infrared beam to detect licks and deliver water via a solenoid valve (Lee Company) during lick training.152 Once mice learned to lick readily for water (~3–4 days), animals were introduced to a virtual reality (VR) environment designed in MATLAB using the open-source software, ViRMEn,153 where they were required to run to the end of a track to receive a 4uL water rewards, after which they were automatically teleported back to the start of the track. Running speed was measured using an optical motion tracker located along the underside of the Styrofoam ball. The reading from this sensor triggered corresponding movement of the VR environment. The virtual linear track was displayed on three 24-inch computer monitors angled around the Styrofoam ball and centered slightly above the mouse’s eye-line. Once mice were running 100 trials within a 1-h session they were considered ready for recording. On average, VR training took 8–10 days. The total time from initial surgery to final recording was approximately 37–43 total calendar days or 5–6 weeks.

Craniotomy and ground wire Implantation surgery

On the day before recording, a craniotomy surgery was performed to expose the brain over the desired recording sites. The dental cement and Kwik Sil covering the skull were removed, a burr hole was drilled over the left cerebellum, and an Ag/AgCl coated wire (Warner Instruments) was slipped between the skull and dura to serve as a reference. The wire was secured in place with cyanoacrylate glue and dental cement. Two craniotomies were drilled over right hippocampus and entorhinal cortex, respectively, each around 1.6 mm in diameter, centered around the desired recording coordinates. Craniotomies were covered with buffered artificial cerebrospinal fluid (ACSF; in mM: 135 NaCl, 5 KCl, 5 HEPES, 2.4 CaCl2, 2.1 MgCl2, pH 7.4).142,154The skull was once again covered with Kwik-Sil and animals were allowed to recover on a heating pad overnight prior to recording. Carprofen (5 mg/kg) and dexamethasone (0.2 mg/kg, Fresenius Kabi), were given subcutaneously following surgery to reduce pain and inflammation.

Acute silicon probe recordings

Silicon probes were obtained through the Masmanidis Lab at UCLA, and their production and distribution were supported by the NSF NeuroNex program (more information available at: https://masmanidislab.neurobio.ucla.edu/images/microprobe_info.pdf). Two 256-channel silicon probes were used in all experiments, with one probe inserted into hippocampus and the other inserted into MEC. Each probe consisted of 4 shanks with 64 channels per shank. Recording sites spanned across the bottom 1.050mm of the shank across three columns with 25 μm vertical spacing and 20 μm horizontal spacing between channels. Probes were electroplated with gold solution (Sifco) to achieve 0.1 to 0.5 MΩ impedance. Prior to recording, probes were coated in Di-I to facilitate later histological verification of probe locations.

On the recording day, the mouse was head-fixed on the Styrofoam ball as usual. Kwik-Sil over the skull was removed and the exposed brain was covered with ACSF. The skull was aligned to sit flat to ensure accuracy of probe targeting, and the ground wire was connected to the amplifiers to serve as a reference. The entire behavioral and recording setup are secured to an air table to reduce the potential influence of any external vibrations on electrophysiological signals. Probes were aligned to bregma and inserted into the brain at roughly the following coordinates: Hippocampus: 1.8mm posterior, 1.35mm right, 2.2mm ventral from bregma or until all hippocampal layers were detected upon examination of physiological signals; MEC: 4.9mm posterior, 3.1mm right, and lowered until the bottom of the probe appeared to reach layer 1 (indicated by a shift in theta phase and reduced spiking activity). Micromanipulators (Sutter Instruments) were used to lower probes into the brain at a rate of 2–5 μm/s, which has been shown to improve cell yield in silicon probe recordings.155 After reaching the desired recording location, probes were left to settle in the brain for at least one hour prior to beginning recording.

Signals were sent to four Intan headstages (RHD 128-Channel Recording Headstage, Intan Technologies, two connected to each probe). An Intan recording controller (RHD2000 Intan 1024ch Recording Controller, Intan Technologies) was used to collect the signals at 25kHz, along with analog signals reflecting the position of the animal on the linear track, water delivery from the lickport, licks sensed by an infrared beam break in front of the water port, and running speed (measured via an optical sensor that recorded ball rotation speed). Recordings lasted between 1.5 and 4 h. Average ages in months by group at time of recording: 6.19 ± 0.16 (WT 6 mo), 6.31 ± 0.16 (3xTg 6 mo), 8.36 ± 0.18 (WT 8 mo), and 8.41 ± 0.28 (3xTg 8 mo).

Histology

Following recording, silicon probes were removed from the brain slowly using the micromanipulators. Mice were perfused with 1x PBS followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were post-fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde overnight, followed by 30% sucrose. After saturation, they were frozen on dry ice and kept frozen at −80° until sectioning. Brains were sectioned sagittally on a cryostat at 80 μm thickness, and sections were stored in 24 well plates in 1x PBS. Immunohistochemistry for NeuN was performed to aid in identifying cell layers in MEC. Procedures for staining were identical to those described above in the “Immunohistochemistry” section, with one exception. Triton was replaced by Tween for this tissue to maintain presence of Di-I in the slices. Locations of recording channels were determined by visualizing the Di-I labeled probe tracts for each animal and cross-referencing with the corresponding physiological signals. Locations of theta phase shifts, peak theta power, peak fast ripple power, relative changes in spiking activity, and overall coherence patterns across channels were used to estimate where each channel on a given shank was located.88,91,142,156,157

MEC layer assignments were made using the theta phase shift in layer 1–2, the relative increase in spiking activity in MEC2 compared to MEC1 and 3, and in some cases the increased phase locking of neurons in MEC2 relative to MEC3.91 For hippocampus, the mid-point of the CA1 pyramidal layer was defined as 1–2 channels before the initiation of a theta phase shift and confirmed by checking for the presence of a peak in fast ripple power. The boundary between radiatum and lacunosum moleculare was defined by the end of the theta phase shift. The boundary between lacunosum moleculare and molecular layer was defined by the presence of a peak in theta power. Remaining hippocampal layer boundaries were defined by plotting coherence across all channels and looking for blocks of high coherence that occur within a layer.

Analysis of local field potential and single-unit data

All data preprocessing was performed using custom scripts in MATLAB 2017A. First, data files were concatenated to form a single file for each channel. For local field potential (LFP) analysis, data was downsampled to 1000 Hz and bandpass filtered for more specific frequency bands as needed. For LFP analyses, one shank from MEC and one shank from HPC were used for each animal. The shank with the best coverage of the target regions and the fewest bad channels (i.e., channels with no signal or poor impedance) was chosen for analysis. To calculate theta power and coherence, data was first filtered for oscillations in the theta frequency band (5–12 Hz). Next, the amplitude of theta oscillations over time was calculated by taking the envelope of the theta filtered signal. This amplitude was then averaged across time, only including time points during which animals were running within a range of speeds between 0.1 and 0.2m/s (10-20cm/s) (see Figure S3). This range was selected to ensure that there were no differences in running speed across groups within the analyzed recording periods (Figure S3C). This subsampling approach was used for LFP and single unit analyses to ensure that our results could not be explained by differences in running speed or time spent running across groups. Coherence of theta filtered LFPs was calculated using an adapted version of the coherency function in the Chronux library158 and averaged across frequencies between 5 and 12 Hz. Coherence was calculated across the recording in one second bins and then subsampled to include only time periods where animals were running at speeds within the pre-defined thresholds.

For power calculations, power was averaged across all channels within a layer for each animal. To generate coherence values for a given pair of layers, coherence between each potential pair of channels was calculated and the resulting matrix was averaged. For frequency calculations in Figure S6, a wavelet transformation was used to calculate power spectral density between 5 and 12 Hz. We found the frequency with the highest power at each time point and averaged across all times within the running threshold. For phase locking analyses, a channel in mid CA1 pyramidal layer prior to any theta phase shift was used as the CA1 theta reference and a channel in mid-MEC2 was used as the MEC theta reference. These reference channels were checked manually to ensure they were free from noise or artifacts.

Current spectral density (CSD) was calculated using code obtained from the Buzsáki lab repository (https://github.com/buzsakilab/buzcode). By taking the second spatial derivative of the LFP data across channels, we were able to isolate local current sources and sinks that were unlikely to be generated by volume conduction. This allows more precise localization and quantification of currents generated within the hippocampus. One row of 21 linear channels (each silicon probe shank contained 3 rows with 64 channels total) was used for analysis, and average CSDs were calculated across all theta cycles that occurred while an animal was running within the speed threshold.

For single unit analysis, data from each recording was background subtracted and converted to a binary file. Kilosort 2.5 was used for initial spike sorting, followed by manual curation in Phy.159–161 An experimenter blinded to experimental group manually inspected all units to ensure that each unit had a plausible waveform (i.e., not noise) and low refractory violations. Over or undersplit clusters were recombined or split manually as needed to ensure well isolated, non-redundant units. Any units with less than 20 spikes on during periods where the animal was running within the speed threshold were excluded from further analysis.

Because our recordings lasted an hour or more, a given cell may only be detectable for a fraction of this time. Drift in probe location is a common challenge in acute silicon probe recordings, and a cell may be near recording sites at the start of the recording but out of range by the end. Thus, calculating an average firing rate across the full length of the recording may not be an accurate reflection of a cell’s firing activity. To account for this, we first divided the recording into one-minute bins and calculated each cell’s firing rate in each, as well as a mean firing rate across all bins. Any bins where the firing rate was less than 10% of the mean firing rate were excluded from further analysis. This allowed us to keep extended periods where a cell was inactive or not detectable from influencing mean firing rate calculations.

Phase locking and firing rate analysis was again restricted to spikes that occurred while animals were running within the speed threshold. Because firing rates and phase locking may differ between running and not running periods, or based on running speed, this again allows us to exclude the possibility that differences between animals/groups are due to differences in running behavior. Phase locking was measured with both R-values (precision of phase preference) and mu-values (mean phase of firing) relative to theta oscillations. For each animal, we performed a Hilbert transformation on the theta filtered LFP data and found the locations of theta peaks(0°) and troughs(180°). For each unit, we found the phase of theta at each spike time and used the circular statistics toolbox in MATLAB to calculate the mean resultant vector length (R) and the mean direction (mu) for each unit.

To assess phase precession, we calculated the difference between the spike train frequency of each single unit and the theta LFP frequency from the corresponding animal. Only units with >100 spikes were included in this analysis to ensure a sufficient number of spike trains were captured. Because neurons exhibiting phase precession have a slightly faster spike train frequency than the theta LFP frequency, this calculation can be used to assess precession-like behavior of neurons without first identifying place cells.91,112 For this analysis we used data during all running times rather than subsampling for a narrow range of speeds to avoid cutting out spikes in a spike train and distorting the results. For each unit, we created an autocorrelogram of spike times occurring within 1 s of another spike. We then found the time in seconds corresponding to the peak of this autocorrelogram within 50-150ms. The spike train frequency was defined as 1/this value. For example, a cell firing at ~8Hz would fire approximately every 1/8 or 0.125 s and thus have a peak in its autocorreleogram at this time point. Each unit’s spike train frequency was then compared to the CA1 theta frequency from the animal that contributed that unit in order to calculate the Unit - LFP frequency value.

QUANTIFICATION AND STATISTICAL ANALYSIS

All statistics were performed in R version 4.2.3 or GraphPad Prism 9.3.1. The Holm method was used to control for multiple comparisons unless otherwise specified. All test statistics, p-values and information about multiple comparison corrections can be found in Table S1. Behavior and cell counting or plaque counting data were analyzed using two-way Genotype x Age type III ANOVA. Posthocs were performed using the emmeans package in R to calculate t statistics. Only posthoc comparisons between genotypes within a given age or between ages within a given genotype were performed.

Power and coherence data were analyzed with two-way Genotype x Layer repeated measures type III ANOVA using the ezANOVA function from the R ez package, followed by posthoc t-tests for each layer where applicable. Due to some animals missing data for some layers in the CSD analysis and in the MEC power analysis, these cases were analyzed using mixed effects models.

Our single unit firing rate and R value data did not meet the ANOVA assumptions of normality and equal variance across groups, so for these analyses the Mann-Whitney test was used to compare groups. Only comparisons between genotypes within a given age or between ages within a given genotype were performed.

For mu-value data, we used the circular package in R. The Kuiper test was first used to determine whether distributions of mu values differed between groups. If the Kuiper test was significant, an equal kappa test was performed to determine whether groups differed in their concentrations. If no differences in concentration were present, we then used the Watson-Williams test to ask if circular means differed between groups. Genotypes were compared at each age and the Bonferroni method was used to control for multiple comparisons. For clarity and consistency, asterisks on Mu (Figures 6F–6I; Figures S8D–S8G) plots reflect a significant Watson-Williams test following a significant Kuiper test and non-significant equal-kappa test.

Unless otherwise stated, all data are presented as mean ± SEM, where n represents the number of cells and N represents the number of animals. In the case of mu values, circular means are shown in Figure 6. All statistical tests with post hoc correction were two-tailed with thresholds of significance set at p < 0.05. p-values less than 0.05 are denoted with asterisks, and all posthoc tests with p-values between 0.05 and 0.075 are noted explicitly in the manuscript. Additional details of all statistical tests, including all nonsignificant p-values, can be found in Table S1.
