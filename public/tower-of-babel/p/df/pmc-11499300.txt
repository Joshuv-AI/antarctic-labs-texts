Mevalonate in blood and muscle: Response to atorvastatin treatment and the relationship to statin intolerance in patients with coronary heart disease
Authors: Trine Lauritzen, John Munkhaugen, Stein Bergan, Elise Sverre, Kari Peersen, Sofia Lindahl, Einar Husebye, Nils Tore Vethe
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractStatin‐associated muscle symptoms are frequently reported and often lead to discontinuation of statin therapy with an increased risk of cardiovascular events. In vitro studies suggest that statin‐mediated inhibition of the mevalonate pathway leads to muscle cell toxicity. We aimed to determine the relationship between mevalonate, LDL‐cholesterol, and atorvastatin metabolites in patients with coronary heart disease and self‐perceived muscle side effects. Furthermore, we assessed the correlation between mevalonate in blood and muscle and the relationship to statin intolerance due to muscle symptoms. We used blood plasma from a randomized crossover trial (n = 70) and muscle biopsies and plasma from a subgroup in a subsequent open intervention study (n = 26). Both studies tested atorvastatin 40 mg/day. Seven patients did not tolerate ≥3 statins throughout the follow‐up and were classified as statin‐intolerant. Mevalonate in blood plasma decreased during atorvastatin treatment (median difference −38%, range −77% to 43%, p < 0.001), whereas mevalonate in muscle tissue was not lowered (0.05%, range −47% to 145%). Mevalonate correlated poorly with LDL‐cholesterol and atorvastatin metabolites (Spearman's rho −0.28 to 0.10). The statin‐intolerant patients had a smaller reduction in circulating mevalonate compared with the tolerant patients; median difference −8.1 (−22 to 3.5) nmol/L versus −25 (−93 to 12) nmol/L, p = 0.028. A similar observation was made for LDL‐cholesterol. Cutoffs based on these biomarkers classified >50% correctly as tolerant. Inhibition of the mevalonate pathway does not appear to be the mechanism underlying statin intolerance in the present study. Further studies of mevalonate as a biomarker for statin tolerance are needed to clarify the potential.

INTRODUCTION

Statins (HMG‐CoA reductase inhibitors) reduce circulating low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C) in blood and are strongly recommended to prevent recurrent cardiovascular events in patients with coronary heart disease (CHD). 1 Statin‐associated muscle symptoms (SAMS) are reported in 7%–29% of statin users. 2 SAMS can lead to treatment discontinuation and thus increased risk of adverse outcomes. 3 , 4 However, randomized, controlled trials (RCT) have shown minor or no difference in the incidence of SAMS between groups receiving statin treatment and placebo. 5 , 6 , 7 In 2019, we conducted a double‐blinded crossover RCT showing that 17% had more muscle symptoms during placebo treatment, whereas 28% had more muscle symptoms during atorvastatin treatment. 8 Further, during a 9‐month follow‐up period, 64 of 70 patients tolerated statin treatment, while six did not tolerate ≥ three statins. 9 These results aligned with other studies 10 , 11 and demonstrated that a high proportion of patients with self‐perceived SAMS erroneously attributes their symptoms to statins. Today, we have no objective diagnostic test besides creatine kinase to distinguish between patients with statin intolerance and muscle symptoms due to other causes. Thus, statin intolerance has to be evaluated through open‐label dechallenge/rechallenge tests, 1 and objective biomarkers are called for. 5

The pathophysiology of statin‐dependent myotoxicity is not fully understood, but several mechanisms have been suggested. A direct, cellular, or subcellular effect, disturbing the mitochondria and the calcium balance of the cells, is one option. 12 Alternatively, deprivation of the mevalonate pathway's intermediates, besides cholesterol, interferes with vital processes within the cells and might induce muscular statin intolerance. Statins compete with 3‐hydroxy‐3‐methyl‐glutaryl‐CoA (HMG‐CoA) for binding to the enzyme HMG‐CoA reductase (HMGCR) and thus inhibit the rate‐limiting step in the mevalonate pathway, that is, the biosynthesis of mevalonate from HMG‐CoA. HMGCR is expressed in the liver and several other tissues, including myocytes of skeletal muscle tissue. 13 Studies have shown that muscle cells are more sensitive than liver cells to enzymatic inhibition and toxicity from statins. 12 , 14 The intermediates farnesyl pyrophosphate (FPP) and geranylgeranyl pyrophosphate (GGPP) are essential substrates for the isoprenylation of proteins involved in signal transduction in cell growth and survival, and for overall cell structure. 12 , 15 , 16 , 17 Hence, reduced availability of FPP, and especially, GGPP can be involved in apoptosis and induce statin‐dependent muscle toxicity. 15 , 18 , 19 Decreased protein prenylation has also been associated with disturbances in the calcium balance, leading to programmed cell death. 16 , 20 In addition, FPP is the precursor of dolichol and ubiquinone (coenzyme Q10; CoQ10), both essential components of cellular processes. 15 , 16 , 20 , 21 , 22

Mevalonate is the common denominator of the possible pathophysiological mechanisms tied to the mevalonate pathway, potentially linking statin treatment to muscular side effects. Previous studies have shown that adding mevalonate to statin exposure in vitro 23 and in vivo (rats) 24 can mitigate the muscular toxic effects. However, clinical studies in patients with SAMS are yet to be conducted. Measuring mevalonate in muscle and blood plasma will provide new insight into the mechanisms underlying statin‐mediated myotoxicity and may potentially serve as a biomarker for statin intolerance. The main aim was to determine the relationship between mevalonate, LDL‐cholesterol, and atorvastatin metabolites in blood plasma in response to treatment with atorvastatin. Secondly, we explored the correlation between mevalonate levels in plasma and skeletal muscle tissue and whether these measures and the atorvastatin metabolites are associated with statin intolerance.

METHODS

Participants

In 2019, 71 patients with CHD and self‐perceived SAMS participated in a placebo‐controlled, double‐blinded, single‐crossover (MUSE RCT, ClinicalTrials.gov NCT03874156) study to determine the effect of atorvastatin on muscle symptoms. Participant characteristics are previously described. 8 After 1 week of washout, participants received either placebo or atorvastatin 40 mg/day for 7 weeks, followed by 1 week of washout and switching treatment for an additional 7 weeks. Blood was collected at the end of each treatment period, at both T0 (24 h after atorvastatin intake, between 9 and 11 a.m.) and T2 (2 h after tablet intake). Participants registered muscle symptoms each week in a diary. Twenty patients reported more symptoms during the atorvastatin than the placebo period, according to predefined criteria. During a 9‐month post‐trial follow‐up led by two cardiologists, six of these patients were clinically intolerant to ≥ three different statins with no or minor increase in creatine kinase. 9 The MUSE RCT also included an age and gender‐matched control group (n = 39) with no history of muscular complaints on statins that completed 7‐weeks open‐label treatment with atorvastatin 40 mg/day. In 2020, 28 patients from the MUSE RCT were included in the open‐label MUSE biomarker study, where they received atorvastatin 40 mg/day for 7 weeks, followed by 8 weeks without any statin. Muscle biopsies (vastus lateralis of the quadriceps muscle) and blood were collected at the end of each period. One of the patients was lost to follow‐up, and one was excluded from the data analyses due to atorvastatin intake shortly before sampling. During the biomarker study, another patient was categorized as intolerant to ≥ three different statins. The patient flowchart and an overview of the datasets are shown in Figure 1. Only three statin‐intolerant patients had muscle biopsies collected both on and off statin. Thus, their mevalonate results in muscle were not analyzed specifically according to intolerance.

Laboratory operations

We measured mevalonate in blood plasma and muscle tissue with liquid chromatography tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) using an Acquity UPLC I‐Class Xevo TQ‐S micro instrument (Waters, Milford, MA). The assays were according to a published method 25 with some local adaptions. The presented results correspond to the sum of mevalonic acid and mevalonolactone in vivo, expressed as nmol/L in blood plasma and nmol/g protein in muscle tissue (analytical coefficient of variation ≤12%). Details of the assays are given in Appendix S1. The entire procedure of measuring mevalonate in muscle was performed twice (in two different homogenates), and results from the second analysis with the largest amount of muscle tissue are presented.

Quantification of atorvastatin and its metabolites (atorvastatin lactone, 2‐OH‐atorvastatin acid and lactone, and 4‐OH‐atorvastatin acid and lactone) in blood plasma were performed with LC–MS/MS as previously described. 26

Statistics

We performed statistical tests with SPSS version 29 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Figures were made with GraphPad Prism 10. For the comparison between groups, we used the nonparametric Mann–Whitney U test or the related samples Wilcoxon signed‐rank test. Correlations were explored by Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (rho). Nonlinear regression (4‐parameter equation for inhibitory response, GraphPad) was used to explore the pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic relationships.

RESULTS

After 7 weeks without statins (on placebo), mevalonate in blood plasma among patients with SAMS had a median of 59 nmol/L, ranging from 18 to 149 nmol/L (n = 70). After 7 weeks on atorvastatin 40 mg/day, the level was 33 nmol/L (range 17 to 81 nmol/L) (Figure 2). In the control group with no muscle symptoms (n = 39), the median was 33 nmol/L (13–56 nmol/L) (Table 1). Median differences between the statin and placebo periods were −21 (−93 to 12) nmol/L and −38% (−77% to 43%) (p < 0.001). The corresponding differences in the biomarker study subgroup (n = 26) were −19 (−75 to 30) nmol/L and −28% (−78% to 46%) (p < 0.001) (Figure S1). Median LDL‐C was 4.1 (1.8–7.8) mmol/L after placebo and 2.1 (1.0–4.1) mmol/L after atorvastatin treatment (n = 70) (Figure 2). In the control group, LDL‐C was 2.1 (1.2–3.9) mmol/L. The median differences in LDL‐C between statin and placebo were −2.1 (−3.7 to 0.8) mmol/L (p < 0.001). The correlations (Spearman's rho) between mevalonate and LDL‐C on and off statin, including the absolute and relative differences, ranged from −0.28 to 0.10 and are shown in Figure S2.

At the end of the period without statin treatment, the mevalonate and LDL‐C levels in plasma were similar in the statin tolerant (n = 63) and intolerant group (n = 7). After 7 weeks on atorvastatin, the statin‐tolerant patients had similar mevalonate and LDL‐C as the control group without any history of SAMS (n = 39), while the statin‐intolerant group had slightly higher levels of both mevalonate and LDL‐C, although not statistically significant (Table 1). The absolute and relative differences in mevalonate and LDL‐C between the period on versus off statin were significantly smaller in the statin‐intolerant patients compared with the tolerant group (Table 1).

Mevalonate and LDL‐C as candidate diagnostic markers for statin intolerance

A reduction in mevalonate of more than 22 nmol/L could classify 54% of the 63 tolerant patients as tolerant (Figure 4). A post hoc analysis showed that a reduction in LDL‐C of more than 2.2 mmol/L could classify 51% as tolerant. Combining the absolute reductions in mevalonate and LDL‐C could differentiate 76% as tolerant to statin therapy (Figure 4). The cutoffs were chosen at the optimal specificity combined with 100% sensitivity, differentiating all statin intolerant.

DISCUSSION

This is the first in vivo study investigating the relationship between statin exposure and mevalonate levels in both blood and skeletal muscle tissue. We found that the systemic level of mevalonate decreased during atorvastatin treatment but remained unchanged in skeletal muscle. The reduction in plasma was neither correlated with LDL‐C nor atorvastatin pharmacokinetic variables. Patients with muscle‐related intolerance to ≥ three statins had a less pronounced reduction in blood plasma mevalonate than the tolerant patients and a parallel observation was made for LDL‐C. These pharmacodynamic responses concurred with an apparent shift of statin metabolism in the intolerant patients, as they tended to have lower levels of atorvastatin metabolites. Our results indicate that inhibition of the mevalonate pathway does not appear to be an underlying mechanism related to statin intolerance.

Mevalonate in blood plasma reflects the HMGCR activity in the liver. 27 We observed a decrease in plasma mevalonate after 7 weeks of atorvastatin. Generally, there was a poor correlation between mevalonate measured in muscle tissue and blood plasma. In muscle, the level of mevalonate was similar on and off the statin, indicating that the mevalonate levels are regulated differently in muscle and systemically. A previous study found a similar pattern for ubiquinone: decreased level in plasma but not in muscle tissue. 28 Our clinical study does not support the hypothesis that decreased levels of mevalonate pathway intermediates are a pathophysiological explanation for statin intolerance, neither in blood nor muscle. On the contrary, we found less lowering of mevalonate and LDL‐C in plasma among the intolerant patients. These findings align with a previous in vitro study indicating that statin‐induced cellular toxicity is not mediated by decreased mevalonate pathway intermediates. 14 In the MUSE biomarker data set, we have previously shown that a pattern of lower levels of atorvastatin acid metabolites and higher lactone/acid ratios, both in muscle and plasma, are characteristic of statin‐intolerant patients. 29 In the present context, low levels of atorvastatin acid metabolites might explain the weaker effect on HMGCR reflected by lower mevalonate and LDL‐C responses in blood plasma. The mechanism of muscle side effects induced by statins may be related to the formation of statin lactones as these metabolites are shown to be muscle‐toxic, 30 potentially by direct inhibition of complex III in the mitochondrial respiratory chain. 31 Based on our findings, future research should pursue the aspects of off‐target effects further, rather than inhibition of the mevalonate pathway, to explain statin intolerance. The proposed cutoffs have the potential to classify a large proportion of the patients with self‐perceived SAMS as being tolerant to statin therapy. Thus, the clinical resources might be deployed more efficiently on the remaining patients, including those with clinical statin intolerance.

Mevalonate has a diurnal variation, with the highest levels at night. 32 , 33 It also varies with the amount of cholesterol in the food and fasting routines. 34 This may explain some of the inter‐individual variations. In our study, patients met fasting for blood sampling between 9 and 11 in the morning to reduce the variability. Still, we observed 5‐fold to 8‐fold inter‐individual variability in the systemic mevalonate levels on and off statin, respectively. The mevalonate response showed high variability between the individuals, and it correlated poorly with the pharmacokinetic variables of atorvastatin, indicating a more extensive pharmacodynamic mechanism than solely reducing mevalonate production by HMGCR. Although both mevalonate and LDL‐C in blood plasma were reduced in most patients during statin treatment, there was a poor correlation between the two variables, also reported in another study. 35 This demonstrates the complexity of cholesterol regulation after the production phase, with organization into different lipoproteins, food intake of cholesterol, and feedback mechanisms. Inhibition of mevalonate and cholesterol production will increase LDL‐receptor expression, but cholesterol depletion will also increase the amount of HMGCR. 36 The intricate interactions were also exemplified by the fact that some patients had high systemic mevalonate levels combined with low LDL‐C during the atorvastatin treatment, which gave an apparent negative correlation. The effectiveness of the feedback responses might decide how well the statins work in the individual. Thus, as observed, the direct pharmacokinetic–pharmacodynamic relationship will be more complex.

Limitations

We only tested atorvastatin 40 mg/day, and extrapolation to other dose levels must be done with caution. The group of statin‐intolerant patients was limited in sample size. Although we found the same pattern for both mevalonate and LDL‐C, which strengthens the results, and the tolerant group was much larger than the intolerant group, our results must be confirmed in a larger cohort of patients. To minimize the number of invasive procedures, we collected muscle biopsies only on a subgroup that comprised approximately one‐third of the initial cohort. The data analyses based on the muscle tissue were thus limited, but we could still assess the influence of atorvastatin on the mevalonate levels. One could suspect that patients with muscle symptoms did not take the tablets to the extent of others, which could explain their lower pharmacodynamic response. However, in this study, we claim to have had reasonable control over adherence in everyone based on extensive patient information, use of personal medication diaries, weekly follow‐up by staff, counting residual tablets, and direct measurement of atorvastatin metabolites in blood samples.

CONCLUSION

Atorvastatin 40 mg daily reduces the median concentration of mevalonate in blood plasma in patients with self‐perceived SAMS, whereas the level of mevalonate in muscle tissue remains unchanged. The systemic mevalonate response correlates poorly with LDL‐C and the plasma concentration of atorvastatin and its metabolites. Inhibition of the mevalonate pathway could not explain the mechanism underlying muscular statin intolerance in the present study. Further studies of mevalonate as a biomarker for statin tolerance are needed to clarify the potential.
