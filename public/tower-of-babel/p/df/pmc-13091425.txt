Response to Letter to the Editor Entitled “Refining the Pathophysiology of Access-Related Hand Dysfunction: Key Methodological Directions”
Authors: Kyoungrae Kim, Terence E Ryan, Salvatore T Scali
Published: 2026

The Author Replies:

We thank Hu et al.1 for their thoughtful comments regarding our study examining access-related hand dysfunction following hemodialysis access placement.2 We appreciate the opportunity to clarify our interpretation and highlight areas for further mechanistic investigation.

First, the suggestion that neuromotor and mitochondrial changes reflect systemic hemodynamic compromise from high-flow access states is not supported by clinical evidence in our previous work. Objective motor, sensory, and patient-reported assessments in the contralateral limb showed no change, arguing against a global hemodynamic mechanism.3 Rather, physiologic perturbations appear localized distal to the access in the ipsilateral extremity. Although “high-flow” steal is often invoked, this construct remains unvalidated, and hemodynamic parameters have not reliably predicted neuromotor outcomes given interpatient variability in collateral adaptation and tissue resilience. Importantly, our cohort was evaluated 6 weeks after access creation, when very high access flows (> 1500–2000 ml/min) are uncommon, and no participants developed clinical evidence of high-output cardiac failure. Consistent with the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative guidance,4 which does not recommend routine access flow surveillance in the absence of clinical concern, our findings support a model in which access-related hand dysfunction primarily reflects regional hemodynamic and biologic responses within the ipsilateral limb rather than systemic hypoperfusion.

Second, Hu et al.1 raise the possibility that increased citrate synthase activity without improved respiration reflects the accumulation of dysfunctional mitochondria rather than insufficient bioenergetic remodeling. We agree that mitochondrial quality control is frequently disrupted in chronic kidney disease and that multiple mechanisms may explain this discordance. However, most previous studies report reduced skeletal muscle mitochondrial content in chronic kidney disease5; thus, the increase in citrate synthase activity observed after dialysis access placement suggests a compensatory response. The inverse correlation between citrate synthase activity and finger pressure supports hemodynamic stimulation. Although this quantitative increase did not confer bioenergetic benefit, additional work is needed to define the underlying mechanisms.

Furthermore, we acknowledge the limitations of bulk RNA sequencing and agree that higher-resolution approaches would provide important cell-type–specific context. Nonetheless, bulk transcriptomics remains an informative approach in small clinical samples such as microbiopsies and supports the conclusion that skeletal muscle remodeling accompanies access-related hand dysfunction while highlighting the need to better define the cellular mediators involved.
