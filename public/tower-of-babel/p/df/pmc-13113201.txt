Immune and Stem Cell‐Mediated Bactericidal Amplification and Bone Remodeling for Infection Clearance and Osteointegration
Authors: Zhenyu Li, Siming Zhang, Jiale Dong, Ning Li, Mo Chen, Kunzheng Wang, Bin Li, Guoqing Pan, Jiaxiang Bai, Chen Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTImplant‐related infections reduce mesenchymal stem cell accumulation near implant sites while suppressing the viability and osteogenic capacity of these stem cells. Here, we show that pH‐responsive biomaterial interfaces exploit infection‐induced acidification to coordinate antimicrobial action with stem and immune cell functional reprogramming. The system can release bactericidal peptides for direct bacterial elimination, whereas indirect bactericidal enhancement occurs in the presence of magnesium ions and cell‐targeting peptides, which enable a positive modulation of immune responses and recruitment of bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells, respectively. This feature results in robust osteogenic differentiation at the implant interfaces, achieving a 280% increase in the interfacial bone volume in rat infection models. Through transcriptomic analysis, we also show that Wnt‐activated stem cells and alternatively activated macrophages jointly upregulate antimicrobial effector programs along with osteogenic genes, contributing to the observed >4‐log bacterial reduction through combined material‐ and cell‐mediated suppression. The coordinated infection clearance and interface regeneration demonstrate that infection‐responsive biomaterials can transform endogenous cells to bifunctional therapeutic agents, defining a strategy that addresses infection and regeneration as integrated biological processes.Keywords: bifunctional effector, bone regeneration, implant infection, stem cell reprogramming, Wnt signaling

Introduction

Implant‐related infections (IRIs) affect up to 20% of revision orthopedic procedures, representing hundreds of thousands of cases annually and imposing over $5 billion in healthcare costs [1, 2]. The pathophysiology centers on a destructive cascade initiated when bacteria colonize implant surfaces, rapidly forming biofilms that shield pathogens from immune clearance [3, 4, 5]. This biofilm establishment triggers excessive inflammatory responses that simultaneously destroy the surrounding bone tissue and exclude bone marrow mesenchymal stem cells (BMSCs), the principal cellular architects of skeletal regeneration, from infection sites [6, 7]. Due to this, a vicious cycle is created in which, while the host inflammatory response successfully combats bacteria, it simultaneously destroys bone tissue and establishes microenvironmental conditions that exclude BMSCs and prevent regeneration, ultimately perpetuating healing failure.

The abovementioned destructive cascade operates through interconnected mechanisms that disable the regenerative responses. Bacterial endotoxins trigger the excessive recruitment of M1 macrophages, establishing an inflammatory microenvironment characterized by pro‐inflammatory cytokine secretion (TNF‐α, IL‐1β) and tissue acidification [8, 9]. This hostile milieu exerts dual detrimental effects on BMSCs; repulsive chemokine gradients actively exclude stem cells from infection sites, whereas pro‐inflammatory cytokines directly impair BMSC migration and inhibit osteogenic differentiation in any stem cell that reaches the implant interface [10, 11, 12]. Critically, BMSC dysfunction persists even after bacterial eradication because the inflammatory memory maintains stem cell paralysis and prevents osseointegration [13, 14]. This sustained BMSC exclusion and dysfunction represents a fundamental barrier to resolving IRIs: the cells required for tissue regeneration are systematically repelled from and inactivated at the sites where they are most needed.

Traditional therapeutic strategies have approached this challenge by attempting to neutralize the hostile environment using antimicrobial coatings and immunomodulatory agents, positioning BMSCs as vulnerable entities requiring protection [15, 16, 17, 18]. Recent innovations, including sequential drug delivery systems, immunomodulatory coatings that polarize macrophages toward pro‐regenerative phenotypes, and hybrid scaffolds combining antimicrobial and cell‐adhesive signals, have achieved a temporal coordination of infection clearance with tissue regeneration [19, 20, 21, 22, 23]. However, these approaches remain fundamentally compensatory. They deliver exogenous therapeutic agents to offset the absence of cellular functions, with the recruited BMSCs serving as passive responders to externally supplied signals. Critically, exogenous factors face inherent limitations such as rapid degradation in inflammatory environments, off‐target effects, and inability to overcome intrinsic stem cell dysfunction following inflammatory exposure. An alternative instructive paradigm would engage BMSCs not as tissues requiring environmental conditioning but as reprogrammable effector cells whose latent therapeutic programs can be unlocked through targeted intracellular signaling.

Recent studies on the intestinal epithelium demonstrate that tissue‐resident stem cells coordinate regeneration with antimicrobial defense through Wnt/β‐catenin pathway activation, simultaneously driving proliferative and antimicrobial effector expressions [24]. If BMSCs possess analogous latent programs, then the therapeutic paradigm could shift from protecting vulnerable cells to activating them as bifunctional effectors. Rather than delivering drugs to compensate for absent functions, biomaterials could serve as “cellular reprogrammers” that recruit endogenous stem cells and trigger coordinated signaling that unlocks latent capacities. Such reprogrammed BMSCs would autonomously execute dual functions, that is, clearing bacteria through secreted antimicrobial effectors while rebuilding bone tissue, addressing infection and regeneration as an integrated biological response. This instructive approach prioritizes engaging the endogenous regenerative machinery over supplying exogenous factors, potentially overcoming drug degradation while harnessing stem cell adaptability.

To realize this paradigm, we developed a mussel‐inspired metal–phenolic coordination coating integrating three functional components: 3,4‐dihydroxyphenylalanine (DOPA)‐conjugated recruiting peptides (DOPA–PFS) that captured BMSCs through integrin‐mediated adhesion; magnesium ions (Mg2 +) that activated antimicrobial programs while polarizing macrophages toward regenerative phenotypes; and DOPA‐conjugated melittin (DOPA–MLT) providing antimicrobial activity. These components self‐assembled through catechol–Mg2 + coordination bonds, forming a unified network on titanium surfaces. Critically, the infection microenvironment, characterized by tissue acidification, triggered coordination bond dissociation and initiated therapeutic responses that adapted to pathophysiological conditions (Figure 1). The recruited BMSCs underwent bifunctional reprogramming, activating endogenous antimicrobial programs and secreting bactericidal effectors that achieved multi‐log bacterial reduction while executing osteogenic differentiation. In an infected rat femoral model, the platform achieved biofilm clearance, immune modulation, and bone regeneration. These findings demonstrated that biomaterials could function as cellular reprogrammers, unlocking latent therapeutic programs to coordinate infection clearance with tissue regeneration.

Immune and stem cell‐mediated bactericidal amplification and bone remodeling for infection clearance and osteointegration. Mussel‐inspired coating integrating DOPA‐PFS, Mg2 +, and DOPA‐MLT assembles on titanium implants via metal‐phenolic coordination, with acidic infection microenvironment triggering therapeutic release. The platform coordinates infection clearance and bone regeneration by reprogramming recruited BMSCs to simultaneously secrete antimicrobial effectors and execute osteogenic differentiation, shifting from passive drug delivery to active cellular reprogramming.

Results

Peptide Synthesis and Characterization

The development of an intelligent, biomimetic, and therapeutic coating for IRIs required addressing the fundamental challenges in biomaterial design: creating molecular components that could simultaneously provide robust surface adhesion and retain distinct biological functions. Traditional approaches often have required a compromise between adhesion strength and bioactivity; however, our objective required both properties within a single, integrated system. Drawing inspiration from the exceptional adhesive mechanisms of marine mussels, we engineered two DOPA‐functionalized peptides to serve as the molecular foundations of our therapeutic platform. DOPA–MLT was designed to deliver potent antimicrobial action through membrane‐disruption mechanisms, and DOPA–PFS was engineered to orchestrate mesenchymal stem cell recruitment and activation (Figure 2a). The molecular architecture of these peptides incorporated the strategic placement of amino acid residues, with the DOPA moieties serving as anchoring points to optimize catechol group interactions with metal surfaces while preserving the conformational integrity essential for biological activity. Comprehensive characterization validated the precision of our synthetic approach. High‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) confirmed exceptional purity levels exceeding 95% for both peptides, with retention times of 9.083 and 11.625 min for DOPA–PFS and DOPA–MLT, respectively (Figure 2c,d). Electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI–MS) provided definitive molecular verification, demonstrating molecular weights of mass‐to‐charge ratio (m/z) 1484.5 and 902.27 for DOPA–PFS and DOPA–MLT, respectively, confirming successful peptide synthesis (Figure S1a,b). Proton nuclear magnetic resonance spectroscopy (1H NMR) confirmed the successful DOPA incorporation through the characteristic peaks at 8.62 ppm, representing the distinctive signature of catechol groups that would serve as the primary anchoring moieties for surface attachment. However, the question was that could, these therapeutic peptides be systematically assembled onto titanium surfaces using rational design strategies?

The strategy required simultaneous optimization of molecular organization, adhesion strength, and environmental responsiveness, which have been traditionally considered mutually exclusive in surface coating applications. Our solution used metal–catechol coordination chemistry, utilizing magnesium ions as molecular organizing centers to direct the self‐assembly process (Figure 2b). Through carefully controlled immersion in solutions containing 0.1 mM concentrations of each of DOPA–MLT and DOPA–PFS with 0.1 mM MgCl2, a spontaneous yet precise coordination‐driven assembly was facilitated. The Mg2 + ions functioned as three‐dimensional (3D) cross‐linking nodes, connecting catechol groups from both peptide species to a unified, stable network that possessed superior mechanical properties compared to conventional dopamine‐based adhesion while retaining dynamic responsiveness. To systematically evaluate our coating strategy, five experimental groups were established: Control (unmodified titanium), DP (DOPA–PFS only), DPMg (DOPA–PFS with Mg2 + coordination), DP@DM (DOPA–PFS and DOPA–MLT dual peptides), and DPMg@DM (complete multifunctional coating system with both peptides coordinated through Mg2 +). Atomic force microscopy (AFM) demonstrated drastic morphological transformations across our modification strategy, documenting the evolution from smooth, native titanium to increasingly complex surface architectures, culminating in a sophisticated DPMg@DM system (Figure 2e). Quantitative analyses confirmed significant increases in surface roughness, which peaked with our complete coating system, creating topographical features optimally suited for cellular engagement and osseointegration (Figure 2f). Water‐contact angle measurements documented concurrent changes in the surface chemistry, demonstrating the transformation from hydrophobic titanium to hydrophilic, biologically compatible surfaces (Figure 2g,h). X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) provided definitive chemical validation, demonstrating substantial increases in carbon and nitrogen content, with the Ti–MLT@PFS group characteristic peptide bond signatures at 400.1 eV (Figure 2i,j). High‐resolution XPS analysis of the C 1s and N 1s regions further confirmed successful surface modification (Figure S2a,b). Ultraviolet‐visible (UV‐vis) absorption spectroscopy provided compelling evidence of successful metal–phenolic coordination, with the absorption spectra demonstrating maximum peaks at 280 nm wavelength (Figure 2k). Notably, the DPMg@DM group (group 5) exhibited the highest absorption intensity, followed by the DPMg group (group 3), whereas groups 2 and 1 showed significantly lower intensities, confirming the successful formation of the metal–phenolic coordination complex on the modified surfaces.

The ultimate validation of our biomimetic coating required demonstrating its capacity for environmental sensing and context‐appropriate therapeutic release, which would distinguish this system from passive drug delivery platforms. The fundamental requirement was for molecular discrimination between normal physiological conditions and the pathological environment characteristics of IRIs. To assess the coating stability and release behavior, we conducted comprehensive studies in Dulbecco's modified eagle medium (DMEM) culture in different pH conditions. The XPS analysis of the DOPA‐PFS layer after immersion in acidic DMEM (pH 5.5) demonstrated remarkable stability with consistent nitrogen signals, indicating stable peptide retention even under infection‐relevant acidic culture conditions (Figure 2l). This stability profile confirmed the ability of the coating to maintain its structural integrity under acidic cell culture conditions. The pH‐responsive behavior of our system was evaluated via the inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP–MS) of the magnesium ion release in DMEM at different pH values (Figure 2m). Under physiological conditions (pH 7.4), the coating maintained a consistent low‐level of Mg release throughout the experimental period, demonstrating robust coordination bond stability, which is essential for long‐term implant applications. Under mildly acidic conditions (pH 6.5), which represent early inflammatory responses, the coating showed intermediate release kinetics, indicating the sensitivity of the system to subtle environmental changes. In contrast, exposure to acidic conditions (pH 5.5), which mimicked the inflammatory microenvironment of infection, triggered drastically different release kinetics. The acidic environment induced rapid Mg release within the first seven days, followed by stabilization at elevated release levels, precisely representing the environmental responsiveness required for targeted therapeutic delivery during infection episodes. This pH‐dependent release mechanism validated our hypothesis that the metal–phenolic coordination bonds could serve as intelligent molecular switches, remaining stable under physiological conditions while responding dynamically to acidic environments, with graded responses depending on the severity of the pH change. Extended immersion studies in simulated body fluid (SBF) over 14 days further confirmed this pH‐responsive degradation behavior, demonstrating sustained Mg2 + release at acidic pH with robust coating stability at physiological pH (Figure S3). Supplementary AFM imaging of the DPMg@DM surfaces before and after exposure to an acidic buffer (pH 5.5) for 24 h provided the visual confirmation of this pH‐responsive behavior, demonstrating morphological changes consistent with the release of the coating component under acidic conditions (Figure S4).

In Vitro Antibacterial Efficacy

Early bacterial colonization is a critical determinant of implant success because initial microbial adhesion disrupts immune homeostasis and triggers cascading failures that ultimately compromise osseointegration. The challenge of implant‐associated infections becomes particularly formidable when considering the complex dynamics of bacterial pathogenesis, involving both biofilm formation and planktonic bacterial persistence. To comprehensively evaluate the antimicrobial performance of our intelligent, biomimetic coating system, we designed a systematic assessment targeting two critical phases of bacterial infection: biofilm formation prevention and planktonic bacteria elimination against clinically relevant pathogens, namely, Escherichia coli and methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) (Figure 3a).

Biofilm formation prevention represented the primary therapeutic strategy, as the prevention of biofilm establishment was significantly more achievable than dealing with established biofilm communities. Crystal violet staining and 3D confocal microscopy analyses demonstrated the remarkable capacity of the coating to inhibit biofilm formation (Figure 3b). The control surfaces supported robust biofilm development with predominantly viable bacteria, whereas the DPMg@DM surfaces showed drastically reduced biofilm formation and an increased prevalence of nonviable bacteria. The quantitative assessments of the biofilm biomass through optical density measurements demonstrated progressive inhibition across the modification groups, with DPMg@DM achieving the most significant reduction in biofilm formation for both E. coli and MRSA (Figure 3d,e). The 3D reconstruction of the biofilm architecture through live/dead fluorescence staining demonstrated marked differences in both biofilm thickness and bacterial viability. The quantitative analysis confirmed a significant reduction in the average biofilm thickness of both E. coli and MRSA biofilms formed on the DPMg@DM surfaces in relation to that for all other groups. (Figure 3f,g).

Planktonic bacterial elimination addressed the challenge of free‐floating bacteria that may escape the initial biofilm formation or be released from disrupted biofilm communities. Colony‐forming unit (CFU) analysis and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) demonstrated drastic differences in bacterial survival across the modification groups (Figure 3c). The control titanium surfaces exhibited dense bacterial colonization with extensive coverage of both E. coli and MRSA, as evidenced by abundant colony formation and confluent bacterial layers observed under high‐magnification imaging. A progressive bacterial reduction was observed across the intermediate coating groups, with the DPMg@DM system demonstrating superior antibacterial efficacy. Quantitative analyses confirmed significant reductions in viable bacterial counts for both pathogens, with E. coli and MRSA survival rates decreasing substantially on DPMg@DM surfaces compared to controls (Figure 3h,i). TEM provided the morphological evidence of bacterial membrane disruption and structural damage to the peptide‐modified surfaces, particularly in the DPMg@DM group, where the few remaining bacterial cells exhibited clear signs of membrane compromise and cellular distortion.

A comprehensive evaluation using flow cytometry provided a definitive quantification of the therapeutic potential of the coating for preventing biofilm formation and eliminating planktonic bacterial populations. The flow cytometry analyses of bacterial viability during biofilm formation prevention demonstrated substantially increased proportions of nonviable bacterial cells on DPMg@DM surfaces, with E. coli showing 81.5% cell death and MRSA demonstrating 65.4% cell death, compared to the minimal cell death observed on control surfaces (Figure 3j–l). These findings demonstrated that our intelligent, biomimetic coating system effectively prevented biofilm formation and eliminated planktonic bacteria, thereby providing comprehensive antimicrobial protection through dual‐targeted therapeutic strategies.

Transcriptomic Alterations in Response to DPMg@DM Treatment

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying the potent antimicrobial efficacy of our smart multifunctional coating, we performed a comprehensive transcriptomic profiling of MRSA following exposure to DPMg@DM treatment. Quality control analysis confirmed the reliability of our transcriptomic data, with consistent gene detection numbers across samples, strong inter‐sample correlations, and a clear separation between treatment groups in the principal component analysis (Figures S5 and S6). Hierarchical clustering analysis demonstrated distinct transcriptional signatures between the control and treated bacteria, with a clear separation of expression patterns, indicating fundamental alterations in bacterial physiology (Figure 4a). The gene ontology enrichment analysis of downregulated genes demonstrated the coordinated disruption of essential biological processes, with significant effects on carbohydrate metabolic networks, protein transport systems, and glucose metabolism (Figure 4b). The Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis further demonstrated comprehensive metabolic dysregulation, particularly affecting quorum‐sensing systems, phosphotransferase pathways, bacterial secretion systems, and critical metabolic processes, including arginine biosynthesis and fatty acid degradation (Figure 4c).

The gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) provided detailed insights into the coordinated transcriptional reprogramming across multiple bacterial systems. Quorum‐sensing pathway genes exhibited substantial negative enrichment in the DPMg@DM‐treated samples, indicating the targeted disruption of bacterial cell‐to‐cell communication, which is essential for virulence regulation and biofilm formation (Figure 4d). The GSEA heatmap analysis demonstrated systematic transcriptional suppression across critical functional categories. Pathogenesis‐related genes showed significant downregulation, indicating that compromised virulence mechanisms were essential for the establishment of infection (Figure 4e). Extracellular region genes displayed coordinated suppression, suggesting disrupted bacterial surface architecture and secreted factors (Figure 4f). Membrane‐associated genes demonstrated marked negative enrichment, reflecting the direct targeting of membrane integrity (Figure 4g). Adenosine triphosphate (ATP)‐binding cassette transporter complex genes exhibited substantial downregulation, indicating impaired nutrient acquisition and multidrug resistance systems (Figure 4h). These findings indicated that the antimicrobial mechanism operated through multifaceted interference with bacterial physiology, extending beyond simple membrane disruption to encompass a systematic metabolic collapse.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) provided a compelling visual validation of the proposed antimicrobial mechanism, demonstrating drastic morphological alterations in MRSA exposed to the DPMg@DM coating (Figure 4i). Similar morphological destruction was also observed in E. coli, confirming the broad‐spectrum antimicrobial activity of the coating (Figure S7). The control MRSA cells displayed a characteristic intact spherical morphology with a smooth surface architecture. In contrast, DPMg@DM‐treated bacteria exhibited severe structural abnormalities, including surface roughening, cellular distortion, and extensive morphological damage. Functional validation assays corroborated these molecular and morphological observations. The reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) analysis of key virulence genes demonstrated a significant downregulation of accessory gene regulator A (agrA) (quorum‐sensing regulator), ATP‐binding cassette subfamily A (abcA) (ATP‐binding cassette transporter), and phosphotransferase system H (ptsH) (phosphotransferase system component) in DPMg@DM‐treated bacteria (Figure 4j). Additionally, bicinchoninic acid (BCA) protein leakage and o‐nitrophenyl‐β‐D‐galactopyranoside (ONPG) outer membrane permeability assays showed significantly enhanced membrane disruption in DPMg@DM‐treated bacteria compared to the controls (Figure 4k). These ultrastructural and functional changes validated the proposed mechanism, in which melittin initiated membrane pore formation, while magnesium ions enhanced permeability through the competitive displacement of membrane‐stabilizing divalent cations.

Biocompatibility Assessment

Ensuring excellent biocompatibility is a critical prerequisite for the clinical translation of any implant coating system because cytotoxicity restricts its therapeutic application. We conducted comprehensive safety evaluations encompassing the cellular responses and systemic compatibility to validate the translational potential of the DPMg@DM coating. In vitro assessment using RAW 264.7 macrophages demonstrated excellent cell viability across all coating formulations after 48‐h exposure periods. Live/dead fluorescence staining demonstrated predominant cell survival with minimal cytotoxicity across all treatment groups, showing results comparable to the control conditions (Figure S8a). Cell counting kit‐8 (CCK‐8) quantification confirmed robust cell viability, with optical density values remaining consistently high for all groups including the complete DPMg@DM system at both day no. 1 and 3 (Figure S8c,d). Similarly, BMSCs, which are essential for osseointegration, maintained excellent viability when exposed to coating materials. Live/dead staining demonstrated preserved cell survival patterns comparable to the control groups (Figure S8b), while quantitative CCK‐8 analysis demonstrated maintained viability levels above 95% on both days. 1 and 3, indicating the preservation of regenerative cell function critical for implant integration (Figure S8e,f). Systemic biocompatibility evaluation in Sprague–Dawley rats following subcutaneous implantation provided compelling evidence for in vivo safety. The histological examination of the major organs after 56 days demonstrated normal tissue architecture without inflammatory infiltration or pathological changes across all treatment groups (Figure S9). The heart tissue maintained normal cardiomyocyte morphology and organization, liver sections showed preserved hepatocyte structure with intact lobular architecture, the spleen demonstrated normal lymphoid tissue organization, lung tissue exhibited clear alveolar structures without inflammatory infiltrates, and kidney sections demonstrated intact glomerular and tubular architecture.

DPMg@DM‐Orchestrated Immune Microenvironment Modulation

Although effective bacterial elimination constitutes the primary defense against implant‐associated infections, the subsequent regulation of immune responses is a critical determinant of whether long‐term osseointegration can be achieved. The inflammatory cascade initiated by an infection frequently persists beyond pathogen clearance, thereby establishing a chronic inflammatory microenvironment that impedes bone regeneration and compromises implant integration. The recognition of this challenge has prompted investigations into whether therapeutic interventions can simultaneously address microbial threats while promoting immune resolution for tissue repair. Our intelligent, biomimetic coating system provided a unique opportunity to investigate this dual therapeutic paradigm through its controlled release of magnesium ions.

The fundamental challenge in immune modulation lies in redirecting macrophage polarization from the destructive M1 phenotype, which is characterized by pro‐inflammatory cytokine production and tissue damage, toward the regenerative M2 phenotype, which promotes tissue repair and angiogenesis (Figure 5a). Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated striking differences in macrophage phenotypic markers across all the treatment groups. Control surfaces and non‐magnesium formulations (DP and DP@DM) exhibited significant expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), a hallmark M1 marker related with inflammatory tissue damage (Figure 5b). By contrast, magnesium‐containing surfaces (DPMg and DPMg@DM) demonstrated substantially reduced iNOS expression, while simultaneously upregulating Arginase‐1 (Arg‐1), a characteristic M2 marker linked to tissue repair and collagen synthesis (Figure 5c). Topographical intensity analysis confirmed these phenotypic transitions, demonstrating distinct spatial distribution patterns that highlighted the localized effects of magnesium ion release on immune cell behavior. Flow cytometry quantification provided the definitive validation of the observed phenotypic shifts, demonstrating significant reductions in CD11b+CD86+ M1 macrophages on magnesium‐containing surfaces compared to controls (Figure 5d,f). The DPMg@DM group achieved the most‐significant M1 suppression, with M1 populations decreasing from 59.6% in the controls to 19.4%. Conversely, CD11b+CD206+ M2 macrophages showed substantial increases on magnesium‐containing surfaces, with the DPMg@DM group demonstrating the highest M2 population (Figure 5e,g). RT‐qPCR analysis corroborated these cellular findings at the transcriptional level, demonstrating the coordinated downregulation of M1‐associated genes including TNF‐α, iNOS, and CD86, while M2‐associated genes, such as IL‐10, CD206, and ARG‐1 were significantly upregulated in magnesium‐containing groups (Figure S10c). Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) further validated the functional consequences of macrophage polarization by quantifying cytokine secretion levels in cell culture supernatants. Magnesium‐containing surfaces significantly promoted the secretion of the M2‐associated cytokines, IL‐13 and IL‐4, which play crucial roles in tissue repair and anti‐inflammatory responses (Figure S10a,b). The DPMg@DM group demonstrated the highest levels of both cytokines, further confirming the important role of magnesium ions in promoting M2 polarization and establishing a pro‐repair microenvironment essential for successful osseointegration.

Building upon these cellular‐level observations, comprehensive RNA‐sequencing analysis demonstrated that our smart coating system orchestrated extensive transcriptional reprogramming in macrophages following magnesium exposure, with volcano plot analysis demonstrating 1634 downregulated and 1496 upregulated differentially expressed genes, indicating fundamental alterations in macrophage gene expression programs (Figure 5h). Time‐course clustering analysis identified distinct temporal expression patterns that characterized the kinetics of magnesium‐mediated transcriptional responses (Figure 5i). Venn diagram analysis identified 117 core differentially expressed genes shared across all comparison groups, representing the fundamental molecular signature of magnesium‐mediated macrophage reprogramming (Figure 5j, Figure S13a–c). Gene ontology enrichment analysis demonstrated significant alterations in cellular components and biological processes central to immune regulation (Figure 5k). KEGG pathway analysis provided deeper insights into the specific signaling networks modulated by magnesium treatment, demonstrating the coordinated downregulation of multiple pro‐inflammatory pathways, including cell cycle regulation, p53 signaling, ferroptosis, and cytokine–cytokine receptor interactions (Figure 5l). GSEA demonstrated the significant suppression of the nucleotide‐binding oligomerization domain‐like (NOD)‐like receptor and Interleukin‐17 (IL‐17) signaling pathways, which are key inflammatory cascades that drive tissue damage (Figure 5m,n). RT‐qPCR validation confirmed the transcriptomic findings, demonstrating significant downregulation of key inflammatory pathway genes, including TNFα, IKKβ, IκBα, p65, and COX‐2 in DPMg@DM‐treated groups compared to controls (Figure S14). This systematic pathway suppression aligned with the observed inhibition of M1 polarization and identified NF‐κB signaling as a central regulatory hub mediating the immunomodulatory effects of magnesium.

BMSC Recruitment and Bifunctional Reprogramming Toward Antimicrobial‐Osteogenic Phenotype

The ultimate success of implant osseointegration extends beyond pathogen elimination and immune modulation to encompass the recruitment of BMSCs and their functional activation. Our coating system was designed to not only recruit BMSCs to infected sites but also reprogram them into bifunctional therapeutic agents capable of simultaneously executing antimicrobial and osteogenic functions through DOPA–PFS peptide‐mediated recruitment and magnesium‐triggered Wnt/β‐catenin pathway activation.

To evaluate the BMSC recruitment and osteogenic capacity, we conducted comprehensive assessments using primary BMSCs cultured on different surface modifications (Figure 6a). Immunofluorescence analysis demonstrated robust BMSC adhesion on PFS‐containing surfaces, with the DP and DPMg groups showing significantly enhanced cell populations compared with the controls (Figure 6b). ELISA quantification demonstrated significantly elevated stromal cell‐derived factor‐1 (SDF‐1) chemokine concentrations in the PFS‐containing groups (Figure 6c) while transwell migration and scratch wound healing assays confirmed superior chemotactic efficacy (Figure S11). For osteogenic differentiation, we employed both indirect mechanisms involving M2 macrophage‐derived factors and direct surface‐mediated signaling. Alizarin Red S and alkaline phosphatase staining demonstrated drastically enhanced mineralization and osteogenic activity in the magnesium‐containing groups (Figure 6d,e, Figure S12a,d,e). Immunofluorescence staining demonstrated upregulation of RUNX2 and OCN expression in the treated groups, while RT‐qPCR analysis confirmed the coordinated upregulation of key osteogenic genes (ALP, RUNX2, OPN, COL1) (Figure S12b,c).

Beyond the osteogenic function, we investigated whether the recruited BMSCs could contribute directly to bacterial clearance. Competitive surface colonization assays in which BMSCs and bacteria were co‐cultured simultaneously demonstrated drastically altered colonization patterns: DPMg and DPMg@DM surfaces exhibited reduced bacterial presence and increased BMSC populations for both MRSA and E. coli compared to control groups, where bacterial colonization dominated (Figure 6f,g). CFU enumeration confirmed a significant bacterial reduction with the coating‐activated BMSCs (Figure S15a,b), suggesting that the recruited BMSCs contribute to limiting bacterial colonization. Cell‐free conditioned medium experiments further demonstrated that culture supernatants from BMSCs cultured on DPMg@DM surfaces exhibited antibacterial activity against both MRSA and E. coli (Figure S16).

To elucidate the molecular mechanisms underlying these bifunctional outputs, we performed transcriptomic analysis, which demonstrated extensive reprogramming with 789 downregulated and 1035 upregulated genes targeting stem cell recruitment, migration, and tissue integration (Figure S17a–c). Gene ontology and KEGG pathway analyses confirmed enhanced extracellular matrix organization and cell adhesion processes, with the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway emerging as one of the most prominently activated pathways (Figure 6h,i). Western blot analysis of the nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions confirmed robust Wnt pathway activation, as evidenced by β‐catenin accumulation and nuclear translocation in the DPMg@DM‐treated BMSCs (Figure 6j). Furthermore, the upregulation of the Wnt targets, Leucine‐rich repeat‐containing G‐protein‐coupled receptor 5 (Lgr5) and axis inhibition protein 2 (Axin2), was observed in the treated groups (Figure S18). The quantification of protein expression levels demonstrated significant increases in nuclear β‐catenin, Lgr5, and Axin2 in DPMg@DM‐treated groups (Figure 6k). The treatment with pathway inhibitors abolished this activation, confirming the causal role of magnesium‐triggered Wnt signaling in orchestrating bifunctional cellular reprogramming. This bifunctional activation enabled stem cells to rebuild tissues while acquiring the capacity to combat infections, representing a paradigm shift from passive tissue builders to active therapeutic effectors.

In Vivo Validation of Multifunctional Coating Performance

To validate the therapeutic efficacy of our multifunctional coating system in vivo, we used a rat femoral condyle implant‐related MRSA infection model over a 56‐d experimental period (Figure 7a). The initial phase of our in vivo assessment focused on validating the antimicrobial efficacy of the pH‐responsive coating system in the context of established bone infection. The comprehensive evaluation on day 7 post‐implantation demonstrated drastic differences in infection control across the treatment groups through multiple assessment modalities (Figure 7b). Bacterial culture analysis demonstrated dense colony formation on the control surfaces, with progressive reduction across intermediate coating groups and virtually complete bacterial elimination in the DPMg@DM group. Histological examination using hematoxylin and eosin staining showed severe inflammatory cell infiltration and tissue destruction in the control group, with extensive neutrophil accumulation and tissue necrosis, which are characteristic of acute osteomyelitis. Giemsa staining provided the direct visualization of bacterial colonization, demonstrating dense bacterial aggregates throughout the bone‐implant interface in the control and single‐component coating groups. SEM provided the high‐resolution confirmation of antimicrobial efficacy, showing dense bacterial biofilm formation on the control implant surfaces, whereas the DPMg@DM‐treated surfaces remained remarkably free of bacterial colonization. By contrast, the DPMg@DM group exhibited minimal inflammatory infiltration and virtually no detectable bacterial presence with preserved tissue architecture, indicating effective infection control. Quantitative analysis confirmed these observations, with the DPMg@DM group demonstrating significantly reduced bacterial colony counts, decreased inflammatory cell infiltration, and minimal bacterial colonization compared to all other treatment groups, achieving a greater than 4‐log reduction in viable bacterial counts (Figure 7c–e, Figure S19a).

The intermediate‐phase evaluation focused on assessing the capacity of the coating to orchestrate favorable immune responses while promoting endogenous regenerative cell recruitment on day 14 post‐implantation. Comprehensive immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated profound differences in the expression of inflammatory mediators and immune cell phenotypes across the treatment groups (Figure 7f). Hematoxylin and eosin staining demonstrated a progressive resolution of inflammatory infiltration in the magnesium‐containing groups, with the DPMg@DM group showing the most favorable tissue architecture. Dual immunofluorescence labeling for M1 (CD86) and M2 (CD163) macrophage markers showed a remarkable shift in macrophage polarization patterns, with the DPMg@DM group exhibiting predominantly M2 macrophage populations at the implant interface, whereas the control groups showed overwhelming M1 macrophage infiltration. TNF‐α immunohistochemical analysis demonstrated drastically reduced pro‐inflammatory cytokine expression in the DPMg@DM group compared to the control and single‐component groups. IL‐10 immunohistochemistry provided additional confirmation of the establishment of an anti‐inflammatory microenvironment, with the DPMg@DM group demonstrating substantially increased expression of this key anti‐inflammatory cytokine. Quantitative analysis of inflammatory markers confirmed these observations, with the DPMg@DM group showing significantly reduced inflammatory infiltration area percentage, decreased TNF‐α expression area percentage, reduced M1 macrophage area percentage, increased M2 macrophage area percentage, and elevated IL‐10 expression area percentage compared to the controls (Figure 7g).

The final phase of the evaluation assessed the ultimate therapeutic objective: successful osseointegration and bone regeneration in the presence of a resolved infection at 8 weeks post‐implantation. Micro‐computed tomography (micro‐CT) demonstrated drastic differences in bone formation and implant integration across the treatment groups (Figure 7h). 3D reconstructions demonstrated extensive interfacial new bone formation at the implant–bone interface of the DPMg@DM‐treated implants, with continuous bone‐implant contact indicating successful osseointegration. Isolated new bone visualization highlighted the superior bone‐regenerative capacity of the multifunctional coating system. Toluidine blue staining of cross‐sectional views provided a detailed morphological assessment of bone‐implant interface integration, demonstrating intimate contact between the newly formed bone and implant surface in the DPMg@DM group. Quantitative bone morphometric analysis confirmed these visual assessments, with the DPMg@DM group achieving the highest interfacial bone volume fraction (BV/TV) among all groups, along with significantly improved trabecular architecture parameters, including reduced trabecular separation, increased trabecular thickness, enhanced bone mineral density, and increased trabecular number (Figure 7i–l, Figure S19b). Comprehensive histological analysis provided an additional validation of osteogenic efficacy through multiple staining modalities (Figure 7m). Masson's trichrome staining demonstrated extensive collagen matrix deposition and organized bone structures in the DPMg@DM group, indicating mature bone formation. Immunohistochemical analysis of bone sialoprotein (BSP) and collagen‐I (COL‐1) confirmed robust osteoblastic activity and bone matrix synthesis in the DPMg@DM group, with expression levels significantly exceeding those observed in the control and single‐component treatments. These comprehensive findings demonstrated that our intelligent, biomimetic multifunctional coating system successfully orchestrated the complex biological processes required for infection resolution and subsequent osseointegration in clinically relevant in vivo conditions.

In summary, our comprehensive in vitro and in vivo evaluations demonstrated that the DPMg@DM coating system successfully coordinated sequential therapeutic delivery, achieving potent antimicrobial activity (>4‐log bacterial reduction), favorable immune modulation (M1‐to‐M2 macrophage polarization), enhanced stem cell recruitment (5.8‐fold increase), and superior implant–bone interface osseointegration (280% increase in the interfacial bone volume fraction) in infected implant environments.

Discussion

IRIs represent a complex clinical challenge requiring simultaneous bacterial elimination and tissue regeneration, which have been conventionally addressed as sequential, independent problems [25]. Here, a pH‐responsive metal–phenolic coordination coating was developed, which integrated antimicrobial peptides (DOPA–melittin), immunomodulatory ions (Mg2 +), and cell‐recruiting peptides (DOPA–PFS), to meet these competing therapeutic requirements. In rat femoral IRI models, the coating system achieved >4‐log MRSA reduction, >96% biofilm eradication, 5.8‐fold BMSC recruitment, and 280% increased bone volume compared to the controls [26, 27]. These outcomes demonstrated the effective coordination of infection clearance with tissue regeneration through sequential pH‐responsive therapeutic delivery. Under infection‐induced acidity, the coating released antimicrobial and immunomodulatory components while subsequently stabilizing cell‐recruiting peptides as the pH normalized. Beyond these therapeutic outcomes, our mechanistic investigations demonstrated an unexpected finding: recruited BMSCs, in addition to their canonical osteogenic function, exhibited an intrinsic antimicrobial capacity that significantly contributed to bacterial clearance. Competitive surface colonization assays demonstrated that coating‐activated BMSCs significantly suppressed bacterial colonization for both MRSA and E. coli, while transcriptomic and pathway analyses identified Wnt/β‐catenin signaling as the coordinator of this bifunctional cellular phenotype. The demonstration that pathway inhibition abolished both osteogenic and antimicrobial outputs established that appropriately designed biomaterials could reprogram recruited stem cells from passive tissue builders into active therapeutic effectors executing osteogenic differentiation with concurrent antimicrobial capacity.

These findings represented a fundamental departure from traditional implant coating strategies, which have been positioning BMSCs primarily as vulnerable entities that require protection through environmental conditioning [28, 29]. Conventional approaches have been fundamentally compensatory, delivering exogenous antimicrobial agents to eliminate bacteria and supplying osteoinductive factors to stimulate passively recruited stem cells. Recent innovations, including sequential drug delivery systems, immunomodulatory coatings that polarize macrophages toward pro‐regenerative phenotypes, and hybrid scaffolds combining antimicrobial peptides with extracellular matrix (ECM) proteins, have achieved temporal coordination of infection clearance with tissue regeneration [30, 31, 32]. While representing substantial advances over simple antimicrobial elution, these approaches share a common limitation: the recruited BMSCs ultimately function as passive responders to externally supplied signals, serving primarily as tissue builders rather than active participants in infection clearance. Exogenous factors face inherent constraints, including rapid degradation in inflammatory environments, off‐target effects from sustained delivery, and the inability to overcome intrinsic stem cell dysfunction following inflammatory exposure [33, 34]. Our instructive paradigm fundamentally reorients this therapeutic logic by prioritizing the engagement of endogenous regenerative machinery over the exogenous factor supply. Rather than compensating for absent cellular functions through drug delivery, the coating triggers intracellular signaling networks (Wnt/β‐catenin pathway) that unlock latent therapeutic capacities intrinsic to BMSCs themselves. This enables reprogrammed stem cells to autonomously execute tissue rebuilding while contributing to infection clearance, addressing regeneration and infection as an integrated biological response rather than sequential independent problems [35].

Our findings that Wnt/β‐catenin pathway activation coordinated antimicrobial and osteogenic programs in BMSCs have important mechanistic implications extending beyond implant infection treatment. Recent evidence from intestinal stem cells demonstrated that Wnt signaling simultaneously drives proliferative gene expression and antimicrobial defense mechanisms, enabling tissue‐resident stem cells to coordinate regeneration with pathogen resistance [36, 37]. Our findings suggest that mesenchymal stem cells possess latent antimicrobial programs beyond their well‐established osteogenic capacity; however, these bifunctional programs remain cryptic under conventional culture conditions, requiring specific activating signals—in this case, magnesium ion‐triggered Wnt pathway engagement—to unlock their expression. The coordinated upregulation of osteogenic regulators along with the activation of antimicrobial programs established Wnt signaling as a master coordinator of stem cell bifunctionality, representing a fundamental biological principle that may apply across diverse stem cell populations [38, 39]. This molecular architecture, wherein a single pathway coordinated multiple therapeutic outputs, provides inherent synergy that is unavailable in combinatorial drug delivery approaches that supply individual factors independently. The demonstration that pathway inhibition abolished bifunctional outputs provides a robust mechanistic validation and suggests opportunities for tuning therapeutic responses through targeted pathway modulation. From a translational perspective, this mechanistic understanding enables the rational design of next‐generation biomaterials that can orchestrate complex biological processes by targeting endogenous signaling networks rather than supplying exogenous therapeutic factors [40].

The clinical implications of biomaterial‐triggered cellular reprogramming extend substantially beyond the immediate therapeutic outcomes demonstrated in this study. IRIs currently necessitate implant removal in 60%–80% of cases, resulting in patient morbidity and healthcare costs exceeding $5 billion annually [41]. Conventional approaches have been operating under the assumption that infected implants cannot support tissue regeneration due to BMSC exclusion and dysfunction. Our findings challenge this paradigm by demonstrating that appropriately designed biomaterials can actively engage BMSCs even within infected environments, transforming them from vulnerable bystanders to therapeutic participants. This conceptual shift offers the possibility of preserving implants while resolving infections, potentially substantially reducing revision surgery rates. The practical advantages further support translation; a single‐step coating application eliminates complex procedural requirements, whereas catechol–metal coordination provides stable attachment without compromising biocompatibility [42]. Beyond orthopedic applications, the principle of engaging endogenous cellular programs through pathway activation has broad applications. For example, cardiovascular devices could recruit endothelial progenitors, dental implants could activate periodontal stem cells, and wound dressings could reprogram recruited cells toward accelerated healing. Future development should prioritize several key directions. Single‐cell transcriptomic analysis could demonstrate heterogeneity in BMSC responses and identify subpopulations with enhanced reprogrammable capacity. Understanding the temporal dynamics of Wnt activation and crosstalk with other regenerative pathways (BMP and Notch) would enable optimized therapeutic window designs [43]. Peptide sequence refinement and expansion to diverse biomaterial substrates would broaden clinical applicability, while long‐term biocompatibility studies and clinical trials would remain essential for validating their safety and durability [44]. This investigation established that biomaterials can function as cellular reprogrammers rather than merely passive supports or drug carriers, fundamentally expanding regenerative medicine toward the intelligent orchestration of endogenous healing programs.

Materials and Methods

Materials and Reagents

DOPA‐MLT and DOPA‐PFS peptides were synthesized by ChinaPeptides Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) using Fmoc solid‐phase peptide synthesis with >95% purity confirmed by high‐performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) and electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (ESI‐MS). Magnesium chloride hexahydrate (MgCl2·6H2O), Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM), fetal bovine serum (FBS), penicillin‐streptomycin, and phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) were purchased from Gibco (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Crystal violet, ARS, ALP kit, and BCA protein assay kit were obtained from Sigma‐Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Primary antibodies against CD44, CD86, CD163, iNOS, Arg‐1, TNF‐α, IL‐10, RUNX2, osteocalcin, bone sialoprotein (BSP), and collagen‐I (COL‐1) were purchased from Abcam (Cambridge, UK). Secondary fluorescent antibodies and DAPI were obtained from Invitrogen (Carlsbad, CA, USA). CCK‐8 cell viability assay kit was purchased from Dojindo Molecular Technologies (Kumamoto, Japan).

Peptide Synthesis and Characterization

DOPA‐MLT (sequence: Ac‐(DOPA)4‐GIGAVLKVLTTGLPALISWIKRKRQQ‐NH2) and DOPA‐PFS (sequence: (DOPA)4‐ PFSSTKT) peptides were synthesized using Fmoc solid‐phase peptide synthesis protocols. For DOPA‐MLT synthesis, Rink Amide‐MBHA resin with substitution degree of 0.38 mmol/g was used as a solid support, while DOPA‐PFS synthesis employed 2‐Chlorotrityl Chloride resin with a substitution degree of 0.84 mmol/g. Both syntheses began with resin swelling in DCM for 30 min, followed by systematic amino acid coupling cycles. Each coupling reaction utilized 3‐fold molar excess of Fmoc‐protected amino acids, HBTU as coupling reagent (3 equiv.), and DIEA (10 equiv.) in DMF, with reaction times of 45–60 min. Fmoc deprotection was performed using 20% piperidine/DMF solution in a two‐step process (5 and 15 min). After completing sequential coupling, DOPA‐MLT was cleaved using 95% TFA, 2% water, 2% EDT, and 1% TIS for 180 min, while DOPA‐PFS employed 94.5% TFA, 2.5% water, 2.5% EDT, and 1% TIS for 120 min. Crude peptides were precipitated with cold diethyl ether and purified using preparative HPLC with gradient elution of water/acetonitrile containing 0.1% TFA at 15 mL /min flow rate with UV detection at 220 nm. Peptide purity was confirmed by analytical HPLC, and molecular weights were verified by ESI‐MS. DOPA incorporation was confirmed by 1H NMR characteristic peaks at 8.62 ppm. Purified peptides were lyophilized and stored at −20 °C.

Surface Modification and Coating Preparation

Titanium discs (diameter 15 mm, thickness 2 mm) were sequentially polished with 400, 800, and 1200‐grit silicon carbide papers, followed by ultrasonic cleaning in acetone, ethanol, and deionized water for 15 min each. A layer‐by‐layer assembly approach was employed to construct metallic phenol coatings using Mg2 + ions as coordinating bridges. Five experimental groups were prepared: Control (unmodified titanium), DP (DOPA‐PFS only), DPMg (DOPA‐PFS with Mg2 +), DP@DM (DOPA‐PFS and DOPA‐MLT), and DPMg@DM (complete coating system). The coating process involved sequential deposition of DOPA‐PFS (0.1 mM, 2 h), Mg2 + solution (0.1 mM, 2 h), and DOPA‐MLT (0.1 mM, 2 h) at pH 8.5 for 24 hours at room temperature under gentle agitation. After each layer deposition, samples were thoroughly washed with deionized water and dried under a nitrogen flow.

Surface Characterization

Surface morphology was analyzed using atomic force microscopy (AFM, Dimension Icon, Bruker, Germany) in tapping mode with silicon cantilevers over 5 × 5 µm scan areas. Surface roughness parameters (Ra) were calculated from three measurements per sample. Water contact angles were measured using a contact angle goniometer (DSA100, KRÜSS, Germany) with 2 µL deionized water droplets at room temperature, with at least three different spots measured for each sample. Chemical composition was determined by X‐ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, ESCALAB 250Xi, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) with Al Kα radiation (1486.6 eV). High‐resolution spectra were collected for C 1s, N 1s, O 1s, and Mg 2p regions with a pass energy of 20 eV, and all binding energies were calibrated using C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. UV‐visible absorption spectroscopy (UV‐2600, Shimadzu, Japan) was performed to confirm metal‐phenolic coordination with maximum detection at 280 nm. Coating stability under acidic conditions was assessed by XPS analysis of DPMg@DM samples after immersion in DMEM at pH 5.5 for extended periods. pH‐responsive release behavior was evaluated by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP‐MS, NexION 300X, PerkinElmer, USA) analysis of magnesium ion release in DMEM at pH 5.5, 6.5, and 7.4 over 28 days, with aliquots collected at predetermined time points (0.5, 1, 3, 5, 7, 14, 21, 28 days). Coating stability and pH‐responsive degradation were further assessed by immersing DPMg@DM‐coated samples in SBF at pH 5.5, 6.5, and 7.4, maintained at 37 °C. Cumulative Mg2 + release over 14 days was quantified by ICP‐MS (n = 3 per group).

Bacterial Culture and Antimicrobial Testing

Escherichia coli (ATCC 25 922) and methicillin‐resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA, ATCC 43 300) were cultured in Luria‐Bertani broth at 37°C with shaking at 180 rpm. For planktonic bacteria assessment, bacterial suspensions (106 CFU/mL) were incubated with coated titanium discs for 24 h. Viable bacteria were quantified by colony‐forming unit counting on agar plates. For biofilm studies, bacteria were cultured on coated surfaces for 48 hours to allow mature biofilm formation. Biofilm biomass was quantified using crystal violet staining with 0.1% crystal violet solution for 15 min, followed by solubilization with 33% acetic acid and absorbance measurement at 590 nm. Live/dead bacterial staining was performed using SYTO 9 and propidium iodide for 15 min in darkness, followed by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM, LSM 880, Zeiss, Germany) examination. Biofilm thickness was measured by CLSM with three‐dimensional reconstruction. For flow cytometry analysis, biofilms were gently scraped and dispersed in PBS, stained with SYTO 9 and PI, and analyzed using flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter, USA) with minimum 10000 events recorded. Bacterial morphology was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Sigma 300, Zeiss, Germany) after fixation with 2.5% glutaraldehyde and dehydration through graded ethanol series, followed by gold sputter‐coating and examination at 15 kV.

Transcriptomic Analysis and Mechanistic Studies

For bacterial transcriptomic analysis, MRSA was cultured with different coating formulations for 24 hours, followed by RNA extraction using TRIzol reagent. RNA quality was assessed using NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer and Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer. RNA sequencing was performed on Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform with paired‐end 150 bp reads. Ribosomal RNA was removed using TIANSeq rRNA Depletion Kit, followed by library construction using VAHTS Universal V6 RNA‐seq Library Prep Kit. Differential gene expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 with significance thresholds of |log2FoldChange| > 1 and p < 0.05. Gene Ontology and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses were performed using DAVID and GSEA software. RT‐qPCR validation was conducted using SYBR Green Master Mix on QuantStudio 5 Real‐Time PCR System with relative expression normalized to 16S rRNA using 2^(‐ΔΔCT) method. Bacterial membrane integrity was assessed through BCA protein leakage assay, measuring protein content in supernatant at 562 nm. Membrane permeability was evaluated using ONPG hydrolysis assay, with bacterial samples incubated with o‐nitrophenyl‐β‐D‐galactopyranoside and absorbance measured at 420 nm. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed to examine bacterial ultrastructure after fixation with 2.5% glutaraldehyde, post‐fixation with 1% osmium tetroxide, dehydration, and ultra‐thin sectioning (70 nm) with uranyl acetate and lead citrate staining.

Cell Culture and Biocompatibility Assessment

RAW 264.7 macrophages (ATCC, TCM13) were cultured in high‐glucose DMEM, while BMSCs were harvested from femurs of 6‐week‐old Sprague‐Dawley rats and maintained in α‐MEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 100 µg/mL streptomycin/penicillin at 37°C in 5% CO2, with medium refreshed every 48 hours. Cell viability was assessed using CCK‐8 assay and live/dead fluorescence staining with calcein‐AM and ethidium homodimer‐1. For biocompatibility evaluation under infection conditions, samples were pre‐incubated in acidic cell culture medium (pH 5.5) for 24 h before cell seeding. Fluorescence staining was performed after 48 hours, and CCK‐8 quantitative analysis was performed after 24 and 72 h of culture.

Macrophage Polarization Studies

For macrophage polarization assessment, RAW 264.7 cells were seeded on coated surfaces at 4 × 104 cells per well, stimulated with lipopolysaccharide (100 ng/mL) and cultured for 48 hours. Immunofluorescence analysis was performed using primary antibodies against iNOS (M1 marker) and Arg‐1 (M2 marker), followed by Alexa Fluor 488‐conjugated secondary antibodies and Phalloidin‐iFluor 647 for cytoskeleton visualization. Nuclear counterstaining was performed using DAPI, and samples were examined under CLSM. Flow cytometry analysis was conducted using fluorochrome‐conjugated antibodies targeting CD11b, CD206 (M2 marker), and CD86 (M1 marker) for 30 min, with proportions of CD11b+/CD206+ and CD11b+/CD86+ populations quantified. RT‐qPCR analysis was performed to assess M1‐associated genes (TNF‐α, iNOS, CD86) and M2‐associated genes (IL‐10, CD206, ARG‐1). ELISA analysis was conducted to quantify IL‐4 and IL‐13 secretion in culture supernatants according to the manufacturer's instructions.

BMSC Recruitment and Osteogenic Differentiation

BMSC recruitment was evaluated using transwell migration assays with 8 µm pore inserts, where cells (5 × 104 cells/well) were seeded in upper chambers and migration toward coating extracts was assessed after 48 h by crystal violet staining and ImageJ quantification. Scratch wound healing assays were performed by creating standardized wounds in BMSC monolayers using 200 µL pipette tips and monitoring closure rates after 0 and 2 days. Whole bone marrow cell adhesion experiments were conducted by placing material samples in bone marrow cell suspension extracted from 8–10 weeks male SD rat femurs, followed by 3 days of culture and immunofluorescence staining with anti‐CD44 antibody. SDF‐1α chemokine levels were quantified by ELISA using standards (0.156–10 ng/mL) with optical density measured at 450 nm. For osteogenic differentiation assessment, macrophage‐conditioned medium (MCM) was prepared by culturing RAW 264.7 cells on different surfaces, collecting and filtering supernatants, then mixing with fresh α‐MEM at 1:1 ratio. BMSCs were cultured in MCM supplemented with osteogenic factors (10 mM β‐glycerophosphate, 0.1 µM dexamethasone, and 0.25 mM ascorbate). Mineralization was evaluated by ARS staining on day 21, ALP activity on day 7, and immunofluorescence analysis of RUNX2 (day 7) and osteocalcin (day 14). Gene expression analysis was performed by RT‐qPCR for ALP, RUNX2, OPN, and COL1 using primers listed in Table S1, with relative expression normalized to GAPDH.

Cocultured System of Cells and Bacteria

The cell proliferation and antibacterial efficacy of Ti, DPMg and DPMg@DM surfaces were assessed by simultaneously seeding BMSCs and MRSA, E. coli bacteria onto surfaces at a ratio of 20:1 (2 × 106 cells per well, 1 × 107 CFUs per 24 well) and incubating them for 24 hours. Subsequently, the samples were washed thrice with PBS, fixed with 4% PFA for 20 min, and labeled with Actin 488 (green) to visualize both BMSCs and bacteria, and DAPI (blue) to label BMSC nuclei. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was employed to observe the distribution of BMSCs and bacteria on the surfaces after PBS washing.

Cell‐Free Conditioned Medium Antibacterial Assay

To assess the antimicrobial capacity of surface‐activated BMSCs, cell‐free conditioned medium experiments were performed. BMSCs were cultured on control Ti or DPMg@DM‐coated surfaces at 2 × 105 cells per well in serum‐free DMEM for 24 hours at 37°C with 5% CO2. Culture supernatants were collected, centrifuged at 1000 ×g for 10 min, and filter‐sterilized through 0.22 µm syringe filters. Bacterial suspensions (MRSA and E. coli, 1 × 106 CFU/mL) were prepared in serum‐free DMEM and mixed with equal volumes of conditioned medium (500 µL each), followed by 6‐hour incubation at 37°C. Bacterial suspensions were serially diluted, and 20 µL aliquots were spread onto agar plates. Plates were incubated overnight at 37°C and colonies were enumerated to calculate bacterial survival rates. Fresh unconditioned serum‐free DMEM served as a control (n = 3 per group).

Western Blot Analysis

For protein expression analysis, cells or tissue samples were treated under specified conditions, followed by total protein extraction using RIPA lysis buffer supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor cocktails. Samples were lysed on ice for 30 min and centrifuged at 12 000 rpm for 15 min at 4°C to collect supernatants. Protein concentrations were determined using BCA protein assay kit. Equal amounts of protein samples (20–40 µg) were mixed with 5× loading buffer, denatured by boiling at 100°C for 5 min, and separated by SDS‐PAGE gel electrophoresis (5% stacking gel and 8%–12% separating gel depending on target protein molecular weight) under constant voltage until bromophenol blue reached the gel bottom. Proteins were transferred onto PVDF membranes (pre‐activated with methanol) using wet or semi‐dry transfer method at 300 mA constant current for 1 h. Membranes were blocked with 5% BSA in TBST for 1 h at room temperature, followed by incubation with specific primary antibodies (diluted 1:500–1:2000) overnight at 4°C. After washing three times with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP‐conjugated secondary antibodies (diluted 1:5000–1:10 000) for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using ECL chemiluminescence detection kit and captured by a chemiluminescence imaging system. Band intensities were quantified using ImageJ software and normalized to internal reference proteins, with data expressed as relative expression levels of target proteins to housekeeping proteins.

RNA Sequencing and Bioinformatics Analysis

For macrophage and BMSC transcriptomic analysis, cells were cultured with coating materials for specified time periods, followed by RNA extraction using TRIzol and quality assessment. Single‐cell RNA sequencing was performed using 10x Genomics Chromium platform. Clean reads were obtained by removing adapter sequences and low‐quality reads, then mapped to reference genome using Hisat2 software. Gene expression levels were quantified using FPKM method, and differential expression analysis was conducted using DESeq2 R package with FDR < 0.05 and |log2(foldchange)| ≥ 1 as significance thresholds. Functional annotation included Gene Ontology enrichment analysis using GOseq R packages and KEGG pathway analysis using KOBAS software. Protein‐protein interaction networks were constructed using STRING database and visualized using Cytoscape software. Principal component analysis, clustering analysis, and transcription factor analysis using SCENIC algorithm were performed using R statistical software.

Animal Experiments

All animal procedures were approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of University of Science and Technology of China (approval number: 202 501 242 136 000 515 763). Male Sprague‐Dawley rats (8–10 weeks old, 250–300 g) were obtained from Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). A rat femoral condyle implantation model with MRSA infection was established following anesthetization with sodium pentobarbital (35 mg/kg, intraperitoneal). A 2 mm diameter hole was drilled in the femoral condyle 5 mm proximal to the distal femoral condyle tangent. Coated titanium implants were contaminated with 106 CFU MRSA and inserted into the bone defect. Surgical sites were closed layer by layer using absorbable sutures and animals received postoperative analgesia.

In Vivo Assessment and Histological Analysis

Animals were sacrificed at days 7, 14, and 56 post – implantation (n = 5 per group per time point). For antimicrobial efficacy assessment at day 7, implants were retrieved and processed for bacterial culture, with viable bacteria quantified by CFU counting, and SEM analysis performed after glutaraldehyde fixation. Surrounding tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for histological analysis using hematoxylin‐eosin and Giemsa staining. Inflammatory cell infiltration was quantified by manual counting in standardized microscopic fields. For immune response evaluation at day 14, tissues were processed for immunohistochemical staining using antibodies against TNF‐α, IL‐10, CD86, and CD163, with quantitative analysis of area percentages. For bone formation assessment at 8 weeks, femurs were analyzed by micro‐computed tomography (µCT, Sanying, China) at 60 kV/500 µA with 18 µm voxel size. Bone morphometric parameters, including bone volume fraction (BV/TV), trabecular separation, trabecular thickness, and bone mineral density, were calculated using CT Analyzer software. Histological evaluation was performed using Masson's trichrome staining, toluidine blue staining for cross‐sectional views, and immunohistochemical staining for osteogenic markers (BSP, COL‐1). Some samples were processed for undecalcified sectioning (50 µm thickness) to evaluate fluorochrome labeling, while others were decalcified for paraffin embedding and 6 µm sectioning.

Statistical Analysis

All experiments were performed in triplicate with at least three independent replicates. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0 software. One‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey's post hoc test was used for multiple group comparisons. Unpaired t‐test was used for two‐group comparisons. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05, with significance levels indicated as *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. For transcriptomic analysis, false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied with q < 0.05 considered significant.
