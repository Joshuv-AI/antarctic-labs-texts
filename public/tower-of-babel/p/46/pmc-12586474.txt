The clinical value and application of augmented reality navigation in brachial plexus schwannoma
Authors: Zirui Yu, Wanling Zheng, Peng Peng, Lingkang Zhu, Xuanyu Zhao, Sihan Hu, Huali Zhao, Yanqun Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study aimed to introduce the application of magnetic resonance imaging (MRI)-derived augmented reality (AR) navigation in the surgical treatment of brachial plexus schwannomas and to evaluate and statistically analyze their clinical outcomes. Preoperative MRI data were converted into 3D reconstructions and AR holograms. Using mixed-reality glasses, surgeons employed these reconstructions for intraoperative tumor localization and surgical planning. Outcomes were assessed through structural visualization metrics (tumor, arteries, veins, and nerves) and clinical parameters (incision length, operative time, intraoperative blood loss, postoperative complications, satisfaction, and Quick-DASH scores). Twelve patients (mean age 44.75 ± 3.76 years) successfully underwent tumor resection. Compared to conventional MRI, AR demonstrated superior visualization of tumors (p < 0.0001), arteries (p = 0.0004), veins (p < 0.0001) and nerves (p < 0.0001). Although there was no significant change in incision length, intraoperative blood loss and Quick-DASH, AR navigation significantly reduced operative time (p = 0.04) and improved satisfaction score (p = 0.03). Introduction

Schwannomas are the most common tumors in the brachial plexus, followed less frequently by neurofibromas and malignant peripheral nerve sheath tumors1–3. Schwannoma usually presents as an isolated firm mass in the supraclavicular region with variable pain or weakness4. A complete surgical resection is the gold standard of brachial plexus schwannoma’s treatment5,6. However, the brachial plexus’s anatomical complexity poses significant intraoperative injury risks and potential neurological deficits. Consequently, improving surgical precision while reducing complications is the focus of current research.

Among all diagnostic tools, MRI was the preferred imaging technique for diagnosis and optimal tumor assessment in brachial plexus schwannoma7. However, conventional T1/T2 sequences often failed to differentiate the nerve tumors from other soft tissue tumors8. Previous work have showed that enhanced heavily T2 3D STIR SPACE provided superior vascular suppression and homogenous fat suppression in brachial plexus imaging9.

Augmented Reality (AR) integrates preoperative three-dimensional (3D) models with surgical region to visualize spatial relationships between the important anatomical structures and tumors10,11. However, whether AR Navigation based on enhanced MRI improves visualization and clinical outcomes for the brachial plexus schwannoma are still unknown. Hence, this study aimed to evaluate this AR technology’s application, assessing both imaging quality and clinical outcomes.

Methods

Population

Data from 12 patients diagnosed with brachial plexus schwannoma in the Department of Hand and Upper Extremity Surgery, Jing’an District Central Hospital, Shanghai, China, between January 1, 2021, and July 1, 2023, were included in our study. Data from MRI scans were derived from all these patients. All patients were treated by a single surgeon who specialized in nerve surgery of the upper limb. The age, gender, tumor size and location, preoperative symptoms, image findings, intraoperative findings, and postoperative complications were evaluated and analyzed to assess. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Jing’an District Central Hospital (2020-22) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. All patients were adequately informed about the purpose and content of the study and provided written informed consent before inclusion.

Contrast-enhanced MRI of the neck was performed on a 3.0-T MRI system (MAGNETOM Trio, Siemens Healthcare, Germany). Before the MRI scan, seven anchor points should be located at: a. Laryngeal cartilage b.c. Medial border of sternocleidomastoid at the level of laryngeal cartilage d. Lateral margin of manubrium sternum e.f. 3 cm outside of point c on the same plane. Preoperative MRI sequence in sequence (T1 and T2) scan and 3-D STIR SPACE sequence scan before and after the administration of gadolinium-based contrast medium was acquired. All patients received 0.15 mmol/kg of contrast medium at a rate of 3.0 mL/s, with a maximum of 120 mL scanned in the arterial phase in accordance with standard protocol12. The special scan parameters were as follows: gre_cemra_cor_post (filed of view = 350 mm, thickness / interval = 1.1 / 0 mm, Resolution = 320 * 220, TR: 4.2ms, TE: 1.8ms); stir_mx3d_cor (filed of view = 340 mm, thickness / interval = 0.8 / 0 mm, Resolution = 480 * 480, TR = 3200ms, TE = 338.5ms); t2_mx3d_sag (filed of view = 240 mm, thickness / interval = 0.7 / 0 mm, Resolution = 480 * 480, TR = 1400ms, TE = 165.2ms) (Supplementary Video 1). Thin section images were saved in the digital imaging and communications in medicine (DICOM) format. One magnetic resonance diagram is shown in (Fig. 1B).

3-D reconstruction

After completion of the MRI scan, the image was reconstructed by a professional and the tumor location was located according to the reconstructed 3-D image. The MRI scans from each patient were loaded as DICOM files and processed by using picture archiving and communication system, HoloNeuro version 1.0 (Laiqiu Medical Technology Co, LTD, Shanghai). Each anatomical structure (brachial plexus tumor, arteries, veins and nerves) was segmented separately and given a different color. The diagram is shown in (Fig. 1C). After the full segmentation was completed, a full view of the tumor and its border separated from the healthy tissue was reconstruction. Segmentations were saved as a stereolithography file (.STL) that was suitable for AR navigation (Supplementary Video 2). An example of a patient’s brachial plexus schwannoma 3-D reconstruction is provided in the model (https://www.licyo.net/mr-web3d/model?accessId=300015).

AR navigation

AR navigation was through a mixed reality headset (HoloLens2; Microsoft Corp), which uses a head-mounted display with a stereo see-through display and a wireless design. This device provides a realistic 3-D image and stimulates the user to examine the hologram from different positions and perspectives. Although each 3-D model was different and unique to each patient’s tumor and anatomy, the architecture and user interface of the application were similar across the sample group. Finally, the application was imported to the headset. An example of a patient’s brachial plexus schwannoma is provided in the (Fig. 1D).

Surgical procedures

Under general anaesthesia, the patient was positioned on a routinely video-monitored and draped operating table. Anaesthesia was induced using intravenous propofol for sedation, sufentanil for analgesia, and rocuronium as a neuromuscular blocking agent, followed by tracheal intubation. General anaesthesia was maintained with a combination of inhalational agents (sevoflurane or desflurane) and continuous intravenous infusion of remifentanil.

Key microsurgical procedures were performed under microscope guidance13. Using MRI or AR navigation, a lateral collar incision was designed in the inferolateral neck. The tumor was dissected layer by layer along the brachial plexus nerve trunk with capsule preservation to enable extracapsular resection. Specific surgical techniques included opening the epineurium, enucleating the tumor, and sectioning the fascicle of origin. Brachial plexus schwannoma resection requires nerve trunk protection and maximal nerve fiber preservation, with intraoperative EMG monitoring to assess functional integrity of nerve branches. Following complete tumor excision, specimens were sent for pathological analysis.

Data acquisition

A panel of 6 experts, consisting of 4 surgeons and 2 radiologists were asked to individually evaluate the MRI and/or AR image for each patient. The experts completed a questionnaire that rated the tumor, arteries, veins, and nerve structures on a scale of 1 to 5 (1 for indicates obvious displacement; 2 for slight displacement; 3 for close; 4 for roughly coincident; and 5 for completely coincident)14. The quality of the image visualization is evaluated.

The age and gender of the patients, tumor size and location, disease time of the tumor, imaging findings, preoperative symptoms and intraoperative data (incision length, operation duration, intraoperative blood loss) were obtained from the medical record, and post-operative complications were obtained from the follow-up survey. The presence of a Hoffmann-Tinel sign (shooting paresthesia in the distribution of the involved nerve) upon percussion of the tumor, atrophy, spontaneous pain, sensory and motor deficits were recorded15. At the last follow-up, subjective evaluation of satisfaction was done with an analytical questionnaire ranging from 0 (dissatisfaction) to 10 (great satisfaction). The ability to carry out daily life activities was 2 by Disabilities of the Arm, Shoulder and Hand Questionnaire Score (Quick-DASH) questionnaire16.

Statistical analysis

Qualitative variables were described using numbers and percentages, and quantitative variables were described using means and standard deviations and compared using t-test. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad prism 7.0 and SPSS 22.0. A P-value less than 0.05 was recognized as statistically significant.

Results

Patient characteristics

This study enrolled 12 patients with brachial plexus schwannomas (6 male, 6 female; mean age 44.75 ± 3.76 years; range 27–71 years).Average disease time before surgical resection was 9.90 ± 8.10 months (range = 1 week − 24 months). Tumor distribution included: upper trunk (n = 2, 16.7%), middle trunk (n = 6, 50.0%), and lower trunk (n = 4, 33.3%). Pre-operatively, 9 patients (75%) had Tinel-like signs, 1 patient (8.3%) had atrophy, 9 patients (75%) had sensory deficit, 1 patient (8.3%) had pain, and no patient had motor deficit. Assessment of all anatomical structures

Postoperative 3D reconstructions and augmented reality (AR) models were generated for research purposes in six patients. AR navigation led to better assessment of the tumor, arteries, veins, and nerves compared with MRI only on PACS in terms of image visualization (tumor: median scores for MRI, 3.83 ± 0.09 vs. AR navigation, 4.72 ± 0.03; p < 0.0001; arteries: MRI, 3.11 ± 0.21 vs. AR navigation, 4.42 ± 0.15; p = 0.0004; veins: MRI, 2.81 ± 0.08 vs. AR navigation, 4.22 ± 0.03; p < 0.0001; and nerves: MRI, 3.20 ± 0.12 vs. AR navigation, 4.33 ± 0.11; p < 0.0001). Assessment of surgical results

Compared with MRI group, AR navigation showed a significant improvement in operation duration (MRI, 100.83 ± 34.70 vs. AR navigation, 191.67 ± 89.81, p = 0.04) and satisfaction score (satisfaction score: MRI, 9.75 ± 0.42 vs. AR navigation, 8.67 ± 0.98, p = 0.03). However, there were no significant differences were observed in incision length, intraoperative blood loss and Quick-DASH score (incision length: MRI, 6.50 ± 0.85 vs. AR navigation, 5.83 ± 0.48, p = 0.51; intraoperative blood loss: MRI, 78.33 ± 64.36 vs. AR navigation, 50.00 ± 24.63, p = 0.69; Quick-DASH score: MRI, 1.89 ± 0.91 vs. AR navigation, 1.14 ± 0.77, p = 0.54). Discussion

Brachial plexus masses encompass various pathologic types, and surgical approaches differ accordingly. Schwannomas slowly grow within the epineurium as an eccentric mass deflecting the peripheral nerve, whereas neurofibromas grow from the perineurium as a centrally fusiform mass17. Good judgment is very important for surgical design. By converting MRI data from brachial plexus schwannomas into 3D reconstructions and AR holograms, we can more clearly visualize the correlation of the mass with peripheral neuro-vessels and thus make more accurate judgments. Our study demonstrates that AR navigation was able to better assess tumor, arteries, veins, and nerves compared to conventional MRI. As our previous report, high-quality 3D reconstructions and AR technology enhance surgeons’ understanding of the relationship between the mass nerves and blood vessels, improving preoperative evaluation and guiding treatment for brachial plexus schwannomas18.

With the assist of AR navigation, all the brachial plexus schwannomas were completely resected without serious complications except for local numbness. Furthermore, when combined with intraoperative electrophysiological monitoring, peripheral nerve damage was minimized, reducing the incidence of postoperative brachial plexus neuralgia and functional decline. Considering the schwannoma is located in the nerve sheath, post-operative innervation numbness often occurs due to intraoperative separation and stretching of the nerve, which usually resolves after the nerve edema subsides19. Although no significant differences were observed in incision length, intraoperative blood loss and Quick-DASH, AR navigation showed a significant improvement in operation duration and satisfaction score. In our research, these surgical results are more closely associated with tumor size and location. Future research with an expanded sample size will enable further comparative analysis.

AR navigation enhances image quality through enhanced MRI, providing valuable guidance for surgical safety and surgical design. This technology enables three-dimensional visualization of lesions, maintaining positional stability relative to the surgeon’s perspective20. However, AR navigation increases the financial cost of the examination and the preoperative preparation time21. Currently, the cost of this examination is funded by research grants, and it is worthwhile from the perspective of maximizing patient benefits.

There are several limitations to the current study. Although the recovery effect is favorable, the sample was relatively small, which may have reduced the statistical power. Besides, maintaining consistency between the patient’s surgical position and MRI scan requires optimization in future research. Hence, future research should be aimed at improving the AR accuracy and expanding its clinical use.

Study limitations and future directions

This study has several limitations. First, the small sample size (n = 12) restricts the generalizability of the findings, and the absence of a statistical power analysis limits the strength of our conclusions. Second, while the augmented reality (AR) navigation system provided enhanced visualization of critical anatomical structures, the accuracy of the image overlay was affected by differences in patient positioning between preoperative MRI (supine) and intraoperative positioning (lateral). Additionally, respiratory motion during surgery contributed to minor misalignments in the AR images. For example, in Fig. 1D, the contralateral vein appears closer to the midline due to these registration discrepancies, a limitation also highlighted in prior research22. These technical limitations highlight the need for improved registration protocols and real-time adjustments to optimize AR navigation accuracy. Future studies should address these issues by expanding the sample size and incorporating quantitative assessments of registration accuracy.

Conclusions

This study demonstrates that AR navigation provides superior visualization of the spatial relationships among tumors, arteries, veins, and nerves compared to MRI, potentially enhancing surgical safety and guiding preoperative planning for brachial plexus schwannomas.
