Zonal load transfer controlled by an L-shaped irregular coal pillar and the mechanism of rockburst induced by static-dynamic load superposition
Authors: Rupei Zhang, Siyuan Gong, Hui Li, Jiapeng Lv
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Under the condition of slicing mining in extra-thick coal seams, the presence of irregular coal pillars is likely to cause stress redistribution in the roadway region and induce rockburst. Taking the "3·22" rockburst event that occurred in the haulage roadway of the 250,101–2 working face in Huating Coal Mine as the engineering background, this paper investigated the occurrence mechanism of roadway rockburst under irregular coal pillar conditions by combining source mechanism inversion, numerical simulation, and theoretical analysis. The results show that the double-couple component is dominant in the moment tensor inversion results, indicating that the source type of this event was shear-type, and that the essence of the rockburst instability was the sudden shear slip of the coal-rock mass under high-stress conditions. The PFC simulation results show that, under the control of an L-shaped irregular coal pillar formed by a 20 m residual section pillar and a 34 m residual pillar, the overburden load developed a zonal load transfer pattern. Specifically, the 20 m pillar constituted the main load transfer channel, while the compacted zone above the 34 m pillar regained a certain bearing capacity after compaction of the caved rock mass and exerted an auxiliary reloading effect on the underlying surrounding rock, resulting in the haulage roadway not being in a fully destressed state. On this basis, a two-segment bearing model of the L-shaped irregular coal pillar was established, and the static stress distribution characteristics in the roadway region under the combined action of the two-segment loads were analyzed based on half-plane elasticity theory. Furthermore, by incorporating the attenuation law of vibration waves, a stress increment estimation model under dynamic loading disturbance was established, and the dynamic stress increment generated on the roadway surface by the "3·22" rockburst event was calculated to be about 4.06 MPa. Finally, the stress concentration characteristics and stress deflection effect under the control of the L-shaped coal pillar structure were discussed. The results show that an increase in the right-wing thickness of the L-shaped coal pillar structure enlarges the stress deflection zone and enhances stress redistribution toward the roadway, thereby increasing the possibility of rockburst under the combined action of high static stress and dynamic disturbance. The research results reveal the occurrence mechanism of roadway rockburst under L-shaped irregular coal pillar conditions, and can provide a theoretical reference for identifying rockburst hazard zones and optimizing working face layout parameters under similar engineering conditions.

Introduction

Rockburst is one of the most sudden and destructive dynamic hazards during deep coal mining. Its essence lies in the rapid instability of the coal-rock mass under a high-stress environment, accompanied by the instantaneous release of elastic energy1–4. As coal resource extraction continues to advance to greater depths, with increasing burial depth, intensified tectonic stress, and aggravated mining-induced disturbance, the conditions for rockburst occurrence have become more complex5. Accordingly, the rockburst process is no longer simply manifested as the result of local static load concentration, but is more often characterized by instability and failure induced by the combined action of long-term development under a high static load environment and instantaneous triggering by dynamic load disturbance6. For slicing mining faces in extra-thick coal seams, owing to historical mining practices and the need to support the load of the overlying strata, coal pillars of a certain width are usually left after extraction of the upper seam (upper residual coal pillars) to ensure the stability of the underground space after mining7. However, coal pillars are the primary zones of stress concentration, and these residual pillars usually have a significant influence on rockburst risk8,9. In particular, when the working face layout is unreasonable, a complex spatial structure in which irregular residual coal pillars coexist with goafs is likely to form, causing the surrounding rock of the underlying roadway to remain for a long time in a stress environment characterized by significant biased loading and frequent stress adjustment, thereby markedly increasing the risk of rockburst.

Around the formation mechanism of roadway rockburst under the influence of overlying coal pillars, a large number of studies have been carried out by scholars both in China and abroad. Existing studies have shown that overlying residual coal pillars can significantly alter the stress distribution in the underlying coal seam and surrounding rock of the roadway through stress concentration and load transfer, thereby inducing asymmetric failure, severe strata behavior, and even rockburst instability10,11. Qi et al.12, based on plastic slip-line field theory and limit equilibrium theory combined with numerical simulation, investigated the bearing failure range of the floor under the concentrated load of section pillars and its influence on the stability of roadway surrounding rock. Li et al.13 studied the disturbance evolution mechanism of the floor stress induced by overlying residual section pillars before and after mining of the underlying coal seam according to the disturbance propagation law of floor stress beneath the overlying residual coal pillars, and further calculated and analyzed the width range of disturbance during extraction of the underlying coal seam beneath the residual pillar area. Sun et al.14 showed that a large number of residual section pillars in close-distance coal seam mining can significantly change the overburden control effect and the loading state of the underlying structure, and may induce pillar instability and roadway failure. Concerning the stress concentration problem associated with residual coal pillars left in overlying goafs, Shao et al.15 pointed out that the residual pillar and the hanging roof structure jointly constitute a high-stress bearing system, and that the essence of the disaster mechanism lies in the continuous concentration and transfer of stress within the pillar-roof system, rather than in the isolated bearing of the pillar itself. This indicates that pillar-controlled rockburst cannot be explained solely by the stress peak beneath the pillar, but must also be analyzed from the overall relationship among the overburden structure, the state of the goaf, and the response of the underlying surrounding rock, so as to clarify the formation path of the static load environment.

At the same time, with the development of microseismic monitoring and source inversion techniques, researchers have been able to identify the dynamic response characteristics of rockburst from such aspects as source rupture type, energy release characteristics, and wave propagation process, thereby providing new analytical means for revealing the triggering role of dynamic load disturbance16,17. Jiang et al.18 investigated the rockburst instability process of wide coal pillars under the superposition of static and dynamic loads, and pointed out that the dynamic instability of coal pillars undergoes three stages, namely energy accumulation, local instability, and dynamic instability. Once dynamic loads such as roof fracture are superimposed on the pre-existing static load and exceed the critical stress and energy conditions, rockburst failure of the pillar may occur. Tang et al.19 conducted a study on a working face beneath residual coal pillars left under a goaf, and found that the area beneath the residual pillars remains the zone with the strongest stress concentration and highest rockburst hazard, and that high-energy microseismic events further cluster in this region as the overburden structure becomes unstable again.

Overall, existing studies have revealed the formation process of rockburst from two aspects, namely static load transfer and dynamic load triggering. However, under slicing mining conditions of extra-thick coal seams, there is still a lack of systematic understanding, at the level of specific engineering structures, of how the irregular overlying coal pillar-goaf structure controls the stress environment of the underlying roadway. Most previous studies on overlying coal pillars have focused on regular pillars or symmetric bearing structures, and usually simplify the residual coal pillar as a single load source, while insufficient attention has been paid to the asymmetric zonal bearing structure jointly formed by the "residual coal pillar-compacted goaf". In particular, quantitative analysis is still lacking on the hierarchical transfer of loads, their spatial redistribution, and the deflection characteristics of stress transfer paths. At the same time, under complex structural conditions, the formation path of the high static load environment around the roadway, the relative contributions of different bearing units, and the triggering effect of dynamic load disturbance remain to be clarified.

Based on this, taking the "3·22" rockburst event on the LW250101-2 of Huating Coal Mine as the engineering background, this study comprehensively employs source mechanism inversion, numerical simulation, and theoretical analysis to investigate the incubation and triggering mechanisms of roadway rockburst under the control of an L-shaped irregular coal pillar. First, the rupture type and dynamic attributes of the rockburst event are identified. Second, the zonal load transfer relationship among the overburden fracture evolution, the 20 m pillar, and the compacted zone above the 34 m pillar under upper and lower slicing mining conditions is revealed. Then, a two-segment bearing model of the L-shaped irregular coal pillar is established to quantitatively analyze the static stress distribution in the roadway region and its controlling factors. Finally, by combining the attenuation law of vibration wave propagation with the relationship between stress increment and dynamic load disturbance, the superposition mechanism of static and dynamic loads for roadway rockburst under a typical strong dynamic load is clarified, and the influence of the L-shaped coal pillar structure on stress concentration and stress transfer deflection is discussed. The results of this study can provide a theoretical basis for the identification of rockburst mechanisms and the prevention and control of rockburst in roadways with similar complex bearing structures under slicing mining conditions of extra-thick coal seams.

Engineering background

Working face layout

Huating Coal Mine is located in the central-southern part of the eastern wing of the Huating Coalfield syncline in Gansu Province, China, with a strike length of 3.2 km and an inclined width of 3.6 km. The mine mainly extracts the No. 5 coal seam, with a dip angle ranging from 4° to 12°. The coal seam thickness varies from 17.6 m to 48.2 m, with an average thickness of 37.51 m, and is classified as an extra-thick coal seam. The seam is mined in three slices.

The LW250101-2 is the second working face in the middle slice of the 2501 mining district in the No. 5 coal seam, with a designed extractable length of 1800 m and a width of 200 m. A secondary anticline passes through the middle of the working face, and the coal seam occurrence within the mining area is relatively gentle. Its northern boundary is the mine boundary, the southern side is the main roadway protective coal pillar, the western side is the goaf of the LW250102-2, the eastern side is solid coal, and the overlying area consists of the goafs of the upper-slice 250101-1 and LW250102-1, between which a 20 m residual section pillar was left. In addition, owing to the unequal mining heights of the two working faces and the staggered layout of the LW250102-2, a 34 m residual pillar was also left between the LW250102-2 and the 20 m residual section pillar. These two pillars together formed an overlying "L"-shaped irregular coal pillar above the 250101-2 haulage roadway. A schematic diagram of the working face layout is shown in Fig. 1. Meanwhile, to investigate the in-situ stress distribution in Huating Coal Mine, two measuring points were arranged in the No. 5 coal seam, and in-situ stress testing was carried out using the stress relief method. Rockburst manifestation

On March 22, 2023, during the mining of LW250101-2, a severely damaging rockburst event occurred in the haulage roadway, hereafter referred to as the "3.22" rockburst. According to field investigation, the rockburst damage was located in the haulage roadway and was mainly manifested as floor heave, accompanied by rib spalling, roof subsidence, and damage to the support system and haulage equipment. Combined with the moment tensor inversion results of this rockburst event, the source mechanism solution shows that the double-couple component is dominant (Fig. 4), whereas the contribution of non-shear volumetric change is weak, indicating that this event was not dominated by simple compaction or tensile cracking, but mainly by sudden shear slip of the coal-rock mass along dominant structural planes or in local high-stress zones. Therefore, the source type of this rockburst can be identified as shear-type. This result indicates that, under the combined action of mining-induced disturbance and tectonic stress, a high-stress environment favorable for shear dislocation formed beneath the L-shaped irregular coal pillar and in the adjacent roadway area. Once the local stress exceeded the strength limit of the coal-rock mass, instantaneous shear rupture was induced and a large amount of elastic energy was released, ultimately leading to the occurrence of rockburst.Fig. 4Source mechanism solution for the rockburst event.

Characteristics of overburden fracture and stress transfer

Establishment of the discrete element numerical model

According to the coal-bearing stratigraphic characteristics and mining technical conditions of Huating Coal Mine, a two-dimensional model was established along the dip direction of the working face using the PFC numerical simulation software. The model dimensions were 500 m × 260 m in length and height, as shown in Fig. 5. The minimum particle radius was 1 m, the particle size ratio was 1.67, and the porosity was 0.12. A total of 79,455 particles were generated, and walls with displacement constraints were installed on the lateral boundaries and bottom of the model to serve as model boundaries. After particle generation, the linear contact model (L-Model) was first adopted to achieve initial equilibrium. Then, according to the actual in-situ stress conditions, confining pressure and axial pressure were applied to the model boundaries for servo-controlled equilibrium. Finally, the parallel bond model (PB-Model) was used to modify the contacts in order to simulate the integral mechanical behavior of the rock strata, while the smooth joint model (SJ-Model) was adopted to simulate the mechanical properties of structural planes between different particle layers23. Characteristics of overburden fracture and force chains

In PFC, the primary mechanism of load transfer between particles is the force chain, and this structure can be used to characterize the stress distribution within the overburden. The force chain network interconnects particles, and its thickness and density reflect the macroscopic force state of the material. Regions where force chains are concentrated also correspond to zones of stress concentration24. Figure 6 shows the displacement of overburden movement and the evolution of force chains after mining of each working face.Fig. 6Overburden displacement and force chain distribution contour.

Overburden displacement and force chain distribution contour.

As can be seen from Fig. 6(a) and Fig. 6(b), after mining of the upper-slice LW250101-1 and LW250101-2, the overlying strata above the goafs on both sides had already undergone obvious subsidence and caving. From the force chain distribution, a relatively continuous and concentrated vertical strong force chain zone had formed above and within the 20 m pillar, whereas the force chains in the region above the 34 m pillar were relatively sparse and scattered. This indicates that, under the condition where only the upper slice had been mined, the overburden load was mainly transferred downward through the 20 mpillar, which had already become the initial primary load transfer channel for the overburden load.

As the mining disturbance in the lower slice intensified (Fig. 6(c) and Fig. 6 (d)), the overburden above the goaf in the region overlying the 34 m pillar underwent further caving, stacking, and compaction. The displacement plot shows an obvious compaction feature in this area, indicating that the caved rock mass had re-established a relatively stable contact relationship and thus possessed the structural basis for recovering part of its bearing capacity. Correspondingly, a certain scale of continuous force chains began to appear within this region in the force chain plot, reflecting that the compacted caved medium had started to participate in load transfer. However, compared with the region of the 20 m pillar, the force chains in this area still exhibited obviously weaker continuity and intensity overall, indicating that its bearing and load transfer capacities remained significantly lower than those of the 20 m pillar. It can therefore be seen that, under the L-shaped coal pillar structure, the final overburden load transfer pattern was characterized by a hierarchical load transfer framework, in which the 20 m pillar dominated load transfer, while the compacted zone above the 34 m pillar provided auxiliary load transfer.

Further analysis of the force chain evolution shows that the stress influence range of the primary load transfer zone controlled by the 20 m pillar was not limited to the area directly beneath it, but extended toward the 34 m pillar and its surrounding area. At the same time, the reloading effect of the compacted zone above the 34 m pillar also made it difficult for the underlying surrounding rock to form a purely destressed environment beneath the goaf. Because the 250,101–2 haulage roadway was located beneath the 34 m pillar, this roadway was not situated in a completely destressed protection zone beneath the goaf, but rather within a stress influence zone jointly controlled by the primary load transfer effect of the 20 m pillar and the reloading effect of the compacted zone above the 34 m pillar.

Rockburst mechanism under static-dynamic load superposition

Zonal bearing model and top load functions

To reveal the controlling mechanism of the combined structure consisting of the overlying 20 m pillar and the adjacent 34 m pillar-compacted zone on the stress environment of the underlying roadway region, the actual engineering profile was appropriately idealized in mechanical terms. Because the strike length of the working face is much larger than the characteristic size of the profile, and this study focuses on the stress transfer and distribution law within the dip section, the problem can be approximately treated as a two-dimensional plane strain problem.

On this basis, the study region was simplified as a loaded continuous body composed of an upper zonal bearing structure and the underlying deep coal-rock mass, as shown in Fig. 7. In this model, the upper 20 m pillar is the concentrated bearing zone, and the load acting on its top is denoted as q1(x); the 34 m pillar, under the load transfer effect of the compacted overburden, corresponds to a secondary bearing zone, and the equivalent transferred load acting on its top is denoted as q2(x). The underlying coal-rock mass is regarded as a homogeneous, continuous, isotropic linear elastic medium, mainly used to characterize the transfer, diffusion, and redistribution of deep stress under the action of the two-segment loads. The boundary line between the 20 m pillar and the LW250101-1 is taken as x = 0, with the positive direction along the coal seam dip from left to right; the top interface of the 20 m pillar is taken as z = 0, with the positive direction downward along the strata. In this way, a two-dimensional plane strain zonal bearing model was established for the L-shaped irregular coal pillar under the action of segmented equivalent top loads. For convenience of expression, the width of the 20 m pillar is denoted by b1, and the width of the 34 m pillar is denoted by b2.Fig. 7Mechanical model of the L-shaped irregular coal pillar.

Unlike the primary bearing zone, the area above the 34 m pillar does not directly sustain the overburden load through an intact coal-rock mass, but mainly transfers stress downward through a compacted load-transfer structure formed by the continuous compaction of caved gangue in the goaf. Meanwhile, previous studies have shown that the stress within the goaf gradually recovers with the progressive compaction of caved gangue and the development of overburden deformation, and that this recovery process is jointly controlled by bulking characteristics, compaction state, and overburden structural constraints. Therefore, the load acting on the top of the 34 m pillar in this region should not be simply treated as a directly concentrated load in an intact medium, but rather as an equivalent transferred load governed by the compaction state of the gangue. Considering that the caved gangue in the goaf is initially in a loose and bulking state, with the continuous subsidence of the roof and repeated action of the overburden load, its internal voids gradually close, while interparticle contact and interlocking continuously increase, thereby progressively restoring the bearing capacity of the goaf. Effect of dynamic disturbance on roadway rockburst

Microseismic dynamic disturbance is a key triggering factor for rockburst in deep mines. During the propagation of vibration waves, owing to the discontinuity and viscoelastic characteristics of the rock mass medium, the wavefront undergoes not only geometrical spreading attenuation, but also strong non-elastic damping absorption. Therefore, the energy of the stress wave and the particle vibration velocity induced by it exhibit nonlinear attenuation with increasing propagation distance. According to the attenuation law of vibration waves in a rock mass medium, the peak particle velocity (PPV) at a distance r from the source can be expressed as:16\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$v_{p}\,=\,v_{p0} \frac{1}{r}e^{-\alpha r}$$\end{document}where vp is the PPV at a distance r from the source, vp0 is the PPV at the source, and α is the attenuation coefficient related to specific geological conditions. When the stress wave reaches the surrounding rock of the roadway, the transient vibration of rock mass particles is converted into dynamic additional stress within the surrounding rock. Based on the theory of one-dimensional elastic stress wave propagation, the stress increment induced by dynamic loading is proportional to the wave impedance of the rock mass, that is, the product of density and wave velocity, as well as to the particle vibration velocity. However, in practical engineering, because it is difficult to distinguish the PPVs of P-waves and S-waves, the maximum value of the recorded resultant velocity is usually taken as the PPV of the vibration wave. Meanwhile, for safety considerations, the P-wave velocity, which is the fastest, is selected for calculation. Discussion

The PFC simulation in Section "Characteristics of overburden fracture and stress transfer" revealed the overburden fracture, caving-compaction process, and force-chain evolution associated with the overlying residual coal pillar–goaf structure, indicating that the 20 m residual pillar and the compacted zone above the 34 m pillar jointly form a zonal load transfer structure. This result suggests that the static loading environment of the roadway is controlled not only by the magnitude of the overburden load, but also by the spatial morphology of the L-shaped irregular coal pillar and the associated stress transfer path. Although PFC can reveal the distribution of force chains and load transfer patterns under complex structures, its strength mainly lies in capturing the formation of overburden fracture, caving-compaction, and load transfer channels, while it is less capable of isolating the independent effect of structural geometry on local stress deflection.

Meanwhile, although the closed-form solution based on half-plane theory can, to a certain extent, reveal the basic law governing the stress distribution in the roadway surrounding rock under the action of two-segment loads, it is difficult to accurately characterize the influence of the evolution of a complex "L-shaped" spatial structure on stress transfer paths and local stress deflection characteristics, owing to the simplifying assumptions imposed by the analytical model on boundary conditions and medium structure. Therefore, this section further adopts the finite difference numerical simulation method to investigate the controlling effect of the evolution of the L-shaped stepped geometry on the stress field distribution in the roadway region. In order to control variables and highlight the independent role of structural factors, large-deformation mode was not activated in this part of the numerical simulation, and the additional load transfer caused by overlying roof caving and compaction was not considered. That is, the effect of q2(x) was neglected, and only the influence of changes in the L-shaped coal pillar structure on stress field distribution and stress transfer paths was analyzed.

At the same time, in order to more intuitively characterize the spatial evolution of stress transfer paths, this study, based on the in-plane stress tensor analysis in the x–z section under the plane strain assumption, developed a calculation program for the stress direction deflection angle using the FISH language, so as to realize the visual representation of the stress transfer direction. According to the vertical stress tensor contours and the stress deflection angle distribution contours, under the combined action of the goafs on both sides, the stress transmitted downward from the roof overburden generally exhibits a "butterfly-like" distribution pattern, and its spatial symmetry is significantly affected by the geometric configuration of the coal pillar structure. As the thickness of the right step of the L-shaped coal pillar increases, more stress from the left roof overburden converges toward the 20 m pillar region, resulting in a higher degree of stress concentration within the 20 m pillar. This indicates that, within the L-shaped coal pillar structure, even though part of the 34 m pillar is not in direct contact with the overlying roof and does not directly receive roof load input, the asymmetric geometric structure it forms can still significantly influence the stress distribution pattern during the downward transfer of roof load, thereby further enhancing the stress concentration effect in the 20 m pillar.

Further analysis shows that, once the overlying load enters the 20 m pillar, a distinctly asymmetric stiffness system is formed on its two sides, because the left side corresponds to a goaf destressing zone, whereas the lower right side is still constrained by the support provided by the 34 m pillar. Under the combined influence of full unloading in the left goaf and the support of the solid coal body on the lower right side, the stress transfer path no longer remains a simple vertical downward transmission, but instead exhibits obvious lateral deflection and local convergence toward the 34 m pillar and the roadway region. As a result, a stress transfer channel deflecting from the 20 m pillar toward the roadway region is formed.

It should be noted that the stress deflection angle characterizes the degree to which the principal stress transfer direction deviates from the vertical reference direction, and does not directly correspond to the disturbance intensity experienced by the roadway. The numerical results show that, as the thickness of the right step of the L-shaped coal pillar increases, stress deflection occurs earlier and its zone of influence expands significantly, gradually forming a wider stress deflection transition zone between the coal pillar and the roadway. In particular, within the widely distributed yellow-green region of 30∘∼60∘in the contour plots, the principal stress direction rotates rapidly and the stress path undergoes pronounced bending, accompanied by strong stress redistribution and an increase in shear stress. Therefore, this region is the key zone controlling the evolution of the roadway stress environment. By contrast, the red region, where the deflection angle approaches 90∘, more strongly reflects that the stress direction has been reoriented to a nearly horizontal state. This region is more indicative of the stress transfer pattern itself, but does not correspond to the location where the additional loading on the roadway is most significant. From the perspective of the mechanical mechanism, this stress deflection process, in which vertical transfer is transformed into lateral diffusion, induces higher shear stress and a stronger principal stress turning effect between the coal pillar and the roadway, thereby creating a more unfavorable combined stress environment near the roof, floor, and ribs of the roadway.

Combined with the results of the discrete element numerical simulation and the half-plane elastic theory analysis, it can be concluded that the thickness of the right step of the L-shaped coal pillar, that is, the thickness of the 34 m pillar, plays a decisive controlling role in the static stress environment of the roadway region. On the one hand, this thickness determines the compaction degree of the overlying roof, thereby affecting the load transfer capacity of q2(x); on the other hand, it controls the transfer intensity of roof load toward the 20 m pillar, thereby influencing the stress concentration level of q1(x). In addition, this structural parameter also constrains the deflected transfer characteristics of stress within the 20 m pillar toward the roadway region. Under the combined action of the above effects, a high static stress environment required for the incubation of rockburst in the LW250101-2 haulage roadway is ultimately formed, and rockburst instability is then triggered under the superposition of dynamic disturbance.

Conclusions

By comprehensively integrating the results of source mechanism inversion, overburden structural evolution analysis, two-segment load theoretical calculation, and microseismic dynamic load estimation, it can be concluded that the rockburst in the haulage roadway of the LW250101-2 in Huating Coal Mine was not caused by a single factor, but was a coupled instability process involving the sustained development of static load concentration and the instantaneous triggering effect of dynamic disturbance under the control of an L-shaped irregular coal pillar structure.
