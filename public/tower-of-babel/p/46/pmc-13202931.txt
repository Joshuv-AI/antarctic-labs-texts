Accuracy of digital interocclusal records at various protrusive mandibular positions: an in vitro study
Authors: Handenur Ayalan, Ahmet Atila Ertan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Accurate recording of the maxillomandibular relationship is a critical step in prosthodontic treatment, as inaccuracies at this stage may be transferred to the definitive restoration and compromise occlusal harmony, function, and patient comfort. Traditionally, maxillomandibular relationships have been recorded using conventional interocclusal registration materials such as waxes, zinc oxide–eugenol pastes, and elastomeric materials. However, these materials are susceptible to deformation, dimensional instability, and handling-related errors, which may affect the reliability of the recorded jaw relationship [1–3].

With the increasing adoption of digital workflows in prosthodontics, intraoral scanners (IOSs) have been widely used for recording dental arches and interocclusal relationships. Digital interocclusal registration offers potential advantages, including improved patient comfort, reduced chairside time, and simplified data transfer within computer-aided design and manufacturing (CAD-CAM) systems [4–6]. Nevertheless, previous studies have demonstrated that the accuracy of digitally recorded maxillomandibular relationships may be influenced by multiple factors, such as scanner type, scanning strategy, operator experience, and recording conditions [7–9].

Most investigations evaluating digital interocclusal registration accuracy have focused on recordings obtained at maximum intercuspal position (MIP). Although MIP represents a clinically relevant reference position, dense occlusal contacts at this position may increase the susceptibility to mandibular displacement and registration errors during digital bite acquisition [10, 11]. In contrast, altered mandibular positions involving changes in vertical dimension or protrusive displacement are frequently required in clinical practice, particularly in full-mouth rehabilitation, occlusal vertical dimension alteration, and the fabrication of mandibular advancement devices [12–14].

Accurate digital interocclusal records are important for correct virtual mounting in digital workflows [15, 16]. Errors in digital bite registration may lead to incorrect occlusal relationships and may require additional intraoral adjustments during treatment [17]. In clinical practice, interocclusal records can be obtained at protrusive mandibular positions, for example during the fabrication of mandibular advancement devices used in the management of obstructive sleep apnea. Therefore, evaluating the accuracy of digital interocclusal records at different protrusive and vertical positions is clinically relevant.

Despite the growing body of literature on digital interocclusal registration, limited information is available regarding the accuracy of digitally recorded maxillomandibular relationships under combined protrusive and vertical mandibular positions. Furthermore, the influence of scanner type on recording accuracy under these altered mandibular conditions remains insufficiently explored. Therefore, the purpose of this in vitro study was to evaluate the trueness and precision of digitally recorded maxillomandibular relationships obtained with two intraoral scanners at MIP and at various protrusive–vertical mandibular positions, and to investigate the potential interaction between scanner type and mandibular position.

The null hypotheses of this study were that; (1) mandibular position would not affect the trueness and precision of digitally recorded maxillomandibular relationships (2), intraoral scanner type would not affect recording accuracy; and (3) no interaction would exist between scanner type and mandibular position.

Materials and methods

Study design

This in vitro study evaluated the trueness and precision of digitally recorded maxillomandibular relationships obtained with two intraoral scanners at maximum intercuspal position (MIP) and at various protrusive–vertical mandibular positions.

Experimental model

A maxillary and mandibular dentate typodont model (Frasaco GmbH, Tettnang, Germany) was mounted on a semi-adjustable articulator (Whip Mix Corp., Louisville, KY, USA) in maximum intercuspal position according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

To standardize vertical mandibular positions, custom-designed platforms were placed at the incisal pin of the articulator. Protrusive mandibular positions were controlled using custom anterior ramps positioned at the condylar elements. All positioning components were digitally designed using Blender (Blender Foundation, Amsterdam, the Netherlands) and fabricated from polylactic acid (PLA) filament using fused deposition modeling (FDM) technology with a three-dimensional printer (Bambu Lab X1C, USA).

The vertical separation levels of 2 mm and 5 mm were selected based on commonly used clinical adjustment ranges with a George Gauge. Protrusive displacements of 2 mm and 4 mm were determined according to advancement ranges reported in previous clinical studies.

During digital interocclusal record acquisition, the mandibular position was stabilized using a polyvinyl siloxane bite registration material (Variotime Bite; Kulzer GmbH, Hanau, Germany) to maintain the predetermined vertical and protrusive displacement. The material was removed after completion of each scanning sequence. This setup enabled reproducible positioning of the mandible at predefined protrusive and vertical displacements (Table 1; Fig. 1).

Intraoral scanners

Digital scans were obtained using two intraoral scanners: Trios 5 (3Shape, Copenhagen, Denmark) and Medit i600 (Medit Corp., Seoul, South Korea). All scans were performed by a single experienced operator under standardized ambient lighting and room temperature conditions.

The Trios 5 scanner does not require routine user calibration. The Medit i600 scanner was calibrated periodically according to the manufacturer’s recommendations throughout the scanning procedures.

Scanning procedures

For each scanner, maxillary and mandibular arches were scanned separately following the manufacturer-recommended scanning strategy. Subsequently, digital interocclusal registrations were recorded at MIP and at six predetermined protrusive–vertical mandibular positions (2 V, 5 V, 2P + 2 V, 2P + 5 V, 4P + 2 V, and 4P + 5 V).

For each mandibular condition and scanner, one polyvinyl siloxane interocclusal record was produced. This record was then scanned 20 times under standardized digital and ambient conditions to evaluate trueness and precision.

In total, 280 digital interocclusal scan datasets (7 mandibular positions × 2 scanners × 20 repeated scans) were included in the analysis.

Reference scan acquisition

A high-accuracy laboratory scanner (Redon Freedom UHD; DOF Inc., Seoul, South Korea; reported accuracy < 5 μm) was used to obtain reference scans of the typodonts at each mandibular position. Reference datasets were exported in STL format and served as the reference standard for trueness evaluation.

Reference markers and measurements

To enable reproducible linear measurements, eight hemispherical reference markers (1 mm diameter) were bonded to the buccal surfaces of the right and left canines and first molars in both arches using a cyanoacrylate adhesive applied in minimal quantity. The markers were positioned prior to scanning and remained fixed throughout data acquisition.

Four predefined linear distances (D1–D4) were established between corresponding markers to evaluate discrepancies in the recorded maxillomandibular relationships.

Trueness and precision analysis

All STL datasets were imported into three-dimensional inspection software (Geomagic Control X; 3D Systems, Rock Hill, SC, USA). Experimental scans were aligned to the corresponding reference datasets using a marker-based best-fit alignment procedure. Alignment regions were restricted to the hemispherical reference markers to standardize superimposition across datasets.

Linear deviations between corresponding reference markers (D1–D4) were calculated to determine trueness (Fig. 2). Precision was defined as the repeatability of the 20 repeated scans per experimental condition and was expressed as the standard deviation of these measurements.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics v26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test.

Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed to evaluate the effects of scanner type and mandibular position, as well as their interaction, on trueness and precision values. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using the Bonferroni correction. The level of significance was set at α = 0.05. Effect sizes were calculated using partial eta squared (η²p). A post hoc power analysis was performed based on the observed effect sizes derived from the two-way ANOVA, indicating adequate statistical power for the primary outcomes.

Results

Trueness

Trueness values differed significantly among mandibular positions and between intraoral scanners (P < 0.001). Reduced trueness deviations were observed at 2 V compared with MIP for both scanners. Trueness values varied across protrusive–vertical combinations, with different patterns observed depending on scanner type (Table 2; Fig. 3).

Discussion

The present in vitro study evaluated the trueness and precision of digitally recorded maxillomandibular relationships at maximum intercuspal position (MIP) and at various protrusive–vertical mandibular positions using two intraoral scanners. The findings suggest that mandibular position and scanner type were associated with variations in digital interocclusal recording performance. A statistically significant interaction between scanner type and mandibular position was also observed, indicating that the influence of positional displacement on recording accuracy may vary depending on the digital system used.

Digital interocclusal registration has been reported to demonstrate comparable or, in some cases, improved precision compared with conventional techniques. In a clinical study, Iwauchi et al. reported smaller discrepancies with digital methods than with conventional interocclusal registrations [18]. Similarly, Solaberrieta et al. reported mean deviations of approximately 0.07 mm when validating a virtual occlusal record procedure, suggesting that digital alignment methods may provide acceptable accuracy under controlled conditions [10]. However, most available studies have focused on recordings obtained at MIP or centric relation, and limited evidence exists regarding altered protrusive–vertical mandibular conditions. The present study aimed to explore combined positional displacements under standardized experimental conditions.

The large effect size observed for mandibular position (η²p = 0.729) suggests that positional displacement may play a notable role in digital recording outcomes. Previous three-dimensional analyses have similarly demonstrated that interocclusal registration methods may induce measurable mandibular deviations, particularly along the vertical axis [19]. Certain vertically separated conditions were associated with reduced trueness deviations compared with MIP. This observation may be related to reduced occlusal contact density and potentially improved stability during buccal scanning procedures. Previous studies have suggested that dense occlusal contacts at MIP may increase susceptibility to mandibular displacement during digital interocclusal acquisition [11, 20, 21]. Additionally, increased interocclusal space has been reported to be associated with improved recording accuracy under specific conditions [21].

Nevertheless, the present findings suggest that the relationship between vertical separation and trueness may not be consistent when protrusive displacement is introduced simultaneously. Although vertical increase alone appeared to be associated with reduced deviations in certain conditions, combined protrusive–vertical positions demonstrated scanner-dependent variability. The significant interaction between scanner type and mandibular position (η²p = 0.250) indicates that positional effects may not be interpreted independently of the digital system used. This observation is consistent with previous reports suggesting that virtual interocclusal record accuracy may be influenced by multiple interacting variables, including scanner brand, alignment algorithms, interocclusal spacing, and software processing characteristics [22–26].

From a clinical perspective, inaccuracies in digital interocclusal records may influence the virtual mounting of digital casts and the resulting occlusal relationships. Even small deviations in bite registration may result in occlusal discrepancies and may require additional intraoral adjustments during treatment. The present findings demonstrate that both mandibular position and scanner selection affect the accuracy of digital interocclusal records. Therefore, these factors should be considered during digital bite registration to improve the reliability of virtual mounting and achieve more predictable clinical outcomes.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The in vitro design does not replicate intraoral factors such as saliva, soft tissue resilience, limited access, or patient movement, which may influence digital scanning performance. Additionally, only two intraoral scanners were evaluated, and mechanically standardized mandibular displacements may not fully represent biologic variability. Further in vivo investigations are needed to determine the clinical applicability of these findings.

Within the limitations of this study, mandibular position and scanner type were associated with variations in digital interocclusal recording performance. Recording accuracy under altered protrusive–vertical conditions may be influenced by the interaction between positional displacement and system-specific characteristics.

Conclusion

Within the limitations of this in vitro study, mandibular position and intraoral scanner type were associated with variations in the trueness and precision of digitally recorded maxillomandibular relationships. A statistically significant interaction between scanner type and mandibular position suggests that the effect of positional changes may vary depending on the digital system used. Increased vertical separation was generally associated with reduced trueness deviations compared with maximum intercuspal position; however, scanner-related differences remained across mandibular conditions. These findings suggest that digital interocclusal recording outcomes may be influenced by the combined effect of mandibular positioning and system-specific characteristics.

Clinical relevance

Mandibular position and intraoral scanner type may influence the accuracy of digital interocclusal records. Inaccuracies in digital bite registration may result in occlusal discrepancies and require additional intraoral adjustments. These factors should be considered when performing digital bite registrations, particularly under altered mandibular conditions.
