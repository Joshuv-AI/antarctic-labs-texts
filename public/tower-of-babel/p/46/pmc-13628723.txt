Models of care for palliative care in residential aged care facilities: a scoping review
Authors: Ishka de Silva, Catriona Parker, Peter Poon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs the number of older people living in residential aged care facilities continues to rise, it is essential that facilities can provide high quality palliative care. However, unmet palliative care needs in residential aged care facilities are leading to inadequate end-of-life care and causing poor outcomes for older people. Various models of care are being implemented to try and address this challenge. This review aims to synthesise existing models of palliative care delivery in the residential aged care sector, to understand how best to provide palliative care in this setting.MethodsA search of three databases (OVID Medline, Psych Info and CINAHL) between January 2009 to June 2024 found 1585 sources and 30 met the inclusion criteria of describing and/or evaluating a model of care for delivering palliative care services to people over the age of 65 years old in the residential aged care sector. Data was extracted related to model characteristics, evidence-basis, implementation factors, and evaluation outcomes.ResultsThe review identified 23 unique models of care. The models were categorised based on the principal service provider. Care was delivered either internally by the residential aged care facility (n = 14), or externally by community-based (n = 5) or hospital-based (n = 4) services. Models were developed using a range of methodologies, including based on literature, consensus-building techniques and establishing expert working groups. Services had been implemented in a range of different contexts, including metropolitan and rural settings and face-to-face or via telehealth. The evaluated models did not provide high-quality evidence of improved clinical outcomes.ConclusionThere are a range of evidence-based models of care that describe comprehensive palliative care service delivery in residential aged care facilities. They have been implemented in various contexts. No model has demonstrated a high quality of evidence that it improves outcomes. Future work is needed to know how best to provide palliative care to RACF residents. This future work should be grounded in implementation science.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12877-026-07915-8.Keywords: Residential aged care, Palliative care, Models of care, Scoping review

Background

It is becoming increasingly common for older people to live in residential aged care facilities (RACFs) towards the end of their life [1]. RACFs provide accommodation and care to people who can no longer live at home due to requiring ongoing support and assistance. More and more, RACFs are becoming the preferred place of death for their residents [2]. Palliative care is person centred care focused on improving quality of life for those with advanced disease and addresses the physical, emotional, spiritual and psychosocial distress of terminal illness [3]. Given the needs and preferences of RACF residents, it is paramount that RACFs are equipped to provide high quality palliative care. However, several inquiries have found that the aged care sector is not meeting the palliative needs of its population [4–7].

Unmet palliative needs in RACFs are causing poor outcomes for older people and the community [4–8]. The Australian Royal Commission into Aged Care found that the end-of-life care being provided by the sector was “severely lacking and fell well short of community expectations” [7]. It described fragmented care that depersonalises residents and does not prioritise quality of life and dignity [8]. Poor delivery of palliative care also contributes to preventable hospital presentations and admissions, which further reduces residents’ quality of life and creates additional costs for health systems [9, 10]. The barriers to providing effective palliative care in the sector include a lack of palliative care knowledge amongst staff, workforce pressures, and a lack of national consistency in service delivery [11]. These pressures on RACFs were exacerbated during the Covid-19 pandemic, leaving a strained system with staff and resource shortages [12]. The global ageing population means that the pressure on RACFs will continue to rise [1]. Additionally, RACF residents are living with more medical comorbidities than ever before [1]. These factors further demonstrate the need for practical and effective ways for RACFs to be able to provide timely and effective palliative care.

Previous reviews have explored strategies that could address the challenges in the sector. They identified strategies such as targeted education initiatives, multidisciplinary meetings, or increasing the use of tools such as advance care planning [13]. However, given one of the key identified factors contributing to poor outcomes is a fragmented system that lacks consistency and cohesion [11], there is an urgent need to examine how care can be holistically organised and coordinated to address this problem. Models of care describe how health services are structured and delivered to meet the needs of the patient journey [14]. They are grounded in best available evidence and consider the unique factors of specific local contexts [14]. They are thus a worthwhile way to explore addressing unmet palliative care needs in RACFs.

Hall et al [15] conducted a review of multicomponent palliative care service delivery in RACFs in 2011 and found a paucity of high-quality evidence. Since then, Kaasalainen et al [16] examined components of palliative care programs in the sector in 2019 and identified key components of a palliative approach to be training and capacity-building; resident, family and staff support; and advanced care planning. However, given the growing burden on RACFs over recent years, there is a pressing need for an up-to-date review of models for palliative care delivery in the residential aged care setting.

This scoping review aimed to understand existing international models of care that provide palliative care services to older people living in RACFs, with a focus on comprehensive and contemporary models. Additional aims included understanding how the models have been developed and implemented, and how their impact on outcomes has been evaluated. Understanding the development of models allows for an understanding of the foundation of the body of evidence. Model implementation demonstrates a level of feasibility, and the learnings from specific contexts are useful for medical administrators and policy makers in similar contexts. Evaluation data provides insight into the efficacy of the model to improve palliative care outcomes. Synthesising this evidence will support the aged care sector in understanding how best to meet the palliative care needs of older people living in RACFs.

Method

A scoping review was conducted to explore existing models of care for the provision of palliative care in RACFs. A scoping review methodology was chosen as it is the most appropriate methodology for exploratory mapping of evidence and identification of key concepts [17]. The review methodology was based on JBI guidance for scoping reviews [18] and was reported according to the PRISMA Extension for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) (https://osf.io/kzphx & supplementary material 1). As only published literature was included in this scoping review, ethical review was not needed.

Inclusion criteria

The scope of the review was developed using the Population, Concept, Context (PCC) framework. The population was adults 65 years and older, the concept was models of care that facilitate the delivery of palliative care, and the context was residential aged care facilities. This informed the eligibility criteria: studies were included if they focused on adults 65 years and older; described and/or evaluated a model of care; the model of care was for delivering palliative care services; and the setting was residential aged care facilities. Studies focused on other care settings such as acute care or personal homes and studies focused on adults under the age of 65 years were excluded. Only English language papers and studies published from 2009 onwards were included. Only original peer-reviewed articles were included; grey literature, conference abstracts, thesis, and reviews were excluded.

There are a variety of ways of defining a model of care [19]. This review defined a model of care according to the definition and guiding principles developed by the New South Wales Agency for Clinical Innovation (NSW ACI) [14]. The agency brings together leading clinicians, the consumer voice, and health services [14]. This collaborative approach ensures the perspectives of different stakeholders are considered and so is suited to the complexity of palliative care service delivery in aged care. Given the aim of this scoping review is to examine comprehensive and contemporary models of care, these guiding principles were most suited to define the parameters of the review and guide selection of sources. The guiding principles were converted into broad parameters that were used in the study selection process (Table 1). They were applied as broad parameters to support the identification of a range of models of care, in keeping with the aims of the scoping review.

Search strategy

A systematic search was conducted on June 30 2024 and included all published literature between January 1 2009 and June 30 2024 using OVID Medline, Psych Info and CINAHL electronic databases. An extensive combination of search terms was used to combine the three key search concepts of models of care, palliative care, and residential aged care (Table 2). It was considered important to obtain results from the past fifteen years to capture a significant period of results, including pre, during, and post pandemic. The end point of 2009 was chosen as this aligns with the development of the Australian National Palliative Care Strategy in 2010 which was a key milestone in strategic direction for the sector.

Study selection and analysis

The search results were downloaded and imported into Covidence. The study selection process is illustrated in the Prisma flow diagram (Fig. 1). Titles and abstracts were screened by co-authors IDS and CP using the eligibility criteria. There were 65 studies included for full text review. Points of uncertainty were discussed amongst the investigators to reach consensus. A data extraction form was used for all selected studies. It collected the study title, authors, year of publication, country of origin, a detailed description of the model, and the methodology for the development of the model.

PRIMSA flow chart of selection process

Extraction was an iterative process, and as key characteristics emerged, these were collected for all sources. Model characteristics extracted were service provider (ie hospital, community, RACF), type of service delivery (ie generalist or specialist palliative care), key staff and stage of progress (ie developed, implemented, evaluated). For models that had been implemented, and additional implementation extraction form was used that collected features of the implementation context including the number of sites and their location/s, whether service delivery was conducted face-to-face or virtually, and if the implementation period was impacted by the Covid-19 pandemic. For studies that included evaluation, data were extracted related to how the evaluation study was conducted, as well as the reported outcomes. This included the study design, study limitations, and magnitude of effect on outcomes. Using the JBI Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) tool [20], the information extracted from the evaluation studies was used to assess the quality of the evidence. These results were summarised in narrative, tabular and diagrammatic form.

Results

The database search yielded a total of 2478 articles, of which 893 were identified as duplicates and removed, leaving a total of 1585 unique sources (Fig. 1). Title and abstract screening removed 1525 articles, based on pre-specified inclusion criteria. Four additional sources were identified through hand searching, yielding 64 articles for full text review. This excluded 34 articles, leaving 30 articles included in the review.

Models of care

The identified publications describe 23 distinct models of care for the provision of palliative care services to residents over the age of 65 years old living in residential aged care facilities (Table 3). Most models (n = 19) have limited supporting evidence as they have only been described in one study. This limits the implications that can be drawn from these models. Some models (n = 3) have been examined in two related studies. However, only one model (n = 1), referred to as Needs Rounds, [21–26] has been investigated in a variety of contexts and study designs.

The models originate from seven countries: the United States of America (n = 7), Australia (n = 5), Canada (n = 3), the United Kingdom (n = 3), Hong Kong (n = 2), Spain (n = 2), and the Republic of Korea (n = 1).

The 23 identified models of care can be categorised into three groups based on the principal provider of the service (Fig. 2). They can be delivered internally by the RACF (n = 14), externally by a community service (n = 5) and externally by a local hospital service (n = 4). A summary of all models can be found in Table 3. Some models deliver a primary care service, suitable for a person with uncomplicated palliative care needs, and some models provide a specialist palliative care service, able to address complex palliative care needs. Primary care models generally use a general practitioner (GP), registered nurses and allied health staff to deliver services. Specialist care models tend to use health professionals who have complete further training in palliative care, including consultant palliative care physicians, clinical nurse consultants/specialists/nurse practitioners, and allied health staff with specialist qualifications in palliative care. Personal care workers are mentioned in some models, they support the work of nursing staff by providing personal care to residents, such as showering and dressing. Personal care workers are a major part of the aged care workforce.

Categories of models of care

The models have varied stages of investigation, for example, some have been developed and proposed as a potential model of care but never implemented (n = 5). Most of the models have been implemented, (n = 18). Twelve of the models have been evaluated (n = 12).

Models where the RACF is the provider (RACF provider model) build upon the internal capacity of RACFs. They either create standalone palliative care teams that are employed directly by the facility and are responsible for all aspects of care, or they use the capabilities of existing RACF staff and have pathways for external specialist support as needed.

The five community provider models deliver palliative care services through an external provider in the local community. Care was provided by facilities hiring a palliative care specialist on a consultation based contract [28, 45], or a specialist community team consulting and providing care to RACFs in a geographic region [21, 44].

Models delivered by hospital services use infrastructure and staff from a hospital in the same region as the RACF. Wong A and Moran [48] and Hui et al [46] advocate for a hospital facility that is specifically for RACF residents during their end of life. The facilities are described as sub-acute medical wards where patients can receive palliative care and are protected from transfer in and out of acute care settings [46, 48]. In contrast, Runacres et al [47] and Wong B et al [49] describe the implementation of a telehealth service providing RACF staff with expertise and advice from local hospital staff.

Development, implementation and evaluation

Models have been developed through various methodologies (Table 4). Most authors cited existing literature as the evidence-base for the development of their model (n = 6). Consensus-building techniques such as the Delphi method [32, 37, 42] and the and RAND Corporation/University of California Los Angeles Appropriateness Method (RAND/UCLA) [33] (n = 4). Similarly, others established working groups made up of subject matter experts and local representatives (n= 4) [39, 41, 44, 45], and a small number consulted relevant stakeholders (n= 2) [29, 40]. Kaasalainen et al [35] used mixed methods with quantitative and qualitative methods (n = 1) and Forbat et al [21] (n = 1) conducted a grounded theory ethnographic study. Wong and Moran [48] hypothesise a model based on economic feasibility (n = 1).

Out of the 23 models, 18 have been implemented to various extents (Table 5). Many (n = 7) have been embedded in one centre only. Most (n = 9) have been implemented at multiple (between 2 and 10) sites, whilst two (n = 2) have had large scale implementations over more than ten sites. Carpenter et al [27] describe operationalising the Nurse Practitioner Driven Palliative and Supportive Care Service at 22 sites across the USA, where all sites were a member of the same umbrella organisation. Smith R et al [41] report that the Specialist Palliative Care in Aged Care is active at over 400 sites across the state of Queensland (Australia) and supported centrally by the Queensland Department of Health and the hospital system. Three models [26, 47, 49] used a telehealth modality, all of which were in response to Covid-19 circumstances. A metropolitan setting was predominant (n = 14), with few (n = 3) exploring a rural context.

Evaluation measures can provide insight into the effectiveness of the model at improving clinical outcomes; these have been reported for ten of the models. This evidence was appraised using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) guidelines [50] (Table 6). Using a GRADE approach, randomised studies were initially considered to be high and non-randomised studies low, they were then up or down grounded based on domains such as study limitations and imprecision [50]. The outcome of this assessment is shown in Table 6. No studies were considered to have a high strength of evidence, one (n = 1) was found to have a moderate level of evidence, but most were found to have low (n = 4) or very low (n= 7). One robust randomised controlled trial reported no change in outcomes [43].

Discussion

This review identified 23 unique models that demonstrate ways to provide palliative care services to older people living in RACFs. A strength of the review findings is that there is a great amount of variety and depth to the development and implementation of these models of care, with them each having contrasting advantages and disadvantages. An advantage to models that rely on internal resources are that they include daily interactions with residents, and so can respond rapidly to new and changing palliative care needs [39, 41]. However, standalone teams are typically costly and so may have low financial viability [28]. Similarly, relying entirely on existing RACF staff can place further burden on a group of people who are typically already managing a high workload [43]. Education and upskilling of RACF staff is a key feature of this category. Community models can be a financially viable way to address palliative care needs in RACFs, however, require availability and adequate incentives for community staff to be effective [28]. Rainsford et al [25] describe the challenges in the availability of palliative care specialists for models like this and limited ways for remuneration under current funding structures. Models delivered by hospital services provide the support of specialist staff to RACFs. They may be able to reduce presentations to hospital. However, further studies are needed to evaluate their impact on resident outcomes.

The models that have been implemented have done so in a range of different contexts. This may be useful for medical administrators and policy makers; they can draw on models that have been developed and implemented in contexts like their own. For example, rural contexts can consider the use of Needs Rounds, [24, 25] RACFs with connection to local hospital networks could examine the Joint Virtual Round [47] or Long-Term Care Plus [49], while RACFs that wish to invest in internal resources can explore the ten models that are driven entirely by RACFs (Table 3).

Most of the models (74%, 17/23) are based in English speaking cultural contexts, either the USA, Australia, UK or Canada. Given the significant cultural differences and attitudes towards death and dying, there may be limited transferability of models to different cultural contexts. It is important to support future studies in a variety of cultural contexts.

A significant weakness of this body of evidence is the absence of high-quality evidence demonstrating that specific models of care improve outcomes for residents. This is generally due to study limitations, including a lack of randomisation, imprecise results, small sample sizes, incomplete data collection, and, in some cases, the lack of a control group. This finding is consistent with numerous reviews on the challenges of conducting research in palliative care and RACFs [51, 52]. Bouca-Machado et al [51] detail the methodological challenges of conducting research in palliative care including difficulties with data collection and achieving randomisation, as well as heterogeneity of clinical outcomes measured. Lam et al [52] present the challenges of research in RACFs, such as staff turnover, medicolegal challenges, and cost barriers. Whilst there are persistent methodological challenges in this study setting, ways to overcome this must be developed to establish the high-quality evidence that is needed to address the worldwide problem of unmet palliative care needs in RACFs.

Grounding future development, implementation and evaluation of models in formalised implementation science structures will likely be beneficial, for example, use of the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research [53]. The use of such frameworks allows for improved understanding of critical components influencing the uptake and implementation of new models of care, for example, understanding the local context, and relevant enablers and barriers to service delivery. It is also critical to have buy-in and foster relationships with key stakeholders for a new model of care to be successful [54]. Collaborative partnerships will need to be formed with researchers, clinicians and administrators. In particular, personal care workers are a key stakeholder group as they provide the majority of bed-side care and support to residents. All stakeholders, including community representatives could be engaged in co-design processes that bring together these groups so as the model of care is designed, implemented and evaluated by the very people tasked with bringing about change and those who it may serve in the future.

This review identified a variety of ways of providing education. These findings are relevant as there is a known need for palliative care education for RACF staff [55]. Reymond et al [39] included pre-implementation workshops for all clinical and non-clinical staff as part of the Residential Aged Care End-of-Life Care Pathway.The authors reported that the RACFs with the most successful implementation had the highest numbers of staff attendance at education workshops [39]. They were also the facilities that retained the same clinical leadership throughout the implementation period [39]. This experience can be contrasted with Improving palliative care through teamwork [42, 43], where a robust RCT found no statistically significant change in clinical outcomes following implementation. Temkin et al [43] outline challenges associated with implementing this model, including only six out of the fourteen RACFs successfully completing education within the implementation timeframe, and high levels of staff turnover in RACF clinical leadership positions contributing to poor uptake. Staff retention may be important for the positive effects of training to be realised, yet, high levels of staff turnover is a known workforce challenge for the sector globally [56],—thusly there is tension between the need for education and the transient nature of the aged care workforce.

A different approach to education is seen in Needs Rounds [23, 24] and the Strengthening a Palliative Approach in Long-Term Care [36], where case-based education is provided during multidisciplinary meetings. In both models, a specialist palliative care practitioner facilitated education and discussion with RACF staff [23, 24, 36]. This approach makes education continuous, as opposed to once off at the beginning of implementation. This may provide greater resistance to the effects of staff turnover. Case-based education embedded into a model of care may be useful to promote education sustainability [23, 24, 36].

It is worth noting that the Covid-19 pandemic placed enormous stress on RACFs [57]. This review found that it also accelerated the development of new approaches to palliative care service delivery. For example, Runacres et al [47] describe how existing technology was employed to commence the Joint Virtual Round in a matter of days. It allowed RACF staff to seek input from local hospital-based specialists and community nursing staff. Long-Term Care Plus [49] provided RACF staff with phone access to a range of hospital medical and nursing services, including 24/7 access to a palliative medical specialist. This model may be useful in preventing avoidable hospitalisations of aged care residents [9, 10]. Needs Rounds [24, 26] had been previously implemented through face-to-face delivery and were modified to facilitate virtual delivery. Importantly, Rainsford et al [24] and Samara et al [26] determined that telehealth implementation of the model was as effective as face-to-face. Models that were implemented during the pandemic, may provide innovative ways to provide care in geographically isolated and resource-poor settings. The demonstrated viability of the provision of care through phone and video calls may be useful in addressing challenges such as workforce shortages and skill gaps [56].

Strengths and limitations

This scoping review has several limitations. Firstly, a specific definition of a model of care was used, creating a clear focus for the review (Table 2). This excluded interventions that were not a comprehensive model of care, for example, education programmes. The aim of the review was to examine holistic models of care that can shape the future of service delivery. Finally, this review did not include the population of RACF residents that were under the age of 65 years old. Those of a younger age and living in RACFs are often vulnerable and may have a disability and limited capacity. This population should be considered in future research as their needs may be different to that of an older population.

Conclusion

There are a range of evidence-based models that employ different mechanisms to provide palliative care services to RACFs. Three categories for service delivery include care provided by the RACF itself, services provided by an external community provider, and palliative care services delivered by a local hospital. The evidence-base of the models include literature, consensus building techniques, and mixed methods studies. Models have been implemented in a range of contexts including face-to-face and virtual modality, at varying scale, in metropolitan and rural settings, and some were in response to the Covid-19 pandemic. It is recommended that organisational leaders and policy makers examine the identified models of care and consider what could be transferable to their local contexts. However, it is important to recognise that there is generally a low quality of evidence that current models improve palliative care outcomes. Future work is needed to know how best to provide palliative care to RACF residents. A deeper understanding of the enablers and barriers to effective service delivery may help to implement effective models of care. Consideration of different cultural perspectives should also be incorporated into future work. This future work should be grounded in implementation science as it may support more robust and rigorous studies.
