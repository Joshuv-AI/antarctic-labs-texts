Obesity and Metabolic Dysfunction‐Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD): A Literature Review on Pathophysiology and Treatment
Authors: Michael Romanos, Juan M Garcia Cordova, Jose Villamarin, Juan D Pazmino Zurita, Andres Acosta
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMetabolic dysfunction‐associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) represents a key hepatic manifestation of obesity and systemic metabolic dysfunction and is a leading cause of chronic liver disease worldwide. Obesity‐driven mechanisms, including adipose tissue dysfunction, insulin resistance and increased free fatty acid flux to the liver, promote ectopic lipid accumulation and lipotoxic injury. These processes trigger mitochondrial and endoplasmic reticulum stress, inflammation, immune activation and disturbances in the gut–liver axis, driving disease progression from simple steatosis to metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis, fibrosis and cirrhosis. MASLD is a highly heterogeneous condition, with differences in fat distribution, body composition and genetic susceptibility giving rise to distinct clinical phenotypes, including lean and non‐lean MASLD, which have important implications for disease risk, progression and therapeutic response. Clinical outcomes are determined primarily by fibrosis stage, which represents the strongest predictor of liver‐related and all‐cause mortality, underscoring the importance of early disease recognition and risk stratification. Therapeutic strategies, therefore, increasingly target upstream metabolic drivers rather than hepatic steatosis alone. Lifestyle interventions remain foundational but are often limited by challenges in long‐term adherence. Pharmacologic therapies, particularly incretin‐based agents such as glucagon‐like peptide‐1 receptor agonists and dual incretin agonists, as well as bariatric and endoscopic metabolic procedures, have demonstrated substantial metabolic and hepatic benefits. Recognition of disease heterogeneity supports phenotype‐guided approaches that integrate metabolic risk, adiposity, body composition and fibrosis assessment to personalise treatment strategies and improve long‐term outcomes across the MASLD spectrum.Keywords: fibrosis, incretin therapies, insulin resistance, MASLD, obesity, phenotype‐guided therapy

Introduction

Obesity is a chronic, relapsing disease with a rapidly increasing global prevalence, representing one of the most significant public health challenges of the modern era [1, 2]. Recently, the Global Burden of Disease projections estimate that more than 2.1 billion adults worldwide were living with overweight or obesity in 2021; by 2050, this number is projected to exceed 3.8 billion individuals, corresponding to more than half of the global adult population [2]. This increasing prevalence of obesity has profound systemic metabolic consequences and represents a major upstream driver of Metabolic dysfunction–associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), type 2 diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease and other obesity‐related complications [3].

MASLD is part of the updated steatotic liver disease nomenclature and represents a diagnostic framework that differs fundamentally from traditional nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) criteria [3]. Whereas NAFLD was historically defined by hepatic steatosis in the absence of significant alcohol intake or other secondary causes, MASLD is diagnosed when hepatic steatosis coexists with at least one cardiometabolic risk factor, including overweight or obesity, prediabetes or type 2 diabetes mellitus, hypertension or dyslipidemia [3]. Adopted in 2023, this revised nomenclature emphasises metabolic dysfunction as the central driver of disease pathogenesis rather than defining the condition by exclusion, thereby conceptualising MASLD as the hepatic manifestation of systemic metabolic disease rather than an isolated liver disorder [4].

MASLD is most prevalent among individuals with overweight or obesity; however, a considerable proportion of cases occur in lean populations, underscoring the heterogeneity of the disease [5]. Non‐lean MASLD is typically driven by adipose tissue dysfunction, insulin resistance and excess visceral fat accumulation, which together promote increased free fatty acid flux to the liver and hepatic lipid overload [6]. Lean MASLD is more commonly observed in certain Asian and other populations and is often associated with genetic susceptibility, sarcopenia and adverse fat distribution despite a normal body mass index and should not be considered a benign phenotype [7]. Across both lean and non‐lean phenotypes, visceral and ectopic fat deposition, rather than body mass index alone, appears to be the primary determinant of MASLD risk and disease severity [6]. For the purposes of this review, non‐lean MASLD refers to individuals with MASLD and a body mass index (BMI) ≥ 25 kg/m2 (or ≥ 23 kg/m2 in Asian populations), whereas lean MASLD describes individuals with MASLD and a BMI within the normal range (< 25 kg/m2 or < 23 kg/m2 in Asian populations) [5].

MASLD carries substantial clinical significance owing to its potential to progress along a histologic spectrum ranging from isolated steatosis to metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), advanced fibrosis, cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [8]. Among individuals with MASLD, fibrosis stage is the strongest predictor of liver‐related and all‐cause mortality, highlighting the importance of early disease recognition and risk stratification [4]. Importantly, among extrahepatic complications, cardiovascular disease represents the leading cause of mortality in patients with MASLD, exceeding liver‐related mortality and reinforcing the systemic nature of the disease [9].

From a public health perspective, MASLD represents an escalating global burden. It is already a leading cause of chronic liver disease and a growing indication for liver transplantation as obesity prevalence rises [10]. The economic burden of MASLD is substantial, driven by healthcare utilisation related to cirrhosis management, hepatocellular carcinoma surveillance, hospitalisations and transplantation, as well as indirect costs related to disability and lost productivity [11]. Consequently, understanding of the pathophysiologic mechanisms linking obesity to MASLD is essential [3]. Elucidating these mechanisms is critical not only for advancing disease understanding but also for informing phenotype‐ and mechanism‐based therapeutic strategies that address the upstream drivers of MASLD rather than its hepatic manifestations alone.

Currently, most MASLD reviews focus either on liver‐intrinsic mechanisms or on therapeutic developments in isolation, without integrating obesity‐related pathways, body composition and ectopic fat partitioning into a unified conceptual framework. Here, we reframe MASLD not as a purely liver‐centric or BMI‐driven condition, but as a downstream manifestation of altered fat distribution, adipose tissue dysfunction, muscle–liver crosstalk and inter‐organ metabolic signalling. This review synthesises obesity‐driven pathophysiological pathways underlying MASLD and their therapeutic implications, prioritising human clinical and translational evidence from the past decade, with a focus on mechanisms linking fat distribution, body composition and ectopic hepatic lipid deposition to clinical heterogeneity, fibrosis risk and treatment response. Together, this framework explains why disease risk and therapeutic response vary markedly across metabolic phenotypes, including lean MASLD.

Pathophysiology: How Obesity Promotes MASLD

Drivers of Positive Energy Balance and Weight Gain

Chronic positive energy imbalance, in which caloric intake persistently exceeds energy expenditure, underlies the development of obesity and promotes preferential expansion of visceral and ectopic fat depots, as shown in population‐based epidemiologic studies and imaging‐defined cohorts evaluating body fat distribution [6]. Central regulation of energy homeostasis is mediated by hypothalamic neuronal circuits, in which anorexigenic pro‐opiomelanocortin/cocaine‐ and amphetamine‐regulated transcript (POMC/CART) neurons promote satiety, while orexigenic neuropeptide Y/agouti‐related peptide (NPY/AgRP) neurons stimulate hunger, a framework established largely through mechanistic animal studies and supported by human neuroendocrine and genetic data. In obesity, genetic predisposition toward orexigenic signalling pathways, together with resistance to leptin and insulin signalling, disrupts this balance and results in sustained activation of orexigenic pathways and hyperphagia [12, 13].

Beyond homeostatic regulation, hedonic and reward‐related neural pathways further reinforce excess caloric intake, with dopaminergic signalling driving consumption of highly palatable foods beyond metabolic need, as shown in functional neuroimaging and controlled human feeding studies [14]. Gut–brain hormonal signalling also contributes substantially to appetite regulation. Postprandial hormones such as glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1), glucose‐dependent insulinotropic polypeptide (GIP) and peptide YY (PYY) promote satiety, whereas ghrelin stimulates hunger during fasting. In obesity, alterations in gut hormone responses are associated with impaired appetite regulation and reduced satiety, as observed in observational human cohorts and confirmed in interventional hormone infusion studies [15, 16]. Moreover, during caloric restriction, compensatory physiological adaptations reduce postprandial satiety hormone responses and increase hunger, a phenomenon consistently demonstrated in controlled weight‐loss interventions and metabolic ward studies [17].

In parallel, obesity is associated with impaired compensatory energy expenditure. Reductions in resting metabolic rate and adaptive thermogenesis limit the body's capacity to dissipate excess energy, thereby promoting weight regain following weight loss as documented in longitudinal human metabolic studies with repeated assessments of energy expenditure [18]. Together, increased energy intake and reduced energy expenditure establish the metabolic conditions that favour adipose tissue expansion and ectopic lipid deposition. Metabolic Dysfunction and Adipose Tissue Expansion

Hepatic steatosis in MASLD results from an imbalance between lipid input and lipid disposal within hepatocytes [19]. Stable isotope tracer studies in biopsy‐confirmed human MASLD cohorts demonstrate that intrahepatic triglycerides derive primarily from adipose tissue–derived free fatty acids, hepatic de novo lipogenesis and dietary fat [20]. Adipose‐derived free fatty acids represent the dominant source, accounting for ~50%–70% of hepatic triglycerides, a contribution amplified by systemic insulin resistance and impaired suppression of adipose tissue lipolysis, particularly from visceral depots [20, 21, 22, 23]. Hepatic de novo lipogenesis is the second major contributor, accounting for ~20%–30% of hepatic triglycerides and up to 35%–40% in individuals with type 2 diabetes, driven by chronic hyperinsulinemia and carbohydrate excess [24, 25, 26]. Dietary fat contributes a smaller fraction (~10%–15%) via chylomicron remnant uptake [20, 23]. The relative contribution of lipid sources varies by metabolic phenotype, with obesity‐associated MASLD dominated by adipose‐derived fatty acids and de novo lipogenesis, whereas lean MASLD often exhibits increased de novo lipogenesis and visceral adiposity despite normal body weight [24, 27].

As adipose tissue expands in obesity, its capacity to safely store excess energy becomes progressively compromised, leading to systemic metabolic dysfunction [6]. In this context, insulin‐resistant adipose tissue fails to appropriately suppress lipolysis, resulting in increased free fatty acid release and enhanced lipid flux to the liver, ultimately promoting hepatic lipid accumulation [28]. Adipocyte hypertrophy, local hypoxia and extracellular matrix deposition further limit adipose tissue expandability and favour lipid redistribution toward metabolically unfavourable visceral and ectopic depots [29].

In addition to altered lipid handling, obesity is characterised by dysregulated adipokine secretion, with reduced adiponectin and increased levels of leptin, tumour necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α) and interleukin‐6 (IL‐6). Observations from human studies summarised in mechanistic reviews show that these adipose‐derived signals act systemically to promote insulin resistance and drive hepatic inflammation and fibrogenesis, in part through activation of inflammatory signalling pathways and hepatic stellate cells [30, 31]. Concurrently, hepatic lipid handling becomes increasingly abnormal. Dysregulated hepatic lipid metabolism accelerates MASLD progression through increased de novo lipogenesis, impaired very‐low‐density lipoprotein (VLDL) assembly and export and accumulation of lipotoxic lipid intermediates. Together, these abnormalities establish a permissive metabolic environment characterised by hepatocellular lipid overload and cellular stress, which in turn drives the staged histologic progression of MASLD [32, 33, 34].

Mechanisms Driving Inflammation and Fibrogenesis

Under sustained metabolic stress, MASLD progression is driven by interrelated mechanisms linking lipotoxicity, cellular stress, inflammation and fibrogenesis, as demonstrated in human metabolic studies and complementary experimental models. An early and central event is hepatic lipotoxicity, in which excess free fatty acids and toxic lipid intermediates overwhelm mitochondrial β‐oxidation, increase reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation and activate pro‐inflammatory signalling pathways such as nuclear factor‐κB and c‐Jun N‐terminal kinase [35, 36].

Mitochondrial dysfunction subsequently becomes self‐perpetuating, with impaired oxidative phosphorylation, reduced ATP generation and accumulation of damaged mitochondria, as demonstrated in human studies of MASLD and MASH [37, 38]. These abnormalities amplify oxidative stress and hepatocyte injury and are further exacerbated by endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, which arises from nutrient overload and chronic activation of the unfolded protein response, promoting apoptosis and inflammatory signalling [36]. Together, mitochondrial and ER stress form a reinforcing loop that sustains cellular injury.

In parallel, obesity‐related alterations in the gut–liver axis contribute to hepatic inflammation. Intestinal dysbiosis and impaired gut barrier integrity facilitate translocation of microbial products into the portal circulation, activating Toll‐like receptor–mediated inflammatory pathways in hepatic immune cells [39, 40]. Disrupted bile acid signalling through receptors such as FXR and TGR5 further promotes hepatic inflammation and metabolic dysregulation [41, 42].

Persistent inflammatory signalling drives coordinated activation of innate and adaptive immune responses. Kupffer cells and recruited macrophages adopt a pro‐inflammatory phenotype, while adaptive immune cells contribute to hepatocyte injury and fibrogenic signalling, as demonstrated in human and experimental studies [43, 44, 45, 46]. Ultimately, chronic inflammation activates hepatic stellate cells, which differentiate into myofibroblasts and deposit extracellular matrix proteins, leading to fibrosis [45, 46]. Fibrogenesis may occur even in the absence of overt steatohepatitis, particularly in individuals with genetic susceptibility or coexisting metabolic disease such as type 2 diabetes [46, 47].

Collectively, these inflammatory and fibrogenic processes define a progressive disease trajectory, such that MASLD follows a continuum beginning with isolated hepatic steatosis, progressing to metabolic dysfunction–associated steatohepatitis (MASH), and potentially advancing to fibrosis, cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma (Figure 2) [35]. Simple steatosis is characterised predominantly by excess intrahepatic triglyceride accumulation and is strongly linked to obesity and metabolic dysfunction; at this stage, disease is generally reversible with sustained metabolic improvement through weight loss, dietary modification and increased physical activity [22, 48]. Progression to MASH reflects the emergence of hepatocellular injury and lobular inflammation, a stage that may remain reversible in the absence of advanced fibrosis when upstream metabolic drivers are effectively addressed [48, 49]. In contrast, once significant fibrosis develops and architectural remodelling advances toward cirrhosis, reversibility becomes progressively limited, with fibrosis regression often incomplete and cirrhosis largely irreversible outside of liver transplantation [50, 51]. From a clinical perspective, these observations emphasise that MASLD progression is driven less by hepatic fat content alone and more by sustained metabolic injury, adipose tissue dysfunction and inter‐organ signalling that perpetuate inflammation and fibrosis. Consequently, reductions in hepatic steatosis do not reliably translate into durable disease modification unless upstream metabolic drivers are concurrently addressed. Given that fibrosis stage is the strongest histologic predictor of liver‐related and all‐cause mortality, early identification of reversible disease stages and high‐risk phenotypes—particularly individuals with type 2 diabetes, sarcopenia or adverse fat distribution—is central to risk stratification, therapeutic escalation and long‐term disease monitoring in MASLD [19, 52].

Determinants of Fat Distribution and Phenotype‐Specific Risk

The risk and severity of MASLD are influenced by multiple factors that determine fat distribution and metabolic phenotype. Evidence from human genetic association studies indicates that genetic predisposition plays a foundational role, with variants such as PNPLA3 I148M, TM6SF2 E167K and MBOAT7 rs641738, which encode genes involved in hepatic lipid processing, consistently associated with increased hepatic fat accumulation and progression to steatohepatitis and fibrosis, independent of body mass index (BMI) [53, 54, 55]. Beyond liver‐specific variants, common obesity‐related polymorphisms, including MC4R and FTO, influence MASLD susceptibility indirectly by promoting visceral fat accumulation and insulin resistance rather than by directly altering hepatic lipid metabolism, as shown in population‐based genetic and metabolic studies [56, 57, 58].

Hormonal regulation represents another major determinant of disease expression, primarily by shaping adipose tissue distribution and systemic insulin sensitivity. Sex‐specific differences represent an important and clinically relevant dimension of MASLD epidemiology and risk stratification. Population‐based studies consistently show higher MASLD prevalence in men at younger ages, whereas women experience a marked increase in risk after menopause, resulting in narrowing of the sex difference later in life [10, 59, 60]. These age‐dependent patterns are thought to reflect hormonal influences on insulin sensitivity and body fat distribution, as premenopausal women typically exhibit relative subcutaneous fat predominance, whereas men demonstrate greater visceral adiposity, a phenotype more strongly associated with hepatic steatosis and metabolic dysfunction [59]. Following menopause, declining oestrogen levels are associated with increased visceral and ectopic fat accumulation, worsening insulin resistance and higher MASLD prevalence [60, 61]. In addition, sex‐related differences in skeletal muscle mass and function may further modify disease risk, as sarcopenia has been associated with MASLD severity and fibrosis independent of adiposity [62]. Collectively, these observations support integrating sex‐specific considerations into MASLD risk assessment and management. Moreover, observational studies demonstrate that postmenopausal women exhibit a higher prevalence of MASLD compared with premenopausal women, as declining oestrogen levels favour visceral fat expansion and ectopic hepatic lipid deposition [44]. Similarly, in reproductive‐age women, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), characterised by androgen excess and insulin resistance, is associated with increased hepatic steatosis and inflammatory activity independent of overall adiposity, as reported in clinical cohort studies [60, 61]. During puberty, sex hormone–driven shifts in adipose tissue distribution and transient insulin resistance may further influence long‐term metabolic trajectories, potentially predisposing susceptible individuals to later MASLD development, based on longitudinal metabolic and developmental studies [59, 63, 64].

Beyond hormonal influences, body composition and tissue functionality play a critical role in MASLD pathogenesis. Human population‐based studies indicate that loss of skeletal muscle mass and strength (sarcopenia) is associated with impaired peripheral glucose disposal and increased hepatic fat accumulation, even in individuals without obesity [62]. Indeed, skeletal muscle is the principal site of postprandial glucose disposal and a major metabolic sink for amino acids and lipids; it also functions as an endocrine organ that regulates hepatic metabolism through myokines and inter‐organ signalling [65, 66]. Sarcopenia and myosteatosis reduce peripheral substrate buffering capacity, increasing circulating glucose, free fatty acids and amino acid flux to the liver, thereby promoting hepatic de novo lipogenesis and ectopic lipid accumulation [67, 68]. Importantly, these effects may exacerbate MASLD severity independently of overall adiposity and not solely through systemic insulin resistance [69, 70]. Beyond impaired insulin signalling, altered muscle–liver crosstalk contributes to hepatic dysfunction through changes in muscle‐derived signalling molecules, including reduced secretion of insulin‐sensitising and anti‐inflammatory myokines and increased release of pro‐inflammatory mediators [65, 71]. These signals can directly impair hepatic mitochondrial function, promote oxidative stress and activate inflammatory and profibrotic pathways within the liver [72, 73]. Consequently, adverse muscle quality and reduced muscle mass may amplify hepatic vulnerability even in the absence of marked obesity [70, 74]. This framework helps explain why lean MASLD undermines traditional obesity‐driven models of disease. Individuals with lean MASLD may have normal or low BMI yet exhibit unfavourable body composition [74, 75]. In this context, total body weight fails to capture metabolic risk, and muscle dysfunction emerges as a critical determinant of hepatic steatosis severity, cardiometabolic complications and fibrosis progression independent of insulin resistance alone [70, 76].

Ethnicity and genetic ancestry add another layer of heterogeneity, as Hispanic ancestry exhibits higher hepatic triglyceride content compared with African or European ancestry, driven in part by differential frequencies of risk alleles such as PNPLA3, as demonstrated in large multi‐ethnic observational studies [77, 78]. Consistent with this, imaging and metabolic studies show that individuals with similar BMI can have markedly different degrees of hepatic steatosis and metabolic risk, underscoring that BMI alone does not capture differences in fat distribution or MASLD susceptibility [79, 80].

Finally, early‐life exposures and environmental modifiers exert durable effects on MASLD risk. Evidence from developmental and epidemiologic studies suggests that developmental programming resulting from intrauterine growth restriction, maternal malnutrition and disruptions in one‐carbon metabolism has been linked to epigenetic changes that increase susceptibility to hepatic steatosis later in life [81, 82]. In adulthood, chronic stress, circadian disruption, sleep deprivation and exposure to medications such as glucocorticoids or antipsychotics can promote visceral adiposity and insulin resistance, thereby worsening the metabolic abnormalities that drive MASLD, as supported by human observational and pharmacologic studies [83, 84, 85, 86].

Therapeutic Approaches

Given that MASLD represents the hepatic manifestation of systemic metabolic dysfunction, effective management strategies focus on addressing the upstream drivers of disease, including excess adiposity, insulin resistance and cardiometabolic risk. Therapeutic approaches therefore span lifestyle modification, pharmacologic interventions and procedural therapies, with increasing emphasis on phenotype‐guided strategies tailored to individual metabolic profiles.

Lifestyle Modification

Lifestyle intervention remains the cornerstone of MASLD management and is recommended for all patients, regardless of disease stage [87]. Randomised controlled trials and meta‐analyses demonstrate that modest weight loss yields clinically meaningful hepatic benefits, with ≥ 5% total body weight loss improving hepatic steatosis and ≥ 7%–10% weight loss typically required for improvement in necroinflammation and histologic activity scores [87, 88, 89]. Weight loss–dependent improvements in intrahepatic triglyceride content, insulin sensitivity, oxidative stress and adipose tissue biology have been consistently observed, as shown in controlled human intervention studies [90]. Despite proven efficacy, long‐term adherence to lifestyle interventions remains challenging, limiting the durability of benefit in real‐world settings [91].

Randomised dietary intervention studies have shown that dietary strategies emphasising caloric restriction and Mediterranean‐style dietary patterns improve hepatic steatosis and cardiometabolic risk factors, even independent of weight loss [89]. Physical activity further contributes to metabolic improvement, as both aerobic and resistance training reduce visceral adiposity and intrahepatic lipid content, including in the absence of significant weight reduction, based on human exercise intervention trials and are essential for preventing or mitigating sarcopenic obesity [92, 93, 94]. In addition, sleep disruption and circadian misalignment are associated with worsened insulin resistance and adverse metabolic outcomes, including increased adiposity, supporting the inclusion of sleep optimisation and behavioural interventions as important adjuncts to lifestyle therapy [95].

Pharmacologic Treatment

Pharmacologic therapy is indicated when lifestyle interventions alone are insufficient to achieve metabolic or hepatic improvement [91]. Among available agents, glucagon‐like peptide‐1 (GLP‐1) receptor agonists represent the most impactful pharmacologic advance in MASLD management to date [96]. In a Phase 3 trial, semaglutide 2.4 mg weekly achieved resolution of steatohepatitis without fibrosis worsening in 62.9% of patients compared with 34.3% with placebo [97] and was also associated with significant reductions in liver fat content and cardiometabolic risk factors in earlier trials [98].

Dual incretin agonism with tirzepatide has demonstrated substantial weight loss of ~15%–22%, accompanied by marked improvements in insulin sensitivity and hepatic steatosis [99, 100]. Imaging‐based analyses show pronounced reductions in liver fat fraction and aminotransferase levels, suggesting strong potential for MASH resolution, although definitive histology‐based outcome trials remain ongoing, based on MRI and biomarker‐based endpoints [101].

Other agents may provide complementary benefits in selected populations. Sodium–glucose cotransporter‐2 (SGLT2) inhibitors modestly reduce hepatic steatosis and aminotransferase levels while conferring cardiovascular and renal protection, particularly in patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus [102]. Pioglitazone improves steatohepatitis histology but is limited by weight gain and safety considerations, as established in biopsy‐based randomised controlled trials [87]. In addition, resmetirom, a thyroid hormone receptor‐β agonist, demonstrated significant reductions in liver fat, increased rates of MASH resolution and fibrosis improvement, and received regulatory approval in 2024 for MASH with fibrosis, based on phase 3 histology‐driven clinical trials [103].

Procedural Therapies

Evidence from large observational cohorts and interventional studies indicates that bariatric surgery provides the most robust and durable improvement in MASLD outcomes and remains the most effective intervention for sustained weight loss in patients with severe obesity [104]. Roux‐en‐Y gastric bypass and sleeve gastrectomy are associated with high rates of MASH resolution, fibrosis regression and reductions in long‐term cardiovascular and all‐cause mortality, with benefits largely driven by the magnitude and durability of weight loss achieved, as demonstrated in longitudinal surgical outcome studies [105].

For patients who are not candidates for surgery or prefer less invasive options, endoscopic bariatric therapies represent effective alternatives [106]. Intragastric balloons typically achieve 10%–15% total body weight loss and are associated with improvements in steatosis indices, liver stiffness and insulin resistance, as shown in prospective interventional studies [107]. Endoscopic sleeve gastroplasty produces sustained weight loss of ~15%–20% at 1 to 2 years and has been associated with improvements in hepatic steatosis and fibrosis markers, based on observational and comparative clinical studies [108, 109].

Phenotype‐Guided Therapy

Recognition of the marked heterogeneity of MASLD supports phenotype‐guided therapeutic strategies that account for differences in adiposity, metabolic dysfunction, body composition and genetic risk, as proposed in integrative pathophysiology frameworks and expert consensus [6]. In non‐lean MASLD, disease is predominantly driven by visceral adiposity and insulin resistance, and interventions that induce sustained weight loss and reduce ectopic fat—such as lifestyle modification and incretin‐based pharmacotherapy—consistently improve hepatic steatosis and inflammatory activity, as demonstrated in randomised trials and interventional studies [104, 110] In contrast, lean MASLD is more often associated with adverse fat distribution, sarcopenia and genetic susceptibility rather than excess total adiposity; in this phenotype, treatment strategies emphasise improving insulin sensitivity, preserving or augmenting skeletal muscle mass and targeting hepatic lipid handling rather than large absolute weight loss [111]. Across phenotypes, sarcopenia confers higher metabolic and fibrotic risk, supporting the incorporation of resistance training and nutritional optimisation as core components of therapy [93]. Emerging precision approaches targeting genetic modifiers of disease severity, including variants in PNPLA3 and HSD17B13, are in early‐phase clinical development and may further refine individualised treatment strategies, as supported by proof‐of‐concept human studies [112].

In obesity, phenotype‐guided therapy has been implemented by classifying patients according to key energy‐balance regulatory components, including gastric emptying, resting energy expenditure, calories to satiation and hedonic or emotional eating assessed using validated questionnaires; phenotype‐tailored dietary or pharmacologic interventions resulted in substantially greater weight loss than standard care [113, 114].

In MASLD, phenotype‐driven management similarly targets the dominant disease drivers for each patient [3, 6, 110](3–5). In non‐lean MASLD, characterised by visceral adiposity and insulin resistance, interventions prioritising sustained weight loss, through lifestyle modification and incretin‐based pharmacotherapy, consistently improve hepatic steatosis and inflammatory activity, with ≥ 7%–10% weight loss associated with steatohepatitis resolution and fibrosis regression [87, 97, 98] (6–8). In contrast, lean MASLD, often associated with adverse fat distribution, sarcopenia or genetic susceptibility, benefits more from strategies focused on improving insulin sensitivity, preserving or augmenting skeletal muscle mass and targeting hepatic lipid handling rather than large absolute weight loss [5, 68, 111]. Across phenotypes, addressing sarcopenia is critical, as low muscle mass amplifies metabolic and fibrotic risk [62]. Overall, aligning therapy with MASLD phenotype may enable more meaningful improvements in liver fat, inflammation and fibrosis than uniform treatment approaches [3, 6].

Additional Considerations

Population‐based epidemiologic studies demonstrate that comprehensive management of MASLD requires aggressive cardiometabolic risk reduction, as cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of mortality in patients with MASLD [9]. Optimal control of diabetes, dyslipidemia and hypertension, including statin therapy when indicated, is strongly recommended based on clinical practice guidelines and outcome data [91]. Alcohol moderation, vaccination against hepatitis A and B, and avoidance of hepatotoxic medications are essential hepatoprotective measures in holistic MASLD care, as outlined in expert consensus and guideline recommendations [27].

Discussion and Future Perspectives

Integration of Pathophysiology and Treatment

Collectively, the evidence synthesised in this review supports a shift from viewing MASLD as a linear consequence of obesity or hepatic steatosis towards a systems‐level disorder defined by obesity‐related fat partitioning, inter‐organ tissue crosstalk and phenotype‐specific vulnerability. The understanding of MASLD pathophysiology has greatly advanced, enabling the development of therapeutic strategies aimed at addressing the metabolic dysfunction and hepatic pathways driving the disease. Insulin resistance, adipose tissue dysfunction, hepatic fibrosis and gut–liver axis disturbances are key contributors to the progression of MASLD, as defined in integrative pathophysiology frameworks and consensus nomenclature statements. Consequently, therapies targeting insulin resistance, improving adipose function, modulating hepatic fibrosis and restoring gut–liver signalling have shown promise. However, effective treatment requires consideration of the interaction between metabolic and hepatic pathways, as interventions with predominant effects on hepatic steatosis reduction or body weight loss, in the absence of adequate modulation of underlying metabolic dysfunction, are unlikely to achieve durable disease modification, as emphasised in expert consensus and disease framework analyses [3, 115]. For instance, while weight loss has proven to improve hepatic steatosis, it may not sufficiently address the associated insulin resistance or hepatic fibrosis, which are critical components in the progression of MASLD to more advanced stages, as observed in clinical practice guidance and interventional studies [91, 98]. Thus, integrated therapeutic strategies targeting both metabolic dysfunction and hepatic damage are required for long‐term disease management and to prevent progression to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma.

Lean Versus Non‐Lean MASLD Implications

MASLD exhibits marked heterogeneity, with distinct phenotypic profiles influencing the approach to treatment. Non‐lean MASLD, predominantly characterised by excess visceral adiposity and insulin resistance, benefits most from weight loss interventions, which reduce visceral fat, improve insulin sensitivity and mitigate hepatic steatosis and inflammation, as supported by clinical practice guidelines and interventional studies [91]. Pharmacotherapies such as GLP‐1 receptor agonists, including semaglutide and tirzepatide, show substantial promise in inducing weight loss and improving metabolic and hepatic outcomes, as demonstrated in randomised controlled trials [97, 100]. On the other hand, lean MASLD, which often occurs in individuals with adverse fat distribution, sarcopenia and genetic susceptibility, requires a more nuanced approach. In these patients, management should emphasise metabolic optimisation and preservation of skeletal muscle through physical activity, with emerging antifibrotic strategies considered as disease advances, based on observational studies and emerging therapeutic concepts, as weight loss alone may be insufficient to fully mitigate disease progression [5, 68]. These insights highlight the need for phenotype‐specific management plans that address not only total adiposity but also fat distribution, muscle preservation and genetic risk.

Challenges in MASLD Management

Despite recent therapeutic advances, substantial challenges remain in the management of MASLD. Resmetirom became the first FDA‐approved therapy for MASH with fibrosis (F2–F3) in 2024, followed by semaglutide in 2025 for the same indication [97, 103]. While these approvals represent major progress, they primarily target select disease stages and metabolic phenotypes [97, 103]. No MASLD‐specific therapies have been validated across the full disease spectrum, including lean MASLD, early‐stage disease without fibrosis and MASLD in PNPLA3 or TM6SF2 carriers. Broader evidence across diverse phenotypes therefore remains a critical unmet need [47].

Additionally, long‐term adherence to lifestyle interventions remains challenging, as they often require substantial behavioural changes, particularly in terms of dietary modifications and physical activity, which are difficult to sustain in the long run, as observed in real‐world and longitudinal studies [91]. Furthermore, the variability in clinical trial endpoints, such as distinguishing between improvements in hepatic steatosis, steatohepatitis and fibrosis, complicates the evaluation and comparison of therapeutic interventions as highlighted in analyses of clinical trial design and outcome measures [97]. This lack of standardisation in endpoints hinders the development of universally applicable treatment guidelines and makes it challenging to assess the effectiveness of new therapies across different stages of disease.

Future Directions

The future of MASLD management will depend on translating advances in disease biology into clinically meaningful trial design and therapeutic strategies. A major unresolved challenge is the lack of standardised endpoint definitions across MASLD clinical trials. Current studies variably rely on reductions in hepatic steatosis measured by imaging (e.g., MRI–proton density fat fraction), histologic resolution of steatohepatitis, improvement in fibrosis stage or composite endpoints combining these measures, thereby limiting cross‐trial comparability and interpretation of treatment efficacy. Importantly, improvements in hepatic fat content alone do not consistently translate into reductions in inflammation, fibrosis or long‐term liver‐related outcomes. Given that fibrosis stage is the strongest predictor of liver‐related morbidity and mortality, harmonisation of trial endpoints around clinically meaningful histologic outcomes and validated non‐invasive fibrosis measures is essential [27, 116, 117].

Beyond endpoint standardisation, integration of multi‐omics approaches—including genomics, transcriptomics, proteomics and microbiome profiling—offers an opportunity to refine risk stratification and advance phenotype‐guided therapy. For example, carriage of the PNPLA3 I148M risk allele is associated with increased hepatic steatosis and accelerated fibrosis progression independent of adiposity, illustrating how genetic profiling can identify individuals at heightened risk despite similar degrees of hepatic fat accumulation [53, 54, 55]. Such approaches may enable enrichment of clinical trials and inform personalised treatment strategies based on underlying molecular drivers of disease.

Sustaining treatment benefits represents another critical challenge. Long‐term maintenance of weight loss and prevention of visceral fat regain are essential, as weight recidivism following treatment withdrawal is strongly associated with recurrence of metabolic dysfunction and disease activity [118]. Maintenance pharmacotherapy and structured behavioural interventions are therefore likely to play an increasingly important role in ensuring durability of treatment responses. Finally, continued identification of molecular targets involved in hepatic inflammation and fibrogenesis remains a priority. Therapeutic strategies targeting bile acid signalling through farnesoid X receptor agonism and pathways governing hepatic stellate cell activation underscore a shift toward fibrosis‐directed therapies aimed at halting disease progression and preventing complications such as cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma [35, 119].

Taken together, these developments highlight the need to move beyond weight‐ and steatosis‐centric models toward biologically informed, phenotype‐guided strategies that align therapeutic development, clinical trial design and patient care across the full MASLD spectrum.
