Manipulation of Oxygen Species on an Antimony-Modified Copper Surface to Tune the Product Selectivity in CO2 Electroreduction
Authors: Pengsong Li, Jiyuan Liu, Yong Wang, Xiang-Da Zhang, Yuqing Hou, Yichao Zhang, Xiaofu Sun, Xinchen Kang, Qinggong Zhu, Buxing Han
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Rational regulation of the electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) pathway to produce desired products is particularly interesting, yet designing economical and robust catalysts is crucial. Here, we report an antimony-modified copper (CuSb) catalyst capable of selectively producing both CO and multicarbon (C2+) products in the CO2RR. At a current density of 0.3 A/cm2, the faradaic efficiency (FE) of CO was as high as 98.2% with a potential of −0.6 V vs reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE). When the current density increased to 1.1 A/cm2 at −1.1 V vs RHE, the primary products shifted to C2+ compounds with a FE of 75.6%. Experimental and theoretical studies indicate that tuning the potential could manipulate the oxygen species on the CuSb surface, which determined the product selectivity in the CO2RR. At a more positive potential, the existence of oxygen species facilitates the potential-limiting step involving *COOH formation and reduces the adsorption of *CO intermediates, thereby promoting CO production. At a more negative potential, the localized high CO concentration coupled with the enhanced adsorption of *CO intermediates due to Sb incorporation facilitates C–C coupling and deep hydrogenation processes, resulting in an increased C2+ selectivity.

Introduction

The electrochemical CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) has garnered great attention due to its potential to produce valuable chemicals and fuels, and offers a promising avenue to simultaneously address environmental concerns and meet the energy storage requirements of renewable sources.1−4 However, the CO2RR involves complex processes with multiple proton and electron transfers, often resulting in sluggish reaction kinetics and diverse product distributions.5−7 Consequently, achieving efficient and selective CO2 conversion through rational regulation of the competitive pathways poses a series of substantial difficulties, necessitating the development of innovative catalysts capable of directing the reaction toward the desired products.8−11

Copper (Cu) is unique among metallic catalysts due to its moderate binding energy with key reaction intermediates, enabling the formation of both C1 and multicarbon (C2+) products.12−17 The compositional and structural evolution of Cu catalysts during the CO2RR has been extensively documented, revealing the crucial role of dynamic surface reconstruction in determining product selectivity.18,19 Recent studies have demonstrated that the surface structure and oxidation state of Cu-based catalysts can greatly influence the selectivity of the CO2RR products. For instance, the N-engineered Cu,20 surface Cl-bonded, low-coordinated Cs-modified Cu(200) nanocubes,21 ionomer planar heterojunction,22 Br-bridged dinuclear Cu(I) complexes,23 CuO-derived Cu,24 Cu-based metal–organic frameworks,25,26 and low-coordinated Cu27 have been demonstrated to exhibit enhanced selectivity toward C2+ products due to the stabilizing effect of key intermediates such as *CO, *CHO, and *CH2CO. Moreover, combining Cu with other metallic elements, such as Zn, Ni, Ag, Au, Sn, Pd, In, or Bi, has been shown to modify the electronic structure of Cu, resulting in enhanced selectivity for certain products.28−32 These bimetallic systems often exhibit synergistic effects, whereby the secondary metal can either modify the local reaction environment or enhance the stability of the reactive intermediate to favor the formation of desired products. For example, CuAg catalysts were found to selectively produce C2+ products with high faradaic efficiency (FE),33 which is attributed to the modified electronic properties and enhanced *CO dimerization on the Cu surface. Similarly, bimetallic CuSb catalysts have shown to selectively produce CO or CH4,34−37 benefiting from the unique electronic interactions between Cu and Sb. These studies indicate that tuning the product selectivity in the CO2RR requires precise control over reaction pathways and reaction kinetics. Nevertheless, controlling the CO2RR pathway to obtain the desired products is challenging due to the simultaneous and competing process of desorption, hydrogenation, and coupling of intermediates during the reaction, resulting in low product selectivity.28,38,39 If the competitive pathways could be precisely regulated, then the controllability of the CO2RR as well as the economics of the entire electrolyzer system could be greatly improved.

Herein, we found that manipulation of oxygen species and Sb incorporation on the surface of a Cu catalyst could tune the CO and C2+ products selectivity in the CO2RR. At a current density of 0.3 A/cm2, the FE of CO was as high as 98.2% with a low potential of −0.6 V vs reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE). As the current density increased from 0.3 to 1.1 A/cm2, the FE of CO gradually decreased and the FE of C2+ products increased. When the current density reached 1.1 A/cm2 at −1.1 V vs RHE, the predominant product was C2+ compounds with a FE of 75.6%. In situ experimental studies and theoretical calculations suggest that the modulation of surface oxygen species on the CuSb catalysts through potential regulation is responsible for the tuning of selectivity of the CO and C2+ products. At a more positive potential, the existence of oxygen species weakened the adsorption capacity of Cu active sites to the *CO intermediates, promoting the generation of CO products. At a more negative potential, the adsorption of the *CO intermediate is enhanced by Sb incorporation, and the high local CO concentration promotes C–C coupling toward high C2+ selectivity.

Results and Discussion

The CuSb catalysts were synthesized via a straightforward one-step coreduction of Cu2+ and Sb3+ at room temperature using NH3·BH3 as the reductant. To prepare CuSb catalysts with different compositions, various Cu2+/Sb3+ feed ratios were employed, in which the Cu2+ amount was kept constant at 2 mmol, and the amount of Sb3+ was adjusted to 0, 0.025, 0.05, and 0.075 mmol. The resulting catalysts were denoted as Cu, CuSb-1, CuSb-2, and CuSb-3, respectively. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images show that the as-prepared catalysts exhibit a typical porous network structure, which was formed through the self-assembly of metal nanoparticles (Figures S1, S2, and 1a). High-resolution TEM (HRTEM) images depicted a lattice spacing of 0.21 nm for both Cu and CuSb catalysts, corresponding to the (111) plane distance of face-centered cubic metallic Cu (Figures S3 and 1b).40 In addition, energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping analysis (Figure 1c) affirmed the uniform dispersion of Cu and Sb species in the CuSb-2. The concentrations of Sb in CuSb-1, CuSb-2, and CuSb-3 were determined by inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectroscopy (ICP-OES) with values of 2.1, 4.5, and 6.2 wt %, respectively (Table S1). In addition, Figures S4 and S5 reveal that Cu, CuSb-1, CuSb-2, and CuSb-3 had similar pore structures with close Brunauer–Emmett–Teller (BET) surface areas of 45, 44, 41, and 39 m2/g, respectively.

The X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns of Cu and CuSb catalysts are presented in Figure S6. As shown in Figure S6a, peaks observed at 43°, 50°, and 74° can be attributed to the (111), (200), and (220) crystalline planes of metal Cu (JCPDS:PDF#04–0836), which confirms that the matrix of CuSb catalysts is predominantly metallic Cu. The emergence of Cu2O in the XRD pattern is ascribed to surface oxidation upon exposure to air. Notably, no discernible signals corresponding to Sb metal or oxide diffraction peaks were observed in the patterns of CuSb-1, CuSb-2, and CuSb-3, ruling out the formation of Sb nanoparticles. Figures 1d and S6b further show that the 111-diffraction peak gradually shifts to a smaller 2θ value with the increase in the concentration of Sb in the catalyst. This shift is attributed to the doping effect of Sb atoms, due to that the atomic radius of Cu (128 pm) is smaller than that of Sb (138 pm). Moreover, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) measurements were conducted to provide insight into the chemical states and compositions of the catalysts. The high-resolution XPS spectra of Cu LM2 and Sb 3d3/2 shown in Figure S7 confirmed the presence of Cu0, Cu+, Sb0, and Sb3+ chemical states in the CuSb-2 catalyst.41−43 Notably, the binding energy of the Cu0 LM2 peak in CuSb-2 exhibited a positive shift of 0.12 eV with respect to Cu (Figure 1e), suggesting that the electrons are transferred from the Cu atoms to the surrounding Sb atoms due to their differences in electronegativity. Furthermore, the Cu+ LM2 peak in CuSb-2 was not as pronounced as that in pure Cu, indicating that the introduction of Sb atoms into CuSb-2 effectively inhibits the oxidation of Cu.

The electronic and coordination structures of the CuSb catalysts were further analyzed using the X-ray adsorption fine spectroscopy measurement. The Cu K-edge X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) (Figure 1f) and the first derivative of XANES spectra (Figure S8a) clearly show that the Cu K edge position of CuSb-2 (8979.3 eV) is higher than that of pure Cu (8979.1 eV), further demonstrating the successful incorporation of Sb atoms. Additional calculations (Figure S8b) indicate that the average valence state of Cu in CuSb-2 is +0.2. Analysis of the Fourier-transform extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) spectrum (Figure 1g) shows that both Cu and CuSb-2 have a local structure featuring metal–metal (Cu–Cu/Cu-Sb) bonds, similar to the case of Cu foil. However, the intensity of the metal–metal bond in CuSb-2 is lower than that of pure Cu, suggesting a decreased coordination number for the Cu atoms in CuSb-2. These results indicate that Sb doping could change the electronic structure of Cu and increase the defective structure in CuSb-2, facilitating the CO2RR process.

The CO2RR performance of the catalysts was investigated in a three-electrode flow cell reactor (Figure S9) using 1 M KOH as the electrolyte. Both Cu and CuSb catalysts exhibit good dispersion in acetone (Figure S10), and they were drop-coated on a gas diffusion electrode to prepare the working electrodes. The linear-sweep voltammetry (LSV) curves were obtained over Cu and CuSb-2 catalysts by feeding CO2 or Ar through the gas channel. As depicted in Figure S11, the CuSb-2 catalyst demonstrated a lower onset potential and superior current density responses under a CO2 flow compared to those under an Ar flow, indicating the reduction of CO2. Furthermore, the current density of the CuSb-2 catalyst under a CO2 flow exceeded that of Cu catalyst at the same potential, which indicates that the doping of Sb in the CuSb-2 catalyst was kinetically favorable for the CO2RR. To compare the intrinsic electrocatalytic activity of the catalysts, the electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) of the catalysts was determined using electrochemical double-layer capacitance (Cdl) measurements.44 Cyclic voltammetry (CV) curves of pure Cu and CuSb-2 catalysts were recorded in the non-Faradaic region at various scan rates (Figures S12a and S12b). The linear slopes in Figure S12c show that the Cdl value of CuSb-2 is higher than that of the Cu catalyst. Normalizing the current density based on the ECSA (Figure S13) further emphasizes that CuSb-2 has a higher normalized current density compared to that of the Cu catalyst. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS, Figure S14) was then conducted to investigate the charge transport kinetics of the catalysts. The results revealed a lower charge transfer resistance (Rct) on CuSb-2 compared to that on pure Cu, suggesting that CuSb-2 facilitates an increase in the reaction rate. The above results suggest that the doping of Sb resulted in more active sites, which is conducive to the enhancement of intrinsic electrocatalytic activity.

The distribution of the CO2RR products for the prepared catalysts was investigated by conducting electrolysis of CO2 at different applied current densities ranging from 0.1 to 1.3 A/cm2. Gaseous products were quantified by gas chromatography (GC), and liquid products were analyzed by using 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H NMR). The current density-dependent FE values of different CO2RR products obtained with Cu and CuSb catalysts are illustrated in Figures 2a–c, S15, and S16. We found that the FE of CO gradually decreased with increasing current density, while the selectivity of C2+ products simultaneously increased over all of the CuSb catalysts. Particularly, across the entire working current density range from 0.1 to 1.3 A/cm2, the CuSb-2 catalyst could achieve CO2 conversion to both CO and C2+ products with high FE, while the H2 evolution reaction was suppressed compared with that of the pure Cu catalyst (Figures 2c, S17, and S18). At a current density of 0.3 A/cm2 with a low potential of −0.6 V vs RHE, the FE of CO over the CuSb-2 catalyst was as high as 98.2%, significantly higher than that of the pure Cu catalyst (47.7%). As the current density increased from 0.3 to 1.1 A/cm2, the FE of CO gradually decreased and the FE of C2+ products increased. When the current density reached 1.1 A/cm2 at −1.1 V vs RHE, the FE of C2+ products could reach 75.6%, whereas it was only 46.8% for the pure Cu catalyst. The C2+/CO ratios could be adjusted from 0/1 to 5/1 over the CuSb-2 catalyst by varying the current densities (Figure 2c). Under the optimal conditions, the highest partial current density of CO and C2+ products on the CuSb-2 catalyst reached 0.43 and 0.85 A/cm2, respectively (Figure 2d). Systematic comparisons with state-of-the-art Cu-based electrocatalysts demonstrate that the FE and partial current density for CO or C2+ products achieved by the CuSb-2 catalyst were very high, ranking among the top performers in the Cu bimetallic-based systems (Tables S2 and S3).

After electrochemical testing at current densities of 0.3 and 1.1 A/cm2, we observed a decrease in the contact angle of the electrodes. However, the contact angles of the Cu and CuSb-2 electrodes remained very close after electrolysis at the same current density (Figure S19). Moreover, no significant flooding was observed on the backside of the electrodes after electrolysis at current densities of 0.3 and 1.1 A/cm2, respectively (Figure S20). These results, taken together, indicate that the Cu and CuSb-2 catalytic electrodes have similar three-phase interfaces under the same operating conditions. We have also conducted numerical simulations of the gas phase CO2 concentration in the gas channel at different current densities over Cu and CuSb-2 electrodes (Figures S21 and S22). It showed that the partial pressure of CO2 on the surface of both Cu and CuSb-2 gas diffusion electrodes decreases at higher current densities. However, the CO2 concentration differs between Cu and CuSb-2 electrodes at a current density of 1.1 A/cm2 (Figures S21b and S22b). It suggests that the intrinsic activities of catalysts for converting CO2 to CO also affect the partial pressure of CO2 at high current densities, thus determining the FE of C2+ products. Furthermore, the CuSb-2 catalyst shows significantly improved selectivity for CO and C2+ products compared to the pure Cu catalyst at both low and high current densities (Figures 2c and S17). We speculate that this improvement is not only related to the applied current density but also closely linked to the structural characteristics and surface properties of the catalyst itself.

The long-term stability experiment (Figure 2e) of the CuSb-2 catalyst was performed at current densities of 0.3 and 1.1 A/cm2. Both the potentials and the FE of CO or C2+ products remained consistently stable over 10 h. In addition, the crystal structure (Figure S23) and morphology (Figure S24) of the CuSb-2 catalyst remained unaltered after 10 h of continuous CO2 electrolysis. XPS analysis and TEM-EDS mapping further indicated that the elemental valence states and element distribution of the CuSb-2 catalyst were well-preserved during the electrolysis (Figures S25 and S26).

To get insight into the electrocatalytic activity of the CuSb-2 catalyst, in situ characterization techniques were employed. Operando X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) was utilized to investigate the structural and valence state changes in Cu elements under the reaction conditions. During the operando XAS measurement, the potential was systematically reduced from the open-circuit voltage (OCV) to −0.5, −0.7, −0.9, and −1.1 V vs RHE. Figure 3a,b reveals that the main peaks corresponding to metallic Cu were observed at negative potentials, affirming that Cu in the CuSb-2 catalyst remained in the metallic phase throughout the CO2 electroreduction process. This suggests that the local structure and electronic state of Cu, as the main species in CuSb-2, remained stable throughout the reaction process. We then conducted in situ Raman spectroscopy measurements to identify the possible reaction intermediates on the Cu and CuSb-2 catalysts using a custom-designed Raman setup. Raman spectra were collected during the CO2RR process at various applied potentials ranging from −0.5 to −1.1 V vs RHE, as shown in Figure 3c,d. Notably, at potentials of −0.5 and −0.6 V vs RHE, a distinct Raman peak at 705 cm–1 emerged on the CuSb-2 catalyst, which was ascribed to the adsorption of preliminary intermediates *CO2–.45 This suggests that the activation of CO2 occurred more readily on CuSb-2 compared to Cu. Furthermore, the vibrational mode of surface oxygen-adsorbed species at 615 cm–1 was noted in a more positive potential range (from −0.5 to −0.8 V) with the CuSb-2 catalyst (forming the CuSb/Oad surface at a more positive potential),46−49 which may be linked to the elevated selectivity of CO in the more positive potential range. The surface oxygen-adsorbed species originated from the surface oxidation of the prepared catalyst when exposed to air (Figure S7). These surface oxides are resistant to full reduction at more positive potentials but can be completely reduced at more negative potentials over the CuSb catalysts. The band located at 365 cm–1 corresponding to the restricted rotation of *CO stretching50,51 was observed on both Cu and CuSb-2. At the potentials of −1.0 and −1.1 V vs RHE, this *CO stretching band vanished over Cu but remained discernible over CuSb-2, indicating that the *CO intermediate has a stronger adsorption on the CuSb-2 catalyst in a more negative potential range.52 A rational interpretation of this result is that the heightened adsorption of CO at high potentials leads to increased coverage of *CO intermediates on the catalyst surface, facilitating C–C coupling reactions and augmenting the selectivity for C2+ products.

To substantiate this hypothesis, density functional theory (DFT) calculations were performed. We constructed the correlative structural models, including Cu, Sb-doped Cu with oxygen adsorption (representing the CuSb/O surface at a more positive potential), and Sb-doped Cu (representing the CuSb surface at a more negative potential) (Figure S27), based on the aforementioned experimental results. Simulations were carried out on the CO2 and CO adsorption over the Cu, CuSb, and CuSb/O structures. The adsorption configurations of *CO2 and *CO on Cu (111), CuSb (111), and CuSb/O (111) surfaces are illustrated in Figure S28. The calculated results show that the adsorption free energy values (Figure 3e) of *CO2 on CuSb and CuSb/O are −0.21 and −0.23 eV, respectively, which are lower than that on the Cu surface (−0.18 eV). This indicates a greater propensity for CO2 adsorption and activation on CuSb and CuSb/O surfaces compared with that on the pure Cu surface. Regarding *CO, the adsorption free energy on CuSb/O is −0.05 eV, which is higher than that on the Cu surface (−0.15 eV). The adsorption of *CO on the CuSb surface is −0.17 eV, which is stronger than that on the Cu surface. This implies that the *CO intermediate can readily desorb to form CO on the CuSb/O surface, whereas the C–C coupling could be promoted on the CuSb surface due to the strong adsorption of *CO. To further confirm the role of oxygen species in promoting CO production at low current densities, we conducted alternating experiments by switching the current density between 0.3 and 1.1 A/cm2. Figure 3f shows that when the current density was switched back from 1.1 to 0.3 A/cm2, the FE of CO decreased from 98% to 61%, accompanied by the formation of C2+ products (25%). This suggests that when the current density was switched back from 1.1 to 0.3 A/cm2, the absence of oxygen species on the CuSb surface enhances the adsorption of *CO intermediates, resulting in the appearance of C2+ products at a low current density. As shown in Figure S29, CuSb-2 without surface oxygen species that was obtained after 1.1 A/cm2 electrolysis (CuSb-2-after electrolysis) was used as the electrode, and its catalytic performance was compared with that of the Cu electrode. As the current density increased, the FE of CO decreased while the FE of C2+ products increased, which shows a similar tendency to that of the Cu catalyst (Figures 2c and S17). Thus, the above results demonstrate that the product selectivity is tuned by both potentials and surface oxygen species of the CuSb-2 catalyst.

We further elucidated the mechanism underlying the regulation of the CO and C2+ products in the CO2RR over the CuSb-2 catalyst through theoretical calculations. Simulation of the reaction pathway from CO2 to CO was performed on the Cu, CuSb, and CuSb/O structures. As shown in Figure 4a, the largest Gibbs free energy difference (corresponding to the hydrogenation of the CO2 molecule to form *COOH) was identified as the potential-limiting step for the formation of CO for all three catalysts. At the potential of −0.6 V vs RHE, the calculated free energies for the formation of *COOH from CO2 were 0.90 eV on the Cu surface, 0.88 eV on the CuSb surface, and notably lower at 0.75 eV on the CuSb/O surface. This implies that the formation of oxygen adsorbates at more positive potential contributes to a decrease in the formation energy of *COOH, which facilitates the generation of CO. To further assess the impact of applied potential on the reaction energy, we calculated the free energies under more negative potential conditions with U set to −1.1 V vs RHE (Figure 4b). Upon introducing an applied potential of −1.1 V vs RHE, the overall reaction energies for the CO pathway decreased significantly. The formation energy of *COOH from CO2 over CuSb (0.38 eV) remained lower than that over Cu (0.40 eV), indicating that CuSb exhibits a preference for CO production at a more negative potential. Furthermore, the free energy of the potential-limiting step over CuSb at the potential of −1.1 V vs RHE was about half that at −0.6 V (Figure S30), favoring CO generation and enhancing the coverage of *CO on the catalyst surface, thus facilitating C–C coupling for the production of C2+ products. This indicates that CO serves as an intermediate in the CO2-to-C2+ process, and the coverage of *CO directly affects the subsequent C–C coupling process. To further verify this conclusion, the CORR was carried out in the same flow cell reactor using the CuSb-2 catalyst. The C2+ products were detected at larger current densities, with the FE reaching up to 90% at the current density of 1.1 A/cm2 (Figure 4c,d). The observed activity in the electroreduction of CO suggests that the catalytic process for the reduction of CO2 to C2+ products at a more negative potential on CuSb-2 follows a sequential pathway, wherein CO2 is initially reduced to CO through a two-electron process, which then undergoes further reduction to C2+ products via C–C coupling and deep hydrogenation processes.

The above results, taken together, suggest that the tuning of C1 and C2 pathways in the CO2RR over the CuSb-2 catalyst comes from the modulation of surface oxygen species on CuSb by regulating the potential, as shown in Figure 4e. When the CuSb-2 catalyst was operated in the CO-producing mode (at more positive potential), the presence of oxygen species weakened the adsorption capacity of Cu active sites for *CO intermediates, leading to the enhancement of CO selectivity. Upon switching to the C2+-producing mode (at a more negative potential), the oxygen species are removed from the CuSb surface, and the doping of Sb enhances the adsorption of *CO intermediates for C–C coupling, resulting in high C2+ selectivity.

Conclusions

In summary, the CuSb catalysts, prepared via a facile one-step coreduction method, exhibit remarkable electrocatalytic CO2 activity in producing both CO and C2+ products. At a current density of 0.3 A/cm2 with a low potential of −0.6 V vs RHE, CO was the dominant product and the FE of CO over the CuSb-2 catalyst was as high as 98.2%. When the current density reached 1.1 A/cm2 at −1.1 V vs RHE, the predominant product shifted to C2+ products and the FE was up to 75.6%. Under the optimal conditions, the highest partial current density of CO and C2+ products on the CuSb-2 catalyst reached 0.43 and 0.85 A/cm2, respectively. This exceptional electrocatalytic performance of the catalysts results from the modulation of oxygen species on the CuSb surface by regulating the potential. At a more positive potential, the surface oxygen species on CuSb-2 is pivotal, as it not only diminishes the adsorption capability of *CO intermediates but also lowers the reaction free energy for the formation of *COOH, promoting the generation of CO. At a more negative potential, Sb doping enhances the adsorption of *CO intermediates and the localized CO concentration on the electrode surface and facilitates C–C coupling and deep hydrogenation processes, thereby elevating C2+ selectivity. This work contributes to an in-depth understanding of catalyst design principles and reaction mechanisms toward different products in the CO2RR, facilitating the rational design of new catalysts for efficient CO2 electrolysis.
