Integrated metabolomics and transcriptomics reveal sugar metabolism associated with cold tolerance in chrysanthemum
Authors: Zhaoqin Kou, Yanhong Zhang, Di Wu, Qiuying Pang, Yuhua Li, Yang Zhang, Yanqiang Gao, Le Guan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe ground-grown chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum morifolium) is an important ornamental species valued for its stress resilience, particularly in cold regions. However, low temperature stress remains a major constraint on its growth and landscape utility. Understanding the molecular mechanism of its cold tolerance is crucial for breeding cold-resistant cultivars.ResultsWe performed metabolomic and transcriptomic analyses on the three chrysanthemum cultivars before and after exposure to the low temperature. Among the 1,371 detected metabolites, a subset of 21 sugar-related differential accumulation metabolites (DAMs) greatly responded to the low temperature. Under the low temperature, the chrysanthemum cultivars ZR and FSD activated metabolic defense mechanisms via the significant accumulation of key osmoprotectants, such as sucrose, trehalose, and raffinose, etc. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) revealed two key modules, red and tan, which were significantly correlated with sugar-related metabolic changes. KEGG enrichment analysis established starch and sucrose metabolism as a central pathway in the cold response. Further transcriptional analysis suggested that transcription factors CmWRKY65 and CmLBD18 potentially integrated cold signaling with this pathway by directly regulating key biosynthesis genes. Additionally, we identified three putative SWEET genes, CHR00080209, CHR00064977, and CHR00048779, which may be involved in sucrose transport into vacuoles and could potentially contribute to mitigating cold-induced damage.ConclusionsThese findings delineate the role of sugar metabolism in the low‑temperature adaptation of Chrysanthemum morifolium and identify pivotal genetic resources for breeding cultivars with enhanced cold resilience. The results provide a mechanistic basis for prolonging the autumnal floral display period, thereby supporting the sustainable application of ground‑cover chrysanthemum in the late‑season landscapes of Northeast China.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09006-7.Keywords: Chrysanthemum, Cold stress, Metabolomics, Sugar metabolism

Introduction

The ground-grown chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum morifolium) represents a valuable germplasm resource in ornamental horticulture, distinguished by a set of agronomically favorable traits [1], including the protracted blooming duration, high floral density, the diverse color spectrum, and considerable stress tolerance [2]. These characteristics have established chrysanthemum as a robust and low-maintenance groundcover, which enhances landscape biodiversity and aesthetics in challenging environments, especially in Northeast China, where the temperature is extremely low. In this context, ground-cover chrysanthemum serves as one of the few vital autumn-flowering species capable of providing landscape coloration during the late fall when most other ornamental plants have senesced due to frost. However, despite its crucial role in extending the visual quality of urban green spaces in high-latitude regions, the physiological and molecular mechanisms underlying the low temperature tolerance of ground-cover chrysanthemum remain poorly characterized. Given that natural low-temperature acclimation in the field encompasses both chilling (0–15 °C) and freezing (< 0 °C) regimes [3], understanding these adaptive responses is essential not only for prolonging the ornamental display period and floret longevity but also for advancing the cultivation strategies that support landscape sustainability in Northeast China.

Soluble sugars and sugar alcohols play a fundamental role in mediating cold adaptation. Soluble sugars, comprising mono-, di-, and oligosaccharides, alleviate cold-induced disruptions via osmotic adjustment, membrane stabilization, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging [4]. For instance, plants often accumulate sucrose to mitigate cold damage [5, 6]. Recent studies have revealed that brassinosteroid (BR) signaling enhances cold tolerance by slowing the sucrose degradation through upregulating the transcription factor PpBZR1, which suppresses the expression of sucrose-degrading enzyme gene PpVIN2 [7]. Trehalose metabolism also significantly contributes to cold tolerance [8]. Arabidopsis thaliana expressing a bifunctional TPS-TPP gene (trehalose 6-phosphate synthase and trehalose 6-phosphate phosphatase) exhibits cold tolerance without growth inhibition, highlighting its importance in stress adaptation [9]. Similarly, fructans act as osmotic adjustment and ROS scavengers in response to drought and salinity [10], suggesting their potential contributions to cold stress.

Sugar alcohols (polyols) represent another critical class of compatible solutes with protective functions against abiotic stresses. Mannitol not only contributes to the osmotic adjustment, but also scavenges the hydroxyl radical (•OH) to protect the crucial enzymes such as phosphoribulokinase from oxidative inactivation—a vital mechanism against drought, salinity, and other oxidative stresses [11]. In Plantago major, sorbitol accumulation increases under potassium-sufficient conditions, thereby improving photosynthetic efficiency and supporting rapid cold acclimation by reducing water loss and oxidative damage [12]. Under the low temperature, sorbitol helps with alleviating excess electron pressure in photosystems by serving as an electron sink to regenerate NADP⁺ from NADPH [13]. Additionally, sorbitol’s multiple hydroxyl groups contribute to its capacity to quench ROS, similar to raffinose and fructans [10, 14].

Moreover, stresses trigger extensive metabolic reprogramming, leading to the accumulation of numerous intermediates. These intermediates are subsequently utilized as substrates by broad-specificity enzymes, thereby generating novel compounds [15]. For instance, glucose 1-phosphate participates in starch biosynthesis within potato tubers [16], while glucose 6-phosphate, fructose 6-phosphate, and 3-phosphoglyceric acid are central intermediates in glycolysis [17]. Furthermore, sucrose 6-phosphate serves as a key precursor regulating sucrose synthesis in plants [18]. Additionally, raffinose biosynthesis utilizes galactosylinositol as the precursor [19]. In addition, some intermediates can act as stress-adaptive signals, for example, elevated trehalose 6-phosphate under abiotic stress promotes daytime starch accumulation while suppressing nocturnal starch degradation, ensuring sustained energy supply [20, 21].

To cope with the cold stress, the central molecular regulatory mechanism involves the CBF/DREB1-COR pathway, where C-repeat binding factors (CBF) bind to CRT/DRE motifs in the COLD-REGULATED (COR) gene promoter. This could induce the biosynthesis of cryoprotective proteins, including LEA and AFP, as well as osmolytes such as soluble sugars [22, 23]. Low temperature significantly enhances soluble sugar accumulation via coordinated regulation of sugar metabolism and compartmentation [24]. In apple, low temperature induces the expression of MdCBF1/2 gene, and thus MdCBF1/2 transcription factor directly binds to the CRT/DRE cis-elements in the promoter region of tonoplast sugar transporter genes MdTST1/2 to activate their transcription. This activation increases sugar sequestration into vacuoles, which is a key mechanism for osmotic adjustment and cryoprotection [25]. Concurrently, low temperature upregulates the expression of genes encoding amylase, such as β-amylase (MdBAM3), which accelerates the conversion of the starch to soluble sugars [26, 27]. Such metabolic reprogramming sustains cytoplasmic sugar homeostasis while facilitating cryoprotectant accumulation within the vacuole to enhance cold tolerance. Similar elevated soluble sugar levels occur in strawberry, kiwifruit, and citrus under chilling stress [28, 29].

In recent years, an increasing number of studies have investigated the cold tolerance of chrysanthemum. A study on three chrysanthemum cultivars reported significantly elevated levels of soluble sugars under low temperature [30]. Beyond metabolite changes, transcriptional and epigenetic regulators have been shown to play critical roles in the cold response of chrysanthemum. For instance, the interaction between DgbZIP3 and DgbZIP2 enhances cold tolerance [31], while the histone deacetylase DgHDA6 and the sirtuin family protein DgSRT2 improve cold tolerance by increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavenging capacity [32, 33]. Despite these physiological and bioinformatics insights into the relationship between cold tolerance and sugar metabolism in chrysanthemum, how specific sugar metabolic pathways are precisely regulated at the molecular level to confer cold tolerance remains unexplored.

In order to elucidate the pivotal function and underlying molecular mechanism of the sugar metabolism in conferring cold tolerance, three chrysanthemum cultivars were selected as plant materials. Exposure to cold stress typically elicits a suite of conspicuous and easily quantifiable damage symptoms in petal tissues, such as tissue browning, wilting, and the deterioration of floral pigmentation [34]. Owing to the visibility and measurable nature of these stress-induced phenotypes, petals constitute a highly sensitive and informative indicator organ for the evaluation of cold tolerance in this species. In this study, metabolomic and transcriptomic profiling was integrated to delineate the dynamic reprogramming of sugar metabolism in chrysanthemum petals under naturally occurring low temperatures. Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was subsequently employed to construct a regulatory network and identify key nodes governing sugar metabolic shifts during cold acclimation. The findings not only elucidate the molecular basis of low temperature adaptation in chrysanthemum but also provide actionable targets for molecular breeding aimed at extending the ornamental display period. This work offers a scientific foundation for enhancing the sustainability of autumnal landscape coloration in Northeast China and other cold regions.

Materials and methods

Plant material

Chrysanthemum cultivars (Chrysanthemum morifolium, a ground-cover Chrysanthemum cultivar) “Xuehaibaifan” (XHBF), “Fenshuangdie” (FSD), and “Zirong” (ZR) were propagated at Northeast Forestry University’s Life Sciences College Flower Research Institute, Harbin, China (126◦63’ E, 45◦71’ N). As previously mentioned, ambient temperature and relative humidity at the sampling site were monitored continuously at 100 min intervals from September 15th to October 24th, 2022 [30]. On September 15th, the diurnal temperature ranged from a minimum of 15.0 °C to a maximum of 22.9 °C, with a daily mean of 18.6 °C. Petals collected on this date were designated as the control group (CK). Following subsequent exposure to naturally declining temperatures, additional petal samples were harvested on October 14th and October 24th to constitute the low‑temperature treatment group (LT). On October 14th, the minimum, maximum, and mean daily temperatures were 3.3 °C, 14.9 °C, and 9.0 °C, respectively, whereas on October 24 the corresponding values were − 4.0 °C, 25.9 °C, and 6.0 °C. For each sampling date, three biological replicates were collected. Each biological replicate consisted of petals pooled from three randomly selected individual plants. In subsequent analyses, the samples from October 14 and October 24 were combined into a single LT group and compared with the CK group (September 15). The developmental stages S5 and S6 were determined based on morphological characteristics as previously described [35]. For both CK and LT sampling, only petals from flowers at exactly S5 and S6 stages were collected and pooled, respectively, ensuring that the observed differences were primarily attributed to temperature rather than developmental variation. All samples were immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for further analysis.

Metabolite extraction and LC‑MS analysis

The metabolomic profiling and cDNA library sequencing were conducted by Metware Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). Briefly, lyophilized petal samples were pulverized cryogenically (30 Hz, 1.5 min), and 100 mg powders were extracted with 1.2 mL of 70% methanol. After overnight incubation (4 °C; periodic vortexing) and centrifugation (10,000×g, 10 min), the supernatants were filtered (0.22 μm) and analyzed on a Q Exactive HF-X mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled to a UHPLC system. Chromatographic separation employed a Waters Acquity C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 2.8 μm, 40 °C) with mobile phases of 0.04% aqueous acetic acid (A) and acetonitrile (B) at 0.35 mL/min, using gradient elution: 5% B (0 min) → 95% B (10 min) → 5% B (11–14 min). MS acquisition parameters: MS1 resolution 35,000, MS/MS resolution 17,500, scan range *m/z* 84–1250, stepped collision energies (30, 40, 50 eV), dynamic exclusion 3 s. Metabolite identification required accurate mass (≤ 5 ppm), MS/MS spectral matching against an in-house library, and retention time alignment (± 0.2 min). Quantification employed isotope-labeled internal standards (CV ≤ 15%). A pooled QC sample was injected every ten injections, exhibiting Pearson correlation ≥ 0.95 and tight PCA clustering. Across biological replicates, ≥ 80% of metabolites displayed CV ≤ 30%, consistent with established LC-MS metabolomics QC guidelines [36].

RNA extraction, library preparation, and sequencing

Total RNA was extracted using the NEBNext® UltaTMmRNA Library Prep Kit (Epicentre) [30]. Poly(A)+ mRNA enrichment via oligo(dT) beads preceded fragmentation and double-stranded cDNA synthesis. Following end repair/adapter ligation (A-Tailing Mix/RNA Index Adapters), amplified cDNA underwent Ampure XP bead purification, resuspension in EB buffer, and quality assessment (Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer). Single-stranded circular DNA (ssCir DNA) libraries were generated through PCR product circularization mediated by splint oligonucleotides before sequencing. Sequencing was performed on the DNBSEQ platform employing combinatorial Probe-Anchor Synthesis (cPAS) technology to generate 150 bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were quality-filtered using fastp to remove adapter sequences and low-quality reads. Clean reads were aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and transcript abundance was quantified as FPKM. Differential gene expression analysis was performed with DESeq2, using an adjusted p-value < 0.05 and |log₂FC ≥ 1 as significance thresholds. RNA-seq reads were aligned to the Chrysanthemum morifolium reference genome [35].

WGCNA

Weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) was performed using the WGCNA package in R (version 1.72) to identify modules of co-expressed genes associated with cold tolerance. The analysis was conducted across all samples from all three cultivars together to capture common co-expression patterns. Genes with low expression were filtered using the IQR-based method (varFilter function in genefilter package), retaining 31,288 genes for subsequent analysis. To construct a scale-free network, the soft-thresholding power β was selected using the pickSoftThreshold function. The lowest power at which the scale-free topology fit index R² reached its maximum value was β = 18, which was used for subsequent network construction.

Module eigengenes were calculated for each module. Module-trait relationships were assessed using Pearson’s correlation coefficient between module eigengenes and cold tolerance traits. Modules with |Pearson correlation coefficient| ≥ 0.5 and Benjamini‑Hochberg adjusted p-value < 0.05 were considered significantly associated with cold tolerance and were selected as key modules for further analysis. Co-expression networks were visualized using Cytoscape (version 3.10.2).

Statistical and bioinformatic analysis

Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using R’s prcomp function on unit-variance-scaled data. Metabolites were mapped to the KEGG pathways based on the KEGG Compound database annotations. Enriched pathways underwent metabolite set enrichment analysis (MSEA) with significance assessed using hypergeometric test p-values. For two-group comparisons, differential metabolites required VIP > 1, FDR < 0.05 (Student’s t-test), and |log₂FC| ≥ 1.0; multi-group analyses used an FDR < 0.05 cutoff (ANOVA). Subcellular localization of SWEET proteins was predicted on the website (https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/).

Protein sequence alignment and phylogenetic analysis

Evolutionary relationships were inferred using the Neighbor-Joining algorithm [37] in MEGA11 [38]. A consensus topology (1000 bootstrap replicates) [39] depicted taxon history, collapsing branches with < 50% support. Bootstrap percentages indicated clade stability. Thirty-five amino acid sequences were aligned, excluding sites with > 50% gaps/missing data (215 final positions).

Integrated analysis of starch, sucrose, and raffinose metabolism under the low temperature

We found that 10 of these 21 sugar-related metabolites were closely associated with the starch and sucrose metabolism pathways, as well as 5 of the metabolites related to the raffinose biosynthesis pathway. To investigate the low-temperature regulatory mechanisms via sugar metabolism, integrated pathway diagrams were constructed for starch/sucrose metabolism (Fig. 6a) and raffinose biosynthesis (Fig. 6b), showing the expression levels of related differentially expressed genes (DEGs).

Transcript levels of BAM genes (CHR00028611, CHR00085443, CHR00093697, CHR00080305, novel. 11853, novel. 35398), involving with starch hydrolysis to maltose, were markedly upregulated across all cultivars under LT (Fig. 6a), which was consitent with the increment of maltose (Fig. 3). In contrast, the expression of AGP, encoding the enzyme responsible for starch hydrolysis to glucose 1-phosphate, was slightly downregulated in all three cultivars under LT (Fig. 6a), which was opposite to the accumulation of glucose 1-phosphate (Fig. 4). Concurrently, key genes in sucrose biosynthesis were significantly upregulated under LT, indicating transcriptional activation of the sucrose synthesis pathway. Specifically, the expression of SUS gene (CHR00029284, CHR00093597, CHR00079911, CHR00081420, CHR00078809, novel. 18281) increased markedly in all cultivars, consistent with the enhanced sucrose accumulation. Similarly, the expression of SPS (CHR00088351, CHR00028407, CHR00027650) was significantly elevated (Fig. 6a), indicating transcriptional activation of the sucrose synthesis pathway.

Subsequently, sucrose could be further converted to glucose and fructose by the INV (Invertase). The transcriptional level of INV (CHR00014087, CHR00048380, CHR00032575) was significantly downregulated, while its inhibitor INH (CHR00074273) was upregulated under LT (Fig. 6a), which was further in agreement with the decreased level of fructose and glucose, and the increased accumulation of sucrose (Fig. 3). Additionally, sucrose can also be catalyzed by SUS to produce UDP-glucose, which subsequently contributes to trehalose biosynthesis. In the process of trehalose biosynthesis, the expression levels of the two key genes TPS (CHR00068671) and TPP (CHR00040435) were significantly upregulated under LT (Fig. 6a). This was in line with the increased accumulation of trehalose and trehalose 6-phosphate (Figs. 3 and 4).

In the raffinose biosynthesis pathway, low temperature generally downregulated the expression of four GAL genes in ZR and FSD, whereas it upregulated their expression in XHBF (Fig. 6b). Conversely, the expression of RS genes (CHR00039572 and CHR00057872) were enhanced in ZR and FSD but reduced in XHBF (Fig. 6b). These expression patterns were consistent with the observed decrease in the precursors, including galactose, inositol, and galactinol, and the concomitant increase in the accumulation of raffinose and stachyose in ZR and FSD (Figs. 3 and 4).

Phylogenetic analysis and correlation networks of SWEET

In plants, soluble sugars primarily accumulate in vacuoles to help with osmoregulation and cryoprotection, contributing to stress resistance. Their transport into vacuoles was facilitated by sugar transporters, including SWEET proteins. We identified 28 SWEET members across the three chrysanthemum cultivars via the transcriptome data, nine of which exhibited trace expression. Therefore, the amino acid sequences of the remaining 19 SWEET members were used to construct a maximum likelihood phylogenetic tree along with the SWEET homologs in Arabidopsis thaliana. Furthermore, the differential expression patterns of the SWEET transporters, represented by fold-change values under LT versus CK, were visualized in the heatmap to reveal their temperature-responsive regulatory patterns (Fig. 7a).

The phylogenetic analysis revealed that CHR00049726 and CHR00080209 shared a monophyletic clade with AtSWEET16 and AtSWEET17, CHR00064977 shared a monophyletic clade with AtSWEET15. The close clustering of CHR00048779 with homologs suggests a common ancestral origin with AtSWEET9. CHR00061684, CHR00017632, CHR00017627, CHR00064977, CHR00078870, and CHR00013960 exhibited significant cold-inducible upregulation across all three cultivars (Fig. 7a). As predicted by the website https://wolfpsort.hgc.jp/ (Supplementary Table S1), the subcellular localization of CHR00080209 was situated in the vacuole, which was consistent with the vacuolar localization of AtSWEET16 and AtSWEET17. In addition, CHR00017632 and CHR00017627 were also predicted to be localized in the vacuole among the SWEET members in chrysanthemum. The shared presence and high conservation of the defining domain architecture—specifically, the “sugar efflux transporter for intercellular exchange” family signature (as identified by InterPro)—among the protein pairs AtSWEET16/17 and CHR00080209, AtSWEET15 and CHR00064977, and AtSWEET9 and CHR00048779 provide strong structural evidence supporting their relationships (Fig. 7b). Furthermore, the multiple sequence alignment not only reinforced this structural conservation but also suggested their high conservation at the amino acid level (Supplementary Fig. S1, Fig. S2, Fig. S3). Subsequently, a correlation analysis was conducted between the 18 SWEET genes and the three substrates (r > 0.6). Correlation analysis revealed that CHR0078870 and CHR0048779 showed a significant positive correlation with sucrose, while a significant negative correlation with glucose (Fig. 7c).

WGCNA uncovers the key module underlying cold tolerance

To further understand the regulatory mechanism of saccharide changes in response to the low temperature, the WGCNA was conducted on the 31,288 identified non-redundant genes to determine the key modules and genes. A total of 17 modules were identified (Fig. 8a). Furthermore, the previous 21 sugar-related metabolites were used as traits to explore their correlations with the 17 modules to unravel the transcriptional network governing their accumulation under the low temperature. The correlation heatmap of the module-trait analysis indicated that the gene expression patterns in the red and tan modules mirrored the dynamics of the associated traits. Consequently, these two modules were selected for further analysis (Fig. 8b). KEGG pathway enrichment analysis suggested that “starch and sucrose metabolism” was significantly enriched in the red module, comprising 50 genes and accounting for 4.95% (Fig. 8c). In the tan module, “starch and sucrose metabolism” included 21 genes, accounting 5.16% of the module (Supplementary Fig. S4). This finding offers strong transcriptional evidence that substantiates our original hypothesis, indicating that the majority of metabolites depicted in the initial metabolite transformation diagram are predominantly involved in the starch and sucrose metabolism pathway. Given the high abundance of sugar-associated genes and the significant representation of the red module in the enrichment analysis, the transcription factors within this module were further investigated.

Regulatory network of transcription factors and sugar metabolism genes in the red module

A total of 164 transcription factors (TFs) were identified within the red module. The NAC and WRKY families were the most abundant, each comprising 14 genes, followed by the AP2/ERF-ERF family with 13 genes (Fig. 9a). Families containing fewer than 6 TFs are listed in the Supplementary Table (Supplementary Table S2). Hierarchical clustering analysis based on the expression profiles of these 164 TFs distinctly separated all samples into CK and LT. TFs expression generally increased under LT, with the most pronounced increase observed in FSD (Fig. 9b). Additionally, 12 structural genes associated with sugar metabolism pathways were identified in this module. Subsequently, the gene co-expression network was constructed incorporating these TFs, all 12 structural genes, and the three reported TFs (MaNAC1, HSFB1, AcbHLH2) homologs implicated in cold resistance (Fig. 9c). The top 10% of genes in the co-expression network ranked by degree value were selected for further analysis, yielding 15 key genes, which were ten structural genes (TPS1, TPS11, SUS-1 (CHR00029284), SUS-2 (novel.9223), SUS2-1 (CHR00078809), SUS2-2 (CHR00079911), BGLU, SPS1, CesA, CesA1), two identified TFs, CmLBD18 (CHR00045520) and CmWRKY65 (CHR00070111), and three unknown TFs (Supplementary Table S3).

CmWRKY65 (CHR00070111) also forms a significant co-expression relationship with multiple structural genes in this network. CmLBD18 and CmWRKY65 were closely related to the identical structure genes, including 9 genes (TPS1, TPS11, SUS-1, SUS-2, SUS2-1, SUS2-2, BGLU, CesA, CesA1). Interestingly, SPS1 and TPS1 were exclusively associated with CHR00082780, which was identified as a member of the Jumonji family.

Discussion

The XHBF cultivar exhibits a flavonoid-enriched metabolic profile under the low temperature

Previously, metabolite profiling revealed that XHBF exhibited a distinct cluster of upregulated metabolites in the heatmap (Fig. 1a), suggesting a cultivar-specific adaptive mechanism. Among the 218 metabolites uniquely upregulated in XHBF (Supplementary Table S4), 53 were identified as flavonoids, representing the most abundant category (23.85%). This result implies that XHBF may rely more heavily on flavonoid-mediated pathways to mitigate low-temperature stress.

Accumulation and regulation of maltose and sucrose metabolism under the low temperature

The coordinated upregulation of starch degradation and sucrose biosynthesis underscores a conserved cold-adaptive strategy in plants. Maltose served dual roles in response to freezing stress: as a compatible solute stabilizing cellular structures, and as a carbon source ensuring continuous energy flux while preventing toxic hexose accumulation [43–45]. The congruent transcriptional and metabolic shifts evident in the cold-tolerant chrysanthemum cultivars (ZR and FSD) imply that Chrysanthemum morifolium similarly relies on this ancestral BAM3-mediated pathway to maintain carbon homeostasis under chilling conditions [43, 46].

Sucrose accumulation significantly enhanced plant cold tolerance through multifaceted mechanisms. It functioned as an osmoprotectant to maintain cellular osmotic balance and membrane integrity [47, 48]. Sucrose also mitigated oxidative damage by scavenging ROS, upregulating the expression of antioxidant genes (including UGLT73B4), and preserving photosynthetic efficiency [47, 48]. Furthermore, sucrose provided essential substrates for energy metabolism and facilitated post-stress recovery [49]. Notably, our findings reveal that chrysanthemum employs a dual “source augmentation and sink restriction” regulatory strategy, characterized by the simultaneous transcriptional activation of sucrose biosynthesis and the antagonistic interplay between invertase (INV) and its inhibitor (INH). For instance, in peach, the vacuolar invertase PpVIN2 was post-translationally inhibited by PpINH1, resulting in reduced sucrose hydrolysis during cold storage and alleviating chilling injury; overexpression of PpINH1 further confirmed these results [50]. Similar INV/INH regulatory interactions modulating sucrose levels and cold tolerance had been documented in tomato and other species [51, 52]. Reduced PpINV activity elevated cellular sucrose accumulation, stabilized membrane integrity, mitigated oxidative damage, and thus improved cold tolerance [7]. This conserved mechanism suggests that the suppression of sucrose cleavage may exert a particularly decisive influence on freezing tolerance in chrysanthemum, a notion further supported by the activation of the raffinose pathway in INV-suppressed transgenic potatoes [53].

Accumulation of other sugars and their potential protective roles under the low temperature

Raffinose and stachyose, both belonging to the raffinose family oligosaccharides (RFOs), were galactosyl extensions of sucrose and were widespread in the plant kingdom [54]. A direct link between low temperature and RFO metabolism had been established in cucumber, where the expression of raffinose synthase (CsRS), its enzymatic activity, and the subsequent contents of raffinose and stachyose were progressively increased in leaves, fruits, stems, and roots under cold stress [19]. The congruent accumulation of RFOs in the cold-tolerant chrysanthemum cultivars ZR and FSD corroborates this pattern, suggesting a conserved role for RFOs in chilling adaptation across diverse taxa. Furthermore, independent research in transgenic rice demonstrated that overexpression of two cold-inducible wheat GolS genes (TaGolS1 and TaGolS2) led to enhanced accumulation of galactinol and raffinose, which was concomitant with improved chilling stress tolerance [55]. Given the rapid depletion of the precursor galactinol observed in our metabolomic data, the net accumulation of raffinose and stachyose implies that a highly efficient, cold-activated raffinose synthase (RS) activity—rather than mere substrate availability—likely drives RFO biosynthesis in chrysanthemum under low temperature.

Our findings indicated that nystose and laminarin accumulation were significantly enhanced under the low temperature, which was consistent with the previous reports (Fig. 5). In Oryza sativa, nystose has been demonstrated to enhance chilling tolerance in the primary roots. Investigations had shown that roots developed from nystose-treated seeds exhibited a reduced browning rate, elevated root activity, and accelerated root growth under cold stress [56]. In Arabidopsis thaliana, it had been demonstrated that laminarin application enhanced thermotolerance and resilience to salt stress. This protective effect was mediated through multifaceted mechanisms involving the upregulation of heat shock proteins, the enhancement of the chloroplast antioxidant system via improved metal ion homeostasis, and the activation of the ethylene signaling pathway [57]. This concordance suggested that these sugars might also play a pivotal role in conferring cold tolerance in chrysanthemums.

Differential expression of SWEET transporters and their correlation with sugar levels

Phylogenetic analysis revealed that several SWEET proteins in our chrysanthemum transcriptome data were homologous to AtSWEET9, 15, 16, and 17. Notably, AtSWEET9, 15, and 16 were implicated in sucrose transport during nectar secretion and seed development [41, 42], while the tonoplast-localized transporters AtSWEET16 and AtSWEET17 were crucial for freezing tolerance by modulating sugar efflux from vacuoles [58, 59]. In alignment with our results, the identification of CHR00080209 and CHR00064977, which clustered with AtSWEET16 and AtSWEET15, respectively, leads us to hypothesize that these transporters may participate in regulating sucrose homeostasis during cold stress, although direct functional evidence is currently lacking. This was supported by significant upregulation of these SWEET genes in all the three chrysanthemum cultivars under the low temperature conditions (Fig. 7a). The transport of sucrose could enhance osmoprotection and stabilize cellular membranes during freezing, which aligned with findings that the overexpression of AtSWEET17 increased cytosolic fructose levels, thereby improving survival rate under cold stress [40]. Moreover, the significant positive correlation observed between CHR0078870 and CHR0048779 with sucrose raises the possibility that these SWEET proteins contribute to the cold-responsive accumulation of this disaccharide (Fig. 7c). It is plausible that these transporters facilitate vacuolar sucrose sequestration or modulate its efflux under stress, thereby contributing to osmotic adjustment and cryoprotection. Conversely, the negative correlation with glucose presents an interesting pattern that merits further investigation, as it may reflect preferential transport activity or indirect regulatory relationships. Furthermore, the cold-induced expression patterns of these transporters might indicate their involvement in complex sugar redistribution processes necessary for acclimatization to low temperatures. Cold-induced expression of SWEET transporters could potentially be regulated by stress-responsive promoter elements (such as ABRE and MYB-binding sites), integrating them into cold signaling pathways [60]. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that definitive assignment of substrate specificity and subcellular localization for these candidate SWEETs awaits experimental validation through heterologous expression systems and subcellular localization assays.

Identification of cold-responsive transcription factors co-expressed with sugar metabolism genes

Among differentially expressed TFs, the NAC family was most prominent. Previous studies suggested that OsNAC5 integrated ABA signaling and ROS homeostasis to regulate cold responses in rice [61], while SINAC3 could negatively impact cold tolerance by promoting ethylene biosynthesis [62]. MaNAC1 acted as a direct target of MaICE1, interacting with MaCBF1 to regulate the core ICE1-CBF cold signaling pathway [63]. VaWRKY65 in Vitis amurensis conferred cold tolerance by directly activating VaBAM3 (promoting osmoprotectant accumulation via starch degradation) and VaPOD36 (enhancing ROS detoxification) [64]. The SlHsfB1 transcript was significantly increased on the third day of refrigeration in cold-resistant cultivars [65]. AcCIB2 (AcbHLH2) enhances Ananas comosus stress resistance by integrating salt, osmotic stress, and ABA signaling pathways [66]. Based on these precedents, it is reasonable to propose that the chrysanthemum homologs CmNAC1, CmWRKY65, CmHsfB1, and CmbHLH2 may participate in cold tolerance regulation in this species. Furthermore, the co-expression network in the present study showed a high correlation between the four TFs and sucrose/trehalose metabolism (Fig. 8c). Therefore, the four TFs might participate in the cold response through mediating the sucrose/trehalose metabolism in chrysanthemum. We also identified a significantly upregulated LOB transcription factor, designated CmLBD18. In Arabidopsis thaliana, LOB family members (including LBD16, LBD17, LBD18, and LBD29) mediated auxin-triggered callus formation and root development processes [67]. Crucially, CmLBD18 expression showed a significant positive correlation with multiple genes involved in sugar metabolism (TPS, TPS11, SUS-1, SUS-2, SUS2-1, SUS2-2, BGLU, CesA, CesA1) (Fig. 9c). These co-expression patterns raise the intriguing possibility that CmLBD18 may act as a positive regulator of sucrose, trehalose, and cellobiose biosynthesis, potentially linking auxin-mediated developmental cues with carbohydrate metabolism during cold stress. Collectively, while these correlation-based inferences must be interpreted with caution, the identified TF networks represent promising candidates for future functional studies aimed at elucidating the transcriptional control of carbohydrate metabolism during cold acclimation in chrysanthemum.

Conclusions

This study elucidates the critical role of sugar metabolism in the cold adaptation of chrysanthemum. Under the low temperature, ZR and FSD activated a metabolic defense mechanism by significantly accumulating key osmoprotectants, such as sucrose, trehalose, and raffinose. We identified a set of SWEET transporters in the chrysanthemum transcriptome, showing significant evolutionary relationships with known SWEET proteins in Arabidopsis thaliana. The phylogenetic analysis highlighted key homologs, including CHR00080209 (AtSWEET16), CHR00064977 (AtSWEET15), and CHR00048779 (AtSWEET9). Furthermore, the transcription factors CmWRKY65 and CmLBD18 potentially integrate cold signaling with sugar metabolism via a co-expression network, likely through the regulation of sucrose/trehalose biosynthetic genes. In conclusion, our findings demonstrate that sugar metabolism is integral to the cold adaptation process in chrysanthemum and provide genetic resources associated with cold resistance.
