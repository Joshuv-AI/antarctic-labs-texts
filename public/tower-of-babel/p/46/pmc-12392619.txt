Effect of high-fidelity human patient simulator manikins combined with standardized patient simulation scenario on clinical thinking in pediatric nursing education
Authors: Long-yi Hu, Si-qi Li, Zi-yun Zhou, Ming-li Wang, Le-shan Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundClinical thinking is essential for nursing students. However, traditional teaching focused on theory tends to reduce clinical thinking.MethodsIn this study, 69 undergraduate (third-year) nursing students at a university located in Hunan Province, China, were recruited from March to July 2023. In pediatric nursing, courses were taught using high-fidelity human patient simulator manikins (HF-HPSM) and standardized patient (SP) simulation scenarios. They participated in two scenario simulations and one skills assessment based on scenario simulation, and then the clinical thinking of nursing students before and after the course was compared. Furthermore, the participants’ nursing skill differences were compared with 58 students who only participated in skill assessments in previous years.ResultsThe simulation groups’ clinical, critical, and systematic thinking were improved compared to their pre-test scores after the pediatric course(p<0.001). Additionally, the simulation group had significantly different skill performance than the control group(p<0.001).ConclusionThe results indicated that the introduction of combined HF-HPSM and SP simulation scenario courses allows for improved clinical, critical, and systematic thinking. Adding simulation with standardized patients portraying family members to the course further increases skill performance compared to theory-based teaching alone.Keywords: Clinical reasoning, Clinical judgement, Nursing education, Simulation scenario

Background

Clinical thinking is crucial for nursing students [1, 2]. It involves the acquisition of knowledge from different fields, prioritizing patients, analyzing information from the most reliable sources, and developing a personalized plan that can be implemented and modified. Clinical thinking differs from critical thinking [3]. Moreover, it applies critical thinking in clinical practice. Critical thinking is a cognitive process used to analyze empirics. This cognitive skill employs pre-existing knowledge and background experience to recognize and evaluate complex issues [4]. Clinical thinking, also called clinical reasoning or clinical judgment [5], is the application of knowledge and experience to clinical situations [1]. Clinical thinking involves three skills: Critical thinking for knowledge analysis, systems thinking to synthesize data from several sources, and systematic thinking [6] is defined as the ability to recognize, understand, and synthesize interactions and interdependencies among various factors to achieve a particular goal. Nursing students with robust systematic thinking skills are more likely to ensure patient safety and improve patient prognosis, while evidence-based thinking helps to identify the best evidence. Evidence-based thinking [7] involves combining research-based evidence for patient care with clinical experience and the patient’s specific requirements. All of which lead to customized patient care—are examples of applying cognitive thinking. Clinical thinking involves both “thinking” and “doing” [8].

Sun et al. found that Chinese nursing students have low critical thinking skills. They concluded that a major aspect was that the conventional pedagogical approach emphasizes theoretical concepts, resulting in “standard answers” in assessments [9]. As a result, students frequently adopt stereotypes and rely on authority figures. Consequently, there are many disparities between teaching and the real-life clinical setting. The development of clinical thinking is not facilitated by traditional education that exclusively emphasizes theory and lacks simulation. Additionally, considering ethical considerations, nursing students encounter challenges when engaging in clinical patient skills, as they are restricted to laboratory-based training. Peng et al. revealed that single-skill training teaches only component skills, removing from the clinical scenario and reducing the overall clinical thinking of the students [10]. In clinical practice, this teaching mode stops nursing students from “thinking and doing like nurses.” Consequently, putting thought into practice is directly expressed in developing nursing abilities in clinical settings.

However, simulation differs from traditional teaching methods by providing nursing students with a realistic clinical environment without possibly causing actual harm to patients [11]. Simulation is a teaching tool in which nursing students can participate in-depth, improving patient care safety [12] and effectively applying their nursing skills. The two common approaches to simulation are high-fidelity human patient simulator manikins (HF-HPSM) and standardized patient (SP). HF-HPSM can be established to display a physiological decrease, and the operator can regulate it in response to students’ intervention [13]. SP involves a person trained to act as a patient with a scripted scenario [14]. Kim et al. examined 40 simulated learning studies and found that simulation fidelity affected academic achievement, learning attitudes, and satisfaction. Furthermore, SP show a bigger impact in high-fidelity simulation than low-fidelity [15]. Hybrid simulation (HS) [16], defined as “the use of two or more simulation modes to enhance the fidelity of a scenario by integrating the environment, physiology, emotions, and dialog of real patient encounters,” can effectively improve the fidelity of the simulation.

Simulation is experiential, case-based learning. Case studies are extensively utilized in various forms of medical education [17]. Simulations usually focus on a specific case, providing students with a concrete scenario to suggest nursing techniques, establish a care plan, and implement it, helping them apply their knowledge to clinical concerns. Simulation links between theory and practice [18]. In addition to critical thinking, systems thinking and evidence-based thinking are also crucial in this process [19]. According to a meta-analysis, nursing students who monitor a patient’s changing circumstances in real-time apply their knowledge to make appropriate clinical judgments in HF-HPSM simulations [20], thus developing the clinical competence of nursing students.

Pediatric patients usually have limited communication ability, and their accompanying parents often communicate most of their information. Pediatric patients are less cooperative, and it is a test to accurately obtain information about the condition and report it to the doctor. Thus, clinical practice with such patients is difficult. Currently, HF-HPSM models are rarely used in pediatric nursing. Moreover, most simulation model research designed for adult patients focuses on simulating the patient alone, with no consideration given to family members. Pediatrics is special in that patients have limited ability to express themselves, and changes in their state are more difficult to detect. Hence the role of family members cannot be neglected in the care process. Current research predominantly utilizes HF-HPSM or SP for simulations. However, there are relatively few studies exploring the combined application of both methods in pediatric nursing education [21].

In order to reconstruct the clinical scenario and explore the impact of HF-HPSM combined with SP on the clinical thinking of nursing students, this study mimics the “child,” the SP plays the role of the “family member,” and the teacher plays the role of a “doctor.” The aim of the study is to evaluate the development of nursing students’ clinical thinking after participating in clinical simulation and to compare the acquisition of nursing skills with students from previous years. This study hypothesized that HF-HPSM combined with SP would enhance nursing students’ clinical thinking skills.

Methods

Study design

A quasi-experimental pretest-posttest design was employed in this study. The study followed the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations of Non-randomised Designs (TREND) checklist [22].

Participants

Sample size: The test was conducted using G*power, setting η2 is 0.06, α is 0.05 and1-β is 0.95, calculating a total sample size of at least 54. The people who took part were 69 full-time third-year nursing students from a university in Hunan, China, between March and July 2023. Before recruitment, participants were informed about the study details and that participation would not affect their academic credits. Recruitment will end once the course begins. Participant consent was sought prior to data collection. Participants were juniors, all of whom were adults. For comparison, between March and July 2022, 59 third-year undergraduate students from the same institution who possessed comparable qualities were chosen in order to assess their performance in nursing skills. Previous students had been taught using traditional teaching methods without simulation-based approaches, and the HF-HPSM and SP were used solely for the skills assessment at the end of the semester. The same teaching team used the same textbook to conduct lectures. See Fig. 1.

Flow diagram of the participants of this study

The inclusion criteria were students who (1) had self-reported normal cognitive communication functions (2), participated voluntarily in the study, and (3) were able to complete the course independently. The exclusion criterion was students who did not complete the study for any reason. The total number of enrolled participants was 69. However, one student quit in the middle; 68 nursing students were included in this study. Participant consent was sought prior to data collection. Participants were juniors, all of whom were adults. This study was reviewed by the Xiangya School of Nursing Ethics Committee on March 17, 2023 (E202352). Clinical trial number: not applicable.

Intervention

Teachers were specially trained and held the title of associate senior or above. The simulation was designed following healthcare simulation standards [23]. This training utilizes NENA Sim (MEDICAL-X, Netherlands), a neonatal simulator with realistic eyes, head, and neck movements. For reliability, silicone skin temperature can be adjusted. The model included an intelligent stethoscope, heart and lung settings, and infant CPR. The educators replicated the disease progression by manipulating the vital signs, such as pulse (P), respiration (R), blood pressure (BP), heart rate, electrocardiogram (ECG), and blood oxygen saturation (SpO2), as well as sounds such as cries and coughs, within the operational environment. Following the completion of the simulation, the computer generated a logbook to record the procedure.

HF-HPSM combined with SP simulation

The course had three simulations, two for teaching and one for evaluation. The teachers devised the first simulation, the students designed the second, and the teachers conducted the third to assess skills. After the simulation, the participating nursing students engaged in self-reflection and proposed their strengths and weaknesses. Students who observed the exercise did the same by offering their opinions sparking criticism. Students had 10–15 min to report. The teacher concluded with an overview. The debriefing process was based on healthcare modeling best practice standards [23]. See Fig. 2.

Intervention flowchart of the participants of this study

Scenario setting

The HF-HPSM symptoms, signs, additional evaluation, diagnosis, and treatment were defined by the educational requirements. For editing and modification, the programmers entered the data into the HSFM software. Based on the scenarios, the educators created SP scripts that included words, gestures, and physiological responses. SPs received training and certifications. Nursing students carried out the essential inquiries, physical assessments, diagnoses, and interventions.

The teaching objectives of pediatric nursing include (a) Knowledge objectives: to master the etiology, clinical manifestations, common nursing diagnosis, primary nursing measures, and health education of common pediatric diseases; (b) Competence objectives: to conduct nursing assessments of children with common diseases, raise nursing problems, and formulate nursing plans; (c) Attitudes objectives: to improve clinical thinking skills and knowledge on how to deal with emergencies. These objectives run throughout the entire learning process and guide the creation of scenario simulations.

The first simulation

The teachers distributed case information to students in advance. During the session, 2–3 nursing students were randomly chosen to create a nursing team to gather supplies and distribute tasks. The students were then trained in responding to the HF-HPSM and SP, which responded dynamically to subjective and objective patient information. The disorders impacting each system were randomly assigned to 1–2 simulated groups. The course also required all students to participate in the simulation.

There are different cases for different systemic diseases. For example, simulation of respiratory diseases in children:

Act 1: A pediatric patient, simulated by the HF-HPSM, experiencing symptoms of cough, wheezing, and fever for three days, as well as increased irritability for half a day, was brought to the pediatric department from the emergency room 30 min ago. The patient was placed under surveillance and given antipyretic medication. The toddler was admitted with a diagnosis of upper respiratory tract infection. The medical recommendations included a penicillin skin test with immediate impact and nebulization every eight hours.

The HF-HPSM cried nonstop after the shift handover, and the SP rang the bell to call the nurse. The vital signs included P: 168 beats per minute (bpm); R: 51 bmp; BP: 96/55 mmHg; SpO2: 94%. The worried SP holds the crying “child”: “My child’s fever has not gone down, and he keeps crying.” If the nurse did not explain the purpose of the intervention or failed to obtain consent, the SP would intervene.

Act 2: During treatment, the HF-HPSM’s monitor showed P: 177 bpm, R: 54 bpm, BP: 98/58 mmHg, SpO2: 93%; temperature (T): 38.6 °C. The HF-HPSM exhibited diaphoresis accompanied by crackles. This finding indicates that the child is undergoing heart failure. Nurses noticed the change. One person should stop nebulizing and start inhaling oxygen, while another should immediately notify the doctor. The doctor provided medical advice after checking the HF-HPSM: “Medicines; intravenous injection; ST.” The nurse administered the medicine as prescribed, after which the vital signs included P: 161 bmp; R: 43 bpm, BP: 92/48 mmHg; SpO2: 95%; T: 38.3℃; and the simulation ended.

The SP would appear agitated and tell the nurses, “The medicine did not work, and he is crying, so what should we do?” if they did not notice any changes in the patient’s condition. P: 181 bmp, R: 56 bpm, BP: 100/57 mmHg, SpO2: 92%, and T: 38.5 °C were all displayed on the monitor. The HF-HPSM cried and shook accompanied by a cyanotic complexion. If the nurses do not observe this, the SP will be angry until the nursing student reports to the doctor.

The second simulation

The instructor distributed the simulation requirements and examples to the nursing students. In groups, the nursing students created scenarios based on their experiences and everyday knowledge, including cases with children of all ages and ailments. It was required of each group to write a script. Following their examination and editing by the teachers, the instances and scenarios were entered into the program. Students wrote SP scripts for various scenarios. The scriptwriters played the SP, while other groups were randomly selected for the simulation. Each group was instructed to draw lots among themselves. To ensure that every group of students participated, each group was asked to draw scripts among themselves, and other groups drew the scripts they wrote for the simulation.

The third simulation

The course concluded with a skills evaluation. All simulation groups’ students participate in the third simulation, while the control students only participated in this simulation for skill assessment. The HF-HPSM was utilized to imitate the child, with a cardiac monitor displaying vital signs. There were three educators, including a “family member,” a “doctor,” and an overall controller. Two to three nursing students were randomly selected to form a team and autonomously allocate labor. Cases were provided before the appraisal. Correct interventions lowered crying sounds and modified vital signs in the model. If the intervention were incorrect, the model would continue to cry. The “family member” prompted and responded based on the student’s adherence to appropriate interventions, health promotion strategies, and monitoring the “patient’s” condition, among other factors. The test is limited to 15 min and assessed by three teachers at the end.

Measures

The main objective was to evaluate the participants’ pre- and post-course clinical thinking after gathering their baseline data. Comparisons with the nursing skills of prior students were also made for more reliable findings.

General information

The general information encompassed gender, being the only kid, place of residence, the occupations of the father and mother, level of affinity towards nursing, and readiness to be employed.

Clinical thinking

The scale compiled by Song et al. [24]. was employed. The scale was evaluated by the students themselves, comprising a total of 24 entries in three subscales, namely, critical thinking (entries 1–6), systematic thinking (entries 7–17), and evidence-based thinking (entries 18–24). Scoring used a 5-point Likert scale and a score range of 24–120, with higher scores indicating better clinical thinking ability. The Cronbach’s α = 0.91, and the Cronbach’s α of each dimension is 0.82, 0.80, and 0.78, respectively [24].

Academic performance

Nursing skills: Following the assessment, the three teachers used a percentage approach to score the nursing students. The scores were based on several criteria, including group labor division (25%), operational abilities (25%), clinical judgment skills (25%), and first aid awareness (25%). This is a widely used clinical skill assessment scoring table in China. A high score means having more proficient clinical skills.

Data collection

A total of 69 undergraduate nursing students at a university in Hunan Province, China, were enrolled between March and July 2023. Clinical thinking scores were assessed using the Clinical Thinking Scale before and after the simulation sessions. Furthermore, following the course, the nursing students’ performance on the nursing skill assessments was evaluated, and compared to a previous cohort of 58 students who did not receive HF-HPSM with SP.

Ethics approval and consent to participate

The ethical review board approved the study protocol before data collection and recruitment. The research followed the Declaration of Helsinki ethical principles. Participants were informed about the study before participation and provided written informed consent.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 25.0 was used to perform all of the analyses. Descriptive statistics of demographic characteristics and scores were calculated and presented as numbers, percentages, means, and standard deviations (SD) for normally distributed variables. Paired t-tests were used to compare clinical, critical, systematic, and evidence-based thinking scores. Two independent sample t-tests were used to compare the academic performance of nursing skills.

Discussion

Impact on clinical thinking

Critical thinking

Nurses with higher levels of critical thinking can better gather information to analyze, reflect on, and ultimately solve clinical problems [25]. The HF-HPSM simulates the physiology of pediatric patients, while the SP (“pediatric family member”) responds to the patient’s situation and may exaggerate their symptoms due to excessive concern. The nursing students’ response is based on the patient’s physiological condition and the “family member’s” description. Nursing students can experience real-life interactions in the simulation-based clinical setting, which makes them more responsible and proactive in their practice.

Cheng et al. [26] suggested that self-reflection helps nursing students develop critical thinking, acquire professional experience, and enhance their self-understanding and ability to cope with emergencies. Nursing students often have difficulty dealing with the pediatric patient and their family members upon their first interaction [27]. In this study, mutual criticism helped participants identify their weaknesses and learn from each other. Moreover, students expressed their concerns through discussions, reflecting on nursing behaviors from different perspectives, thus developing their capacity for critical thinking.

Li et al. [28] concluded that critical thinking requires prolonged and constant practice. The duration and timing of the HF-HPSM simulation influence the nurses’ critical thinking. For this study, three simulations were created. One of these was developed by nursing students who are actively involved in teaching. This simulation required their independent learning, allowing students to think from a different perspective and so understand the meanings underlying various nursing behaviors.

Systematic thinking

The ability to identify, comprehend, and integrate the linkages and connections between various components in order to achieve a goal is known as “systematic thinking.” Strong systematic thinking abilities in nursing students can guarantee patient safety and enhance their prognosis [6]. A multi-center study indicated that simulation enhances systematic thinking skills among students from various healthcare professions [29]. Since pediatric patients usually have limited communication ability, nursing students can acquire information on various elements of the healthcare setting, such as the cries of the “baby” and changes in vital signs indicated by instruments when assessing patients. Furthermore, the family member represented by the SP is also a crucial source of information. The role of the SP might vary from that of a facilitator or an obstructionist, depending on the student’s performance. The nursing student must be more empathetic and communicate well so that the SP can understand the significance of the interventions and assist in the care. According to Wang et al., after interacting with an SP, students’ communication competence in patient education greatly improved [30]. Nursing students need to identify the reasons and severity of the change in condition and then respond by treating the condition and using the “Situation- Background-Assessment-Recommendation” methodology to provide a report to the doctor. This type of collaboration is highly beneficial in improving the pupils’ ability to think systematically. Kucukkelepce et al. conducted interviews with 86 nursing students and discovered that the students developed multidimensional thinking abilities while completing the SP simulation course [31].

Evidence-based thinking

The scores of evidence-based thinking after the course were greater than those at the pre-course level; however, the difference was not statistically significant. This may be because the evidence-based reasoning scale assessed scientific research skills. The teaching staff regarded the current course workload as appropriate when constructing evidence-based tasks, which may have increased nursing students’ learning pressure. As a result, no further learning based on evidence was pursued. Future studies may explore evidence-based instructional practices, such as the implementation of retrieval techniques, utilization of emerging technologies, and adoption of innovative methodologies.

Impact on the application of clinical thinking

Nursing skills

The HF-HPSM-SP simulation improved nursing skills in students. Nursing students are encouraged to exercise their talents in the simulation’s safe and non-threatening environment. Most students show increased confidence in their nursing abilities after participating in simulations [12, 32]. Pediatric nursing differs from the nursing of adult patients, and operations learned in other disciplines do not always apply to children. Thus, the HF-HPSM allows nursing students to encounter such cases more successfully. A study indicated that the clinical competence and self-confidence of nursing students were effectively increased when they used the HF-HPSM in conjunction with an SP simulation scenario [33]. Furthermore, Lavoie et al. reported that the more realistic the simulation, the better the learning experience of the student [34]. In the simulation, the HF-HPSM not only cried but swung its body, providing a more authentic representation of the actual circumstances in the therapeutic setting. It can be demonstrated that HF-HPSM paired with the SP simulation scenario benefits nursing students’ skills as they get more familiar with the operational stages and are more flexible in dealing with different situations, enabling them to cope with responsible clinical settings.

Conclusion

The results showed that applying the combined HF-HPSM and SP simulation scenario improved the clinical thinking of nursing students and is suitable for teaching pediatric nursing. Many patients resemble pediatric patients because they cannot express themselves adequately and are thus accompanied by family members. Therefore, the combined application of HF-HPSM and SP simulation scenarios should be promoted in order to provide nursing students with a lifelike experience, increased nursing skills, and clinical thinking.
