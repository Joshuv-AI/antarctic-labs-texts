Fe-doped phase-transition nanodroplets for synergistic photothermal and starvation-enhanced ferroptosis in cancer therapy
Authors: Yuhang Tian, Xiang He, Yanchi Yuan, Chunyue Wang, Mengchi Zhang, Hui Jiang, Huajing Yang, Kuikun Yang, Hui Jing
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFerroptosis therapy has emerged as a promising antitumor strategy by utilizing the Fenton reaction to destroy cancer cells, where Fe2+ catalyzes the decomposition of H2O2 into hydroxyl radicals (•OH). Despite the great potential of ferroptosis therapy in suppressing tumor growth, inadequate catalysts and reactants within tumors remains a major challenge before its clinical translation. Herein, we developed glucose oxidase (GOx)-loaded phase-transition nanodroplets (PND) modified with Fe-tannic acid (TA) networks (PND@GOx@Fe-TA) for enhanced antitumor efficacy of ferroptosis therapy via synergistic photothermal and starvation therapy.ResultsPND@GOx@Fe-TA can convert glucose into H2O2, which not only provides sufficient H2O2 for Fenton reaction, but also consumes glucose to exert starvation therapy. In addition, the Fe-TA networks of PND@GOx@Fe-TA can be degraded upon reaching the tumor site, thus generating Fe2+ from Fe3+ via reduction by the overexpressed glutathione (GSH) in the tumor microenvironment. The Fe2+ then reacts with the in situ-generated H2O2 for enhanced Fenton reaction and induces ferroptosis of cancer cells. Additionally, the PND@GOx@Fe-TA exhibits photothermal effects under 808 nm laser irradiation, which not only accelerates the Fe2+-mediated Fenton reaction but also gasifies the liquid core of the PND, enabling its use as a contrast agent for contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS), photoacoustic imaging (PAI) and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI).ConclusionsIn summary, the PND@GOx@Fe-TA represents a promising approach for multimodal imaging-guided antitumor therapy by synergistic starvation, photothermal and enhanced ferroptosis therapy.Graphical Abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12951-025-03726-y.Keywords: Phase-transition nanodroplets, Ferroptosis therapy, Photothermal therapy, Starvation therapy, Glucose oxidase

Introduction

Ferroptosis has been established as a potent mechanism for destroying tumor cells [1–3], characterized by iron-dependent dysregulation of lipid peroxidation and generation of reactive oxygen species (ROS) via the Fenton reaction [4–6]. Consequently, numerous ferrous-based nanomaterials have been developed to promote ferroptosis therapy by enhancing the efficacy of the Fenton reaction [5–7]. However, despite the elevated concentration of H2O2 in tumors compared to normal tissues [8], the •OH generation remains constrained by the limited availability of H2O2 within tumor tissues [9, 10], thus compromising therapeutic outcomes of ferroptosis therapy. Furthermore, the mild acidic tumor microenvironment (TME) and body temperature also impede the Fenton reaction [11]. It is crucial to enhance the Fenton reaction by enriching both the catalyst (Fe2+) and H2O2 within tumor tissues, lowering the pH of the TME as well as increasing the local temperature, thus improving the efficacy of ferroptosis-based cancer therapies [12, 13].

It is widely recognized that the uncontrolled proliferation of tumors relies on the continuous nutrients supply [14–17]. Consequently, starvation therapy, which aims to inhibit tumor growth by cutting off its nutrient supply, has emerged as a highly promising strategy in cancer treatment [14, 18]. Given the critical role of glucose in tumor metabolism, the use of glucose oxidase (GOx) to deplete glucose and disrupt nutrient supply within tumors has been identified as an effective approach for starvation therapy. GOx catalyzes the conversion of glucose into gluconic acid and H2O2, both of which play a pivotal role in enhancing the Fenton reaction [19, 20]. The generated H2O2 serves as a crucial substrate for the Fenton reaction, thus promoting the production of highly toxic hydroxyl radicals (•OH), while gluconic acid lowers the pH of the TME, facilitating the Fenton reaction by creating an optimal acidic condition. Therefore, the combination of GOx-mediated starvation therapy and Fenton reaction-based ferroptosis not only starves tumors of essential nutrients but also enhances the production of cytotoxic •OH, offering a potent and multifaceted therapeutic strategy against cancers.

Mild photothermal therapy (∼45 °C) has become an ideal approach in cancer treatment as the temperature is sufficiently high to ablate tumors with minimum damages to normal tissues [21]. Recent studies highlighted that Fe-TA networks are outstanding candidates for tumor photothermal therapy due to their ease of synthesis, high biosafety, stable photothermal property and attractive photoacoustic (PA) and magnetic resonance (MR) imaging ability [22, 23]. Interestingly, the photothermal effect can significantly accelerate the Fenton reaction rate by up to fourfold [24, 25]. Therefore, the combination of photothermal therapy and ferroptosis not only directly damages tumor cells but also amplifies the production of cytotoxic hydroxyl radicals (•OH) through the enhanced Fenton reaction, thereby significantly improving overall treatment efficacy [26, 27].

Herein, we propose glucose oxidase (GOx)-loaded phase-transition nanodroplets modified with Fe-TA networks (PND@GOx@Fe-TA) for synergistic photothermal and starvation-enhanced ferroptosis antitumor therapy (Scheme 1). The phase-transition nanodroplets were employed as carriers to encapsulate and protect GOx from biodegradation, while Fe-TA networks were conjugated on their surface to form PND@GOx@Fe-TA. Upon internalization into tumor cells, the acidic lysosomal environment triggers the disassembly of the external Fe-TA networks, leading to the pH-responsive release of Fe3+. Subsequently, Fe3+ is reduced to Fe2+ by intracellular glutathione (GSH), serving as a Fenton agent to catalyze the conversion of endogenous H2O2 into highly toxic hydroxyl radicals (•OH). Simultaneously, the Fe-TA networks act as a photothermal agent, inducing the phase transition of the nanodroplets into a gaseous state under near-infrared (NIR) laser irradiation, thereby providing enhanced ultrasound contrast for precise tumor visualization and monitoring. The photothermal effect not only directly kills cancer cells but also accelerates the Fenton reaction to amplify ferroptosis. The encapsulated GOx plays a dual role by depleting glucose to starve tumor cells and generating H2O2 and gluconic acid in situ, which supplies the necessary reactant and lowers the pH of the TME to augment the Fenton reaction. By integrating photothermal and starvation therapy to promote tumor ferroptosis, PND@GOx@Fe-TA represents a highly promising multimodal therapeutic strategy for effective cancer eradication.

Results and discussion

Synthesis and characterization

The synthesis of PND@GOx@Fe-TA involves two main steps. First, PND@GOx was prepared through thin-film hydration and ultrasonic emulsification. Thereafter, TA, a natural polyphenol with multiple galloyl groups, can coordinate with Fe3+ to form a cross-linked film on the surface of PND@GOx to afford PND@GOx@Fe-TA [28]. PND@Fe-TA without encapsulation of GOx was also fabricated as the control group. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images revealed that PND, PND@Fe-TA, and PND@GOx@Fe-TA all exhibited a spherical morphology (Fig. 1a and S1). Dynamic light scattering results indicated that the average particle size of PND was 203.2 ± 36.7 nm. As modifications proceeded, the size of PND@GOx@Fe-TA ultimately reached 267.2 ± 37.9 nm (Fig. 1b). Zeta potential analysis provided additional evidence for the successful synthesis process of PND@GOx@Fe-TA. The zeta potential decreased from + 50.2 ± 3.5 mV for PND to + 33.7 ± 1.7 mV for PND@GOx@Fe-TA (Figure S2). The sizes and PDI of PND@GOx@Fe-TA showed negligible changes for 9 days in PBS, indicating the superior stability of PND@GOx@Fe-TA (Figure S3).

The absorption characteristics of PND@Fe-TA and PND@GOx@Fe-TA were evaluated and compared with those of PND, Fe-TA, and GOx (Figure S4). Compared with PND, distinct absorbance peaks appeared at approximately 560–600 nm for the Fe-TA complex, PND@Fe-TA, and PND@GOx@Fe-TA, indicating the successful formation of Fe-TA networks [29]. The Fe encapsulation efficiency of PND@GOx@Fe-TA was 42.8% as revealed by the inductively coupled plasma (ICP) analysis, further confirming the incorporation of the Fe-TA networks onto the nanoparticles. Additionally, the PND@GOx@Fe-TA spectrum exhibited a peak at 455 nm, which aligns with the characteristic absorption peak of GOx, suggesting the successful loading of GOx into the PND@Fe-TA nanoparticles. The encapsulation efficiency of GOx was calculated to be approximately 21.6%, based on a standard curve derived from UV-vis absorption spectra (Figure S5).

The pH-responsiveness of the Fe-TA networks was investigated by evaluating the release of Fe3+ from PND@GOx@Fe-TA at two different pH values: 7.4 (mimicking physiological conditions) and 6.0 (mimicking the acidic TME). As shown in Fig. 1c, the release of Fe3+ from PND@GOx@Fe-TA was significantly promoted at pH 6.0 (63.9%) than at pH 7.4 (17.3%), demonstrating their ability to remain stable under physiological conditions (pH 7.4) during blood circulation, while selectively disassembling in the acidic TME (pH 6.0) to release Fe3+ for triggering the Fenton reaction.

The release behavior of GOx from PND@GOx@Fe-TA was also evaluated. GOx slowly leaked out of PND@GOx@Fe-TA over time without external stimulation. However, upon laser irradiation, the release of GOx was significantly accelerated (Fig. 1d). After five on-off irradiation cycles, the cumulative release of GOx reached 72.8%, while only 10.1% of GOx was released in the absence of laser exposure. The rapid GOx release under laser irradiation can be attributed to the photothermal effect, which causes the rupture of the phospholipid layer to readily release GOx. Additionally, the catalytic activity of the encapsulated GOx was assessed by monitoring the generation of gluconic acid and O2 consumption. The changes in pH were used to indicate gluconic acid generation. In the absence of glucose, the pH of the PND@GOx@Fe-TA solution remained stable at around 7.1. However, when glucose was introduced, a significant decrease in pH from 7.1 to 4.8 was observed (Fig. 1e). This pH drop is ascribed to the generation of gluconic acid via GOx-catalyzed glucose oxidation. The changes of O2 concentration were measured using a dissolved oxygen meter. As shown in Fig. 1f, in the presence of both PND@GOx@Fe-TA and glucose, the O2 concentration decreased from 8.4 mg/L to 1.3 mg/L within 9 min. In contrast, no significant decrease in O2 concentration was observed in the solution without glucose. These findings provided strong evidence for the capability of GOx in PND@GOx@Fe-TA to catalyze glucose and also confirmed that PND@GOx@Fe-TA could effectively consume glucose to starve tumor cells.

As GSH has the ability to convert released Fe3+ into Fe2+, the efficacy of PND@GOx@Fe-TA in depleting GSH was evaluated. As shown in Fig. 1g, GSH depletion was observed in a time-dependent manner, indicating the efficient GSH consumption by PND@GOx@Fe-TA. Thereafter, we assessed the •OH generation by PND@GOx@Fe-TA with MB as an indicator. As depicted in Fig. 1h, the absorbance of MB decreased gradually upon incubation with PND@GOx@Fe-TA, confirming the abundant generation of •OH. Compared with the MB and MB + H2O2 groups, the absorbance of MB remarkably decreased upon incubation with PND@GOx@Fe-TA and H2O2 at pH 6.0 (Fig. 1i). However, no significant reduction in MB absorbance was observed following the identical treatment at pH 7.4. This might be attributed to the limited release of Fe3+ in neutral environments. It is noteworthy that, to simulate the mild photothermal effect of PND@GOx@Fe-TA, the absorbance of MB at 43 °C was significantly lower than at 37 °C (Figure S6), indicating that photothermal conditions could accelerate the Fenton reaction rate, consistent with the previous report [30]. In addition, the absorption of MB incubated with PND@GOx@Fe-TA + glucose gradually decreased over time, even in the absence of H2O2, suggesting the progressive generation of •OH in the presence of glucose (Figure S7). These results confirmed that GOx was gradually released from PND@GOx@Fe-TA to effectively catalyze glucose to produce H2O2 and lower the pH of the TME, thereby accelerating the Fenton reaction and promoting ferroptosis.

Photothermal effects of PND@GOx@Fe-TA

The photothermal performance of PND@GOx@Fe-TA under 808 nm laser irradiation was investigated to evaluate its photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE). It was found that the photothermal effect of PND@GOx@Fe-TA depended on both the concentration of PND@GOx@Fe-TA (Fig. 2a and f) and the power density of the laser irradiation (Fig. 2b and S8). During three heating-cooling cycles, no significant attenuation in maximum temperature was observed, demonstrating the satisfactory photothermal stability of PND@GOx@Fe-TA (Fig. 2c). The PCE of PND@GOx@Fe-TA was calculated to be 32.5% (Fig. 2d and e), which exceeded earlier reports [31, 32], indicating the remarkable photothermal effect of the nanoparticles.

PND@GOx@Fe-TA mediated ferroptosis in vitro

Next, we explored the primary mechanisms of PND@GOx@Fe-TA mediated ferroptosis. First, the intracellular GSH levels, serving as a molecular marker for ferroptosis, were measured following various treatments (Fig. 3a). Compared to the control and laser groups, the GSH levels in the PND@Fe-TA or PND@GOx@Fe-TA groups significantly decreased, indicating the ability of Fe3+ to deplete GSH and evoke the Fenton reaction. GOx promoted the Fenton reaction by efficient H2O2 generation and pH reduction in the TME, as revealed by the enhanced GSH depletion in PND@GOx@Fe-TA than PND@Fe-TA. Notably, GSH depletion was further increased in the PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L group, likely owing to the promoted Fenton reaction by the photothermal effect. Furthermore, we utilized western blot analysis to evaluate glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), a marker related to ferroptosis, after the corresponding treatment. As shown in Fig. 3b and c, compared with the control group and the laser group, the expression of GPX4 was decreased in the other three treatment groups. The PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L group exhibited the most significant downregulation of GPX4 among all groups, indicating the most pronounced ferroptosis promoted by GOx-mediated starvation and Fe-TA-induced photothermal effects.

The depletion of GSH disrupts the intracellular redox balance, resulting in the accumulation of excessive ROS and promoting the ferroptosis of tumor cells via peroxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids. Malondialdehyde (MDA), the final product after the reaction between free radicals and polyunsaturated fatty acids, serves as a direct indicator of the intracellular oxidative stress levels. As depicted in Fig. 3d, cells treated with PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L showed the most significant increase in MDA content among all groups, suggesting the most pronounced ferroptosis induced by the nanoparticles under irradiation. The lipid peroxidation (LPO) assay yielded similar results (Fig. 3g). After treatment with PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L, the tumor cells displayed the most intense green fluorescence, indicating the strongest ferroptosis induction. Consequently, the tumor cell viability notably declined upon treatment with individual PND@GOx@Fe-TA but substantially recovered when pretreated with ferroptosis inhibitor or iron chelator, confirming that ferroptosis was the primary mechanism of the cell death induced by the PND@GOx@Fe-TA (Fig. 3e and f).

Effect of photothermal/starvation/ferroptosis therapy in vitro

The synergistic therapeutic efficacy of PND@GOx@Fe-TA was assessed in vitro for its potential in cancer treatment. We utilized the DCFH-DA probe to evaluate the ROS levels in 4T1 cells following various treatments (Fig. 4a and S9). There was no notable green fluorescence in the control and laser groups, suggesting that laser alone cannot generate ROS in tumor cells. However, the PND@Fe-TA and PND@GOx@Fe-TA treated cells all exhibited remarkable green fluorescence likely owing to the generation of •OH through the Fenton reaction. The stronger fluorescence in the PND@GOx@Fe-TA group indicated that the GOx can enhance the Fenton reaction by supplying more H2O2 and lowering the pH in the TME. The cells treated with PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L showed the strongest fluorescence among all treatment groups, suggesting the promoted ferroptosis by photothermal effects.

We subsequently assessed the cellular uptake of PND@GOx@Fe-TA in 4T1 cells through ICP-MS. As shown in Fig. 4b, the Fe content in 4T1 cells treated with PND@GOx@Fe-TA increased rapidly in comparison with the cells treated with PBS, confirming the successful internalization of PND@GOx@Fe-TA by tumor cells. The cytotoxicity of PND@Fe-TA and PND@GOx@Fe-TA was further assessed using the CCK-8 assay in two cell lines: breast cancer cells (4T1) and normal cells (MCF-10 A). As shown in Fig. 4c and S10a, PND@Fe-TA exhibited no significant cytotoxicity on MCF-10 A cells, even at high concentrations up to 600 µg/mL, indicating excellent biocompatibility of the nanoparticles. However, PND@Fe-TA demonstrated selective cytotoxicity toward 4T1 cells, with cell viability dropping to 71% at a concentration of 300 µg/mL. This selective cytotoxicity can be attributed to the more acidic TME where accelerated release of Fe3+ significantly promoted the Fenton reaction to generate more ROS. Of note, PND@GOx@Fe-TA exhibited more significant cytotoxicity on tumor cells than PND@Fe-TA, likely owing to GOx-mediated starvation as well as promoted ferroptosis (Fig. 4d and S10b).

The multimodal therapeutic efficacy of PND@GOx@Fe-TA was also evaluated using 4T1 cells to confirm its potential for combined photothermal, starvation, and ferroptosis therapy. As shown in Fig. 4e, PND@GOx@Fe-TA and PND@Fe-TA-treated cells exhibited viability of approximately 57% and 70% without laser irradiation, which can be further reduced upon laser irradiation, demonstrating the synergistic effect of photothermal, starvation, and ferroptosis therapy. The photothermal effect induced by laser irradiation not only directly damages tumor cells but also facilitates the Fenton reaction by increasing the local temperature. To further validate these findings, fluorescent live/dead cell staining was performed (Fig. 4f). Cells treated with PND@GOx@Fe-TA under laser irradiation showed the most remarkable increase in dead cells (stained red) among all treatment groups (Figure S11), confirming the augmented antitumor effects by photothermal/starvation-enhanced ferroptosis therapy.

in vitro and in vivo multimodal imaging profile

The imaging capabilities of PND@GOx@Fe-TA were extensively evaluated, demonstrating its potential as a versatile agent for photoacoustic imaging (PAI), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) imaging. Given the strong photothermal properties of PND@GOx@Fe-TA, it was inferred that the nanocomplex could serve as an excellent PAI agent. As shown in Figure S12, PND@GOx@Fe-TA exhibited a robust PA response in the wavelength range of 700–760 nm, which increased with higher concentrations of PND@GOx@Fe-TA (Fig. 5a and b). In vivo PAI experiments on 4T1 tumor-bearing mice revealed that the PA signal intensity reached the maximum at 6 h post-intravenous injection (Fig. 5c and S13), suggesting the optimal time window for further in vivo studies. These findings highlight PND@GOx@Fe-TA as a highly efficient PAI agent with significant potential for biomedical imaging applications.

Nanoparticles formed through the interaction of Fe3+ and tannic acid (TA) are known to exhibit T1-weighted MRI capabilities. The MRI performance of PND@GOx@Fe-TA was evaluated in vitro, revealing a concentration-dependent increase in MR signals (Fig. 5d). The r1 relaxivity of PND@GOx@Fe-TA was calculated to be 4.21 mM⁻¹ s⁻¹ (Fig. 5e), which is comparable to clinically used Gd-based T1 contrast agents [33]. In vivo T1-weighted MRI of 4T1 tumors demonstrated significant contrast enhancement at 6 h post-injection (Fig. 5f and S14), confirming the potential of PND@GOx@Fe-TA as an effective MRI contrast agent.

The Contrast-Enhanced Ultrasound (CEUS) imaging potential of PND@GOx@Fe-TA was also investigated. In vitro experiments showed that the ultrasound contrast signal intensified with increasing concentrations of PND@GOx@Fe-TA following laser irradiation (Fig. 5g and h). In vivo CEUS imaging of tumors at 6 h post-injection revealed a significant enhancement in gray scale intensity after laser irradiation compared to the pre-irradiation state (Fig. 5i and S15). This enhancement is attributed to the Fe-TA networks’ ability to absorb light energy and convert it into heat, triggering the phase transition of liquid fluorocarbon nanodroplets and generating exceptional ultrasound contrast. Compared with the clinically used ultrasound contrast agent (SonoVue), PND@GOx@Fe-TA exhibited similar ultrasound imaging performance in tumor (Figure S16).

In vivo antitumor activity

The effective in vitro suppression of tumor cell growth by the nanoparticles encouraged us to explore their antitumor efficacy in vivo. First, we evaluated the photothermal performance of PND@GOx@Fe-TA (Fig. 6a and b). PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L exhibited a rapid temperature increase of 9.4 °C, whereas the PBS + L group only led to a slight temperature increase of 2.7 °C. The temperature in PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L rose to 43.5 °C, which is suitable for mild photothermal therapy with minimum damage to the surrounding healthy tissues. Then, the antitumor effect was evaluated by monitoring the changes of tumor volume after different treatments (Fig. 6c). Compared to the PBS group, there was no significant difference in the tumor volume in the PBS + L group, suggesting that laser irradiation alone had no anticancer effect. However, PND@Fe-TA and PND@GOx@Fe-TA exhibited a promising therapeutic efficacy against tumors, attributed to the Fenton reaction catalyzed by the Fe-doped nanoparticles. The starvation therapy induced by GOx enhanced the Fenton reaction by generating more H2O2 and creating a more acidic environment, thereby resulting in a more pronounced therapeutic effect. Furthermore, when subjected to 808 nm laser irradiation, the PND@GOx@Fe-TA group exhibited more pronounced tumor inhibition than all the other groups (Fig. 6d and e), suggesting the enhanced antitumor efficacy via synergistic photothermal therapy and promoted ferroptosis of cancer cells by elevated local temperature.

The biodistribution of PND@GOx@Fe-TA was monitored using Cy5.5 fluorescence. As shown in Figure S17, the Cy5.5-PND@GOx@Fe-TA signal increased over time and peaked at 6 h, indicating maximal tumor accumulation at 6 h. Ex vivo fluorescence was then measured in major organs and tumors collected 24 h post-injection. As shown in Figure S18, Cy5.5-PND@GOx@Fe-TA was highly enriched in the liver and spleen at 24 h, likely reflecting the metabolism of Cy5.5-PND@GOx@Fe-TA by liver and spleen. In addition, we evaluated the excretion profile of PND@GOx@Fe-TA. As shown in Figure S19, Fe was gradually eliminated via feces and urine, indicating efficient clearance from the body and supporting its safety for biomedical applications.

To further validate the ferroptosis-induced antitumor effect, H&E staining and immunohistochemical analysis of GPX4 in tumor sections were investigated (Fig. 6g and S20). The PND@Fe-TA, PND@GOx@Fe-TA, and PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L groups all displayed significant cancer cell death, while negligible tumor damage was observed in the other two groups. Additionally, the GPX4 expression in the PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L group was much lower than the other groups, indicating substantial GPX4 inactivation and most significant ferroptosis in the PND@GOx@Fe-TA-treated group. These results illustrated that the multimodal therapy combining photothermal, starvation, and enhanced ferroptosis was significantly more potent and efficient than individual therapeutic approach. Furthermore, no noticeable changes in weight (Fig. 6f) or histological damage in the major organs (Figure S21) were observed in all mice, suggesting the excellent biocompatibility of the nanoparticles which was also verified by the blood routine analysis (Figure S22) and hemolysis assay (Figure S23). Therefore, the PND@GOx@Fe-TA-mediated synergistic therapy holds great promise for cancer treatment.

Conclusions

In this study, we developed GOx-loaded phase-transition nanodroplets coated with Fe-TA networks (PND@GOx@Fe-TA) for multimodal breast cancer therapy. The encapsulated GOx catalyzed intratumoral glucose into gluconic acid and H2O2, which not only disrupted the tumor cell energy supply, but also enhanced the Fenton reaction by increasing H2O2 levels and creating a more acidic TME. Under 808 nm laser irradiation, PND@GOx@Fe-TA exhibited a mild photothermal effect, which can both destroy cancer cells and increase the Fenton reaction rate, thereby enhancing ferroptosis therapy. Moreover, PND@GOx@Fe-TA showed excellent performance as a multimodal imaging agent for PAI, MRI, and CEUS. As a result, the innovative multifunctional nanodroplets exhibit remarkable potential for clinical cancer diagnosis and therapy.

Materials and methods

Materials

Distearoylphospha-tidylcholine (DSPC), 1,2-distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-N-(methoxy(polyethylene glycol)−2000 (DSPE-PEG2000), 3-(N-(N′,N′-dimethyl-laminoethane)-carbamoyl-cholesterol (DC-CHOL), and Perfluoropentane (PFP) were purchased from Avanti Polar Lipids (Alabaster, USA). Tannic acid (TA) and Iron (III) chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O) were from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Glucose oxidase (GOx), glutathione (GSH), methylene blue (MB), and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2, 30 wt%) were from Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1) and deferoxamine mesylate (DFOM) were from Med Chem Express (Shanghai, China). Cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay, 5,5′-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB), Bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit, 2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA), Calcein-AM, propidium iodide (PI), Glutathione (GSH) assay kit, and Malondialdehyde (MDA) assay kit were from Beyotime Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). GPX4 and GAPDH antibodies were from Proteintech Group, Inc. (Wuhan, China). 4T1 cells (triple negative breast cancer cells), MCF-10 A cells (breast normal cells), fetal bovine serum (FBS), and phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were from Pricella Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China).

Preparation of PND and PND@GOx

The phase-transition nanodroplets (PND) were prepared by using thin-film hydration and ultrasonic emulsification method [34]. DSPE-PEG2000, DC-CHOL and DSPC were mixed together at weight ratio of 2:2:5. Next, the lipid mixture was dissolved in chloroform, and then transferred into a rotary evaporator. The solvent was evaporated at 50 °C with continuous rotation for 2 h, resulting in formation of a lipid thin film. Then, the lipid film was hydrated in 3 mL PBS. Subsequently, 100 µL perfluoropentane (PFP) was added into the lipid film solution, and emulsified with a sonicator (125 W) for 5 min. Finally, PND was obtained after centrifugation. To prepare PND@GOx, 6 mg GOx was also added into the lipid solution before emulsification. The synthesis of Cy5.5-loaded nanoparticles followed the same procedure as for PND@GOx, except that DSPE-PEG2000 was replaced with Cy5.5-DSPE-PEG2000.

Preparation of PND@Fe-TA and PND@GOx@Fe-TA

1 mg PND was dispersed into 2 mL ultrapure water and followed by adding 10 µL tannic acid (TA) solution (40 mg/mL) and 20 µL FeCl3 solution (10 mg/mL), and then under vortex. Afterward, NaOH solution (0.1 mol/L) was added into the solution to neutralize. Subsequently, the mixed solutions were centrifuged to acquire PND@Fe-TA. Finally, the as-prepared PND@Fe-TA was rinsed with ultrapure water for future use. The preparation of PND@GOx@Fe-TA was similar, except for the replacement of PND with PND@GOx.

In vitro Fe release assay

To measure pH-triggered release of Fe, 1 mg PND@GOx@Fe-TA was dispersed in 1 mL PBS under different pHs (6.0 and 7.4). The suspension was dialyzed in 10 mL corresponding buffer medium for 24 h (cut-off 500 Da MW). After that, 1 mL dialysis solution was removed at the selected time interval, and measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, PerkinElmer, USA), meanwhile an equal volume of fresh buffer medium was added into dialysis solution.

In vitro GOx release assay

4 mL PND@GOx@Fe-TA solution (1 mg/mL) was divided into two groups, one group was irradiated with 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2, 5 min) at the selected time interval. Then, the solution was centrifuged after each irradiation, and the content of released GOx in supernatant was quantified through UV-vis spectrophotometer (PerkinElmer, USA) and calculated on the basis of the standard curves. The other group went through the same treatment without laser irradiation.

Detection of gluconic acid generation

Since glucose could be catalyzed to produce gluconic acid and lower pH, we measured the pH of system to detect the gluonic acid generation with or without glucose. First, 2 mL PND@GOx@Fe-TA solution (1 mg/mL) was irradiated with 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2, 5 min). Subsequently, 5 mL glucose solution (1 mg/mL) was added to the above solution, and the pH of system was measured at regular time interval using a pH meter (INESA, China).

Detection of O2 consumption

The O2 consumption was detected by an O2 content meter in 10 mL Hepes buffer. The concentrations of glucose and PND@GOx@Fe-TA were set at 0.1 mg/mL and 0.2 mg/mL, respectively. Prior to measuring the O2 content, PND@GOx@Fe-TA solution was irradiated with 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2, 5 min).

Detection of GSH consumption

PND@GOx@Fe-TA (200 µg/mL) was dispersed in PBS solution (pH 6.0) containing GSH solution (1 mM), and the supernatant was removed at predefined time points (0, 1, 2 and 4 h). After mixing with 5,5′-dithiobis (2-nitrobenzoic acid) (DTNB) solution, the absorbance of the sample was measured by using UV-vis spectrophotometer.

Inspection of •OH generation

The degradation of MB was detected via classical colorimetry to evaluate the hydroxyl radical (•OH) generation ability of PND@GOx@Fe-TA. Firstly, PND@GOx@Fe-TA was dispersed into 5 mL phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) containing H2O2 (10 mM) and MB (5 µg/mL), and incubated at 37 ◦C. After centrifugation, the UV-vis spectrophotometer was used to determine the absorbance of supernatant at 665 nm with different time intervals. Subsequently, the •OH generation ability of PND@GOx@Fe-TA was studied ulteriorly under different pHs (6.0 and 7.4). The PND@GOx@Fe-TA nanocomplexes were similarly dispersed into 5 mL phosphate buffer with different pHs (6.0 and 7.4) containing H2O2 (10 mM) and MB (5 µg/mL), followed by incubation at 37 ◦C for 2 h. Then, the •OH-induced MB degradation was detected by UV-vis spectrophotometer. As a contrast, the MB solution and MB/H2O2 mixture solution were also monitored under the same conditions. Thereafter, PND@GOx@Fe-TA was suspended in 5 mL phosphate buffer (pH 6.0) containing H2O2 (10 mM) and MB (5 µg/mL), and incubated at either 37–43 °C. The absorbance of MB was subsequently determined using a UV-vis spectrophotometer. Finally, we investigated the •OH generation ability of PND@GOx@Fe-TA with glucose. Glucose (1 mg/mL) and MB (10 µg/mL) were added into the PND@GOx@Fe-TA solution and incubated at 37 °C. After centrifugation, the absorbance of supernatant at 665 nm with different time intervals was measured by UV-vis spectrophotometer.

In vitro photothermal property

An 808 nm laser (2 W/cm2) was used to irradiate 200 µL aqueous dispersion of PND@GOx@Fe-TA with different concentrations (0, 75, 150, 300, and 600 µg/mL) for 10 min. Water was used as control. Similarly, PND@GOx@Fe-TA solution at a concentration of 300 µg/mL was irradiated using an 808 nm laser with different irradiation intensities (0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 W/cm2) for 10 min. The temperature was recorded by a thermal infrared camera (E6, Inc, USA) every 30 s. After turning off the laser, the sample was naturally cooling for another 10 min. Three cycles of laser on/off were employed to study the photothermal stability of PND@GOx@Fe-TA. Photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE) of PND@GOx@Fe-TA was calculated according to previously report [35].

Intracellular GSH, MDA, and GPX4 detection

4T1 cells in six-well plates were incubated with (1) Control, (2) Laser, (3) PND@Fe-TA, (4) PND@GOx@Fe-TA, and (5) PND@GOx@Fe-TA + Laser (PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L) for 12 h. For laser groups, an 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2) was employed to irradiate cells for 5 min. The GSH amount was detected using GSH assay kit and malondialdehyde (MDA) amount was evaluated using MDA assay kit. The glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) protein expression was examined by western blot assay.

Intracellular LPO assay

4T1 cells were plated into confocal culture dishes and incubated overnight. Then, the cells were divided into five groups and subjected to the following treatments: (1) Control, (2) Laser, (3) PND@Fe-TA, (4) PND@GOx@Fe-TA, and (5) PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L. Group (2) and (5) were exposed to an 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2) for 5 min. After these treatments, the cells were stained with C11-BODIPY581/591 fluorescent probe for 30 min prior to imaging.

Cellular ferroptosis Inhibition assay

4T1 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate and cultured for 24 h. Then, the cells were co-incubated with PND@GOx@Fe-TA (600 µg/mL) and two types of ferroptosis inhibitors including Liproxstatin-1 (Lip-1) (2, 4, and 8 µM) and deferoxamine mesylate (DFOM) (50, 80, and 100 µM). After another 24 h incubation, the cell viability was measured by CCK-8.

Cellular uptake assay

The cellular uptake of PND@GOx@Fe-TA was confirmed by analyzing the Fe content using ICP-MS. 4T1 cells were seeded in a 12-well plate and incubated overnight. PND@GOx@Fe-TA was added to the cells and incubated for different time periods. After incubation, the cells were rinsed three times with PBS and digested using trypsin. Next, cells were counted and mixed with aqua regia (a mixture of nitric acid and hydrochloric acid in a 1:3 volume ratio) for 24 h. Subsequently, ICP-MS was conducted to quantify the Fe content within each sample. PBS treatment group was used as control.

Cell cytotoxicity assay

Breast cancer cell (4T1) and breast normal cell (MCF-10 A) were used to investigate the cytotoxicity of PND@GOx@Fe-TA. The cells were seeded into 96-well plate and cultured for overnight. Then the fresh medium containing PND@Fe-TA or PND@GOx@Fe-TA at different concentrations (0, 75, 150, 300, and 600 µg/mL) were added to the well and incubated for 24–48 h. Finally, CCK-8 assay was used to determine the cell viability.

To study the therapeutic efficacy of PND@GOx@Fe-TA under laser irradiation, 4T1 cells were seeded in a 96-well plate. Then, the cells were treated with (1) Control, (2) Laser, (3) PND@Fe-TA, (4) PND@GOx@Fe-TA, and (5) PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L. Group (2) and (5) were exposed to an 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2) for 5 min. After different treatments, the cell viability was analyzed by CCK-8.

Intracellular ROS detection

4T1 cells were seeded into 24-well plate and cultured overnight. Subsequently, the cells were divided into five groups: Group 1 incubated with 4T1 cells only; Group 2 incubated with Laser; Group 3 incubated with PND@Fe-TA; Group 4 incubated with PND@GOx@Fe-TA; Group 5 incubated with PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L. Group 2 and 5 were exposed to an 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2) for 5 min. After different treatments, the cells were dyed with 2.7-dichlorofluorescin diacetate (DCFH-DA) for 30 min. The fluorescence of DCF was captured by fluorescence microscope. ImageJ software was used to analyze fluorescence intensity.

In vitro Live/dead cell staining

4T1 cells were seeded into 24-well plate and cultured overnight. Then the cells underwent the same treatment as described in the intracellular ROS Assay and stained by Calcein-AM and PI. Finally, the cells were washed with PBS and observed under fluorescence microscope.

Animal treatment

All animal studies were conducted in accordance with the standards outlined in China’s National Regulations for the Care and Utilization of Laboratory Animals and after approval by the ethical committee of Harbin Medical University Cancer Hospital.

In vitro and in vivo PA, MR, and CEUS imaging

To assess the potential of PND@GOx@Fe-TA as a contrast agent for PA imaging in vitro, PND@GOx@Fe-TA with various concentrations ranging from 25 µg/mL to 250 µg/mL were analyzed using the Vivo LAZR PA imaging system (VisualSonics, Canada). Additionally, BALB/c mice bearing 4T1 tumors were chosen to evaluate the PA imaging performance in vivo. The mice received PND@GOx@Fe-TA via tail vein injection, and PA signals from the tumors were recorded at different time points (0, 2, 6, and 12 h).

To investigate the potential of PND@GOx@Fe-TA as a contrast agent for MRI in vitro, different concentrations of PND@GOx@Fe-TA were tested using a Bruker 9.4T MR scanner (Bruker, Ettlingen, Germany). 4T1 tumor-bearing BALB/c mice were utilized to evaluate the MR imaging property in vivo. The mice were injected with PND@GOx@Fe-TA via the tail vein, and MRI signals from the tumor sites were captured at different time intervals (0 and 6 h).

To study the function of PND@GOx@Fe-TA as a contrast agent for in vitro ultrasound imaging, Contrast enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) images of PND@GOx@Fe-TA were obtained at various concentrations (50, 100, 200, 300, and 400 µg/mL) using a Resona R9 device (Mindray, China). Following exposure of PND@GOx@Fe-TA to an 808 nm laser at 1 W/cm² for 5 min, CEUS images were captured for each concentration group. For in vivo ultrasound imaging, the mice were injected intravenously with PND@GOx@Fe-TA, and tumor images were acquired before and after laser irradiation (808 nm, 1 W/cm2, 10 min). Meanwhile, SonoVue was utilized as a positive control.

In vivo antitumor therapy

4T1 cells were subcutaneously administered into BALB/c mice to establish a tumor model. Once the tumor volume had reached approximately 100 mm3, the mice were randomly assigned to five different groups, including PBS, PBS + L, PND@Fe-TA, PND@GOx@Fe-TA, PND@GOx@Fe-TA + L. Both PND@Fe-TA and PND@GOx@Fe-TA at an equivalent dose of 100 µL were administered via the tail vein with a concentration of 1 mg/mL. After 6 h of injection, the mice were irradiated with an 808 nm laser (1 W/cm2, 10 min). A thermal infrared camera was used to record the photothermal images and temperatures at various points. The tumor volumes and body weights were recorded every other day for 14 days. The tumor volume (mm³) was calculated using the formula: V = (L × W2)/2, where W represents the minimum diameter and L represents the maximum diameter. The relative tumor volume was determined by calculating the ratio V/V0, where V0 denotes the tumor volume prior to treatment and V denotes the tumor volume following treatment.

In vivo distribution and excretion study

To assess the biodistribution, tumor-bearing BALB/c mice were injected with Cy5.5-PND@GOx@Fe-TA via tail vein and imaged at 2, 4, 6, 12, and 24 h. The mice were then euthanized at 24 h for ex vivo imaging of organs and tumor tissue.

To evaluate the excretion of PND@GOx@Fe-TA, the mice were intravenously administered PND@GOx@Fe-TA and then housed in metabolic cages to facilitate sample collection. Urine and feces were collected, digested with aqua regia, and analyzed by ICP-MS.

In vivo histological and hematological examinations

At the end of treatment, the tumor-bearing mice were euthanized at 14 days post-injection, and mouse tumors were then collected for photographing and subsequent staining with Hematoxylin and Eosin (H&E) as well as GPX4. The staining intensity of GPX4 immunohistochemical was graded as negative (score 0), weak (score 1), moderate (score 2), or strong (score 3). The H‑score for each slide was computed as ∑pi × i, where “pi” denoted the percentage of cells at intensity level “i”, and “i” was the corresponding staining intensity. Additionally, the vital organs, including the heart, liver, spleen, lung, and kidney, were sectioned and stained with H&E to assess the histological alterations induced by the various treatments.

Fresh blood collected from post-treated mice on the 14th day was subjected to routine hematological analysis using an automated hematology analyzer. Additionally, the hemolysis study was performed to evaluate biosafety. Briefly, mice fresh blood was centrifuged, and the red blood cells were resuspended in either normal saline (negative control), distilled water (positive control), or varying concentrations of PND@GOx@Fe-TA. All samples were incubated for 2 h and then centrifuged at 1500 g for 10 min. The supernatant’s absorbance at 545 nm was measured using a UV-vis spectrophotometer, and the hemolysis rate was calculated using the following formula: hemolysis rate (%) = (sample absorbance − saline absorbance)/(water absorbance − saline absorbance) × 100%.

Statistical analysis

All data were described as mean ± standard deviation (SD). A one-way ANOVA was used to analyze the significant difference among multiple groups. Besides, paired t-test was conducted for comparison with pairs of groups (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, ****p < 0.0001).
