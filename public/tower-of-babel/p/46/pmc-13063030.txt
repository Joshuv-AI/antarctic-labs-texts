Influence of cage material, plating and vertebral bone quality on subsidence and long-term outcomes following anterior cervical discectomy and fusion—a retrospective analysis
Authors: Taylor Moglia, Ara Khoylyan, Jason Salvato, Frank Vazquez, Arpitha Pamula, Alex Tang, Tan Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAnterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a surgical procedure used to treat degenerative conditions of the cervical spine. Cage subsidence is a known post-operative complication, resulting in loss of indirect decompression, deformity, and radicular symptoms. The objectives of this study are to: (I) investigate associations between cage-plate constructs and postoperative subsidence after single-level ACDF; (II) determine relationships between cervical vertebral bone quality (C-VBQ) scores, subsidence, and fusion construct; and (III) identify potential risk factors that may help guide patient outcomes.MethodsRadiographic subsidence was defined as a collapse of one-third or greater of cage height. C-VBQ scores were measured from C2–C7 on preoperative T1-weighted magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) images. Comparative analysis was performed between cage material groups. Pre- and post-operative patient reported outcome measures (PROMs) were analyzed.ResultsSubsidence was present in 22 of 117 patients. Subsidence was not associated with plate usage (P=0.78) or cage type (0.520). C-VBQ score was associated with a significantly increased odds of cage subsidence [odds ratio (OR) 15.264, P<0.001]. In the total population, plates were associated with decreased odds of returning to the operating room (OR 0.196, P=0.049) and lower rate of adjacent segment disease (P=0.046), despite no difference in post-operative PROM scores. In the polyetheretherketone (PEEK) plate sub-group, while there was no difference in subsidence rate (P=0.40), there were higher mean C-VBQ scores (P=0.041) and a lower rate of patient-reported outcomes measurement information system (PROMIS) minimal clinically important difference (MCID) achievement (P=0.041).ConclusionsSingle-level standalone and cage-plate ACDF constructs demonstrated similar rates of post-operative subsidence. Subsidence rates were also similar between PEEK, titanium, and structural allograft cages. For PEEK constructs, plate use may have a potential protective mechanism against subsidence with worsening bone mineral density. Plate use may also demonstrate lower odds risk of returning to the operating room. These findings can better inform decision-making and post-surgical planning for ACDF surgery.Keywords: Subsidence, anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF), vertebral bone quality (VBQ), plate, standalone

Highlight box

Key findings

• Subsidence occurred in 22 of 117 patients (18.8%).

• Subsidence was not associated with cage type or plate usage.

• Higher cervical vertebral bone quality (C-VBQ) scores were strongly associated with increased subsidence (odds ratio 15.264, P<0.001).

• In polyetheretherketone (PEEK) constructs, plate usage may have a potential protective mechanism against subsidence in patients with worsening bone mineral density.

What is known and what is new?

• Cage subsidence is a common complication after anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) and is influenced by cage design, plate presence, and bone mineral density.

• This study identifies C-VBQ as a predictor of subsidence, independent of cage material or plate usage. It also highlights a potential protective role of PEEK plates for patients with worsening bone mineral density.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• These findings highlight that cage subsidence following single-level ACDF was not influenced by cage material or plate use but may be predicted by preoperative bone mineral density. Surgeons should consider preoperative bone mineral density as indicated by C-VBQ, as well as construct type and material when planning ACDF procedures.

Introduction

Anterior cervical discectomy and fusion (ACDF) is a well-established surgical procedure used to treat degenerative conditions of the cervical spine (1-3). ACDF is typically performed by incorporating an intervertebral cage made of either bone allograft, autograft, polyetheretherketone (PEEK), or titanium alloy with or without a plate system between one or multiple vertebrae to promote stability and redistribute the load of stress placed on the spine (4-7). A postoperative complication that may arise from ACDF procedures is cage subsidence, which may result in neurologic stenosis, recurrence of radiculopathy, deformity, and neck pain (8,9).

Cage subsidence is defined as the settling of an interbody into adjacent vertebral bodies and can be measured based on the loss of disc height on lateral spine radiographs. It can further be classified or graded based on its severity, with severe subsidence being linked to increased rates of reoperation and recurrence of preoperative symptoms by leading to loss of disc height, instability, and even compression of neural structures (10,11). Subsidence may be influenced by various factors such as cage location, cervical alignment, age, use of plates, type of cage utilized, bone resorption and graft collapse, and bone mineral density (10-14).

Designs for cage materials have changed drastically over time and have evolved into the currently used titanium, allograft, or non-threaded, wedge-shaped PEEK cages (15). Bone grafts including allograft or less commonly, autograft, can be used and are linked to high rates of patient satisfaction (16). Additionally, the decision to use a standalone or a cage-plate construct typically comes down to surgeon preference and other factors such as bone mineral density, deformity, and severity of pathology, since there are mixed results on the utility of both constructs in the literature (17-20). For degenerative cervical pathologies, evidence suggests that cage-plate systems are beneficial for patients with multi-level degenerative pathologies to obtain better mechanical stability, whereas standalones may be more beneficial for patients with higher comorbidities due to lower complication rates, and may provide increased rates of resorption when using allograft bone (21).

Bone mineral density is another important factor to consider when choosing a graft and construct, as lower bone mineral density may be linked to a higher occurrence of cage subsidence following ACDF (22). Some modalities commonly used to assess bone mineral density include dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DEXA), computer tomography (CT) techniques, and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) techniques (23). Recently, there is increasing evidence that a specific metric, the vertebral bone quality (VBQ) score, has utility in assessing bone mineral density and may be a metric to predict postoperative subsidence in ACDF procedures (11,23).

This study aims to investigate the association between standalone and cage-plate constructs with the occurrence of cage subsidence after a single-level ACDF for degenerative pathology. Specifically, we sought to determine the association between the use of allograft bone or synthetic cage-plate systems and postoperative subsidence risk. As a second aim, we sought to determine any correlation between the VBQ scores and the use of allograft bone and synthetic cage-plate systems, and if there is a correlation for increased risk of subsidence. Finally, our third objective was to identify risk factors using clinical and radiographic data that may help provide valuable insight to guide surgical planning, postoperative management, and patient outcomes in cervical spine surgery. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jss.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jss-2025-aw-196/rc).

Methods

A retrospective analysis was performed to identify patients who underwent an elective single-level ACDF between November 2019 and April 2023. Patients were identified using current procedural terminology (CPT) code 22551 and subsequently screened for inclusion. Inclusion criteria were as follows: patients of at least 18 years of age, who had a single-level ACDF procedure, with evidence of degenerative disc disease at those levels, and had available pre-operative MRI and intra-operative or immediate post-operative radiographic imaging for review. Patients were excluded if they had a non-degenerative diagnosis, history of revision cervical fusion surgery, multilevel ACDF procedures, corpectomy, most recent follow up less than a month after surgery, and pre-operative MRI studies greater than two years prior to the surgery. Data was collected through manual chart review which included patient demographic information such as age, body mass index (BMI), substance use (tobacco and alcohol), and gender. Surgical data including level of fusion, use of a plate during index ACDF procedure, and intervertebral cage material type (PEEK, titanium, bone graft, or combined) were also collected. PEEK and titanium cages were classified as “Synthetic” and allograft were classified as “Biological”. All standalone constructs had some form of bone fixation, including screws or sharp anchors.

Imaging analysis

After a patient was deemed eligible for inclusion, their immediate post-operative and latest follow up weight bearing lateral radiograph (>1-month post-operative) was evaluated to determine if they had evidence of cage subsidence. The cage material used was also collected from the operative chart note. Elapsed time between radiographs was measured and included in multivariate analysis. Cage subsidence was assessed using a 4-grade system based on cage position and vertebral body overlap as described by Yamagata et al. (24) (Figure 1). On this grading scale, no subsidence is defined as grade 0, less than one-third subsidence is defined as 1, greater than one-third but less than two-third subsidence as 2, and greater than two-third subsidence as 3. Grades 0 and 1 were classified as no subsidence, while grades 2 and 3 were classified as radiographic subsidence. If a patient had a grade of 2 or 3 on the immediate post-operative radiograph, they were excluded from analysis.

Sample determination of post-operative subsidence grade based on difference in pre-operative and post-operative subsidence measurements. In this case, determined to be Grade 3 severity due to greater than two-third decrease in intervertebral disk height.

The second radiographic variable measured was the cervical vertebral bone quality (C-VBQ) score of the cervical spine. This is a measurement described by Ehresman et al. (25) in which T1 weighted MRIs are analyzed by highlighting regions of interest in the medullary bone of each vertebra (C2–C7) and in the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) in the cisterna magna, inferior to the cerebellum as demonstrated by the figure (Figure 2). The region of interest (ROI) for vertebral assessment was 20–40 mm2 and for CSF was 10–15 mm2 (26). This study utilized T1-weighted MRIs, rather than T2-weighted MRIs, as it allowed us to visualize bone marrow and fat content within the bone marrow more precisely, which was critical for the assessment and quantification of C-VBQ. Vertebrae that had lesions or fractures were omitted from the analysis. C-VBQ is calculated by dividing the median signal intensity of C2–C7 by the signal intensity of the CSF space.

All radiographic measurements were made using tools native to the hospital’s picture archiving and communication system (PACS). C-VBQ and subsidence grades were measured by two independent raters (J.S. and T.M.). Any discrepancies or complex radiographic measurements were confirmed with a fellowship-trained orthopaedic spine surgeon. An intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) of 0.81 was calculated, ensuring reliability amongst the observers.

Patient reported outcome measures (PROM)

Scores for the Neck Disability Index (NDI) and patient-reported outcomes measurement information system (PROMIS) Global Health, Physical Health, and Mental Health were collected pre-operatively and post-operatively at one year. Minimal clinically important difference (MCID) was set at eight-point improvement in the NDI surveys and ten-point improvement in PROMIS surveys based on prior established literature (27,28).

Statistical analysis

Patients were divided into plate and standalone cohorts. Demographic data, follow up time, surgical data (length of stay, blood loss), cage material type, PROMs, post-operative cage subsidence, and C-VBQ scores were compared between the two groups using Student’s t-test for continuous data and chi-squared for categorical data. Multivariate and univariate logistic regression analysis was completed between the C-VBQ, cage type, and plate presence as the independent variables and subsidence as the dependent variable to determine the odds ratio (OR). Covariates in this analysis included age, gender, BMI, substance use (alcohol or tobacco), and follow-up time, in order to ensure outcome reliability. PROMIS and NDI scores were compared between the plate and standalone group using a Student’s t-test. Further sub-analysis integrating these components was performed on the groups with PEEK, titanium, and bone graft cage types. P-values ≤0.05 were determined to be statistically significant. All statistical calculations were completed using SPSS v25 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY).

Ethical considerations

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Geisinger Institutional Review Board (No. 00008345) and individual consent for this retrospective analysis was waived.

Results

Population characteristics

A total of 117 patients undergoing elective single-level ACDF met inclusion criteria for the study. The population was 62% female, with a mean age of 54.7±12.6 years, BMI of 31.2±6.5. Average post-operative follow-up time was 15.4±12.4 months. The mean C-VBQ score was 2.3±0.5. There was a mean intra-operative blood loss of 31.2±111.1 mL and post-operative length of stay of 2.2±1.5 days. Standalone cages were utilized in 43% of cases (50/117). Radiographic subsidence was present in 19% of the population (22/117). The prevalence of each subsidence grade was 39% [0], 42% [1], 16% [2], 3% [3]. Titanium cages were utilized in 45 cases (39%), PEEK cages in 30 cases (26%), and allograft in 42 cases (35%) (Table 1). No cases utilized autograft cages.

Risk and detection of cage subsidence

C-VBQ was significantly higher in the subsidence cohort (No subsidence: 2.2±0.5, Subsidence: 2.7±0.6; P<0.001) (Table 2). C-VBQ was associated with significantly greater odds of radiographic subsidence on multivariate analysis with age, BMI, sex, and tobacco/alcohol use as covariates {Multivariate OR [confidence interval (CI)] =15.3 [3.4–62.6], P<0.001} (Table 8). Plate usage and cage material type did not demonstrate any association with subsidence based on univariate and multivariate analyses (Table 8). A C-VBQ score of 2.1 demonstrated 90% sensitivity and 39% specificity for detecting subsidence [area under the curve (AUC)=0.747] (Table 9).

Plate usage vs. cage subsidence

In the total population, there was no statistically significant difference in rate of subsidence between the two cohorts (Standalone: 18%, Plate: 20%; P=0.78). C-VBQ score was significantly higher in the plate cohort (Standalone: 2.2, Plate: 2.4; P=0.02) (Table 3).

Upon sub-analysis of individual cage types, the PEEK group had a significantly greater C-VBQ score in the plate cohort (Standalone: 1.7, Plate: 2.4; P=0.04), though there was no difference in rate of subsidence between the two cohorts in the PEEK group (Standalone 0%, Plate: 15%; P=0.40) (Table 5).

No significant differences in C-VBQ scores and subsidence rates were observed between plate and standalone cohorts in the titanium [Standalone C-VBQ: 2.2, Plate C-VBQ: 2.1; P=0.81) (Standalone Subsidence Rate: 19%, Plate Subsidence Rate: 0%; P=0.41)] and allograft [Standalone C-VBQ: 2.2, Plate C-VBQ: 2.5; P=0.25) (Standalone Subsidence Rate: 25%, Plate Subsidence Rate: 24%; P=0.95)] cohorts (Tables 4,6).

Plate usage vs. post-operative complications

Discussion

This study provides insights into the long-term outcomes associated with standalone vs. cage-plate constructs in single-level ACDF, particularly regarding cage subsidence and functional recovery. Subsidence remains a challenging complication in ACDF, as it can compromise spinal alignment, lead to recurrent symptoms, and affect patient quality of life (14,27,29,30). Our findings suggest that the presence of a cage-plate construct does not appear to reduce the risk of cage subsidence following ACDF and does not confer improved subjective post-operative outcomes as determined by one-year NDI and PROMIS surveys.

Comparing our findings to existing literature, we see a complex relationship between construct type, cage material, and subsidence risk. Previous research has documented varying rates of subsidence among different cage materials. Although titanium cages are commonly used for cervical interbody fusion, there is a risk of several complications including migration, structure failure, and subsidence (28,31) with some studies reporting higher rates of subsidence with the use of titanium cages compared to PEEK cages (32). Cancellous allograft has historically been used in the field of orthopedics, but PEEK cages are showing more promising results recently due to their biocompatibility and comparable elasticity to that of allograft bone (33). However, some studies suggest that PEEK cages may be more prone to subsidence than titanium or allograft due to differences in modulus of elasticity (34,35). Despite mixed findings in the literature, our results did not indicate any significant difference in subsidence rates across cage materials. These findings suggest that factors beyond cage material, such as bone mineral density and construct stability, may play a more dominant role in determining subsidence risk. For the assessment of differences in bone mineral density, C-VBQ scores were measured utilizing pre-operative T1-weighted MRI studies. To better understand the impact of construct stability, the use of cage-plate constructs was assessed. Given that standalone cages have gained popularity due to the reduced need for less prominent anterior hardware and shorter surgical times, the biomechanical implications of standalones in comparison to cage-plate constructs warrant consideration. For instance, the lack of supplemental fixation in standalone cages may contribute to micro-instability at the fusion segment, potentially leading to subsidence (36). However, biomechanical studies have shown that cage-plate constructs provide greater initial stability and are associated with higher vertebral fusion rates and lower subsidence rates compared to standalone cages (37-39). Although our findings indicate that there were no significant differences regarding subsidence in the plate or the standalone cohorts, these relationships warrant further investigation with reference to bone mineral density.

Our assessment of the relationship between C-VBQ and post-operative cage subsidence yielded findings comparable to those by Soliman et al. (11), who also demonstrated that bone mineral density is a critical factor in ACDF outcomes, with higher VBQ scores correlating with greater subsidence risk. In our study, the plate cohort had a significantly greater mean C-VBQ score than the standalone cohort in the total patient population group (2.4 vs. 2.2, P=0.02) and in the PEEK sub-group (2.4 vs. 1.7, P=0.04), despite equal rates of subsidence. C-VBQ values were equal between the standalone and plate cohorts in the titanium and allograft cage sub-groups; both cage material groups also demonstrated equal subsidence rates between the two cohorts as well. We noted a C-VBQ threshold of 1.7 to be 19% specific and 2.4 to be 61% specific for detecting subsidence in our population. Those with a C-VBQ ≥2.4 demonstrated significantly greater odds of developing subsidence than those below that threshold, with a Youden’s index of 0.416. Though prior studies have recommended C-VBQ thresholds for subsidence risk that markedly exceed this, with values ranging from 2.68 to 2.70 (23,40,41). These findings may suggest that cage-plate constructs may potentially be protective of cage subsidence in those with PEEK cages, while those with allograft or titanium cage-plate configurations do not experience a similar benefit, and this relationship ought to be further explored.

Interestingly, in the PEEK sub-group, only patients in the plate cohort developed subsidence despite findings suggesting a protective mechanism with plate use. This was likely a function of the significantly smaller portion of patients having standalone constructs in this group (N=4 vs. 26), which is also a limitation of our findings. The rate of subsidence between the cohorts was ultimately statistically similar, as mentioned previously. Lack of available comparison between the two cohorts in the PEEK group did however limit further evaluation of association between plate usage and subsidence, though it is reasonable to assume that, given the significant disparities in C-VBQ despite equal rates of subsidence, controlling for C-VBQ can help demonstrate a negative association between plate usage and post-operative subsidence in those with PEEK cages. In sum, we surmise that cage-plate constructs are not protective of cage subsidence with allograft or titanium cages in patients of similar bone mineral density. Further research would be warranted evaluating the effect on those with differing bone qualities as assessed by VBQ, T-score, or CT Hounsfield unit (HU) measures. Additionally, a larger population would be necessary to definitively substantiate the protective effect of PEEK cage-plate constructs against subsidence in those with poorer bone mineral density.

Regarding reoperation rates and postoperative complications, cage-plate constructs may be associated with a lower risk of postoperative complications including ASD and adjacent segment disease (42-44). Current recommendations are that cervical plates be placed at least 5 mm away from adjacent disc spaces to decrease the likelihood of the development of adjacent-level ossification and ASD (44,45). Samartzis et al. (46) assessed single-level non-plated and plated ACDF procedures with autograft and discovered that none of the non-plated patients required reoperation whereas 9.7% of the plated patients underwent reoperation. However, they found no statistically significant difference between non-plating and rigid plating, as well as comparable clinical outcomes between the two cohorts which were assessed on the last follow-up using the Cervical Spine Outcomes Questionnaire and rated as excellent, good, fair, or poor based on Odom’s criteria (46). Wu et al. (47) discovered that ASD was higher in the anterior cervical plate and cage (ACPC) cohort compared to the usage of a zero-profile anchored space (ROI-C) cohort (65.5% vs. 44.0%, P=0.03). Additionally, other factors found to increase the risk of developing ASD included a low preoperative Cobb angle, a low preoperative segmental angle, and loss of Cobb angle. Our findings demonstrated the opposite, since we found that patients with cage-plate constructs were less likely to need revision surgery in the composite patient population, specifically demonstrating a lower rate of return for adjacent segment fusion. Due to the limited sample size of patients that required reoperation, as well as a majority of the constructs including plates, the correlation between standalone and cage-plate constructs with respect to nonunion rates needs to be explored further with a larger sample size.

Although the non-union rate was equal between plated and standalone cohorts in the composite group, those in the allograft sub-group with a standalone construct seemed to demonstrate a significantly lower rate of non-union following ACDF. However, sample size was small with a total of four patients with allograft standalone constructs. Our findings oppose findings from Cohn et al. (48), where propensity scoring was utilized to assess reoperation rates between stand-alone structural allograft and plate-secured structural allograft construct cohorts. They discovered that the use of anterior cage-plate constructs is associated with higher fusion rates and lower subsidence rates compared to the use of stand-alone cage constructs. Although there was radiographic evidence of subsidence, there were no significant differences in reoperation rates between the two cohorts (48).

We also investigated the association between cage material type and re-operation, discovering that those with a titanium cage had a significantly greater rate of returning to the operating room than their PEEK counterparts. None of the thirty patients with a PEEK cage required revision surgery, while six (13%) patients with a titanium cage returned. Those with a titanium cage were particularly prone to re-operation due to degenerative adjacent segment disease. Overall, synthetic cages were trending towards a significantly lower likelihood of requiring revision surgery in total, and more specifically due to non-union, in comparison to allograft constructs. This trend was largely influenced by the PEEK group, given that re-operation rates between those with titanium and allograft cages were equal. These findings are in line with the prior literature which discusses the risk of migration, structure failure, and subsidence with the use of titanium cages compared to PEEK cages (28,31,32). This may potentially be due to PEEK cages having higher biocompatibility and comparable elasticity to bone, although there is some evidence that the elasticity modulus may make PEEK cages more prone to subsidence compared to titanium or allograft bone (34,35).

While one-year post-operative PROM scores were equal between two cohorts, pre-operative scores on several surveys including NDI, PROMIS Global Overall Health Scale, and PROMIS Global Mental Health Scale were significantly more favorable in the plate cohort than the standalone cohort. In the PEEK sub-group, patients with standalone constructs demonstrated a significantly greater one-year post-operative MCID achievement rate with the PROMIS Global Mental Health Scale than those with cage-plate constructs. This was likely due to the significantly poorer mean pre-operative survey score. These findings suggest that patient-reported outcomes up to one year following ACDF are not statistically significant whether a cervical plate is used or not. Furthermore, patients who are more likely to get a standalone construct have worse pre-operative subjective scores on average yet reach equal subjective health status post-operatively. While the implications of our findings are limited due to small sample sizes, these preliminary results are in line with prior established literature which has demonstrated that plate use does not significantly impact post-operative PROM scores including NDI, and in fact has shown lower rates of MCID achievement for neck pain and disability, which may be due to factors such as a longer recovery time due to the additional operative trauma of anterior plating, decreased segmental range of motion leading to increased neck stiffness, and the possibility of early symptoms of adjacent disc level degeneration (49).

Tabanli et al. discovered that although postoperative disc height increased in patients who underwent ACDF vs. cervical disc replacement, heterotopic ossification was lower in patients who underwent ACDF (50). These findings highlight the prevalence and clinical impact of heterotopic ossification, which may potentially limit segmental range of motion and long-term mobility, providing valuable biomechanical context for interpreting postoperative subsidence, long-term stability, and plating strategies in cervical spine surgery. Although heterotopic ossification was not directly assessed in our study, these findings provide valuable additional insight for surgical considerations.

From a clinical standpoint, these findings highlight the importance of individualized treatment planning, particularly in patients with compromised bone mineral density. For patients with elevated VBQ scores and thus lower bone density, a cage-plate construct may be preferable despite the lack of overall reduction in subsidence risk, as it may provide other biomechanical benefits that translate to improved patient outcomes. Conversely, in patients with adequate bone mineral density, the choice between standalone and cage-plate constructs might be guided more by surgeon preference and the specific anatomical and biomechanical requirements of each case. This personalized approach can balance the benefits and risks of each construct type and thus optimize patient outcomes.

Limitations

Despite the strengths of this study, including a well-defined cohort and detailed subgroup analyses, several limitations should be noted. As a retrospective study, there is a risk of selection bias, and the findings may not be fully generalizable to populations outside the single-level ACDF cases for degenerative pathology considered here. The data was extracted over the course of a four-year period in one institution located primarily in a rural setting, which limits the sample size and generalizability to broader populations. Additionally, the reliance on radiographic criteria for subsidence, while widely accepted, may not capture the full functional implications of cage migration over time. Continual follow-up images were not available for all patients, which further limited the sample size. Of the patients who did meet inclusion criteria, most did not undergo DEXA scanning prior to surgery, which served as another limitation since DEXA scans can provide a more comprehensive assessment of bone mineral density. Additionally, there was a high variability with follow-up time. Although this was factored in as a covariate in regression analysis, this ought to be taken into consideration due to the potential impact on outcome variability. Another limitation is the small sample size for the PROM comparisons. It is also important to acknowledge that variability in subjective assessment could affect the consistency of our findings since clinical subsidence grading was performed by five independent reviewers. Although an intraclass correlation analysis was conducted to assess inter-rater reliability and was deemed acceptable, this introduces another potential limitation of our study. Future research would be valuable to further explore these relationships and to assess whether alternative strategies—such as novel cage designs or adjunctive therapies to enhance bone mineral density—might better address the challenges posed by poor bone density in the cervical spine.

Conclusions

Cage subsidence following single-level ACDF is not influenced by cage material or plate use. We recommend further exploration into the usage of plates in PEEK constructs and its potential for protection against subsidence. ACDF plates do demonstrate protection against non-union and adjacent segment disease. However, functional outcomes, measured by one-year NDI and PROMIS surveys, are comparable between standalone and cage-plate constructs, reinforcing the need for individualized treatment planning based on patient-specific factors. These findings suggest that a combination of personalized construct selection, considering bone mineral density and biomechanical needs, and appropriate patient counseling on expectations can help optimize long-term outcomes in ACDF procedures.
