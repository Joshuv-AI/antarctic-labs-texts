Controlling the structural, optical, electrical, and compositional properties of silica-graphite core-shell for supercapacitor applications
Authors: Nguyen Tam Nguyen Truong, Nguyen Hoang Lam, Jae-Hak Jung, Hamid Shaikh, Arfat Anis, Mohaseen S Tamboli, Jinjoo Jung, Chang-Duk Kim, Younjung Jo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe graphite-silica (SiO2@GP) core-shell particles were formed via thermal chemical vapor deposition at 1100 °C. The synthesis’s growth times were varied at 1, 5, 10, 30, and 60 min. The properties of SiO2@GP particles with different growth times were systematically analyzed and investigated. At a growth time of 10 min, major SiO2 peaks and the principal of the graphene were observed. The SiO2 particles and thin GP sheets covering their surfaces are clearly observed. An optimized SiO2@GP structure with a growth time of 10 min (SiO2@GP-10) was subsequently employed as an electrode in a three-electrode supercapacitor configuration. Electrochemical performance was evaluated using cyclic voltammetry, galvanostatic charge–discharge, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy analyses. At a current density of 1 A g− 1, the SiO2@GP-10 composite achieved a specific capacitance of approximately 291 F g− 1 and maintained over 40.14% of its capacitance at higher current densities.Keywords: Core–shell structures, Supercapacitors, Specific capacitance, Thermal chemical vapor deposition, Specific capacity, ElectrochemicalSubject terms: Chemistry, Energy science and technology, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

Silica (SiO2) is a naturally occurring compound and one of the most abundant materials on Earth. It exists in various forms, including crystalline SiO2, SiO2 gel, SiO2 fumes, amorphous SiO2, and precipitated SiO21. Depending on the desired properties—such as particle size, sharpness, and porosity—different methods can be used to synthesize SiO2. Some common examples include sol-gel synthesis, precipitation, and simple green synthesis2–4.

SiO2 is highly valued for its excellent properties, including its large surface area, good chemical stability, and superior electrical insulation capabilities. Given these properties, it has found widespread applications across diverse domains. For example, SiO2 gel is often used as an adsorbent and desiccant due to its porosity and large surface area5. Furthermore, SiO2 serves as a support for metal catalysts, such as platinum, in hydro generation reactions6. In the semiconductor industry, it is used as an insulating material due to its high dielectric strength and dielectric properties7.

Graphene (GP) has a single layer of carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice. It is considered one of the most promising materials due to its unique properties. Depending on the desired quality, scalability, and properties, GP can be synthesized through various methods. Prominent examples include mechanical exfoliation8, chemical vapor deposition (CVD)9, liquid-phase exfoliation10, and high-pressure high-temperature synthesis11. GP exhibits many superior characteristics, making it highly valuable for advanced technologies. For instance, GP is an excellent conductor of electricity, attributed to the extremely high mobility of its charge carriers. It is the strongest material known, with a tensile strength more than 100 times greater than that of steel. It is also lightweight and flexible12. GP is also nearly transparent and has a large surface area and excellent thermal conductivity13,14.

The exceptional properties of GP enable its use in various applications, including transparent conductive films15, electronics thermal management systems16, touch screens17, solar cells18, and other optoelectronic devices. A single layer of GP exhibits an extremely high surface area, making it suitable for sensors, batteries, and supercapacitors19. Ni nanoparticles were directly incorporated into graphene nanosheets through mechanical ball milling20,21. The Ni NPs were well dispersed and attached to the GP nanosheet without any agglomerations. The Ni@GP electrode showed superior performance at high current densities, exhibiting a capacitance of 190 F g− 1 at a current density of 5 A g− 1 and a capacitance of 144 F g− 1 at a current density of 10 A g− 1. Graphene is an excellent candidate for energy storage device applications due to its exceptionally high theoretical specific surface area. However, its practical application is hampered by its inherent hydrophobicity and strong π-π stacking tendency (re-stacking)22. In contrast, the incorporation of hydrophilic silica (SiO2) fundamentally reconfigures the electrode-electrolyte interface. In SiO2@GP composite materials, SiO2 nanoparticles act as strategic “nano-cushioning agents” to inhibit graphene aggregation, maintaining a stable 3D porous structure. This structural change significantly enhances the wettability of the electrolyte by reducing the contact angle between surfaces, thereby maximizing the electrochemically active surface area. Combining SiO2 and GP into a core-shell composite structure creates a promising material for next-generation electronic devices, particularly for energy storage and supercapacitor applications. Supercapacitors are electrochemical capacitors that store energy through the accumulation of electrostatic charge, offering advantages such as high-power density, fast charge/discharge rates, and long cycle stability. The combination of two or more materials can bring stability to the supercapacitor’s performance because of the more stable charge storage mechanisms, such as the hard/soft CoFe2O4/NiSc0.03 Fe1.97O4 nanocomposite or quasi-solid-state bio-polymer23,24. SiO2@GP core-shell composite materials are considered promising candidates for supercapacitor applications due to the unique properties of both materials.

In this study, SiO2@GP was synthesized using thermal CVD. Its structural, optical, and electrical properties have been comprehensively characterized. The influence of growth time on these characteristics has been systematically studied. Based on the results obtained, SiO2@GP grown at 1100 °C for 10 min was selected for electrode fabrication for supercapacitor applications.

Results and discussion

The morphologies of SiO2@GP samples synthesized at different growth durations were characterized using SEM, as depicted in Fig. 1. When the growth time increases, the surface area created by the number of graphene layers increases. helps reduce excessive aggregation of the SiO2@GP core-shell structure. The findings revealed that the granular morphology of SiO2 particles was retained throughout the synthesis process (Fig. 1a). At shorter growth times (approximately 1–5 min), GP did not fully graphitize or develop a well-organized layer, as depicted in Fig. 1b, c. Under these conditions, more disordered GP structures with a higher concentration of oxygen-containing functional groups were present. Similarly, these short synthesis durations resulted in incomplete formation of the SiO2 matrix or the production of smaller SiO2 particles. In contrast, longer growth times (10, 30, and 60 min) promoted more complete graphitization, leading to the formation of a more robust SiO2 network (Fig. 1d, e, f). Over time, the particles were uniformly enclosed by a thin graphite coating, confirming the successful deposition of multilayered GP. The structural and compositional changes in SiO2@GP with varying growth times were further investigated using XRD and XPS.

SEM images of the synthesized SiO2@GP composites at different growth times: (a) before growth, (b) 1 min, (c) 5 min, (d) 10 min, (e) 30 min, and (f) 60 min.

The structural characteristics of the SiO2/graphite composite were confirmed using XRD, as presented in Fig. 2. The resulting XRD pattern corresponded closely to the reference standard for SiO2/graphite composites (Joint Committee on Powder Diffraction Standards card no. 01–086-1269). The XRD results demonstrated that variations in growth time did not alter the crystalline structure of SiO2. The primary diffraction peak of SiO2 typically appears at 2θ = 26.6°, corresponding to the (100) plane, while additional peaks at 2θ = 20.8° and 2θ = 36.5° are attributed to the (101) and (110) planes, respectively. The presence of graphite is indicated by a distinct peak at approximately 2θ = 26.5°, corresponding to the (002) plane, associated with the interlayer spacing between GP sheets or graphite layers. However, given the close proximity of the SiO2 (2θ = 26.6°) and graphite (26.5°) peaks, distinguishing the graphite signal after different growth times is challenging. Additional insights were subsequently obtained through XPS analysis.

(a) XRD patterns of the synthesized SiO2@GP composites at growth times of 1, 5, 10, 30, and 60 min. (b) Variation in XRD peak intensities as a function of growth time.

The elemental composition and chemical and electronic states of SiO2@GP composites synthesized at growth times of 1, 5, 10, 30, and 60 min were analyzed using XPS, as presented in Fig. 3. This analysis yielded valuable insights into the bonding interactions and surface chemistry between SiO2 and GP. Notably, the Si 2p, C 1 s, and O 1 s XPS spectra of SiO2@GP samples synthesized at various growth durations displayed several characteristic features. First, a pronounced peak appeared at approximately 284.3 eV in the C 1 s region, corresponding to C–C bonding within GP. The shape and intensity of this peak offered insights into the degree of functionalization or oxidation of the GP surface. Second, peaks were observed in the 532–533 eV range in the O 1 s region, corresponding to Si–O bonds within SiO2. Finally, peaks appeared between 103 and 104 eV in the Si 2p region, attributed to Si–O bonding within SiO2. Figure 3a‒c depicts the influence of growth time on the formation of SiO2/GP composites. As the growth time increased from 1 to 60 min, pronounced Si–O and Si–C–O bond peaks emerged. At a growth time of 10 min, major SiO2 peaks were observed at 103 eV (Si 2p region) and 532 eV (O 1 s region), while the principal GP peak, corresponding to C–C bonding, appeared at 284 eV (C 1 s region). Further increases in growth time to 30 and 60 min resulted in a decrease in the intensity of the primary peaks or slight shifts in their binding energies. This decline in peak intensity correlated with reductions in the O/C and Si/C atomic ratios. Notably, the optimal O/C and Si/C ratios in SiO2/GP composites can be adjusted depending on the synthesis method and the specific requirements of the intended application25.

XPS spectra of the synthesized SiO2@GP composites at growth times of 1, 5, 10, 30, and 60 min: (a) Si 2p, (b) C 1 s, and (c) O 1s.

Figure 4 and Table 1 present the O/C and Si/C ratios of the SiO2/GP composites. The O/C ratio reflects the degree of oxygenation on the GP surface, which can substantially enhance the interaction between SiO2 and GP and influence critical composite properties, including mechanical strength and electrical conductivity. Excessive oxygenation can degrade the conductivity of GP, whereas insufficient oxygenation may limit effective bonding with SiO2. To balance surface functionalization and electrical performance, an O/C ratio within the range of 0.1–0.5 is typically maintained. Similarly, the Si/C ratio indicates the relative proportions of SiO2 and GP in the composite. SiO2 is generally present in lower amounts than GP, as GP predominantly governs the electrical and thermal conductivity of the SiO2/GP system. To preserve the performance of GP-based materials, the Si/C ratio is maintained at a low level to prevent excessive SiO2 concentration, which can degrade the composite’s overall properties. An Si/C ratio in the range of 0.1–1 is typically targeted to balance the contribution of SiO2 without adversely affecting the composite’s electrical characteristics. Based on the results presented in Fig. 4 and Table 1, the SiO2@GP composite sample synthesized at a growth time of 10 min exhibited an O/C ratio of 0.355 and an Si/C ratio of 0.203, aligning with previously reported optimal conditions for SiO2/GP composites26,27. The thickness of the graphite layer on the SiO2 particle surface was confirmed to be tunable by adjusting the synthesis time. Numerous methods have focused on optimizing the physicochemical properties of electrode materials, specifically SiO2-based supercapacitors, through morphology control, interface engineering, and structural tailoring to enhance electrochemical performance28–30. Precise structural manipulation facilitates efficient ion and electron transport by generating multifaceted diffusion pathways, thereby significantly increasing power density. Furthermore, the integration of carbon-based SiO2 core-shell architectures establishes robust conductive networks that minimize internal resistance. Systematic control over synthesis parameters and core-shell morphology directly correlates with superior electrochemical outcomes, including elevated specific capacity, extended cycling stability, and enhanced energy density.

The morphology of SiO2@GP synthesized at a growth time of 10 min was characterized using TEM, as illustrated in Fig. 5. Specifically, Fig. 5a displays a low-resolution image, whereas Fig. 5b presents a high-resolution (HR-TEM) image of the SiO2@GP sample. In the HR-TEM image presented in Fig. 5b, SiO2 particles and thin GP sheets covering their surfaces are clearly observed. The interlayer distance of the sheet layers was measured to be approximately 0.35 nm, closely matching the typical interlayer spacing of 0.34 nm found in graphite31. We hypothesized that the thin layers formed on the surface of the SiO2@C composite are composed of graphite. To further investigate the atomic distributions within the composite, Fig. 5c–f presents the elemental mappings of C, O, and Si obtained via drift-corrected EDX imaging in the scanning transmission electron microscopy with high-angle annular dark-field imaging mode. These mappings confirm a well-defined core–shell structure, consisting of SiO2 cores surrounded by graphite sheet layers32. SiO2@GP structures synthesized at various growth times were employed as electrode materials in energy storage applications. Their electrochemical properties were evaluated to investigate the suitability of SiO2@GP as a supercapacitor electrode material. Electrochemical measurements, including CV, EIS, and GCD testing, were conducted in a 3 M KOH electrolyte solution using a CHI 760E instrument.

(a) Low-magnification and (b) HR TEM images of the SiO2@GP-10 composite synthesized at a growth time of 10 min. (c–f) Elemental mapping images showing the distribution of C, O, and Si.

Figure 6a presents the cyclic voltammograms of the SiO2@GP electrodes made from composites synthesized at different growth times (1–60 min), measured at a scan rate of 100 mV/s. The SiO2@GP electrode fabricated from the composite grown at 10 min exhibited a larger CV integral area and more pronounced oxidation and reduction peaks within the voltage range of 0–0.4 V. A larger integral area indicates a higher specific capacitance and faster redox kinetics. As the growth time increased from 1 to 10 min, the redox peaks shifted, and the current density increased. However, as the growth time increased to 30 and 60 min, the redox peaks shifted, and the current density decreased. The effects of growth time on specific capacitance and specific capacity were further investigated through GCD measurements. Figure 6b presents the variation in charge–discharge curves with growth time, measured at a current density of 1 A g− 1 within a potential window of 0–0.4 V. The specific capacitance (Cs) and specific capacity (Csc) were also calculated at this fixed current density, as illustrated in Fig. 6c. Notably, as the growth time increased from 1 to 5 and then to 10 min, Cs increased from 235.14 to 265.57 and 291 F g− 1, respectively. When the growth time was extended to 30 and 60 min, Cs decreased to 245.28 and 216.85 F g− 1, respectively. Similarly, Csc increased from 82.3 to 92.95 and 101.85 F g− 1 as the growth time increased from 1 to 5 and then to 10 min, respectively. Further, Csc decreased to 85.84 and 75.89 Fg− 1, respectively, as growth time increased to 30 and 60 min. Notably, the sample synthesized at a growth time of 10 min exhibited the highest Cs and Csc. Figure 6d presents the Nyquist plots of the SiO2@GP electrodes as a function of growth time (1, 5, 10, 30, and 60 min). The quasi-vertical lines observed in the low-frequency region indicate near-ideal capacitive behavior, while the absence of semicircles in the high-frequency region suggests excellent ionic conductivity. The equivalent series resistance of all samples, determined by the intersection of the curve with the real axis, was approximately 0.9 Ω, indicating efficient electrolyte permeation into the electrode. Accordingly, the optimized SiO2@GP sample synthesized at a growth time of 10 min (SiO2@GP-10) was selected to fabricate electrodes for use in energy storage devices.

Figure 7 illustrates the supercapacitor performance of the SiO2@GP-10 sample. Specifically, Fig. 7a presents the cyclic voltammograms of the SiO2@GP-10 structure recorded at scan rates ranging from 5 to 100 mV/s. At 100 mV/s, the SiO2@GP-10 electrode exhibits a large CV integral area and pronounced oxidation and reduction peaks within the voltage range of 0–0.4 V. This large integral area reflects a higher specific capacitance and faster redox kinetics. Notably, the shift in the redox peaks with increasing scan rate is attributed to rapid redox reactions at the electrode surface and efficient ionic and electronic transport at the electrode–electrolyte interface33. Moreover, the consistent shape of the CV curves across all scan rates indicates stable pseudocapacitive behavior.

As depicted in Figs. 7c and d, the obtained GCD results were used to estimate the specific capacitance and specific capacity at various current densities. Notably, the specific capacitance of the electrode reached 291 F g− 1 at 1 A g− 1 and decreased to 134 F g− 1 at 10 A g− 1. Moreover, the electrode maintained more than 40.14% of its initial capacitance at higher current densities, attributed to shortened diffusion paths that enhanced redox activity, as shown in Fig. 7d. The performance of SiO2-based carbon composites has been reported35,36; they have shown the maximum specific capacitance of 210 F g− 1 at the current density of 0.5 A g− 135, and the highest specific capacitance of 166.9 F g− 1 at the current density of 0.1 A g− 136. In this work, the device performance was improved by controlling the structural, optical, electrical, and compositional properties of SiO2@GR composites. We obtained a specific capacitance of approximately 291 F g− 1 at a current density of 1 A g− 1, which is higher than previously reported values. This suggests it could be useful for supercapacitor electrode applications in the future.

Conclusion

In summary, we developed a novel synthesis method for SiO2@GP composites and characterized their properties. Specifically, the influence of synthesis time on the structural and electrochemical properties of the SiO2@GP composites was systematically investigated. The SiO2@GP-10, synthesized with a 10 min growth time, was employed as an electrode in supercapacitor devices. The device incorporating the SiO2@GP-10 electrode exhibited a high specific capacitance of 291 F g− 1 at a current density of 1 A g− 1. The SiO2@GP-10 electrode exhibited remarkable long-term stability, maintaining 90% of its initial capacitance after 5000 cycles, with negligible performance deterioration. These findings highlight the strong potential of SiO2@GP-10 composites for future energy storage applications.

Methods

SiO2@GP synthesis and characterization

The thermal CVD process for SiO2@GP synthesis was conducted in a horizontal quartz tube. Silicon (IV) oxide (SiO2, 99.5%) and sulfuric acid [2 N H2SO4, 1 M] were sourced from commercial suppliers. All reagents were of analytical grade and used without further purification. SiO2 powder (~ 2 μm in diameter) was loaded into an alumina boat, placed in a furnace, evacuated to approximately 2 × 10− 2 Torr, and subsequently heated to 1100 °C. To eliminate contaminants and unstable oxygen species from the prepared SiO2, a reduction step was performed using 500 sccm of H2 gas for 20 min. SiO2@GP was then synthesized by introducing 50 sccm of C2H2, 500 sccm of H2, and 500 sccm of Ar for 5 min, followed by natural cooling to room temperature.

The structural and compositional properties of the synthesized SiO2@GP particles were characterized using X-ray diffraction (XRD, PANalytical MPD) and Raman spectroscopy (Renishaw inVia Reflex, 532 nm excitation). Morphology and lattice spacing were examined using transmission electron microscopy (TEM, FEI Tecnai G2 F20) and scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Hitachi S-4800). Additionally, chemical bonding states were analyzed via X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, VG Microtech MT 500/1).

Electrochemical measurements

The electrochemical performance of the prepared SiO2@GP composites in supercapacitor applications was evaluated using a Metrohm Autolab PGSTAT 302 N instrument under three- and two-electrode configurations. All measurements were performed using a three-electrode setup, with Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode, a Pt mesh as the counter electrode, and 3 M KOH as the aqueous electrolyte. Working electrodes were prepared by spreading a slurry composed of active materials and polyvinylidene fluoride (95:5 mass ratio) in N-methyl pyrrolidone onto carbon felt. The coated electrodes were dried in a vacuum oven at 165 °C for 12 h. The mass of SiO2@GP was determined by weighing the electrodes before and after slurry application using a microbalance. An average mass loading of 2.5 mg cm− 2 was obtained for the active material. Electrochemical characterization, including cyclic voltammetry (CV), galvanostatic charge-discharge (GCD) testing, and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS), was conducted in a 3 M KOH aqueous electrolyte solution37.
