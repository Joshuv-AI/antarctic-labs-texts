Multimodal approach in the treatment of recurrent keloids: Surgery, triamcinolone and 5-FU injections, and PLT, a case-report

Published: 2025

Introduction

Keloids are benign dermal fibroproliferative disorders that result from an exaggerated wound healing response, characterized by excessive collagen particularly type III deposition and persistent fibrotic tissue formation beyond the original wound boundaries [1]. Although they can develop after minor injuries, surgeries, or acne, their exact pathophysiology remains unclear. Multiple factors including genetic predisposition, local skin tension, and inflammatory cytokines contribute to their formation and recurrence [1,2].

Management of keloids is notoriously challenging due to their high recurrence rate, which exceeds 70 % when surgical excision is used alone [2]. Common modalities such as intralesional corticosteroids, cryotherapy, silicone sheets, laser therapy, and radiotherapy have shown varying degrees of success. The posterior trunk, shoulders, and chest areas under high mechanical tension pose additional difficulties due to continuous stress on wound sites and complex healing dynamics [3].

Recent studies emphasize the importance of tension-reducing sutures and multimodal approaches to prevent recurrence [3,4]. Furthermore, adjunctive therapies like Polarized Light Therapy (PLT), which utilizes a broad light spectrum (400–3400 nm), have demonstrated promising anti-inflammatory and pro-regenerative effects in wound healing and hypertrophic scar treatment [4].

This case report presents a comprehensive therapeutic approach combining surgical excision, tension-adapted closure techniques, intralesional triamcinolone and 5-FU injections, and PLT in a patient with multiple recurrent keloids. The aim is to highlight how integrating mechanical and biological strategies can lead to improved clinical outcomes in resistant cases.

This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 criteria [5].

Case presentation

Patient characteristics

A 27-year-old right-handed male presented with recurrent keloid scars on the posterior trunk, accompanied by itching, functional limitations, and aesthetic concerns. He had a 10-year history of keloid formation secondary to acne, with additional hypertrophic scars and keloids on his hands, chest, upper trunk, arms, thighs, and knees. The patient had previously undergone multiple treatments including surgical excision, cryotherapy, repeated intralesional corticosteroid injections, and CO₂ laser therapy, all of which failed to achieve sustained remission.

He reported that despite these interventions, the scars continued to enlarge over time, with changes in color and increased itching. The patient remained physically active throughout the treatment process, maintaining bodybuilding exercises. A family history of keloid formation was present on the maternal side (his mother and aunt). He also developed a keloid on his knee following a minor biking injury in childhood. Additionally, depressed acne scars were observed on his face. His symptoms included itching, pain while lying on the scars, and a significant negative impact on his quality of life due to the size and visibility of the lesions.

Methods

Diagnostic assessment and preoperative planning

The diagnosis of keloid was based on clinical history and physical examination. Scar severity was assessed using the Japan Scar Workshop Scale, with a total score of 21 (11 for risk factors, 10 for current symptoms), supporting a strong clinical suspicion of keloid. No imaging was required. Tissue samples were submitted for histopathological confirmation following surgical excision.

Interventions

Surgical excision and wound closure

All three keloids were completely excised to the level of the superficial fascia. Layered closure was performed using 3-0 Vicryl sutures for the fascia and subcutaneous layers, and 4-0 Nylon for skin approximation.

To manage tension appropriately, different suturing techniques were used:

Scar A (right side): The central high-tension zone was closed using pulley sutures, while the lateral edges were closed with simple interrupted sutures.

Scar A (right side): The central high-tension zone was closed using pulley sutures, while the lateral edges were closed with simple interrupted sutures.

Scars B and C (left side): Closed entirely with simple interrupted sutures.

Scars B and C (left side): Closed entirely with simple interrupted sutures.

Surgical sites were irrigated with saline and dressed with petroleum gauze, sterile pressure dressings, and surgical tape.

Postoperative care and follow-up

Stitches were removed on post-op day 14, and Surgical tape was continued for 10 additional days, which was the maximum duration tolerated by the patient due to excessive sweating. The patient was instructed to apply gentamicin eye ointment twice daily for two weeks on Scar A, where mild healing delay and dead space formation were observed (Fig. 4: B, C, D). Surgical sites were irrigated with saline and dressed with petroleum gauze, sterile pressure dressings, and surgical tape and activity modification to reduce tension. The patient resumed normal physical activity gradually, with emphasis on avoiding strain to the surgical area. Follow-up continued for 9 months, during which no signs of recurrence or major complications were observed.

Results

Histopathological examination of the excised lesions confirmed the clinical diagnosis of keloid. The epidermis showed hyperkeratosis, and the dermis contained broad, homogenous, brightly eosinophilic collagen bundles arranged in a haphazard pattern.

Scar A (Right Back, 3 × 5 cm):

Postoperative healing in Scar A was initially delayed, with dead space formation and slower epithelialization. The patient applied topical gentamicin ointment twice daily, which promoted local healing. After the second injection, the scar began to show mild elevation and firmness, suggestive of an early hypertrophic response. However, following the third intralesional injection, the lesion became completely flat and pliable, with no further signs of abnormal scarring (Fig. 4: E, F, G).

The patient reported a subjective reduction in itching and discomfort following each PLT session, particularly after the second and third. The only remaining postoperative finding was a mild area of superficial hyperpigmentation and shallow contour depression, which gradually resolved. No recurrence was observed over the 9-month follow-up.

Scar B (upper left back, 2 × 4 cm)

Healing progressed smoothly, with full re-epithelialization by postoperative week two. No signs of hypertrophy, pigmentation, or inflammation were observed. The scar remained flat, asymptomatic, and stable during follow-up, with no recurrence or local complications (Fig. 4: E, F, G).

Scar C (lower left back, 3 × 6 cm)

Scar C also demonstrated uncomplicated healing, with no evidence of pathological scar formation. It remained soft and non-tender, with no signs of pigmentation or recurrence throughout the observation period (Fig. 4: E, F, G).

Outcome

All three scars responded well to the combined treatment approach of surgical excision, intralesional injections, and polarized light therapy. No recurrence, secondary infection, or functional limitation was observed during the 9-month follow-up (Fig. 4). The patient expressed high satisfaction with the aesthetic and symptomatic outcomes, especially in light of previous treatment failures and long-term suffering.

Discussion

Tissue tension is a known risk factor for keloid recurrence. Simple interrupted sutures offer ease of application and uniform tension distribution. Pulley sutures, although designed for high-tension areas, may cause localized ischemia if not balanced properly. In this case, the pulley-sutured area showed delayed healing, underscoring the importance of technique selection based on individual anatomical and activity factors [7]. In this specific case, the successful treatment of the keloids on the patient's back using a multimodal protocol surgical excision, intralesional injections of triamcinolone and 5-FU, and Polarized Light Therapy (PLT) resulted in complete resolution with no recurrence over a 9-month follow-up.

Anatomical considerations in surgical and suture method selection

The anatomical location of the wound greatly influences the choice of surgical technique and suture type. In the posterior and shoulder areas, the skin is thicker and more elastic, but also subject to high mechanical tension due to continuous movement and stress from daily activities. Additionally, these regions are prone to keloid and hypertrophic scar formation. Consequently, suture techniques should be selected to minimize tension on wound edges and reduce the risk of recurrence [8,9].

Surgical considerations

Excision is often necessary for large keloids; however, it carries a high recurrence risk if used alone. In this patient, excision was essential due to the size and anatomical location of the lesions. A layered closure using both simple and tension-reducing techniques was employed, especially in the high-tension posterior trunk [7].

Simple interrupted sutures

This technique was selected for its simplicity, effective wound edge approximation, and ability to evenly distribute tension suitable for areas under moderate mechanical stress, such as the lateral back [10].

Pulley sutures

This technique was selected for wounds under high tension. Pulley sutures, due to their mechanical advantage similar to a pulley system, help achieve better wound edge approximation and reduce excess tension. This technique was particularly used in the central parts of the wound, which experience the most stretching, to prevent wound dehiscence and optimize healing [[11], [12], [13]].

Despite their mechanical advantage, the area closed with pulley sutures in this case demonstrated delayed healing, likely due to focal ischemia and excessive localized pressure. Furthermore, the patient's right-handedness and high physical activity likely increased tension on the right-sided scar, possibly contributing to incomplete approximation and early hypertrophic changes, which were later resolved with subsequent therapy.

Role of intralesional injections of triamcinolone and 5-FU

Triamcinolone is a corticosteroid that reduces inflammation and collagen synthesis, while 5-FU is an antimetabolite that inhibits fibroblast proliferation. Combined injection therapy has been shown to be more effective than monotherapy in reducing keloid size and recurrence [14,15]. In our case, three intralesional injections were administered with notable regression in scar thickness and firmness prevention, particularly in Scar A, where early hypertrophic activity was observed and reversed after the third injection.

Role of polarized light therapy (PLT)

PLT uses non-coherent, low-energy polarized light (480–3400 nm) to enhance tissue regeneration by stimulating anti-inflammatory and photobiomodulatory pathways. Studies have demonstrated its benefits in promoting angiogenesis, collagen remodeling, and fibroblast regulation. Treatments such as Photodynamic Therapy (PDT) induce apoptosis (programmed cell death) in overactive fibroblasts and prevent excessive type III collagen production. These processes help accelerate wound healing and reorganize tissue [16,17]. In this case, the patient reported subjective symptom relief reduced itching and discomfort after each session. Scar A showed visible improvement in pliability and surface flattening after the third session. This supports the potential synergistic role of PLT alongside standard pharmacologic and surgical therapy.

Patient perspective and residual effects

The only postoperative sequelae observed were mild superficial hyperpigmentation and focal atrophy in Scar A. These were transient, non-progressive, and deemed aesthetically acceptable by the patient. Considering the absence of recurrence and long-standing burden of prior scars, the patient expressed high satisfaction with the treatment outcome and functional recovery.

Prior treatment failures

Over the past seven years, the patient had received multiple monotherapies, including surgical excision, corticosteroid injection, and cryotherapy, all of which failed to provide durable relief. The limited effectiveness of these approaches highlights the need for comprehensive protocols targeting multiple mechanisms, as demonstrated in this case.

Strengths and limitations

The main strength of this case lies in its demonstration of a structured multimodal therapeutic approach combining surgery, pharmacotherapy, and phototherapy for recurrent keloids. The use of PLT as an adjunctive, non-invasive tool may provide additional benefit by promoting early wound modulation. However, as a single-case report with a 9-month follow-up, the generalizability of the findings is limited. Further studies are warranted to evaluate the reproducibility and long-term efficacy of this treatment protocol.

Conclusion

This case highlights the potential efficacy of a multimodal treatment approach combining surgical excision, intralesional triamcinolone and 5-FU injections, and polarized light therapy in managing recurrent, treatment-resistant keloid scars. The absence of recurrence, minimal adverse effects, and high patient satisfaction over a 9-month follow-up suggest that such integrative protocols may offer significant clinical benefit. While encouraging, these results should be interpreted with caution due to the single-patient design. Further clinical studies involving larger cohorts and longer follow-up are needed to validate the reproducibility and long-term outcomes of this treatment strategy.
