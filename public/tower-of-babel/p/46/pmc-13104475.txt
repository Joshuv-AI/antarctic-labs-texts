Integrated omics reveal the effects of vitamin D deficiency on gut microbiota and plasma metabolism in experimental autoimmune uveitis
Authors: Zhijun Chen, Wanyun Zhang, Yang Deng, Yinan Zhang, Guannan Su, Yao Wang, Peizeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractVitamin D deficiency (VD-deficiency) has been implicated in various autoimmune diseases, including uveitis, yet its mechanistic role remains unclear. Emerging evidence suggests that gut microbial dysbiosis and metabolic disturbances may serve as critical intermediates linking VD-deficiency and autoimmune disease pathogenesis. Here, using an experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU) model induced in B10RIII mice, we demonstrate that VD-deficiency exacerbates EAU severity, accompanied by enhanced Th1 and Th17 responses, suppressed regulatory T cells, and disruption of both intestinal and blood–retinal barrier integrity. Integrated microbiota and metabolomic profiling revealed a coordinated pattern of gut dysbiosis, characterized by enrichment of opportunistic pathogens and depletion of beneficial commensals, together with host metabolic reprogramming in VD-deficient EAU mice. KEGG analysis highlighted disruption of vitamin-related pathways, particularly the vitamin digestion and absorption pathway. Mechanistically, VD-deficiency associated disturbances were linked to LRAT downregulation in retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells, accompanied by a pro-inflammatory intraocular microenvironment characterized by increased IL-6 and CXCL family chemokines expression. Gain- and loss-of-function studies in complementary in vivo and in vitro RPE-based models further demonstrated that LRAT functions as a negative regulator of retinal inflammatory responses, with LRAT knockdown enhancing, whereas LRAT overexpression suppressing, the production of IL-6, CXCL1, CXCL2, and CXCL8. Collectively, our findings suggest that VD-deficiency exacerbates autoimmune uveitis by disrupting effector–regulatory T cell balance and compromising blood–retinal barrier and intestinal integrity, accompanied by considerable alterations in gut microbiota and systemic metabolism, while identifying LRAT as a potential regulator linking VD-deficiency to retinal inflammatory amplification.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03765-8.Keywords: Vitamin D deficiency, Uveitis, Gut microbiota, Metabolism

Background

Vitamin D (VD) is an essential fat-soluble vitamin primarily synthesized in the skin upon ultraviolet radiation exposure, with dietary sources providing supplementary intake [1]. Beyond its well-established role in bone and calcium homeostasis, VD exhibits a significant immunomodulatory function in both innate and adaptive immunity [2, 3]. Particularly, it suppresses T helper (Th) 1 and Th17 cell-mediated inflammatory responses while promoting regulatory T cell (Treg) development [4, 5]. Vitamin D deficiency (VD-deficiency) has reached pandemic proportions, affecting 35–55% of the global population [6]. Growing evidence links VD-deficiency to various autoimmune diseases, including uveitis [7, 8]. Clinical studies have demonstrated significantly lower VD levels in patients with noninfectious uveitis compared to healthy individuals, with hypovitaminosis D correlating with increased ocular inflammation risk [9].

Uveitis, characterized by intraocular inflammation affecting the uveal layers and adjacent tissues, represents a significant cause of visual impairment [10]. The pathogenesis of noninfectious uveitis involves T cell dysfunction, particularly the imbalances of Th1, Th17, and Treg populations [11, 12]. Additional factors, including genetic predisposition, gut microbiota dysfunction, and host metabolic disorders, have been implicated in triggering the aberrant response to this disease [13–16]. Experimental autoimmune uveitis (EAU) mice serve as a well-established model for human uveitis, particularly when induced in B10RIII mice using a mixture of the interphotoreceptor retinal-binding protein (IRBP) peptide 161–180, which creates acute panuveitis mimicking human disease [17]. Here, we used the EAU mice model to explore the effects of VD-deficiency on the gut microbiome and metabolomic profiling in uveitis.

Despite the established association between VD-deficiency and uveitis, the underlying mechanisms remain poorly understood. This study aimed to explore how VD-deficiency affects immune responses to retina-specific antigens using an integrated omics analysis, focusing on T cell populations, gut microbiota composition, and host metabolism in EAU mice.

Methods

Animals

Animals were conducted in strict accordance with the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Chongqing Medical University (IACUC-CQMU) following relevant guidelines and regulations. B10RIII mice were acquired from Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME, USA) and housed under specific pathogen-free conditions at the Animal Care Service of Chongqing Medical University. Animals were housed in a normal light and dark cycle for 12 h, and were randomly assigned to four groups: (1) control group, (2) VD deficiency group, (3) EAU group, and (4) EAU with VD deficiency group. Mice in the VD deficiency group and the EAU with VD deficiency group were fed a VD–deprived diet (0 IU VD/kg), whereas mice in the control and the EAU groups received a VD–sufficient diet (1000 IU VD/kg). For animals depleted of VD, cages were placed in a room under artificial UV-free lamp. The feed was provided by Trophic Animal Feed High-tech Co Ltd. Both groups received ad libitum access to food and deionized water. To verify the effectiveness of dietary VD deprivation, blood was collected via cheek pouch bleeding at the time of dietary intervention period (7 weeks of age), as described in previous studies [18–20].

Induction and evaluation of EAU

EAU was induced in B10RIII mice by immunization with 50 µg IRBP₁₆₁–₁₈₀ peptide (SGIPYIISYLHPGNTILHVD, Shanghai Sangon, China) emulsified in an equal volume of complete Freund’s adjuvant (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) [21, 22]. On day 14 post-immunization, anterior chamber inflammation was assessed by slit-lamp microscopy and graded on a 0–5 scale for severity. Fundus examination was then performed using a fundus camera to evaluate posterior segment involvement, including optic disc swelling, vascular leakage, and retinal inflammatory lesions, and scored on a 0–4 scale [23]. Mice were then sacrificed under anesthesia for subsequent experiments. Eyes were enucleated, fixed in 4% glutaraldehyde, dehydrated, and paraffin-embedded. Sections were prepared and stained with hematoxylin-eosin (H&E),then scored for histological inflammation on a 0–4 scale according to Caspi’s criteria, as described previously [24]. Scoring was conducted in a masked manner by two independent observers to ensure objectivity.

Evans Blue permeability assays

The microvascular permeability of blood-retinal barrier (BRB) in EAU was measured using Evans Blue (Sigma-Aldrich, USA), as previously described [25, 26]. Briefly, 9-week-old mice were administered 2% Evans Blue (4 mL/kg) via tail vein injection 2 h before sacrifice. Following deep anesthesia, mice were transcardially perfused with phosphate buffer solution (PBS) to remove intravascular dye. Eyes were enucleated, and retinas were carefully dissected and weighed. For quantitative analysis, each retina was homogenized and sonicated in 150 µL formamide, followed by incubation at 65 °C for 18 h. After centrifugation, the supernatant was collected, and measured with excitation at 620 nm and emission at 680 nm. Evans Blue concentration was calculated using a standard curve. For visualization of retinal vascular leakage, eyes were fixed in tissue fixative solution. Retinas were dissected, washed three times with PBS, and prepared as flat mounts. Evans Blue fluorescence was observed using an immunofluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany).

Real time quantitative PCR

At 9 weeks of age, retina, spleen and draining lymph nodes (DLNs) were collected from mice. Prior to tissue collection, mice were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with cold PBS to remove circulating blood. Freshly isolated retinas were homogenized by low-temperature ultrasonication and centrifuged for total RNA extraction. Spleens and DLNs were mechanically dissociated and passed through a cell mesh to obtain single-cell suspensions. As previously reported, mononuclear cells were then isolated by Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation using the lymphocyte separation medium (Haoyang, China) [27, 28]. Cells were rinsed thrice times using PBS and prepared for subsequent RNA extraction. Trizol reagent (Invitrogen, USA) was used to extract total RNA from the samples. cDNA was acquired using Prime Script RT reagent Kit (Takara, Japan). The mRNA levels were quantified using SYBR Green Master Mix (Takara, Japan) by real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). Primers sequences are displayed in Table S1.

Western blotting

Colon tissues were acquired from EAU mice on day 14 after immunization. Proteins were extracted by radio-immunoprecipitation assay buffer containing 1% protease inhibitor (Beyotime, China), and then quantified by bicinchoninic acid approach. Equal amounts of proteins were electrophoretically separated by SDS-PAGE before transferring to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies overnight at 4℃, followed by secondary antibodies for 1 h at 37℃. Immunoblot signal was quantified by Gel densitometry (Bio-Rad Laboratories). The primary antibodies used in this study were lecithin retinol acyltransferase β-actin (1/3000, ABclonal, China), (LRAT, 1/1000, Abcam, UK), claudin-1 (1/1000, ABclonal, China), claudin-3 (1/500, ABclonal, China), occludin (1/500, ABclonal, China), RBP2 (1/1000, Proteintech, China), and ARAT (1/600, Proteintech, China).

ELISA

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) Kit of 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D) (Jianglai Biotechnology, China) and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) (USCN Life Science, China) were used to assess the serum 25(OH)D and LPS levels in EAU. The supernatants of cultured retinal pigment epithelial (RPE) cells were collected to determine IL-6, IL-8, CXCL1, and CXCL2 levels using ELISA kits (Elabscience, China). By drawing a standard curve and taking the absorbance value of samples, the content could be obtained according to instructions.

Flow cytometry analysis of retina, spleen, and draining lymph nodes

As described previously [29–33], retinas were carefully dissected from enucleated eyes and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min in RPMI-1640 medium (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco, USA), collagenase D (1 mg/mL, Roche, Switzerland), and DNase I (100 µg/mL, Sigma-Aldrich, USA). The resulting cell suspensions were filtered through a 70 μm strainer and resuspended in cold PBS for downstream flow cytometry. Spleens and DLNs were mechanically dissociated into a sterile plate and passed through cell strainer to obtain single-cell suspensions. Mononuclear cells were then isolated by Ficoll-Hypaque density gradient centrifugation prior to flow cytometric analysis. Cells were first labeled with the viability dye Zombie NIR Fixable Viability Kit (BioLegend, USA) to exclude dead cells, followed by surface staining with anti-mouse CD45-PerCP (BioLegend, USA), CD4-APC (BioLegend, USA), and CD25-PE/Cy7 (eBioscience, USA) to identify total leukocytes, CD4⁺ T cells, and the CD25⁺ subset of regulatory T cells (Tregs). Foxp3/Transcription Factor Staining Buffer (eBioScience, USA) was used to fix cells, and Foxp3-PE anti-mouse antibody (BioLegend, USA) was used for staining. Regarding Th1/Th17 cells, cells were stimulated for 5 h with cell activation cocktail (BioLegend, USA), containing optimal concentrations of phorbol myristic acetate, ionomycin, and brefeldin A. The cells were then washed, fixed, and permeabilized using fixation/permeabilization buffer (eBioScience, USA). Cells were stained with specific anti-mouse T-helper cell antibodies: CD4-APC for 30 min and IFN-γ-PE/Cy7 (eBioScience, USA) and IL-17 A-PE (eBioScience, USA) for 1 h at 4 ℃. CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, USA) was used to collect flow cytometric data.

Immunofluorescence analysis

Immunofluorescence staining was performed to detect RPE65, a marker specific to retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) cells, following a previously described method [34]. Briefly, cells were seeded on sterile glass coverslips and allowed to adhere. Cells were then fixed, permeabilized, and blocked, followed by incubation overnight at 4 °C with an anti-RPE65 primary antibody (1/500; Huaan Biotechnology Co., Ltd., China). After washing, cells were incubated with an Alexa Fluor 488–conjugated secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and coverslips were mounted onto glass slides using an antifade mounting medium. Images were acquired using an immunofluorescence microscope (Leica, Germany).

16S rDNA sequencing

Fecal samples were obtained from EAU mice on day 14 after immunization. PCR was used to amplify DNA with primers for V3–V4 high variant region of 16 S rDNA gene. Sequences with ≥ 97% similarity were grouped into the same operational taxonomic unit. Using QIIME software, dimensionality reduction of original variable was performed by principal component analysis (PCA). Shannon index and Observed Species were used to calculate the α-diversity. Unweighted unifrac distance for principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was used to assess the β-diversity. Linear discriminant analysis (LDA) of effect size (LEfSe) was used to identify the differences of taxonomic abundances, and taxa with p < 0.05 and a log LDA > 2 were ultimately considered. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG was conducted by R software (version 3.4.2). Differential abundance score analysis of KEGG was used to analyze the proportion of down-regulated or up-regulated differential metabolites in the pathway to determine the activation or inhibition status of the pathway.

LC-MS analysis

Plasma of EAU mice was collected for metabolomics analysis on day 14 after immunization. 200uL chilled methanol and 200uL acetonitrile were put into 100uL plasma sample, and then were vortexed and centrifuged (14000 × g, 15 min, °C). Supernatants were dried in a vacuum centrifuge before being redissolved in 100µL acetonitrile and equal amounts of water solvent. Finally, liquid phases were performed using UHPLC-Q-TOF MS analysis (Shanghai Applied Protein Technology Co., Ltd). Orthogonal partial least-squares discriminant analysis (OPLS-DA) was performed to reduc data dimensionality. Variable importance in the projection (VIP) was used to measure variable value to show its impact on classification. 7-fold cross-validation and response permutation testing was used to assess this model’s robustness.

AAV9-mediated LRAT knockdown and overexpression in vivo

Recombinant adeno-associated virus serotype 9 (AAV9) vectors were used to modulate LRAT expression in vivo. For LRAT knockdown, AAV9 encoding short hairpin RNA targeting mouse LRAT (AAV-shLRA), and the corresponding negative control (AAV-shNC) were constructed and packaged using the GV478 vector (U6-MCS-CAG-EGFP) by Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The titers of AAV-shLRAT and AAV-shNC were 1.12 × 10¹³ and 2.94 × 10¹³ v.g/mL, respectively. For LRAT overexpression, an AAV9 vector encoding full-length mouse LRAT (AAV-oeLRAT) was generated using the GV825 vector (CMV-MCS-3Flag-FT2A-EGFP-WPRE-BGH) by Shanghai Genechem Co., Ltd., with the corresponding empty vector serving as control (AAV-vehicle). The titers of AAV-oeLRAT and AAV-vehicle were 1.87 × 10¹³ and 6.43 × 10¹³ v.g/mL, respectively. Prior to injection, viral stocks were diluted with sterile PBS to a working concentration of 2 × 10¹² v.g/mL. Subretinal injections (1 µL per eye) were performed as previously described [35–37] under an operating microscope using a blunt Hamilton syringe mounted on a micromanipulator. Successful delivery was confirmed by the formation of a localized subretinal bleb, and only eyes without observable complications were included in subsequent analyses.

RPE cell culture, LRAT knockdown and overexpression in vitro

Primary mouse RPE cells were isolated from posterior eyecups by enzymatic digestion following established methods [38, 39] and cultured under standard conditions. Human primary retinal pigment epithelial cells (hRPE) were obtained from donors at the Chongqing Eye Bank, as previously described [40]. The RPE cell line (ARPE-19) was provided by American Type Culture Collection (Manassas, USA). Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium/Nutrient Mixture F12 medium (DMEM/F12, Gibco, USA) containing 100U/ml penicillin, 100 µg/ml streptomycin, and 10% FBS was used for cells culture. After being starved for 24 h in serum-free medium, RPE cells were stimulated with LPS (1ug/ml, Sigma-Aldrich, USA) for 24 h. To knock down LRAT expression, cells were transfected with small interfering RNA targeting LRAT (siLRAT) using Lipofectamine 2000 (Invitrogen, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions, with a non-targeting siRNA (siNC) as the negative control (Shanghai Sangon, China). Lentiviral construct (LV-h-LRAT-3xflag-ZsGreen-PURO) expressing the LRAT coding sequence was purchased from Shanghai Sangon, China. Then the hRPE and ARPE-19 cells were transduced with a lentiviral vector encoding LRAT (LV-oeLRAT; Shanghai Sangon, China) at a multiplicity of infection (MOI) of 20. Cells transduced with the corresponding empty lentiviral vector (LV-vehicle) served as controls.

Statistical analysis

Graph preparation and statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10.0 (GraphPad Software, Inc.). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired Student’s t-test for normally distributed data or the Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by appropriate post hoc multiple-comparison tests was applied. Correlation Analysis was analyzed by Spearman correlation analysis. A P < 0.05 was considered as significantly different.

Results

Vitamin D deficiency exacerbates the severity of EAU

To investigate the effects of VD-deficiency on intraocular inflammation, B10RIII mice were randomly assigned to receive either a VD–deficient diet or a control diet starting at 3 weeks of age and maintained for 4 weeks. EAU was then induced according to group assignment, and mice were sacrificed on day 14 post-immunization, corresponding to the peak of intraocular inflammation (Fig. 1A). The 25(OH)D levels below 20 ng/mL was considered as VD-deficiency, 21 to 29 ng/mL as VD insufficient, and above 30 ng/mL as VD sufficient for optimum musculoskeletal health [41]. As expected, serum 25(OH)D levels were markedly reduced in the VD-deficiency group (13.36 ± 2.01 ng/mL) and the EAU in VD-deficiency group (12.18 ± 2.40 ng/mL), with both values falling below 20 ng/mL. These levels were significantly lower than those observed in controls (39.88 ± 3.09 ng/mL) and EAU group (38.55 ± 3.22 ng/mL) (Fig. 1B). To evaluate whether VD-deficiency affects baseline ocular homeostasis, we first assessed ocular inflammation in mice receiving a VD-deficient diet in the absence of EAU induction. The VD-deficiency group exhibited no signs of spontaneous ocular inflammation, as evidenced by normal slit-lamp and fundus findings, preserved retinal histology, and intact vascular permeability, comparable to controls (Figs. 1C–F). Following EAU induction, pronounced intraocular inflammation developed, which was significantly exacerbated in VD-deficiency. Compared with the EAU group, VD-deficient EAU mice exhibited more severe anterior segment inflammation and higher clinical scores, as well as aggravated posterior segment pathology, including optic disc inflammation, perivascular cuffing, retinal edema, and elevated fundus scores (Figs. 1C–D). Histological analysis further revealed increased inflammatory infiltration, retinal folding, and focal detachment, resulting in higher histopathological scores (Fig. 1E). Consistently, Evans Blue assays showed markedly increased retinal vascular leakage in VD-deficient EAU mice compared with EAU mice (Fig. 1F). Collectively, these data demonstrate that VD-deficiency aggravates intraocular inflammation, retinal structural damage, and vascular permeability.

Vitamin D deficiency disrupts the balance of effector and regulatory T cells in the retina

Infiltration of inflammatory cells, especially pathogenic T cells, plays a central role in retinal tissue damage during EAU [32]. To determine whether VD-deficiency alone affects retinal immune status, we analyzed retinal T-cell subsets in control mice and VD-deficient mice. A very low proportion of CD45⁺ leukocytes was observed in the retina of control and VD-deficient mice in the absence of EAU induction (Fig. S1A). Therefore, further subdivision into Th1, Th17, and Treg cell subsets was not feasible under baseline conditions. Following IRBP₁₆₁–₁₈₀ immunization, mice were analyzed on day 14 post-immunization, corresponding to the peak of disease severity. At this time point, infiltration of CD45⁺ leukocytes into the retina was increased in EAU and VD-deficient EAU mice (Fig. S1A). The gating strategy used to identify retinal T-cell populations in EAU and VD-deficient EAU mice is shown in Fig. S1B. Compared with EAU mice, VD-deficient EAU mice exhibited significantly increased frequencies of IFN-γ⁺ Th1 and IL-17⁺ Th17 cells, along with a reduction in CD25⁺FoxP3⁺ regulatory T cells (Figs. 2A–C). Consistent with these findings, RT-qPCR analysis of whole retinas isolated from perfused mice on day 14 post-immunization showed significantly elevated expression of IFN-γ and IL-17 and reduced IL-10 levels in VD-deficient EAU mice compared with EAU mice. In contrast, under non-immunized conditions, no differences were observed between control and VD-deficient mice (Fig. 2D).

Vitamin D deficiency disrupts the balance of effector and regulatory T cells in the draining lymph nodes and spleen

Given that pathogenic T-cell priming and polarization predominantly occur in DLNs during EAU [31, 42], we next examined whether VD-deficiency also affects effector and regulatory T-cell balance in the DLNs. EAU was induced by IRBP₁₆₁–₁₈₀ immunization, and mice were analyzed on day 14 post-immunization. Consistent with retinal findings, EAU induction led to increased frequencies of Th1 and Th17 cells and a reduced proportion of Tregs in DLNs. Importantly, VD-deficiency further exacerbated this imbalance, as evidenced by higher frequencies of IFN-γ⁺ Th1 and IL-17⁺ Th17 cells and lower percentages of CD25⁺FoxP3⁺ Tregs in VD-deficient EAU mice compared with EAU mice (Figs. 3A–C). Correspondingly, RT-qPCR analysis of mononuclear cells isolated from DLNs on day 14 post-immunization revealed increased IFN-γ and IL-17 mRNA levels and decreased IL-10 expression in VD-deficient EAU mice compared with EAU mice (Fig. 3D). In contrast, VD-deficiency alone had little effect on baseline T-cell composition or cytokine expression in mononuclear cells isolated from DLNs of non-immunized mice (Figs. 3A–D). Similar alterations in effector and regulatory T-cell balance were observed in the mononuclear cells isolated from spleens, as shown in Fig. S2.

Vitamin D deficiency leads to breakdown of intestinal barrier function and alters the gut microbiota composition

The intestinal barrier and gut microbiota are essential for systemic immune homeostasis, and their disruption permits microbial translocation that can exacerbate autoreactive T-cell responses and promote autoimmune diseases, including uveitis [43, 44]. VD is known to support epithelial integrity and mucosal immune regulation, suggesting that its deficiency may compromise the intestinal barrier and perturb the gut microbiota [45, 46]. To investigate this, we examined intestinal barrier function among the four groups. Compared with EAU mice, VD-deficient EAU mice exhibited significantly reduced mRNA and protein levels of the tight junction proteins claudin-1, claudin-3, and occludin in the colon and elevated serum LPS levels. In contrast, there were no differences between control and VD-deficient mice in tight junction protein expression or LPS levels (Figs. 4A–E). To assess whether VD-deficiency alters gut microbial composition, 16 S rDNA sequencing was performed. Microbial profiles differed markedly between EAU and VD-deficient EAU mice at the phylum, family, and genus levels (Fig. S3A). There was a significant difference in β-diversity (Fig. 4F), PCoA analysis showed clear separation between the two groups (Fig. 4G), and this difference in community structure was statistically supported by ANOSIM (R = 0.318, p = 0.007; Fig. 4H). In contrast, α-diversity remained comparable between the groups (Fig. 4I). LefSe analysis highlighted enrichment of several opportunistic pathogens, e.g., Bacteroides and Paraprevotella, and sulfate-reducing bacteria (SRB), Desulfovibrio, in the VD-deficient EAU, whereas Bifidobacterium and Mix enterotype were enriched in EAU (Figs. 4J–K). The taxa heatmap showing clear clustering patterns (Fig. S3B). KEGG results showed alterations in several pathways, including Metabolism of Cofactors and Vitamins (Fig. S3C). Conversely, no differences were observed in α-diversity or β-diversity between control and VD-deficient mice (Figs. S3D–E). PCoA analysis showed no clear separation between the groups (Fig. S3F), and ANOSIM confirmed no significant difference (R = 0.014, p = 0.285, Fig. S3G). Collectively, these findings indicate that VD-deficiency reshapes the gut microbiota specifically in the context of EAU, favoring Gram-negative taxa that may contribute to increased circulating LPS and systemic inflammatory and metabolic disturbances.

Vitamin D deficiency leads to host metabolic disorders and correlates with gut microbiota alterations

To determine whether these intestinal alterations are accompanied by systemic metabolic changes, we next performed untargeted metabolomic profiling in EAU. OPLS-DA results revealed a clear separation between vitamin D–deficient EAU and EAU groups (Fig. 5A), with permutation test confirming model robustness (Fig. S4A). Metabolites meeting fold change (FC) > 1.5 or < 0.67 and p < 0.05 were defined as differential metabolites and visualized by volcano plots (Fig. 5B). Seventeen differential metabolites that met VIP > 1 and p < 0.05 are listed in the Table S2, and hierarchical clustering analysis further illustrated their distinct patterns between groups (Fig. S4B). The proportion of the identified metabolites in each chemical category is shown in Fig. S4C. KEGG results revealed that 29 pathways were markedly affected, mainly involving amino acid metabolism and biosynthesis, lipid and carbohydrate metabolism, mineral and vitamin absorption, and neuroactive signaling (Fig. 5C). Notably, the vitamin digestion and absorption pathway was markedly altered, consistent with the observed enrichment of vitamin-related pathway identified in the gut microbiota KEGG analysis, suggesting coordinated disturbances in vitamin-associated metabolic processes.

To further explore the relationship between gut microbial alterations and host metabolism, an integrated correlation analysis was performed between differential bacterial genera and metabolites. In total, 80 bacterial–metabolite pairs showed significant associations (p < 0.05), including 31 highly significant pairs (p < 0.01; Fig. 5D). Notably, glycerophosphocholine was positively correlated with Bifidobacterium but negatively correlated with Desulfovibrio, Bacteroides, and Paraprevotella (p < 0.01; Fig. 5E). The interaction network, centered on Desulfovibrio, Bacteroides, Paraprevotella, and glycerophosphocholine, is shown in Fig. 5F. Together, these findings suggest that VD-deficiency promotes gut dysbiosis and systemic metabolic disturbances, underscoring a close interplay between gut microbial composition and host metabolic homeostasis during EAU.

Knockdown or overexpression of LRAT modulates inflammation in vivo and in vitro

As described above, microbiome and metabolomic KEGG analyses indicated that VD-deficiency in EAU mice was associated with marked alterations in vitamin-related pathways, particularly the Vitamin digestion and absorption pathway, which is directly relevant to our nutritional deficiency model. These findings prompted us to further examine key enzymes involved in this pathway, including LRAT, RBP2, ARAT, APOA1, and APOB, in modulating homeostasis within the retina. At 9 weeks of age, i.e., two weeks after IRBP₁₆₁–₁₈₀ immunization, RT-qPCR analysis of the retina from VD-deficient EAU mice revealed that the LRAT, RBP2, and ARAT mRNA levels were significantly downregulated, whereas APOA1 and APOB did not show any changes at mRNA expression, compared to EAU mice (Fig. 6A). Western blotting results confirmed that only LRAT exhibited a consistent and significant reduction at the protein level (Figs. 6B–C). Given its pivotal role in the vertebrate visual cycle and its predominant expression in RPE cells, where it maintains retinoid homeostasis, LRAT was therefore prioritized for the subsequent mechanistic investigations [47–49]. Primary RPE cells isolated from mice were validated by RPE65 immunostaining (> 95% purity) (Fig. 6D). The results showed that LRAT expression at both mRNA and protein levels was significantly reduced in RPE cells isolated from VD-deficient EAU mice compared with those from EAU mice, whereas no differences were observed between control and VD-deficient non-immunized mice (Figs. 6E–G). As RPE cells have been reported to promote ocular inflammation through the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines and chemokines, including interleukin-6 (IL-6), C-X-C motif chemokine ligand 1 (CXCL1), and CXCL2 [50], we next assessed the expression of these mediators. The levels of IL-6, CXCL1, and CXCL2 were significantly elevated at both mRNA and secretion levels in primary RPE cells from VD-deficient EAU mice compared with those from EAU mice, while no differences were detected between control and VD-deficiency groups (Figs. 6H–I).

Building on these in vivo observations, we first overexpressed LRAT in VD-deficient EAU mice via subretinal delivery of AAV-oeLRAT (Fig. 7A). Robust EGFP signal indicated successful subretinal delivery and effective AAV transduction (Fig. 7B). AAV-oeLRAT markedly restored LRAT expression in RPE cells (Figs. 7C–D), and significantly reduced IL-6, CXCL1, and CXCL2 expression by RPE cells isolated and cultured from VD-deficient EAU mice (Figs. 7E–F). Conversely, subretinal delivery of AAV-shLRAT in EAU mice efficiently suppressed LRAT production (Figs. 7G–I), and significantly increased expression of IL-6, CXCL1, and CXCL2 by RPE cells isolated and cultured from these mice (Figs. 7J–K). To corroborate these results in vitro, gain- and loss-of-function experiments were performed in hRPE cells and ARPE-19 cells (Fig. 8A). The results showed that LV-oeLRAT significantly increased LRAT expression at mRNA and protein levels, whereas siLRAT markedly reduced LRAT level. LRAT overexpression significantly suppressed IL-6 and CXCL8 expression at mRNA and secretion levels by hRPE cells and ARPE-19 cells, whereas its knockdown enhanced their expression (Figs. 8B-M). Collectively, these data identify LRAT as a key molecule linking VD-deficiency to RPE-driven inflammatory responses (Fig. 9).

The role and mechanism of VD-deficiency in EAU

Discussion

This study shows that VD-deficiency exacerbates the severity of EAU, accompanied by an increased expression in Th1 cells and Th17 cells and a decreased expression in Treg cells in the retina, DLNs, and spleen (Figs. 1, 2 and 3 and Fig. S1–2). Beyond immune polarization, our findings highlight the complex interplay between immune dysregulation, gut microbiota alterations, and host metabolic disturbances under VD-deficiency condition (Figs. 4 and 5 and Fig. S3–4). Importantly, mechanistic analyses further demonstrate that VD-deficiency induced downregulation of LRAT in RPE cells promotes a pro-inflammatory retinal microenvironment by enhancing IL-6 and CXCL chemokine production, as evidenced by complementary in vivo and in vitro gain- and loss-of-function experiments (Figs. 6, 7 and 8). Collectively, our integrated analyses indicate that VD-deficiency aggravates EAU through multiple converging mechanisms, including the skewing of T cell phenotypes toward proinflammatory subsets, disruption of intestinal and retinal barrier integrity, and profound changes in both gut microbial communities and metabolic profiles, highlighting the broad systemic impact of VD status on autoimmune pathogenesis.

In this study, VD-deficiency aggravated EAU severity and promoted a more pronounced inflammatory phenotype (Fig. 1). This exacerbation was closely associated with an imbalance between effector and regulatory T-cell responses. Flow cytometric analysis of total cell suspensions from enzymatically digested whole retinas, as well as mononuclear cells isolated from DLNs and spleen, revealed increased frequencies of IFN-γ⁺ Th1 and IL-17⁺ Th17 cells accompanied by reduced CD25⁺FoxP3⁺ Treg proportions. Consistent with these alterations in T-cell subsets, RT–qPCR analysis showed elevated expression of IFN-γ and IL-17—effector cytokines predominantly produced by Th1 and Th17 cells, respectively—and reduced expression of IL-10, a key immunoregulatory cytokine closely associated with Treg function, in whole retinal tissues and mononuclear cells from DLNs and spleen (Figs. 2 and 3 and Fig. S2). As whole retinal tissues contain infiltrating leukocytes following EAU induction, these cytokine alterations most likely reflect enhanced effector T-cell infiltration and diminished regulatory T-cell activity under VD-deficient conditions. Given the well-established roles of IFN-γ and IL-17 in promoting ocular inflammation and tissue injury, as well as the essential function of IL-10 in maintaining immune tolerance, this shift toward effector dominance likely represents a central immunological mechanism by which VD-deficiency aggravates EAU.

VD has emerged as a pivotal regulator of gut microbial communities, and its deficiency has been consistently associated with microbial dysbiosis in both experimental models and clinical settings [51, 52]. In our study, VD-deficiency in the context of EAU was associated with a distinct microbial signature, characterized by enrichment of pathogens, e.g., Desulfovibrio, Bacteroides and Paraprevotella, alongside depletion of beneficial commensals such as Bifidobacterium (Fig. 4 and Fig. S3). Desulfovibrio is recognized for its production of hydrogen sulfide, a potent neurotoxin that irritates mucosa and impairs intestinal epithelium [53], and its enrichment has been closely linked to multiple autoimmune disease [54–56]. Similarly, Bacteroides and Paraprevotella are known sources of lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and proteolytic activity, respectively, which can exacerbate epithelial permeability and promote microbial translocation [57–59]. Conversely, Bifidobacterium plays a protective role in maintaining barrier function and limiting inflammatory signaling [60]. As a well-known probiotic, Bifidobacterium has been widely used in the prevention or treatment of various autoimmune diseases [61–63]. Notably, an intriguing result is that VD-deficiency alone did not alter the baseline composition of the intestinal microbiota. This suggests that microbial dysbiosis observed in VD-deficient EAU mice is not pre-existing but rather emerges in response to inflammatory challenge. One possible explanation is that VD-deficiency establishes a primed intestinal microenvironment that remains phenotypically stable under steady-state conditions but exhibits increased susceptibility to immune perturbation. Upon EAU induction, systemic immune activation and inflammatory signaling may disrupt this fragile equilibrium, thereby facilitating secondary microbiota remodeling. In this context, VD-deficiency may lower the threshold for inflammation-driven gut barrier dysfunction and microbial shifts, functioning as a sensitizing factor rather than a direct initiator of dysbiosis. Collectively, these findings support a model in which VD-deficiency amplifies inflammation-associated microbial remodeling during EAU, contributing to a pro-inflammatory microbial environment that may reinforce immune activation.

Consistent with microbial dysbiosis and increased gut permeability, metabolomic profiling revealed substantial perturbations in host metabolic pathways under VD-deficiency conditions. Notably, several anti-inflammatory metabolites within the glycerophospholipid pathway, including glycerophosphocholine, stearoyl-2-hydroxy-sn-glycero-3-phosphorylcholine and glycerol 3-phosphat, were significantly reduced (Fig. 5 and Fig. S4). These metabolites are known to modulate inflammatory signaling, oxidative stress, and immune cell function, particularly in the context of LPS exposure [64–66]. Integrated microbiome–metabolome correlation analysis further demonstrated strong associations between pathogenic bacterial genera and depletion of these protective metabolites, whereas beneficial taxa such as Bifidobacterium positively correlated with glycerophosphocholine levels (Figs. 5D–F), which are consistent with previous studies [67, 68]. This coordinated dysregulation highlights a functional microbiome–metabolome axis through which VD-deficiency may amplify systemic inflammatory in EAU.

Importantly, both microbiome and metabolomic analyses converged on vitamin-related pathways, notably the Vitamin digestion and absorption pathway. While disruption of this pathway was observed in the VD-deficient EAU model, accumulating evidence indicates that it represents a broader metabolic hallmark of VD-deficiency across diverse pathological contexts, including immune- or inflammation-related diseases. For instance, in a model of age-related macular degeneration, lipidomics analysis similarly revealed significant disturbances in the Vitamin digestion and absorption pathway of RGR‑d deficient mice fed a high-fat diet, accompanied by markedly reduced VD levels [69]. Critically, this alteration extends beyond animal models to systemic human studies. A serum proteomic analysis in individuals with VD-deficiency found differentially expressed proteins significantly enriched in the Vitamin digestion and absorption pathway, concurrently linking VD-deficiency to abnormal complement protein expression, insulin resistance, and glucose metabolism disorders [70]. Together, these observations across systemic and ocular contexts suggest that disruption of this pathway constitutes a fundamental and shared metabolic axis downstream of VD-deficiency. In EAU, our data further indicate that this general metabolic disturbance may intersect with autoimmune priming against retinal antigens, thereby fostering a permissive microenvironment that exacerbates or sustains ocular inflammation. Consequently, targeting this pathway, through VD restoration or broader metabolic interventions, may represent a translationally relevant strategy, potentially applicable not only to uveitis but also to other VD-deficiency associated immune- or inflammation-related disorders.

Mechanistically, our findings identify LRAT as a key molecular link connecting VD-deficiency and retinal inflammation in EAU. We observe that LRAT expression is downregulated in the retina and RPE cells under VD-deficient EAU conditions (Figs. 6A–G), and that modulation of LRAT expression in vivo and in vitro directly regulates the production of pro-inflammatory mediators such as IL-6 and CXCL chemokines (Figs. 7 and 8). LRAT is a core enzyme of the vertebrate visual cycle and is predominantly expressed in RPE cells, where it maintains retinoid homeostasis [47, 71]. Under VD-deficiency conditions, suppression of LRAT in RPE cells was associated with enhanced production of pro-inflammatory mediators, e.g., IL-6 and CXCL family chemokines. Cytokines and chemokines within the retinal homeostasis play a central role in EAU pathogenesis by regulating immune cell recruitment and effector T-cell polarization. IL-6 is a well-established driver of disease severity, promoting Th17 differentiation and compromising blood–retinal barrier integrity, thereby facilitating leukocyte infiltration [72]. Likewise, CXCL8 and its murine functional homologs CXCL1 and CXCL2 act as potent chemoattractants and contribute to inflammatory cell accumulation within the retina [73]. In this context, our data support a model in which VD-deficiency promotes a pro-inflammatory retinal milieu through LRAT downregulation in RPE cells, leading to enhanced local production of IL-6 and CXCL chemokines, which favors sustained immune activation in EAU. Together, these findings suggest a LRAT-dependent axis through which VD-deficiency may influence retinal inflammatory responses, highlighting an intersection between altered vitamin status and local cytokine-driven immune dysregulation during EAU.

Interestingly, LRAT is a key regulator of retinoid homeostasis. Emerging evidence underscores the functional interplay between vitamin A (VA) and VD [74, 75]. Both are fat-soluble vitamins that share overlapping pathways in intestinal absorption, hepatic storage, lipid transport, and nuclear receptor mediated signaling, and deficiencies in these vitamins frequently coexist, suggesting mechanistic interconnection rather than independent nutritional events [76–78]. Population studies have revealed a positive correlation between serum retinol—a well-established biomarker of systemic VA status—and 25-hydroxyvitamin D levels, indicating coordinated regulation of their metabolic homeostasis [79]. At the molecular level, VA metabolites (retinoids) and VD exert their effects via heterodimerization of the retinoid X receptor (RXR) with the VD receptor (VDR) [80–82]. Consequently, alterations in VA availability may modulate RXR availability, potentially influencing VDR signaling and downstream VD-dependent immunoregulatory pathways. Additionally, deficiencies in both vitamins are associated with disturbances in lipid metabolism, which may further impair their transport and bioavailability [83]. In this context, LRAT downregulation under VD-deficiency conditions may represent a critical node through which disrupted vitamin absorption and retinoid metabolism translate into altered immune regulation within the eye. Collectively, these findings highlight the translational potential of targeting LRAT and related metabolic pathways to modulate retinal inflammation in VD-deficiency associated ocular autoimmune diseases.

This study has some limitations. First, the study period was relatively short, and centered on a single time point corresponding to peak disease activity (day 14 post-immunization), potentially limiting dynamic observation of disease progression. Second, our findings highlight the role of VD-deficiency in mechanisms mediated by the Th1/Th17/Tregs imbalance, and did not assess other immune cell populations such as macrophages or B cells, which may also contribute to VD-deficiency associated disease exacerbation. Third, while our in vivo and in vitro data support a role for LRAT in regulating retinal inflammation, VD rescue experiments were not performed and will be essential to establish therapeutic causality and therapeutic relevance. Furthermore, future studies incorporating dose and timing varied supplementation, and multi‑cellular phenotyping in both animal models and clinical cohorts are needed to overcome these limitations, and to translate our findings into therapeutic strategies.

In conclusion, our study highlights that VD-deficiency aggravates EAU severity, promotes a Th1/Th17/Treg imbalance, accompanied by considerable gut microbial and metabolic alterations. Mechanistically, LRAT is identified as a critical mediator linking vitamin signaling to retinal inflammation, indicating its potential as a therapeutic target in VD-deficiency associated ocular autoimmune disorders. These findings underscore the importance of maintaining adequate VD levels to prevent or mitigate autoimmune uveitis and potentially other autoimmune inflammatory conditions. Notably, this study provides experimental evidence supporting a direct link between VD-deficiency and autoimmune ocular inflammation, thereby reinforcing preventive strategies aimed at reducing VD-deficiency associated disease burden, particularly in populations at risk due to insufficient dietary intake or limited sunlight exposure.
