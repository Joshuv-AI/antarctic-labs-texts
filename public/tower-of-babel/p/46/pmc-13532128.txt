The Spectrum of Congenital Hypogonadotropic Hypogonadism: A 30‐Year Experience at a Tertiary Paediatric Centre
Authors: Minha Kook, Richard G. McGee, Geoffrey Ambler, Yoon Hi Cho
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTCongenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (CHH) is a rare group of disorders of gonadotropin deficiency, either isolated or as a part of multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies (MPHD).We aimed to describe the spectrum of presentation, diagnosis, and management practices of CHH spanning 30 years at an Australian tertiary paediatric centre.This is a retrospective cohort study of patients with CHH seen at the Children's Hospital at Westmead between January 1994 and December 2024 (n = 96, 66% male), categorised by diagnostic subtypes: Kallmann Syndrome (KS), normosmic HH, MPHD and other syndromes (including CHARGE).ResultsIn patients with KS (n = 31, 87% male), the most common presenting features were micropenis and/or cryptorchidism (45%) or pubertal delay (45%), with two peak ages of presentation: 0.6 [IQR 0.3–0.8] and 14.6 [11.5–15.7] years. Patients with MPHD (n = 23, 52% male) presented with neonatal hypoglycaemia in 52%. Comorbidities stratified by diagnostic subtypes (KS, MPHD, and other syndromes) included hearing impairment (10%, 4%, 100%), visual impairment (7%, 17%, 77%), cardiac anomalies (3%, 9%, 77%), and intellectual disability (13%, 26%, 46%). Genetic testing was performed in 32% in 1995–2009 vs 66% in 2010–2024. Pathogenic gene variants associated with HH were found in 61% of those tested, including CHD7 (n = 10), ANOS1 (n = 4), and FGFR1 (n = 3). Pubertal induction was commenced in males at a mean age of 14.1 ± 1.6 years using testosterone (n = 27 oral; 14 intramuscular; 1 subcutaneous) or gonadotropins (n = 5), and in females, at a mean age of 14.6 ± 1.8 years using oestradiol (n = 21 oral; 8 transdermal) ± progesterone.ConclusionsCHH is a heterogeneous group of disorders, with varying prevalence of associated comorbidities according to diagnostic subtypes. Our data highlight the paediatric‐specific modes of presentation of CHH, and in KS, the bimodal distribution of age at presentation in males and the under‐representation and delayed diagnoses in females. Despite advances in genetics in the past three decades, there remain areas for further research in the diagnostics and therapeutics of CHH in the paediatric age group.

Introduction

Congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (CHH) is a group of rare disorders marked by gonadotropin deficiency, as a result of defective synthesis or release of gonadotropin‐releasing hormone (GnRH) or gonadotropins. Isolated gonadotropin deficiency associated with anosmia or hyposmia, known as Kallmann syndrome (KS), is due to defects in olfactory neuron development and migration of GnRH neurons from the embryonic nasal placode to the hypothalamus [1, 2]. Cited incidence of KS in the literature varies from 1 in 48 000 in the Finnish population based on hospital records, and within this group, 1:30,000 in males [3], to 1 in 10,000 in French males conscripted to the military [4]. Isolated gonadotropin deficiency with an intact sense of smell is commonly termed normosmic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism (NHH). CHH may also occur as a part of multiple pituitary hormone deficiencies (MPHD) or as a part of other congenital syndromes, such as CHARGE syndrome.

Despite its congenital aetiology, the diagnosis of CHH is often delayed until young adulthood, when individuals typically present with a lack of pubertal development or infertility [1]. Correspondingly, most retrospective studies of isolated gonadotropin deficiency include mixed adolescent and young adult cohorts with median ages at presentation in the transition period to adult care [5, 6], but few studies examine an exclusively paediatric cohort. The opportunities for an early diagnosis may be missed if “red flag” signs, such as cryptorchidism and micropenis in male infants, are not recognised as potentially arising from CHH [2, 7]. For females, the lack of such signs during infancy and the only recent recognition of non‐X‐linked CHH have also historically contributed to a delayed diagnosis [2, 8]. During the pubertal years, diagnostic challenges arise in the differentiation between CHH and the more common and transient entity of constitutional delay of growth and puberty (CDGP). Despite increased accessibility of genetic testing, a genetic diagnosis is only established for 21–51% of patients [8]. In terms of therapeutics, recombinant gonadotropins offer the possibility of physiological replacement during critical periods in paediatric care in males with CHH: for pubertal induction [9, 10, 11] and restoration of mini‐puberty [12]. While there has been interest in using gonadotropin treatment for male pubertal induction in HH for two decades, there remains a paucity of data to inform evidence‐based protocols [13, 14]. Further data is much needed in the paediatric‐specific presentations of this condition to inform future research, particularly to characterise paediatric clinic populations to determine the feasibility of clinical trials.

This study therefore aims to describe the spectrum of presentation, diagnosis, and management for all causes of CHH of a paediatric cohort over 30 years at a tertiary children's hospital in Australia.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the Sydney Children's Hospitals Network Human Research Ethics Committee (2024/ETH02197).

This is a retrospective cohort study of all patients diagnosed with CHH at the Children's Hospital at Westmead (CHW), who attended at least one endocrine clinic visit from the 1st of January 1994 to the 31st of December 2024. The Endocrine Patient Management System (EPMS) is an electronic database utilised at CHW in which each patient's diagnosis is coded. Diagnostic codes and free‐text searches included “congenital hypogonadotropic hypogonadism”, “idiopathic hypogonadotropic hypogonadism”, “congenital hypopituitarism”, “Kallmann syndrome”, “central hypogonadism”, “hypothalamic hypogonadism”, “multiple pituitary hormone deficiency”, “CHARGE” and “anosmia”. Secondary ascertainment of recent and current patients was conducted through recollection from the department's paediatric endocrinologists. Data from all past clinic visits were then reviewed from the scanned documents and electronic medical records, and the first clinic visits dated back to 1978.

CHH was confirmed by documentation of the diagnosis by the treating clinician in the medical records or, if the final diagnosis was undecided, defined by biochemical evidence of HH and characteristic clinical features of CHH (e.g. anosmia, micropenis and cryptorchidism). Exclusion criteria included having a diagnosis of MPHD without HH (including those who did not have neonatal evidence of HH and who had not yet reached pubertal age to confirm HH at the last visit), acquired forms of HH (e.g. due to intracranial lesions, surgery or irradiation), primary and mixed hypogonadism, and where there was no clear differentiation between HH and CDGP at the last visit.

Based on database retrieval by diagnosis, 185 patients were identified, and of these, 89 patients were excluded on medical record review based on the above criteria, resulting in a total of 96 patients being included in the study cohort.

Data extraction fields were cross‐referenced with the international HH data dictionary [15]. Extracted data included clinical information on anthropometry, pubertal staging, treatment modalities, concomitant medications, associated congenital abnormalities (including cleft lip and/or palate), syndromic features (including anosmia, synkinesis, dental abnormalities, skeletal abnormalities, hearing impairment and visual impairment) and comorbidities (including intellectual disabilities, behavioural disorders and mood disorders). Presenting symptoms were recorded as documented by the endocrinologist and if there was more than one major symptom, the primary and secondary concerns were noted. Birth, neonatal and family history (including CHH, delayed puberty and fertility treatment), as well as biochemistry results, bone age x‐rays, MRI findings and genetic test results, were extracted from the medical records. Genetic test results were classified according to methodology: gene panel, single gene, whole exome sequencing (WES), whole genome sequencing (WGS), chromosome microarray, or unknown.

Statistical analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics Version 25. Results were reported as mean and standard deviation for normally distributed data and median and interquartile range (IQR) for non‐normally distributed data. Categorical data were reported as frequencies and percentages. Two variables were compared using a t‐test for normally distributed data and the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test for non‐normally distributed data. Multiple variables were compared using ANOVA for normally distributed data and the Kruskal Wallis test for non‐normally distributed data. Multiple categorical variables were compared using the Pearson Chi‐Square test.

Results

The five diagnostic categories for CHH were: KS, NHH, partial isolated HH, MPHD, and other syndromes (predominantly, CHARGE syndrome).

Clinical Presentation

The median age at presentation for CHH for the total group (n = 96) was 6.0 years [IQR 0.5–14.2]. The clinical characteristics for each of the five diagnostic categories are outlined in Table 1. The majority of patients with MPHD presented in the first 2 years of life, at a median age of 3.5 months, while patients with other diagnoses had a wider age range of presentation (Figure 1). Age at presentation for patients with KS displayed a bimodal distribution, with 39% (12/31) presenting within the first 13 months of life at a median age of 0.6 years [0.3–0.8], and the remainder at a median age of 14.6 years [11.5–15.7](Figure 2). Females represented 34% of the cohort, and, apart from MPHD, which was diagnosed during infancy, all other forms of CHH were diagnosed in later childhood or adolescence (Figure 2).

Investigations

Of the 64 patients who had a brain MRI performed, radiological abnormalities were reported in 49 patients. The most common finding was isolated hypoplastic/absent pituitary gland or stalk and/or ectopic posterior pituitary bright spot (n = 20; 31%), followed by inner ear structural abnormalities consistent with CHARGE syndrome (n = 13; 20%), and isolated hypoplastic/absent olfactory bulbs and sulci (n = 12; 19%). One individual had abnormalities in both the pituitary gland and olfactory structures. Other abnormalities (n = 3; 5%) included structural abnormalities of the corpus callosum, ventricular enlargement, and hypoplastic cerebellar structures.

Genetic testing results were available for 36 patients, and 61% (22/36) had a genetic diagnosis for HH (Table S1). All patients with KS born since 2003 were offered genetic testing. Pathogenic variants were detected in 7/12 (58%) tested patients with KS: ANOS1 (n = 4), including one patient who had negative results on initial gene panel testing but returned a genetic diagnosis upon periodic re‐analysis by the laboratory, FGFR1 (n = 2), CHD7 (n = 1), and TUBB3 (n = 1). An additional 2/12 (17%) with KS had ANOS1 and CHD7 variants of uncertain significance with potential clinical relevance. Of the four patients with NHH who underwent genetic testing, variants were detected in FGFR1 (n = 1) and GNRHR (n = 1). Of the 14 with other syndromes, 10 were confirmed to have a CHD7 mutation consistent with CHARGE syndrome, while one patient considered to have a clinical variant of CHARGE syndrome was found to have a CHD4 mutation, a genetically distinct syndrome associated with HH (Sifrim–Hitz–Weiss syndrome) [16]. Genetic testing was performed in 5/23 patients with MPHD: results included a novel GLI2 mutation (n = 1) and pathogenic variants in both copies of EXOC2 (n = 1). Clinical Progression and Management

Of the 63 male patients, 29 (46%) were treated with a short course of intramuscular testosterone for a micropenis during the period of mini‐puberty of infancy. Of the 46 male patients who underwent pubertal induction, initial treatment was commenced at a mean age of 14.1 ± 1.6 years. The most common initial route of administration was oral testosterone undecanoate (n = 27; 59%), while the most common treatment at the last visit was intramuscular testosterone esters (n = 20; 43%). Five boys (11%) were treated with gonadotropin therapy (since December 2023).

Of the 29 female patients who underwent pubertal induction, initial treatment was commenced at a mean age of 14.6 ± 1.8 years. The most common route of initial treatment overall was oral oestrogen valerate (n = 17; 59%) followed by transdermal 17‐β oestradiol (n = 8; 28%). By the last clinic visit, the majority used an oral combined oestrogen and progestogen (n = 23; 79%). For females in the most recent time period (2010–2024), the majority (n = 5; 83%) commenced on transdermal oestrogen initially.

Discussion

This retrospective cohort study of CHH over 30 years, with 45 years of clinical data, takes a unique paediatric perspective on the clinical presentation and characteristics of all forms of CHH and examines the changes in diagnostics and management over this period.

Despite CHH being a congenital condition, the age at presentation varies widely depending on the underlying diagnosis. Infants with MPHD tend to present early during infancy, prompting medical attention due to acute issues arising from other pituitary hormone deficiencies, such as neonatal hypoglycaemia or jaundice. In our cohort, MPHD patients with HH involvement most frequently presented with hypoglycaemia during infancy, with 39% having coexisting micropenis and 30% having cryptorchidism upon assessment. It is possible that there are patients with MPHD without documented gonadotropin involvement during infancy who may eventually be confirmed to have HH during their pubertal years, but were not included in our study. In a recent large single‐centre study of children with MPHD, analysis of gonadotropin deficiency within the cohort was specifically excluded due to the difficulty in assessing gonadotropin measurements during infancy and the delayed diagnoses until the pubertal years [17]. In another large retrospective cohort study at a paediatric centre, there were no patients with gonadotropin deficiency detected at initial diagnosis of MPHD, although micropenis and cryptorchidism were present in more than one third of the infant boys with MPHD [18]. These studies demonstrate that gonadotropin deficiency can be difficult to diagnose even within the MPHD cohort. Patients with CHARGE syndrome were diagnosed based on other syndromic features and later referred to a paediatric endocrinologist in early childhood. On the other hand, individuals with NHH presented later in adolescence, with features of micropenis and cryptorchidism, possibly pre‐existing for many years but becoming clinically more evident with linear growth.

In our paediatric KS cohort, we identified two peak periods of clinical presentation: in infancy (median age 0.6 years) and during adolescence (median age 14.6 years), in line with the absence of the expected periods of physiological activity of the hypothalamic‐pituitary‐gonadal axis [1]. Of note, 40% of patients with KS were diagnosed in infancy. In contrast, most reported studies in the literature describe late presentations of older adolescent and young adult cohorts who present with delayed puberty [1, 2]. A national cohort study in Italy also found median ages of pre‐pubertal presentation to be 18.3 years in KS and 19.9 years in NHH [5]. Our paediatric tertiary centre cohort therefore represents a skewed cohort and does not capture the patients who were never referred for endocrine assessment during childhood and adolescence. Of the male patients with KS who presented in adolescence in our cohort, 40% had a history of cryptorchidism, consistent with the adult data of 30–50% of men with KS having a history of cryptorchidism [2, 19], and 60% had a history of micropenis, higher than reported in adult data [3, 19]. Even in our paediatric patient group, there remains a subset of adolescents whose diagnoses could have been confirmed at an earlier age.

Those diagnosed with KS and NHH in our cohort were disproportionately male (87%), even more so than the reported male‐to‐female ratio from adult data of 2.6‐3.6:1 [20, 21]. In contrast, there was a close‐to‐equal sex distribution of patients with MPHD and other syndromes, likely attributed to the presence of other clinical features outside of the HH phenotype. The male predominance in the former diagnoses can be explained by the lower rates of female presentation with delayed puberty compared to male adolescents in clinical practice, as well as the lack of recognition of the occurrence of KS and NHH in females, which is even more exaggerated in the paediatric age group than reported in the young adult literature [2]. It has also been recognised that delayed puberty for girls is often treated empirically without comprehensive investigation [2, 22]. Additionally, female infants do not display the typical “red flag” signs of male infants with CHH. Our paediatric data supports the notion that many female patients with isolated HH remain without a diagnosis or are misdiagnosed well into adulthood.

Children and adolescents with CHH have a high prevalence of comorbid conditions. Anosmia and hyposmia form a part of the clinical diagnosis of KS. Patients with CHARGE syndrome have a characteristic pattern of hearing impairment, visual impairment, and cardiac abnormalities that can be identified during the neonatal period and early childhood [23]. Of note, this study documents the relatively high prevalence of intellectual and behavioural disabilities in this group of disorders marked by CHH, as described in the literature, although the mechanisms underlying these associations remain unclear [24]. These features provide opportunities for phenotypic recognition of the syndromic forms of CHH and highlight the medical burden of care for these patient groups [25].

There is no definite diagnostic threshold for biochemical confirmation of HH on dynamic testing, although thresholds have been proposed [26]. Our data is consistent with literature indicating that dynamic testing in HH produces variable results [1, 2, 7]. Inhibin B and anti‐Mullerian hormone (AMH) levels were infrequently measured in our cohort and therefore not reported, but may be considered as biochemical adjuncts to confirm diagnosis [27]. The characteristic findings on MRI for each diagnostic category of CHH confirm the clinical role of MRI scans in the diagnostic workup of paediatric patients with CHH.

In parallel with advancements in genetic testing and its accessibility in recent years, a higher proportion of CHH patients have been found to have confirmatory genetic diagnoses in recent times. Since the identification of ANOS1 (formerly KAL1) mutations causing CHH, over 60 genes have been implicated in the pathogenesis of CHH [6, 28]. In the past two decades, genetic testing was offered to all patients with KS born in the past two decades, but infrequently pursued for patients with MPHD. Overall, 61% of tested patients were found to have a pathogenic gene variant associated with HH, with the highest yield in CHARGE syndrome.

In our study, 46% of male infants received a 3‐month course of testosterone during the mini‐puberty window, as a part of local clinical practice for the treatment of micropenis associated with HH. On the other hand, gonadotropin treatment in male infants with CHH has the potential to achieve the biochemical changes of mini‐puberty, promote testicular growth and descent, in addition to increased penile length [13]. While there are studies in the literature exploring gonadotropin use in mini‐puberty [13], the majority are small case series, and few report long‐term follow‐up into the pubertal period [29]. Further well‐designed long‐term studies are necessary in this field. Our data confirms that approximately half of males with CHH diagnosed at a paediatric tertiary referral centre present during infancy, providing an opportunity for further research in intervention during the period of mini‐puberty.

Age at pubertal induction was later than the physiological norm for pubertal onset. This was paradoxically later in females than in males, despite the physiological onset of puberty in females being earlier than in males, highlighting the need for clinicians to be vigilant in the timely diagnosis of CHH in females with pubertal delay. Five male patients in this cohort were commenced on gonadotropin therapy in the most recent study year, coinciding with increased use of gonadotropins in other international centres with the goal of improving fertility outcomes for adult males with CHH [30]. There may be a potential added benefit of inducing testicular descent with gonadotropin deficiency and undescended testes, even in the adolescent age group [31, 32]. While induction of puberty using gonadotropins has been explored for more than two decades [14, 33], there remains only one randomised controlled trial in a paediatric cohort to date [11] and a limited number of studies reporting long‐term outcomes of gonadotropin use in puberty [34], highlighting the gap in high‐quality evidence and the need for more paediatric‐specific studies on gonadotropin treatment.

Limitations of this study include its retrospective design, resulting in missing data. Patient ascertainment largely depended on the accuracy of the initial diagnosis coding. While we were able to verify the complete retrieval of the current clinic cohort through clinician recall, we did not have a secondary source of confirmation for the older time periods. We may not have adequately captured syndromic forms of CHH outside CHARGE syndrome if CHH was not specifically coded into our database. It is also possible that patients with MPHD are under‐represented, particularly in females, if found to have CHH in the pubertal age without prior documentation of neonatal evidence of gonadotropin deficiency. Nevertheless, this study cohort from a tertiary paediatric centre covering a large geographical catchment area over three decades allowed for a relatively large sample size for the Australian population and a unique paediatric perspective for this rare condition. While it appears that patients were diagnosed at a younger age in the earlier time periods, this more likely reflects the tertiary referral patterns during those time periods. Furthermore, there may have been incomplete data ascertainment for the earliest time period, dependent on scanned medical records. It would also be important to recognise that this study cohort does not capture the group who never present to a tertiary paediatric endocrine centre, and cannot estimate the true population prevalence and lifelong burden of this condition.

Conclusions

The spectrum of CHH presentation to a tertiary paediatric centre is diverse, and despite advances over the 30‐year period of this study, there remain gaps in diagnostics and therapeutics in this field. Females with isolated HH are disproportionately under‐represented and under‐diagnosed compared to males, even more than reported in adult cohorts. At the same time, this study also identifies the expected cohort of male patients with CHH who present during infancy to tertiary paediatric centres who may be considered for early intervention studies to optimise lifelong outcomes for adults with CHH.
