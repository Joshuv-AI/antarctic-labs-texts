Reporting Quality of Randomized Controlled Trials in Elective Hand Surgery: A Systematic Review
Authors: Panagiotis Bompolas, Wameth A. Jamel, Ming Zien Yu, Yaqoob H. Al Saidi, Sina Dehnadi, Sreehari S. Pillai, Senthooran Kathiravelupillai, Raed Alderhali, Kian Daneshi, Apoorva Khajuria
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:The Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials—Nonpharmacologic Treatment guidelines aim to enhance the reporting quality of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) by promoting transparency, reproducibility, and reduced bias. Compliance of RCTs with these checklist criteria ensures transparency and reproducibility. Poor methodological reporting undermines study reproducibility and reliability of clinical recommendations. This systematic review assessed the adherence of RCTs in elective hand surgery (EHS) to the latest guidelines.Methods:After PROSPERO registration (CRD42025630289), a search was conducted in January 2025 across Cochrane, Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science for RCTs in EHS published since 2017. Two independent reviewers assessed studies against 42 Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials—Nonpharmacologic Treatments subcriteria. Overall compliance to each subcriterion and overall compliance of each RCT were calculated.Results:Fifty-eight RCTs met inclusion criteria, with a mean compliance of 66.1%. The most consistently reported items were “scientific background and rationale” and “participant allocation details” (100% each). The least reported were “attempts to limit bias without blinding” (13.2%) and “modifications to outcomes post commencement” (17.2%). The “methods” section had the lowest section-wise compliance (55.7%). A weak but significant correlation was found between author count and compliance (P = 0.0123, R2 = 0.103); no significant correlation was observed with journal impact factor (P = 0.26, R2 = 0.01) or citations per year (P = 0.1105, r = 0.2112).Conclusions:Reporting quality of RCTs in EHS remains moderate, with critical gaps in methodological transparency. Stronger collaboration among researchers, journals, funding agencies, and guideline developers is necessary to enhance adherence and ensure high-quality evidence.

Takeaways

Question: What is the effectiveness of reporting quality of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) in elective hand surgery according to the latest reporting guidelines?

Findings: This systematic review of 58 RCTs published since 2017 found a mean adherence of 66.1% to Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials—Nonpharmacologic Treatments criteria. Although items such as rationale and allocation were fully reported, key methodological details—such as bias control without blinding (13.2%) and postcommencement outcome changes (17.2%)—were frequently missing.

Meaning: RCTs in elective hand surgery show moderate adherence to reporting standards, with critical gaps that may limit reproducibility and evidence quality.

INTRODUCTION

Elective hand surgery constitutes a significant portion of plastic surgical care worldwide. For example, the incidence of carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) has been estimated at 424 diagnoses per 100,000 person-years.1 Carpal tunnel release remains one of the most frequently performed upper extremity procedures, with an estimated annual surgical volume ranging from 400,000 to 600,000 cases in the United States.2 In the United Kingdom, approximately 20.5 per 100,000 individuals undergo surgical correction of Dupuytren disease each year.3 Additionally, an average of 26,204 trigger finger release procedures are performed annually in the United States.4 In recent decades, such operations have become increasingly common, with a rise of 36% in related referrals and 34% in actual operations in the United Kingdom.5 CTS is the leading underlying diagnosis prompting referral, with cases nearly doubling from 59.7 to 112 per 100,000 people annually between 1990 and 2000, whereas carpal tunnel decompression operations in the United Kingdom increased by 29% between 2000 and 2005.5,6 The COVID-19 pandemic significantly reduced the number of elective hand operations; Uhlman et al7 reported a decrease of 42%, whereas Ghoshal et al8 reported a 72% reduction, but the latter’s analysis revealed that numbers have returned to 95% of prepandemic numbers after distribution of vaccines. Based on projections, it is estimated that in 2025, approximately 145,000 such operations will be performed in the United Kingdom, demonstrating the significant uptick in elective hand operations occurring nationwide, representing a heightened need for large-scale, high-quality hand trials.9

Concurrently, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) provide the highest level of evidence to evaluate and compare the efficacy of surgical interventions, thus providing the framework for critically appraised and standardized treatment protocols and, subsequently, guidance for safe clinical decision-making.10 With the aim to ensure robustness, transparency, reliability, and reproducibility of findings from RCTs, the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials for Nonpharmacological Treatments (CONSORT-NPT) checklist was introduced in 2008 and received its latest extension in 2017.11,12 This 42-item checklist provides guidelines for reporting various components of RCTs, including trial design, randomization, blinding, outcome measures, data analysis, implementation, and context. Failure to adhere to these guidelines and poor reporting practices jeopardize transparency, validity, and reproducibility of outcomes while also hindering accurate result interpretation and raising significant concerns regarding bias, data omission, and financial inefficiency.13 Considering the rising demand for these procedures and the fact that hand surgeons in Europe dedicate more than half of their working time to performing them, it is essential for researchers in the field to actively address these challenges.14

This systematic review assessed the overall and individual parametric compliance rates with the CONSORT-NPT extension checklist in RCTs involving elective hand operations. Furthermore, it highlighted particular subcriteria that RCTs demonstrate the lowest compliance rates with, while also emphasizing potential repercussions of suboptimal adherence. Its ultimate goal was to highlight the vital significance of strict compliance with the CONSORT-NPT, thereby encouraging journals to implement a robust and rigorous verification process and, potentially, assess the need of mandating full compliance as a guideline, instead of recommendation for optional adherence.

METHODOLOGY

Search

After registration of this systematic review in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (ID: CRD42025630289) on January 17, 2025, a systematic literature review was performed by 3 independent authors (P.B., W.A.J., S.S.P.) using Cochrane, Embase, PubMed, and Web of Science databases to identify relevant RCTs. The Peer Review of Electronic Search Strategies checklist was consulted for the development of the search strategy.15 After initial search, databases were browsed to include relevant keywords. The detailed search strategy is available in Supplemental Digital Content 1. (See Supplemental Digital Content 1, which demonstrates the search strategy used across databases, https://links.lww.com/PRSGO/E434.)

Double-blinded title and abstract screening of all identified relevant articles was performed by 2 independent authors (S.S.P., W.A.J.) with the aim to identify RCTs in the field of elective hand surgery published since 2017. After completion of this stage, blinding was overruled and disagreements between the 2 authors were resolved through consensus. The lead author (P.B.) was consulted to resolve all persisting discrepancies where deemed necessary. On finalizing the selection, full-text articles of all included studies were obtained and analyzed for data extraction. A structured outline of the search process in the form of Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) flow diagram is found in Figure 1.16 This systematic review adhered to the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for the conduct and reporting of systematic reviews. However, to evaluate the reporting quality of RCTs, we used the CONSORT-NPT checklist. This dual approach ensured that our review was methodologically transparent (per PRISMA) while allowing a structured appraisal of how well included RCTs complied with reporting standards (per CONSORT-NPT).

Scoring

Scoring process included assessing adherence of each RCT to each of the 42 subcriteria separately. Three outcomes were possible for each subcriterion. (1) If the study sufficiently reported the CONSORT-NPT subcriterion, it was awarded a “yes” (Y) and 1 point. (2) If the study did not sufficiently report the CONSORT-NPT subcriterion, it was awarded a “No” (N) and 0 points. (3) If a subcriterion was reported to be “not applicable” in 1 study, it received an “N/A” and 0 points; it was also subtracted from the total score for each study, to prevent skewing of the data. To ensure consistency in the application and assessment of the CONSORT-NPT checklist, 2 independent reviewers (P.B., W.A.J.) assessed each study for compliance with the checklist items. Interrater reliability was evaluated using Cohen kappa coefficient. Any discrepancies were resolved through discussion between the 2 reviewers and consensus with involvement of a senior author (K.D.).

Risk of Bias, Quality Assessment, and A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews-2

All included studies were analyzed for potential bias using the Cochrane Risk of Bias (RoB) 2.0 tool by 2 independent double-blinded authors (W.A.J., S.D.).17,18 As for the quality assessment of included studies, the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) system was implemented to evaluate quality of evidence and strength of recommendations.19–22 Guidance on the implemented method of quality assessment via the GRADE system was provided by Ryan and Hill.21 The methodological quality of our systematic review was critically appraised by the application of the A Measurement Tool to Assess Systematic Reviews-2 (AMSTAR-2) tool, originally introduced by Shea et al.23 We performed a systematic comparison of our study with 2 studies that assessed CONSORT compliance of RCTs in the field of surgery in the past.24,25

Statistical Analysis

Statistical correlation analysis was performed after input of all extracted data into Google Sheets (Mountain View, CA). Primary outcome measures were overall compliance of each study and overall compliance to each CONSORT-NPT subcriterion among all studies. Correlation coefficient (r), degrees of freedom (df), t statistic, R2, and P value were calculated to analyze association between overall compliance and number of authors, as well as overall compliance and journal impact factor (IF). A P value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Scoring was independently validated by a secondary reviewer (K.D.), with discrepancies resolved through consensus or consultation with the lead author (P.B.). Descriptive statistics were applied to analyze compliance trends and identify common reporting shortcomings.

RESULTS

Study Selection and Characteristics

The initial search yielded 1499 records, with 388 duplicates being removed. Titles and abstracts of 1111 studies were screened, leading to 74 full-text reviews. Of these, 16 were excluded due to being study protocols, non-RCTs, pilot trials, postoperative rehabilitation studies, nonelective procedures, or pre-2017 publications. Consequently, 58 studies met inclusion criteria for analysis.26–83 The year 2019 had the highest number of studies published (n = 19). In terms of continental and country output, Asia was most represented (n = 28) with China leading in country-specific contributions (n = 8). No multicountry studies were identified. Mean journal IF was 1.4. Across all studies, 5103 participants were included, with a mean age of 49.2 years; men constituted 48.1%, with CTS being the most investigated condition (n = 22, 37.9%).

Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials—Nonpharmacologic Treatments Compliance and Reporting Quality

Mean compliance across all subcriteria was 66.1%. Only subcriteria C2a and C13a were reported in 100% of studies. The lowest adherence was to C11c (13.2%), raising concerns regarding mitigation of performance and detection bias. Section-wise compliance showed that “introduction” (90.5%), “title and abstract” (93.1%), and “discussion” (81%) were well reported. Conversely, “methods” (55.7%), “results” (67.1%), and “other information” (54%) were suboptimal, impacting study reproducibility. Notably, 81% of studies failed to document adherence strategies (C5d). Additionally, 30.3% omitted funding disclosures (C25). Calculation of Interrater Reliability

Interrater reliability was evaluated with Cohen kappa coefficient (κ), which yielded a value of 0.91, indicating almost perfect agreement between reviewers.

Statistical Correlational Analysis

As seen in Figure 2, no significant correlation between CONSORT-NPT compliance and journal IF (P = 0.26, R2 = 0.01) was observed, suggesting IF does not predict reporting quality. Notably, RCTs published in journals with the highest IF had compliance rates of 76%, 89%, and 97% for IFs of 96.2, 19.6, and 9.1, respectively (r = 0.15, df = 56, t statistic = 1.14).

DISCUSSION

This systematic review highlighted significant variability in compliance scores among RCTs in elective hand surgery, with an overall compliance range from 28% to 100% and an average of 66.1%. This suggests moderate reporting quality, raising concerns about reproducibility and reliability in clinical research. Although sections like “introduction” (90.5%) and “title and abstract” (93.1%) are well reported, the “methods” section exhibits substantial deficiencies (55.7%). This discrepancy undermines the ability of future researchers to replicate studies and critically appraise their designs, as poor methodological reporting can lead to biases and misinterpretations of trial outcomes.84–86 Notably, 6 subcriteria in the “methods” section had adherence rates less than 25%, a level of deficiency not observed elsewhere. With fewer than 1 in 4 studies reporting these elements, reliability, transparency, and reproducibility are compromised. Inadequate reporting of adherence by healthcare providers and participants suggests minimal attention to ensuring interventions were consistently delivered and documented. Without clear documentation, it is impossible to determine if nonadherence influenced trial outcomes, increasing variance and reducing study power.86 The poorest compliance among all subcriteria was observed for reporting methods to minimize bias when blinding was impossible (C11c, 13.2%). Bias introduced by researchers, care providers, and participants when blinding is infeasible can distort treatment efficacy and safety profiles. Hróbjartsson et al87 found that trials without patient blinding tend to overestimate treatment effects. Lack of blinding also increases the risk of performance and detection biases, affecting study credibility. Similarly, a critical analysis of RCTs in hand, wrist, and elbow surgery revealed widespread methodological deficiencies, including inadequate blinding and poor reporting standards.88 Given that blinding is often challenging in surgical trials, failure to disclose bias mitigation strategies compromises study credibility and can mislead clinical decision-making. A scoping review of RCTs in hand surgery also identified blinding difficulties as a key challenge limiting high-quality research.89 These deficiencies may deter journals, regulatory bodies, and funding agencies from investing in future research, significantly hindering the field’s scientific development. Yin et al90 concluded that journals with higher IFs are more likely to endorse CONSORT guidelines, improving adherence. However, our review found no significant statistical correlation between CONSORT compliance and journal IF. This suggests that additional factors influence adherence, such as mandatory CONSORT endorsement. Among the journals analyzed, 62.1% mandated CONSORT-NPT adherence, whereas 37.9% only endorsed it. However, compliance rates between these groups were similar (67.1% versus 64.5%, P = 0.5534), suggesting that mandatory endorsement alone does not significantly enhance adherence. This finding demonstrates the complexity of compliance factors, which may include editorial enforcement, reviewer awareness, and author education. Geographic location of authors and study centers may also affect CONSORT compliance. Uetani et al91 found significant areas for improvement in RCT reporting in Japan, suggesting regional influences. Our review found no statistically significant difference in compliance among major continents. Studies from Asia (n = 28) had an average compliance of 63%, compared with 70.5% for studies from the Americas (n = 10) and 68% for European studies (n = 20) (P = 0.234). This suggests that although geographic variation may exist, other factors such as national funding priorities, institutional policies, and academic culture could play a more significant role in determining adherence to reporting guidelines.

The lack of correlation between CONSORT-NPT adherence and IF or mean citation count per year was surprising. One possible explanation is that editorial enforcement and peer-review stringency vary independently of journal prestige. Additionally, studies with high citation numbers may address clinically popular topics rather than exhibit superior reporting quality. Moreover, in the surgical field, citation numbers are often based on technique or clinical outcomes rather than methodological rigor of the study. Future studies could explore the influence of journal policies, author training, and institutional norms on reporting standards.

Our findings are consistent with previous systematic reviews assessing CONSORT compliance in surgical RCTs, which have reported moderate-to-low adherence rates across various specialties. However, it is worth noting that CONSORT compliance also varies by specialty. Ngah et al92 reported 67.3% compliance in RCTs on tuberculosis vaccines, whereas Yao et al93 reported significantly lower compliance (39%) in RCTs on ophthalmic surgery. Our review in elective hand surgery found a compliance equal to 66.1%. These differences highlight the need for further reviews across medical and surgical fields to improve reporting and ensure high-quality evidence. Elagami et al94 found that RCTs with higher RoB had lower CONSORT compliance (P < 0.001). However, our study found no statistically significant correlation between RoB and compliance (r = −0.176, P = 0.187). Although a weak inverse relationship suggests that lower-risk studies tend to have slightly higher compliance, the lack of statistical significance implies that other factors may play a more substantial role in adherence.

This study has limitations. The analysis included RCTs published since 2017 and only in English, limiting the ability to assess long-term trends. Excluding non-English studies may have introduced selection bias, omitting high-quality evidence in other languages. RCT protocols and conference abstracts were also excluded, preventing analysis of their CONSORT adherence. Moreover, although journal IF and CONSORT enforcement were considered, other factors, such as editorial policies and peer-review rigor, were not accounted for. RoB and quality assessments using RoB 2.0 and GRADE may have been influenced by subjectivity and interrater variability.95 Another limitation is that the thresholds used to categorize CONSORT compliance percentages are not universally standardized across the literature. Different studies have proposed their own classification systems based on specific research contexts, leading to potential variability in the assessment of reporting quality.20,92 As a result, the interpretation of CONSORT compliance may differ depending on the criteria applied by individual authors.

Improving RCT quality in elective hand surgery is essential. Increased compliance correlates with CONSORT adoption by journals; thus, we recommend that journals implement robust CONSORT guidelines both before submission and during peer review. The EQUATOR (enhancing the quality and transparency of health research) Network emphasizes that publishing a reporting guideline alone is insufficient; an implementation plan is needed to ensure researchers adopt it.96 Strategies include journal and organizational endorsement, conference presentations, and diverse dissemination methods. Additionally, funding agencies and academic institutions should incorporate reporting standards into research training programs to ensure compliance. The International Committee of Medical Journal Editors advises journals to provide clear author instructions on reporting standards, which can be facilitated through collaboration with guideline developers.97

CONCLUSIONS

This systematic review highlights moderate adherence to CONSORT-NPT guidelines in RCTs on elective hand surgery, with persistent gaps in methodological transparency. Although journal IF did not predict better compliance, multiauthor studies were associated with improved adherence, underscoring the value of collaborative research. Future efforts should focus on institutional and editorial strategies, such as CONSORT training, rigorous peer review, and funder-driven requirements, to improve reporting quality and ensure robust, transparent evidence for clinical decision-making.
