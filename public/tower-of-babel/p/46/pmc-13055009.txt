Transcranial direct current stimulation of the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex improves dual-task performance in people with stroke
Authors: Siyue Li, Peiming Chen, Yu Qin, Thomson W. L. Wong, Shamay S. M. Ng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGait and cognitive dysfunction in people with stroke are often exacerbated during dual tasks, limiting their ability to perform daily tasks. This study aimed to investigate the effects of anodal transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) over the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC) on dual-task performance in individuals with chronic stroke. Unlike previous studies that primarily targeted motor cortical regions (e.g., M1), our innovative approach focuses on stimulating the DLPFC, a key cognitive region, to enhance cognitive-motor resource allocation, reduce dual-task costs (DTCs), and ultimately improve both mobility and cognitive performance.MethodsIn this sham-controlled, within-subject crossover study, 53 participants with chronic stroke underwent two randomized tDCS sessions (active and sham) over 1 week. Following 30 min of stimulation, a comprehensive assessment was conducted to evaluate both motor and cognitive DTCs, gait parameters, cognitive functions across various single- and dual-task conditions.ResultsAnodal tDCS over the left DLPFC significantly improved lower limb mobility, including gait speed (p < 0.001), stride length (p < 0.001), and foot strike angle (p < 0.001), compared to sham stimulation. Enhanced cognitive performance was observed during dual-task conditions, with significant improvements in Random Number (p = 0.001), Counting Forward (p < 0.001), and Serial Subtraction (p = 0.001) tasks. Notably, tDCS reduced DTCs by improving cognitive response rate during Serial Subtraction (p = 0.002). These effects were specific to dual-task conditions, as no significant changes were observed during single-task conditions.ConclusionsAnodal tDCS over the left DLPFC reduces DTCs by enhancing cognitive-motor integration, leading to significant improvements in both mobility and cognitive efficiency in individuals with chronic stroke. This novel approach highlights the DLPFC as a promising therapeutic target for addressing dual-task impairments in stroke rehabilitation.Trial registration: This trial was registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06818240).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-026-01890-2.

Introduction

Daily activities often require individuals to perform motor and cognitive tasks simultaneously, such as walking while remembering directions or engaging in social interactions [1, 2]. Stroke survivors are particularly susceptible to dual-task interference, which can manifest as slower gait speed, reduced walking adaptability, and impaired cognitive task performance during dual-task walking (DTW) [3]. This heightens the risk of falls post-stroke, as dual-task capacity often remains considerably impaired despite rehabilitation [4].

Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS) is a non-invasive neuromodulation technique that delivers low-amplitude direct currents to targeted cortical regions via scalp electrodes. The current flows between an active (stimulating) electrode and a reference electrode, modulating neuronal excitability and influencing synaptic plasticity. When the active electrode serves as the anode, it depolarizes underlying neurons to increase excitability, whereas the cathode hyperpolarizes neurons to decrease excitability [5, 6]. In post-stroke patients, tDCS has been shown to enhance both cognitive and motor functions, potentially by increasing cortical excitability, improving functional connectivity, and modulating cerebral perfusion [7–10]. The concurrent activation of neurons induced by tDCS can elicit long‑term potentiation (LTP)‑like increases in synaptic strength, as supported by mechanistic evidence that anodal tDCS produces N‑methyl‑D‑aspartate (NMDA)‑receptor–dependent, use‑dependent plasticity in the human motor cortex [11]. Emerging evidence from studies involving healthy young adults [12], healthy older adults [1, 13], and older adults with slow gait and mild-to-moderate executive dysfunction [14] suggests that tDCS may enhance dual-task performance and reduce dual-task costs (DTCs), highlighting its potential as a complementary intervention for improving dual-task abilities.

However, evidence regarding the effects of tDCS on people with stroke in dual-task performance remains limited and inconclusive. A recent double-blinded randomized controlled trial in chronic stroke survivors compared different tDCS montages and found that a single session of bilateral tDCS (anode over the ipsilesional primary motor cortex [M1] and cathode over the contralesional M1) and cathodal tDCS (cathode over the contralesional M1 and anode over the contralateral supraorbital ridge), in which the cathodal (negative) electrode decreased cortical excitability in the targeted area, was associated with increased contralesional corticomotor inhibition and improved dual‑task gait performance [15]. In a subsequent study from the same research group, cathodal tDCS followed by treadmill training improved cognitive dual-task walking and modulated contralesional cortical activity, whereas bilateral tDCS combined with treadmill training did not yield additional benefits for either cognitive or motor dual-task walking in chronic stroke survivors [16]. Moreover, a pilot randomized controlled trial in subacute stroke participants examined five sessions of dual-tDCS (anodal over the lesioned M1 and cathodal over the non-lesioned M1) combined with task-specific training, and found no significant improvements in gait or lower-limb performance compared with training alone [17].

Collectively, previous studies on tDCS interventions for dual-task performance in stroke survivors have primarily focused on stimulating motor cortical regions (e.g., M1), with results varying across stimulation montages, stroke phases (chronic vs. subacute), and training combinations, yielding inconsistent results. These mixed outcomes led us to speculate that the efficacy of tDCS may depend on the stimulation sites used [18]; stimulating the M1 alone may not adequately modulate the cognitive processes required for dual-task performance. Therefore, exploring alternative cortical targets, such as the left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC), a region critically involved in executive function, attentional control, and task-switching [12, 19, 20], may provide new insights into enhancing dual-task performance in stroke rehabilitation. Additionally, the left DLPFC is particularly specialized for verbal working memory, logical reasoning, and resource allocation, aligning with the cognitive demands of dual-tasking [21]. Damage to this region following a stroke can impair task-switching, dividing attention, and maintaining focus on multiple activities simultaneously [22, 23]. Emerging evidence highlights the potential of targeting the left DLPFC to enhance dual-task performance. For instance, studies have shown that simultaneously targeting both M1 and the left DLPFC during complex walking tasks significantly reduces dual-task gait costs in older adults [13]. Likewise, in patients with Parkinson’s disease (PD), a single session of tDCS over the left DLPFC led to greater reductions in dual-task costs compared to sham stimulation, particularly under conditions of higher task complexity [24]. Furthermore, repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) over the left DLPFC has demonstrated a clinically significant improvement in dual-task gait speed compared to M1 stimulation in individuals with chronic stroke [3]. Although rTMS applies magnetic pulses to induce transient neuronal depolarization and tDCS delivers a low-intensity direct current to modulate cortical excitability, both methods enhance neural plasticity and promote functional improvements within targeted networks [25]. Thus, we anticipate that targeting the left DLPFC with tDCS may yield comparable behavioral effects by targeting shared neural circuits involved in gait regulation and cognitive control in stroke rehabilitation. These findings collectively underscore that the left DLPFC is a superior target for enhancing dual-task performance, as it is a core hub for integrating cognitive and motor functions. Importantly, many previous studies have primarily assessed dual-task effects by focusing on motor changes in gait speed, which may be insufficient to capture the complexity of dual-task performance. Both motor and cognitive domains, as well as their relative changes, should be evaluated when interpreting the effects of tDCS on dual-task performance.

Therefore, in this study, we specifically targeted the left DLPFC to investigate the effects of anodal tDCS on dual-task mobility and cognitive performance in individuals with chronic stroke. Anodal stimulation was chosen because it enhances cortical excitability and promotes synaptic plasticity in the stimulated region, whereas cathodal tDCS typically exerts inhibitory effects [5, 26]. Enhancing excitability in the DLPFC is particularly relevant, as this region supports executive functions, attentional control, and task coordination that are central to dual-task performance [27–29]. Unlike prior studies that predominantly focused on motor outcomes, we employed a comprehensive dual-task assessment, evaluating both gait and cognitive performance and quantifying DTCs associated with transitioning from single-task to dual-task conditions. We hypothesized that anodal tDCS over the DLPFC would enhance both motor and cognitive performance during dual tasks and effectively reduce DTCs compared to sham stimulation in individuals with chronic stroke.

Methods

Study design

This was a randomized, double-blind, crossover, sham-controlled study (ClinicalTrials.gov; ID: NCT06818240). The study protocol was approved by the University Ethics Committee (Reference Number: HSEARS20240116001) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Sample size calculation

The sample size for this study was estimated using G*power 3.1.9.7. An effect size of Cohen’s d = 0.87 was obtained from a previous study [15] that found that a single session of 20 min anodal tDCS over the ipsilesional M1 [median (interquartile range) = 3.7 (0.5, 6.2)] was superior to sham tDCS [median (interquartile range) = 1.0 (0.2, 4.0)] in increasing the walking speed of people with chronic stroke in a cognitive dual task. The alpha level and power were assumed to be 0.05 and 80%, respectively. The allocation ratio of the two groups was set at 1:1. The minimum sample size was calculated to be 22 per group. To account for potential dropouts and to strengthen the robustness of the findings, a total of 56 participants were initially recruited. After accounting for attrition, the final sample size included in the analysis was 53.

Participants

Overall, 53 participants with chronic stroke were recruited from patient self-help groups and non-governmental organisations in Hong Kong between November 2024 and January 2025. The participants’ characteristics, including stroke diagnosis, age, gender, stroke type, lesion site, and post-stroke duration, are summarized in Table 2. The inclusion criteria were (1) aged 50–80 years; (2) at least 6 months post-first-ever stroke with unilateral motor deficits; (3) ability to walk independently for at least 10 m without the use of walking aids; and (4) a score of 22 or above on the MoCA [30]. The exclusion criteria were: (1) unstable medical conditions, such as heart failure, uncontrolled hypertension, or cancer; (2) presence of other comorbidities that could interfere with gait; (3) history of contraindications to tDCS, such as epilepsy or metal implants; and (4) history of head injury, head surgery, or lower limb orthopaedic issues. The study was conducted in the Neurorehabilitation Laboratory at The Hong Kong Polytechnic University.

Study protocol

A flowchart summarizing the study design and sequence of procedures was depicted in Fig. 1. Each participant completed two testing sessions (anodal tDCS and sham tDCS), with a 1-week washout period between sessions to minimize potential learning effects. Randomization was conducted using a computer‑generated sequence, and both participants and assessors were blinded to the stimulation condition. tDCS was administered for 30 min (anodal or sham, randomized). Following stimulation, participants performed a set of single‑ and dual‑task conditions in a randomized counterbalanced order to control for potential order effects.

Transcranial direct current stimulation

In the active tDCS session, stimulation was delivered via a current stimulator (DC-STIMULATOR MC, NeuroConn, Germany) using saline-soaked 4 × 6 cm² electrodes. The anode was positioned over the left DLPFC, identified as F3 according to the 10–20 international EEG system, and the cathode over the right supraorbital ridge. A constant current of 2 mA was applied for 30 min, with a 30-s ramp-up and ramp-down for comfort [31]. Continuous monitoring of electrode impedance ensured stable connections throughout the session. Studies had shown that that typical tDCS therapeutic protocols, involving 10–30 min sessions, can induce short-term neurophysiological changes that last up to several hours [5, 32–34]. For the sham condition, no active current was applied throughout the stimulation phase except for an electrical current of 1 mA, which was delivered for the first and last 60 s (30-s ramp-up and ramp-down periods). This protocol mimicked the initial sensation of stimulation to control for potential sensation bias, ensuring subjects could not distinguish between active and sham tDCS interventions [35].

To quantify the stimulation strength of all simulated tDCS configurations, we used SimNIBS to extract the normal component of the electric field in realistic head models based on MRI-derived anatomy [36]. We modelled an anodal electrode positioned over the left DLPFC at the F3 electrode location, with the cathodal electrode placed over the right supraorbital region (Fp2). The electric field distribution was computed during active and sham tDCS conditions, with sham modelling involving a negligible current to simulate placebo effects. The resulting field distributions were then interpolated over the cortical surface to visualize the spatial patterns of the normal component of the electric field (Fig. 1B).

Single‑ and dual‑task descriptions

Participants performed the Stand and Walk for a 20-m Round Trip (SWR) test, which simulated daily functional mobility and was used to assess gait performance. The task involved maintaining standing stability for 10 s, initiating gait, walking 10 m forward, turning around, and then returning 10 m to the starting point [1]. Before testing, all participants received clear instructions and practiced the task to ensure understanding. A 60‑second rest interval was provided between each trial to minimize fatigue.

Under single-task conditions, participants either completed the SWR test at their self‑selected normal walking speed or performed one of several cognitive tasks while standing still. The cognitive tasks included Random Number, Counting Forward, Serial Subtraction, and Word Association tasks (details refer to Table 1). For these trials, the total SWR completion time and the number of correct cognitive responses were recorded separately.

Under dual-task conditions, participants simultaneously performed the SWR test while engaging in either a cognitive or a motor task. Participants were instructed to evenly distribute their attention between walking and cognitive tasks. For each dual‑task trial, both the SWR completion time and the number of correct responses were recorded. These tasks had been widely used and validated in dual-task performance research as being able to reliably engage cognitive processes, such as attention, working memory, and executive function, and as being sensitive to changes in cognitive load [37–40].

Blinding procedures

Randomization and programming of the tDCS unit were conducted by an independent researcher who was not involved in data collection or analysis. Sham group experienced a similar sensation during the first and last seconds of stimulation to make participants believe they were receiving active stimulation. Subjects remained blinded to the stimulation condition throughout the study. Blinding effectiveness was evaluated at the study endpoint by asking participants, ‘Do you think you received real brain stimulation?’ Responses were scored as 1 for correct answers and 0 for incorrect answers. The blinding procedure was effective, as indicated by the Chi-square test results (χ²(1) = 2.43, p = 0.118), showing no significant difference in participants’ ability to correctly identify sham versus active tDCS, thereby indicating a balance in placebo effects.

Gait parameters

Kinematic data were collected using a motion tracking system, the MobilityLab system (APDM Inc. Portland, OR, USA), and the Opal IMU sensor module (with 128-Hz sampling frequency). Eight sensors were affixed using Velcro belts, positioned on the sternum, the lumbar region, and each of the upper legs, lower legs, and feet. This configuration was sufficient to estimate stand-to-walk transitions and turning movements using established algorithms.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS version 26.0 and GraphPad Prism 10 software. For normally distributed data, results were presented as the mean ± standard deviation (SD), and paired-samples t-tests were used to compare differences between the anode tDCS and sham groups under single- and dual-task conditions. For non-normally distributed data, results were presented as median (25th, 75th percentile), and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used to identify significant differences between the two groups. Bonferroni correction was applied to address the issue of multiple comparisons across the various dual-task conditions and outcome measures. Statistical significance was set at the corrected threshold. Effect sizes (r) are reported for all key comparisons to aid interpretation of clinical relevance.

tDCS enhanced lower limb mobility in cognitive dual-task conditions

We examined lower limb gait parameters under various cognitive and motor dual-task conditions, as summarized in Table 3. The results revealed that active tDCS demonstrated a robust ability to enhance gait performance during cognitive dual-task conditions. Gait speed showed notable improvements during the Word Association task, with both the affected leg (t(52) = 3.75, p < 0.001, r = 0.95), and the unaffected leg (Md = 0.03, W = 701, p < 0.001, r = 0.94). Similarly, stride length increased significantly in both legs following active tDCS compared to sham stimulation. During the Counting Forward task, improvements were observed for the affected leg (t(52) = 3.53, p = 0.001, r = 0.96) and the unaffected leg (t(52) = 3.79, p < 0.001, r = 0.97). The Word Association task also showed significant increases in stride length for the affected leg (t(52) = 4.17, p < 0.001, r = 0.96) and the unaffected leg (t(52) = 4.21, p < 0.001, r = 0.95).

Additionally, the foot strike angle on the unaffected side showed significant improvements during cognitive tasks. Specifically, these enhancements were observed in the Random Number (t(52) = 3.44, p < 0.001, r = 0.94), Counting Forward (t(52) = 4.18, p < 0.001, r = 0.93), Serial Subtraction (t(52) = 4.24, p < 0.001, r = 0.93), and Word Association (t(52) = 4.76, p < 0.001, r = 0.93). However, mobility parameters during motor dual-task conditions, such as Head Turns and Visual Fixation tasks, did not show significant changes with tDCS.

tDCS improved cognitive performance in dual-task conditions

The effects of tDCS on cognitive function under both single-task and dual-task conditions were summarized in Table 4. Under dual-task conditions, tDCS markedly enhanced the word generation rate during the Random Number (Md = 0.07, W = 699, p < 0.001, r = 0.62), Counting Forward (Md = 0.05, W = 821, p < 0.001, r = 0.83), and Serial Subtraction (t(52) = 3.46, p < 0.001, r = 0.90) tasks. These results demonstrated the efficacy of tDCS in improving cognitive performance when participants were engaged in cognitive-demanding dual-task conditions.

In contrast, tDCS had minimal effects on cognitive performance under single-task conditions, except for the Word Association task. Here, active tDCS significantly enhanced the response rate compared to sham stimulation in both dual-task (t(52) = 6.33, p < 0.001, r = 0.81) and single-task conditions (t(52) = 2.84, p = 0.006, r = 0.82). These findings highlighted the specific benefit of tDCS in boosting cognitive performance during demanding tasks, particularly those requiring verbal and numerical processing, while its effects under less challenging conditions remain limited.

tDCS reduced cognitive DTC in dual-task conditions

To further investigate the impact of tDCS on mobility and cognitive performance under different dual tasks, we calculated the DTC on mobility speed and cognitive response speed in Table 5. Meanwhile, Fig. 1further visualized the patterns of cognitive- motor interference across tasks.

Although tDCS enhanced participants’ overall walking performance, as reflected by increased gait speed during the Word Association task, there was no statistically significant improvement in DTC-mobility speed during the same task. While both the affected (t(52) = 2.51, p = 0.015, r = 0.65) and unaffected (t(52) = 2.64, p = 0.011, r = 0.64) lower limbs showed a trend toward improvement, these changes did not reach significance after Bonferroni correction. Furthermore, no significant differences in DTC-mobility speed were observed across other dual-task conditions, such as the Random Number, Counting Forward, Head Turns, or Visual Fixation tasks (p > 0.09- 0.88). This suggested that, despite some positive trends, tDCS did not produce robust improvements in DTC-mobility speed across the range of cognitive-motor dual tasks assessed.

Regarding DTC on cognitive speed, a statistically significant improvement was observed in the Serial Subtraction task following tDCS (Md = 10.19, W = 674, p < 0.002, r = 0.23), even after Bonferroni correction. Additionally, the Word Association task (Md = 6.1, W = 559, p = 0.013, r = 0.20) and the Random Number task (Md = 7.65, W = 435, p = 0.054, r = 0.22) demonstrated trends toward reduced cognitive DTC, although these did not reach statistical significance.

Additionally, as shown in Fig. 2, both panels (A and B) depict DTC distributions across all tasks under sham and active tDCS conditions. The figure reveals slight upwards shifts in the box‑and‑whisker plots for cognitively demanding tasks, whereas simpler tasks show minimal or no visible change, indicating that tDCS effects were primarily observed under higher cognitive loads. The Serial Subtraction and Word Association tasks consistently exhibited the most negative baseline DTC values, around − 17.96% in cognitive DTC for Serial Subtraction and to − 19.21% in mobility DTC for Word Association, indicating that these two tasks posed the greatest challenge for participants under dual-task conditions. Following tDCS, the median DTC values for these tasks increased. For Serial Subtraction, cognitive DTC increased from − 17.96% to − 7.77%, corresponding to a 10.2‑point (≈ 57%) reduction in interference. Similarly, in the Word Association task, tDCS reduced cognitive DTC by 42%. Mobility DTC in Word Association improved from − 19.21% to − 15.74%, equivalent to a 3.5‑point (≈ 18%) reduction. The Serial Subtraction task exhibited a statistically significant improvement in cognitive DTC (p = 0.002), whereas the Word Association task showed a moderate improvement that approached significance (p = 0.015). Altogether, tDCS produced a mean ~ 40–60% improvement in cognitive DTC across demanding tasks but only ~ 10–20% improvement in mobility DTC, indicating that cognitive control benefited more strongly than gait control. In contrast, Random Number, Counting Forward, Head Turns, and Visual Fixation tasks showed minimal change (ΔDTC < 2 points or < 10%), and none reached statistical significance (p > 0.05). These tasks had relatively small baseline interference (–8% to − 13%), leaving limited potential for additional improvement.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this was the first study to investigate the effects of left DLPFC stimulation on dual-task performance in individuals with chronic stroke. By identifying the DLPFC as a novel therapeutic target, our study provided new insights into enhancing cognitive-motor integration. Notably, unlike previous studies that primarily focused on mobility outcomes, this was also the first to prioritize cognitive responses during dual tasks in stroke survivors. This approach offered a more comprehensive perspective on dual-task performance, bridging the gap between cognitive and motor domains in stroke recovery. Specifically, our findings demonstrated that tDCS significantly improved both gait speed and cognitive response rates compared to sham stimulation, with the most pronounced benefits observed during complex dual-task conditions. Notably, the Serial Subtraction task, which showed the greatest cognitive DTC, and the Word Association task, which had the highest mobility DTC, both exhibited marked improvements following tDCS. Moreover, tDCS produced a greater reduction in cognitive DTCs than in mobility DTCs across all tasks, highlighting its ability to enhance cognitive processing during dual-task conditions. These results suggested that tDCS targeting the left DLPFC could alleviate cognitive load by optimizing resource allocation and increasing the efficiency of prefrontal networks, thereby supporting better integration of cognitive and motor functions in individuals with chronic stroke. This novel insight positioned left DLPFC stimulation as a promising therapeutic strategy to enhance dual-task performance and functional independence in people with stroke.

DLPFC: targeted tDCS boosts cognitive efficiency in dual-task stroke rehabilitation

Studies had shown that anodal tDCS targeting the left DLPFC improved dual-task performance by enhancing processing speed and reducing time delays in healthy adults [49–51]. Our study aligned with these findings and extended them to the stroke population, representing a novel approach. Our findings revealed that tDCS targeting the left DLPFC primarily enhanced cognitive task performance, with the greatest impact observed in the DTC on cognitive speed.

Specifically, significant improvements in cognitive efficiency were restricted to the Serial Subtraction task, where active tDCS led to a substantial 57% reduction in cognitive. This selective enhancement is highly relevant when considering the known neural substrates of different cognitive tasks, particularly arithmetic. The task of Serial Subtraction requires exact arithmetic calculations, which have shown to be strongly associated with the language-dependent network involving the left inferior frontal lobe and angular gyrus [52]. The left DLPFC, which overlaps and interacts significantly with these left-lateralized language and executive function areas, is ideally positioned to modulate the cognitive processes required for exact calculation and verbal retrieval during the Serial Subtraction task. The boosting of performance in this specific task, which relies heavily on language-dependent coding of arithmetic facts, reinforces the interpretation that anodal tDCS over the left DLPFC preferentially facilitates the verbal or executive network [52].

In contrast, although cognitive DTC in the Word Association task was reduced by 42% following tDCS, this improvement did not reach statistical significance. The Word Association task, while also verbal, primarily taxes verbal fluency and retrieval, which may be less dependent on the immediate, rapid processing boost afforded by the DLPFC stimulation compared to the structured retrieval of arithmetic facts. Furthermore, the limited impact on mobility DTC (e.g., in the Word Association task, mobility DTC improved by only 18%) aligns with the understanding that the DLPFC predominantly modulates executive and working-memory control rather than direct neuromotor output. Gait control is largely mediated by motor networks such as M1 and the cerebellum. These results, therefore, highlight the cognitive and task specificity of prefrontal tDCS, suggesting that its primary benefit in dual-task stroke rehabilitation lies in facilitating left-lateralized, language-associated cognitive operations, such as exact arithmetic calculation, rather than providing a generalized boost to all cognitive processes or mobility components.

Interestingly, our additional analyses revealed a significant negative correlation (r = -0.397, p = 0.003) between baseline cognitive function (measured by MoCA score) and the improvement in cognitive dual-task performance, specifically in the Word Association task. This indicated that participants with lower baseline cognitive function experienced significantly greater improvement in this task following anodal tDCS. This finding suggested that tDCS may be particularly beneficial for stroke patients with cognitive impairment, especially in tasks requiring verbal fluency, which often play a critical role in daily life (e.g., engaging in conversations, naming objects, or problem-solving under dual-task conditions). For other dual tasks, such as Counting Forward, we did not observe significant correlations. One possible explanation was that these tasks may be less reliant on the DLPFC and more dependent on other neural circuits or automatic processes, which were less affected by tDCS [53]. This differentiation in task reliance on the DLPFC highlighted the specificity of tDCS effects on certain cognitive domains, particularly those involving verbal fluency and executive function.

tDCS-induced cognitive improvements in dual-task performance probably stemed from the DLPFC’s role in cognitive–motor integration. The DLPFC was critical for executive functions [54], attention [55], short-term memory [27], multitasking [56], and postural control and gait regulation [57–60]. As part of the executive control network, the left DLPFC played a key role in interference control and managing competing task demands during dual-task processing [61]. Anodal tDCS probably enhanced its capacity to manage task interference and optimize task shielding, thereby increasing the efficiency of stimulus response processing and decreasing task crosstalk. Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) studies had further demonstrated that DLPFC-targeted tDCS enhances dual-task performance by reducing prefrontal overactivation [62], increasing neural efficiency and optimizing information processing [14, 63]. Additionally, tDCS appeared to facilitate more focal and efficient task-related activity in critical neural areas while enhancing functional connectivity across broader anatomical networks [64]. Studies using functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) had consistently shown that DLPFC activity increases during dual-tasking, reflecting its central role in managing task demands. Moreover, tDCS had been shown to change neurotransmitter concentrations, directly affecting cognitive functions [65]. tDCS over the DLPFC also modulated dopamine and GABA activity in the basal ganglia-cortical circuit, indicating that its effects extended to deeper brain regions critical for executive functioning [66].

tDCS improves gait metrics in dual-task stroke rehabilitation

Stroke survivors typically exhibited cognitive-motor interference, characterized by slower walking speed, impaired gait stability, and reduced cognitive accuracy during dual-task performance [67–69]. Evidence suggested that the application of tDCS during dual tasks positively influences gait metrics, resulting in increased gait speed, reduced stride time variability, and decreased postural sway in individuals with stroke [49, 70].

In the present study, we observed improvements following tDCS began at the distal joint level, particularly in parameters such as foot strike angle and toe-off angle, which subsequently translated into better stride length and single-limb support, ultimately improving overall performance metrics, including increased gait speed, cadence, and reduced cycle duration. Interestingly, under the cognitive dual-task conditions, the foot strike angle of the unaffected limb normalized, reflecting better inter-limb coordination and less reliance on a compensatory heel-strike strategy. This finding aligns with previous observations that, in people with chronic stroke, the unaffected limb typically landed with an exaggerated heel angle to secure stability, while the paretic limb exhibited a markedly reduced toe-off and shortened single-limb support [71]. Concurrently, the affected limb generated a larger toe-off angle, indicating better propulsion and muscle strength during the push-off phase, thereby distributing workload more evenly. Additionally, tDCS appeared to enhance single-limb support on the unaffected side during challenging tasks like the Word Association task, suggesting greater stability on the paretic limb and less reliance on the non-paretic limb. Improvements in single-limb support and push-off dynamics contributed to increased stride length, cadence, and gait speed, a key indicator of walking efficiency and recovery. Notably, the enhancements after tDCS were particularly pronounced in the unaffected limb, which generated greater propulsion and provided additional stability. By taking on more of the mechanical demand, the unaffected limb lessened the compensatory load on the affected side, leading to better motor control, reduced gait asymmetry, and improved overall walking performance during dual-task conditions.

The observed improvements in mobility can be explained by a task prioritization model [72], which suggested that attentional resource allocation depends on factors such as postural reserve, hazard estimation, individual traits, and task complexity. Dual-tasking often required serial rather than parallel processing, leading to diminished performance in one or both tasks [73–76]. tDCS over the DLPFC had been shown to enhance cognitive flexibility [77], attentional control, and working memory [78], allowing individuals to allocate cognitive resources more efficiently between walking and concurrent tasks. At baseline, the participants appeared to prioritize gait performance over cognitive tasks, probably at the expense of cognitive performance to minimize the risk of falls [79, 80], particularly evident during the challenging Serial Subtraction task, which had the lowest DTC-cognitive speed. This indicated high cognitive and mobility DTCs, reflecting the difficulty of balancing working memory and attention with gait stability, as more difficult cognitive tasks required more activation of the DLPFC, and other brain regions, such as the cortical areas along the inferior frontal sulcus, the middle frontal gyrus, and the intraparietal sulcus, which were involved in dual-task performance [56]. However, after tDCS, the participants appeared to distribute attention more evenly, managing both cognitive and mobility tasks more effectively. This resulted in enhanced mobility and improved gait metrics.

Clinical application and limitations of tDCS in dual-task stroke rehabilitation

Anodal tDCS over the left DLPFC had significant potential to improve dual-task performance in individuals with stroke, particularly in challenging tasks that require verbal fluency and memory abilities. For example, it was especially effective in tasks such as walking while performing the Word Association or Serial Subtraction task, mimicking real-life situations like walking while engaging in meaningful conversations or recalling a shopping list while navigating a crowded area. In such situations, where cognitive load was high and motor coordination was also required, the benefits of tDCS were most pronounced as it helped to optimize the integration of cognitive and motor functions. Similarly, tasks involving executive and attentional functions, such as the Counting Forward, or Random Number, which resemble everyday situations like walking while making simple decisions (e.g., choosing the fastest route home), responding to a single external stimulus (e.g., noticing a traffic light change), or maintaining focus on a singular task (e.g., counting steps while walking). Being less demanding, these tasks showed relatively smaller benefits from tDCS compared to more complex dual-task situations. Additionally, patients with more pronounced deficits in executive or attentional functions may benefit from targeted tDCS stimulations. For stroke patients with mild to moderate cognitive impairments who retain sufficient functional capacity to engage in dual-task training, a structured tDCS protocol paired with progressively challenging dual-task exercises (e.g., obstacle navigation while performing verbal tasks), could be implemented. Patients with severe impairments, may require modified approaches, such as passive motor tasks or virtual reality-based cognitive training, to maximize engagement and benefit.

Despite improvements in cognitive dual-task cost were observed, the effect sizes were modest, and certain tasks showed only trends toward improvement without reaching statistical significance. This raised a critical question regarding the retention of tDCS effects and their translation into long-term clinical applications. In our study. We utilized only a single session of tDCS with dual-task assessments conducted immediately after stimulation. Evidence from prior studies in other neurological conditions suggested that repeated stimulation over several weeks may enhance neuroplasticity, leading to more enduring effects [81, 82]. Future research is recommended to explore multi-sessions of tDCS in supporting long-term functional recovery and independence in stroke survivors. Furthermore, in the context of stroke rehabilitation, combining multi-session tDCS with task-specific training (e.g., dual-task walking exercises) holds promise for strengthening neural pathways involved in cognitive-motor integration [83].

This study had several limitations. First, the mechanisms by which tDCS improved dual-task performance remain unclear. It is uncertain whether the observed effects were due to specific modulation of the left DLPFC, broader changes in brain excitability, or interactions with cortical networks. Cathodal stimulation of the right prefrontal cortex may suppress cortical excitability and thereby attenuate the cognitive improvement effects of tDCS [5, 27]. Such modulation could, in turn, alter the cognitive–motor interplay observed during dual‑task performance. To minimize such effects in future studies, alternative electrode montages, namely, using extra-cephalic reference electrodes or smaller electrodes to reduce non-specific results [53]. Additionally, future studies should employ neurophysiological tools such as TMS, fMRI, fNIRS, or EEG to clarify these mechanisms. Second, as other brain regions (e.g., M1, parietal cortex, contralateral prefrontal cortex) [84, 85] also contributed to dual-task performance, future research could investigate multi-site stimulation protocols to assess whether distributed stimulation is more effective than single-site approaches. Third, the absence of follow-up assessments limited our understanding of the duration and potential cumulative benefits of tDCS, making it difficult to determine whether the observed effects were sustained over time or if additional sessions could have further enhanced outcomes. Future research should also include tDCS protocols tailored to individual patients based on stroke severity, lesion location, cognitive-motor deficits and multi-session protocols to optimize treatment effects. Forth, the sample size was estimated based on a previous study with a similar design. However, caution is warranted, as the study design and objectives of that prior research may not fully align with those of the current study. This discrepancy may have introduced inaccuracies in determining the optimal sample size required. Increasing the sample size in future research could help address this limitation and reduce the risk of insufficient statistical power.

Conclusions

In summary, anodal tDCS targeting the left DLPFC improved dual‑task performance in individuals with stroke, primarily through reductions in the cognitive dual‑task cost observed in high‑demand executive and verbal tasks, such as Serial Subtraction and Word Association. These findings suggest that tDCS may mitigate task interference by enhancing cognitive control, thereby facilitating coordination between cognitive and motor processes in stroke rehabilitation.
