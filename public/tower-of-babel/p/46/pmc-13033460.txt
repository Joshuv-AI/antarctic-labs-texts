Global burden of neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution: a systematic analysis based on the global burden of disease 2021 study
Authors: Yan Wang, Fengcheng Cai, Xiaoxia Jiang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNeonatal respiratory infections (NRIs) remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality in the first 28 days of life, and air pollution is an important environmental risk factor. However, the global, regional, and temporal burden of NRIs attributable to air pollution has not been comprehensively characterized. Using data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study 2021, this study quantified deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from NRIs and their subcategories attributable to air pollution, including both ambient particulate matter pollution and household air pollution from solid fuel use. We further examined the global, regional, and socio-demographic index (SDI)-level distribution and temporal trends of these burdens. Data on deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) of neonatal respiratory infections (NRIs) attributable to air pollution from 1990 to 2021 were obtained from the GBD 2021 database. Temporal trends were assessed using Joinpoint regression, socioeconomic inequality was evaluated using the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) and Concentration Index (CIndex), frontier analysis was used to assess relative performance across development levels, and autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) models were applied to project future trends. In 2021, air pollution contributed to 64,405 neonatal deaths and 5.8 million disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) from respiratory infections worldwide. Death and DALY rates declined significantly from 1990 to 2021, with an estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) of -3.84%. Lower respiratory infections accounted for the majority of the attributable burden, and male neonates consistently experienced higher mortality and DALY rates than females. Marked geographic and socioeconomic inequalities were observed, with the heaviest burden concentrated in low-SDI regions, particularly South Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa; household air pollution contributed a larger share in lower-SDI settings, whereas ambient particulate matter became relatively more prominent in more developed settings.Conclusion: Although the global burden of neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution has declined over the past three decades, substantial geographic and socioeconomic inequalities persist, with the heaviest burden concentrated in low-SDI settings. Coordinated strategies that combine air-quality improvement with strengthened maternal and neonatal health services are needed to further reduce this burden and promote health equity. Introduction

Neonatal respiratory infections (NRIs) refer to infections of the upper or lower respiratory tract occurring within the first 28 days after birth and remain an important cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality worldwide. NRIs primarily include pneumonia, bronchiolitis, acute bronchitis, and acute upper respiratory infections. Common pathogens include respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Escherichia coli [1]. Because neonates have immature immune systems and incompletely developed lungs, severe lower respiratory tract infections are more likely to progress rapidly to critical illness and death in this population [2].

Air pollution is an important environmental risk factor for respiratory health and has been increasingly associated with the occurrence and progression of neonatal respiratory infections. The World Health Organization estimates that approximately seven million deaths worldwide each year are attributable to diseases related to air pollution, with respiratory infections, including pneumonia, among the major associated causes [3]. Studies have shown that exposure to fine particulate matter (PM2.5) can trigger and aggravate respiratory diseases, including asthma and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) [4]. In addition, pollutants such as PM2.5, NO2, SO2, and O3 may impair airway barrier function and alveolar development through oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and immune dysregulation, thereby increasing susceptibility to infection [5, 6]. Evidence also suggests that even moderate levels of air pollution are associated with increased respiratory symptoms among healthy infants and may prolong the duration of respiratory infections, particularly in regions with high pollution levels [7]. In many low- and middle-income countries, the widespread use of solid fuels such as firewood and coal in poorly ventilated households further exacerbates household air pollution. Studies have shown that infants and young children living in such environments face a significantly increased risk of acute respiratory infections, with the risk reported to be 1.91-fold higher for exposure to open fires and 3.62-fold higher among users of solid fuels [8]. Consistent evidence has also been reported in China, where each 10 μg/m3 increase in the daily mean concentrations of PM2.5 and PM10 was associated with a significant increase in outpatient visits for acute respiratory infections among children [9].

Nevertheless, existing research remains limited in several respects, including insufficient evaluation of specific neonatal subgroups such as the early and late neonatal periods, limited cross-regional comparisons, and inadequate assessment of differences across pollution sources and risk patterns. These gaps constrain a comprehensive understanding of the burden of neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution and may hinder the development of more targeted prevention and control strategies.

Using the most recent data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 study, we conducted a systematic analysis of the global burden of neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution from 1990 to 2021. Specifically, we quantified deaths and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), examined spatiotemporal patterns and temporal trends across countries, regions, sexes, age subgroups, and socio-demographic index (SDI) levels, assessed health inequalities, and explored future burden trajectories. By providing a more comprehensive and up-to-date assessment of burden and disparity, this study seeks to inform targeted strategies for neonatal respiratory infection prevention and air-pollution control.

Materials and methods

Data sources

Data for this study were obtained from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021 database, provided by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), University of Washington [10]. The GBD database systematically collects epidemiological indicators—including incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) for 204 countries and territories worldwide from 1990 to 2021.We extracted data on neonatal respiratory infections (NRIs) attributable to air pollution, including death rate, death number, DALY rate, and DALY number. In this study, neonates were defined as infants aged less than 28 days, encompassing early neonates (0 ~ 6 days) and late neonates (7 ~ 27 days). All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R (version 4.4.2) and JD_GBDR (V2.37, Jingding Medical Technology Co., Ltd.). All data were publicly available, aggregated estimates that did not involve individual-level or identifiable personal information.

Risk factors and sub-categories of disease

According to the Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021), the risk factor for neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution is primarily defined as “air pollution,” which encompasses both ambient particulate matter pollution and household air pollution from solid fuels. The corresponding sub-categories of these respiratory infections and their ICD-10 codes are upper respiratory infections (J00–J06), lower respiratory infections (J09–J22, P23, U04, U07, U09–U10), and otitis media (H65-H66) [10].

Socio-demographic index

The sociodemographic index (SDI) was utilized to quantify the level of socio-demographic development of a nation or territory concerning per capita income, average years of education among individuals aged 15 years and older, and the total fertility rate under the age of 25 [11]. The SDI ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater sociodemographic development, which generally refers to wealthier countries with more modernized health systems.

Temporal trend analysis using EAPC and Joinpoint regression

Two types of indicators were used to assess the temporal trends in mortality rate, number of deaths, DALY rate, and number of DALYs attributable to air pollution-related neonatal respiratory infections (NRIs). First, to describe the overall linear trend of each indicator during 1990 ~ 2021, a log-linear regression model was fitted to the full study period, and the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) with its 95% confidence interval (CI) was calculated; the corresponding results are presented in Table S1. Second, Joinpoint regression analysis was performed to characterize segmented temporal trends in these indicators. The annual percentage change (APC) and its 95% CI were calculated for each identified segment, and the average annual percentage change (AAPC) was further estimated as a weighted average of the APCs, with the length of each segment used as the weight, to comprehensively reflect the overall temporal trend in the presence of potential trend changes over time. Trend interpretation was based on the following criteria: when the estimated APC, AAPC, or EAPC was greater than 0 and the lower bound of its 95% CI was also greater than 0, the indicator was considered to show an increasing trend; when the estimated value was less than 0 and the upper bound of its 95% CI was less than 0, the indicator was considered to show a decreasing trend; when the 95% CI included 0, the trend was considered statistically nonsignificant and therefore stable [12].

Cross-country and cross-regional social inequalities analysis

To assess the magnitude of distributional inequality in NRIs across countries and GBD regions, we conducted an inequality analysis using two standard metrics: the slope index of inequality (SII) and the concentration index (CIndex) [13].The SII quantifies the absolute linear gradient between a health outcome and socioeconomic position (SEP), and is interpreted as the absolute health gap between the most and least disadvantaged groups. The CIndex is a relative measure that captures the extent to which health or health-care utilization is distributed unevenly across SEP groups defined by income, education, wealth, or similar indicators. Rooted in the concepts of the Lorenz curve and the Gini coefficient, the CIndex indicates whether health resources are disproportionately concentrated among the better-off or the worse-off.

Frontier analysis

To evaluate the relative efficiency of countries and regions in reducing the burden of neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution, we conducted a frontier analysis [14]. The SDI was used as the independent variable, and the death rate or DALY rate of neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution was used as the dependent variable. An efficiency frontier was then constructed using locally weighted regression (LOESS). The LOESS smoothing parameter (span) was set at 0.20 to balance local fitting sensitivity and overall curve stability, thereby enabling the fitted curve to better characterize the lower-envelope relationship between SDI and burden indicators while minimizing unnecessary local fluctuations caused by overfitting. Subsequently, the horizontal distance between each country’s or region’s observed data point and the frontier curve was calculated to derive the Frontier Index (FI), which was used to quantify the gap between current performance and the theoretical optimum. A lower FI value indicates that a country or region has achieved higher efficiency in controlling disease burden at its corresponding level of development.

Forecasting analysis

To forecast future trends in the burden of NRIs attributable to air pollution, we performed a time-series analysis using the autoregressive integrated moving average (ARIMA) model [15]. This method fits and predicts temporal trends in death rates, death numbers, DALY rates, and DALY numbers based on historical data. The model parameters (p, d, q) were selected according to the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). The KPSS test was used to assess the stationarity of the original time series; when non-stationarity was identified, differencing was applied to satisfy the assumptions required for modeling. The final selected model was ARIMA(1,2,0). Subsequently, the Ljung-Box test was used to evaluate the randomness of the residuals from the final model, with the lag set at 12. The model selection process and related diagnostic statistics are presented in Table S11.

Results

Global, regional, and national burden of NRIs attributable to air pollution

In 2021, neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution were responsible for an estimated 64,405 deaths and 5,796,698 disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) worldwide, corresponding to death and DALY rates of 660.89 and 59,482.53 per 100,000 population, respectively (Table S1). From 1990 to 2021, both indicators declined steadily, with estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs) of −3.84 for deaths and DALYs, indicating a sustained global reduction in burden (Table S1). Nevertheless, male neonates consistently exhibited higher death and DALY rates than female neonates in 2021, with death rates of 737.78 and 578.66 per 100,000 population, respectively, and DALY rates of 66,400.85 and 52,082.93 per 100,000 population, respectively. These findings indicate substantial global progress, while sex-based disparities in burden persist.

A pronounced gradient was observed across SDI quintiles. In 2021, DALY rates increased from 483.15 per 100,000 population in high-SDI regions to 111,971.50 per 100,000 population in low-SDI regions, while death rates rose from 5.30 to 1,244.24 per 100,000 population (Table S1). The most rapid declines were observed in high-middle SDI regions (EAPC = −8.75), whereas low-SDI regions experienced the slowest improvement (EAPC = −3.68). Across all SDI quintiles, male rates remained higher than female rates, and the magnitude of this difference increased with decreasing SDI (Table S2-S3). Together, these findings suggest that, despite overall improvement, the burden remains disproportionately concentrated in less developed settings.

At the regional level, the highest DALY rates in 2021 were observed in Oceania (156,976.45 per 100,000 population), Western Sub-Saharan Africa (142,856.15 per 100,000 population), and South Asia (91,379.04 per 100,000 population), followed by Eastern Sub-Saharan Africa and Central Sub-Saharan Africa (Table S1). By contrast, the lowest DALY rates were recorded in High-income North America (79.84 per 100,000 population), Western Europe (130.76 per 100,000 population), and High-income Asia Pacific (155.01 per 100,000 population). Between 1990 and 2021, the burden declined most rapidly in East Asia, Eastern Europe, and Tropical Latin America, whereas Oceania and the Caribbean experienced only limited improvement (Table S1).

Substantial heterogeneity was also evident across the 204 countries and territories included in the analysis (Fig. 1; Tables S4-S6). The highest DALY rates were recorded in the Central African Republic, Chad, Papua New Guinea, and Nigeria, whereas the lowest rates were observed in Andorra, Sweden, San Marino, and Finland. Spatial analyses further demonstrated that high-burden clusters were concentrated in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, whereas most high-income regions maintained comparatively low burden levels and more favorable temporal trends (Fig. 1A-D).

Analysis of age composition indicated that the burden was concentrated in the early neonatal period. Globally, early neonates accounted for 59.6% of deaths and 59.6% of DALYs, and for 80.8% of both the death rate and DALY rate (Figure S1). This pattern became more pronounced with decreasing SDI: the proportion of deaths and DALYs occurring during the early neonatal period increased from approximately 47% in high-SDI settings to more than 60% in low-SDI settings, and exceeded 80% in parts of Sub-Saharan Africa and Oceania. These findings identify the first week of life as the period of greatest vulnerability, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Burden of NRIs attributable to air pollution by SDI

A strong inverse association was observed between the Socio-demographic Index (SDI) and the burden of neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution (Fig. 2A-D). At both the national and regional levels, DALY rates and death rates declined markedly with increasing SDI. At the national level, the correlations between SDI and the death rate and DALY rate were −0.9081 and −0.9082, respectively, at the regional level were −0.8867 and −0.8869 (Fig. 2A-D). In addition to the overall downward trend, the dispersion of observed values narrowed substantially at higher SDI levels, indicating that countries and regions with greater socioeconomic development tended to cluster more closely around the expected burden trajectory. These findings suggest that socioeconomic development is strongly associated with lower disease burden and with more consistent health performance across settings.

Health-inequality analysis further demonstrated that the burden remained disproportionately concentrated in lower-SDI countries, although absolute inequality declined over time (Figure S2, Figure S3, Table S7). From 1990 to 2021, the SII for the death rate decreased from −3,869.41 to −986.56, and the SII for the DALY rate decreased from −348,180.08 to −88,790.33, indicating a substantial narrowing of the absolute gap across SDI ranks. However, relative inequality persisted. Over the same period, the CIndex changed from −0.51 to −0.57 for both the death rate and DALY rate, indicating that the burden remained concentrated among countries with lower SDI levels and that this relative concentration did not improve in parallel with the reduction in absolute inequality.

Frontier analysis provided additional insight into heterogeneity in performance at similar levels of development (Figure S4, Table S8). Although the death rate and DALY rate declined nonlinearly with increasing SDI, many countries remained above the theoretical minimum frontier, indicating unrealized health gains. From 1990 to 2021, most countries moved progressively closer to the efficiency frontier, suggesting broad improvements in burden reduction efficiency. Nevertheless, marked gaps persisted, particularly in low- and middle-SDI settings. In 2021, countries such as Singapore, the United Kingdom, the Netherlands, and Taiwan (Province of China) were located near the frontier, reflecting comparatively favorable performance for their level of development. By contrast, Somalia, Niger, Chad, and the Central African Republic remained far from the frontier, with substantially higher burden than would be expected based on SDI alone. Together, these findings indicate that while socioeconomic development is a major determinant of disease burden, differences in policy implementation, health-system capacity, and environmental control likely contribute to additional variation across countries.

Risk factor-specific and disease-specific patterns of NRIs burden attributable to air pollution

Marked differences were observed in the contributions of specific air-pollution risk factors to the burden of neonatal respiratory infections (Fig. 3A-B). Globally, particulate matter pollution accounted for the greatest burden, with a death rate of 660.89 per 100,000 population and a DALY rate of 59,482.53 per 100,000 population, followed by household air pollution, which contributed 482.60 deaths and 43,433.58 DALYs per 100,000 population, and ambient particulate matter pollution, which contributed 178.15 deaths and 16,036.22 DALYs per 100,000 population (Fig. 3A-B). Overall, household air pollution accounted for the larger share of the global burden attributable to particulate matter pollution, whereas ambient particulate matter pollution made a smaller, but still substantial, contribution.

These patterns varied considerably across SDI levels. The highest burden was consistently observed in low-SDI regions, where particulate matter pollution was associated with 1,244.17 deaths and 111,971.50 DALYs per 100,000 population, followed by household air pollution, with 1,035.17 deaths and 93,162.68 DALYs per 100,000 population, and ambient particulate matter pollution, with 208.77 deaths and 18,788.35 DALYs per 100,000 population. The burden declined progressively with increasing SDI and reached minimal levels in high-SDI regions, where all three pollution categories contributed fewer than 6 deaths and 500 DALYs per 100,000 population. A clear shift in the composition of risk factors was also observed across development levels. In low- and low-middle-SDI regions, household air pollution remained the dominant contributor after overall particulate matter pollution. In contrast, in middle- and high-middle-SDI regions, the contribution of household air pollution decreased substantially, whereas ambient particulate matter pollution became relatively more prominent (Fig. 3A-B). Similar patterns were observed for the absolute numbers of deaths and DALYs (Fig. 3A-B).

Disease-specific analyses further showed that lower respiratory infections (LRIs) consistently accounted for the majority of the neonatal respiratory infection burden attributable to air pollution across all regions and SDI strata (Figure S5). Globally, LRIs ranked first in both death rate and DALY rate, far exceeding upper respiratory infections (URIs) and otitis media, which ranked second and third, respectively. The burden of LRIs was especially high in low-SDI regions and in Sub-Saharan Africa, where death rates ranged from 645.68 to 1,744.25 per 100,000 population and DALY rates ranged from 58,097.93 to 156,943.39 per 100,000 population. By contrast, URIs contributed substantially lower rates, whereas otitis media made only a minimal contribution in nearly all settings. Although the overall burden declined markedly with increasing SDI, the relative ordering of disease subtypes remained unchanged, with LRIs consistently contributing the greatest burden, followed by URIs and otitis media. These findings indicate that LRIs are the principal drivers of the air pollution-attributable burden of neonatal respiratory infections worldwide, particularly in resource-limited settings.

The joinpoint regression analysis of NRIs attributed to air pollution

Joinpoint regression analysis showed a sustained decline in the global burden of neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution from 1990 to 2021, with a marked acceleration in the late 2010 s (Fig. 4A-D; Table S9). Across all four indicators, the overall trend remained consistently downward, with average annual percentage changes (AAPCs) of −4.15633% for the death rate, −4.23421% for the number of deaths, −4.15637% for the DALY rate, and −4.23402% for the number of DALYs.The rate-based indicators exhibited highly similar temporal patterns. The death rate declined by 2.21177% annually during 1990–2000, 4.20781% annually during 2000–2018, and 10.33055% annually during 2018–2021 (Fig. 4A). Similarly, the DALY rate decreased by 2.21158% annually during 1990–2000, 4.20733% annually during 2000–2018, and 10.23785% annually during 2018–2021 (Fig. 4C).A comparable pattern was observed for the count-based indicators. The number of deaths decreased by 2.55421% annually during 1990–2002, 3.65802% annually during 2002–2016, and 9.87927% annually during 2016–2021 (Fig. 4B). The number of DALYs followed an almost identical trajectory, with annual percentage changes of −2.55402%, −3.65763%, and −9.87837% across the same periods, respectively (Fig. 4D).Overall, these findings indicate that the burden of neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution has declined steadily over the past three decades, with the pace of reduction accelerating substantially in recent years.

Predictive analysis of the burden of NRIs attributable to air pollution

Future projections suggested a continued global decline in the burden of neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution through 2035 (Fig. 5A-D; Table S10). Both rate-based and count-based indicators were projected to maintain the downward trajectory observed during 1990–2021. Globally, the death rate was projected to decrease from 660.89 per 100,000 population in 2021 to 149.98 per 100,000 population in 2035, while the DALY rate was projected to decline from 59,482.53 to 13,503.27 per 100,000 population over the same period (Fig. 5A, C). Similarly, the number of deaths was projected to decrease from 64,405.33 in 2021 to 11,124.59 in 2035, and the number of DALYs from 5,796,697.68 to 1,001,621.56 (Fig. 5B, D), corresponding to reductions of more than 80% in both absolute indicators.Sex-stratified projections indicated that the burden remained consistently higher among males throughout the forecast period. In 2035, the projected death rate was higher in males than in females, at 201.59 and 103.26 per 100,000 population, respectively. A similar pattern was observed for the DALY rate, with projected values of 18,148.94 and 9,298.44 per 100,000 population in males and females, respectively. Likewise, males were projected to have higher numbers of deaths and DALYs, reaching 7,680.87 deaths and 691,486.98 DALYs, compared with 3,723.43 deaths and 335,285.88 DALYs in females. Although the absolute sex difference narrowed over time, the burden remained consistently greater in males across all four indicators.

Discussion

This study provides a comprehensive global overview of the burden of neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution from 1990 to 2021. Our findings indicate that, although global mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) declined substantially over the study period, the magnitude of improvement varied markedly across Socio-demographic Index (SDI) levels. Low-SDI regions continued to bear the greatest burden, with death rates and DALY rates in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia remaining far higher than those observed in high-SDI settings. In addition, Joinpoint regression showed that the burden declined steadily over the past three decades, with a more rapid reduction observed in recent years. This inflection point coincided with strengthened air-quality policies in multiple countries, improved critical-care services for neonates, and expanded maternal–newborn health programs [16].Together, these findings suggest that, despite substantial global progress, pronounced geographic and socioeconomic disparities in burden remain.

Our findings further indicate that the composition of air pollution-attributable neonatal respiratory burden differs across levels of socioeconomic development. Globally, household air pollution accounted for a larger share of the attributable burden than ambient particulate matter pollution, although the relative contribution of these sources varied substantially by SDI level. In low- and low-middle-SDI settings, household air pollution remained the predominant contributor after overall particulate matter pollution, whereas in middle- and high-middle-SDI settings, the contribution of household air pollution declined and ambient particulate matter pollution became relatively more prominent. These patterns underscore the need for context-specific intervention strategies, particularly in lower-income regions where exposure to household air pollution remains high and maternal–newborn health systems are often under-resourced.At the same time, inequality analyses showed that, although absolute inequality in burden declined over time, relative inequality persisted, indicating that the burden remained disproportionately concentrated in lower-SDI populations. The projection analyses further suggested continued declines in mortality and DALYs through 2035, but substantial interregional differences were projected to remain. Consistent with this pattern, frontier analysis indicated that although some countries were approaching the lowest observed burden for their level of development, many low-SDI countries remained far from this benchmark, suggesting considerable scope for further improvement. Together, these findings indicate that continued progress will likely depend on sustained reductions in air pollution exposure together with strengthened neonatal and maternal health services, particularly in high-burden settings.

The geographical distribution of the air pollution-attributable burden of neonatal respiratory infections was highly heterogeneous. Estimates published in the State of Global Air 2023 attributed approximately 500,000 neonatal deaths in 2019 to air pollution, with solid-fuel combustion accounting for a substantial proportion of this burden [17]. The largest absolute mortality was recorded in sub-Saharan Africa. Although these estimates were based on a broader outcome definition than that used in the present study, they are directionally consistent with the regional distribution observed here. In our analysis, Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia accounted for the largest share of global neonatal deaths and DALYs in 2021, whereas Western Europe, North America, and the high-income Asia Pacific region contributed only a very small proportion of the total burden. In several lower-middle-SDI countries, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa and South Asia, the DALY burden of neonatal respiratory infections remained many times higher than the global average. This pronounced disparity may reflect several interrelated factors [18–20], including continued reliance on solid fuels for cooking and heating in poorly ventilated households, rapid population growth and urbanization without corresponding improvements in access to clean energy and household ventilation, limitations in maternal and newborn health services, and recurrent exposure peaks related to biomass burning, dust storms, and seasonal agricultural fires. By contrast, the substantially lower burden observed in high-income regions may be related to broader access to clean energy, stronger air-quality regulation, and more developed maternal and neonatal care systems. Inequality metrics further supported this pattern: although the Slope Index of Inequality (SII) for neonatal deaths improved from −3,869 to −986 between 1990 and 2021, the Concentration Index (CIndex) remained at approximately −0.57, indicating that relative inequality persisted despite reductions in absolute inequality. Together, these findings suggest that global progress has occurred alongside persistent inequities in exposure and vulnerability.

Our age-stratified analysis showed that approximately 60% of air pollution-attributable neonatal deaths and DALYs occurred within the first 6 days of life, and this proportion exceeded 80% in low-SDI settings. This pattern may reflect the combined influence of prenatal vulnerability and early postnatal exposure. Fine particulate matter and household combustion-related pollutants may cross the placental barrier, impair fetal lung and immune development, and contribute to low birth weight or preterm birth, all of which are important determinants of infection risk immediately after birth [20, 21]. During the late neonatal period, continued alveolar maturation, the transition from maternally derived antibodies to endogenous antibody production, and progressive adaptation to environmental microbial exposure may contribute to the gradual development of pulmonary and immune function, accompanied by a decline in susceptibility to respiratory infections [22]. The higher burden observed among male neonates in our analysis is also consistent with previous evidence suggesting delayed pulmonary and immunological maturation in male infants [23]. Together, these findings underscore the importance of preventive strategies targeting both pregnant women and newborns, particularly in settings with high levels of air pollution exposure.

Within the risk-factor decomposition framework of the GBD hierarchy, the burden attributable to air pollution was primarily linked to particulate matter (PM) pollution, with household air pollution (HAP) accounting for the larger share and ambient PM pollution contributing a smaller proportion. The contribution of household sources was particularly pronounced in low-SDI settings, but declined substantially in high-SDI regions, a pattern that may reflect the energy-ladder effect, whereby persistent reliance on solid biomass fuels in lower-income populations sustains indoor pollutant concentrations that often exceed outdoor levels [24]. In the disease-specific analyses, lower respiratory infections (LRIs) accounted for the overwhelming majority of air pollution-attributable neonatal deaths and DALYs, far exceeding the contributions of upper respiratory infections and otitis media. This pattern suggests that interventions aimed at reducing fine-particle exposure in pregnant women, neonates, and young infants, together with timely diagnosis and treatment of LRIs, may have the greatest potential to reduce the associated health burden.

Frontier analysis indicated substantial variation in performance relative to development level. Several high-SDI countries, including Singapore, the United Kingdom, and the Netherlands, appeared close to the lowest observed burden for their level of development, whereas many low-SDI countries, such as Chad, Somalia, and the Central African Republic, remained considerably farther from this benchmark. This pattern suggests that economic development alone is insufficient to achieve optimal outcomes. Other factors, including governance capacity, implementation of clean-energy policies, integration of maternal and child health services, and the availability of exposure monitoring, may also play important roles. Countries that combined progress in energy transition with improvements in neonatal care appeared to experience greater reductions in burden. Therefore, narrowing the “efficiency gap” may require not only broader implementation of effective interventions, but also stronger policy coordination, monitoring systems, and equitable access to health and environmental resources [25].Examples from several settings support this interpretation. In China, implementation of the Clean Air Action Plan (2013–2020) was associated with an approximately 40% reduction in annual mean PM₂.₅ concentrations, coinciding with marked declines in infant mortality from respiratory infections [26]. Similarly, India’s Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana program, which provided more than 90 million clean-cooking fuel connections to low-income households, has been associated with reduced household air pollution exposure and lower childhood pneumonia risk [27]. In Latin America, the “Aire Limpio” initiative and fuel-quality standards implemented in Mexico City further illustrate the potential health benefits of multisectoral intervention approaches [28]. However, translating these experiences to other settings requires adequate institutional and financial capacity. In many African countries, real-time air-quality monitoring and neonatal infection surveillance systems remain limited. Taken together, these findings suggest that continued progress will depend not only on reducing emissions, but also on strengthening neonatal resilience through more equitable health systems and environmental governance.

Our findings highlight the need for targeted and equity-oriented strategies to reduce the burden of neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution. In low-SDI settings, where household air pollution remains a major contributor, priority areas may include improving access to clean cooking fuels, enhancing household ventilation, and strengthening the integration of maternal and newborn health services. Measures such as clean-fuel subsidy programs and perinatal home-based interventions may contribute to meaningful health benefits in high-exposure settings. At the same time, improving neonatal intensive-care capacity and strengthening early recognition and management of infection may help reduce vulnerability among high-risk newborns. At national and global levels, reducing air pollution exposure should be more closely integrated into maternal and child health strategies, including efforts to link environmental monitoring with neonatal health outcomes. Cross-sectoral approaches that combine clean-energy policies, air-quality regulation, and newborn-care programs are likely to offer the greatest potential for sustained impact. In addition, high-SDI countries may support lower-SDI settings through technology transfer, technical assistance, and capacity building. Overall, further reductions in burden will likely depend on continued improvements in air quality together with more equitable access to maternal, neonatal, and environmental health resources.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, all attributable estimates were derived from the GBD 2021 comparative risk-assessment framework and therefore depend on modeled exposure distributions, relative risks, and counterfactual assumptions rather than direct measurements in every setting. In countries with sparse air-pollution monitoring and incomplete vital registration, this uncertainty likely yields conservative central estimates and may underestimate the true burden, particularly in resource-constrained settings. Second, ambient particulate matter pollution and household air pollution from solid fuels are not fully independent exposures; in many low-SDI settings, mothers and newborns are exposed to both simultaneously. Therefore, the source-specific estimates should not be interpreted as additive independent effects, and the contrast between household and ambient contributions may be overstated. Third, the associations with SDI, as well as the inequality and frontier analyses, were based on country- and region-level aggregated data. These ecological relationships are useful for describing broad inequities, but they cannot be interpreted as individual-level causal effects and may overstate the direct protective role of SDI itself, which also captures differences in health systems, poverty, and environmental governance. Fourth, the ARIMA forecasts are extrapolations of historical trends and do not explicitly account for future policy changes, technological transitions, economic shocks, conflict, or climate-related disruptions. If air-quality control and neonatal-care interventions accelerate, our projections may overestimate future burden; conversely, worsening pollution or stalled policy implementation could lead to underestimation. Finally, residual confounding by factors such as prematurity, nutrition, coexisting infections, and access to care cannot be fully excluded. Nevertheless, the consistency of the main patterns across deaths, DALYs, sex, age strata, and SDI groups supports the overall robustness of our principal conclusions.

Conclusion

In summary, although the global burden of neonatal respiratory infections attributable to air pollution has declined over the past three decades, substantial and inequitable disparities remain—particularly among early neonates, male infants, and populations in low-SDI regions. Sustained reduction of this burden requires coordinated strategies that combine long-term air-quality improvement and clean-energy transition with strengthened maternal and neonatal health systems. Integrating environmental health considerations into neonatal health policies, supported by continuous monitoring and equity-oriented resource allocation, is essential to achieving further progress. Continued efforts to address these environmental and health system determinants are necessary to ensure that all newborns benefit equally from ongoing global health and development advancements.
