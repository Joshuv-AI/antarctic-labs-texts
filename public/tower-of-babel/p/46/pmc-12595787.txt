Ferroptosis-related genes MDM2 and CDKN1A as potential biomarkers for COPD
Authors: Rui Shi, Lueli Wang, Chunyun Bai, Fang Wang, Chaozhong Li, Chuang Xiao, Alex F Chen, Weimin Yang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveChronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is characterized by inflammation and an immune response. However, the relationship between ferroptosis and COPD remains unknown. We aim to identify pivotal ferroptosis-related biomarkers in COPD and explore their roles in immune infiltration landscapes.MethodsDifferentially expressed genes (DEGs) were obtained from all current datasets of peripheral blood and lung tissues associated with COPD. DEGs were intersected with ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) from FerrDb database to obtain FRDEGs. Hub FRDEGs were evaluated using WGCNA, GO, and KEGG enrichment, PPI network analysis, LASSO-COX, and ROC curve analysis, and validated in blood of COPD patients. The association between hub FRDEGs and COPD was investigated. The role of hub FRDEGs in 17 types of respiratory tract diseases was analyzed, and potential drugs targeting these FRDEGs were predicted via CMAP drug database. Importantly, MDM2 and CDKN1A expressions were identified and verified by H&E and Masson staining, and Western blot analysis in the CS and LPS-induced COPD mice.ResultsMDM2 and CDKN1A were identified as hub genes in all COPD patients, and their expressions were significantly upregulated in the lung tissues of COPD mice. 17 types of respiratory tract diseases were markedly associated with MDM2 and CDKN1A. The 2 genes were correlated with neutrophils. MDM inhibitor (AMG-232) was screened as a potentially key drug affecting MDM2.ConclusionMDM2 and CDKN1A could be potential targets for COPD by regulating neutrophil-involving inflammation. One drug with potential clinical application value was identified.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12950-025-00468-4.Keywords: COPD, Ferroptosis, Immune infiltration, MDM2, CDKN1A

Introduction

Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) is a chronic respiratory inflammatory disease characterized by incompletely reversible limitation in airflow caused by increased resistance of the small conducting airways and increased compliance of the lung, which leads to either chronic bronchitis or emphysema. COPD kills about 3 million people annually, ranking as the third leading the top 10 cause of death worldwide, and is the fifth leading disease burden all over the world (https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/the-top-10-causes-of-death [1]). Main risk factors for COPD include Tobacco smoking, air pollution, and genetic factors are the main risk factors of COPD [2, 3]. The pathogenesis of COPD is associated with a chronic innate and adaptive inflammatory immune response of the host to lifetime exposure to these risk factors, such as inhalation of toxic particles and gases, mostly chronic inhalation of cigarette smoking [4]. The inflammatory mucous exudates in the small airway lumen, infiltration of inflammatory cells in the airway walls, and deposition of connective tissue in the airway walls by connective tissue increased as COPD progressed [4]. These were dramatically associated with infiltrated immune cells, containing the accumulation of neutrophils, macrophages, CD4 cells, gamma delta T-lymphocytes (γδ T-cells), and dendritic cells, and the increase in absolute volume of CD8 cells and B cells in the progression of COPD [5].

Neutrophils and eosinophils are the 2 key mediators of the inflammatory endotypes in the airways of COPD patients, which manifest as mucus hyper-secretion and emphysema [6]. In addition to neutrophils, macrophages also increase most in mucous exudates of patients with COPD, caused by the increased secretion of neutrophil and monocyte chemotactic mediators in the lungs [7]. An increase of proinflammatory T helper (Th) 1 and Th17 cells, both types of cells are subsets of CD4 T-lymphocytes and secrete inflammatory cytokines (such as IL-17) to modulate the immune response in COPD [8, 9]. Previous results also demonstrated that cigarette smoke (CS) exposure primes natural killer (NK) and γδ T-cells to produce more IL-17A protein in COPD mice [10]. Furthermore, studies showed that CS activates dendritic cells, which induces CD8 cytotoxicity and B-cell responses and results in the development of lymphoid follicles in chronic inflammation [11].

Ferroptosis is an iron-dependent form of cell-regulated death in which a large amount of iron accumulation promotes lipid peroxidation. The metabolic disorder of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and phospholipid membranes containing polyunsaturated fatty acid chain(s) (PUFA-PL), catalyzed by excess ions causes the inactivity of GPX4, disrupts the redox balance, and leads to cell death [12, 13]. Massive observations have confirmed the positive effect of ferroptosis on inflammation, and several compounds, such as GPX4, have shown anti-inflammatory effects as ferroptosis inhibitors [14, 15]. In addition, ferroptosis can regulate the innate and adaptive immune cells in inflammation. M1 cells but not alternatively activated M2 cells inhibit arachidonate 15-lipoxygenase (ALOX15), reduce NO production, and exert higher resistance to ferroptosis, which finally promotes inflammatory responses [16]. Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs) activate ferroptosis by inducing m6A modification in alveolar epithelial cells [17]. Besides, CD8 + T cells stimulate ACSL4 and orchestrate ferroptosis and immunity in tumors [18]. Above all, we speculate that ferroptosis modulates inflammation by activating immunity in COPD.

We aim to screen ferroptosis-related hub genes and their association with immune cell infiltration in all COPD peripheral blood and lung tissues compared with normal ones via integrative multiple datasets. Additionally, the potential drug in COPD will be investigated by CMAP database analysis. We further intend to verify the expression of key target genes in COPD mice.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition

The gene expression microarray datasets GSE112811, GSE103174, GSE54837, GSE133096, and GSE55962 of COPD peripheral blood and GSE162635, GSE92511, GSE37768, GSE73395, and GSE76925 of COPD lung tissues were downloaded from the GEO database (http://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). This raw data expression matrix contains 579 COPD and 365 normal individuals.

Data processing

The normalized expression matrix of these datasets was realized by limma analysis. We combined these 10 datasets with the R software package in Silico Merging [DOD:10.1186/1471-2105-13-335] Then, we used the remove Batch Effect function of the R software package limma (version 3.42.2) to remove the batch effect and finally obtained the merged dataset. The visualization of datasets before and after the merger was then performed using a boxplot, density graphic, and UMAP chart.

Identification of ferroptosis-related differentially expressed genes (FRDEGs)

We used the limma R package (version 3.42.2) to evaluate the different significance of each gene in the COPD and the control samples, The P-value obtained after the t-test and calculated the P.adjust of each gene using the Benjamini & Hochberg (BH) method, and finally obtained the differentially expressed genes with |log2 fold change (FC)|> 0.995 and P < 0.05. Subsequently, ferroptosis-related genes (FRGs) that prevent (suppressor), promote (driver), and indicate (marker) ferroptosis were downloaded from the FerrDb database (http://www.zhounan.org/ferrdb). Then, the genes of differential expression analysis and FRGs were intersected to obtain crossover genes, which were considered FRDEGs.

Weighted Gene Co-Expression Network Analysis (WGCNA)

GSE73395 was applied to further screen the key FRDEGs in COPD by a weighted correlation network. WGCNA analysis was performed in WGCNA R package. The Median Absolute Deviation (MAD) of each gene was first calculated by using a gene expression profile, and the genes with the largest variance (50%) were selected. The filtered samples that remove outliers were removed by the good Samples Genes method.Then, WGCNA was used to construct a scale-free co-expression network as follows. At first, Pearson's correlation matrices and average linkage method were both performed for all pair-wise Genes. Then, a weighted adjacency matrix was constructed using a power function A_mn =|C_mn|^β (C_mn = Pearson's correlation between Gene_m and Gene_n; A_mn = adjacency between Gene m and Gene n). β was a soft-thresholding parameter that could emphasize strong correlations between Genes and penalize weak correlations. After choosing the power of 4, the adjacency was transformed into a topological overlap matrix (TOM), which could measure the network connectivity of a Gene defined as the sum of its adjacency with all other Genes for network Gene ration, and the corresponding dissimilarity (1-TOM) was calculated. To classify Genes with similar expression profiles into Gene modules, average linkage hierarchical clustering was conducted according to the TOM-based dissimilarity measure with a minimum size (Gene group) of 30 for the Genes dendrogram. The sensitivity value was selected as 3. To further analyze the module, we calculated the dissimilarity of module eigengenes, chose a cut line for the module dendrogram, and merged some modules. In addition, we combined several modules with a threshold value (< 0.25) and finally obtained the co-expression modules.

FRDEGs enrichment analyses

Gene ontology (GO) terms and Kyoto encyclopedia of genes and genomes (KEGG) pathways enrichment analyses were conducted to explore the 24 FRDEGs' biological functions using the clusterProfiler package in R. GO functional categories contained biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). P < 0.05 was considered the screening criteria. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed using org.Hs.eg.db (version 3.1.0) and KEGG rest API (https://www.kegg.jp/kegg/rest/keggapi.html) to obtain GO terms and KEGG pathway gene annotation, respectively. Using the clusterProfiler R package (version 3.14.3) to obtain gene set enrichment results. A minimum gene set of 5 and a maximum gene set of 5000 were selected. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Protein–protein interaction (PPI) network construction and module analyses

Using the STRING (version: 11.0) (http://string-db.org) to analyze protein–protein interactions of the FRDEGs. The results were downloaded from String database. Then, PPI networks were constructed and visualized using Cytoscape software 3.9.1. The top 14 genes of hub genes were screened by the cytoHubba plugin.

Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) regression

To improve the accuracy of FRDEGs in COPD, LASSO regression algorithm was further used to construct a risk model by integrating survival time, survival state, and gene expression using the R package glmnet. Correlation coefficients and Parial Likelihood Deviance at lambda. min was set as the final model. The risk scores were first calculated. Subsequently, survival analysis was performed using the survival R package. Then the prognostic ability of these features in 944 samples was weighed by using the Cox method. LASSO-Cox regression was generated and visualized using Sangerbox (version: 3.0) online tool (http://www.sangerbox.com/home.html).

Validation of the hub FRDEGs with receiver operating characteristic (ROC)

ROC curve and area under the curve (AUC) were applied and measured via pROC R package to evaluate the diagnostic value of FRDEGs, respectively. The online tool (https://www.xiantao.love/products/) was applied.

Immune cell infiltration and correlation analyses

Immune infiltration differences of 22 types of immune cells were assessed by the CIBERSORT algorithm between COPD and normal groups (https://cibersortx.stanford.edu/). Then Pearson correlation coefficient between each kind of immune cell and FRDEGs was calculated, respectively. The above results were analyzed and visualized by barplot, boxplot, scatter plot, and Sankey diagram by Sangerbox (version: 3.0) web tool (http://www.sangerbox.com/home.html).

Connective Map Database (CMAP) drug database analyses

The top 150 up-regulated and 150 down-regulated DEGs were uploaded to the CMAP (http://www.broadinstitute.org/cmap/) to investigate the potential small-molecule agents for the treatment of COPD.

FRDEGs all regulatory target gene sets analyses

To further predict the regulatory target gene of identified hub genes based on the gene expression profile of patients with COPD, we used gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) for gene set enrichment analysis in Sangerbox (http://www.sangerbox.com/home.html), and the c3.all.v7.4.symbols.gmt gene set was downloaded from the Molecular Signatures database, with the threshold set at P < 0.05.

Comparative Toxicomics Database (CTD) database analyses

To estimate the inference scores of hub genes in a total of 17 types of respiratory tract diseases, the online database of CTD was used (http://ctdbase.org/).

Animal model of COPD induced by CS and LPS

All animal care and experimental protocols were approved by the Animal Experimental Ethical Committee of Kunming Medical University, and all animals received humane care in compliance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines.

6–8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice were kept in specific pathogen-free facilities with free access to food and water for 1 week before experiments. After a 1-week acclimatization, mice were given 5 µg of LPS by intratracheal instillation (it) under isoflurane anesthesia (Escherichia coli O55:B5, Sigma Aldrich, Missouri) on week 1. Mice were placed in an oral and nasal exposure system (Beijing Huironghe Technology, China) and exposed to CS (1 cigarettes/1 mouse, once/day, 6 days/week) for 16 weeks, except for the day with LPS administration. The relevant parameters of exposure were as follows: dilution flow of 10 L/min, air extraction flow of 13 L/min, oxygen concentration of 20 ± 0.5%, and air humidity of 60 ± 5%. The cigarette suction parameters included the following: suction time of 2 s, time interval of 2 s, suction frequency of 10 s, and suction flow of 35 ml. Cigarettes (Hongqi Canal® Filter tip cigarette, smoke of each cigarette containing 11 mg tar, 0.7 mg nicotine, and 13 mg carbon monoxide) were obtained from Henan Tobacco Industry (Zhengzhou, China).

Pulmonary function examination

Pulmonary function in conscious mice was assessed every two weeks by whole-body plethysmography (EMKA Technologies, Canada). The respiratory parameters obtained using this technique include expiratory flow at 50% tidal volume (EF50), inspiratory time (Ti), tidal volume (TV), expiratory time (Te), relaxation time (RT), and respiratory rate (f). Invasive lung function was measured with a FlexiVent system (SCIREQ, Montreal, Canada). A plastic cannula was inserted into mouse tracheas and connected to the FlexiVent system. Vecuronium bromide (6 mg/kg) was injected intraperitoneally to maintain muscle relaxation. Then, mechanical ventilation was initiated to measure compliance respiratory system (Crs), static compliance (Cst), elastance respiratory system (Ers), inspiratory capacity (IC), newtonian resistance (Rn), respiratory resistance (Rrs), forced vital capacity (FVC), forced expiratory volume in 0.1 s (FEV0.1), forced expiratory volume in 0.2 s (FEV0.2), forced expiratory volume in 0.05 s (FEV0.05), forced expiratory volume in 0.1 s/forced vital capacity (FEV0.1/FVC), forced expiratory volume in 0.2 s/forced vital capacity (FEV0.2/FVC), and forced expiratory volume in 0.05 s/forced vital capacity (FEV0.05/FVC). The data obtained were analyzed using the FlexiVent software (SCIREQ, Montreal, Canada).

Haematoxylin–eosin (H&E) and Masson staining

Lung tissues of the mice were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h and embedded in paraffin, followed by sectioning into 5-μm sections. The tissues were stained using H&E and Masson according to the standard protocols. The pathological characteristics of the tissues were evaluated by light microscopy.

Western blot analysis

Lung tissues from each mouse were homogenized in lysis buffer (RIPA: Cocktail: PMSF: Phosstop = 0.657: 0.143: 0.1: 0.1) to extract total protein. Total proteins (30 μg) were loaded onto 10% SDS-PAGE and transferred to PVDF membranes (Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA). The membranes were blocked for 1 h with TBS containing 0.05% Tween 20 (TBST) supplemented with 5% non-fat dry milk at room temperature and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-MDM2 (Cat. bs-20790R, Bioss, Beijing, China), anti-CDKN1A (Cat. bs-55160R, Bioss, Beijing, China), or anti-β-actin (Cat. #93473, Cell Signalling Technology, MA, USA). The membranes were incubated for 2 h at room temperature with a horseradish peroxidase-conjugated secondary antibody and then developed using an enhanced chemiluminescent detection kit (Cat.WBKLS0500, Millipore Corporation, Billerica, MA).

Statistical analysis

Data were displayed as mean ± SEM. All statistical analyses were conducted in Prism software (version 8.0). The Student's t-test was used for comparison between two groups, and then the Student–Newman–Keuls model was selected for analysis. One-way ANOVA was employed for comparison between multiple groups, Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) to evaluate lung function and body weight between the control group and COPD mice at different time points, and then the Turkey test was selected for analyses. P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Identification of FRDEGs in COPD

The COPD peripheral blood datasets and COPD lung tissue datasets were downloaded from the GEO database and then were pooled (Fig. 1). The boxplot and density graphic indicated that the sample distribution of each dataset differs greatly (Fig. 2A and E), and as shown in Fig. 2C, the datasets were discrete. The above results indicate that there is a batch effect in the COPD datasets. We found that the datasets were merged after removing the batch effect, validating that the batch effect was successfully erased (Fig. 2B, D, and F). We obtained the DEGs in the peripheral blood and lung tissues of COPD samples (Fig. 2G and H). A volcano plot was drawn to further show the DEGs (Fig. 2I). Subsequently, the DEGs were intersected with FRGs and identified 280 FRDEGs (Figure S3A-B).

Enrichment analyses of 24 key FRDEGs

To explore the Biological activities and pathways of 24 ferroptosis-related genes among COPD cases, GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were performed. The GO term results exhibited that these FRDEGs were mainly involved in cellular response to chemical stimulus (BP), regulation of cell proliferation (BP), positive regulation of cell communication (BP), positive regulation of signaling (BP), response to endogenous stimulus (BP), positive regulation of immune system process (BP), positive regulation of protein kinase B signaling (BP), ubiquitin-like protein ligase binding (BP), regulation of leukocyte activation (CC), positive regulation of cell death (MF), and regulation of T cell activation (MF) (Fig. 4B). Besides, as revealed by the KEGG enrichment analyses, the 24 FRDEGs mainly participated in the p53 signaling pathway, C-type lectin receptor signaling pathway, FOXO signaling pathway, B cell receptor signaling pathway, PI3K-Akt signaling pathway, T cell receptor signaling pathway, TNF signaling pathway, natural killer cell-mediated cytotoxicity, Jak-STAT signaling pathway, acute myeloid leukemia, cAMP signaling pathway, Toll-like receptor signaling pathway, and HIF-1 signaling pathway (Fig. 4C). We then analyzed and constructed a protein–protein interaction network (PPI) of the 24 FRDEGs through the String database, and further visualized using the Cytoscape software (Fig. 4D). 14 hub genes were obtained based on the Cytoscape software using the CytoHubba plugin method. These 14 FRDEGs interacted with each other as shown in Fig. 4E, and F displayed the interaction degree of each gene.

Validation of the Hub gene signatures in COPD

The gene expression levels of 24 FRDEGs were validated in merged COPD datasets after removing the batch effect, total of 14 FRDEGs was shown to be significantly expressed in COPD compared with normal samples, including PEX3, MDM2, PIK3CA, SAT1, ALOX15B, CDKN1A, AKR1C3, CREB5, CGAS, KIF20A, MTDH, AKR1C2, ENPP2, and BiD. Subsequently, the significant 14 FRDEGs were intersected with the 14 hub genes in PPI network and obtained 8 overlap genes (Fig. 5A). Moreover, the 8 genes were used to construct a risk model using LASSO regression (Fig. 5B, C). Finally, the prognostic value of 8 genes was evaluated. MDM2 and CDKN1A were retained with P < 0.05 and were visualized with a forest plot (Fig. 5D, E). AUC of risk score was 0.6 (Fig. 5F). In addition, ROC curves were also created to verify the predictive accuracy of the 2 gene signatures in COPD, as shown in Fig. 6A. The expression of MDM2 and CDKN1A gene signatures was then validated, and they were all significantly expressed between COPD and normal samples in the training dataset (Fig. 6B, C).

Immune cell infiltration landscape in the COPD dataset

The infiltrating characteristics of various immune cells in peripheral blood and lung tissues with COPD were analyzed using the CIBERSORT method. Figure S5A shows the distribution of 22 types of infiltrating immune cells. The correlation between the immune cells was visualized by a heatmap (Figure S5B). Figure S5C details the differential infiltration of 9 types of immune cells in patients with COPD and controls. T cells CD4 naive, macrophages M0, and Mast cells activated were all more abundant in patients with COPD than in the healthy control group, while B cells naive, T cells CD8, T cells CD4 memory activated, dendritic cells resting, neutrophils, and eosinophils were less expressed in the COPD patients than in healthy controls.

Correlation between hub FRDEGs and immune-infiltrated cells in COPD

Furthermore, we calculated the correlation between 22 types of infiltrating immune cells and the 2 hub FRDEGs. A positive correlation was detected between MDM2 and Macrophages M0, and neutrophils. Instead, MDM2 had negative correlations with T cells follicular helper and T cells regulatory (Fig. 9A). Whereas CDKN1A was significantly positively correlated with neutrophils, dendritic cells activated, mast cells activated, and negatively correlated with T cells CD8, T cells CD4 memory resting, macrophages M2, and mast cells resting (Fig. 9B). Meanwhile, the link between these immune cells and MDM2 and CDKN1A was displayed in the heatmap (Fig. 9C). Moreover, the relationship between MDM2 and CDKN1A and immune-infiltrated cells, and immune infiltration was consistent with the COPD (Fig. 9D-E). The main regulatory target genes of MDM2 and CDKN1A, as illustrated in Fig. 13, MDM2 displayed a mainly positive association with RAG1and ATF5 and a negative correlation association with AREB6, AMD3, and HEN1(Fig. 10A-C). Meanwhile, CDKN1A primarily positively correlated with SVIL, STN1, EGFR, PGBD5, MDM2 and negatively with BAHD1, CHAMP1, ZNF169, TRIP13, ADA2 (Fig. 10D-F). Interestingly, these results demonstrate that MDM2 might be a target of CDKN1A, and CDKN1A could positively regulate the expression of MDM2.

The role of hub genes in respiratory tract diseases

The comparative toxicomics database evaluated the association between MDM2 and CDKN1A and respiratory tract diseases under chemical or environmental exposure. A total of 17 types of respiratory tract diseases were chosen including respiratory tract diseases, lung diseases, lung diseases (Interstitial and Obstructive), respiratory tract infections, bronchial diseases, respiration disorders, pneumonia, respiratory distress syndrome, acute lung injury, respiratory insufficiency, idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, pleural diseases, bronchial hyperreactivity, pulmonary edema, pulmonary disease, and COPD. The inference scores of CDKN1A in different respiratory tract diseases were higher than those of MDM2 (Fig. 11). The results implied that 2 hub genes might be potential treatment targets in multiple respiratory diseases.

The analyses of potential drugs for COPD

Based on the 2 predicted FRDEGs in COPD, we further investigated the potential drug-targeting pathways in the CMAP database. The top 50 key mechanisms of action in COPD and relevant drugs were evaluated using normal connectivity scores in the CMAP database. The mechanism of MDM inhibitor (AMG-232) was dramatically enriched by targeting MDM2-ferroptosis-related gene (Fig. 12). These findings could provide new ideas for the treatment of COPD.

MDM2 and CDKN1A were upregulated in CS and LPS-induced COPD mice

Lastly, we investigated the expression of MDM2 and CDKN1A in lung tissues of CS and LPS-induced model mice to further increase our confidence in the findings. EF50, Ti, TV, Te, and RT increased while f decreased in model mice, compared with the control group, indicating the lung function in conscious mice declined (Fig. 13A). Meanwhile, invasive lung function data showed that CS exposure decreased the lung function ventilation parameter Crs, Cst, IC, FVC, FEV0.1, FEV0.2, FEV0.05, FEV0.1/FVC, FEV0.2/FVC, and FEV0.05/FVC, and increased the resistance parameters Rn, Rrs, and Ers in model mice (Fig. 13B). H&E staining also showed a normal bronchoalveolar structure with few inflammatory cells in the control group, whereas those from the CS/LPS group showed increases in inflammatory cell infiltration and airway wall thickness (Fig. 13C). In the COPD group, the mice showed significant collagen deposition, few collagen fibers were deposited around the airways in the control group (Fig. 13C). An obvious decline in body weight was observed in COPD mice (Fig. 13D).

Then, we found a significant increase in neutrophils in COPD mice (Fig. 14A). Compared with the control group, the protein levels of ferroptosis biomarkers ACSL4 were up-regulated and SLC7A11 and GPX4 were down-regulated in model mice (Fig. 14B). Meanwhile, the expression of MDM2 and CDKN1A in lung tissues of COPD mice was more significantly increased than in the control group (Fig. 14C). Collectively, these results indicated that COPD induces ferroptosis by upregulating the key ferroptosis-related genes MDM2 and CDKN1A expression.

Discussion

COPD led to 3.2 million deaths globally in 2015 [19]. In 2017, the incidence of COPD was about 3.9% and caused a mortality of 41.9 per 100,000 individuals around the world [20]. In the United States, there are 12% of adults (30 million) have COPD, and it is the fourth leading cause of death (126,000 deaths per year) [21]. Moreover, COPD prevalence was higher in Chinese men (11.9%) than in women (5.4%), and 13.7% of Chinese aged 40 years suffer from COPD [22]. Neutrophils, macrophages, lymphocyte subsets, and dendritic cells are both types of immune-infiltrating cells and play a critical role in the airway inflammation and lung destruction of COPD [23]. Ferroptosis is a new method of cell death, which positively participates in the inflammation and immune responses [24, 25]. In the study, we aim to investigate the regulatory role of ferroptosis-related genes between the immune infiltration cells and DEGs in COPD, resulting in screening the potential hub FRDEGs for the treatment of COPD.

There is no research about incorporating the lung tissues and peripheral blood datasets together to study COPD through Bioinformatics analysis. Therefore, we downloaded a total of 10 datasets consisting of 5 COPD peripheral blood datasets and 5 COPD lung tissue datasets. We obtained differentially expressed genes from the merged dataset after removing the batch effect. According to these DEGs, we identified the potential drugs for COPD through the CMAP database. The results of the action mode of the top 50 compounds implied the co-action mechanism of oxprenolol, naftopidil, and labetalol via adrenergic receptor antagonist, whereas SR-57227A and mosapride interacted via serotonin receptor agonist. The interaction mechanism for AMG-232 via the MDM inhibitor restraining the ferroptosis gene MDM2. There is a need to further understand whether these drug-targeting pathways and treatments are effective in treating COPD, especially MDM inhibitors.

Next, the DEGs combined with ferroptosis-related genes, and validated by WGCNA analyses, 24 FRDEGs were identified in patients with COPD compared with normal controls from the training dataset. Subsequently, functional enrichment analysis of GO terms indicated that FRDEGs were mainly enriched in the oxidation–reduction process, cell death, and immune system. KEGG pathway analysis showed that the FRDEGs were enriched in the p53 signaling pathway. Thus, we speculated that cell death may play an important role in COPD. In addition, 14 out of 24 genes were statistically significant in the merged dataset. We constructed a PPI network of FRDEGs and identified 14 genes with high scores, and 8 genes (MDM2, PIK3CA, CDKN1A, AKR1C3, CREB5, KIF20A, MTDH, and AKR1C2) were statistically significant in the training dataset. LASSO-COX regression and ROC analysis of these DEGs were also performed, and finally, we validated 2 hub FRDEGs: MDM2 and CDKN1A. Correspondingly, the COPD dataset consisting of blood and lung tissues was used to confirm the expression of MDM2 and CDKN1A. There was also a significant difference in the level of these two genes in COPD patients compared with that in normal controls. This indicated that the two candidates may mainly participate in COPD by ferroptosis.

To further explore the function of these two critical genes in COPD, the training COPD dataset was divided into a high-expression and a low-expression group according to the median value of MDM2 and CDKN1A. We then applied GO and KEGG analysis to the DEGs. The enrichment results also suggested that their functions were mostly involved in ferroptosis and immune response. In addition, we further investigated the correlation between MDM2 and CDKN1A expression and infiltrated immune cells in COPD samples. Showing that several kinds of significant immune cells have different performances in the two groups. Macrophages M0, neutrophils, activated dendritic cells, and mast cells were positively correlated with MDM2 and CDKN1A expression, respectively. Whereas, T cells follicular helper, T cells regulatory, and T cells CD8, T cells CD4 memory resting, macrophages M2, and mast cells resting were negatively correlated with MDM2 and CDKN1A expression, respectively. Neutrophils had a positive correlation in both MDM2 and CDKN1A, which indicated that the expression of both MDM2 and CDKN1A was critically involved in neutrophils.

It is worth noting that through machine learning algorithms, we discovered that the expression of neutrophils was reduced in COPD patients. But in vivo experiments, it was verified that the Ly6G-neutrophils in COPD mice showed a significantly higher expression compared to the control group. The possible reasons for this discrepancy include the following points: The CIBERSORT algorithm, as a machine learning algorithm, primarily yields results regarding immune cells with statistical differences. Nevertheless, the ultimate validation still predominantly depends on in vivo and in vitro experiments. Secondly, studies have revealed that neutrophils are more sophisticated cells than initially anticipated. Unique subsets of neutrophils displaying distinct morphologies and functions have been discovered. They can alter their phenotypes to adapt to different physiological stressors and stimulus [26]. These neutrophil subpopulations display phenotypical and functional heterogeneity and play crucial roles in health and disease [27]. Neutrophil subsets include tumor-associated neutrophils (TANs), low-density neutrophils (LDNs), normal-density neutrophils (NDNs), and myeloid-derived suppressor cells [28]. Additionally, neutrophils with different functions were identified by a collection of cell surface markers [28, 29]. Finally and most importantly, neutrophils in peripheral blood are navie neutrophils (CXCR1/2high). Under conditions such as inflammation and infection, CXCR1 and other chemokine receptor induce neutrophils to migrate from peripheral blood into tissues, becoming activated and infiltrated neutrophils (CXCR1/2low). Meanwhile, senescent neutrophils (CXCR4highCXCR1/2low) in tissues can also return of the cell to the bone marrow [30]. However, neutrophils are recognized as the central cellular mediator in COPD pathogenesis. Irrespective of underlying clinical phenotype, the airways of patients with COPD are dominated by neutrophilic inflammation, directly correlating with severity of airway obstruction and accelerated decline in lung function based on forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) [31]. Above all, we detected the total neutrophils with a Ly6G + phenotype. In our future research, it is essential to detect which subset of neutrophils plays a dominant role in COPD and which subset is mainly regulated by MDM2/CDKN1A. This will provide a scientific foundation for subsequent targeted neutrophil depletion strategies or interventions targeting MDM2/CDKN1A.

CDKN1A (p21) belongs to a key cell cycle protein and is an important member of cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors [32, 33]. CDKN1A is best known for senescence-associated biomarkers and is transcriptionally controlled by p53 and p53-independent pathways [33, 34]. Several recent studies verified that CDKN1A is a facilitator of ferroptosis and has a positive effect on COPD [35, 36]. However, the precise role of CDKN1A in the progression and development of COPD is still unrevealed. According to our study, we speculate that CDKN1A promotes the development of COPD by ferroptosis. MDM2 is an E3 ubiquitin ligase that regulates target proteins through ubiquitination [37]. The full-length MDM2 (MDM2-FL) is the founding protein of targeting wild-type p53 for degradation by the proteasome, but the functions of the various splice variants of MDM2 need to be explored [38]. Among these results, we found that both MDM2 and CDKN1A had a correlation with p53, which was a pivotal target for aging [39, 40]. Besides, cellular senescence is now considered a crucial driving mechanism for chronic lung diseases, particularly COPD [41, 42]. We speculated that these two highly expressed hub genes in the COPD group may get involved in cell senescence by regulating ferroptosis and thus affect the infiltrating immune cells residing in lung tissues or recruited from peripheral blood. Meanwhile, we found that MDM2 might be an important target gene of CDKN1A. MDM2 and CDKN1A are markedly upregulated in CS and LPS-induced COPD mice.

In addition, as the two hub genes may carry out the dominant role, we further continued to analyze the interaction between the two genes and respiratory tract diseases. The inference scores of CDKN1A in all 17 respiratory tract diseases were higher than those of MDM2 and provided references for CDKN1A as a viable therapeutic target for COPD.

There were obvious limitations in our study. First, the ferroptosis-related regulators were obtained by bioinformatic methods based on GEO and FerrDb databases, which might lead to a high false-positive rate. Second, the in vivo function of these two genes should be verified in more experimental studies.

Conclusions

Above all, our study identified that MDM2 and CDKN1A are significantly associated with COPD, which is related to ferroptosis and immune response. MDM2 and CDKN1A might be the potential therapeutic targets for COPD.
