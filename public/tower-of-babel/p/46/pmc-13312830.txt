Oral fungal microbiome in gout: composition, cross-kingdom interactions, and effects of intervention
Authors: Menghui Yao, Yingdong Han, Chengjin Huang, Mengmeng Dai, Xiaohan Zhang, Yun Zhang, Xuejun Zeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOral fungi, despite their low abundance, may influence microbial interactions, host responses, and disease progression; however, their role in gout remains poorly understood.Materials and methodsBetween March and December 2024, patients with chronic gouty arthritis (CGA), acute gouty arthritis (AGA), and age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HC) were enrolled. Unstimulated saliva underwent ITS2 sequencing. Analyses included α/β-diversity, differential abundance, and genus-level co-occurrence networks. Cross-kingdom associations were assessed using partial Spearman correlation integrating 16S rRNA data. A subset received lifestyle intervention and urate-lowering therapy with follow-up.ResultsA total of 76 participants (35 CGA, 17 AGA, 24 HC) were included. Alpha-diversity showed no significant difference, while β-diversity distinguished patients from HC ( P = 0.0018). Ascomycota and Basidiomycota predominated. Alternaria, Eutypella, and Saccharomyces was enriched in CGA. Whereas Eutypella was enriched in AGA. Network analysis revealed progressive loss of fungal–fungal and fungal–bacterial interactions, with strengthened pathogenic fungi–bacteria associations. Follow-up indicated partial restoration of fungal–fungal networks preceding compositional recovery.ConclusionThis study provides the first characterization of the oral mycobiome in gout, highlighting weakened microbial connectivity and reinforced pathogen associations. Interventions may restore network integrity before compositional normalization, suggesting the oral mycobiome as a potential target in gout.

Introduction

Gout is a metabolic arthritis caused by the deposition of monosodium urate (MSU) crystals, secondary to elevated serum uric acid (SUA), which triggers joint inflammation [1]. It typically begins as acute monoarthritis, often precipitated by diet or alcohol, and may progress to chronic gouty arthritis (CGA) with persistent pain, crystal deposition, and joint damage [2]. With lifestyle and dietary changes, the prevalence of hyperuricemia (HUA) and gout has risen in China, affecting about 13.3% and 1–3% of the population, respectively [3]. However, HUA alone does not fully explain gout pathogenesis: many individuals with elevated SUA remain asymptomatic, and even in severe HUA (≥10 mg/dL), fewer than half develop gout within 15 years [4,5]. This discrepancy suggests that additional factors, including host–microbiome interactions, may contribute to gout pathogenesis.

Recent evidence highlights a critical role of the microbiota in metabolic and inflammatory disorders [6,7]. The oral cavity, as a critical ecological niche of the human body, harbours a highly diverse community of microorganisms, including bacteria, fungi, viruses, archaea, and protozoa [8]. While bacterial dysbiosis has been linked to gout and other metabolic diseases, fungi—though representing only a small proportion of the oral microbiome (<0.1%)—are increasingly recognised as key players in microbial community stability, immune regulation, and inflammatory responses [9,10]. Emerging evidence further suggests that low-abundance fungal taxa may function as ecological ‘keystone’ species or functional triggers, exerting disproportionate biological effects through metabolite production, interkingdom interactions with bacterial communities, or modulation of host immune responses. Consequently, their functional and pathological relevance may extend far beyond what is implied by their relative abundance alone [10,11].

Growing research indicates that fungal dysbiosis is associated with a wide spectrum of metabolic diseases. A recent study on metabolic dysfunction–associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) reported that the abundance of Mucor ambiguus in the oral cavity was positively correlated with body mass index (BMI) and blood lipid levels [12]. Liu et al. analysed the oral mycobiome in patients with schizophrenia and found reduced fungal richness, with certain fungi such as Malassezia positively correlated with peripheral pro-inflammatory cytokines, whereas Candida albicans was negatively correlated [13]. These findings suggest that oral fungal dysbiosis is also associated with systemic inflammation. Although direct evidence in gout is lacking, these studies collectively support a possible link between fungal imbalance, chronic inflammation, and metabolic alterations. Gout is strongly influenced by diet and lifestyle, which also shape the oral microbiome. Compared with other inflammatory arthritis, this may render the oral microbial community particularly relevant in gout, highlighting its potential role in disease development and progression.

More importantly, fungal effects should be viewed in the context of their interactions within the oral microbiome, which are essential for microbial balance and host health. Studies have shown that the majority of oral fungi interact with at least one bacterial or fungal partner, and these cross-kingdom interactions are essential for sustaining microbial equilibrium; disruption of fungal homoeostasis, however, can promote the expansion of pathogenic communities, thereby increasing host susceptibility to opportunistic infections [14]. With the advent of high-throughput sequencing technologies, our understanding of the oral mycobiome has deepened, revealing its roles in microbial balance, immune modulation, and metabolic pathways [15]. These advances not only underscore the significance of fungi in both oral and systemic diseases but also highlight that elucidating the complex mechanisms of oral fungal activity may open new avenues for disease prevention and therapeutic strategies.

Despite growing insights into the oral mycobiome, its role in gout remains largely unexplored. This study aims to investigate changes in the oral fungal community across different stages of gout, examine its cross-kingdom interactions with bacteria, and evaluate the effects of follow-up interventions on the oral mycobiome.

Methods

Study population

This study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Peking Union Medical College Hospital (I-24PJ0142). From March to December 2024, patients with CGA and acute gouty arthritis (AGA) attending the gout clinic were consecutively recruited, along with age- and sex-matched healthy controls (HC).

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Participants were ≥18 years old and had not used antibiotics, probiotics, fermented foods, or immunosuppressants within the past month. Participants were classified by experienced gout clinicians based on clinical judgement and expert consensus: AGA was defined as acute joint swelling and pain of less than one week, with persistent symptoms at the outpatient visit, VAS >5, no glucocorticoid use, and no formation of tophi; CGA was defined by the presence of tophi on physical examination, ultrasound, or dual-energy CT, or evident gout-related bone erosion or destruction on imaging [16]. Exclusion criteria included severe systemic comorbidities (e.g. cardiovascular disease, autoimmune disease, diabetes, hepatic or renal dysfunction, infection, or malignancy), oral diseases (caries, periodontitis, oral abscess, oral mucosal diseases, or oral cancer), recent dental scaling or periodontal treatment within the previous 3 months, and gingival bleeding during saliva collection. Additionally, a preliminary oral examination was performed at enrolment to exclude obvious visual abnormalities, including caries, gingival swelling or redness, oral ulcers, tooth mobility or loss, and oral masses. All participants provided written informed consent. Demographics, comorbidities, urate-lowering therapy (ULT), and laboratory data were obtained from medical records.

Saliva collection

Approximately 4 mL of unstimulated whole saliva was collected following the NIH Human Microbiome Project protocols. Participants fasted and refrained from oral hygiene for at least 4 hours prior to sampling. Samples were transported on ice and stored at −80 °C within 2 hours of collection. Upon arrival at the laboratory, each saliva sample was divided into aliquots, with one stored at −80 °C as a backup and the remaining portion further split for ITS2 sequencing (fungal profiling) and 16S rRNA sequencing (bacterial profiling). All samples were processed and handled under identical conditions.

ITS2 sequencing and analysis

Genomic DNA was extracted from saliva using a magnetic-bead based genomic DNA extraction kit (NMG1251, NanoMagBio) that combines mechanical, chemical and thermal lysis steps optimised for environmental fungi and yeast. Briefly, ~200 µL homogenised saliva was transferred to bead-beating tubes containing 400 µL Buffer A, 60 µL Buffer B and 10 µL RNase A. Samples were bead-beaten at 60 Hz for 30 s with a 10 s pause and this cycle was repeated three times, followed by incubation at 70 °C for 10 min. After clarification and addition of Buffer C, nucleic acids were bound to functionalized magnetic beads, washed twice, air-dried briefly, and eluted in 100 µL elution buffer at 65 °C. Negative extraction and blank controls were included in each batch, and all sample handling was performed in a biological safety cabinet to minimise potential environmental contamination. Extracted DNA was quantified using a Qubit dsDNA HS assay and inspected by agarose gel electrophoresis prior to library preparation.

Fungal community composition was profiled by PCR amplification of the ITS2 region with primers ITS2F (5′-GTGARTCATCGAATCTTT-3′) and ITS2R (5′-GATATGCTTAAGTTCAGCGGGT-3′). Libraries were prepared following the standard Metabiote (GenoScreen) protocol, including indexing and purification steps, and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq platform (300 bp paired-end reads). PCR conditions were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 3 min; 30 cycles of 95 °C for 30 s, 55 °C for 30 s and 72 °C for 45 s; final extension at 72 °C for 5 min. Amplicons were purified and quantified prior to pooling.

Raw reads were quality filtered and processed using established pipelines. Primer sequences were removed and reads were quality trimmed (bases with Phred score < 20 and reads with ambiguous bases were discarded); paired reads were merged and chimeric sequences were removed using UCHIME/USEARCH. Sequences were clustered into OTUs at 97% similarity using USEARCH; Representative OTU sequences were taxonomically assigned against the UNITE ITS reference database (version 12_11).

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are expressed as medians (IQR) and compared using Kruskal–Wallis or Mann–Whitney U tests; categorical variables using χ² test. α-diversity was evaluated by Simpson and Chao1 indices. β-diversity was assessed via unweighted uniFrac distances, with group differences evaluated using Kruskal–Walli’s test and Dunn’s post hoc comparisons. Rare or low-abundance taxa (present in < 20% of samples or with mean relative abundance < 0.01%) were first filtered, and genus-level differential abundance was subsequently analysed using ANCOM-BC2 (FDR < 0.20).

Functional prediction of fungal taxa was performed using FUNGuild [17]. Genus-level data were CLR-transformed and used to construct fungal co-occurrence networks within each study group. Significant associations were defined based on Spearman correlations (|R| ≥ 0.4, P ≤ 0.05). To compare network connectivity across groups, 500 bootstrap resamplings were performed per group. Core microbial taxa were identified based on topological features of the co-occurrence network. For each taxon, degree, betweenness centrality, closeness centrality, and eigenvector centrality were first z-standardised and then summed to generate a composite centrality score. The top five taxa with the highest composite scores were selected as putative hub taxa; in cases where the fifth and sixth taxa shared identical scores, both were included. High degree indicates extensive direct connections within the network, high betweenness reflects a bridging role between modules, high closeness denotes efficient connectivity to all other nodes, and high eigenvector centrality highlights influence through association with other central taxa. Integrating these metrics allows robust identification of taxa potentially critical to oral microbial community stability. Gout-associated fungal genera were defined as those that were either differentially abundant between groups or identified as core nodes in the CGA or AGA networks. The clinical relevance of gout-associated fungal genera was assessed using partial Spearman correlations adjusted for ULT and disease group. A two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Finally, to explore interactions between bacterial and fungal communities, bacterial abundance data derived from 16S rRNA sequencing of the same saliva samples (Yao et al., 2025, unpublished data) were integrated with the ITS2 dataset. Spearman partial correlation analyses were performed after adjusting for age, BMI, disease duration, and ULT. Potential hub taxa were identified based on the Zi–Pie topological roles. Specifically, module hubs were defined as nodes with Zi > 1.5 and Pie < 0.6, whereas connectors were defined as nodes with Zi < 1.5 and Pie > 0.6. The Zi value reflects the extent of a node’s connectivity with other nodes within the same module, while Pie represents the extent to which a node links different modules [18,19]. The identified taxa were considered ‘potential’ hub taxa, as their ecological importance has not yet been experimentally validated.

Longitudinal follow-up and treatment response

A subset of gout outpatients was prospectively followed to evaluate the impact of standardised management on the oral mycobiome. These participants received structured patient education, lifestyle modification counselling, and guideline-based ULT. Clinical assessments, including SUA levels and gout flare frequency, were recorded at baseline and during follow-up. Follow-up saliva samples were collected when SUA had decreased by more than 20% from baseline and gout flares were markedly reduced. Unstimulated saliva samples were collected under identical conditions as at baseline. Sample handling, ITS2 sequencing, and bioinformatic processing were performed as described above.

Longitudinal analyses included paired comparisons of α- and β-diversity metrics, paired differential abundance testing using ANCOM-BC2, and evaluation of intrafungal co-occurrence networks to assess treatment-associated changes in community topology and stability. Follow-up duration was included as a covariate in relevant statistical analyses.

Bacterial 16S rRNA sequencing and data analysis

Methods for bacterial 16S rRNA sequencing and data analysis used in this study are described in detail in the Supplementary Methods.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

76 participants were enroled, including 35 CGA, 17 AGA, and 24 HC (Table 1). All were male, with no significant age differences among groups. Overweight and obesity were more common in-patient groups but not statistically significant. Compared with AGA, CGA patients had longer disease duration (P < 0.001), and 82.9% presented with tophi. In contrast, WBC and NEU were significantly higher in AGA than CGA (P = 0.011 and 0.009), suggesting greater systemic inflammation; C response protein (CRP) was also higher but not significant. Lipid profile, smoking, alcohol, and urate-lowering therapy showed no group differences.

After merging and quality-filtering raw sequences, OTU clustering at 97% similarity identified a total of 411 OTUs: 328 in CGA, 225 in AGA, and 209 in HC. A Venn diagram showed 126 OTUs shared across all groups, with group-specific OTUs numbering 122 (CGA), 33(AGA), and 31(HC) (Figure 1A). Microbial diversity and composition

α-diversity, assessed by Chao1 and Shannon indices, showed no statistically significant differences among CGA, AGA, and HC groups, although a decreasing trend was observed in CGA (Figure 1B). β-diversity analysis using unweighted UniFrac distances revealed significant differences in overall community structure among groups (P < 0.001), with pairwise comparisons showing separation between HC and both patient groups, while CGA and AGA were similar (Figure 1C). Principal coordinates analysis(PCoA) further illustrated these group-specific patterns, and PERMANOVA (adonis test) confirmed significant differences in fungal composition (R2 = 0.05, P = 0.0018) (Figure 1D).

At the phylum level, communities were dominated by Ascomycota and Basidiomycota, with HC showing a higher proportion of Ascomycota (84.3%) than CGA (78.4%) and AGA (75.6%) (Figure 1E). At the genus level, Penicillium was dominant across all groups (CGA 50.4%, AGA 49.1%, HC 59.0%), while Candida was most abundant in CGA (7.4%) and lowest in AGA (4.0%). Other genera, including Malassezia, Purpureocillium, Rhinocladiella, Talaromyces, Fusarium, Aspergillus, and Saccharomyces, exhibited minor variations among groups (Figure 1F).

Differential abundance analysis

At the genus level, oral fungal communities differed significantly among CGA, AGA, and HC groups, exhibiting stage-dependent patterns. Compared with HC group, CGA group showed enrichment of Alternaria, Eutypella, and Saccharomyces, while genera such as Rhinocladiella and Aspergillus were depleted (Figure 2A). In AGA group relative to HC group, Eutypella and Cercospora were increased, whereas Hypomontagnella and Pichia were decreased (Figure 2B). Comparing CGA group with AGA group, Pichia, Hypomontagnella, Monascus, Alternaria and Cercospora were enriched, while Eutypella and Rhinocladiella were reduced (Figure 2C). Overall, these results indicate that oral fungal community composition changes dynamically across different stages of gout, highlighting stage-specific structural shifts.

Fungal community trophic modes

To evaluate the trophic strategies of oral fungal communities, OTUs were annotated using FUNGuild and classified into specific nutritional and functional guilds. Approximately 45.5% of OTUs were predicted as ‘highly probable’ or ‘probable’ for a given lifestyle. In HC, the dominant trophic modes were pathogens (33.0%), pathogen–saprotrophs (23.0%), and pathogen–saprotroph–symbionts (23.0%). In AGA, pathogens (29.9%), pathogen–saprotroph–symbionts (23.4%), and saprotrophs (24.3%) were dominant. In CGA, saprotrophs were most abundant (29.8%), followed by pathogens (27.2%) and pathogen–saprotrophs (19.9%) (Figure 2D). These findings suggest that different gout states may influence the composition and ecological balance of oral fungal communities, potentially promoting a shift toward composite and disease-associated trophic strategies.

Fungal interaction networks

Genus-level oral fungal interaction networks exhibited significant differences in topology among CGA, AGA, and HC groups, which were confirmed by 500 bootstrap resamplings. Compared with HC, the CGA network showed a marked reduction in both nodes and edges (adjusted P < 0.001), indicating a sparser and less connected network. The AGA network displayed a similar number of nodes to HC (adjusted P = 0.90) but a significantly lower number of edges (adjusted P < 0.001), representing an intermediate or transitional state (Figure 3A–E).

Core microbial taxa were identified based on composite centrality scores integrating degree, betweenness, closeness, and eigenvector centralities. In the CGA network, the top five hub taxa were Penicillium, Aspergillus, Purpureocillium, Cladosporium, and Cercospora (Supplementary table S1). In the AGA network, the top six taxa (Fusarium, Sterigmatomyces, Malassezia, Penicillium, Purpureocillium, and Talaromyces) were selected due to identical scores for the fifth and sixth ranks (Supplementary table S2). In the HC network, the top five hub taxa were Sterigmatomyces, Penicillium, Rhinocladiella, Thermoascus, and Talaromyces. These hub taxa likely play key roles in maintaining the structure and stability of the oral fungal community (Supplementary table S3). Overall, these results suggest a progressive shift in oral fungal networks from stable interactions in healthy controls toward functional imbalance during different stages of gout.

Fungal correlations with clinical parameters

A total of 15 gout-associated fungal genera (Alternaria, Aspergillus, Cercospora, Cladosporium, Eutypella, Fusarium, Hypomontagnella, Malassezia, Penicillium, Pichia, Purpureocillium, Rhinocladiella, Saccharomyces, Sterigmatomyces, Talaromyces) were identified, defined as those exhibiting differential abundance or serving as key genera within the fungal interaction networks of CGA and AGA. Correlation analysis revealed significant associations between gout-related fungal genera and clinical parameters in patients with gout. Alternaria showed positive correlations with lymphocytes, high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-c), and creatinine. Sterigmatomyces was negatively correlated with Ly but positively correlated with the neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR). Malassezia and Pichia displayed positively correlations with tophi. Notably, no fungal genera were found to be significantly associated with SUA levels (Supplementary Figure S2).

Fungal–bacterial community interactions

We next investigated whether cross-kingdom interactions between fungal and bacterial communities differed across disease phenotypes. For each group, genus-level co-occurrence networks were constructed incorporating 15 gout-associated fungal genera and all bacterial genera with a mean relative abundance >0.01% and present in at least 50% of samples (Yao et al., unpublished data) (Supplementary Figure S3-5). The analyses demonstrated that, compared with HC, the fungal–bacterial network in CGA was markedly sparser, as reflected by reduced numbers of nodes and edges. To further delineate disease-specific associations, we analysed correlations between the 15 gout-associated fungal genera and 37 gout-associated bacterial genera (Yao et al., unpublished data, gout-associated bacterial genera were defined using the same criteria as for gout-associated fungal genera). Interestingly, although the overall fungal–bacterial connectivity was attenuated in CGA, the associations between disease-related fungal genera and pathogenic bacterial taxa were stronger than those observed in HC (Figure 4A−C). To identify topologically important taxa, network roles were classified based on Zi–Pie metrics. In the CGA network, potential hub fungi were notably more numerous, including Malassezia, Rhinocladiella, Saccharomyces, Fusarium, and Sterigmatomyces, with Malassezia showing the highest Zi score. Malassezia was positively correlated with anaerobic bacterial genera such as Fusobacterium, Gemella, and Catonella, and negatively correlated with Roseburia and Lawsonibacter. The bacterial genus Roseburia was identified as a connector, bridging different modules. In contrast, the AGA network contained fewer fungal hubs (Eutypella, Purpureocillium, Alternaria), and the HC network included only Fusarium, Eutypella, and Malassezia as hubs, with no prominent cross-module bacterial connectors observed (Supplementary Figure S6). Collectively, these findings indicate that cross-kingdom microbial networks are substantially remodelled in gout, with an increased number of key fungal and bacterial taxa contributing to CGA-associated network architecture.

Treatment response and longitudinal assessment

Elevent gout outpatients underwent education, enhanced lifestyle management, and regular urate-lowering therapy. Oral saliva samples were recollected for ITS2 sequencing after a mean follow-up of 6.5 ± 1.9 months. During this period, SUA levels declined substantially compared with baseline, accompanied by a marked reduction in the frequency of gout flares (Figure 5A−B). Microbiome analysis revealed no significant differences between baseline and follow-up samples in either α-diversity or β-diversity (Figure 5C−D). Paired differential abundance testing with ANCOM-BC2 similarly identified no genera with significant changes. Interestingly, following standardised treatment, the fungal networks showed an increased number of edges and a denser overall structure compared with the more dispersed networks observed before treatment, suggesting a partial restoration of community stability (Figure 5E−H).

Discussion

This study is the first to comprehensively depict oral fungal composition and abundance changes across different stages of gout. While α-diversity showed only a slight reduction in CGA patients, β-diversity analyses revealed significant alterations in overall community structure, with patient groups clearly separated from healthy controls. At the taxonomic level, Ascomycota and Basidiomycota dominated all groups. Notably, Candida was enriched in CGA, consistent with opportunistic growth under inflammatory conditions [20]. These findings indicate that gout is associated with subtle yet discernible changes in oral fungal composition, which may influence local immune responses and systemic inflammatory susceptibility.

We observed a significant enrichment of Alternaria in the oral cavity of patients with CGA. As a common indoor fungal allergen, Alternaria has been extensively implicated in asthma, where it disrupts epithelial barriers and promotes chronic inflammation through activation of protease-activated receptor-2 (PAR-2) [21,22]. Its overgrowth in the oral environment may similarly impair mucosal integrity, facilitate microbial translocation, and increase susceptibility to inflammation [23]. Correlation analyses further linked Alternaria to lymphocyte count, serum creatinine, and HDL-c, suggesting its involvement in immune and metabolic alterations in gout.

We also found that Eutypella was increased in both CGA and AGA patients. Secondary metabolites produced by Eutypella possess anti-inflammatory and immunosuppressive properties, including suppression of lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-induced inflammation and modulation of immune cell function. Thus, the expansion of Eutypella may represent a counter-regulatory response aimed at limiting excessive inflammation [24,25]. Interestingly, another fungus with potential anti-inflammatory activity, Hypomontagnella, was reduced in AGA patients. Its metabolites have been shown to inhibit the expression of inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), tumour necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), interleukin-1β (IL-1β), and interleukin-6 (IL-6), thereby attenuating LPS-induced macrophage inflammation [26]. This contrasting pattern may reflect distinct ecological niches or differences in host–microbe interactions.

FUNGuild trophic-mode analysis showed marked functional shifts in the oral fungal community under different disease states, supporting the notion of ecological reprogramming in gout patients. In HC, fungi annotated as pathogens and related composite modes made up a large proportion of the community. Many of these taxa are opportunistic pathobionts that coexist with the host under immune homoeostasis without causing disease [27]. In CGA, saprotrophic fungi were dominant, suggesting increased substrate availability in the oral microenvironment, including cellular debris, protein degradation products and inflammation-associated residues. This dominance may reshape the local metabolic profile and in turn influence host inflammatory responses and tissue homoeostasis.

Fungi are generally considered important for maintaining community stability [14]. In this study, we constructed genus-level co-occurrence networks based on pairwise correlations between microbial genera, capturing ecological relationships such as mutualism or competition. This network-based approach facilitates the exploration of community interaction architecture, the identification of key taxa, and the comparison of microbial organisation patterns under different conditions [28]. Our genus-level network analysis revealed a marked reduction in fungal–fungal associations in patients with CGA. Compared with HC, the fungal network in CGA exhibited substantially lower connectivity, suggesting impaired resilience and buffering capacity of the fungal community. Cross-kingdom interactions between fungi and oral bacteria also warrant attention. These interactions can facilitate adhesion and colonisation, with fungi providing both mechanical support and a favourable substratum for bacterial attachment [29]. Our cross-kingdom analysis showed an overall decline in fungal–bacterial interactions, whereas specific associations between gout-related fungi and bacteria were reinforced. This pattern of diminished global connectivity accompanied by strengthened, potentially pathogenic local couplings may exacerbate dysregulated microbe–microbe and microbe–host interactions [28]. Well-documented examples, such as the interaction between Candida albicans and Streptococcus species, demonstrate how cross-kingdom cooperation can modulate biofilm formation, virulence, and host immune responses, suggesting that these altered networks may directly contribute to oral and systemic inflammation [30].

Among the key fungal hubs in the CGA network, Malassezia is widely distributed on the oral mucosa and, due to its lipid-dependent metabolism, can alter local fatty acid composition, potentially affecting nutrient availability for neighbouring bacteria [31]. It can also form mixed biofilms with bacteria, influencing community stability and host immune responses. In CGA, Malassezia showed positive correlations with anaerobic genera such as Fusobacterium, Gemella and Catonella, suggesting shared niches or cooperative colonisation. Conversely, it was negatively associated with Roseburia and Lawsonibacter, which are linked to short-chain fatty acid production (SCFA) and metabolic homoeostasis [32]. This pattern indicates that Malassezia may reshape the oral microbial network in CGA, favoring inflammation-associated taxa while limiting beneficial bacteria, thereby contributing to dysbiosis. Notably, Roseburia acted as a network connector, bridging modules and facilitating inter-module communication. As a SCFA-producing bacterium involved in mucosal homoeostasis and immune regulation, its negative association with Malassezia highlights a potential ecological balance between lipid-dependent fungi and SCFA-producing bacteria, which may influence microbial stability and inflammatory status in CGA [32].

Following patient education, lifestyle modification, and guideline-based urate-lowering therapy, the fungal–fungal interaction network exhibited early signs of topological recovery. In contrast, conventional diversity metrics (α-diversity and β-diversity) and paired differential abundance analyses did not reveal significant compositional shifts during the six-month follow-up. Taken together, these results indicate, on the one hand, that the oral mycobiome displays relative compositional stability, and on the other hand, that network-level recovery may precede detectable taxonomic alterations [33]. This suggests that ecological reorganisation and the restoration of interspecies interactions could serve as early and sensitive indicators of microbiome recovery. Nevertheless, the limited longitudinal data highlight the need for confirmation in larger prospective cohorts.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size and inclusion of only male participants may limit the generalisability of our findings. Second, ITS2 sequencing provides taxonomic resolution primarily at the genus level; future metagenomic studies may yield more precise species-level and functional insights. Third, the fungal co-occurrence and bacteria–fungi interaction networks were inferred from statistical correlations and may not fully reflect true ecological interactions; thus, experimental validation is warranted in future studies. In addition, comprehensive periodontal assessments (e.g. probing depth, clinical attachment loss, and plaque index) were not performed, which may allow residual confounding from local oral conditions. Future studies will include full periodontal examinations to better control for oral factors affecting the salivary mycobiome.

In conclusion, this study provides the first comprehensive characterisation of the oral mycobiome in gout, identifying differences in fungal community structure and interaction patterns across disease stages. As the disease progresses to a chronic stage, both intra-fungal co-occurrence patterns and overall fungal–bacterial associations appear to be attenuated, while associations between certain pathogenic fungi and bacteria tend to be more evident. Notably, intervention was associated with partial restoration of network connectivity, which may precede detectable changes in overall community composition. These findings suggest that the oral mycobiome may represent a potential target for further investigation in gout, although causal relationships require further validation.
