TMS-EEG signatures of the effects of transcranial static magnetic field stimulation (tSMS) on cortical excitability
Authors: Sumiya Shibata, Hideaki Onishi, Tatsuya Mima
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In transcranial static magnetic field stimulation (tSMS), a strong and small magnet placed over the head can modulate cortical functions below the magnet as well as those in the region remote from the magnet. We studied the neuromodulation induced by tSMS using transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) combined with simultaneous electroencephalography (EEG) to clarify the neurophysiological underpinnings of tSMS. tSMS or sham stimulation was applied over the left primary motor cortex (M1) for 20 min in 15 healthy subjects. Single pulse TMS was delivered over the left M1 before and after the intervention, while recording EEG. The amplitude around the P30 of the TMS-evoked potentials (TEPs) in the left primary sensorimotor area (SM1) significantly decreased after the real tSMS, and that around the N60 of the TEPs in the right SM1 significantly increased after the real tSMS. In addition, the alpha power of the TMS-induced oscillatory responses (IORs) in the left and right SM1 significantly decreased after the real tSMS. TMS-EEG is a powerful tool for studying local and global cortical reactivity to external stimuli at high temporal resolution. tSMS altered TEPs and IORs both at the stimulated cortex and at the contralateral cortex. These findings would be related to the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying the neuromodulation induced by tSMS.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-72875-1.

Introduction

Transcranial static magnetic field stimulation (tSMS) is a newly developed non-invasive brain stimulation1–6. By holding a strong and compact NdFeB magnet over the head, tSMS can suppress brain functions just below the magnet and cause behavioural effects on cognition, motor control, learning, and memory4,7–9. Furthermore, tSMS can modulate cortical functions in the region remote from the magnet via brain network5,10,11.

The neurophysiological mechanisms underlying the neuromodulation of tSMS still remain to be elucidated. In cellular systems, the static magnetic field is assumed to influence cell membranes and embedded channels12. The deformation of cell membranes may change functions of the ion channels, leading to the alteration of the intrinsic electrical properties of neurons. In human brain activities, tSMS over the primary motor cortex (M1) increased the theta electroencephalography (EEG) power at the stimulation site6, while tSMS over the occipital and parietal cortex increased the alpha EEG power at the stimulation site3,4. Since these studies are based on EEG recordings during rest, the effects of tSMS on brain responses to external perturbance are still unknown.

Concurrent transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and EEG (TMS-EEG) is a powerful tool for studying local cortical reactivity to external stimuli at high temporal resolution13,14. Since TMS-EEG can record from the whole head, it also permits investigation of brain networks involved in the propagation of the cortical reactions. Main outcome measures of TMS-EEG are TMS-evoked potentials (TEPs) and TMS-related cortical oscillations. TEPs are brain waveforms time-locked to the TMS pulses, reflecting excitability for the brain area. Especially the TEP in the M1 elucidated by TMS over the M1 is well characterized by positive and negative peaks13. TMS-related cortical oscillations are brain rhythms evoked and/or induced by the TMS pulses15. Evoked oscillatory responses (EORs) are signals phase-locked to the TMS pulses, while induced oscillatory responses (IORs) are non-phase-locked signals. IORs reflect the EEG responses with variable phase relationship to the stimulus onset which are cancelled out toward zero during the averaging for the computation of EORs.

Here, we studied the local and network effects of tSMS over the M1 on the responses to external perturbance using TMS-EEG. Stimulus-locked and non-stimulus-locked brain responses were assessed by TEPs and IORs respectively.

Discussion

The aim of this study is to investigate the modulatory effects on local and global cortical areas induced by 20 min of tSMS over the left M1. For this purpose, we measured the TEPs and the IORs in the left and right SM1 by means of TMS-EEG recordings before the intervention, immediately after, and 20 min after the end of the intervention. Single-pulse TMS was applied over the left M1. Our results showed that the amplitude around the P30 of the TEPs in the left SM1, which was a tSMS target, significantly decreased after the real tSMS, and that that around the N60 of the TEPs in the right SM1, which was contralateral to the stimulation site, significantly increased after the real tSMS. In addition, we also found that the alpha power of the IORs in the left SM1 significantly decreased after the real tSMS, and that the alpha power of the IORs in the right SM1 significantly decreased after the real tSMS.

TEPs are thought to result from temporal and spatial summation of excitatory and inhibitory post-synaptic potentials13,16. The P30 amplitude in the left SM1 stimulated by TMS was positively correlated to the amplitude of the MEP from the opposite hand in healthy people17. In the progressive myoclonic epilepsy patients, the P30 amplitude was larger than the control, indicating hyperexcitability of the motor cortex18. These findings suggest that the P30 potential reflects the excitability of the corticospinal system. Actually, low and high dosages of cathodal tDCS over the M1, which reduced the excitability of the corticospinal system, reduced the P3019. The P30 is likely generated through the cortico-basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical loops20, although the origin of the P30 is only partially known. In our study tSMS decreased the P30 in the area below the magnet. This might indicate that tSMS decreases the cortical excitability of the M1 below the magnet1 through the inhibition of the cortico-basal ganglia-thalamo-cortical loops. However, all of the inhibitory brain stimulation modalities did not reduce the P30. 1-Hz repetitive TMS, which also inhibits the cortical excitability, over the M1 did not change P3021. The mechanisms underlying the effects of the tSMS on P30 remain for future studies.

Brain oscillation in specific frequency bands is attributed to distinct brain functions22. TMS-EEG is a powerful tool for probing the frequency tuning of some cortical areas23. TMS is employed to directly perturb the cortical area and EEG is used to measure the ensuing oscillations. IORs are the cortical oscillatory responses non-phase-locked to TMS. They are thought to reflect the naturally occurring oscillatory activities of the cortical area prior to TMS24. Single-pulse TMS over the M1 induced an early (30–200 ms after TMS) synchronization in the alpha and beta frequency in IORs at the stimulated SM1. The synchronization is thought to reflect the direct excitation of the oscillation-generating cortico-cortical and cortico-subcortical circuits25. In this study the real tSMS decreased the alpha oscillation in the stimulated SM1. The frequency power alteration may result from the modulation of the cortico-cortical and cortico-subcortical circuits by tSMS. The physiological alpha and low beta rhythm at the central region is known as the Rolandic mu rhythm26. The mu rhythm reflects sensorimotor processing as well as perception-to-action processing27. Since tSMS over the SM1 altered the excitability of the cortex below the magnet1,2 and motor learning7, these alterations may be associated with the decrease of the IORs in the alpha frequency shown in this study.

We also found the modulatory changes of TEPs and IORs in the right SM1, which was contralateral to the stimuli (TMS and tSMS). The propagation of the activities evoked by TMS can be pursued via the waveforms and spreads of TEPs28. Rhythmic brain activity reflects not only intrinsic neuronal properties but also interactions with other brain areas22. The investigation of the spatiotemporal distribution of TEPs and IORs are useful for evaluating cortico-cortical connectivity28. In this study the prominent negative peak, N60, was noted in the right SM1. The negative peak at the contralateral central region was also observed in previous TMS-EEG studies despite of small difference in the latency29,30. The amplitude of N60 was increased by tSMS. tSMS facilitated the excitability of the contralateral cortex through the attenuation of interhemispheric inhibition (IHI)10. Two types of IHI with different underlying mechanisms are known: short latency IHI (SIHI) and long latency IHI (LIHI)31. SIHI is likely mediated by direct transcallosal pathways while LIHI is likely mediated by multi-synaptic pathways widely distributed in motor related cortex. Since LIHI was elicited at interstimulus intervals of ~ 50 ms31, the facilitation of N60 by tSMS may reflect the modulation of LIHI by tSMS. This is consistent with the previous study10. These findings indicate that tSMS may inhibit multi-synaptic pathways from the left SM1 to the right SM1. Event-related synchronization of alpha is proposed to play an active role for the inhibitory control32. The decrease of alpha power in the right SM1 as well as the left SM1 may also reflect the attenuation of LIHI.

The neurophysiological mechanisms of tSMS over the M1 were investigated using TMS-EEG. In TEPs tSMS decreased the early positive peak (P30) in the stimulated SM1 and increased the early negative peak (N60) in the contralateral SM1. In IORs tSMS decreased the alpha oscillation in the stimulated SM1 and the contralateral SM1. The findings of the TEP components and the IORs may be related to the neuronal circuit or pathway modulated by tSMS. The findings in this study would be related to the neurophysiological mechanisms underlying the alteration of the brain function by tSMS.

Methods

Subjects.

Fifteen healthy volunteers (nine men, 21–25 years of age, mean ± standard deviation age 22.3 ± 1.1 years, the same below) participated in this study. All of them were right-handed on the Edinburgh handedness inventory33. The sample size was estimated with G-Power 3.1.9.7 software using the following parameters: an effect size of 0.25, alpha error of 0.05, statistical power of 0.8, ANOVA: Repeated measures, within-between interaction34. The required sample size was calculated to be 14 per group (total 28 for two groups). The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committees of Niigata University of Health and Welfare (Niigata, Japan). Written informed consent was obtained from all subjects. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Experimental design (Fig. 4)

Fig. 4 Experimental setup and protocol. Real or sham tSMS was applied over the left M1 for 20 min. TMS-EEG was recorded before (baseline), immediately after (post1), and 20 min after (post2) the intervention.

Experimental setup and protocol. Real or sham tSMS was applied over the left M1 for 20 min. TMS-EEG was recorded before (baseline), immediately after (post1), and 20 min after (post2) the intervention.

Each experimental session involved the measurement of EEG responses to single-pulse TMS targeting the left M1 before (baseline), immediately after (post1), and 20 min after (post2) real or sham tSMS over the left M1 for 20 min. To avoid carryover effects, the interval between the real and sham tSMS was set to be more than three days2,6.

Materials

TMS was delivered using Magstim 2002 magnetic stimulator connected to a 70 mm figure-eight coil (Magstim Co., Whitland, UK), generating a monophasic current waveform. The left M1 was identified as the coil position which led to the largest motor evoked potentials (MEPs) of the right first dorsal interosseous (FDI) muscle. The MEPs were recorded through a pair of Ag-AgCl surface electrodes placed over the right FDI muscle, using a belly-tendon montage. Electromyogram signals were amplified, band-pass filtered (5–1000 Hz), and digitized at 10 kHz (Map 1200 system; Nihon-Santeku Co., Osaka, Japan). A neuronavigation system (Visor2, ANT neuro, Hengelo, Netherlands) was used for verifying the correct placement of the TMS coil during TMS-EEG recordings.

EEG signals were recorded using a 64-channel electrode cap (EASYCAP, Herrsching, Germany) with TMS-compatible electrodes and an EEG amplifier (Brain Products, Gilching, Germany), at a sampling rate of 5 kHz and a 1350 Hz anti-aliasing filter. FCz and Fpz were used as the reference and ground, respectively. We maintained an impedance of less than 10 kΩ for each electrode. Eye movements were recorded with three additional electrodes placed near the right eye. During the EEG recordings, subjects were instructed to fixate on a cross at the center of the paper in front of them to minimize eye movements. The subjects were acoustically isolated through continuous pink noise provided via noise cancelling earphones (Bose QuietComfort 20, Framingham, MA, USA) and their ears were covered with earplugs. The volume of the noise was adjusted to the level that the subjects could not hear the speech sounds and did not feel uncomfortable.

For tSMS, a cylindrical nickel-plated (Ni–Cu–Ni) NdFeB magnet with a 50 mm diameter and 30 mm thickness (Model N-50; NeoMag, Chiba, Japan) was used6. The maximum energy density was 406 kJ/m3, with a nominal strength of 863 N. The surface magnetic flux density was approximately 5340 G. A non-magnetic stainless-steel cylinder of similar size and appearance to the real magnet was used for the sham stimulation. The magnet and the non-magnetic cylinder were set using an arm-type light stand (C-stand; Avenger, Cassola, Italy) over the representational area for the right FDI muscle identified by TMS (the left M1).

Procedure

Subjects were seated in a comfortable reclining chair throughout the experiment. The optimal TMS coil position for eliciting MEPs in the right FDI muscle was determined as the left M1 where TMS at a slightly suprathreshold intensity consistently provoked the largest MEPs. Following identification of the left M1, resting motor threshold (rMT) was defined as the minimum stimulator output required to elicit an MEP of > 50 µV peak-to-peak amplitude in at least five of 10 consecutive trials35. In a single session of TMS-EEG recording, TMS pulses were applied to the left M1 at an intensity of 100% rMT 120 times. TMS at 100% rMT provoked no or liminal MEP during EEG recording. Therefore, EEG signals were not contaminated by motion artifacts and somatosensory afferent signals from muscle twitches36. Student’s t-test showed no significant difference between the stimulation intensity in the real session and that in the sham session [real, 69.8 ± 10.4% of maximum stimulator output (MSO); sham, 68.1 ± 9.7% of MSO; p = 0.652]. The inter-stimulus interval was set between 4 and 6 s by the pulse control system (Pulse Timer II; Medical Try System, Tokyo, Japan). TMS-EEG at baseline was recorded before tSMS. TMS-EEG at post1 and post2 was recorded immediately and 20 min after the end of tSMS intervention respectively.

EEG data pre-processing

EEG analyses were performed in MATLAB (MathWorks Inc., Natick, MA, USA) using EEGLAB37 and TESA38,39 toolboxes, according to the analysis pipeline40. Bad channels were identified through visual inspection and were removed. Student’s t-test showed no significant difference between the number of the bad channels in the real session and that in the sham session [real, 2.3 ± 1.5; sham, 2.2 ± 1.2; p = 0.758]. Data were epoched around the TMS stimulus (± 1500 ms) and baseline corrected (− 600 to − 10 ms) (relative to the TMS stimulus, the same below). The large amplitude artifact associated with the TMS was then excised by replacing data from − 6 to 15 ms with cubic interpolation. Data were then down-sampled to 1 kHz, and noisy epochs were removed. Student’s t-test showed no significant difference between the number of the analyzed epochs in the real session and that in the sham session (real, 99.8 ± 14.5; sham, 95.8 ± 16.3; p = 0.214). Source-estimate-utilizing noise-discarding (SOUND) filtering was applied39,41 with replacement of missing electrodes and re-reference to a common average reference. A regularization parameter of 0.1 was used and 10 iterations were completed. Following this, an independent component analysis (ICA) was conducted using the FastICA algorithm42, and large components representing the tail of the TMS-associated artifact were removed. Data were then band-stop filtered (48–52 Hz) before conducting a second round of ICA. Second ICA was conducted, and the components associated with blinks, eye movements, muscle activities, and other artifacts were removed. The resulting data were band-pass filtered (0.01–100 Hz), baseline corrected (− 1000 to − 10 ms) and re-epoched (± 1000 ms) to remove boundary effects.

Since later responses may be contaminated by sensory-evoked responses and auditory-evoked responses14, we investigated early responses (between 26 and 100 ms after TMS).

TEP

TEPs were calculated by averaging the EEG signals over the pre-processed epochs. For TEP in the left SM1, TEPs were averaged across FC1, FC3, FC5, C1, C3, C5, CP1, CP3, and CP5. For TEP in the right SM1, TEPs were averaged across FC2, FC4, FC6, C2, C4, C6, CP2, CP4, and CP6 (Fig. 5). The spatial resolution of EEG is no better than 5 cm43. We set the region of interest (ROI) on the nine electrodes around the M1 of the hand, which is usually localized between FC3 and C3 or between FC4 and C445. The four early peaks were identified in the TEPs in the left SM1: P30, N45, P55, and N10013 (Fig. 1a). The negative early peak (N60) was identified in the TEPs in the right SM1 (Fig. 1b).

A non-parametric cluster-based permutation test45 was conducted for the analysis of TEPs. Cluster-based permutation tests offer a data-driven approach to identify the difference among EEG waveforms without prior assumptions for the analysis time window and are well suited to correct the problem of multiple comparisons45,46. T-values were calculated between the pairs (baseline vs. post1 and baseline vs. post2) of the TEPs in the left SM1 and those in the right SM1 for each timepoint, and 1000 permutations for each timepoint were performed to create a distribution of the t-values for each pair of the TEPs in the left SM1 and those in the right SM1. If the original t-value was not between the 2.5th percentile and the 97.5th percentile of the distribution, the timepoint was considered as significant. The adjacent significant timepoints was identified as a cluster. The cluster including the largest number of adjacent timepoints was considered as a significant cluster.

IOR

TEP was subtracted from each trial. By applying the band-pass filters of 4–8, 8–12, and 12–30 Hz to each of the subtracted trial, the theta, alpha, and beta components were obtained respectively. A finite impulse response (FIR) filter (fir1 function in MATLAB) was used for the bandpass filter. A Hilbert transformation was applied to the filtered signals. The power was z scored for each trial according to baseline (− 1000 to − 10 ms) and averaged through the trials. The z scored power was averaged across FC1, FC3, FC5, C1, C3, C5, CP1, CP3, and CP5 for the left SM1, and across FC2, FC4, FC6, C2, C4, C6, CP2, CP4, and CP6 for the right SM1 (Fig. 5. The ROIs for IORs analyses were same as those for TEPs analyses). Then, the mean power was averaged from 26 to 100 ms (Fig. 2a–c).

Two-way repeated-measures ANOVA with Time (baseline, post1, and post2) and Intervention (real and sham) was performed using SPSS (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The Greenhouse–Geisser method was used to adjust for sphericity if needed. P values < 0.05 were considered statistically significant. In case of significant effects, post hoc analyses were performed with paired-samples t-test with a Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. The corrected p values were reported.
