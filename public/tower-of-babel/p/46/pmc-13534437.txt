Ridge catalysis unlocking water oxidation activity of pentlandite
Authors: Lulu Zhang, Ju Rong, Yunxiang Lin, Yongqiang Yang, Xiaohua Yu, Dehui Deng, Hui-Ming Cheng, Gang Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The ridge at the intersection of two adjacent non-equivalent facets of single crystalline particles holds great potential to harmonize catalytic activity and durability. However, conventional facet engineering usually yields crystals with sparse ridges, impeding ridge catalysis. Here, we implement a programmed growth strategy via a decomposable evaporative eutectic system, enabling alternating BFDH-driven <422> -preferential growth and Wulff-guided <111> -preferential growth in pentlandite to produce dense ridges between {111} and {200} facets. The facet-dependent surface reconstruction induces the selective formation of NiOOH and (Fe,Ni)OOH on the {111} and {200} facets, respectively, to create highly active NiOOH/(Fe,Ni)OOH interfacial sites precisely at their ridges, which overcomes the Fe/Ni-equivalent homogeneity constraints of pentlandite to unlock its activities in oxygen evolution reaction (186 mV at 10 mA cm−2 and >1000 h stability at 100 mA cm−2). In an anion-exchange membrane water electrolyzer, such pentlandite microcrystals deliver the current density of 1.0 A cm−2 at 1.95 V with 160 h stability at 500 mA cm−2. This work establishes ridge engineering as a paradigm for stable, high-activity catalysts.

Introduction

Facet engineering, particularly through selective exposure of specific facets and edges, has long been pivotal for optimizing catalytic activity1–4. Specifically, conventional edge catalysis relying on undercoordinated sites to boost reactant adsorption succeeds in simple reactions like hydrogen evolution reaction5–7, precisely because they undergo minimal surface reconstruction. However, for sluggish reactions under harsh conditions (e.g., oxygen evolution reaction (OER))8,9, pronounced surface reconstruction masks these edge sites via amorphous overlayers, while structural relaxation further degrades their geometric distinctiveness10–12, thus fundamentally limiting edge catalysis in sluggish, reconstruction-prone systems.

To address this, we propose ridge catalysis, a distinct paradigm exploiting interactions between adjacent non-equivalent facets at their intersection. Unlike the edges between equivalent facets, which are rendered inactive by isotropic reconstruction (Fig. 1a), the facet-dependent reconstruction across the ridges between adjacent non-equivalent facets could create the atomic-scale heterogeneity of flexible oxidation states and optimal coordination environments (Fig. 1b), enabling dynamic electronic interactions with reaction intermediates to facilitate multi-step reactions13–16. Crucially, unlike traditional heterointerfaces that suffer from reconstruction-induced metal leaching and phase segregation during prolonged operation11,17–19, the single-crystalline nature of the ridges features intrinsic stability. Yet, implementing the ridge catalysis in electrocatalysis poses inherent challenges, for the conventional facet engineering favors flat facets over step-terraces with rich ridges and the controlled synthesis of such ridge-rich crystals remains unexplored2,20,21. In principle, crystal growth is primarily governed by two factors, namely intrinsic crystallographic characteristics and the surface energies of distinct facets. Correspondingly, the underlying growth mechanisms as well as the facet engineering strategies are formalized as Bravais-Friedel-Donnay-Harker (BFDH) law and Wulff’s rule2,21,22, respectively. BFDH law indicates that crystals grow preferentially perpendicular to facets with the smallest interplanar spacings (dhkl) and the largest center-to-face distance (D(hkl)), thereby exposing facets with the largest dhkl values and smallest D(hkl)23. Wulff’s rule states that growth occurs perpendicular to facets with the highest surface energies, thus favoring the exposure of facets with the lowest surface energies2. Therefore, in current facet engineering methodology, a given growth environment can only yield crystals exhibiting the exposure of defined facets and sparse ridges, thus impeding the realization and development of ridge catalysis.

Moreover, to exclude confounding factors like inter-facet metal segregation that amplify the interfacial effects, rigorous validation of ridge catalysis mandates a model material with structural stability and compositional homogeneity. Pentlandite (Fe9Ni9S16, Pn-FeNiS), due to its intrinsic electronic homogeneity originating from sulfur-bridged Fe/Ni equivalency in bulk structure24,25, serves as an exemplary model material. Notably, while Fe-Ni based materials are usually expected to exhibit prominent activities in OER26–29, a critical process for sustainable energy applications30–32, previous reports from different groups have confirmed the modest OER performance of pentlandite Fe9Ni9S16 itself without further modification33–35. It was speculated that the Fe/Ni-equivalent homogeneity in Pn-FeNiS constrains itself from generating the heterogeneity to meet the requirements of OER processes involving multiple intermediates (*OH, *O, *OOH) and concomitant structural/compositional evolution of electrocatalysts under operational conditions14,36,37. To this end, constructing ridge-rich Pn-FeNiS crystals represents an optimal strategy to validate the potential of ridge catalysis in unlocking its OER activity while retaining stability.

Here, we implement a programmed growth strategy via a decomposable evaporative eutectic system, enabling alternating BFDH-driven <422>-preferential growth and Wulff-guided <111>-preferential growth in Pn-FeNiS, and yielding Pn-FeNiS microcrystals with abundant ridges formed in {200}/{111} step-terraces (Pn-FeNiS-S/T). Operando and theoretical investigations reveal facet-dependent reconstruction by forming NiOOH on {111} facets and (Fe,Ni)OOH on {200} facets, respectively, creating highly active NiOOH/(Fe,Ni)OOH interfacial sites precisely at the ridges. This overcomes the Fe/Ni-equivalent homogeneity constraints and unlocks the OER activities of pentlandite. In conventional three-electrode configurations for powder catalyst assessment, Pn-FeNiS-S/T microcrystals achieve the OER current density of 10 mA cm−2 at 186 mV overpotential and over 1000 h stability at 100 mA cm−2. In anion-exchange membrane electrolyzers (AEMWEs), Pn-FeNiS-S/T anodes deliver the current density of 1.0 A cm−2 at 1.95 V and sustain 500 mA cm−2 for 160 h, demonstrating industrial viability while validating our crystal growth engineering for realizing ridge catalysis within monolithic crystals.

Results and discussion

Initiating crystal engineering for Pn-FeNiS with dense ridges

In principle, the BFDH law governs crystal growth through intrinsic crystallographic characteristics, where the facets with thinner slab dimensions exhibit faster growth rates along specific crystallographic directions, ultimately favoring exposure of facets with minimal slab thickness. Conversely, Wulff’s rule dictates crystal growth based on surface energy minimization, where the facets with higher surface energies demonstrate faster growth rates under given environmental conditions. In fact, crystal facet engineering strategies have predominantly leveraged Wulff’s rule to override BFDH-determined growth tendencies by introducing specific ions that adsorb onto target facets, thereby reducing their surface energies and growth rates3. This approach has enabled selective exposure of dominant facets in well-defined crystals for photocatalytic, electrocatalytic, and thermochemical applications2,3,38. However, developing crystals with ridge architectures necessitates concurrent adherence to both BFDH law and Wulff’s rule during their growth, requiring fundamentally distinct growth rate hierarchies under the two competing mechanisms.

To initiate crystal growth engineering in Pn-FeNiS, our strategy starts with analysis on its intrinsic crystallinity-dependent growth rates governed by BFDH law and surface energy modulation through foreign anion adsorption as per Wulff’s rule. As shown in Supplementary Table 1, BFDH analysis of the intrinsic lattice structure of Pn-FeNiS identifies the results of {111} slabs with minimal growth distances (maximal thickness) and {422} slabs with maximal growth distances (minimal thickness) (Fig. 1c, upper and Supplementary Table 2). Based on BFDH law, preferential growth along <422> and dominant {111} facet exposure would form tetrakaidekahedral crystals with 78% {111} and 22% {200} facet coverage (Fig. 1d, Ⅰ, Ⅱ). On the other hand, with Cl− covering the surfaces, {111} and {200} facets exhibit the maximum and minimum surface energies, respectively, in which the preferential growth along <111> and predominant {200} exposure would form hexagonal pyramids based on Wulff’s rule (Fig. 1c, lower; Fig. 1d, Ⅲ, and Supplementary Table 2). Thus, the distinct growth hierarchies under BFDH law and Wulff’s rule create the thermodynamic basis for alternating growth mode controls.

To realize alternating BFDH/Wulff-guided growth, we developed an eutectic-mediated synthesis involving thermal treatment (600 °C) of FeCl3·6H2O, NiCl2·6H2O, and thiourea (CS(NH2)2) in inert atmosphere, yielding Pn-FeNiS microcrystals with abundant ridges on step-terraces (Pn-FeNiS-S/T). Non-equivalent facets across the ridges on step-terraced Pn-FeNiS crystals

The Pn-FeNiS-S/T displays a high density of periodic nanoscale step-terraces on its surface. The long-range order of these step-terrace structures gives rise to an average equivalent facet defined by two crystallographic facet vectors. Since the widths of the {111} and {200} facets within the step-terrace are very close, this average facet can be obtained by vector addition of their normal vectors, corresponding to the [211] direction and the (422) facet (Fig. 2a). In the XRD pattern (Fig. 2b), the enhanced (422) diffraction peak in Pn-FeNiS-S/T provides direct evidence for such a long-range order step-terrace structure. For comparative analysis, conventional Pn-FeNiS (Pn-FeNiS-RS) was synthesized via high-temperature (> 1000 °C) solid-state reaction of elemental Fe, Ni, and S powders, mimicking natural pentlandite formation24. All the XRD peaks for Pn-FeNiS-S/T and Pn-FeNiS-RS matched the reference patterns for pentlandite (Fe9Ni9S16, PDF #01-075-2024). Inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry confirms Pn-FeNiS-S/T and Pn-FeNiS-RS possess nearly identical Fe/Ni molar ratios (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Importantly, although [−422], [2–42], and [22–4] are also perpendicular to the (111) facet (Supplementary Fig. 2), the Pn-FeNiS-RS—dominated by (111) facets from complete cleavage along (111) (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4)—exhibits negligible (422) diffraction, further validating the direct correlation between the (422) diffraction peak and the characteristic step-terrace structure in the Pn-FeNiS-S/T. Furthermore, high-angle annular dark-field aberration-corrected scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) images acquired along the [−101] (Fig. 2d) and [01–1] (Fig. 2e) zone axes provided front and side views of the ridge structures, which were formed by exposure of highly crystalline {111} and {200} facets on opposite sides of the nano-sized step-terraces, with an interfacial angle of 125°. In contrast, observations of fractured Pn-FeNiS-RS crystals revealed irregular morphologies lacking ridge features, with a predominant exposure of {111} facets (Supplementary Fig. 3)—consistent with geological data indicating that pentlandite exhibits perfect {111} cleavage. Furthermore, HAADF-STEM analysis of Pn-FeNiS-S/T explicitly excluded the presence of edge or screw dislocations near the ridge regions.

Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping from the HAADF-STEM image (Fig. 2f) demonstrated uniform Fe, Ni, and S distribution in Pn-FeNiS-S/T, confirming the absence of elemental segregation in crystals formed via Cl−-containing eutectic liquid-phase nucleation. Additionally, considering the sulfide nature of Pn-FeNiS-S/T, the step-terrace structures at an acceleration voltage of 80 kV were characterized to minimize potential electron beam irradiation effects (Supplementary Fig. 6), and the transmission electron microscope (TEM) results further confirm that the step terraces consist of the {200} and {111} facets. The consistent match between the crystal structure model, HAADF-STEM image, and EDS maps firmly validates the facet assignment for the step-terrace structure. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 7) revealed comparable Fe and Ni chemical states in both Pn-FeNiS-S/T and Pn-FeNiS-RS24,39, with additional oxide signals attributable to surface oxidation upon air exposure.

Additionally, a series of control experiments—including synthesis temperature variations (500–1000 °C) and substitution of Fe/Ni chlorides with Fe/Ni acetates or sulfates as precursors—was also conducted to validate the essential role of Cl− in achieving both phase-pure Fe9Ni9S16 crystals and preferential <422> oriented growth for Pn-FeNiS-S/T formation (Supplementary Notes 1 and 2 and Figs. 8–11).

Unlocked electrochemical activities via ridge catalysis

To probe the potential of Pn-FeNiS-S/T in OER induced by ridge structures and uncover the origin of Pn-FeNiS-RS’s OER inertness, we performed operando Raman spectroscopy on both Pn-FeNiS-S/T and Pn-FeNiS-RS across a potential range of 1.40–1.60 V (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 12). The characteristic NiIII-O vibrational modes at 476 and 556 cm−1, corresponding to (Fe-doped) Ni oxyhydroxides40,41, were employed as spectral markers for active phase formation. For Pn-FeNiS-RS, detectable NiIII-O signals emerged only after 1 h of polarization at 1.48 V, indicating sluggish surface reconstruction. In striking contrast, Pn-FeNiS-S/T exhibited accelerated surface transformation: NiIII-O vibrations appeared at 1.44 V without prolonged polarization, indicating that Pn-FeNiS-S/T is more prone to undergoing activation compared to Pn-FeNiS-RS. The OER inertness of Pn-FeNiS-RS was further corroborated by operando X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) analysis (Supplementary Fig. 13), which revealed unappreciable changes in Fe K-edge and Ni K-edge spectra during OER operation (Fig. 3b, upper). By sharp contrast, the operando Fe and Ni K-edge XANES spectra for Pn-FeNiS-S/T exhibited progressive positive energy shifts from 1.3 to 1.5 V vs RHE (Fig. 3b, lower), confirming the dynamic conversion of Fe/Ni sulfides to active Fe/Ni oxyhydroxide species42,43. Notably, electrolyte-induced edge energy shifts suggest interfacial phenomena–potentially arising from catalyst-electrolyte electrostatic interactions or OH− adsorption44. Collectively, these operando observations establish that ridge structures enabled pentlandite activation through the preferential stabilization of active Fe-doped Ni oxyhydroxide ((Fe,Ni)OOH) at low potentials. Furthermore, the operando electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) was carried out to evaluate the electron transfer dynamics under working conditions (Fig. 3c, Supplementary Figs. 14 and 15, and Table 5). One phase peak could be identified at the low-frequency ranges in Bode plots of Pn-FeNiS-S/T and Pn-FeNiS-RS, which mainly reflected their surface oxidation reactions45,46. Combined with the corresponding Nyquist plot and fitted resistance parameters, the Pn-FeNiS-S/T provided lower charge-transfer resistance than Pn-FeNiS-RS, indicating its facile charge-transfer process and thus the easier activation and OER processes triggered by the ridge structure compared to Pn-FeNiS-RS.

The critical role of ridge structures in Pn-FeNiS-S/T in enabling its OER performance was systematically evaluated (Supplementary Fig. 14). As shown by iR-corrected linear sweep voltammetry (LSV) on glassy carbon electrodes (Fig. 3d), Pn-FeNiS-S/T achieves current densities of 10, 100, and 200 mA cm−2 at overpotentials of 186, 248, and 288 mV–significantly outperforming both Pn-FeNiS-RS (276 mV at 10 mA cm−2) and commercial RuO2. The reproducible LSV tests of Pn-FeNiS-S/T and Pn-FeNiS-RS and the potential error bars measured at different current densities further verify the difference in their OER catalytic activity (Supplementary Figs. 16 and 17). At 1.52 V vs RHE, Pn-FeNiS-S/T delivers a current density of 204 mA cm−2, 13-fold greater than that of Pn-FeNiS-RS (15.5 mA cm−2). Tafel analysis and Nyquist plots further demonstrate accelerated reaction kinetics and reduced charge-transfer resistance for Pn-FeNiS-S/T relative to Pn-FeNiS-RS (Supplementary Figs. 18 and 19). While the higher double-layer capacitance (Cdl = 3.80 vs 2.03 mF cm−2) suggests increased reactive site density in Pn-FeNiS-S/T, its ECSA-normalized current density confirms enhanced intrinsic activity compared to Pn-FeNiS-RS (Supplementary Figs. 20 and 21). Control experiments with FeNiS-AC without defined facet exposure further validate the essential role of ridge structures in OER catalysis (Supplementary Note 3 and Fig. 22). Moreover, the near-100% Faradaic efficiency for O2 evolution, confirmed by quantitative measurements of generated O2 and the exclusion of peroxide formation (Supplementary Fig. 23), verifies that Pn-FeNiS-S/T serves as a highly efficient electrocatalyst for OER.

Long-term stability is a critical metric for practical OER applications. When loaded onto carbon paper, Pn-FeNiS-S/T demonstrated electrochemical stability during continuous OER operation, maintaining 100 mA cm−2 for over 1000 h without degradation (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Fig. 24). In stark contrast, Pn-FeNiS-RS exhibited rapid degradation under identical testing conditions. Comprehensive comparison with a broad range of literature demonstrates that Pn-FeNiS-S/T with ridge catalysis exhibits competitive OER activity and stability (Supplementary Fig. 25 and Supplementary Table 6).

To assess the industrial viability of Pn-FeNiS-S/T in water splitting systems, we constructed an anion-exchange membrane water electrolyzer (AEMWE) with Pn-FeNiS-S/T as the anode and commercial Pt/C as the cathode (Supplementary Fig. 26). Current–voltage (I–V) characterization reveals that the electrolyzer achieves the current density of 1.0 A cm−2 at 1.96 V (Fig. 3f). High-frequency resistance (HFR) measurements indicated consistently low ohmic resistance over a range of current densities47,48. Following HFR correction, the cell voltage needed to attain the current density of 1.0 A cm−2 was reduced to 1.905 V (Supplementary Fig. 27). Notably, during prolonged operation at 60 °C and 0.5 A cm−2 for 160 h, the system maintained stable performance with a degradation rate below 1.6 mV h−1 (Fig. 3g). While this work centers on the controlled crystal growth and the underlying catalytic mechanism, a comparison between Pn-FeNiS-S/T-based AEMWEs and recently reported Fe/Ni-based AEMWEs was still conducted, as presented in Supplementary Table 7. This comparison verifies the competitive performance of Pn-FeNiS-S/T when employed as the anodic catalyst in AEMWEs.

Facet-dependent reconstruction across the ridges

The in-situ Fe and Ni K-edge X-ray absorption fine structure (XAFS) spectra acquired during OER at high oxidative potential of 1.57 V over 1–24 h provided critical insights into the facet-dependent formation of Fe-Ni hydroxides on {111} and {200} surfaces. In Pn-FeNiS-RS, Ni K-edge XANES spectra exhibited a distinct positive edge shift after 24 h OER operation at 1.57 V (Fig. 4a and Supplementary Fig. 28), confirming the inevitable oxidation of Ni to form NiOOH43,44. Fourier-transform extended X-ray absorption fine structure (FT-EXAFS) analysis of post-Pn-FeNiS-RS revealed Ni-O (~ 1.7 Å) and Ni-M (M = Ni/Fe, ~2.7 Å) coordination shells (Supplementary Table 8), characteristic of formed NiOOH species. Notably, the results of the Ni K-edge XANES shifts or the characteristic FT-EXAFS Ni-O/Ni-M signals were observed only after the initial 3 h of OER operation. Conversely, Fe K-edge XANES spectra displayed inconspicuous Fe oxidation within the initial 5 h period. Notably, after 24 h, the Fe K-edge spectrum underwent severe distortion, rendering EXAFS analysis unreliable. This phenomenon suggests near-total depletion of Fe from the catalyst surface layer (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 29, and Supplementary Table 9). Therefore, the complementary Fe/Ni XAFS analyses demonstrate Fe dissolution from Pn-FeNiS-RS, ultimately yielding NiOOH as the sole stable OER-active phase. This mechanistic understanding accounts for the intrinsic OER inertness of unmodified pentlandite Fe/Ni sulfides. Combined with the identification of inherent {111} cleavage planes through microscopic analysis (Supplementary Figs. 3 and 4), which confirmed predominant {111} facet exposure in Pn-FeNiS-RS, these results unequivocally demonstrate that pentlandite’s {111} facets serve as exclusive stabilization sites for surface-bound NiOOH species during OER.Fig. 4Facet-dependent (Fe,Ni)OOH/NiOOH formation across the ridges.XANES spectra and the fitting curves of k3-weighted FT-EXAFS spectra tested after 1, 3, 5, and 24 h at the potential of 1.57 V (vs RHE) for Ni K-edge and Fe K-edge of (a, b) Pn-FeNiS-RS and (c, d) Pn-FeNiS-S/T, respectively. e Schematic illustration of the facet-dependent reconstruction in Pn-FeNiS-S/T during OER. In contrast, for Pn-FeNiS-S/T, the Ni K-edge XANES spectra exhibited distinct positive shifts in rising-edge positions within a short timeframe. Concurrently, the oxidation state of Ni increased progressively during OER, confirming efficient reconstruction of nickel oxyhydroxide species (Fig. 4c and Supplementary Fig. 30). FT-EXAFS spectra of the Ni K-edge revealed unambiguous Ni-O (~ 1.7 Å) and Ni-M (M = Ni/Fe, ~2.7 Å) bond signatures after 1 h OER at 1.57 V. Coordination number evolution further evidenced the fast nickel oxyhydroxide accumulation in Pn-FeNiS-S/T (Supplementary Table 8). Analogous behavior was observed in Fe K-edge XANES spectra, where rising-edge positions progressively shifted toward higher energies throughout OER operation (1−24 h, Fig. 4d). Notably, Fe K-edge FT-EXAFS spectra displayed early emergence of Fe-O (~ 1.7 Å) and Fe-M (M = Fe/Ni, ~2.7 Å) coordination environments within the first hour, with sustained high coordination numbers after 24 h OER at 1.57 V (Supplementary Table 9 and Supplementary Fig. 31). These observations confirm the stability and catalytic relevance of iron oxyhydroxide species on Pn-FeNiS-S/T during sustained OER operation. The combined retention of Ni and/or Fe oxyhydroxides and ridge architecture enables facet-dependent stabilization: (Fe,Ni)OOH on {200} facets and NiOOH on {111} facets (Fig. 4e). Furthermore, the changes in the coordination environment revealed by the k3-weighted EXAFS signal of Ni/Fe K-edge of Pn-FeNiS-S/T and Pn-FeNiS-RS further validate the facet-specific reconstruction in the {200}/{111} step-terraces (Supplementary Figs. 32–37). This spatially resolved reconstruction mechanism rationalizes the OER performance of Pn-FeNiS-S/T.

Moreover, the synchrotron radiation high-energy X-ray powder diffraction (SR-XRD), highly sensitive to tiny sample amounts, was employed to further track the evolution of the powder diffraction patterns of pristine Pn-FeNiS-S/T, and Pn-FeNiS-S/T after OER for 2 h, as presented in Supplementary Fig. 38. Given the strong correlation between the (422) peak and the long-range periodic step-terrace structure, the preferential attenuation of the (422) peak during OER, along with the absence of new diffraction peaks, confirms the simultaneously anisotropic reconstruction of the {111} and {200} facets and the formation of highly active sites on the ridges. Besides, the morphology and elemental distribution of Pn-FeNiS-S/T after 1000 h of OER validated the anisotropic reconstruction of (Fe,Ni)OOH on {200} facets and NiOOH on {111} facets across ridges can be preserved and remain catalytically active (Supplementary Fig. 39).

The active sites precisely on the ridges

In pentlandite Pn-FeNiS, Fe and Ni atoms exhibit sixfold coordination within the close-packed {111} facets, while adopting partial four-coordinate geometry on the {200} facets. This structural anisotropy establishes the foundation for the non-equivalent reconstruction behavior demonstrated in our experimental findings. The interface binding energy (Einterface binding) was employed to describe the thermodynamic tendency of interface formation under ideal theoretical conditions. It is defined as the total energy decrease upon the formation of the relaxed MOOH (M = Fe/Ni) on Pn-FeNiS slab relative to the total energy of relaxed isolated MOOH and isolated Pn-FeNiS prior to interfacial interaction. Density functional theory (DFT) simulations revealed that the (Fe,Ni)OOH on Pn-FeNiS exposed {200} facet (Pn200) demonstrated a lower interface binding energy of −32.7 eV versus −25.81 eV for NiOOH on Pn200, while the NiOOH on Pn-FeNiS exposed {111} facet (Pn111) exhibited lower interface binding energy (−55.3 eV) compared to the (Fe,Ni)OOH on Pn111 (-46.08 eV), validating the facet-dependent reconstruction of (Fe,Ni)OOH on Pn200 and NiOOH on Pn111, respectively (Fig. 5a, b and Supplementary Table 10). These computational results aligned with experimental X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) data, which rationalized the exclusive NiOOH as the ultimate active species of {111}-dominated Pn-FeNiS-RS for the OER. As for the Pn-FeNiS-S/T, the coexistence of stabilized (Fe,Ni)OOH derived from Pn200 ((Fe,Ni)OOH@Pn200) and NiOOH derived from Pn111 (NiOOH@Pn111) phases will result in interfacial synergy across ridge ((Fe,Ni)OOH@Pn200|NiOOH@Pn111) under operational OER conditions. DFT-optimized structures reveal atomically resolved configurations of the thermodynamically stable (Fe,Ni)OOH@Pn200|NiOOH@Pn111 structure, as visualized in the cross-sectional view of Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 40. The statistical bond lengths in this structure are presented in Supplementary Fig. 41. OER involves four elementary steps with multiple oxygenated intermediates, demanding active electrocatalysts to mediate each intermediate reaction efficiently. The formation of O–O bond presents particular challenges, with *OOH generation universally recognized as the rate-determining step (RDS)42,49. To pinpoint the active sites in Pn-FeNiS-S/T, adsorption energetics of intermediates were systematically evaluated across three configurations: NiOOH@Pn111, (Fe,Ni)OOH@Pn200, and (Fe,Ni)OOH@Pn200|NiOOH@Pn111, based on the adsorbate evolution mechanism (AEM), for that the lattice oxygen mechanism could be excluded in Pn-FeNiS-S/T (Supplementary Fig. 42). The calculated free-energy landscape reveals distinct RDS characteristics: NiOOH@Pn111and (Fe,Ni)OOH@Pn200 displayed RDS energy barriers of 3.05 and 2.89 eV, respectively, while (Fe,Ni)OOH@Pn200|NiOOH@Pn111 demonstrated a substantially reduced *OOH formation barrier of 1.80 eV, leaving the deprotonation process as the RDS with a low energy barrier of 2.35 eV (Fig. 5d and Supplementary Table 11). DFT calculations reveal that the oxygen species adsorb at the interfacial Ni atom, with the schematic OER pathways for this active site provided in Supplementary Figs. 43 and 44. Spin-polarized d-orbital PDOS for active Ni sites revealed similar spin polarization but an upshifted d-band center with higher Fermi-level electron density in (Fe,Ni)OOH@Pn200|NiOOH@Pn111, thus promoting OER intermediate adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 45). These computational results establish (Fe,Ni)OOH@Pn200|NiOOH@Pn111 as the dominant active centers in Pn-FeNiS-S/T, contrasting with the NiOOH@Pn111 active sites in Pn-FeNiS-RS. This mechanistic understanding explains the enhanced OER performance of Pn-FeNiS-S/T relative to Pn-FeNiS-RS. Moreover, theoretical investigation on the reconstruction behavior of virtual {111}|{111} and {200}|{200} edges, together with their four-step OER reaction barriers, clearly demonstrates that edge geometry alone is insufficient to fundamentally boost OER performance (Supplementary Figs. 46 and 47). This further validates the crucial role of anisotropic reconstruction at the {111}/{200} ridge in achieving OER performance.

With combined experimental and theoretical insights, the ridge architecture comprising {200}/{111} step-terraces promotes facet-dependent formation of (Fe,Ni)OOH and NiOOH, respectively. This architecture generates numerous (Fe,Ni)OOH@Pn200|NiOOH@Pn111 interfacial sites, which drive efficient OER catalysis (Fig. 5e). Therefore, the abundance of ridges in Pn-FeNiS-S/T enables OER performance through the creation of highly efficient and stable active sites.

Discussion

In this work, we address the fundamental challenge of catalytic activity with structural stability through ridge-engineered single-crystal architectures. Leveraging facet-dependent reconstruction behaviors, we develop a crystal engineering paradigm creating synergistic active sites on the ridges between {111} and {200} facets in pentlandite (Fe9Ni9S16) microcrystals. This architecture ensures structural integrity through bulk homogeneity while enhancing activity via ridge catalysis within step-terrace configurations. Multimodal characterization combining operando spectroscopy and DFT simulations reveals facet-specific reconstruction dynamics, in which {111} facets stabilize NiOOH domains while {200} facets host (Fe,Ni)OOH species, their interfacial synergy significantly lowering the OER energy barrier compared to isolated facets. In particulate electrocatalyst evaluations under oxygen evolution conditions, the ridge-rich pentlandite system demonstrates competitive performance among conventional Ni/Fe-based catalysts in both activity metrics and operational stability. The ridge catalysis mechanism, enabled by programmable facet interactions within monolithic crystals, establishes a designed framework for advanced electrocatalysts, with broad applicability to multi-step catalytic processes requiring stability-activity balance.

Methods

Chemicals

Iron chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O) (analytical reagent, ≥99.0%), nickel chloride hexahydrate (NiCl2·6H2O) (Analytical reagent, ≥98.0%), thiourea (CS(NH2)2) (analytical reagent, ≥99.0%), potassium hydroxide (KOH) (Analytical reagent, ≥85.0%) were purchased from Sinopharm Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd. Ruthenium dioxide (RuO2) (≥ 99.9%) and Nafion®117 solution (5%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Potassium titanium oxide oxalate dihydrate (K2TiO(C2O4)2·2H2O) (Analaytical reagent, ≥90.0%) and carbon black (XC-72) were purchased from Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Co., Ltd. All chemicals were used without further purification. Deionized water was used in all experiments.

To prepare the electrolyte used for operando/in situ tests and performance measurements, 11.222 g of KOH was dissolved in 200 mL of deionized water to obtain 1.0 M KOH. The pH value of the 1.0 M KOH electrolyte was measured to be 13.997 ± 0.006 (mean ± SD, n = 3) via a Mettler Toledo FiveEasy Plus pH benchtop meter, FE28. In all cases, the electrolyte was freshly prepared and used immediately, and no long-term storage was involved.

Catalysts synthesis

The synthesis of the Pn-FeNiS-S/T. Firstly, 0.05 m mol NiCl2·6H2O, 0.05 mmol FeCl3·6H2O, and 0.65 mmol thiourea (CS(NH2)2) were added into 10 mL of water. After being stirred for 30 min, the solution was freeze-dried for 24 h. Then, the mixture was ground for over 30 min. Next, the powder was heated at 600 °C for 120 min in Ar atmosphere and cooled down to room temperature naturally. Finally, the Pn-FeNiS-S/T product was collected.

The Pn-FeNiS-RS was synthesized according to the previous report24. In short, the high-purity elements Fe (1.67 g, 29.8 mmol), Ni (1.75 g, 29.8 mmol), and S (1.70 g, 53.1 mmol) were ground and annealed at 700 and 1100 °C. After cooling down to room temperature, the Pn-FeNiS-RS could be obtained.

Material characterizations

A Rigaku diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.54056 Å) was used to obtain the conventional XRD patterns of all the samples. The scanning electron microscopy tests were conducted on a Nova Nano SEM 430. The aberration-corrected HAADF-STEM images, HR-TEM(high-resolution transmission electron microscope), and EDS mapping images were collected using FEI Tecnai-F20 and JEM-ARM200F. XPS spectra were obtained at a Thermo Escalab 250 XI spectrometer with a monochromatic Al Kα X-ray source and all the binding energies were calibrated according to C1s peak (284.6 eV) arising from adventitious carbon. Inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectroscopy analysis was conducted on an iCAP-6000. Raman spectra were collected by LabSpec 5 Raman spectrometer with 532 nm laser.

XAFS measurements were conducted in Shanghai Synchrotron Radiation Facility (SSRF) at the beamline BL11B and Beijing Synchrotron Radiation Facility (BSRF) at the beamline 1W1B. High-energy synchrotron radiation X-ray powder diffraction (SR-XRD) was conducted in SSRF at the beamline BL14B1, with X-rays of 18 keV (λ = 0.6887 Å). The XAS data were analyzed by ATHENA and ARTEMIS software50. k3-weighted EXAFS data were used to obtain the WT-EXAFS spectra by using Matlab code51. The Morlet wavelet was used as mother function with a sigma and Kappa value of 1.0 and 5.0 for all spectra at the k-range of 2–12 Å−1.

Electrochemical performance measurements

The operando/in situ XAS measurements

MEA fabrication and AEMWE evaluation

For the anode, the prepared Pn-FeNiS-S/T catalysts were mixed with the ionomer (PiperION-A5-HCO3) in an aqueous solution (water: isopropanol = 1:3, v/v). BFDH calculations on pentlandite crystals

The crystal morphology of pentlandite was predicted using the Bravais-Friedel-Donnay-Harker (BFDH) algorithm implemented in Mercury55, a state-of-the-art crystallographic visualization suite. As a facet-prediction methodology, the BFDH approach enables identification of dominant crystallographic facets that govern surface-related properties23. Subsequent surface energy calculations were performed to validate the thermodynamic stability of the predicted facets, with particular emphasis on their potential exposure in structural configurations.

Calculations on the surface energy and reconstruction behaviors

In addition, a 20 Å vacuum layer along the z-direction was set up to eliminate periodic mirror interactions. And the convergence criteria for the energy and force of each atom are set to 10−6 eV and 0.02 eV/Å, respectively.
