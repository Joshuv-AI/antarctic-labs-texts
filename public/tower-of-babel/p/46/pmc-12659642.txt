Association between Intracerebral Artery Stenosis and Cortical Infarcts, Lacunes, White Matter Hyperintensities and Brain Atrophy: Results from a Population-Based Study
Authors: Liv-Hege Johnsen, Marit Herder, Torgil Riise Vangberg, Jørgen Gjernes Isaksen, Ellisiv B. Mathiesen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionIntracranial artery stenosis (ICAS) is a significant contributor to cortical infarcts, and studies have also shown associations between ICAS and structural brain changes, such as lacunes, white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), and brain atrophy. We aimed to assess the associations between ICAS and cortical infarcts, lacunes, WMHs, and brain parenchymal fraction (BPF) in a general population.MethodsWe included 1,842 participants (981 women and 861 men) aged 40–84 years who participated in a cross-sectional population-based study. Three-dimensional time-of-flight magnetic resonance angiography was used for the assessment of ICAS, while 3D T2-fluid-attenuated inversion recovery sequences, 3D T1-weighted sequences, and susceptibility-weighted images were used for evaluation of brain structure. ICAS was defined as a ≥50% focal narrowing of the intracranial arterial flow diameter. WMHs were assessed by the Fazekas score and automated volumetric measurements. BPF was used as a proxy for brain atrophy.ResultsThe 111 participants (6.0%) with ICAS had significantly more cortical infarcts (23.4% vs. 5.5%, p < 0.001) and lacunes (27.0% vs. 7.7%, p < 0.001) compared to participants without ICAS (1,731). Multivariable-adjusted analysis showed an independent association between ICAS and cortical infarcts (OR: 2.23, 95% CI: 1.27 to 3.90, p = 0.005) and lacunes (OR 2.05, 95% CI: 1.24 to 3.41, p = 0.005), mainly driven by lacunes in the posterior circulation (OR: 4.76, 95% CI: 1.75 to 12.97, p = 0.002). ICAS was independently associated with moderate-to-severe periventricular Fazekas score (OR 2.04, 95% CI: 1.26 to 3.28) and BPF (β-coefficient −0.0131, 95% CI: −0.0232 to −0.0030). There was no significant association between ICAS and deep moderate-to-severe Fazekas score or WMH volumes.ConclusionICAS was associated with both cortical infarcts in the anterior and posterior circulation and with lacunes in the posterior circulation. The results also indicate a relationship between ICAS and periventricular WMHs and brain atrophy.

Introduction

Intracranial atherosclerotic stenosis (ICAS) is an important cause of ischemic stroke, affecting 5–10% of Western populations and up to 25–50% of Asian populations [1]. Symptomatic ICAS is associated with high risk of stroke recurrence and mortality [2, 3]. The most common stroke pattern is cortical and/or subcortical infarcts within the territory supplied by a single stenotic artery [4].

ICAS and intracranial artery calcification is also associated with lacunar strokes and white matter hyperintensities (WMHs), conditions predominantly attributed to small vessel disease [4–10]. The relationship between ICAS and brain atrophy has been less extensively studied. ICAS was associated with brain atrophy in a population-based study [5], while another study found no association between symptomatic ICAS burden and total brain volume [11]. The aim of this study was to investigate the relationship between the presence of ICAS and cortical infarcts, lacunes, WMHs, and brain parenchymal fraction (BPF) as a proxy for whole brain atrophy in the general population.

Methods

We included 1,842 men and women aged 40–84 years who took part in the MRI examination of the 7th survey of the Tromsø Study, a population health study to which all residents in the municipality of Tromsø, Norway, aged 40 years and above were invited [12]. Participants were scanned with a 3 Tesla Siemens Skyra MR scanner (Siemens Healthcare, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 64-channel head coil. 3D-TOF-MRA images were acquired with a 3D transversal fast low-angle shot sequence with flow compensation, as described in detail previously [13]. 3D-FLAIR images were acquired with a 3D turbo spin-echo sequence with variable flip angle and 3D-T1-weighted images were acquired with a 3D magnetization prepared rapid acquisition gradient-echo sequence. Visual rating of images was done by two experienced neuroradiologists. ICAS was defined as a ≥50% focal narrowing of the intracranial arterial flow diameter assessed on 3D-TOF-MRA [13]. Stenosis location was defined as the petrocavernous and the supraclinoid segments of the internal carotid artery, the A1/A2 segments of the anterior cerebral artery, the M1/M2 segments of the middle cerebral artery, the intracranial vertebral arteries, the basilar artery, and the P1/P2 segments of the posterior cerebral arteries.

Brain infarcts were assessed according to the STRIVE-2 criteria [14]. Cortical infarcts were defined as hyperintense lesions >4 mm on FLAIR images located in the cerebral cortex with or without loss of tissue or extension into the deep white matter and registered according to vascular territory. A lacune was defined as a round or ovoid subcortical, fluid-filled cavity up to 15 mm in diameter. We assessed WMHs of presumed vascular origin on FLAIR images with Fazekas four-point scale [15]. Automated segmentation of WMHs was done with a fully convolutional neural network (U-Net architecture) algorithm [16]. Segmented WMH was divided into deep WMHs and periventricular WMHs based on their proximity to the ventricles using a 10 mm threshold [17].

FreeSurfer (v.6.0, surfer.nmr.mgh.harvard.edu) was utilized to estimate intracranial volume (ICV) and brain parenchymal volume (BPV) from the T1-weighted images. BPF, a proxy of whole brain atrophy, was defined as the ratio between BPV and ICV [18].

The participants underwent measurements of blood pressure, height, weight, and blood samples, while information on smoking status, alcohol use, medication use, and cardiovascular disease history were obtained through questionnaires. Ultrasound examinations of both carotid arteries were performed to assess atherosclerotic plaque. Diabetes was defined as HbA1c ≥6.5% and/or self-reported diabetes and/or use of diabetes medication. Hypertension was defined as a mean systolic blood pressure ≥140 mm Hg and/or mean diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mm Hg and/or use of blood pressure-lowering medication and/or self-reported hypertension. Hyperlipidemia was defined as serum total cholesterol ≥7 mmol/L and/or serum LDL ≥5 mmol/L and/or use of cholesterol-lowering medication.

Statistical analyses were conducted with Stata for Mac (version 17: StataCorp LP, TX, USA). Continuous variables are presented as means with standard deviations or as medians with interquartile range, depending on the distribution. Categorical variables are presented as counts (percentages). Differences between participants with and without ICAS were compared by two-sample t tests for continuous variables and chi square for categorical data. BPF and WMH volumes were log-transformed to approximate normal distribution. Cohen’s kappa (κ) for interobserver agreement on Fazekas grading was 0.80 (SE 0.02).

Logistic regression models were fitted to calculate the odds ratio (OR) for moderate-to-high Fazekas score and presence of lacunes or cortical infarcts and linear regression to assess the association between ICAS and WMH volumes and BPF, in unadjusted models (model 1), adjusted for age and sex (model 2), and adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, current smoking, diabetes, frequency of alcohol consumption, atrial fibrillation, and presence of carotid plaque (model 3). As only four participants had subcortical infarcts larger than 15 mm, the association with ICAS could not be analyzed due to insufficient power. Subgroup analyses were conducted to explore the association between ICAS and lacunar/cortical infarcts in different vascular territories and regions. Two-sided p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Participants with ICAS (n = 111) were older and the proportion of men was higher than in participants without ICAS (Table 1). The ICAS groups also had higher proportions of participants with hypertension, diabetes mellitus, cardiovascular disease, use of lipid-lowering and antihypertensive medication.

Cortical infarcts were more frequent in participants with ICAS compared to those without ICAS (23.4% vs. 5.5%, p < 0.001, Table 2). This was most pronounced in the anterior circulation. In the multivariable-adjusted analysis, there was an independent association between ICAS and presence of cortical infarcts (OR: 2.23, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.27 to 3.90, p = 0.005, Table 3, model 3). The proportion of cortical infarcts in the vascular territory of a stenosis was higher compared to the proportion of all cortical infarcts in those without ICAS, both in the anterior (14.5% vs. 3.6%, p < 0.01) and posterior circulation (16.7% vs. 3.4%, p < 0.01). An independent association between ICAS and cortical infarcts within the vascular territory of the stenosis was found both in the anterior (OR: 2.52, 95% CI: 1.26 to 5.06, p = 0.009) and the posterior circulation (OR: 2.74, 95% CI: 1.02 to 7.34, p = 0.045) (Fig. 1).

The proportion of participants with lacunes was higher in participants with ICAS compared to those without ICAS (27.0% vs. 7.7%, p < 0.001, Table 2), most pronounced in the posterior circulation. As shown in Table 3, there was an independent association between ICAS and the presence of lacunes (OR: 2.05, 95% CI: 1.24 to 3.41, p = 0.005). This was primarily driven by lacunes in the posterior circulation (OR: 4.76, 95% CI: 1.75 to 12.97, p = 0.002) (shown in Fig. 1). There was no independent association between ICAS and lacunes in the anterior circulation (OR: 1.43, 95% CI: 0.77 to 2.66, p = 0.3).

The proportion with moderate-to-severe Fazekas score (≥2) was higher in the ICAS group than in the non-ICAS group, evident in both deep (41.4% vs. 22.0%) and periventricular (67.6% vs. 29.8%) WMHs. The volumes of total, deep, and periventricular WMHs were significantly larger in the ICAS group (Table 2). ICAS was associated with moderate-to-severe periventricular WM Fazekas score (OR: 2.04, 95% CI: 1.26 to 3.28, p = 0.003) (Table 3, model 3) but not with deep WM Fazekas score (OR: 1.12, 95% CI: 0.72 to 1.76, p = 0.6) nor periventricular WMHs volume (β-coefficient: 0.155, 95% CI: −0.052 to 0.362, p = 0.14).

Participants with ICAS had lower BPF compared to those without ICAS (0.65 vs. 0.69, p < 0.001, Table 2). ICAS was independently associated with lower BPF (β-coefficient: −0.0131, 95% CI: −0.0232 to 0.003, p = 0.011) (Table 3).

Discussion

As expected, ICAS was independently associated with the presence of cortical infarcts, in line with previous studies that have shown consistent associations between with cortical infarcts and large artery atherosclerosis [1–4]. We also identified an independent association between the presence of ICAS and lacunes, which in general is a considered to be a feature of small vessel disease. However, independent associations between ICAS and lacunes have been shown in previous studies on Asian populations [5, 6]. The Warfarin-Aspirin Symptomatic Intracranial Disease (WASID) study reported that 15% of the ischemic strokes in the anterior and 36% in the posterior circulation were of perforator pattern origin, defined as a subcortical infarct in the distribution of perforating vessels originating at the site of a stenosis [4]. In our study, the association was primarily driven by lacunes in the posterior circulation, whereas no independent association was found between ICAS and anterior circulation lacunes.

We found that the proportion with moderate-to-severe Fazekas scores in the deep and periventricular white matter was higher in the ICAS group than in the non-ICAS group and that moderate-to-severe periventricular Fazekas score was independently associated with ICAS, while no significant association was found between ICAS and deep moderate-to-severe Fazekas score or with WMH volumes. The Chinese Shunyi Study found an association between ICAS and WMH [6], and similar associations was shown between intracranial arterial calcification and WMH volume in the Rotterdam Study [7]. The PRECISE Study from China showed an independent association between intracranial atherosclerotic burden and modified WMH burden, but not with WMH burden [5]. Previous studies have shown that Fazekas scores largely correspond with distinct ranges of WMH volume [19, 20]. However, Fazekas scoring, being qualitative, evaluates features such as bridging between lesions and confluency of the WMHs, which may not necessarily correlate to the quantitative measurement of WMH volumes.

We found an independent association between ICAS and lower BPF, as a proxy of brain atrophy. The Shunyi Study also reported an association between ICAS and general brain atrophy assessed as BPF [6] and larger calcification volumes were linked to smaller white matter volumes in the Rotterdam study [21]. Clinical studies have shown diverging results. A previous study on 65 patients with acute stroke-like symptoms showed that high grade of calcifications in the cavernous carotid artery was independently associated with central brain atrophy but not cortical atrophy [22]. Conversely, the SMART-MR Study on 130 patients with symptomatic atherosclerotic disease did not observe any association between burden of vessel wall lesions and brain atrophy [14]. Brain atrophy is a common finding in patients with atherosclerotic disease [23]. However, the precise underlying causes driving the progression of brain atrophy remain largely unknown.

Strengths and Limitations

The main strengths of our study are the population-based design, with a relatively large number of participants. However, we cannot exclude that selection bias may have occurred due to lower participation among the elderly and among individuals with certain medical conditions, such as stroke, cognitive impairment, and conditions limiting participation in an MRI study. This could have led to underestimation of the true prevalence of both ICAS and structural brain changes. Study participants were predominantly White Europeans, and the results may thus be limited to these similar populations and ethnic groups. The cross-sectional design does not allow for causal inferences.

Differentiation between anterior and posterior circulation stenosis and lesions within their respective vascular territories, together with the application of the newly revised STRIVE-2 criteria to assess lacunes, are strengths of this study. However, accurate assessment of lacunes can be challenging and require thorough evaluation as perfect differentiation of lacunes from perivascular spaces is not always possible based on size or T2-hyperintense rim alone [14]. Assessing the exact vascular territory in the border zones is also challenging. Ideally, T2-weighted sequences would have been better for evaluation of enlarged perivascular spaces. T1-weighted images allowed for some degree of differentiation between lacunes in WMHs and enlarged perivascular spaces. However, there is a possibility that lacunes have been misinterpreted as perivascular space and vice versa and that both lacunes and cortical infarcts may have been inaccurately placed within vascular territories. Finally, since the number of participants with structural changes was limited, this may have increased probability of both type I and type II errors.

Conclusions

In this population-based study, we found that ICAS was associated with cortical infarcts in both the anterior and posterior circulation and with lacunes in the posterior circulation. ICAS was also associated with periventricular WMHs assessed by Fazekas score and a lower BPF, indicating a relationship between ICAS and periventricular WMHs and brain atrophy.
