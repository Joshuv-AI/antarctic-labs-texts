Neonate with hepatorenal sequelae and multi-organ failure following massive intrapartum haemorrhage
Authors: Blgeis Elgadra, Mohammad A A Bayoumi, Einas Elzubier Elmalik
Published: 2025

Description

The patient was born to a primigravida mother in her late 20s. The pregnancy was complicated by type IV placenta previa, first detected at 24 weeks of gestation. There was no clinical antepartum haemorrhage at any point. Maternal blood pressure and glucose were normal throughout. At 33 weeks and 5 days of gestation, the mother presented to the emergency department with labour pain. Uterine contractions were confirmed on cardiotocography (CTG). A trial of tocolytics failed to suppress labour. She received a single dose of betamethasone (12 mg), 9 hours before delivery.

Before delivery, the mother’s haemoglobin level was 11.2 g/dL with a normal mean corpuscular volume and mean corpuscular haemoglobin, a slightly low mean corpuscular haemoglobin concentration of 30.8 g/dL and a high red cell distribution width of 17%. Platelet counts and the coagulation profile were normal. Her blood group was Group A, Rh positive, with no detected antibodies. There was no suspicion of infection aside from the onset of preterm labour itself. Group B Streptococcus screening was not performed, but the antenatal congenital infection screen was unremarkable. The parents were non-consanguineous, and the family history was unremarkable.

The patient was delivered via emergency caesarean section with combined spinal-epidural anaesthesia. The uterine incision traversed the placenta before delivery of the fetal head, requiring forceps assistance due to difficulty with extraction. The delivery was complicated by a massive intrapartum haemorrhage with an estimated maternal blood loss of 3000 mL. The mother received two units of blood intraoperatively.

The male neonate had APGAR scores of 7 and 9 at 1 and 5 min, respectively. The cord pH was 7.27, with a base excess of +1.8. Birth weight was 2690 g at birth (96th percentile for gestational age and sex). The neonate looked pale and exhibited a weak cry initially, which improved on stimulation during resuscitation. Due to recurrent apnoeic episodes, positive pressure ventilation was initiated, and the patient was intubated at 13 min of age and transferred to the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU). Intramuscular vitamin K was administered in the delivery room as per hospital protocol.

Birth hospital NICU course

In the NICU, the infant remained intubated and ventilated with a fractional inspired oxygen up to 0.6. Prophylactic antibiotics for early-onset sepsis were started. IV dextrose 10% water (D10%) was administered at 80 mL/kg/day; however, due to recurrent significant hypoglycaemia, both the rate and concentration of dextrose fluids were increased, necessitating the insertion of a central line (umbilical venous catheter (UVC) for higher concentration infusions.

The initial complete blood count (CBC) at 1 hour of age revealed marked normochromic normocytic anaemia for age (haemoglobin 11.1 g/dL, red blood cell count was 3.0×10ˆ6 /µL). Platelet count was adequate at this point. The patient’s blood group was A, Rh positive, with no antibodies. The glucose-6-phosphate dehydrogenase screen was normal. A packed red blood cell (PRBC) transfusion of 10 mL/kg was initiated.

Early blood gases showed mild respiratory acidosis with sodium of 137 mmol/L and potassium of 5.5 mmol/L; however, later on, progressive hyponatraemia and hyperkalaemia developed (which began before the blood transfusion), with sodium of 130 mmol/L and potassium of 8.1 mmol/L at 6 hours of age. By this time, only 5 mL of urine had been produced. At 9 hours of age, sodium dropped to 127 mmol/L and potassium rose to 9.1 mmol/L. At 13 hours of age, creatinine was 93 µmol/L (normal 21–75 µmol/L), and a concurrent blood gas showed the development of metabolic acidosis in addition to the pre-existing respiratory acidosis; pH 7.05, PCO2 66 mmHg, Na mmol/L 123, K 10.3 mmol/L, Ca 0.93 mmol/L, lactate 9.9 mmol/L, haemoglobin 9.2 g/dL (after transfusion), bilirubin 33 µmol/L, base excess −11.9 and bicarbonate 14.5 mmol/L.

Due to persistent anuria and hyperkalaemia, an ECG was obtained showing peaked T waves, a wide QRS complex and prolonged QT (figure 1). Hyperkalaemia management was maximised. Although congenital adrenal hyperplasia was deemed unlikely, hydrocortisone stress dosing was started after endocrinology consultation. Sepsis was also considered, and antibiotics were adjusted to non-nephrotoxic agents. Furosemide boluses failed to stimulate urine output. Despite sodium supplementation and potassium-lowering therapies (calcium gluconate, insulin infusion with dextrose, salbutamol, sodium bicarbonate, potassium-binding resin and furosemide), electrolyte disturbances persisted.

The infant’s respiratory status deteriorated with tachycardia and tachypnoea in addition to worsening hypercarbia and hypoxia. High-frequency oscillation ventilation and surfactant therapy were initiated. An echocardiogram showed a small patent ductus arteriosus, a patent foramen ovale and flattening of the interventricular septum during systole, with moderate pulmonary hypertension (mild tricuspid regurgitation with ventricular systolic pressure 38 mmHg, while systemic blood pressure was 50 mm Hg). Despite minimal sedation, the patient remained hypoactive.

Haemoglobin declined to 6.8 g/dL, prompting another PRBC transfusion (15 mL/kg). At 30 hours of age, bleeding was noted from the UVC site and the orogastric tube. Coagulation studies showed an elevated activated partial thromboplastin time of 109.9 s. A further PRBC transfusion (20 mL/kg), fresh frozen plasma and vitamin K were administered. Ultrasounds revealed echogenic kidneys and heterogeneous liver and splenic parenchyma, but no internal bleeding. Urinary catheterisation confirmed ongoing anuria. Repeated CBC showed persistence of anaemia (7.9 g/dL) and the development of thrombocytopenia (20×10ˆ3 /µL). A Kleihauer–Betke test was requested, and it was positive. Metabolic and haemodynamic management continued with broad supportive therapy.

Due to refractory acidosis, refractory hyperkalaemia, renal dysfunction and multi-organ failure, the patient was transferred for dialysis.

Transfer hospital NICU course

Peritoneal dialysis (PD) was initiated; however, the catheter was suboptimal, and renal function worsened. The PD catheter was discontinued, and continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) was initiated, leading to normalisation of renal function tests. During PD catheter replacement, a small intestine perforation was discovered and repaired. PD resumed, and creatinine/blood urea nitrogen (BUN) stabilised. Urine output commenced. MRI of the kidneys showed cortical renal signal abnormalities. Serial kidney ultrasounds revealed progressive parenchymal damage, abnormal echogenic kidneys with loss of corticomedullary differentiation, cystic degeneration and reduced renal volumes (figure 2). Recurrent urinary tract infections prompted prophylactic antibiotics. With stable creatinine, BUN, electrolytes and urine output, dialysis was discontinued.

Immediately before the transfer, hepatic transaminitis developed and aspartate aminotransferase peaked at approximately 1500 IU/L, followed by the development of conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia and coagulopathy. While liver enzymes and coagulation parameters decreased with time, hyperbilirubinaemia was persistent and progressive. Eventually, ascites developed and progressed to be massive, as coagulopathy set in again. Liver imaging initially revealed a coarse, heterogeneous liver (figure 3), but ultimately showed evolving fibrosis. Diuretics and therapeutic paracentesis were required. A liver biopsy confirmed fibrosis with foci of necrosis and inflammation, with no features of other underlying disorders. During the investigation of possible causes of liver failure, exocrine pancreatic insufficiency was diagnosed, and replacement therapy was prescribed.

The initial anaemia improved after the first week of life; thrombocytopenia persisted. Erythropoietin was started. Primary causes of bone marrow failure, like haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH), were excluded. Bone marrow suppression was attributed to hypoperfusion or sepsis. Serial ultrasounds showed splenic infarcts, prompting penicillin prophylaxis.

After the initial improvement in renal status, the respiratory status also improved, and the patient was extubated to non-invasive ventilation and later to room air. Repeated echocardiography showed normal cardiac function. Whole-exome sequencing was negative. The brain MRI showed only white matter changes, likely due to prematurity.

With progressive ascites and hepatic failure, the renal function began to worsen again with a gradual decrease in urine output and a rise in creatinine and BUN. The massive ascites compromised breathing; subsequently, re-intubation and mechanical ventilation were required. Coagulopathy progressed to disseminated intravascular coagulation, with bleeding from multiple sites, including the peritoneal cavity. Hyperammonaemia ensued, followed by seizures.

The differential diagnoses of the case included hypoxic ischaemic encephalopathy, multiple congenital anomalies, fulminant septicemia, mitochondrial hepatopathy, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura, congenital dyserythropoietic anaemia type one and adrenal haemorrhage. A detailed history, physical examination and specific lab and radiological results excluded these probabilities.

A multidisciplinary team meeting concluded that survival was unlikely without a liver and kidney transplant, which was not feasible. After discussions with the family, dialysis was not started. A do not attempt resuscitation order was placed, and palliative care commenced. The patient passed away at 110 days of age due to cirrhotic liver failure and end-stage renal disease.

Discussion

The case demonstrates a complex and ultimately fatal course of a preterm neonate complicated by massive intrapartum haemorrhage due to type IV placenta previa. Despite appropriate antenatal care, steroid administration and timely delivery, the birth was difficult, and the neonate developed progressive multi-organ dysfunction, culminating in cirrhotic liver failure and end-stage renal disease. The temporal progression of the clinical course and resistance to management raises several diagnostic and pathophysiological considerations.

Perinatal hypoxic-ischaemic insult

Placenta previa has long been associated with preterm delivery and an increased risk for neonatal mortality and morbidity.1 2 Although the neonate had a reassuring APGAR score and cord pH, the pale appearance, initial poor respiratory effort and later multi-organ dysfunction suggest a hypoxic-ischaemic insult. The massive maternal blood loss (estimated at 3000 mL) could have led to fetomaternal compromise. Additionally, the difficulty in extraction through the placenta and the need for forceps assistance may have exacerbated the stress on the neonate.36

Acute kidney injury and persistent anuria

The early onset of anuria and severe electrolyte disturbances (particularly hyperkalaemia) indicates an acute tubular necrosis pattern, possibly due to perinatal hypoperfusion.5 7 8 Despite supportive care and PD, renal function did not recover initially and required CRRT.9 This progression is consistent with severe ischaemic perfusion injury.10 The later development of cortical abnormalities and cystic changes on imaging further supports irreversible renal parenchymal damage.11

Progressive liver dysfunction

The development of transaminitis, followed by conjugated hyperbilirubinaemia, coagulopathy and progressive fibrosis, is notable. While neonatal liver failure can be caused by infections, metabolic diseases or congenital anomalies,12 13 none were identified despite thorough evaluation, including whole-exome sequencing and liver biopsy. It is possible that the hepatic failure was secondary to ischaemic hepatitis (shock liver), compounded by recurrent sepsis and prolonged total parenteral nutrition-related cholestasis.1214 The rapid fibrosis, however, remains unusual, if not unheard of.12

Bone marrow suppression and haematologic abnormalities

Anaemia, thrombocytopenia and splenic infarcts suggested bone marrow hypoplasia or failure. HLH was ruled out, and no metabolic or genetic cause was identified. Hypoperfusion and systemic inflammation likely played a major role. Splenic infarction may reflect thromboembolic events or severe hypotension and is rarely reported in neonates.1520

In conclusion, this case highlights the potential devastating consequences of massive intrapartum haemorrhage and resultant hypoperfusion-related multi-organ injury in neonates. It emphasises the need for vigilance in the immediate postnatal period, early identification of end-organ damage and a multidisciplinary approach in complex NICU. It also calls to attention the need for further research into the effects of massive fetomaternal haemorrhages on different organ systems, including the spleen and pancreas.

Learning points

Massive intrapartum haemorrhage can cause occult fetal hypovolaemia, even in the presence of reassuring Appearance (skin colour), Pulse (heart rate), Grimace (reflex response), Activity (muscle tone), and Respiration (breathing) scores and cord gases. Attentiveness to early signs of multi-organ dysfunction is crucial.
