Bereavement Groups for Parents Who Have Lost a Child: A Qualitative Study of Parental Experiences in a Danish Context
Authors: Malene Beck, Malene Missel, Heidi Bergenholz, Michael A van Manen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundBereavement support for families experiencing the loss of a child is a crucial aspect of Specialized Paediatric Palliative Care (SPPC). As an offering of SPPC, bereavement groups have the potential to provide meaningful support. Limited research, however, exists considering parents' direct experiences of these groups and considerations for how to approach them in a Scandinavian context.AimTo explore parents' experiences of bereavement support groups to explicate thematic priorities that speak to the design, facilitation, and other practical considerations for delivering these groups in a Scandinavian context.MethodsA qualitative study, inspired by a phenomenological approach, was conducted in a regional Danish hospital‐based bereavement program. Parents who had lost a child and participated in a bereavement group were invited to share their experiences in a focus group and/or individual interview. Thematic analysis methods were utilized to explicate thematic priorities.ResultsThree thematic priorities were articulated for delivering bereavement support groups: (1) to cultivate a sense of belonging; (2) To share and bear memories that sustain presence; and, (3) to support parents make sense of past experiences in the present. Additional specific practical considerations were elucidated.ConclusionsIn a Scandinavian context, hospital‐initiated bereavement groups may aspire to provide a shared space for parents to engage with existential aspects of loss. This study contributes to evolving understandings of best practices for bereavement support in regional paediatric palliative care programs.Keywords: bereavement groups, hospital, human science research, loss, parents, support

Introduction

Losing a child is recognized as one of the most profound and devastating experiences a person can endure. In our region of Denmark, bereavement support is coordinated by a paediatric palliative care team, which follows a model of Specialized Paediatric Palliative Care (SPPC). When possible, support is individualized through existing relationships with the palliative care team sensitive to a family's unique circumstances. Nonetheless, a family may feel isolated in their bereavement. This study is situated in a Danish context, where hospital‐initiated bereavement groups are a new practice that has been implemented to support grieving parents by creating a shared space of understanding and recognition. While the findings are grounded in this specific setting, they speak to broader issues of loss, community, and meaning‐making in the aftermath of child loss.

Existing Knowledge

SPPC is directed to support both a child and their family during illness, death, and bereavement [1]. For example, home‐based SPPC may improve quality of life and symptom management while significantly increasing the likelihood of being able to support palliation, death, and bereavement in the community [2]. As an integrative model of care, SPPC is positioned to respond to the psychological distress of bereavement [3, 4]. Following a child's death, parents generally value the ongoing involvement of the hospital team, which offers a sense of continuity and validation of their loss [5]. However, SPPC services remain underdeveloped in many regions, including nordic countries, where models of care vary widely and educational opportunities for healthcare providers are limited [6].

While bereavement support is widely recognized as a core component of paediatric palliative care, important gaps persist between guidelines and practice [1, 3, 7], Many families describe inadequate emotional and psychological follow‐up after their children's deaths [7, 8]. Systematic reviews show wide variation in the design, content, and facilitation of bereavement interventions for parents, with mixed or uncertain evidence of effectiveness and inconsistent outcome measures complicating evaluation [9, 10, 11].

Bereavement support groups are recommended as part of integrated SPPC, providing opportunities for peer connections and professional guidance [3, 4, 11, 12]. These groups can support contact between bereaved families, but there is limited empirical evidence on how they should be structured and delivered, particularly in Scandinavian contexts where SPPC remains unevenly developed and under‐resourced [1, 2, 6, 13]. While parents generally value the continuity offered by hospital teams [5, 12], there is minimal qualitative research capturing parents' own reflections on the relevance, demands, or perceived benefits of participating in such groups [3, 9, 14].

The existing literature informing the design of bereavement groups in Scandinavian contexts is broad in scope, addressing bereavement as a general phenomenon, while also being directed toward specific populations of bereaved parents. For example, Kreicbergs et al. [15] examined long‐term outcomes among cancer‐bereaved parents and the impact of professional and social support. Lykke et al. [16, 17] studied parents' experiences of anxiety, depression, and communication with healthcare providers following a child's death. Mørk et al. [18] investigated grief trajectories after pregnancy and neonatal loss. Ahmadi and Zandi [19] examined cultural‐based coping strategies in bereaved parents. Røseth et al. [20] explored existential aspects of inclusive bereavement care for bereaved lesbian mothers after perinatal loss. Titlestad et al. [21] documented trauma‐related grief among Norwegian parents bereaved by drug‐related deaths, showing high rates of prolonged grief. A recent Danish study highlighted parental perspectives on the location of death for children with cancer, illustrating the complexity of preferences, symptom management, and healthcare involvement around end‐of‐life care [22].

While the existing body of work underscores the need to understand bereavement experiences within specific healthcare contexts, it does not offer specific considerations for the conduct of bereavement groups. In other words, none of these studies fully examines the design, organization, facilitation, or other aspects involved in delivering bereavement support groups in situations of mixed‐diagnoses, hospital‐initiated bereavement groups for parents who have lost a child due to congenital conditions, chronic illness, and/or unexpected medical events, particularly in regions with large geographic distances and fragmented service provision [2, 3, 6, 13]. This lack of context‐sensitive, practice‐informed evidence limits efforts to develop equitable and sustainable bereavement supports for families regardless of diagnosis, geography, or prior healthcare engagement.

Addressing these gaps is essential to align services with international palliative care recommendations [23], and ensure bereavement care meaningfully responds to the lived realities of families facing the profound challenges that come with a child's death. Our study aims to address this gap by exploring bereaved parents' experiences of participating in hospital‐initiated bereavement support groups within a regional Danish context, in order to inform and improve the design and facilitation of such groups.

Study Design

Our study was inspired by a phenomenological human science research approach [24, 25], whereby we understand bereavement not as a clinical condition to be categorized, but as a profoundly human relational phenomenon. This stance allowed for exploring how parents navigate their loss through relational dynamics, mutual recognition, and shared meaning‐making within a bereavement group setting. We aimed to explicate thematic priorities that pragmatically speak to the design and facilitation of bereavement groups. These themes can be understood to reflect how participants perceived, experienced, and reflected on their involvement in the groups. There is a normative sense to these themes as they express those experiences that participants found helpful, valuable, or otherwise meaningful.

Setting

Guidelines from the Danish Health Autority in Denmark emphasize the importance of structured, accessible, and culturally‐sensitive support [26]. Drawing on these national guidelines, our SPPC unit launched parental bereavement groups in December 2023. In this study, the term “children” refers to individuals under the age of 18 years, in accordance with the definition set out in the United Nations Convention on the Rights of the Child, which is adopted by the World Health Organization. On average, our unit provides care for up to 40 families at any given time, referred from hospital departments or other SPPC units. The majority of cases involve non‐cancer diagnoses, such as neurological or genetic disorders and organ failure, while cancer‐related cases primarily include brain tumours and sarcomas. These diagnostic categories reflect the broader clinical population served by the SPPC unit.

The bereavement group approach adopted by our SPPC unit reflected both sympathy and pragmatism. Parents who had lost a child wished to participate and still had a connection to the unit were invited to join. The groups were, therefore, assembled from the parents residing in the region who could and wished to participate, with the explicit aim of delivering meaningful support while addressing the realities of working with a small, dispersed population. The group was facilitated by members of the SPPC team consisting of a physician, two nurses, a social worker, a psychologist, and a dietitian. Since no evidence‐based guidelines exist for the structure, facilitation, or evaluation of such groups, the groups themselves were organized based on discerning reading of the literature, disciplinary knowledge, and clinical experience.

The meetings of the bereavement group took place in a facility provided by an independent institution, a house designed to support cancer survivors and their families. This setting provided a home‐like atmosphere with private group rooms and access to shared spaces, including a kitchen, lounge, and sofas. Participants and Recruitment

Of six eligible families invited across Region Zealand, all agreed to take part. Two participants had separated from their partner after their child's death; the former partner had no contact with the SPPC team and was, therefore, not included in recruitment. Participants were parents of children who had been referred to a single specialized paediatric palliative care (SPPC) unit due to severe and life‐threatening illness, in accordance with current clinical guidelines. Referral due to severe and life‐threatening illness constituted the primary inclusion criterion for paediatric palliative care involvement in this study. In Denmark, specialized paediatric palliative care is provided by five regional teams; participants in this study were referred to one of these teams. Referrals included both cancer‐related and non‐cancer‐related trajectories. Non‐cancer trajectories comprised congenital conditions, chronic and progressive illnesses, and organ failure, while cancer‐related cases primarily involved brain tumours and sarcomas. Families were referred from hospital departments or via general practitioners.

Parents who had lost a child and participated in a hospital‐facilitated bereavement group organized by the SPPC Team were invited to participate in this study. These parents had partaken together in the same bereavement support group and were, therefore, known to each other prior to the study. Participants were recruited by the palliative care team verbally and/or in writing, approximately 2 months before the group's initiation. Informed consent was obtained from all participants.

Qualitative Material Collection

Qualitative material was collected in two activities based on participant preference:

Focus group: A focus group was scheduled after the last bereavement group meeting, allowing participants to share their experiences and perspectives of participating in the bereavement group. This focus group was prepared and conducted as a separate and distinct activity from the bereavement group to respect participants' voluntariness to participate [27, 28]. Five parents participated in this manner, and the focus group lasted approximately 45 min.

This focus group was prepared and conducted as a separate and distinct activity from the bereavement group to respect participants' voluntariness to participate [27, 28]. Five parents participated in this manner, and the focus group lasted approximately 45 min.

Individual interviews: A phone interview presented an alternative option whereby participants could share their experiences and perspectives in a one‐on‐one fashion [29, 30, 31]. These interviews were scheduled after the final bereavement group session around the participant's availability to minimize disruptions to daily routines, work, and family life. Four parents participated in this fashion, with interviews lasting on average 20–30 min.

These interviews were scheduled after the final bereavement group session around the participant's availability to minimize disruptions to daily routines, work, and family life. Four parents participated in this fashion, with interviews lasting on average 20–30 min.

Both the focus group and individual interviews were conducted as flexible, dialogical conversations rather than through a fixed or systematically applied interview guide. The moderator/interviewer adapted questions continuously in response to participants' contributions and interactions. Across both formats, participants were invited to reflect on their experiences of participating in the bereavement group through open‐ended questions such as: “When you think back on the group meetings, what has your experience been?”, “Can you give an example of something that felt particularly helpful?”, “Were there aspects of participating in the group that you found challenging?”, and “Is there anything you wished had been different?”

Participants were also invited to reflect on their expectations prior to participation and how the group had met these expectations. To support reflective meaning‐making, conversational exercises such as word association (e.g., inviting participants to share three words describing their experience of the group) and sentence‐completion invitations (e.g., “Participating in the group has meant that…”, “A particular moment in the group that stands out to me is…”) were used flexibly. Follow‐up questions emerged naturally in the dialogue to invite elaboration, clarification, or reflection, rather than to direct participants toward predefined categories.

The interviews were relatively brief, reflecting deliberate sensitivity to participants' emotional vulnerability and to avoid overburdening them during discussions of bereavement [14, 32]. Conversations about death and loss demand careful timing and sensitivity to avoid further distress [33]. As Barbour [27] notes, the value of focus groups lies not in their duration but in the quality of interaction and appropriateness of group composition. The focus group and interviews were conducted in Danish and transcribed verbatim.

Analysis and Interpretation

The transcriptions formed the basis for a collaborative analysis within the research team, in which the experiential data were discussed and translated as part of an ongoing process of pre‐briefing, debriefing, and dialogic meetings. This approach supported collective thoughtfulness, wonderings, and reflections to ensure shared understanding and analytical rigour [34]. Consistent with a phenomenologically inspired and reflexive analytic approach, rigour was pursued through dialogic engagement and collective reflection across the research team, rather than through procedural validation techniques.

Thematic analysis methods were engaged to explicate thematic priorities for the design and facilitation of the bereavement groups. Methodologically, the thematic analysis was inductive as themes were drawn from the qualitative material (rather than pre‐existing empirical or theoretical sources) following a period of prolonged engagement with the collected material. The inductive analysis aimed to go beyond the explicit or surface meaning of the qualitative material (semantic meaning) to also explicate implicit or taken‐for‐granted assumptions (latent meaning) [35]. To support understanding of the thematic statements, we developed reflective writing from the gathered material and included excerpts of the qualitative material. Pragmatic considerations for bereavement support group design and facilitation were also drawn from the qualitative material.

Consistent with our analytic approach, we privileged patterns of shared meaning across participants' words rather than attributing themes to individual cases. Quotations are, therefore, presented as illustrative of the analytic themes, rather than as direct representations of a single participant's unique positions. This approach supports an understanding of bereavement experiences as socially and relationally constructed, highlighting the collective dimensions of meaning‐making that inform group facilitation practices [35]. We recognize the need to balance analytic transparency with our ethical obligation to protect participant confidentiality [36]. Theme development was grounded in systematic pre‐ and post‐briefings connected to each bereavement group meeting, where we shared observations, discussed emerging patterns, and critically examined assumptions together. These sessions supported interpretations to be shaped collaboratively. The team's diverse clinical and research backgrounds in palliative care and rehabilitation, with members both inside and outside the region, supported reflexive awareness of our positions and how these influence interpretations.

Ethical Considerations

In accordance with Danish legislation, non‐biomedical studies must obtain approval from the Research Ethics Committee. Although this project did not require formal approval, the committee was consulted, and they confirmed the ethical compliance of the study. The project is registered in the regional university hospital system (p‐2024‐16572) as per established procedures. Transcribed materials were securely stored adhering to the General Data Protection Regulation guidelines, as required by the region's regulations. Excerpts of qualitative material have been anonymized to protect the anonymity of participants. We recognized the potential power imbalance involved in recruiting bereaved parents [14, 32, 33] and, therefore, sought to mitigate these risks by prioritizing clear, respectful communication, voluntariness, and making participation nonburdensome. In addition, participation was designed to minimize cognitive and emotional burden. Interview duration and facilitation were therefore kept flexible and relatively short, in line with ethical guidance for research involving bereaved and emotionally vulnerable participants [32, 37].

The bereavement support groups were structured to ethically to create a welcoming atmosphere by greeting parents on arrival and clearly displaying the session plan and timings. An external home‐like venue with a kitchen, sofas and a small garden to support informal breaks and comfort was used. Flexible session arrangements were adapted to families' needs, including times after 4 p.m. to accommodate two parents' work schedules. The location had parking facilities. Childcare was provided in a nearby room to reduce practical barriers to participation. Bereavement support groups also emphasized sensitive facilitation by repeating key information, allowing pauses, and explicitly confirming individual's right, but not obligation, to speak. These considerations are essential in a palliative care setting with bereaved parents, given their emotional vulnerability, the risk of perceived obligation in relationships, and the need to minimize additional practical or emotional burdens [14]. This approach aligns with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki [38], particularly the emphasis on safeguarding participants' well‐being and maintaining a clear and independent researcher‐participant relationship.

Results

Participants Characteristics

The following section provides an aggregated overview of participant characteristics to contextualize the subsequent analysis.

Nine parents (five women and four men) represented families of six children aged 15 months to 16 years at the time of death. Causes of death included congenital conditions, chronic illnesses, and unexpected death due to undiagnosed medical conditions. Time since death ranged from 8 months to 4 years. All parents were biological parents. For all, it was their first child who had died; several had additional children afterwards. All participants had Danish ethnic backgrounds and spoke Danish. The group included parents with cultural ties to Christianity (including non‐practicing or non‐believing), as well as parents who described themselves as spiritual. Participants lived in both urban and rural areas within the region, with the largest city in the region having 53,000 inhabitants. For six parents, distance to the meeting location meant they had to travel more than 30–45 min by car to attend. Most families had chosen to remain living in the same home they had occupied when their child had passed. There was representation from manual trades, academic professions, and care‐related occupations. Marital status also varied, with some parents separated or divorced (yet without new partners) and others living as couples.

The circumstances surrounding the children's deaths and families' engagement with the healthcare system varied. Some parents had provided care for their child at home and interacted with municipal services. Others had longstanding contact with the SPPC team or had been referred to other hospitals for specialized treatment. Few had spent time at a children's hospice.

Three parents had no prior experience with group‐based support; others had previously connected with peers through diagnosis‐specific offerings, such as cancer groups. Three participants had created social media accounts on behalf of their children. For all participants, this was their first time taking part in a regional bereavement support group spanning different diagnoses and involving families across the region.

To protect anonymity, we provide this context in aggregate form, and specific participant characteristics such as age, occupation, and number of children are not reported. Given our small regional context, even limited contextual identifiers risk the anonymity of participants, especially as some have publicly shared parts of their stories. In line with Morse and Coulehan [39], the details provided aim to offer descriptive context while safeguarding confidentiality.

Analysis

When I'm just working or spending time with others, we don't talk about my child's death. I protect myself, which makes everyday life harder because the loss is still there. The challenge is hearing my own story again. It's my own story that I fear the most. I struggle to acknowledge what has happened, even though I know it is necessary. It hurts, but it also helps.

In line with a phenomenological sensitivity inspired by Van Manen [25] and a reflexive thematic analysis approach [35] this introductory account offers an anecdotal overview of the material, intended to orient the reader to the experiential whole before the themes are elaborated.

Bereaved parents found the groups to be a relational space where they could openly share existential dimensions of their loss. This was something they often lacked elsewhere. While parents' daily life and work provided structure and routine, they also functioned as a severance against confronting their loss. Within the group, however, parents could engage with their loss in ways that felt both painful and necessary. This tension between the unpleasant vividness of the loss of a child and the felt need to share the experience was a focal aspect of their parental bereavement group experience. Together, the themes describe how a sense of belonging formed the relational foundation for sharing memories, which in turn supported parents' efforts to make sense of their loss over time. The following themes express priorities for the design and facilitation of the paediatric bereavement groups:

To Cultivate a Sense of Belonging

Sharing the experience with the group has meant that I have been part of a like‐minded community, which in some way creates a sense of security. There's an understanding that you are not alone, that there are others out there you could call if everything collapsed. Because we share things in a confidential space, it makes you feel like you're part of something bigger. We're going to be taken care of. You have many needs met in a few hours.

Being together, with spoken and unspoken words, was described as a valued experience cultivating bonds and created a sense of community, meaning a shared understanding and mutual recognition that did not always require explicit discussion of their loss. Parents described the loss of a child as profoundly exhausting and isolating, marked by a sense of being overlooked in the wider social world, that is, feeling that others failed to acknowledge, understand, or make space for their ongoing grief. Specifically, they highlighted the lack of support for their own needs, including time leading up to and following their child's death. This experience was encapsulated by the term “no‐mindedness” as an expression for their felt perspective that society was indifferent to their struggles. However, their participation in the bereavement groups allowed this sense of isolation to shift toward belonging. Most significantly, they found others who understood their loss intuitively, offering empathy without the need for explanation. This exchange enabled a sense of “like‐mindedness” as a shared understanding and sense of belonging was encountered. Parents emphasized the importance of safety, trust, and acceptance that simply showing up was meaningful, making them feel valued and purposeful.

We have this community in the fact that we have lost. No one knows our loss, what it is we have just under the skin, right next to the heart. Unless they've experienced it themselves. Otherwise, no one has been there. There's a kind of loneliness in it. It's been the most important thing for me to look at them (in the group) and just know that we share something.

Hearing each other's stories related to the loss of a child was a sensitive, emotional experience. While friends and family may sympathize, it is perhaps only another parent who may truly understand the unique isolation that comes from mourning not only a child's absence, but also the future they never had: birthdays that will never be celebrated, milestones that will not be reached, dreams left unfulfilled. Parents explained how it left a significant impression on them to hear others' stories of loosing a child and living with the loss of a child. It was particularly meaningful to hear of these experiences alongside the sharing of their own, and witness how some parents, especially those who had recently lost a child, gradually began to participate more actively in the group with each session. Parents described how they had been touched by seeing other's living with their loss, and how, in its own way, offered a form of hope in an otherwise sadden times. An openness within sharing and the empathic connections within the group created a sense of community, where it was not only loss that was shared, but also the small steps toward reconciling, healing, and acceptance of each other's experiences.

The group has made me more open. Sharing experiences with the group has meant that I no longer feel alone in the world. I've discovered that the world is not black and white, and it was really special, or quite remarkable, when we had to tell our story and show a picture.

To Share and Bear Memories That Sustain Presence

Sharing and bearing meaningful memories allowed the child to remain present in parents' lives, even as social and institutional contexts rendered the loss invisible. Parents spoke of the value of sharing memories of those events that had come to pass (e.g., the events leading up to their children's deaths) and also those memories that had yet to be (e.g., unfulfilled dreams for their children's shortened lives). We may appreciate how the sharing of memories valued not only the lives of their deceased children but also the parents' lives as parents. At the same time, parents reflected how their loss felt overlooked by society.

It was when he died, I needed a helping hand. I was informed by the government, that my child had been removed from the ‘system’. It was painful and a shock, as though my child had never existed, and it was exactly at that point that having these groups would have been invaluable.

Parents experienced a close connection with the SPPC members facilitating the groups, as the staff were familiar with their stories from the SPPC unit, having been there with them during challenging times. This familiarity allowed the staff to support parents recall and rediscover memories during the groups, as well as witness their stories, especially, when these were difficult to tell or blurred by loss. The parents explained how it was important that it was the staff from the SPPC unit who facilitated the groups, as they could proactively ensure that the parents could participate in their own pace, offering the necessary support. The groups not only provided a space for shared memories of their children and conversations about their children, but also offered a welcome witness, at a group level, to their children's existence.

They've (staff) stood by us, holding our hands every step of the way. So, it makes perfect sense that it's them and not strangers leading the groups. They've followed us. Literally. And we trust them. We feel secure with them. There are so many things we don't have to go through first. It's much more straightforward, right from the start. No formalities, no pleasantries. In that way, the groups, led by them, have given me some relief in dealing with my loss. And I would almost say that it's also brought me some joy. Because we've smiled, there have been reunions, and we've laughed.

The humour and lightness that emerged during the group sessions helped to ease the heaviness of loss, providing parents with unexpected moments of relief and connection to each other. The other parents and staff, by being both compassionate and able to inject humour into the conversations, created a space where they could find comfort, even in memories, and where communal laughter became part of the healing process. The very experience of feeling joy and laughing together over, for example, an absurd yet meaningful memory was welcomed by the parents, as they had longed for a space where this was possible, without fearing they would hurt others with their memories or having to explain too much beforehand.

To Make Sense of Past Experiences in the Present

The group has made me remember again and, in some way, grow. I even want to say, feel happy again. It has put things into perspective—my view on sickness, the system, ambulances, and hospitals was deeply connected to my loss. Now, through sharing with you, it feels more bearable. Without this group, it would have only been memories. But through sharing and growing close, it has ended differently.

In the groups, the parents experienced opportunities to revisit and reconsider their understandings of the loss of their children, which they might not have previously acknowledged or allowed themselves to feel. The death of a child was recognized as an event that may shatter the natural order of life, leaving parents, siblings, grandparents, and other family members in unimaginable grief. Parents disclosed how in the groups they were allowed to draw on others to express challenging experiences (e.g., choosing a burial site, feelings of inadequacy for the siblings, and so forth). Sharing unique experiences from the past in the bereavement group helped the parents gain perspective on their experiences of losing their child. More so, these sharing moments also supported opportunities of recognition, “aha moments,” as participating parents made sense of their past and current experiences. The parents highlighted how there were still hidden, unresolved feelings connected to their loss, which, through sharing in the groups, and by telling their stories, were easier to open up about.

I've discovered, within being a part of this group, that I've actually come to a good place. I can talk about August in another way here, than other places, where I might would have hold back, or wonder how much to say, how much not to say. But here, it's as if there's no filter. So, I've discovered that when I can just talk freely without a filter, it's actually nice, and it makes it easier for me to talk without a filter other places e.g. when calling the municipality, because I've tried it here. I've tried myself out."

Being in a paediatric bereavement group meant being vulnerable and, at times, exposed. This exposure, however, was within an empathic space, in which the parents were safe to engage in telling, experience other ways of knowing, or quite simply to speak about their child as they remembered them. The exposure was described as difficult at times, but nevertheless a necessary activity. Thus, in the group interactions, loss became not just something to process individually, but also something that was collective, shared, and validated through the experiences and testimonies of other participants. This created a sense of community and understanding that was essential for the parents, as it offered a different understanding of themselves, seen in the light of others.

There have been several forgotten ‘things’ that I hadn't thought about before, and the groups have provided insight into how deep my loss actually is. I think that can be an inspiration, to come to a place like this and hear how others actually cope. Like, wow, you can also do that, you can allow yourself to feel that. And then, you also gain strength, maybe from the help of others' stories, to do it. You learn from each other. We inspire each other, without knowing it. Just by telling and listening to each other.

Discussion

Although a child's death is a deeply personal experience, our analysis suggests that bereavement groups may offer a valuable sense of shared understanding and belonging for many parents in a Scandinavian context. From being with others who have experienced the loss of a child, parents may derive mutual support and understanding, to feel seen and heard confronting loss individually and communally [3, 4, 40]. While themes such as belonging and shared remembrance resonate with prior studies of bereavement support groups (e.g., [3, 4]), the present study extends this work by situating these experiences within a regional Scandinavian context and by explicating how they translate into concrete priorities for the design and facilitation of hospital‐initiated bereavement groups.

According to Frank [41], the act of sharing one's story allows for a “bigger” experience of the world, where the boundaries between individuals begin to dissolve as they realize they are not alone in their suffering. Fostering a sense of belonging expresses this orientation. This community of parents provided a space where loss could be collectively shared and, importantly, where the very act of witnessing one another's painful experience could support healing.

The sharing of memories speaks to sharing past experiences and imagined futures. The loss of a child may feel fundamentally unfair; parents, after all, expect to protect their children and watch them grow such that to lose them seems profoundly unjust, leading to a manifold of emotions and reactions (guilt, helplessness, or questioning life's purpose). A sense of being ignored aligns with the notion of “testimonial injustice” [42], where voices and emotional needs are not fully acknowledged, making it difficult to process difficulties within a societal context. The intensity of the loss of a child may leave a parent devastated, and the emotional and physical resources required to care for their dying child may leave little space for addressing their own needs. Lack of recognition by society may create a barrier to understanding and speaking about the loss of a child, and, more broadly, a barrier to democratic conversations about difficult or taboo subjects. Our study speaks to the societal responsibility to support bereaved parents as they live with the loss of their children.

Supporting sense‐making speaks to how connected a parent's identity might be to their deceased child. We may appreciate both acutely and over time that parental bereavement may be accompanied by questioning: Should we have kept going? Who am I now? Did we do everything? What is my role in life? The paediatric bereavement groups provide a shared space for exploring such questions, including those shifts in perspectives that come from living with loss. The relational space of a bereavement group could support parents to navigate their loss in the present moment and reorient their loss through shared experiences. Ahmed's notion of “affective orientation” [43] signals how individuals may emotionally reorient themselves through relationships and interactions. Following, sharing memories in bereavement groups may offer parents an opportunity to reframe their loss, creating a space where it is not only acknowledged but also socially constructed and shared. This may support the telling of a coherent narrative of loss.

Our SPPC unit initially had concerns about forming bereavement groups: What happens when we bring together grieving parents who are vulnerable? How can we address sensitive topics without causing harm? And how do we navigate the complexity of parental grief without complicating it further? These reflections closely resemble the ethical and practical considerations described by October et al. [8] for clinicians supporting bereaved parents. It is important to note that many families had been searching for support before joining the bereavement group, often turning to social media in a Scandinavian context. Psychological safety on informal platforms can be uncertain, as these spaces are often open rather than closed, requiring parents to navigate them carefully [44, 45], reflecting broader gaps in structured bereavement services identified in the literature [11]. Online platforms also generally lack professional, structured support.

Our research highlights professional facilitation as a key consideration for bereavement group design, consistent with evidence that hospital‐based bereavement support can foster trust and meaningful engagement [4, 12]. Parents valued the presence of familiar palliative care staff, describing how this fostered trust, safety, and openness when discussing sensitive topics in a Scandinavian context. Such experiences point to the importance of skilled, interdisciplinary facilitation that can sensitively manage complex grief processes and provide a supportive, ethical space for sharing. Viewed through a caring science lens, these findings may also be understood through the lens of caring science, where relational presence, recognition, and shared meaning are central. In this sense, professionally facilitated bereavement groups may be understood as an expression of transpersonal care, as described by Watson [46], where relational connection extends beyond instrumental support to address the existential and emotional dimensions of loss. This understanding aligns with evidence that structured, professionally led bereavement interventions deliver reliable, compassionate support while containing emotional distress [7, 11, 47, 48].

Studies in paediatric palliative care emphasize the importance of finding a delicate balance in providing support for bereaved parents, as their grief may differ significantly from more general grief experiences [8, 49]. As noted by Lannen et al. [50], paediatric grief often involves complex emotions not only related to the child's death but also to the caregiving experience itself, which makes psychological safety in the group particularly important. Our bereavement group was supported by an interdisciplinary team offering various professional expertise, which has been shown to enhance the emotional and psychological support provided by bereavement groups for parents dealing with the loss of a child [51, 52]. More so, the parents were already familiar with some of the team members, which supported trust and community. Healthcare team members also served as a valuable connection for families who needed individual resources in navigating psychological, physical, and other health needs [12].

Overall, this study speaks to the significance of parental bereavement groups in regional Scandinavian settings. Supporting belonging, shared remembrance, and meaning‐making are ultimately interrelated activities requiring tactful facilitation. By foregrounding how these interrelated processes are shaped through professional, hospital‐based facilitation and long‐term relational continuity, this study offers practice‐relevant insights that complement and nuance existing bereavement literature. These reflections also speak to the importance of adequately resourcing bereavement groups. The process is far from straightforward, and while a bereavement group may offer significant value, we remain aware of the risk of harm if facilitation is not handled with care. While the study foregrounds experiences that parents found meaningful and supportive, it is important to acknowledge that in‐person bereavement groups may also involve challenging or less supportive experiences, which were not prominent in the present material and therefore remain insufficiently explored. Like many other programs, we do not have unlimited resources, and we recognize that the bereavement groups cannot continue indefinitely. However, we acknowledge that the bereavement group informing this study is being maintained, which speaks to its value.

Strength and Limitations

Our study took a flexible, person‐centered approach reflecting the aspirations of the bereavement group itself. Strengths include the team's qualitative expertise and contextual familiarity: three authors work clinically in palliative care and one in rehabilitation, with two researchers based in the region and all engaged in regional healthcare research. It is also important to acknowledge that the groups were facilitated by familiar SPPC staff. Existing research highlights the role of professional support in promoting trust and safety in bereavement support groups [5, 12]. This context may be considered both a strength, in supporting rich and meaningful discussion, and a limitation in terms of transferability to settings where such established relationships do not exist.

In line with the exploratory and phenomenological aim of the study, the analysis focused on how parents made sense of their participation in bereavement support groups and how these experiences informed priorities for group design and facilitation. As such, experiences of dissatisfaction, discomfort, or other potential drawbacks of in‐person bereavement group participation were less prominent in the material. This may reflect the voluntary nature of participation and the fact that parents for whom group participation was experienced as particularly challenging may have opted out or chosen not to participate.

It is worth highlighting that we conducted this study in a region of Denmark characterized by long‐travel distances and well‐documented healthcare inequalities, issues that are prominent on the political agenda [53, 54]. The decision to offer a regional group was shaped by these structural conditions, with the aim of supporting access to bereavement support for families regardless of specific diagnosis or location. While this pragmatic approach enables providing bereavement support in sparsely populated areas, it may limit comparability with settings where groups are organized around single diagnoses or more homogeneous participant backgrounds. Future studies could usefully explore dynamics in different settings, including staff perspectives, to better understand their role in creating and maintaining supportive group environments.

Conclusion

This study explored bereaved parents' experiences and meaning‐making in hospital‐initiated bereavement support groups in a regional Danish setting, to inform their design and facilitation. Importantly, this study does not just affirm that bereavement groups are helpful but illuminated how they enable meaningful sense‐making for parents in a local context with limited formal bereavement support. We hope the identified thematic priorities and additional considerations articulated may serve healthcare providers and bereaved families as they navigate living with the loss of a child. Support from professionals, the chance to see their own experience in others' stories, and the opportunity to make sense of the past in order to speak about their loss may serve as key aspects to living a bereavement life.

Clinically, the findings suggest that paediatric bereavement groups benefit from a clear and predictable structure combined with flexible facilitation that supports parents' readiness to share existential aspects of loss. This highlights the importance of continuous, skilled facilitation by healthcare professionals with expertise in paediatric palliation and bereavement, as well as access to multidisciplinary support. From an educational perspective, the results point to a need for targeted training in group‐based bereavement support, relational and existential dimensions of parental grief, and professional self‐care. Future research should further examine how different organizational formats and facilitation approaches influence parents' experiences over time and across diverse clinical and cultural contexts.
