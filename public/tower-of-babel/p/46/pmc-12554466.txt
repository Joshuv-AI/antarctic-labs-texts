Prognostic difference of single metastasis in different sites of hepatocellular carcinoma: a population-based study
Authors: Yingjie Zhuang, Qing Chang, Wenhui Liu, Shiping Xu, Xiangye Yin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThere is a notable difference in prognosis between single and multiple metastases from hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). We aimed to identify the prognostic differences among HCC patients with single metastasis to different sites.MethodsThe study focused on patients with single metastasis to different organs from HCC from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results database between 2010 and 2015. We used the Kaplan-Meier method to determine overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS). Univariate and multivariate Cox regression analysis, as well as floating absolute risk (FAR), were introduced to compare prognostic differences among the single metastasis. We applied the conditional survival (CS) to calculate the time-varying survival probability.ResultsThe study included 2,197 individuals. In multivariate Cox analysis, single lung metastasis [hazard ratio (HR) =1.293, 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.053–1.587, P=0.01] was linked to worse OS than single intrahepatic metastasis, while single lung metastasis (HR =1.395, 95% CI: 1.103–1.764, P=0.005) and single bone metastasis (HR =1.306, 95% CI: 1.021–1.67, P=0.03) possessed shorter DSS than single liver metastasis. FAR revealed that OS and DSS for single pulmonary metastasis were inferior to those of bone metastasis. In the subgroup analysis, chemotherapy played an interactive role in the comparison of DSS in single bone metastasis vs. liver metastasis group, as well as single lung metastasis vs. liver metastasis group, suggesting that chemotherapy may improve the survival. CS analyses showed a marked improvement in patients with single metastasis over time.ConclusionsThe prognosis of single pulmonary metastasis was worse than that of single bone metastasis and single intrahepatic metastasis. The prognosis of single brain metastasis could not be determined temporarily due to data limitations.

Highlight box

Key findings

• Single lung metastasis emerged as the most prevalent in the single metastatic types of Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), followed by bone. The prognosis for single pulmonary metastasis was less favorable than that of single bone metastasis and single intrahepatic metastasis. The prognosis of single brain metastasis was significantly poor.

What is known and what is new?

• HCC is a primary liver malignancy originating from hepatocytes and ranks as the sixth most prevalent cancer globally. Most HCC patients are diagnosed at advanced stages. Previous studies have shown difference between single and multiple metastases from HCC, however, the difference of single metastasis of HCC to different sites remains unclear.

• Currently, limited research exploring the characteristics of intrahepatic and extrahepatic metastasis in HCC and the specific variations in mortality risk at distinct sites of single metastases remain uncertain. This is the first time that the prognosis of single metastasis of HCC to different organs have been specifically compared.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• In the era of precision medicine, it is essential for decision makers of treatment to evaluate and explore the difference in prognosis of HCC with single metastatic foci. This is the first time that the prognosis of single metastasis of HCC to different organs have been specifically compared. This research offers an in-depth examination of the variations in prognosis among the patients with four single metastatic foci and may contribute to personalized treatment strategies for patients with different metastatic types.

Introduction

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), arising from hepatocytes, is globally ranked as the sixth most widespread type of cancer worldwide and is the third leading cause of cancer-related mortality (1). Annually, there are over 850,000 new instances of liver cancer globally every year, with approximately 90% attributed to HCC (2). Despite the successful introduction of vaccination programs for hepatitis B virus (HBV), the incidence and mortality rates of HCC are increasing, particularly due to the obesity pandemic which leads to non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). It is projected that global HCC mortality will increase by an additional 41% by the year 2040 (3,4). However, most patients are diagnosed at advanced stages because early-stage HCC typically does not present any symptoms (5). Hence, it is imperative for oncologists and hepatologists to recognize the epidemiological characteristics and prognostic determinants for patients with advanced HCC.

Prior studies concentrated on the clinical and tumor characteristics of advanced HCC, including factors such as insurance, race, gender, different Tumor Node Metastasis (TNM) stage at diagnosis, chemotherapy, and surgery (6-8). Currently, limited research exploring the characteristics of intrahepatic and extrahepatic metastasis in HCC, primarily due to the lack of available data on HCC metastasis. A study conducted by Zhan et al. categorized them into single and multiple metastases (9). However, the specific variations in mortality risk at distinct sites of single metastases remain uncertain.

The Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) database is a frequently utilized and comprehensive repository containing detailed clinical data pertaining to cancer patients (10). To address the issue, we selected HCC patients with single metastasis, including bone, brain, liver, and lung metastasis, from SEER database with full clinical information. Based on the Cox proportional hazard model, floating absolute risk (FAR) and conditional survival (CS) rate, we explored the difference of prognosis in the study population. Our research aimed to identify the full range of clinical characteristics and compare the prognostic differences among HCC patients who have single metastasis to different organs, such as lung, bone, liver, or brain. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://tcr.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/tcr-2025-683/rc).

Methods

Database

In our study, individuals were enlisted from SEER covering the period from 2010 to 2015. The SEER database (URL: https://seer.cancer.gov/) represented a reliable and publicly accessible repository of data concerning cancer occurrence, death rates, prevalence, lifetime risk data, and survival rates in the United States. Access to the data was obtained by SEER-Stat software (version 8.3.5). The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments.

Variables

Variables such as age at diagnosis, race, sex, marital status, American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) 8th TNM stage, tumor size, tumor grade, number of lesions, metastasis site, history of chemotherapy, surgery, survival time, overall survival (OS) and disease-specific survival (DSS) have been encoded and available in SEER database. The variable “Number.1” indicates the number of metastatic lesions, while the variable “Is.primary” indicates whether the patient has a prior history of malignant tumors. In terms of OS, any death was classified as an event, while individuals who survived were treated as censored. In the analysis of DSS, deaths resulting from HCC were deemed as events, while deaths due to other causes and survivors were marked as censored. Patients were categorized based on their age at diagnosis into three groups: those not older than 60 years old, those between 61 and 70 years old, and those older than 70 years old. In terms of marital status, patients were categorized into four groups: the married group, the divorced/separated group, the single/unmarried group, and the widowed/others group. To classify the patients based on race, they were categorized into three groups: white, black, and individuals belonging to other racial backgrounds.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

This research utilized data from SEER and specifically focused on patients with HCC. As per the guidelines outlined in the SEER database coding manual, inclusion criteria for this study encompassed patients diagnosed with the Histologic Type, as classified by the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD-O-3) with the code 8170/3, and with the primary tumor site restricted to the liver. Among them, we further selected those with four types of single metastasis, including liver, bone, brain, and lung metastasis. Metastatic status was defined according to the following four variables in the database: Mets.bone (bone metastasis), Mets.brain (brain metastasis), Mets.liver (liver metastasis), and Mets.lung (lung metastasis). The value of each variable was coded as 1 indicating metastasis at the corresponding site, 0 indicating no metastasis, and NA representing missing or unknown data. The following criteria were applied to ensure the inclusion of only patients with single metastasis: single bone metastasis: Mets.bone =1 & Mets.brain =0 & Mets.liver =0 & Mets.lung =0; Single brain metastasis: Mets.brain =1 & Mets.bone =0 & Mets.liver =0 & Mets.lung =0; Single liver metastasis: Mets.liver =1 & Mets.bone =0 & Mets.brain =0 & Mets.lung =0; single lung metastasis: Mets.lung =1 & Mets.bone =0 & Mets.brain =0 & Mets.liver =0. Additionally, patients with distant metastasis in other sites (e.g., lymph nodes or other distant organs) were further excluded based on the variable “Mets.Distant/Other” or relevant fields. Patients who did not have a clear survival prognosis or T0 stage based on AJCC 8th TNM staging system were excluded. Additionally, patients with insufficient information regarding important prognostic factors, including surgery and radiotherapy, were also excluded from the analysis (Figure 1).

Statistical methods

Descriptive statistics were utilized to analyze the baseline characteristics of the included HCC patients. The primary endpoint used for comparison were OS and DSS. Survival estimates of all variables were generated by the Kaplan-Meier approach, and then compared using the log-rank test. The Cox regression model was utilized for univariate and multivariate analysis to assess hazard ratio (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs). For comparisons involving four single metastases groups, 95% CIs were determined using the FAR method. This approach was employed to ensure accurate comparisons between any two groups rather than just the reference group (11). Stratified analyses were also carried out to examine the prognostic differences among the four single metastases within each covariate subgroup, as well as their interaction with each covariate. The concept of “conditional probability of survival” refers to the likelihood of surviving to a specific “Y” years after diagnosis, given that survival to “X” years (where X is less than Y) has already occurred. This allows for detailed descriptions of patient survival at different time stages (12). For our study, we utilized R (version 4.2.2) and International Business Machines Corporation (IBM) Statistical Product and Service Solutions (SPSS) (version 22). Statistical significance was determined by a two-sided P value <0.05.

Results

Baseline information

The research group was comprised of 2,197 individuals, with 1,791 being male (82%) and 406 being female (18%). Upon initial diagnosis, 938 (43%) of the patients presented with bone metastasis, 33 (2%) with brain metastasis, 110 (5%) with liver metastasis, and 1,116 (51%) with lung metastasis. The majority of patients were white race (66%). As for staging system, there were more patients stayed as AJCC T3–T4 status (891, 41%) than T1–T2 (656, 30%). Of all the patients, 82 (4%), 1,032 (47%), and 565 (26%) received surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy, respectively. Survival analysis

In this study, 1,953 deaths (89%) were observed. Summarily, the median OS and DSS of patients with single lung metastasis from HCC may worse than the bone or liver metastasis, but the maximum survival time of brain metastasis is no more than 2 years (P<0.001) (Figure 2). In detail, median OS in lung, bone, liver, and brain metastasis was 3, 5, 4 and 4 months, while median DSS was 3, 6, 6 and 6 months, respectively. As seen in Figures S1-S14, it was routinely observed that female, white race, lower grade, AJCC T status, surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy maybe related to longer OS and DSS. Among them, patients who underwent surgery enjoyed significantly longer OS and DSS. The median DSS time was 15 months (95% CI: 11–24) for patients who underwent surgery while only 4 months (95% CI: 4–5) for patients who did not receive surgery (P<0.001).

Regression analysis

The Cox hazard ratio model evaluated and compared the prognostic differences and related factors in patients with single metastasis to four solid organs from HCC. For univariate analysis, male (HR =1.13, 95% CI: 1.007–1.269, P=0.04), single/unmarried (HR =1.125, 95% CI: 1.004–1.26, P=0.042), AJCC T3–T4 (HR =1.344, 95% CI: 1.21–1.494, P<0.001) were found associated with worse OS (Figure 3A). Meanwhile, male (HR =1.139, 95% CI: 1.002–1.294, P=0.046), single/unmarried (HR =1.133, 95% CI: 1.001–1.283, P=0.049), AJCC T3–T4 (HR =1.405, 95% CI: 1.25–1.579, P<0.001), Grade III–IV/unknown (HR =1.374, 95% CI: 1.208–1.563, P<0.001), were also found associated with worse DSS (Figure 3B). Besides, patients who underwent surgery (OS: HR =0.381, 95% CI: 0.293–0.494, P<0.001; DSS: HR =0.406, 95% CI: 0.306–0.538, P<0.001),chemotherapy (OS: HR =0.612, 95% CI: 0.559–0.669, P<0.001; DSS: HR =0.636, 95% CI: 0.576–0.702, P<0.001), or radiotherapy (OS: HR =0.687, 95% CI: 0.62–0.762, P<0.001; DSS: HR =0.748, 95% CI: 0.669–0.836, P<0.001) exhibited decreased risk of mortality compared to those who did not.

These factors were subjected to additional examination through multivariate Cox analysis. It revealed that surgery was remarkably correlated with improved OS (HR =0.37, 95% CI: 0.284–0.483, P<0.001) and DSS (HR =0.429, 95% CI: 0.323–0.57, P<0.001) independently. Besides, gender, AJCC T status, Grade, tumor number, chemotherapy and radiotherapy were confirmed as independent variables impacting the OS and DSS of HCC patients with single metastatic site.

As for the differences between the four single metastases, lung metastasis was correlated with poorer OS (HR =1.293, 95% CI: 1.053–1.587, P=0.01) and DSS (HR =1.395, 95% CI: 1.103–1.764, P=0.005) than liver metastasis. Moreover, bone metastasis was found to be associated with poorer DSS (HR =1.306, 95% CI: 1.021–1.67, P=0.03) than liver metastasis.

FAR

The FAR method was utilized to determine the adjusted HR and the corresponding 95% CI for OS and DSS within each category, including the reference category. This facilitated easy comparisons to be made between the various categories of metastasis. The adjusted hazard ratios with FAR for OS were 1.168 (95% CI: 1.077–1.267) for bone metastasis, 1.293 (95% CI: 1.207–1.384) for lung metastasis (Figure 4). Similarly, the adjusted hazard ratios for DSS were 1.306 (95% CI: 1.194–1.427) for bone metastases, 1.395 (95% CI: 1.292–1.506) for lung metastasis (Figure 4).

Subgroup analysis between liver and other metastasis

Subsequently, liver metastasis group and other different single metastasis groups were respectively included in subgroup analysis. In the bone metastasis vs. liver metastasis group (n=1,048), patients with single bone metastasis from HCC had longer OS than those with single liver metastasis in those with tumors larger than 5cm in size (HR =0.328, 95% CI: 0.111–0.968, P=0.043) (Figure 5A). In the brain metastasis vs. liver metastasis group (n=143), there was no significant statistical difference in OS among the subgroups (Figure 5B). In the lung metastasis vs. liver metastasis group (n=1,226), patients with lung metastasis from HCC had shorter OS than those with liver metastasis in those with AJCC N stage at N+/unknown (HR =1.391, 95% CI: 1.016–1.903, P=0.04), or those with solitary metastatic lesion (HR =1.269, 95% CI: 1.017–1.583, P=0.04) (Figure 5C).

AJCC, American Joint Committee on Cancer; CI, confidence interval; DSS, disease-specific survival; HR, hazard ratio; N, nodal; OS, overall survival; T, tumor.

Furthermore, patients with single bone metastasis have shorter DSS than single liver metastasis if they did not receive chemotherapy (HR =1.533, 95% CI: 1.070–2.196, P=0.02) in the bone metastasis vs. liver metastasis group (Figure 5D). In the brain metastasis vs. liver metastasis group, there was no significant statistical difference in DSS among the subgroups (Figure5E). In the lung metastasis vs. liver metastasis group, patients with lung metastasis experienced shorter DSS than those with liver metastasis in patients over 70 years old (HR =1.863, 95% CI: 1.100–3.154, P=0.02), female patients (HR =1.835, 95% CI: 1.021–3.297, P=0.042), widowed/others patients (HR =2.087, 95% CI: 1.088–4.002, P=0.03), patients whose tumor size was unknown (HR =1.278, 95% CI: 1.006–1.623, P=0.044), or patients with solitary metastatic lesion (HR =1.316, 95% CI: 1.041–1.664, P=0.02) (Figure 5F).

Moreover, patients who received chemotherapy had longer OS (HR for interaction = 0.645, 95% CI: 0.426–0.974, P for interaction =0.04) and DSS (HR for interaction =0.436, 95% CI: 0.271–0.703, P for interaction <0.001) than those who did not receive chemotherapy (Figure 5A,5D). Similarly, patients received chemotherapy had longer DSS (HR for interaction =0.587, 95% CI: 0.367–0.941, P for interaction =0.03) than patients not received chemotherapy (Figure 5F).

CS rate

Next, our study demonstrated a marked enhancement in the CS of the patients who had single metastasis in a time-dependent manner. The likelihood of patients surviving for additional years increases as their survival duration lengthens. This growth plateaus after a certain number of years. The 5-year conditional overall survival (COS) increased from 1% directly after diagnosis to 6%, 20%, 43%, and 60%, given 1, 2, 3, and 4 years already survived in patients with bone metastasis, respectively (Figure 6A). The 5-year COS increased from 3% directly after diagnosis to 18%, 38%, 53%, and 67%, given 1, 2, 3, and 4 years already survived in patients with liver metastasis, respectively (Figure 6B). Similarly, the 5-year COS increased from 3% directly after diagnosis to 18%, 30%, 48%, and 78%, given 1, 2, 3, and 4 years already survived in patients with lung metastasis, respectively (Figure 6C). Notably, the maximum survival time of brain metastasis group is only 2 years, so the COS could only be predicted after one year at most (Figure 6D). In addition, the 5-year conditional disease-specific survival (CDSS) rates for patients who have single bone metastasis rose from an initial 3% at the time of diagnosis to 11%, 28%, 57%, and 80% after 1, 2, 3, and 4 years, respectively (Figure 6E). The 5-year CDSS increased from 6% directly after diagnosis to 21%, 38%, 53%, and 67%, given 1, 2, 3, and 4 years already survived in patients with liver metastasis, respectively (Figure 6F). The 5-year CDSS increased from 6% directly after diagnosis to 27%, 40%, 61%, and 89%, given 1, 2, 3, and 4 years already survived in patients with lung metastasis, respectively (Figure 6G). As for the brain metastasis group, the CDSS could not be predicted because no patients survived beyond 2 years in the group (Figure 6H).

Discussion

Based on the Global Cancer Statistics 2020, the occurrence and mortality rates of HCC remain high (3). Typically, HCC does not display noticeable symptoms in the early stages, which often results in a delay diagnosis and a grim prognosis for most patients (13,14). Consequently, the treatment of HCC becomes restricted and challenging (15). There exists a notable difference in prognosis between single metastasis and multiple metastases in advanced HCC patients. In the current age of precision medicine, it is essential for decision makers of treatment to evaluate and explore the difference in prognosis of HCC with single metastatic foci. A previous study has shown that in addition to intrahepatic metastasis, the most common organs involved in distant metastasis of HCC are lung, bone, and brain (16). Therefore, we discussed the prognostic differences among single intrahepatic metastasis, lung metastasis, bone metastasis and brain metastasis. Additionally, this is the first time to compare the prognosis of single metastasis to different single organ among HCC patients.

According to data from SEER, it was determined that lung (51%) stands as the predominant site of metastasis among the four organs, with bone (43%) coming in second. Solitary brain metastasis (2%) is the least common. Multivariate Cox regression analysis demonstrated that single lung metastasis had a shorter OS than intrahepatic metastasis (HR =1.293, 95% CI: 1.053–1.587), and single bone metastasis (HR =1.306, 95% CI: 1.021–1.67) and single lung metastasis (HR =1.395, 95% CI: 1.103–1.764) possessed shorter DSS than liver metastasis. FAR analyses demonstrated that the prognosis for single pulmonary metastasis was less favorable than that of bone metastasis in OS and DSS. Subgroup analysis reveals that chemotherapy played an interactive role in the comparison of DSS in single bone metastasis vs. liver metastasis group as well as single lung metastasis vs. liver metastasis group, and that chemotherapy may improve the survival. Moreover, CS analyses showed a marked improvement in the patients with single metastasis over time.

Previous studies have shown that sex, T stage, grade and therapy are remarkably associated with OS of advanced HCC patients, which aligns with our results (6,17,18). By conducting both univariate and multivariate Cox regression analyses, it was determined that sex, AJCC T status, Grade, number of tumors, surgery, chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and single lung metastasis were confirmed as independent risk factors for OS and DSS. Numerous studies have indicated lung as the most prevalent location for distant metastases, with an exceedingly dismal prognosis for HCC patients with pulmonary metastasis (19-21). In a study by Ye et al., it was revealed that the median follow-up period for individuals with pulmonary metastasis from HCC was only 1 months (22). Our study provides further proof that HCC patients with single lung metastasis have poorer outcomes than single bone or liver metastasis. In our study, the information of the HCC patients with single brain metastasis who survived more than 2 years could not be collected in the SEER database. Accordingly, the prognosis of brain metastasis has the potential to be extremely poor, as previous studies have confirmed (23-25). Perhaps the limited number of HCC patients with solitary brain metastasis hindered the ability to differentiate the difference, although this is not a definitive explanation. As of now, there are no better established guidelines for treating HCC patients with the four single metastatic site (26-28). Chemotherapy is recognized as an important treatment for tumors, including HCC (29-31). Hu et al. suggested that chemotherapy may bring about a better prognosis for patients with HCC experiencing single bone metastasis and could serve as an independent prognostic determinant (32). The research by Chen et al. demonstrated that chemotherapy improved prognosis in individuals with lung metastasis from HCC (33). This again aligns with the subgroup analysis results.

Our study has some notable strengths. Since more than groups of comparisons are involved, we use FAR to estimate confidence intervals to prevent from too wide confidence intervals of each group (11,34). Based on the FAR, we found that single lung metastasis may have worse OS and DSS than single liver or bone metastasis. Secondly, we applied the CS to assess the dynamic survival rate of advanced HCC patients. OS is considered as the duration from diagnosis to death from any cause. However, this method has its limitations. Gaps between OS and COS, even DSS and CDSS, are noticeably greater for the patients with high risk factors. Compared to traditional cumulative survival, CS could provide a more accurate guide to prognosis, especially for long-term survivors with high risk (35). Our study revealed that the CS rate for liver, bone, and lung metastasis continuously increased over time.

While this research offers an in-depth examination of the variations in prognosis among the patients with four single metastatic foci, there are still some limitations. Firstly, the majority patients are from SEER database so that the conclusions could not be extrapolated to other races. Secondly, it faces the limitations commonly associated with retrospective researches, which may compromise the quality of the evidence. Finally, certain crucial data pertaining to patients’ prognosis, including details on surgical procedures, chemotherapy approaches, and radiotherapy dosages, are absent.

Conclusions

Single lung metastasis emerged as the most prevalent in the four single metastatic types of HCC, followed by bone. The prognosis for single pulmonary metastasis was less favorable than that of single bone metastasis and single intrahepatic metastasis. The prognosis brain metastasis may be very poor, but it could not be confirmed in our study due to the limited data available. Ultimately, this research provides the statistical proof regarding the prognostic difference among HCC patients with single metastasis based on the Cox proportional hazard model, FAR and CS rate.
