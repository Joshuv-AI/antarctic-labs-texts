Japanese Version of the Quality of Dying and Death in the PICU Instrument: Development and Validation in Eight Centers in Japan
Authors: Atsuko Takayama, Atsushi Kawaguchi, Wataru Irie, Mitsunori Miyashita, Takuhiro Yamaguchi, Nobuyuki Yotani, Hitoshi Shiwaku, Norihiko Tsuboi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
OBJECTIVES:The quality of dying and death (QODD) of children on the PICU is essential for them, their families, and carers at the end-of-life. Currently, there are no standard instruments for measuring QODD in PICUs in Japan (J). We have developed a Japanese version of PICU-QODD (PICU-QODD-J) instrument, which has been culturally adapted to the Japanese context, and evaluated its psychometric validity.DESIGN:The study comprised two phases: 1) development of the PICU-QODD-J instrument, conducted with input from experts, clinicians, and bereaved families and 2) a multicenter, prospective, cross-sectional validation involving PICU clinicians. Data for the validation phase were collected between May 2023 and June 2024.SETTING:Eight PICUs in Japan.PARTICIPANTS:We recruited 231 clinicians (112 physicians, 119 nurses) who provided end-of-life care for 65 children in the PICU and their families.INTERVENTIONS:None.MEASUREMENTS AND MAIN RESULTS:The PICU-QODD-J instrument was developed through forward-backward translation, reviewed by a panel of five experts, and evaluated by 12 clinicians and seven bereaved family members. The PICU-QODD-J instrument comprises 21 items and demonstrated acceptable internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.92) and good test-retest reliability with intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.77 (95% CI, 0.64–0.85). The inter-rater reliability (IRR) was low (0.09 [95% CI, 0.06–0.13]). Standardized PICU-QODD-J instrument scores showed moderate positive correlations (r = 0.42–0.57; p < 0.001) with evaluations of overall quality of end-of-life care, quality of the moment of death, and fulfillment of family needs, supporting good construct validity.CONCLUSIONS:The PICU-QODD-J instrument demonstrated good reliability and validity and can be used to assess and improve end-of-life care in PICUs in Japan. However, given the low IRR, evaluations should be interpreted from multiple perspectives rather than a single assessment.

The PICU is a setting where life-saving treatments are administered; however, it is also where the majority of in-hospital pediatric deaths occur. Death in the PICU can profoundly affect bereaved families, frequently contributing to complicated grief, depression, and posttraumatic stress disorder (1–3). Clinicians involved in end-of-life care may likewise experience emotional distress, moral conflict, and burnout (4–6). Consequently, ensuring high-quality end-of-life care is a crucial component of PICU practice.

In the adult setting, numerous instruments have been developed to assess the quality of dying and death (QODD), and these instruments can be psychologically validated and translated for international use (7–9). Conversely, pediatric death encompasses unique circumstances and needs, thereby making the use of instruments specifically designed for children very important. One such tool is the PICU-QODD-20 instrument, which was developed for children in the PICU and their families (10). A scoping review of literature 2000–2021 identified 11 instruments for evaluating pediatric end-of-life care, with the PICU-QODD-20 instrument recognized as having the most comprehensive content (11). However, to date, the PICU-QODD-20 instrument is largely limited to English-speaking countries since it has only been translated into Portuguese (12).

In Japan, there are no validated instruments for measuring the QODD of children and their families in the PICU. To address this gap, we aimed to translate and culturally adapt the PICU-QODD-20 instrument for use in Japan as well as evaluate its psychometric properties in a multicenter study.

METHODS

Our PICU-QODD instrument study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Tohoku University Graduate School of Medicine (approval numbers 2021-1-441 and 2023-1-401) and by the ethics committees of all participating institutions. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the 1975 Declaration of Helsinki. In phase 1, written and verbal informed consent was obtained from physicians, nurses, and bereaved family members. Bereaved families were selected by a representative of a bereaved families’ association who was informed of the study in advance, and only those whose child had died older than 1 year earlier were approached to minimize psychologic distress. In phase 2, completion of the questionnaire was considered to imply consent, and the data were collected under an opt-out process announced on each facility’s website.

Phase 1: Translation Into Japanese and Cross-Cultural Adaptation

We used best practice guidelines for adapting and translating the QODD instrument for Japanese families in the PICU (13, 14). We went through ten steps, including a meeting of experts and interviews with physicians and nurses from multiple PICUs, as well as bereaved families. Japanese translation and cultural adaptation were carried out for face and content validity. PICU-Quality of Dying and Death-20

The PICU-QODD-20 instrument was developed in the United States, based on the QODD model in adults (15), and its reliability and validity have been verified (10). It is a proxy assessment instrument that measures the QODD in the last 3 days of children in the PICU and their families. The instrument consists of 20 items and medical staff rate them on an instrument from 0 (terrible experience) to 10 (perfect experience).

Translation and Expert Meeting

In steps 2 and 8, a translation company provided forward and back translations to ensure conceptual equivalence. Two bilingual translators developed two versions, which were integrated by a PICU-experienced member (A.T.). In steps 3, 5, 7, and 9, an expert panel of five specialists (one pediatric palliative care physician, one palliative care researcher, and three pediatric nursing researchers), all experienced in end-of-life care and instrument development, conducted repeated reviews. They assessed the appropriateness, clarity, and cultural relevance of the Japanese terms. The final version was developed with consensus in all expert panel members. In steps 4 and 6, the provisional version of the PICU-QODD-J instrument was revised using analyses of interviews with physicians, nurses, and bereaved families. Item concepts were confirmed with the original author when necessary. In step 10, the final version of the PICU-QODD-J instrument was approved by the original author.

Assessment of Face and Content Validity

Face and content validity were assessed by four physicians and eight nurses working in the PICU, each with greater than 3 years of clinical experience and involvement in at least three end-of-life care cases in the PICU. They completed two evaluations using a pre-assessment questionnaire. First, they recalled a deceased PICU patient and rated their QODD over the last 3 days using the provisional PICU-QODD-J instrument (0 = strongly disagree, 10 = strongly agree). Subsequently, cognitive interviews (16) were conducted to assess understanding, recall, and the ability to rate item concepts. Second, these individuals rated content validity (1 = not relevant, 4 = very relevant).

Content validity was also evaluated by seven bereaved family members, consisting of parents (six mothers and one father) of children who had died in Japan between the ages of 0 and 15 years. No specific time frame after the child’s death was set for inclusion, as the aim was to assess the relevance of each item from the family’s perspective. The raters evaluated the items of the provisional version of the PICU-QODD-J instrument using a 4-point Likert scale. Based on interview results, experts revised item content. Items with an item-level content validity index (I-CVI) of below 0.78 were removed from the Japanese version (17).

Phase 2: Validation of the Psychometric Properties of the PICU-QODD-J Instrument

The index cases were children who died in the PICU and their families, evaluated by clinicians. Eligibility criteria were defined separately for cases and clinicians. Eligible cases included children 0–15 years old who died at participating institutions between May 1, 2023, and April 30, 2024, after at least 48 hours of PICU admission, along with their parents or guardians. Cases were excluded if the parents or guardians were unable to visit the child before death. All eligible cases during the study period were included. In this phase, only clinicians were surveyed using the PICU-QODD-J instrument. Clinicians comprised physicians and nurses who were most familiar with the patient’s end-of-life condition during the final 3 days of life. The principal investigator at each participating institution selected two physicians and two nurses who best represented those involved in each case, resulting in a total of 130 physicians and 130 nurses across all participating sites. The study covered the period from May 2023 to June 2024.

To minimize bias, target case numbers were predetermined for each participating facility, and cumulative case counts were reviewed every 3 months. Data collection concluded once each site achieved its assigned target. Principal investigators obtained patient data from the medical records of each case, including age, sex, cause of death, and end-of-life care elements. Clinicians were surveyed regarding their background characteristics (e.g., age, occupation, and number of end-of-life care cases experienced); responses to the 21 items of the PICU-QODD-J instrument; and three single-item global questions theoretically related to overall assessments (the quality of end-of-life care, the quality of the moment of death [defined as how peaceful, comfortable, and consistent with the family’s wishes the death was], and the fulfillment of family needs) (10, 15, 18).

Clinician questionnaires were distributed in four waves via Research Electronic Data Capture (19) in August 2023 and November 2023, and February 2024 and May 2024. Each wave included two rounds conducted 3 weeks apart. In each survey, clinicians evaluated the QODD of children who had died in the preceding 3 months and their families using the PICU-QODD-J instrument and three single-item global questions, focusing on the child’s final 3 days of life.

The sample size was calculated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) from the reliability test. Assuming an ICC of 0.7, four raters (two physicians and two nurses), and a se of 0.05, a total of 70 cases was determined as the required sample size (20).

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and proportions, and percentages when the denominator of a proportion was greater than 50. Ordinal and continuous variables were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs) or means with sds. In validating the PICU-QODD-J instrument, responses were excluded if greater than 15% of items were missing or “don’t know” was selected for over half. Valid responses underwent item analysis, including response distribution (0 and 10), skewness, and “don’t know” rates. Missing and “don’t know” responses were imputed with the individual mean. A sensitivity analysis excluding imputed data was also conducted to confirm the robustness of the results. Standardized scores were calculated by dividing total scores by the number of items and multiplying by 10 (18).

Psychometric validation included assessments of internal consistency, test-retest reliability, construct validity, and inter-rater reliability (IRR). For all analyses except IRR, standardized scores were averaged across raters to yield one value per case. Internal consistency was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α > 0.7 considered acceptable) (21). Test-retest reliability was assessed using ICCs between the first and second standardized scores. IRR was analyzed separately using a linear mixed-effects model (LMM) with random effects for cases and raters, based on individual standardized scores to account for correlations among patients and raters. ICCs greater than 0.75 were considered good (22). Construct validity was examined using Pearson correlation coefficients between standardized scores and the three single-item global questions, which were hypothesized to correlate positively with the PICU-QODD-J instrument.

Additional analyses using LMM were conducted to explore associations between clinicians’ characteristics and their standardized scores.

Statistical significance was set at p value of less than 0.05. All analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Mac, Version 29 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY).

RESULTS

Phase 1: Translation Into Japanese and Cultural Adaptation

Modifications to Adapt to Japanese Culture and Superficial Validity

The original version of the PICU-QODD-20 instrument was revised by the expert panel to reflect the Japanese cultural context. For better comprehension, option expressions were modified from “0 = strongly disagree” to “10 = strongly agree.” The item “hospital clergy or chaplains were available” was removed owing to cultural irrelevance. Although the term “spiritual” has no direct Japanese equivalent, it remains an essential aspect of end-of-life care; therefore, the item was retained. Instead, culturally adapted expressions, including “religion,” “values and beliefs,” “child- and family-like,” and “culture,” were used, with clarifying examples added. This process resulted in a provisional 22-item Japanese version.

The provisional version was revised based on the results of cognitive interviews with physicians and nurses. For example, many of the participants found it difficult to answer the original item “the child was free of pain” because they were unsure whether it referred to physical or mental pain. Additionally, because the cognitive function of children is still developing, they may struggle to distinguish physical from psychologic pain, so answering posed a challenge. Therefore, in the Japanese version, to be more specific, the item was revised to “The child did not have any physical pain.” The original item “the child was free of other troubling symptoms” was divided into two more specific statements: “The child did not have any distressful physical symptoms other than pain” and “The child did not have any distressful emotional symptoms (anxiety, fear, loneliness, etc)” because the concept was not correctly understood by the participants, who had different ideas about what it meant. The newly created concepts “Clinicians considered and respected the values and beliefs of the child and family” and “Clinicians respected the child’s and family’s unique personality” were overlapping concepts, so the two were combined into “Clinicians considered and respected the qualities unique to the child and his/her family (values, beliefs, personality, and true self, etc).”

Content Validity

The I-CVIs for physicians and nurses ranged from 0.75 to 1.00. The item “The clinicians respected the religion that the family believed in” had the lowest score (0.75). Among bereaved families, the I-CVIs ranged within 0.71–1.00, with the same item showing the lowest score (0.71), whereas all other items achieved an I-CVI of 1.00. These findings indicate that bereaved families considered all other items relevant to the child’s end-of-life care. Because this item showed low I-CVIs across all groups, it was removed from the Japanese version. Therefore, the final version of the PICU-QODD-J instrument includes 21 items (Appendix S1, https://links.lww.com/PCC/C726).

Reliability and Validity

Cronbach’s alpha was 0.92, indicating acceptable internal consistency. The test-retest reliability was good, with an ICC of 0.77 (95% CI, 0.64–0.85). The IRR was low, with an ICC of 0.09 (95% CI, 0.06–0.13). The standardized PICU-QODD-J scores showed moderate positive correlations (r = 0.42–0.57; p < 0.001) with three single-item global questions, confirming good construct validity (Table 4).

DISCUSSION

In this study, PICU-QODD-J instrument was developed following a step-by-step process, and its psychometric properties were assessed. The PICU-QODD-J instrument was confirmed to be reliable and valid in our study including eight PICUs in Japan. In this process, three key modifications were made to develop the PICU-QODD-J instrument. First, option phrasing was adjusted to better suit Japanese respondents. Second, culturally incompatible items—especially those related to religion and spirituality—were removed or replaced with equivalent terms. Third, examples were added in parentheses to aid understanding. Religion and spirituality are important components of end-of-life care in the PICU (23, 24); however, they often present challenges in Japanese translation (25). Our approach facilitated the development of an instrument that is both culturally appropriate and practical for Japanese clinicians while contributing to the broader framework of cross-cultural adaptation in end-of-life care research.

Although the PICU-QODD-J instrument demonstrated good reliability and validity, the IRR was low, indicating variability among raters. Our results also indicate that clinicians’ individual experience with end-of-life care in the PICU may influence their PICU-QODD-J instrument ratings. Recent studies including clinicians caring for adult and pediatric patients at the end of life have similarly highlighted differences in perceptions related to level of experience and professional roles (26–29). Our findings may offer a new perspective on these factors. In the unique environment of the PICU, children at the end of life and their families present complex and diverse needs, including the management of physical symptoms and medical interventions, addressing emotional and spiritual distress, supporting parental decision-making and fulfillment of the parental role, maintaining dignity, and coordinating multidisciplinary care to ensure continuity, communication, and family-centered care (30–35). Clinicians provide this care from their respective professional perspectives, and such diversity constitutes an essential component of high-quality end-of-life care. Therefore, the observed variation may represent clinically meaningful differences rather than inconsistencies. Accordingly, when using the PICU-QODD-J instrument for clinician-based proxy evaluations, it is crucial to integrate assessments from multiple clinicians and interpret the results from a multiperspective viewpoint rather than relying on a single rating.

Our study has four main limitations. First, although the study involved eight centers, the sample size was relatively small, and model fit indices could not be evaluated. Second, because evaluations were conducted up to 3 months after a child’s death, recall bias may have occurred. Although this delay did not affect test-retest reliability, it should be considered when interpreting the quality-of-death results. Third, the PICU-QODD-J instrument scores were assessed by clinicians and therefore may not fully capture the actual experiences of children and their families. Nevertheless, clinician-based evaluations represent a practical and ethically appropriate approach, given the challenges of directly engaging children and families in this activity. Finally, the perspectives of palliative care specialists regarding end-of-life care in the PICU, as described in recent PICU literature (36–38), were not included. However, the PICU-QODD-J instrument incorporates core palliative care elements and may facilitate improved evaluation of end-of-life care practices and constructive engagement with pediatric palliative care in Japanese PICUs (39, 40). Future studies incorporating family and palliative care team assessments and perspectives are recommended to provide a more comprehensive understanding and interpretation of our findings.

In conclusion, our study confirmed that the PICU-QODD-J instrument has good face validity, content validity, internal consistency, test-retest reliability, and construct validity for measuring the quality of end-of-life care. It can be used to systematically improve the quality of care in PICUs in Japan. Furthermore, the framework of translation, development, and validation employed in this study can be used by other international groups who want to use a version of the PICU-QODD-20 in non-English-speaking environments.
