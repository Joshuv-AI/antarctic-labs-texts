A Nonparametric Global Win Probability Approach to the Analysis and Sizing of Randomized Controlled Trials With Multiple Endpoints of Different Scales and Missing Data: Beyond O'Brien–Wei–Lachin
Authors: Guangyong Zou, Lily Zou
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTMultiple primary endpoints are commonly used in randomized controlled trials to assess treatment effects. When the endpoints are measured on different scales, the O'Brien rank‐sum test or the Wei–Lachin test for stochastic ordering may be used for hypothesis testing. However, the O'Brien–Wei–Lachin (OWL) approach is unable to handle missing data and adjust for baseline measurements. We present a nonparametric approach for data analysis that encompasses the OWL approach as a special case. Our approach is based on quantifying an endpoint‐specific treatment effect using the probability that a participant in the treatment group has a better score than (or a win over) a participant in the control group. The average of the endpoint‐specific win probabilities (WinPs), termed the global win probability (gWinP), is used to quantify the global treatment effect, with the null hypothesis gWinP = 0.50. Our approach involves converting the data for each endpoint to endpoint‐specific win fractions, and modeling the win fractions using multivariate linear mixed models to obtain estimates of the endpoint‐specific WinPs and the associated variance–covariance matrix. Focusing on confidence interval estimation for the gWinP, we derive sample size formulas for clinical trial design. Simulation results demonstrate that our approach performed well in terms of bias, interval coverage percentage, and assurance of achieving a pre‐specified precision for the gWinP. Illustrative code for implementing the methods using SAS PROC RANK and PROC MIXED is provided.

Introduction

The efficacy of a new treatment is commonly assessed in randomized controlled trials by comparing measurements between groups at a specific time point, usually at the last assessment time point [1]. It is customary for randomized controlled trials to incorporate multiple endpoints to assess treatment effects. For such trials, several approaches can be taken. First, one may designate one endpoint as the primary endpoint and leave the rest as secondary. Second, one can create a composite endpoint using a scoring algorithm [2] or conduct a prioritized treatment‐control pairwise comparison according to a clinical hierarchy [3, 4, 5]. Third, one can analyze each endpoint separately with adjustments for multiplicity [6, 7]. Finally, one may use joint or global tests. The last approach is particularly advantageous for complex diseases such as stroke [8], Parkinson disease [9], schizophrenia [10], and depression [11].

Motivated by a randomized trial involving 11 diabetes patients with 34 electromyographic endpoints to measure nerve function, O'Brien [12] proposed two parametric tests and one nonparametric rank‐sum test. All three tests are directional in the sense that the treatment is expected to have a positive or no harm effect on all endpoints. Recognizing that multiple endpoints may be measured on different scales, O'Brien's parametric tests first involve standardizing the data for each endpoint by subtracting the overall endpoint mean from each observation and dividing by the pooled within‐group sample standard deviation. The resulting endpoint‐specific scores are then analyzed using ordinary least squares or generalized least squares to account for the correlations among the endpoints. Both tests garnered widespread attention, see for example, Pocock, Geller, and Tsiatis [13], Lauter [14], and Dallow, Leonov, and Roger [15]. However, one limitation of the parametric tests is the difficulty in specifying the underlying multivariate distribution without assuming normality, which is an untenable assumption in many situations. This problem is exacerbated in situations where the endpoints are measured using a mixture of different scales [16].

We therefore focus on the nonparametric approach due to the robustness and flexibility it provides when handling multiple endpoints on different scales. O'Brien [12] constructed the nonparametric test by first pooling the data for each endpoint across treatment groups and replacing the data with their rank values. Then, for each participant, the ranks were summed across endpoints, and the resulting rank‐sums were analyzed using a two‐sample t‐test. O'Brien's rank‐sum test received less attention, with a few exceptions: Huang et al. [17] improved O'Brien rank‐sum test by applying the idea of the Satterthwaite t‐test to the rank‐sums. One may improve the power of O'Brien's test by specifying weights for the endpoints. However, selecting a weighting scheme can be difficult; see Ramchandani, Schoenfeld, and Finkelstein [18] for a review on various weighting schemes. Huang, Woolson, and O'Brien [19] derived the corresponding sample size formulas, where the global treatment effect (GTE) is defined as the mean of the endpoint‐specific effects. For each endpoint, the treatment effect was defined as the difference between the probability that a random participant in the treatment group has a better score than a control participant and the probability that a random participant in the control group has a better score than a treated participant. This difference can be recognized as Somer's d [20] or the Mann–Whitney difference [21]. Under the general term of the Wei–Lachin test based on a paper published by Wei and Lachin in 1984 [22], Lachin [21] described a test for “stochastic ordering” with an underlying parameter that is identical to the GTE used by Huang et al. [17, 19]. Thus, we will refer to the methods by O'Brien [12] and Wei and Lachin [22] as the O'Brien–Wei–Lachin (OWL) approach. The OWL approach has been applied to various randomized controlled trials on a wide range of diseases including Parkinson disease [9], traumatic brain injury [23], and vestibular disorders [24].

However, in the context of randomized controlled trials, the OWL approach is limited in three aspects. First, the implementation of the OWL approach requires specialized software [19, 21]. Second, the OWL approach is unable to handle missing data. Finally, the OWL approach cannot adjust for baseline measurements despite it being well‐known that such adjustments can result in an improvement in statistical efficiency and power.

The purpose of this paper is to present a regression approach that goes beyond the OWL approach for the analysis and sample size planning of randomized clinical trials with multiple endpoints. Specifically, we define the treatment effect as the probability that a random participant in the treatment group has a better score than (or win over) a random participant from the control group [25]. Zou, Zou, and Qiu [26] identified a dozen terms for this probability but preferred the term “win probability” (WinP) due to its intuitiveness. Similar to the O'Brien tests, each observation is first converted into a win fraction to quantify the proportion of times that this observation “wins over” the observations in the comparison group. We then obtain estimates of the endpoint‐specific WinPs and the associated variance‐covariate matrix by modeling the win fractions using the mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM) with an unstructured covariance structure [1, 27]. The MMRM regression model allows baseline measurement adjustments and accommodating missing data via the within‐participant correlations among endpoints. The global treatment effect is estimated using the mean of endpoint‐specific WinPs. Inference proceeds by invoking the multivariate central limit theorem. Data analysis can be carried out with any standard software that can rank data and carry out mixed‐effects model analysis.

The rest of the article is organized as follows. In Section 2, we present a motivating example based on a previously published clinical trial that used three different endpoints. In Section 3, we develop methods for confidence interval (CI) estimation and sample size planning for the mean of the WinPs, termed the global WinP (gWinP). We then demonstrate that our approach encompasses the OWL approach as a special case. In Section 4, we illustrate our methods using data from the motivating example. In Section 5, we assess the accuracy of our method in terms of CI coverage and sample size estimation using a simulation study based on the motivating example. We examine the performance of our approach under four missing data mechanisms: no missing data, missing completely at random, missing at random, and missing not at random as defined by Rubin [28]. We close the article with a discussion.

A Motivating Example: MLN02 for Ulcerative Colitis

This article was motivated by a randomized trial conducted by Feagan et al. [29] that evaluated the efficacy of vedolizumab (previously known as MLN02) for ulcerative colitis (UC), an inflammatory bowel disease. The trial randomized 181 adult patients to receive either MLN02 at a dose of 0.5 mg/kg, MLN02 at a dose of 2.0 mg/kg, or a placebo administered at baseline and day 29. For illustrative purposes, we have combined the two MLN02 groups since there is no evidence to show a difference between the two dose groups. The trial measured UC disease severity from a clinical, endoscopic, and histological perspective at baseline and Week 6. Clinical severity was assessed using the UC clinical score (UCCS) which consists of four items (stool frequency, rectal bleeding, functional assessment by patient, and global assessment by physician) with total scores ranging from 0 (normal) to 12 (severe). Endoscopic severity was assessed using endoscopic videos and quantified using a modified Baron score (MBS), which ranges from 0 (normal) to 4 (severe). Histological severity was determined from rectal‐biopsy specimens and measured using the modified Riley score (MRS), which ranges from 0 (no inflammation) to 7 (severe acute inflammation).

A total of 169 patients (59 placebo and 110 MLN02) had measurements for all three endpoints at baseline. At Week 6, all patients had data for UCCS, 51 patients (86.4%) in the placebo group and 93 patients (84.5%) in the MLN02 group had data for MBS, and 49 patients (83.1%) in the placebo group and 92 patients (83.6%) in the MLN02 group had data for MRS.

Rank‐Based Methods

Notations and Definitions

Consider a randomized trial with a parallel‐group design randomizing N participants to either the treatment or control group using a pre‐specified randomization ratio. At a pre‐specified follow‐up time, K primary endpoints are observed for each participant, denoted as Yij=Yij1,Yij2,…,YijKT, where i=1 for the treatment group and i=0 for the control group, and j=1,2,…,ni with n1+n0=N. For participants with missing data, we denote the endpoints as Yij=Yij1,Yij2,…,YijkijT with kij<K. We assume each participant has baseline measurements for all K endpoints, Yij0=Yij10,Yij20,…YijK0T.

We assume that Yik∼Fik, where the distribution of Fik is unspecified. We regard a higher outcome value as more favorable. For endpoint k, k=1,2,…,K, define the endpoint‐specific WinP as (1) θk=∫F0kdF1k=PrY0k<Y1k+0.5PrY0k=Y1k,k=1,2,…,K

The case where a lower outcome value is defined as favorable may be defined analogously. The WinP has appeared in the literature under at least a dozen of terms [26], including ridits [30] and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve [31]. The global win probability (gWinP) may be defined as (2) gWinP=1K∑k=1Kθk which can be recognized as a 1‐to‐1 function of the global treatment effect (GTE) defined by Huang, Woolson, and O'Brien [19], that is, GTE=2×gWinP−1

The WinP may also be directly mapped to other well‐known effect measures. For example, if the outcome are normally distributed, that is, Yik∼Nμik,σik2,i=0,1, the WinP is given by, θk=PrY0k<Y1k=Φμ1k−μ0kσ0k2+σ1k2 where Φ(⋅) is the cumulative standard normal distribution. When σ0k2=σ1k2=σ2, θk=Φμ1k−μ0kσ2=Φdk2 where dk can be recognized as Cohen's effect size with the benchmark values 0, 0.2, 0.5, and 0.8 corresponding to a “null,” “small,” “moderate,” and “large” effect size, respectively. Therefore, the benchmark values can be mapped to the WinP values of 0.50, 0.56, 0.64, and 0.71, respectively. For binary endpoints, θk=RDk/2+0.5 where RDk is the risk difference between two groups. The WinP can also be used to calculate the number needed to treat (NNT), which represents the number of patients that need to be treated for one additional patient to experience a favorable outcome. It has been demonstrated for binary outcomes that [32], NNTk=12θk−1 while for all other types of outcomes, NNTk=1θk−0.5 which suggests that point estimates and CIs for NNT are readily available from results for WinPs.

A Regression Approach to Extending the OWL Approach

We now describe a multivariate regression approach for analyzing win fractions for the purpose of adjusting for baseline measurements and handling missing data. To this end, we denote the win fractions based on the follow‐up data for participant j in group i by Wij, i=1,0 and j=1,2,…,ni, with Wij0 for the corresponding win fractions based on the baseline measurements and applying Equations (6) and (7), if available. Recall that the win fractions of different participants are asymptotically uncorrelated. The multivariate mixed model for a participant with all K endpoints observed can be written as (12) Wij=Xijβ+eij where, with ⊗ denoting the Kronecker product and Gij being a vector of 1 and 0 for the respective comparison groups, Xij=1Gij⊗IK,andβ=β01,…,β0K,β11,…,β1KT when ignoring baseline measurements, or Xij=1Gij⊗IKdiagWij0,andβ=β01,…,β0K,β11,…,β1K,β21,…,β2KT when adjusting for baseline measurements. The error term eij is assumed to be multivariate normally distributed with mean 0K and a K‐by‐K group‐specific variance–covariance matrix ∑i.

The model in Equation (12) is commonly known as the mixed model for repeated measures (MMRM). It has been recommended for analyzing longitudinal continuous outcomes collected at fixed time points due to several notable advantages [1]. First, it is robust to model misspecification since no assumption is made for the response trajectory over time. Second, it allows for the use of partial data from participants who had missing endpoints. Under the assumption that the data is MAR, missing information is compensated for by the observed data via the within‐participant correlations [1, 27]. Finally, by specifying a group‐specific “unstructured” (UN) covariance matrix for within‐subject correlations, the MMRM is robust to misspecification of the covariance matrix and the presence of variance heterogeneity. It has also been pointed out that this model is fairly robust to violations of the normality assumption for the residuals [38, 39] and the non‐linearity assumption for covariates [40]. In the present context, endpoint type plays the role of time in the MMRM, and thus most of the advantages are retained for the analysis of win fractions.

Sample Size Planning for Estimating Global Win Probability With Precision and Assurance

We calculate sample sizes based on the estimation of the gWinP, because the CONSORT statement and other guidelines now emphasize the estimation of effect sizes over the solitary presentation of the p value [43]. Specifically, we determine the sample size needed to estimate the gWinP with pre‐specified precision and assurance, where the assurance probability can be seen as equivalent to the statistical power of a hypothesis test. Specifically, to determine the minimum sample size needed to ensure that, with a given assurance probability (e.g., 80% or 90%) the lower limit of a 95% CI for the gWinP is above a given value (e.g., 0.50) we present formulas for three different scenarios. Data for all endpoints are available from pilot studies or previously published trials. If the specified distributions of the disease severity scores for all K endpoints for the two groups are provided by pilot data, then the values for the gWinP and the group‐specific variances‐covariance for the win fractions may be obtained using Equations (9) and (11). Illustration With the Motivating Example: The MLN02 Trial

Estimation of the Global Win Probability

We now illustrate the methods described in Section 3 using the motivating example presented in Section 2. The illustrative SAS code used for the analysis is presented in the Appendix. We first apply the OWL approach, which requires complete data. Deletion of participants with missing endpoints resulted in n0=49 and n1=92 participants with complete data. After converting the data for each endpoint to win fractions using Equations (6) and (7), we applied Equations (9) and (10) to estimate the endpoint‐specific WinPs, the gWinP and the associated 2‐sided 95% CIs. Identical results may also be obtained with SAS PROC LOGISTIC, which implemented the method for areas under the receiver‐operating characteristic curves by DeLong, DeLong, and Clarke‐Pearson [31]. Recall that this approach may provide valid but inefficient results under the restrictive assumption of data missing completely at random.

Since our approach can handle missing data under the more realistic assumption of data missing at random, we first use Equations (6) and (7) to convert all available data for all three endpoints at baseline and at Week 6 to win fractions. We then apply the multivariate regression model in Equation (12) and conduct two analyses where we either ignore or adjusted for baseline measurements. All results are presented in Table 2. Compared with our multivariate analyses that accommodate missing data, the OWL approach yielded the largest estimate for the gWinP with the widest CI. Adjusting for baseline measurements resulted in narrower CIs for the endpoint‐specific WinPs and the gWinP. The WinPs for the UCCS, modified Baron score, and Riley scores are estimated to be 62.9% (54.0–71.1, p = 0.0051), 62.2% (53.3–70.3, p = 0.0074), and 64.5% (55.4–72.6, p = 0.0020), respectively, suggesting that MLN02 had consistent effects in reducing the severity of UC from clinical symptom, endoscopic, and histological perspectives. Overall, we can conclude that, compared with a patient treated with the placebo, a patient treated with MLN02 for 6 weeks had a 63.3% (95% CI, 55.4–70.4, p value = 0.0011) probability of having better UCCS, modified Baron score, and Riley scores, when adjusting for baseline measurements of the indices.

Simulation

Since the methods for data analysis and sample size planning in Section 3 are developed using large sample theory, their performance in finite samples must be evaluated. For the data analysis methods, we aimed to evaluate performance in terms of bias for estimating the gWinP and the empirical coverage and tail errors of the associated 2‐sided 95% CIs. For sample size estimation, we assessed empirical assurance probabilities of 80% and 90% that the lower limits of the CIs are above 0.50.

We designed the simulation study based on the motivating example described in Section 2. Specifically, each sample size Na was estimated using the formula in Equation (19). We generated a dataset with three endpoints Y1, Y2, and Y3, with 13, 5, and 8 categories, respectively. For the follow‐up data, we used the probability for each category in Group G (1 for treatment and 0 control) from the motivating example as shown in Figure 1 for Week 6. To emulate randomization, we used the probabilities for the placebo group at baseline for both the treatment and control groups. The 6‐dimensional (3 endpoints each measured at baseline and at follow‐up) ordinal data for each participant were generated using the “mean mapping method” as implemented by Wicklin [46], with correlations given in Table 1.

We first evaluated the performance of the methods when there no missing data. We then examined a scenario where Y1 has no missing data, while Y2 and Y3 had missing data following the three missing mechanisms: MCAR, MAR, and MNAR according to the following setup: MCAR:lnoddsofmissingY2,Y3=−0.85both groups missing30%MAR:lnoddsofmissingY2,Y3=−0.5×G−0.5×G×Y1,G=0missing50%,G=1missing12%MNAR:lnoddsofmissingY2=−0.0408×Y1+0.1245×G×Y2,G=0missing45%,G=1missing53%lnoddsofmissingY3=−0.0712×Y1+0.1344×G×Y3,G=0missing40%,G=1missing58%

The resulting dataset was analyzed using the multivariate mixed model [12] to obtain the 2‐sided Wald‐type 95% CIs for the gWinP, after converting the observations for endpoints to endpoint‐specific win fractions. The OWL approach was implemented by deleting participants with any missing values before creating win fractions.

We repeated the above steps 10 000 times for 48 different scenarios (2 randomization ratios, 3 sample size methods, 4 data missing mechanism, and 2 assurance probabilities). The empirical 95% CI coverage was calculated as the percentage of times that the interval contains the true value of the gWinP. The left tail error rate was estimated as the percentage of upper confidence limits less than the true value of the gWinP while the right tail error rate was estimated as the percentage of lower confidence limits greater than the true parameter values. The bias was estimated as the average of (gWinP^−gWinP)/gWinP×100%. The empirical assurance probability was estimated as the percentage of times that the lower limits of the 2‐sided 95% CIs were above 0.50.

Table 3 shows the simulation results for estimating the gWinP with an 80% assurance probability. When there is no missing data, the results of the OWL approach are identical to the mixed model without adjusting for baseline measurements. In this case, the empirical CI coverage percentages of the three approaches are very close to the nominal level. The observed empirical assurance probabilities for sample sizes estimated by Methods 1 and 2, which require prior information, show that the formulas are fairly accurate, while Method 3 produced conservative sample sizes (similar to those by Huang, Woolson, and O'Brien [19]). When data missing under MCAR, all three approaches resulted in good CI coverage percentages. Meanwhile, the OWL approach yielded a much lower assurance probability in comparison to the mixed model approaches.

Discussion

The primary question of interest in randomized controlled trials is: “what is the probability that a participant in the treatment group has a better outcome score (or a win over) a participant in the control group in terms of endpoints” [25]. This question has rarely been directly answered in practice [47], especially when the endpoints are measured on a scale with units that do not have a clear interpretation. A common approach is to use the standardized mean difference, also known as Cohen's effect size when data are assumed to be normally distributed, which is an untenable assumption in many situations. Even if the data are normally distributed, Cohen's effect size dose not lend itself readily to interpretation [48]. The concept of the win probability, which has appeared under a dozen terms in the literature [33, 49], is rooted in the Wilcoxon–Mann–Whitney test and has long been called for to be used in the assessment of health technologies [47].

Inspired by the OWL approach, we quantified the treatment effect using the global win probability (gWinP), defined as the average of multiple endpoint‐specific win probabilities, that measures a treatment's overall benefit across multiple endpoints [19, 50]. Inference on the gWinP was made simple by first converting the raw scores of each endpoint to win fractions using overall and treatment group‐specific ranks. Because the win fractions are asymptotically uncorrelated [35], they can be modeled using a multivariate linear mixed model with an unstructured covariance structure [1, 27]. Appropriate point estimators for the gWinP and the associated variance can be identified using the results from the multivariate analysis. We have also provided corresponding sample size planning formulas based on the availability of prior information. The simulation results suggest that our proposed methods are robust to data missing completely at random or missing at random, in contrast to the OWL approach, which performed well only when there was no missing data. Going beyond the OWL approach, our multivariate mixed model approach it requires no specialized software to implement, can handle missing data, and adjusts for baseline measurements. The simulation results also suggested our sample size formulas performed well in terms of maintaining the nominal assurance probability (power).

There has been extensive discussion in the literature on the use of multivariate outcomes in randomized controlled trials. In particular, many proposed methods have focused inference on the raw measurements of the endpoints by assuming the data follow a multivariate normal distribution [10, 13, 15, 51]. However, as Yoon et al. pointed out, “this assumption is less tenable in many studies,” especially when the multiple endpoints in question are a mixture of discrete and continuous outcomes. Another strategy is to dichotomize endpoint data and apply the generalized estimating equations method as done by the authors of the National Institute of Neurological Disorders and Stroke rt‐PA Stroke Study Group [8, 52]. However, it is well known that this approach can result in a dramatic reduction in efficiency. Finally, one may adopt a latent variable approach [16, 53, 54], which makes the normality assumption for a set of latent variables. Unfortunately, this approach may result in effect measures that are not easily interpretable [16].

Our article has several limitations. First, we only considered randomized trials with a single follow‐up time point. However, the mixed model for win fractions discussed above can be extended to trials with multiple primary endpoints and multiple follow‐up time points, that is, longitudinal trials with multivariate endpoints [52, 55]. Second, we did not consider studies randomizing intact units to different groups, commonly known as cluster randomization trials. We leave for future research the extension of the methods presented here in combination with the methods in Zou [56] to handle cluster trials with multiple endpoints. Finally, we did not consider scenarios with time‐to‐event data, which can be complicated due to censoring. However, the concept of converting censored data to win fractions using a scoring system similar to the approach proposed by Finkelstein and Schoenfeld [3], followed by a regression analysis may still be useful in developing methods for multivariate survival data.

In summary, we have presented a unified regression approach for analyzing and sample size planning of randomized controlled trials with multiple endpoints of different types. Rather than following the conventional approach which focuses on hypothesis testing, we emphasized treatment effect estimation as it is more informative. Our approach is a generalization of the nonparametric OWL approach that can handle missing data and adjust for baseline measurements. Resolving these issues is of interest, as the limitations of the OWL approach may have hindered the broader adoption of the global test for analyzing randomized trials with multiple endpoints [10, 11, 57], despite its advantages over analyzing each endpoint separately with multiplicity adjustments [6, 7]. Our nonparametric global methods can be implemented easily using standard statistical software. The illustrative SAS code is presented in the online Appendix S1.
