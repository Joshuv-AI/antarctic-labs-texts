Association between female HPV infection, inflammatory markers and depression: a cross-sectional and mediation analysis
Authors: Yuexin Zhang, Shaolei Ma, Zhi He
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFemale HPV infection and depression have been linked to inflammation. However, the role that inflammation plays in the relationship between female HPV infection and depression remains unclear. This study investigates the interplay between female HPV infection, inflammatory markers, and depression through a mediation analysis.MethodA cross-sectional analysis was conducted using data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES). HPV infection status, depression, and inflammatory markers (neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, platelets, and derived indices such as NLR, PLR, SII, and LMR) were measured. Multiple linear and logistic regression models were applied to examine associations, and mediation analysis was conducted to evaluate the potential mediating effects.ResultsFemale HPV infection was associated with an increased risk of depression (OR: 1.2902, 95% CI: 1.0186–1.6343) after adjusting for confounders. Inflammatory markers (SⅡ, NLR, PLR, Lymphocyte) were nonlinearly associated with depression and were positively associated with depression after reaching the cut point. Inflammatory markers mediated this association, with monocytes demonstrating the strongest mediation (PM = 1.64%, p < 0.0001), followed by lymphocytes (PM = 0.72%, p < 0.0001) and neutrophils (PM = 0.69%, p < 0.0001). PLT, SII, NLR and LMR showed weaker mediation (p < 0.0001).ConclusionFemale HPV infection was associated with an increased risk of depression. Inflammatory markers partially mediate the association between female HPV infection and depression, supporting potential role of immune dysregulation in HPV-related depressive symptoms.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11590-2.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, one of the most prevalent sexually transmitted infections globally, affects approximately 43% of women aged 18 to 59 in United States [1, 2]. HPV is categorized as low-risk or high-risk based on its oncogenic potential to cause cancer or precancerous lesions. High-risk HPV (HR-HPV) infections demonstrate a particularly strong association with gynecological cancers [3]. For example, persistent high-risk HPV strains are strongly associated with cervical cancer and other malignancies [4]. In addition, longitudinal studies confirm that persistent HR-HPV infection elevates risks of penile, anal, and oropharyngeal cancers for men [5]. Beyond its physical health implications, emerging evidence highlights the profound psychosocial burden of HPV infection, particularly anxiety, depression, and social isolation, which significantly impair women’s quality of life [6]. Those with lower educational attainment, unemployment, younger age, or unmarried/non-cohabiting status are disproportionately prone to adverse psychological responses—including shame, anxiety, stigma, and worry—upon receiving a positive HPV diagnosis [7]. HPV-positive results are typically preceded by abnormal gynecological cytology findings, a context that frequently triggers cancer-related fears, perceptions of injustice, and efforts to identify the source of infection [8]. Existing literature confirms that a positive HPV diagnosis increases psychological burden and anxiety [9–12]. While some studies suggest these psychological impacts may be transient, subsiding within months of diagnosis [9–12], others report persistent anxiety and mood disturbances long after initial diagnosis, challenging the assumption of time-limited effects [13]. Nevertheless, there is a lack of robust research on the association between female HPV infection and depression.

Female HPV infection induces chronic inflammation that drives cervical cancer through coordinated interactions among reactive oxygen/nitrogen species (ROS/RNS), cytokine/chemokine networks, growth factors, enzymatic regulators like cyclooxygenase (COX) and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), prostaglandins, and inflammation-modulating miRNAs [14]. In addition, the relationship between inflammatory pathways and depression pathogenesis is well-established, with pro-inflammatory states directly contributing to neuropsychiatric manifestations [15]. Clinical evidence demonstrates that interferon-alpha administration induces mood disturbances in almost half of patients, illustrating cytokine-mediated depression mechanisms [16, 17]. Modern antidepressants exert dual therapeutic effects by both reducing pro-inflammatory mediators (IL-6, TNF-α, CCL2) and enhancing anti-inflammatory IL-10 production, as demonstrated in longitudinal biomarker studies [15, 18].

However, the role that inflammation plays in the comorbidity of female HPV infection and depression remains poorly understood. This study aimed to explore the effect of inflammation on female HPV infection and depression in a cross-sectional adult cohort. The inflammatory markers used in this study included the complete blood count (CBC)-derived parameters: Neutrophil count, Lymphocyte count, Monocyte count, and Platelet count, along with their derived ratios and indices. These encompassed the Neutrophil-to-Lymphocyte ratio (NLR), Lymphocyte-to-Monocyte ratio (LMR), Platelet-to-Lymphocyte ratio (PLR), and the Systemic Immune-Inflammation Index (SII). These indices collectively reflect systemic inflammatory status through cellular immune interactions, with NLR and SII particularly recognized for their prognostic value in chronic diseases and immune dysregulation [19].

This study aims to: (1) investigate the association between female HPV infection, inflammatory markers and depression; (2) examine if inflammatory markers mediated the relationship between female HPV infection and depression. We hypothesized that (1) female HPV infection, inflammatory markers, and depression are correlated; (2) inflammatory markers could partially mediate effects of female HPV infection on depression.

Methods

Study design and data sources

All data in this analysis were extracted from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), a cross-sectional study to assess the overall health and nutrition status of children and adults in the United States. In this study, we obtained data from six survey cycles (2005–2006, 2007–2008, 2009–2010, 2011–2012, 2013–2014, 2015–2016) of the NHANES.

All NHANES studies passed the National Center for Health Statistics (NCHS) Ethics Review Board and written informed consent was obtained from all participants (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/about/erb.html). Individuals with missing data about alcohol consumption, smoking status, or essential covariates such as age, sex, ethnicity, education, marital status, pregnant status, poverty income ratio and BMI were excluded from the analysis. Given the age range for HPV polymerase chain reaction (PCR) test data was 18–59 years, we included female participants aged 18–59 years with complete data on HPV PCR test results. We also excluded individuals with missing data about inflammatory markers such as complete blood count and NLR, LMR, PLR, SⅡ. Finally, the study included 5515 participants.

Measurements of HPV

HPV infection was assessed by laboratory data. Samples of vaginal swabs were tested using Roche Linear Array HPV Genotyping test. Participants with “positive” test results were classified as HPV positive group, those with “negative” test results were classified as HPV negative group.

Detailed procedures and methods can be found in the NHANES website (https://wwwn.cdc.gov/Nchs/Data/Nhanes/Public/2015/DataFiles/HPVSWR_I.htm#LBDRPCR).

Measurements of depression

A nine-item depression screening instrument, also called the Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9) was administered to determine the frequency of depression symptoms over the past 2 weeks [20]. Response categories for the nine-item instrument “not at all,” “several days,” “more than half the days,” and “nearly every day” were given a point ranging from 0 to 3. The instrument incorporates DSM-IV depression diagnostic criteria, a dichotomous variable indicating no depression (PHQ-9 score < 10) or elevated depressive symptoms (PHQ-9 score ≥ 10) was created using a threshold score of 10 [21].

Measurements of inflammatory markers

In the 2005–2016 NHENES cycles, the CBC is performed on the Coulter® DxH 800 analyzer using the study participant’s EDTA blood tubes. The CBC including the Neutrophil count, the Lymphocyte count, the Monocyte count, the Platelet count was used in our study. NLR was obtained by calculating the ratio of the Neutrophil count to the Lymphocyte count. LMR was obtained by calculating the ratio of the Lymphocyte count to the Monocyte count. PLR was obtained by calculating the ratio of the Platelet count to the Lymphocyte count. The SII is a novel inflammatory biomarker as reported in the study [22]. SII is calculated using the following formula: SII = Platelet count×Neutrophil count/Lymphocyte count.

Covariates

This study included the following covariates: age, race, education, marital status, family poverty income ratio (PIR), smoking status, drinking status, pregnancy status at exam, body mass index (BMI), hypertension, diabetes and antidepressant use. We have categorized race into five categories: Mexican American, Non-Hispanic Black, Non-Hispanic White, Other Hispanic, and Other Race - Including Multi-Racial. Educational level was categorized as middle and high school, college and higher. Martial was categorized as married/living with partner, never married and widowed/divorced/separated. Income was quantified using the PIR, calculated by dividing household income by a factor specific to household size and composition, which was categorized as < 1, 1–3, and > 3 [23]. Other health-related variables included smoking (current smoking was defined as having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in lifetime and smoke every day or some days; never smoking was defined as having not smoked at least 100 cigarettes in lifetime; former smoking was defined as having smoked at least 100 cigarettes in lifetime and don’t smoke now) and drinking (former drink: had ≥ 12 drinks in 1 year and did not drink last year, or did not drink last year but drank ≥ 12 drinks in lifetime; never drink: had < 12 alcohol drinks in lifetime; now drink: drink in the last year). Pregnancy status was categorized as No and Yes for females at the time of MEC exam. BMI was categorized as < 18.5, 18.5–25, 25–30, and > 30. Health status involved hypertension and diabetes were directly obtained from the self-reported questionnaire. Antidepressant use was obtained from the self-reported questionnaire. NHANES participants were asked if they had taken any prescription drug in the past 30 days. Those who answered “yes” were asked to show the interviewer the medication containers of all prescription drugs. For each drug reported, the interviewer recorded the product’s complete name from the container. Antidepressants were identified using the second level of drug categorical codes, specifically code 249. Antidepressant use in our study was defined as the use of at least one antidepressant agent in the prior thirty days.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis utilized R software. In our study, “WTMEC2YR”, a sample weighting code for the MEC exam from 2005 to 2016, was used as a weighted variable. To account for the combination of six NHANES cycles (2005–2016), we calculated the composite weight by dividing each 2-year cycle weight by six and summing them across cycles. Detailed information about the survey sample design and methods for calculating weights can be found in this website (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/).

Descriptive statistics summarized categorical variables (e.g., HPV infection, depression) as frequency (percentage), while continuous variables such as inflammatory markers and age were expressed as Mean ± SD. To compare categorical variables between groups, the Chi-square test was employed. The t-test was used to analyze normally distributed continuous variables, while the Mann Whitney U test was utilized to compare skewed data between groups for continuous variables.

Weighted multiple logistic regression models evaluated associations between HPV infection (exposure) and depression (outcome). Weighted multiple linear regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between HPV infection and inflammatory markers. In our study, we constructed 3 models by adjusting for different confounding variables. The model 1 remained unadjusted and model 2 was adjusted for demographic covariates (age, race, education, martial, PIR) and BMI, model 3 was adjusted for all the covariates (age, race, education, martial, PIR, BMI, smoking, drinking, pregnancy, hypertension, diabetes and antidepressant use).

In order to better characterize the associations between inflammatory markers and depression, we performed restricted cubic spline and threshold effect analyses. When non-linearity was detected, threshold effects were investigated using two-piecewise linear regression models, with optimal inflection points determined by likelihood ratio tests. To objectively determine whether threshold effects significantly improved model fit beyond linear relationships, we employed ​likelihood ratio tests when non-linearity was detected. This nested model comparison approach evaluates whether a segmented (two-piecewise) regression provides a statistically superior fit to the data compared to a standard linear mode. The inflammatory markers were transformed into natural logarithm (ln) to achieve an approximately normal distribution and raw values were presented only for descriptive purpose.

Mediation analyses were performed to examine whether inflammatory markers mediated the relationship between the exposure variable (HPV infection) and outcome (Depression). Thousand bootstraps were used in our analysis. The indirect pathway effect, proportion of the mediating effect, and p-value for the mediating effect are all shown in the results. Subgroup analyses stratified by race, education, martial, PIR, smoking status, drinking status, BMI, Pregnancy were performed.

A bilateral P < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance in all analyses.

Results

Population characteristics

Out of 60,936 participants interviewed, 30,784 female participants were included. Exclusions included individuals with missing HPV testing and PHQ-9 data (n = 19708), missing data on covariates (n = 2457), missing data on inflammatory markers (n = 3104). Finally, a total of 5515 participants from NHANES (2005–2016). Figure 1 provides a comprehensive visualization of the inclusion and exclusion processes. Female participants who had an original PHQ-9 score ≥ 10 was considered women with depression. The study included a total of weighted population: 41,769,975, with 9.4% (n = 3931099) classified as having depression. The race/ethnicity distribution of participants was 64.04% non-Hispanic White, 12.39% non-Hispanic Black, 9.78% Mexican American, 6.24% other Hispanics, and 7.55% other race/multiple races. Correlations of female HPV infection with depression and inflammatory markers

Female HPV infection was significantly associated with depression (Table 2. β = 1.2902, 95% CI = 1.0186–1.6343, p = 0.0350) after adjusting for potential confounders (age, race, education, martial, PIR, BMI, smoking, drinking, pregnancy, diabetes, hypertension and antidepressant use). Among inflammatory markers, HPV infection was associated with monocyte counts, reaching significance in Model 1 (β = 0.0312, 95% CI: 0.0107–0.0518, p = 0.0033), Model 2 (β = 0.0257, 95% CI: 0.0051–0.0463 p = 0.0149, Model 3 (β = 0. 0.0194, 95% CI: 0.0021–0.0408 p = 0.0459). A transient association was observed for lymphocyte counts in the unadjusted Model 1 (β = 0.0327, 95% CI: 0.0109–0.0546, p = 0.0038), but this effect was nullified after covariate adjustments in Model 2 and Model 3 (p > 0.10). In addition, PLT counts demonstrated an initial significant association (β = 0.0186, 95% CI: 0.0020–0.0352, p = 0.0286 in Model 1 without adjustments; however, this effect vanished following covariate adjustments in Model 2 and Model 3(p > 0.10). No statistically significant associations were found between HPV infection and neutrophil counts or composite inflammatory indices (PLR, SII, LMR, NLR) across any model (p > 0.05 for all).

Mediating effect of inflammatory markers on female HPV infection and depression

As shown in Table S1 and Fig. 3, inflammatory markers significantly mediated the association between female HPV infection and depression (Neutrophil: proportion mediated [PM], 0.69%; average causal mediation effects [ACME] = 0.000137 [95% CI = 0.000131–0.000142], p < 0.0001; Lymphocyte: PM, 0.72%; ACME = 0.000144 [95% CI = 0.000138–0.000150], p < 0.0001; Monocyte: PM, 1.64%; ACME = 0.000327 [95% CI = 0.000313–0.000341], p < 0.0001; PLT: PM, 0.44%; ACME = 0.000089 [95% CI = 0.000074–0.000103], p < 0.0001; SⅡ: PM, 0.38%; ACME = 0.000077 [95% CI = 0.000071–0.000084], p < 0.0001; NLR: PM, 0.01%; ACME = −0.000001 [95% CI = −0.000002– −0.000001], p < 0.0001; LMR: PM, 0.13%; ACME = 0.000027 [95% CI = 0.000020–0.000033], p < 0.0001; PLR: PM, 0.41%; ACME = −0.000081 [95% CI = −0.000085 – −0.000077], p < 0.0001).

Subgroup analysis for the association between female HPV infection and depression

The overall analysis demonstrated a significant association between HPV infection and depression in women (Figure S1. OR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.5–2.29, p < 0.001). Stratified analyses revealed heterogeneity across subgroups: Non-Hispanic White women (Fig. S1. OR = 2.01, 95% CI: 1.46–2.76, p < 0.001) and Other Race/Multi-Racial women (Figure S1. OR = 2.61, 95% CI: 1.15–5.93, p = 0.025) exhibited the strongest associations, while no significant effects were observed in Mexican American, Non-Hispanic Black, or Other Hispanic subgroups (p > 0.05). College and higher-educated women (Figure S1. OR = 1.89, 95% CI: 1.39–2.57, p < 0.001) and those with middle/high school education (Figure S1. OR = 1.59, 95% CI: 1.20–2.11, p = 0.002) showed elevated risks, though education did not significantly modify the association (P for interaction = 0.412). Married/living with partner women had the higher risk (Figure S1. OR = 2.18, 95% CI: 1.66–2.85, p < 0.001), with marital status significantly interacting with the association (P for interaction = 0.019), whereas no interactions were observed for PIR, pregnancy, drinking, smoking, or BMI (P for interaction > 0.05).

Discussion

This study was to investigate the association between female HPV infection and depression in the United States and to determine whether this association is partly mediated by inflammatory markers. The results revealed a statistically significant positive correlation between female HPV infection and depression, underscoring that women with HPV infection were linked to an increased risk of depression. Female HPV infection is now considered to be a necessary cause for cervical cancer [24]. While the Pap smear stands as the cornerstone of cervical cancer prevention through early detection, mounting research reveals that routine screening participation imposes multidimensional burdens on women [25]. The procedure’s inherent physical demands—including discomfort from speculum insertion and cervical cell sampling—frequently coincide with psychological distress stemming from genital exposure anxiety and post-screening diagnostic uncertainty. Compounding these challenges are logistical hurdles such as clinic scheduling difficulties and privacy apprehensions in healthcare environments [26, 27]. These interwoven physiological, emotional, and practical barriers collectively undermine screening adherence across global populations, persisting despite decades of technological advancements in gynecological care. Given the complex interplay between female HPV infection and depression, further studies are critically needed to delineate the temporal and causal relationships between HPV infection and the risk of depression in women, particularly addressing neuroimmune interactions, stigma-mediated psychological sequelae, and socioeconomic mediators of mental health outcomes.

Our results also showed that female HPV infection was associated with higher Monocyte count in female participants. Monocytes circulate in the blood and are recruited to mucosal tissues or inflammation sites, where they can differentiate into monocyte-derived macrophages or monocyte-derived dendritic cells [28, 29]. Macrophages can be divided into two groups, namely the classical M1 and the alternative M2, which promote inflammation and tumor progression [30]. Later studies revealed that M2 (Tumor-associated macrophage, TAMs) were associated with high-risk HPV infection and were positively correlated with cervical carcinogenesis [31]. Our findings suggest that HPV-associated monocytosis may contribute to cervical carcinogenesis by fueling the tumor microenvironment with M2-polarized precursors. The elevated monocyte of women with HPV infection observed in our study may reflects systemic immune activation, where circulating monocytes are recruited to cervical lesions and undergo M2 polarization [31].

Additionally, our study found that inflammatory markers were associated with depression in a nonlinear pattern, and inflammatory markers were positively associated with depression after reaching the cut points. We identified a U-shaped relationship between inflammatory markers (SII, NLR, PLR, Lymphocyte) and female depression. When ln SII exceeded 6.17, a positive correlation was identified. A recent study demonstrated that when log2-SII was ≥ 9.71, SII showed a positive association with depression incidence and each unit increase in log2-SII corresponded to an 24% rise in depression incidence of female participants [32]. However, another study found that there is no non-linear association of SII with depression risk in adults, this study indicated that each 100 units increase in SII was associated with a 2% increase in the risk of depression [33]. We also found that when ln NLR was less than 0.45, a significant negative association with depression was observed. Conversely, when ln NLR exceeded 0.45, a positive correlation was identified. Previous study discovered that a possible U-shaped connection between NLR and depression in women. The risk of depression was lowest when the ln NLR was approximately 0.53 [34]. However, Moshui Shan et.al [35] found no significant correlation between NLR and depression after adjustment for all covariates. Interestingly, when they used PHQ-9 score to estimate the severity of depression, a U-shaped relationship between NLR and depression severity was observed in this study. In our study, when ln PLR was less than 4.88, a significant negative association with depression was observed. While the association above the cutoff (ln PLR > 4.88) lacked statistical significance (p > 0.05), the model’s overall differentiation was significant (log-likelihood p < 0.001), suggesting that PLR’s contribution to depression risk is partial. In addition, consistent with our results, a previous study indicated that there was a significant negative association between PLR and depression in all the models [35]. We also found that the risk of female depression was lowest when ln Lymphocyte was approximately 0.96. However, when ln Lymphocyte exceeded 0.96, a 334.14% increase in the probability of depression for each unit increase in ln Lymphocyte, which was highest among other inflammatory markers. Neuroinflammation, characterized by abnormal immune activation in the brain, is now widely acknowledged as being associated with major depressive disorder [36]. A Mendelian randomization (MR) study indicated that IgD + CD38dim %B cell, Activated Treg AC, HLA DR on CD14- CD16- and the other remaining 12 immune cells may lead to increased risk of major depressive disorder [37]. We found that elevated lymphocyte counts are associated with an increased risk of depression, potentially reflecting dysregulated immune activation. Our results are in general agreement with the literature supporting the association of elevated inflammatory markers with increased risk of female depression, demonstrating the important role of inflammation in the pathogenesis of depression.

Our findings suggest that inflammatory markers modestly mediate the association between female HPV infection and depression. Inflammation is hypothesized to play an important role in modulating female HPV infection or depression [38, 39]. In our study, Monocytes, Neutrophils and Lymphocytes demonstrated the strongest mediating roles, with monocyte levels showing the highest proportion mediated (PM = 1.64%), followed by lymphocytes (PM = 0.72%) and neutrophils (PM = 0.69%). PLT and SⅡ exhibited weaker but statistically significant mediation (PM = 0.44% and 0.38%, respectively), while NLR and LMR showed minimal mediation (PM ≤ 0.09%). Notably, PLR displayed a paradoxical negative mediation effect (ACME = −0.000085, PM = 0.41%), suggesting potential suppressor effects or context-dependent bidirectional immune interactions. The observed minimal mediation effect of inflammatory biomarkers in the HPV-depression pathway warrants critical contextualization within the study’s methodological constraints and theoretical frameworks. Several factors may explain this limited mediation. Firstly, the choice of peripheral biomarkers (e.g., NLR, PLR, LMR) may inadequately capture neuroinflammation mechanisms more directly relevant to depression pathogenesis, as peripheral-central immune coupling is often nonlinear and context-dependent. Secondly, the timing of biomarker measurement—a single baseline assessment—fails to account for dynamic fluctuations in inflammatory states induced by HPV persistence or episodic immune activation, thereby diluting temporal mediation effects. In addition, elevated levels of inflammatory markers may not be the dominant mechanism underlying the relationship between female HPV infection and depression. The cross-sectional design precludes robust causal mediation inference, as unmeasured confounders could bias effect estimates despite covariate adjustment. The contribution of elevated inflammatory marker levels due to female HPV infection to increased risk of depression may take longer to emerge.

This study is the first to identify inflammatory markers mediating the relationship between female HPV infection and depression. A central limitation of this study arises from its cross-sectional design, which inherently precludes definitive conclusions regarding whether the observed association between HPV infection and depressive symptoms reflects an acute psychological response or a chronic neuroinflammatory process. We used Neutrophil, Lymphocyte, Monocyte, Platelet, along with NLR, LMR, PLR, and the SII levels as markers of inflammation, which have the advantages of being economical and easy to use clinically. Although this study provides novel insights into the mediating role of inflammatory markers in the relationship between HPV infection and depression among women, with strengths including a large multi-ethnic sample and clinically practical biomarkers, its cross-sectional design limits causal inference among HPV infection, inflammation, and depression. Future longitudinal or experimental studies are needed to clarify temporal and causative pathways. Additionally, unmeasured confounders such as lifestyle factors, comorbidities, or specific HPV subtypes may influence these associations, warranting further investigation. Expanding research to include diverse populations and exploring broader inflammatory mechanisms or biomarkers could strengthen generalizability and deepen understanding of the biological interplay underlying HPV-related mental health outcomes.

This study showed that inflammatory markers (Neutrophil, Lymphocyte, Monocyte, Platelet, NLR, LMR, PLR, and SII) mediate the association between female HPV infection and depression, highlighting the potential role of immune dysregulation in HPV-related depressive symptoms. However, the cross-sectional design limits causal inference, and future longitudinal studies are needed to confirm whether female patients with HPV infection are susceptible to inflammation-driven depression. Additionally, mechanistic research should explore specific immune pathways (e.g., cytokine profiles, neuroinflammation) to better understand the biological basis of this relationship. Our results underscore the importance of integrating mental health screening and inflammatory biomarker assessment into HPV management protocols. Addressing both viral infection and psychological distress through multidisciplinary care may improve outcomes for women with HPV infection.
