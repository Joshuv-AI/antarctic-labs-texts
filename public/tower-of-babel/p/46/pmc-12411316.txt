First molecular characterization and haplotype analysis of Ligula pavlovskii (Cestoda: Diphyllobothriidae) from Podiceps cristatus in Türkiye: a novel geographic record with phylogenetic insights
Authors: Mehmet Öztürk, Can Nacar, Şinasi Umur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Ligulosis is an important parasitic disease of freshwater fish, causing serious economic losses in the fisheries industry. In this study, we report the distribution of Ligula pavlovskii in avian definitive hosts in Türkiye and the first genetically confirmed presence of this species in Podiceps cristatus (great crested grebe). To achieve this, the mitochondrial cox1 gene region was used for molecular characterization and phylogenetic assessment. The population structure was evaluated by applying haplotype diversity, DNA polymorphism, and neutrality tests to the sequences obtained. Visual representations of Ligula isolate from Türkiye were created using alluvial diagrams (molecularly identified) and sunburst diagrams (morphologically identified). Although only the cox1 marker was used in this study, the results provide reliable molecular support for species identification. The data obtained provide important contributions to the literature on the distribution and genetic diversity of Ligula spp. in Türkiye. Introduction

The genus Ligula (Cestoda: Diphyllobothriidae) exhibits a complex life cycle involving two intermediate hosts and one definitive host. These parasites inhabit the intestines of piscivorous birds. Freshwater copepods serve as first intermediate hosts, while freshwater fish act as second intermediate hosts (Kesik et al. 2024).

Species distribution within the genus Ligula varies considerably. Ligula intestinalis occurs throughout the Holarctic region; L. colymbi is predominantly Eurasian, while L. pavlovskiiDubinina, 1980 was initially reported from the Black Sea-Caspian region and has recently been documented in Europe (Dubinina 1980; Ryzhikov et al. 1985; Kesler et al. 2009; Nazarizadeh et al. 2023). Ligula pavlovskii commonly infects gobiid fish as second intermediate hosts, particularly Benthophilus stellatus (caspian tadpole goby), Neogobius fluviatilis (monkey goby), Neogobius melanostomus (round goby), Ponticola kessleri (kessler’s goby), Ponticola syrman (syrman goby), Pomatoschistus marmoratus (marbled goby), and Knipowitschia species (Yuryshynets et al. 2017; Vital et al. 2021). Definitive hosts include piscivorous birds, with morphological identification confirmed in Sterna albifrons (Ryzhikov et al. 1985). Experimental infections have demonstrated that Podiceps cristatus (great crested grebe) and Larus ridibundus (black-headed gull) also serve as competent final hosts (Ryzhikov et al. 1985).

Plerocercoid larvae of the genus Ligula typically attain considerable size within the fish abdominal cavity. These larvae cause compression atrophy of internal organs, including the liver and intestines (Arme and Owen 1968; Sweeting 1977; Capasso et al. 2024). The larvae also deplete host nutritional resources, leading to reduced growth rates, diminished body condition, cachexia, and increased mortality (Arme and Owen 1968; Carter et al. 2005). Infected fish exhibit behavioral modifications consistent with parasite manipulation to facilitate transmission to avian definitive hosts. These include surface swimming behavior, reduced locomotory activity, and increased vulnerability to predation by piscivorous birds (Poulin 2000; Gabagambi et al. 2019). One of the most significant pathological effects is reproductive impairment resulting from suppression of gonadal development, leading to considerable economic impact on fish populations (Carter et al. 2005; Bouzid et al. 2008). Plerocercoid larvae develop into adults within the intestines of avian definitive hosts. While infections are typically subclinical, severe cases may present with enteritis, intestinal lesions, emaciation, and decreased appetite (Fard et al. 2015).

Research on Ligula species in Türkiye is limited, with previous studies focusing exclusively on the second freshwater fish (intermediate host) (Fig. 1). Molecular-based investigations of infections in fish-eating birds (definitive hosts) are lacking in the literature. This represents a significant knowledge gap regarding the biogeographical distribution, species diversity, and host spectrum of Ligula species in Türkiye.Fig. 1Graphical representation of molecularly confirmed Ligula isolates from Türkiye using an alluvial diagram

This study investigated Ligula species infections in their definitive hosts in Türkiye using molecular approaches for the first time. The cox1 gene region of the obtained isolate was sequenced; haplotype analysis was conducted using the sequence data; and phylogenetic relationships with other Ligula isolates retrieved from GenBank were assessed using standard phylogenetic methods. Additionally, genetic diversity and population structure of Turkish isolates were evaluated through population genetic analyses. This study therefore provides insights into the status of Ligula species in Türkiye from systematic, epidemiological, and biogeographical perspectives.

Material and methods

This study aimed to identify Ligula spp. parasitic infections in wild avian species that consume fish and were admitted to the Department of Wild Animal Diseases, Faculty of Veterinary Medicine, Ondokuz Mayıs University, but failed to respond to administered therapeutic interventions. The study was conducted under official authorization from the Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry, General Directorate of Nature Conservation and National Parks (permit dated September 26, 2022, reference number: E-21264211–288.04–7092178).

A total of 74 individuals representing eight different taxonomic orders from 23 distinct wild bird species were utilized as study specimens (Table 1). These specimens were discovered deceased in the Kızılırmak Delta due to natural factors and transferred to the laboratory for post-mortem examinations. Following parasitological examinations, Ligula spp. specimens were detected. The recovered parasite samples were initially rinsed with 0.9% physiological saline solution and subsequently preserved in 70% ethyl alcohol solution for morphological identification. The posterior segments of each parasite sample were excised and preserved in 70% ethanol for molecular studies. Permanent slide preparations were stained with Semichon’s carmine dye following standard procedures and mounted using Canada balsam (Upton 2005). Taxonomic identification of the parasites was performed using light microscopy (Nikon Eclipse 80i), based on morphological characters previously established in the literature (Ryzhikov et al. 1985). Diagnostically significant morphological characteristics were measured and photographed using a camera (Nikon DS-L1) attached to the microscope. Genomic DNA was extracted from the last two proglottids of the Ligula cestode obtained from Podiceps cristatus using the GeneJet Genomic DNA Purification Kit (Thermo Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The extracted gDNA samples were stored at − 20 °C until molecular studies were conducted. For molecular identification of Ligula species, amplification was performed using COIA2 (5′-CATATGTTTTTGATTTTTTTGG-3′) as the forward primer and COIB2 (5′-AKAACATAATGAAAATGAGC-3′) as the reverse primer targeting the cox1 gene segment. The PCR mixture was prepared in a total volume of 50 μl, which consisted of 25 μl Maxima Hot Start PCR Master Mix (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), 2 μl of each primer (10 μM concentration), 3 μl genomic DNA (25–50 ng), 0.25 U Taq polymerase enzyme, and nuclease-free water to complete the total volume. The amplification protocol was based on the methodology of Bouzid et al. (2008) and carried out under the following conditions: initial denaturation for 3 min at 94 °C, followed by 40 cycles of 1 min denaturation at 94 °C, 1 min annealing at 46 °C, 1 min elongation at 72 °C, and a final extension for 7 min at 72 °C (Bouzid et al. 2008).

DNA purification and sequencing procedures were conducted by the commercial service provider Bm Laboratory Systems. Sequencing analyses using identical primers were performed by Macrogen (Amsterdam, The Netherlands) employing the BigDye Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing kit (Applied Biosystems) and ABI PRISM 3130xl automated sequencing system. Quality evaluation of chromatograms from DNA sequences obtained bidirectionally was performed with Vector NTI Advance 11.5 (Invitrogen) genetic analysis software. Forward and reverse sequences were assembled using the Contig Express module of the same software, and consensus nucleotide sequences were generated for each isolate. The obtained sequences were searched in the GenBank database (Altschul et al. 1997) via the blastn platform (http://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), and homology percentages of the isolates among themselves and with global isolates were determined. Multiple sequence alignment was performed using the BioEdit software (Hall 1999) with the Clustal W algorithm. Haplotype groups of the isolates were determined using DnaSP v6.10 (Rozas et al. 2017) software; subsequently, the median-joining network was constructed using PopART v.1.750 (Leigh and Bryant 2015).

Genetic distances among isolates were calculated using MEGA version 10.1.7 software based on the Kimura two-parameter (K2P) model (Kumar et al. 2004). Bayesian inference (BI) analysis was performed using MrBayes version 3.2.3 software (Ronquist et al. 2012) to elucidate phylogenetic relationships. The most appropriate DNA evolution model was determined using jModelTest version 2.1.6 (Darriba et al. 2012). Bayesian analyses were conducted with four Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) simulations running in parallel for a total of one million iterations, sampling every 100 iterations. The convergence status of the chains was assessed every 1000 generations. Before the majority rule-based consensus tree was constructed, the first 2500 samples in each analysis were treated as burn-in period and excluded from the calculations. Echinococcus oligarthrus (NC009461) was selected as the outgroup in Bayesian phylogenetic trees. The phylogenetic trees were displayed with FigTree software (version 1.4) (http://tree.bio.ed.ac.uk/software/figtree/).

For population genetic analysis, haplotype number (n), haplotype diversity (Hd), and nucleotide diversity (π) were calculated using DnaSP v6.10 software. Additionally, Tajima’s D test (Tajima 1989), Fu’s Fs test (Fu 1997), Fu and Li’s D and F test (Fu 1993), Strobeck’s S test (Strobeck 1987), and Achaz Y test (Achaz 2009) were performed using DnaSP v6.10 to evaluate the conformity of the sequences to neutral evolution expectations and to assess historical population demography.

The sequences of the cox1 gene region were successfully obtained from the L. pavlovskii isolate, which was morphologically identified and collected from the Podiceps cristatus. BLASTn analysis of the cox1 gene region in GenBank identified 16 isolates of L. pavlovskii (including PV639621) (Table 2). The haplotype analysis revealed that these isolates belong to 7 different haplotypes (Fig. 3). The intraspecific genetic distance between cox1 sequences of L. pavlovskii varies from 0.4 to 1.0 (see Sub 1), and the haplotype network showed clear geographic structuring among isolates. The Turkish isolate represented a unique haplotype, supporting a potentially localized lineage. The phylogenetic tree constructed based on cox1 gene sequences revealed well-supported clades descending from L. pavlovskii, other Ligula species, and nearby species. All L. pavlovskii isolates formed a monophyletic group and were clearly separated from other species with high bootstrap support. The optimal DNA model for the generated data set was selected as GTR + I + G. The obtained sequence data were recorded in GenBank under the accession number PV639621 (Fig. 4). The population genetic analyses of L. pavlovskii revealed the following patterns. Analysis of 16 isolates from a 396 bp fragment identified nine polymorphic sites and seven haplotypes. Haplotype diversity (Hd) and nucleotide diversity (π) were 0.842 and 0.00659, respectively, with an average number of pairwise differences (k) of 2.61 bp. These values indicate moderate levels of intraspecific genetic diversity. Tajima’s D statistic was slightly negative (D = − 0.14) but not statistically significant (p > 0.10), consistent with neutral evolution. Similarly, Fu’s Fs (− 0.927), Fu and Li’s D* (− 0.051), F* (− 0.087), and Achaz’s Y* (− 0.173) were negative but non-significant. Strobeck’s S value (0.874) also supported neutrality.

Discussion

In this study, the Ligula specimen (accession number PV639621) obtained from the host species Podiceps cristatus (great crested grebe) from Türkiye was placed in the L. pavlovskii clade with a high support value (97%) in phylogenetic analyses. This finding indicates that the sequence is clearly genetically distinct from other species. The fact that it forms a monophyletic group with other European specimens of L. pavlovskii (Hungary, Germany, and Poland) supports the phylogenetic integrity of this taxon. This first L. pavlovskii sequence reported from Türkiye suggests that the species is restricted not only to Central Europe but also has the potential to be more widely distributed in the Western Palearctic region. Furthermore, the fact that the specimen was isolated from an avian host suggests that fish-eating plays an important role in the life cycle of L. pavlovskii and supports the ecological plasticity of the species. The data obtained are consistent with previously reported studies (Waeschenbach et al. 2017) that point to genetic diversity and the presence of cryptic species among Ligula species. In this context, it can be concluded that morphologically similar Ligula species can be clearly separated at the molecular level, and this distinction should be taken into account for species definitions and epidemiological evaluations.

The median-joining haplotype network of L. pavlovskii isolates revealed seven distinct haplotypes (Fig. 3). Two dominant haplotypes, Hap-2 and Hap-4, were exclusively found in Hungarian and Ukrainian sequence samples, suggesting shared genetic ancestry in the Carpathian Basin. Conversely, German and Polish isolates formed separate haplotypes, indicating regional genetic differentiation. The presence of multiple haplotypes among Hungarian isolates suggests high genetic diversity in this region. The geographical distribution pattern shows that haplotype diversity is predominantly observed in Eastern Europe and the Black Sea region. The distinct haplotype identified suggests possible geographic structuring or local adaptation, in line with the patterns observed in Eastern European populations (Vital et al. 2021; Nazarizadeh et al. 2023).

The haplotype distributions determined in this study allow ecological and biogeographical inferences regarding the geographical distribution and genetic diversity of L. pavlovskii. Our sample harboring a unique haplotype indicates the presence of an endemic genetic lineage, while shared haplotypes between Ukraine and Hungary suggest genetic connectivity between populations from these regions. This pattern may be associated with the migration patterns of avian hosts that facilitate parasite dispersal. Türkiye is positioned along the Eastern Europe-Eastern Africa and Central Asia-Anatolia-Africa migratory routes (Karataş et al. 2021), and avian hosts likely facilitate the dispersal of L. pavlovskii across different regions via these routes. Therefore, these migration corridors may influence both the geographical spread of the parasite and the formation of genetic differentiation among populations. However, more comprehensive phylogeographic studies incorporating molecular data from both parasite and host bird species are needed to confirm these hypotheses.

The phylogenetic differentiation between L. pavlovskii and L. intestinalis observed in this study supports the taxonomic validity previously established by Dubinina (1980) and corroborated by recent molecular studies (Vital et al. 2021). High bootstrap support values and the formation of a distinct monophyletic clade confirm that L. pavlovskii represents a separate species-level lineage within the genus Ligula.

The majority of the studies on Ligula species in Türkiye are morphologically based and have been conducted in different geographical regions (Fig. 5; Sup 2). However, no studies on fish-eating birds, the definitive hosts that play a critical role in the life cycle of the parasite, were found in the literature. To date, only L. intestinalis has been identified in morphological and molecular studies. Ligula pavlovskii was reported morphologically for the first and only time in Türkiye by Öztürk (2000) in Gobius fluviatilis (Syn.: Neogobius fluviatilis). Ligula pavlovskii was found with an average prevalence of 4.7% on the Ukrainian coast of the Black Sea, 11.9% in the northwestern Black Sea, and 3.1% in the Dniester Delta region (Kvach 2010, 2005, 2002). Conclusion

This study provides the first molecular confirmation of L. pavlovskii in a definitive host bird species in Türkiye. The results provide valuable data on the distribution and genetic diversity of the species and emphasize the need for more extensive molecular research in the region. Sequence data (accession number: PV639621) were uploaded to GenBank, and the first record of L. pavlovskii from Türkiye was obtained. Haplotype analysis and DNA polymorphism tests were performed to evaluate the population structure of L. pavlovskii. In addition, previous molecular and morphological studies in Türkiye were visualized with alluvial and sunburst diagrams, respectively, and the current status of Ligula spp. parasites in the country was summarized.
