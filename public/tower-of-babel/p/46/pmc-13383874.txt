Identification and functional validation of a four-gene signature associated with radiotherapy resistance in oral squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Weipeng Tang, Shaonan Hu, Jianhao Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Integrative multi-omics approach: Combined single-cell RNA-seq, bulk transcriptomics (TCGA/GSE), and advanced bioinformatics (Scissor/hdWGCNA/RSF) to identify a novel radiotherapy-associated four-gene signature (KPNA2/P4HA1/ARL6IP1/TUBA1B) for OSCC.•Robust prognostic stratification: The four-gene model effectively stratified OSCC patients into high-/low-risk groups, with strong predictive performance in both TCGA training cohort (AUC=0.849 for 5-year survival) and GSE41613 validation cohort.•Radioresistance-specific expression: IHC validation confirmed significant upregulation of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B in radioresistant OSCC tissues compared with radiosensitive tissues and adjacent normal tissues.•Functional validation in vitro: The four genes were highly expressed in radioresistant OSCC cell lines (HSC-3/SCC9); their knockdown significantly enhanced radiosensitivity by impairing irradiation-induced metabolic activity, colony formation, and invasion in SCC9 cells.•Mechanistic insights: The signature correlates with tumor immune remodeling, malignant cell heterogeneity, oxidative stress, ferroptosis, and ECM remodeling, providing key clues to OSCC radioresistance mechanisms.Keywords: Oral squamous cell carcinoma, Radiotherapy resistance, Single-cell RNA sequencing, Prognostic risk model, KPNA2/P4HA1/ARL6IP1/TUBA1B

Introduction

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) is the predominant oral cavity malignancy and a major subtype of head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC). Despite therapeutic advances, OSCC still shows poor outcomes due to recurrence, metastasis, therapeutic resistance, and interpatient heterogeneity [[1], [2], [3]]. Its development involves genetic alterations, environmental exposures, chronic inflammation, and tumor microenvironmental remodeling [[4], [5], [6], [7]]. Although clinicopathological parameters remain clinically important, they cannot fully explain OSCC biological diversity or treatment response. This limitation is particularly relevant to the early clinical identification of radioresistant patients, because these parameters mainly reflect anatomical tumor burden and histopathological morphology rather than the intrinsic radiosensitivity of malignant cells. Patients with similar tumor stage, nodal status, histological grade, or margin status may still harbor markedly different molecular programs related to DNA damage repair, hypoxia adaptation, cancer stemness, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, metabolic reprogramming, immune escape, and tumor microenvironmental remodeling, all of which have been implicated in radiotherapy resistance [8]. Moreover, radioresistant OSCC cells may acquire more aggressive phenotypes and distinct transcriptional programs, suggesting that residual malignant phenotypes after irradiation cannot be reliably predicted by routine pathological assessment alone [9]. Therefore, robust molecular biomarkers and mechanistic targets are needed for improved risk stratification and individualized therapy.

Radiotherapy is an important treatment modality for OSCC, particularly in the postoperative adjuvant setting and in patients with locally advanced disease. Landmark clinical studies have established the value of postoperative radiotherapy or concurrent chemoradiotherapy in selected high-risk HNSCC patients [[10], [11], [12]]. However, intrinsic or acquired radioresistance remains a major cause of treatment failure. The biological basis of radioresistance involves multiple processes, including enhanced DNA damage repair, altered cell-cycle checkpoint regulation, apoptosis resistance, cancer stemness, epithelial–mesenchymal transition, hypoxia, oxidative stress adaptation, autophagy, metabolic reprogramming, extracellular vesicle-mediated communication, and immune escape [[13], [14], [15],8]. In OSCC specifically, radioresistant cell models and molecular profiling studies have revealed that resistant cells may acquire more aggressive phenotypes and distinct transcriptional programs [9,16]. Nevertheless, clinically applicable biomarkers that can predict OSCC radiotherapy response and guide radiosensitization strategies remain limited.

High-throughput sequencing has enabled OSCC heterogeneity to be analyzed at both bulk-tissue and single-cell resolution. Bulk RNA sequencing can identify differentially expressed genes, prognostic signatures, molecular subtypes, and risk models, but mainly reflects averaged signals from mixed cell populations [[17], [18], [19]]. Single-cell RNA sequencing overcomes this limitation by resolving malignant, immune, stromal, and endothelial cell populations, thereby revealing tumor ecosystem heterogeneity, differentiation states, cell–cell communication, and resistance-related microenvironmental features [[20], [21], [22], [23]]. Integrative tools such as Seurat, Harmony, Scissor, hdWGCNA, DESeq2, ssGSEA, CellChat, CytoTRACE, Monocle, and scTenifoldKnk further support cell-type annotation, phenotype-associated cell identification, co-expression analysis, immune profiling, trajectory inference, communication analysis, and virtual perturbation [[24], [25], [26], [27]]. Thus, this study focuses on radiotherapy resistance-associated malignant-cell programs rather than general OSCC heterogeneity, aiming to identify malignant-cell states and gene networks linked to unfavorable radiotherapy-related phenotypes and tumor-intrinsic radioresistance [23,27].

Several molecular candidates have been implicated in OSCC progression, therapeutic resistance, and poor prognosis. KPNA2 is involved in p53 nuclear translocation, autophagy, oncogenic features, and immune infiltration in OSCC or tongue squamous cell carcinoma [28,29]. P4HA1 is associated with extracellular matrix remodeling, hypoxia-related collagen deposition, invasion, and tumor aggressiveness [30,31]. ARL6IP1 has been linked to apoptosis regulation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, and cancer-cell survival [32,33], whereas TUBA1B is recognized as a prognostic biomarker related to tumor progression, immune modulation, and therapy response [34,35]. Together, these four genes may reflect a shared radioresistance-related malignant-cell program rather than isolated biomarkers. KPNA2 and ARL6IP1 are linked to autophagy and stress-survival regulation [29], whereas P4HA1 and TUBA1B may contribute to extracellular matrix remodeling, cytoskeletal plasticity, invasion, and therapy-response heterogeneity [36]. Thus, these genes may jointly represent stress adaptation, structural remodeling, matrix interaction, and maintenance of aggressive malignant states during radiotherapy resistance. Nevertheless, whether KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B jointly define a radiotherapy-associated malignant-cell program in OSCC and functionally contribute to radioresistance remains insufficiently clarified.

In this study, we integrated OSCC single-cell RNA-seq data with TCGA-OSCC and GEO bulk transcriptomic cohorts to identify radiotherapy-associated malignant-cell programs and construct a prognostic model. By combining Scissor, hdWGCNA, bulk RNA-seq differential expression analysis, and survival modeling, we established a four-gene signature composed of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B. We further characterized its associations with immune infiltration, immune checkpoints, malignant-cell heterogeneity, pseudotime trajectories, cell–cell communication, and virtual knockout-induced perturbations. Finally, IHC staining and in vitro functional assays were used to validate gene expression and biological relevance in radioresistant OSCC tissues and SCC9 cells, providing potential biomarkers and candidate functional mediators for OSCC radioresistance.

Material and methods

Single-cell RNA-seq data preprocessing, quality control, and initial clustering

Single-cell RNA-seq datasets GSE215403 and GSE172577 were processed using Seurat. Raw count matrices were imported with CreateSeuratObject and filtered using dataset-specific criteria: for GSE215403, cells with 200–6000 detected genes, fewer than 15,000 RNA counts, and mitochondrial gene percentage below 20% were retained; for GSE172577, cells with 200–7000 detected genes, fewer than 60,000 RNA counts, and mitochondrial gene percentage below 20% were retained. After quality control, the two datasets were integrated using Harmony, and doublets were removed with scDblFinder, leaving 87,483 high-quality singlet cells. Highly variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures with the vst method and nfeatures = 5000, followed by scaling, PCA, and selection of the top 20 principal components based on JackStraw, ScoreJackStraw, and elbow plot analyses. UMAP dimensionality reduction and clustering were then performed at a resolution of 0.2, generating 14 initial cell clusters for subsequent annotation and downstream analyses.

Cell-Type annotation, functional enrichment, and metabolic activity analysis

Cell-type annotation was performed using Seurat based on canonical marker genes from the literature and CellMarker2.0. The integrated dataset was annotated into eight major cell types: T cells, malignant cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, B cells, and mast cells. Representative markers included CD3D/CD2/CD3E for T cells, KRT5/KRT17/KRT14 for malignant cells, CD68/AIF1/CYBB for myeloid cells, LUM/COL1A1/DCN for fibroblasts, EPCAM/KRT19/KRT18 for epithelial cells, EGFL7/VWF/PECAM1 for endothelial cells, CD79A/MS4A1/IGHM for B cells, and TPSAB1/CPA3/HPGDS for mast cells. Marker expression was visualized using heatmaps, dot plots, and UMAP plots, and cell-type proportions were calculated. ReactomeGSA was used to assess pathway enrichment across annotated cell types, while scMetabolism was applied to quantify single-cell metabolic activity, with both results visualized as heatmaps. These analyses were exploratory and descriptive, aiming to characterize relative functional and metabolic differences among cell types rather than provide direct mechanistic or causal evidence, and were used to support subsequent radiotherapy-associated cell identification, gene screening, and experimental validation.

Identification of radiotherapy-associated cells and differential expression analysis

To identify radiotherapy-associated cell populations, Scissor was used to integrate TCGA-OSCC bulk transcriptomic data with the integrated OSCC single-cell matrix. The radiotherapy phenotype was defined by TCGA radiotherapy receipt status, encoded as 1 for received radiotherapy and 0 for no radiotherapy, with missing cases excluded; it reflected radiotherapy exposure rather than response, recurrence, or survival outcome. Cells were classified as Scissor+, Scissor−, or background cells, representing positive, negative, or no transcriptomic association with the radiotherapy phenotype. Scissor-classified cells were visualized by UMAP and summarized across annotated cell types using bubble plots. Because malignant cells contained the largest number of Scissor+ cells, they were selected for downstream analysis. Differential expression between Scissor+ and Scissor− malignant cells was performed using Seurat FindMarkers, with |log2FC| > 0.25 and p < 0.05 defining radiotherapy-associated DEGs, which were visualized by volcano plot.

Identification of malignant cell-associated modules and candidate genes

hdWGCNA was performed on malignant cells from the integrated OSCC single-cell dataset to identify malignant cell-associated co-expression modules. A soft-thresholding power of 28 was used to construct the network, the grey module was excluded, and kME values were calculated to identify hub genes within each module. Module activity was visualized by UMAP, dot plots, and violin plots. M2, M4, and M6 were selected because they showed preferential module eigengene expression in malignant cells on dot plot and violin plot analyses, while kME values were used for hub-gene identification rather than module selection. In parallel, DESeq2 was used to identify OSCC-related DEGs in the TCGA-OSCC cohort, with |log2FC| > 0.5 and adjusted p < 0.05 as thresholds, and the results were visualized using ggplot2 and ComplexHeatmap. Candidate genes were then obtained by intersecting OSCC-related DEGs, Scissor-derived radiotherapy-associated malignant-cell DEGs, and hdWGCNA-derived malignant cell-associated module genes. The 141 overlapping genes were prioritized because they were simultaneously linked to OSCC differential expression, radiotherapy-related malignant-cell features, and malignant-cell co-expression modules. This intersection strategy reduced redundant genes and enriched candidates with both disease relevance and malignant-cell specificity, thereby strengthening the biological relevance of the final prognostic model. Overall, the screening strategy followed a stepwise narrowing framework to gradually filter redundant genes at each stage: Scissor analysis identified radiotherapy phenotype-associated malignant cells, differential expression analysis defined radiotherapy-associated malignant-cell DEGs, hdWGCNA identified malignant cell-associated co-expression modules, and TCGA-OSCC DESeq2 analysis retained OSCC-related DEGs. Only the overlapping candidate genes passing all four screening layers were then further filtered by survival analysis, and the final gene set was incorporated into the RSF model to construct the prognostic signature. This hierarchical multi-layer screening framework clarifies the progressive gene filtration logic of each analytical tool.

Construction and validation of the prognostic risk model

Candidate gene expression profiles and survival data were extracted from the TCGA-OSCC cohort to identify prognosis-related genes. Univariate Cox regression was used to screen genes associated with overall survival, with p < 0.05 considered significant, and genes satisfying the proportional hazards assumption test with p > 0.05 were retained as prognosis-related candidates. Based on these genes, an RSF model was constructed using the randomForestSRC R package in the TCGA-OSCC training cohort. RSF was selected because it can handle censored survival data and capture potential nonlinear relationships and gene–gene interactions among prognostic genes, which may not be fully modeled by conventional linear approaches. The model was built with ntree = 600 and mtry = 1, and individual risk scores were calculated for both the TCGA-OSCC training cohort and the GSE41613 validation cohort using the predict function. Patients were stratified into high- and low-risk groups using optimal cutoffs determined by the survminer R package with minprop = 0.35; the cutoff values were 32.75561 for TCGA-OSCC and 36.01287 for GSE41613. Different cutoff values were used because the optimal thresholds were determined separately within each cohort, which may differ in sequencing platform, expression distribution, and patient characteristics. Therefore, these dataset-specific cutoffs were used for model validation, while a standardized cutoff would be required before clinical application. Kaplan–Meier analysis with the log-rank test was used to compare overall survival, and time-dependent ROC curves generated by survivalROC were used to evaluate 1-, 3-, and 5-year predictive performance. The expression levels of the final model genes were also compared between OSCC and control samples in TCGA-OSCC and visualized by box plots.

Immune infiltration and immune checkpoint analysis

Immune cell infiltration associated with the four-gene risk model was evaluated in the TCGA-OSCC cohort using ssGSEA with signatures of 28 immune cell types, implemented with the GSVA R package and visualized using ggplot2. Differences in immune infiltration between high- and low-risk groups were assessed by Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, with p 〈 0.05 considered significant and significance annotated as ns, *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001. Spearman correlation analysis was then performed using the psych R package to examine associations between KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, TUBA1B and differentially infiltrated immune cells, with |r| 〉 0.3 and p < 0.05 defined as significant. For immune checkpoint analysis, expression profiles of 79 checkpoint genes were extracted from TCGA-OSCC, differential expression between OSCC and control samples was assessed by Wilcoxon rank-sum test with p < 0.05, and significant checkpoint genes were further correlated with the four risk genes using Spearman analysis. Correlation results were visualized by dot plots, with dot color representing correlation coefficients and dot size indicating significance.

Malignant cell subclustering, differentiation state analysis, and pseudotime trajectory inference

To investigate intratumoral heterogeneity, malignant cells were extracted from the annotated single-cell dataset and re-clustered using UMAP. Marker genes for each subcluster were identified with Seurat FindAllMarkers, and functional annotation was performed based on GeneNMF C5 Gene Ontology biological process gene sets. According to representative markers and enriched functions, malignant cells were classified into four subtypes: KRT1+ malignant cells, TGFBI+ malignant cells, RACK1+ malignant cells, and CLU+ malignant cells. CytoTRACE analysis was used to assess differentiation potential, with higher scores indicating less differentiated states; score distributions, associated genes, and differentiation states were visualized by box plots and UMAP projection. Monocle was then applied to infer pseudotime trajectories of malignant cell subtypes, and the dynamic expression patterns of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B were plotted along the pseudotime axis across different developmental states.

Cell–Cell communication and virtual knockout analysis

Cell–cell communication was analyzed using CellChat to investigate ligand–receptor interactions among annotated OSCC cell populations, including T cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, B cells, mast cells, and four malignant cell subtypes: KRT1+ malignant, TGFBI+ malignant, RACK1+ malignant, and CLU+ malignant cells. A CellChat object was constructed from the normalized single-cell expression matrix and cell annotations, using CellChatDB.human as the reference database. Overexpressed ligands, receptors, and ligand–receptor pairs were identified, and communication probabilities, interaction numbers, interaction strengths, and incoming/outgoing signaling patterns were visualized using bubble plots, heatmaps, and circle plots. To examine radiotherapy-associated communication, Scissor+ cells were extracted and subjected to the same CellChat workflow. Virtual knockout analysis of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B was then performed in malignant cells using scTenifoldKnk. Gene co-expression networks were constructed, knockout-induced transcriptional perturbations were estimated, and significantly perturbed genes were visualized by bubble plots and bar plots. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were further used to summarize the biological processes, cellular components, molecular functions, and pathways affected by virtual knockout of the four risk model genes.

Immunohistochemical staining and quantitative analysis

Formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded human OSCC specimens and adjacent normal tissues were collected from Southern Medical University with ethical approval from the Ethics Review Committee of Southern Medical University (approval no EC—CT-[2022]21), and written informed consent was obtained. The samples included adjacent normal tissues, radiosensitive OSCC tissues, and radioresistant OSCC tissues, with 12 cases in each group. Radiosensitive cases were defined as patients with local disease control after radiotherapy, whereas radioresistant cases were defined as patients with documented local recurrence or disease progression based on clinical and imaging follow-up records. Tissues were fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 4 μm, deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-mediated antigen retrieval in citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide for 10 min, followed by goat serum blocking for 30 min. Sections were incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against KPNA2 (Proteintech; Cat. No 10,819-1-AP; 1:200), P4HA1 (Proteintech; Cat. No 12,658-1-AP; 1:200), ARL6IP1 (Abcam; Cat. No ab24228; 1:100), and TUBA1B/alpha-tubulin (Proteintech; Cat. No 11,224-1-AP; 1:200), followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (ZSGB-BIO; PV-9001) for 30 min at room temperature. Staining was visualized with DAB, counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared, and mounted. Negative controls were prepared by replacing the primary antibody with PBS or species-matched rabbit IgG, and positive controls included FFPE tissues known to express the corresponding targets and preliminarily confirmed positive OSCC sections. Images were captured using an Olympus BX53 microscope, and positive staining areas were quantified with ImageJ by investigators blinded to tissue grouping.

Cell culture and irradiation treatment

Human oral keratinocyte HOK cells were obtained from ScienCell Research Laboratories (Carlsbad, CA, USA). Human OSCC cell lines CAL-27, SCC25, and SCC9 were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA), and HSC-3 cells were obtained from the Japanese Collection of Research Bioresources Cell Bank (JCRB Cell Bank, Osaka, Japan). HOK cells were cultured in oral keratinocyte medium (ScienCell Research Laboratories, Carlsbad, CA, USA), while OSCC cell lines were maintained in DMEM or DMEM/F12 medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and 1% penicillin-streptomycin solution (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified incubator containing 5% CO₂. For irradiation treatment, cells were seeded and allowed to adhere for 24 h before exposure to X-ray irradiation. CAL-27, SCC25, HSC-3, and SCC9 cells were irradiated with 0, 2, 4, or 6 Gy, and cellular metabolic activity was measured 24 h after irradiation. SCC9 cells were irradiated with 6 Gy after siRNA transfection, and cellular metabolic activity was detected 24 h after irradiation.

Quantitative real-time PCR

HOK, CAL-27, SCC25, HSC-3, and SCC9 cells were collected when they reached approximately 70–80% confluence. SCC9 cells were collected 48 h after siRNA transfection to evaluate knockdown efficiency. Total RNA was extracted using TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA), and RNA concentration and purity were determined using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Complementary DNA was synthesized using a reverse transcription kit, such as PrimeScript RT reagent Kit (Takara Bio Inc., Shiga, Japan) or HiScript III RT SuperMix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed using SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme Biotech, Nanjing, China; or Takara Bio Inc., Japan) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). The mRNA expression levels of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B were normalized to GAPDH, and relative expression was calculated using the 2^−ΔΔCt method. PCR primers were synthesized by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The primer sequences used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table S1. Each reaction was performed in triplicate, and melting curve analysis was used to confirm amplification specificity.

siRNA transfection

For gene knockdown experiments, SCC9 cells were seeded into six-well plates and transfected with specific small interfering RNAs targeting KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, or TUBA1B, or with a negative control siRNA. The siRNAs were synthesized by GenePharma Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) or RiboBio Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China). Transfection was performed using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX Transfection Reagent (Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Briefly, SCC9 cells were seeded into six-well plates at a density of 2.0 × 10⁵ cells/well and transfected when cell confluence reached approximately 50–60%. siRNAs were used at a final concentration of 50 nM, and Lipofectamine RNAiMAX was diluted in Opti-MEM reduced-serum medium (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) before mixing with siRNA. The siRNA–lipid complexes were incubated for 10–15 min at room temperature and then added dropwise to the cells. After 6 h, the medium was replaced with complete culture medium. After 48 h of transfection, the knockdown efficiency was verified by qRT-PCR. For radiosensitivity evaluation, transfected SCC9 cells were subsequently exposed to 6 Gy irradiation, and cell metabolic activity was detected using the CCK-8 assay.

CCK-8 assay

Cellular metabolic activity was assessed using a Cell Counting Kit-8 assay (CCK-8; Dojindo Laboratories, Kumamoto, Japan). CAL-27, SCC25, HSC-3, and SCC9 cells were seeded into 96-well plates and cultured for 24 h to allow cell attachment. For the radiosensitivity screening assay, cells were seeded into 96-well plates at 3.0 × 10³ cells/well in 100 μL complete medium. For siRNA-based radiosensitivity experiments, transfected SCC9 cells were reseeded into 96-well plates at the same density before irradiation. Each experimental condition included at least five technical replicate wells, and each experiment was independently repeated three times. The cells were then exposed to 0, 2, 4, or 6 Gy irradiation. At 24 h after irradiation, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution was added to each well and incubated at 37 °C for 1–2 h. SCC9 cells were transfected with siRNAs targeting KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, or TUBA1B for 48 h, followed by 6 Gy irradiation. At 24 h after irradiation, CCK-8 detection was performed as described above. The absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a BioTek Synergy H1 Microplate Reader (Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and relative cellular metabolic activity was calculated based on the absorbance values.

Colony formation assay

For the colony formation assay, SCC9 cells were seeded into six-well plates (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA) and cultured for 24 h before transfection. At 48 h after transfection, SCC9 cells were exposed to 6 Gy X-ray irradiation using an X-RAD 320 biological irradiator (Precision X-Ray Inc., North Branford, CT, USA). After irradiation, cells were trypsinized with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), reseeded into six-well plates at 1000 cells/well for each group, and cultured for 10–14 days to allow colony formation. The medium was replaced every 3 days. Colonies were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 30 min. After washing with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), colonies containing >50 cells were counted and photographed. The colony survival fraction was calculated based on the number of colonies formed relative to the corresponding control group. Quantitative analysis was performed using ImageJ software.

Transwell invasion assay

The invasive capacity of SCC9 cells was evaluated using a Matrigel-coated Transwell invasion assay. At 48 h after transfection, cells were exposed to 6 Gy X-ray irradiation. After irradiation, cells were cultured for an additional 24 h, harvested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution (Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA), and resuspended in serum-free DMEM/F12 medium. The upper chambers of 24-well Transwell inserts with 8-μm pores (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA) were pre-coated with Matrigel matrix (Corning Inc., Corning, NY, USA), which was diluted at 1:8 in cold serum-free DMEM/F12 medium, and 50 μL of diluted Matrigel was added to each upper chamber and allowed to polymerize at 37 °C for 1 h. SCC9 cells were then seeded into the upper chamber, while medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; Gibco, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) was added to the lower chamber as a chemoattractant. After incubation at 37 °C with 5% CO₂ for 24 h, non-invaded cells on the upper surface of the membrane were gently removed with cotton swabs. Invaded cells on the lower surface were fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 20 min and stained with 0.1% crystal violet solution (Solarbio, Beijing, China) for 30 min. Representative images were captured using an Olympus BX53 light microscope (Olympus Corporation, Tokyo, Japan). For quantification, five randomly selected microscopic fields per membrane were photographed at 200× magnification, and invaded cells were counted using ImageJ software by investigators blinded to group allocation. The relative invasive capacity was calculated and compared among the indicated groups.

Statistical analysis

All bioinformatics analyses were performed in R. Differential expression was analyzed using Seurat for single-cell data and DESeq2 for bulk RNA-seq data; radiotherapy-associated DEGs were defined as |log2FC| > 0.25 and p 〈 0.05, and bulk RNA-seq DEGs as |log2FC| 〉 0.5 and adjusted p < 0.05. Prognosis-related genes were screened by univariate Cox regression, followed by random survival forest modeling, Kaplan–Meier analysis with log-rank tests, and time-dependent ROC analysis. Immune differences were assessed by Wilcoxon rank-sum tests, correlations by Spearman analysis, and enrichment by ReactomeGSA, GO, and KEGG analyses. Experimental data are shown as mean ± standard deviation from at least three independent experiments. qRT-PCR, CCK-8, colony formation, Transwell invasion, and IHC data were analyzed using GraphPad Prism. Two-group comparisons used unpaired Student’s t-test, and multiple-group comparisons used one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05 and denoted as ns, not significant; *, p < 0.05; **, p < 0.01; ***, p < 0.001; and ****, p < 0.0001.

Results

Quality control, batch correction, and initial clustering of OSCC single-cell transcriptomic data

To establish a reliable OSCC single-cell transcriptomic atlas, scRNA-seq datasets GSE215403 and GSE172577 were processed through quality control, Harmony-based batch correction, scDblFinder doublet removal, and dimensionality reduction. After filtering low-quality cells based on detected genes, total RNA counts, and mitochondrial gene percentage, 9080 doublets were removed and 87,483 singlet cells were retained from 96,563 initial cells for downstream analyses (Fig. 1A–C). Subsequently, 5000 highly variable genes were identified using FindVariableFeatures, including MUC7, ZG16B, STATH, IGLC2, PIP, IGHA1, HBB, SPRR2F, FDCSP, and C6orf58 (Fig. 1D). After scaling and PCA, JackStraw analysis and the elbow plot supported the selection of the top 20 principal components (Fig. 1E). UMAP clustering was then performed at a resolution of 0.2, generating 14 cell clusters labeled 0–13 (Fig. 1F). These results indicate that a high-quality integrated OSCC single-cell dataset was generated for subsequent annotation, radiotherapy-associated cell identification, and functional analyses.

Cell-Type annotation and functional characterization of the integrated OSCC single-cell atlas

Based on canonical markers and CellMarker2.0, the integrated OSCC single-cell dataset was annotated into eight major cell types: T cells, malignant cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, B cells, and mast cells. Representative markers, including CD3D/CD2/CD3E, KRT5/KRT17/KRT14, CD68/AIF1/CYBB, LUM/COL1A1/DCN, EPCAM/KRT19/KRT18, EGFL7/VWF/PECAM1, CD79A/MS4A1/IGHM, and TPSAB1/CPA3/HPGDS, supported reliable annotation (Fig. 2A). UMAP visualization confirmed distinct cell-type distributions (Fig. 2B), and malignant cells accounted for the largest proportion of cells (49.94%), followed by T cells (23.05%) and myeloid cells (10.29%) (Fig. 2C). ReactomeGSA analysis revealed cell-type-specific enrichment of pathways related to alanine metabolism, NADPH regeneration, biogenic amine metabolism, NEIL3-mediated interstrand crosslink repair, PAPS transport and metabolism, and electric transmission across gap junctions (Fig. 2D). scMetabolism further showed heterogeneous metabolic activity, with malignant and epithelial cells displaying relatively higher activity across multiple metabolic pathways (Fig. 2E). This higher metabolic activity may reflect metabolic reprogramming and active tumor-related epithelial and malignant cell states that support proliferation, biosynthesis, stress adaptation, and survival. Because radiotherapy response is influenced by metabolic adaptation, oxidative stress balance, and repair capacity, these exploratory metabolic scores provide descriptive context for the subsequent identification of malignant cells as the major radiotherapy-associated population, but should not be interpreted as direct mechanistic evidence. Together, these findings define the cellular composition, functional heterogeneity, and metabolic features of the integrated OSCC single-cell atlas.

Identification of radiotherapy-associated malignant cells and differentially expressed genes in OSCC

To identify radiotherapy-associated cell populations, Scissor integrated TCGA-OSCC radiotherapy phenotype information with the OSCC single-cell expression matrix. The annotated UMAP showed eight major cell types, including T cells, malignant cells, myeloid cells, fibroblasts, epithelial cells, endothelial cells, B cells, and mast cells (Fig. 3A). Scissor classified cells into Scissor+, Scissor−, and background populations (Fig. 3B). Scissor+ cells were interpreted as cells transcriptomically and positively associated with the radiotherapy-related phenotype, rather than clinically confirmed radioresistant cells. Malignant cells contained the largest number of Scissor+ cells, with 907 cells, and were selected for downstream analysis because they are the direct target of radiotherapy and central to tumor-intrinsic radiosensitivity or radioresistance (Fig. 3C). Differential expression analysis between Scissor+ and Scissor− malignant cells identified 4968 DEGs, including 3160 upregulated and 1808 downregulated genes in Scissor+ cells. The volcano plot highlighted representative upregulated genes, including RPL35A, FTL, and TXN, and downregulated genes, including S100A7, KRT6C, and DSG1 (Fig. 3D). These results suggest that radiotherapy-associated malignant cells exhibit distinct transcriptional features, supporting subsequent identification of radiotherapy-related molecular candidates in OSCC.

Identification of malignant cell-associated gene modules and candidate genes in OSCC

To identify malignant cell-associated gene modules, hdWGCNA was performed on malignant cells from the integrated OSCC single-cell dataset. A soft-thresholding power of 28 was selected to construct the weighted co-expression network (Fig. 4A), and hierarchical clustering identified seven modules after excluding the grey module (Fig. 4B). kME analysis identified hub genes within each module (Fig. 4C), and UMAP visualization showed distinct module activity patterns (Fig. 4D). Dot plot and violin plot analyses showed that M2, M4, and M6 were preferentially associated with malignant cells, with M2 showing the highest expression in malignant cells; merging these three modules yielded 293 malignant cell-associated genes (Fig. 4E–F). In parallel, DESeq2 analysis of TCGA-OSCC identified 7191 DEGs between OSCC and control samples, including 3550 upregulated and 3641 downregulated genes (Fig. 4G–H). Intersection of OSCC DEGs, Scissor-derived radiotherapy-associated malignant-cell DEGs, and hdWGCNA-derived malignant cell-associated genes identified 141 overlapping candidate genes for subsequent prognostic modeling and mechanistic analyses (Fig. 4I).

Construction and validation of a four-gene prognostic risk model in OSCC

Among the 141 candidate genes, univariate Cox regression and proportional hazards testing identified four prognostic genes: KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B, all with hazard ratios greater than 1, indicating that higher expression was associated with increased survival risk (Fig. 5A). Based on these genes, an RSF model was constructed in the TCGA-OSCC training cohort and applied to both TCGA-OSCC and GSE41613 to calculate individual risk scores. Kaplan–Meier analysis showed significantly poorer overall survival in the high-risk group in both cohorts (Fig. 5B). Time-dependent ROC analysis showed AUC values of 0.811, 0.839, and 0.849 for 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival in TCGA-OSCC, and 0.617, 0.636, and 0.621 in GSE41613, respectively (Fig. 5C). In addition, KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B were significantly upregulated in OSCC tissues compared with control tissues in TCGA-OSCC (Fig. 5D). These findings indicate that the four-gene RSF-based model has prognostic relevance and may identify biomarkers associated with OSCC progression and unfavorable clinical outcome.

Immune infiltration and immune checkpoint landscape associated with the four-gene risk model in OSCC

Among 79 immune checkpoint genes, 38 were differentially expressed between OSCC and control samples, including 35 upregulated in OSCC and three upregulated in controls (Fig. 6D). BTN2A1 and CD276 were positively correlated with multiple risk model genes (Fig. 6E), suggesting that the four-gene model is associated with immune infiltration and checkpoint regulation in OSCC.

Identification and characterization of malignant cell subtypes in OSCC

To characterize malignant-cell heterogeneity in OSCC, malignant cells were extracted and re-clustered by UMAP, identifying four subclusters: KRT1+ malignant cells associated with skin development, TGFBI+ malignant cells associated with RNA processing, RACK1+ malignant cells associated with translation, and CLU+ malignant cells associated with apoptotic processes (Fig. 7A). CytoTRACE analysis showed that TGFBI+ malignant cells had the highest score, indicating a less differentiated state, followed by KRT1+ malignant cells. Genes positively correlated with CytoTRACE scores included RPL37, RPL35A, RPL8, FAU, RPS28, RPS16, RPL39, RPS15, RPS29, and RPLP1, whereas negatively correlated genes included ATP5ME, ATP5MPL, ELOB, ATP5MG, ATP5MD, EEF1G, IGKC, ATP5F1E, RACK1, and MTRNR2L12 (Fig. 7B). Monocle pseudotime analysis revealed distinct developmental states, with TGFBI+ malignant cells mainly located in state 1, suggesting an early or less differentiated population (Fig. 7C). Along pseudotime, P4HA1 gradually decreased, while KPNA2, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B showed dynamic increase–decrease–increase patterns (Fig. 7D). These results indicate pronounced malignant-cell heterogeneity and distinct temporal expression patterns of the four risk genes during malignant progression.

CellChat and virtual knockout analyses reveal communication networks and functional perturbations in malignant cells of OSCC

CellChat analysis was performed to examine communication among major OSCC cell populations and four malignant subtypes: KRT1+ malignant, TGFBI+ malignant, RACK1+ malignant, and CLU+ malignant cells. In the overall network, TGFBI+ malignant cells showed high outgoing and incoming signaling, while myeloid cells had the strongest incoming activity; KRT1+, TGFBI+, and CLU+ malignant cells displayed abundant interactions (Fig. 8A). TGFBI+ malignant cells showed stronger IGFBP/CALCR outgoing and EGF/PTN/MK/CypA incoming signaling; KRT1+ malignant cells showed KLK/GALECTIN signaling; RACK1+ malignant cells showed MIF signaling; and CLU+ malignant cells showed PTN and IL1-related signaling. Key ligand–receptor pairs included MIF–(CD74+CXCR4), MIF–(CD74+CD44), and PPIA–BSG (Fig. 8B). In Scissor+ cells, CLU+ malignant cells showed high outgoing and incoming signaling, myeloid cells again showed the strongest incoming activity, and KRT1+, TGFBI+, and CLU+ malignant cells had high communication events (Fig. 8C). Scissor+ communication involved CypA, PROS, MIF, CD137, BMP, PTN, KIT, MK, and prominent MIF–(CD74+CXCR4), MIF–(CD74+CD44), and PPIA–BSG pairs (Fig. 8D). Virtual knockout of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B identified top perturbed genes including P4HA1, ATP5F1E, RACK1, KPNA2, TUBA1B, ARL6IP1, FTL, FTH1, CLPTM1L, and AKR1C3 (Fig. 8E). Enrichment analysis showed involvement of xenobiotic response/metabolism, collagen-containing ECM, endoplasmic reticulum lumen, oxidoreductase activity, glutathione metabolism, ferroptosis, ROS-related chemical carcinogenesis, apoptosis, and ECM–receptor interaction (Fig. 8F). These findings suggest that TGFBI+ and CLU+ malignant cells are active communication hubs, while the four risk genes may affect OSCC radioresistance through metabolic stress, oxidative stress, ferroptosis-related pathways, and ECM remodeling.

KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and tuba1b are significantly upregulated in radioresistant OSCC tissues

To further validate the expression patterns of radiation-related key genes in oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC) tissues, immunohistochemical staining was performed to detect the expression of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B in adjacent normal tissues, radiosensitive OSCC tissues, and radioresistant OSCC tissues. The results showed that all four proteins exhibited weak positive staining in adjacent normal tissues, whereas their expression levels were increased in OSCC tissues. Notably, the staining intensity and positive staining area in radioresistant OSCC tissues were markedly higher than those observed in radiosensitive OSCC tissues (Fig. 9A). Further quantitative analysis demonstrated that the positive staining areas of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B were significantly elevated in radiosensitive OSCC tissues compared with adjacent normal tissues, and were further significantly increased in radioresistant OSCC tissues (Fig. 9B). These findings suggest that KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B may be closely associated with the radioresistant phenotype of OSCC and could serve as potential histological biomarkers for predicting radiotherapy response in OSCC.

Radiation-Related key genes are highly expressed in OSCC cell lines and associated with differential radiosensitivity

To further evaluate the expression characteristics of radiation-related key genes in OSCC cells, the mRNA expression levels of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B were examined in HOK cells and four OSCC cell lines. The results showed that, compared with HOK cells, all four genes were significantly upregulated in OSCC cell lines, with relatively higher expression levels observed in HSC-3 and SCC9 cells (Fig. 10A). Subsequently, to compare the radiosensitivity of different OSCC cell lines, CAL-27, SCC25, HSC-3, and SCC9 cells were exposed to different doses of irradiation, and changes in cellular metabolic activity were assessed. The results demonstrated that cellular metabolic activity decreased in a dose-dependent manner with increasing irradiation doses in all four OSCC cell lines. Among them, CAL-27 and SCC25 cells exhibited a more pronounced reduction in metabolic activity, indicating higher radiosensitivity, whereas HSC-3 and SCC9 cells maintained relatively higher metabolic activity under the same irradiation doses, suggesting stronger radioresistance (Fig. 10B). Collectively, these findings indicate that KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B are upregulated in OSCC cells and may be associated with the radioresistant phenotype of OSCC.

Knockdown of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, or tuba1b enhances the radiosensitivity of radioresistant SCC9 cells

To further investigate whether radiation-related key genes contribute to the radioresistant phenotype of OSCC cells, SCC9 cells, a relatively radioresistant OSCC cell line, were selected for gene knockdown experiments. SCC9 was chosen because it showed a stable radioresistant phenotype and was suitable for siRNA transfection and downstream CCK-8, colony formation, and Transwell assays. The knockdown efficiency of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B was first verified by qRT-PCR. Compared with the si-Control group, transfection with specific siRNAs significantly reduced the mRNA expression levels of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B, confirming successful gene silencing in SCC9 cells (Fig. 11A). Subsequently, the effects of gene knockdown on cellular response to irradiation were evaluated. The results showed that irradiation at 6 Gy significantly decreased the relative metabolic activity of SCC9 cells, while knockdown of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, or TUBA1B further reduced cellular metabolic activity under irradiation compared with the si-Control + 6 Gy group (Fig. 11B). Notably, knockdown of KPNA2, ARL6IP1, or TUBA1B alone had no significant effect on cellular metabolic activity, whereas P4HA1 knockdown alone caused a moderate but significant decrease. These findings suggest that KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B may participate in maintaining the radioresistant phenotype of SCC9 cells, and their knockdown enhances irradiation-induced suppression of cell viability.

(B) Relative metabolic activity of SCC9 cells after knockdown of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, or TUBA1B with or without 6 Gy irradiation. Knockdown of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, or tuba1b suppresses colony formation and enhances radiosensitivity in radioresistant SCC9 cells

To determine whether KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B contribute to the radioresistant phenotype of SCC9 cells, colony formation assays were performed after siRNA-mediated knockdown with or without 6 Gy irradiation. Representative images showed abundant colony formation in the si-Control group, while 6 Gy irradiation markedly reduced colony formation (Fig. 12A). Under non-irradiated conditions, KPNA2 or ARL6IP1 knockdown slightly decreased colony formation and significantly reduced the survival fraction, whereas P4HA1 or TUBA1B knockdown showed no obvious effect. Importantly, compared with the si-Control + 6 Gy group, knockdown of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, or TUBA1B combined with 6 Gy irradiation further reduced colony formation and survival fraction (Fig. 12B). These results suggest that the four genes help maintain clonogenic survival after irradiation, and their knockdown enhances the radiosensitivity of radioresistant SCC9 cells.

To assess the effects of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B on invasion and radiotherapy response, SCC9 cells were subjected to individual gene knockdown followed by Transwell assays with or without 6 Gy irradiation. Discussion

Radiotherapy resistance is a major challenge in OSCC, leading to recurrence, metastasis, and poor survival. OSCC shows marked molecular heterogeneity, genomic complexity, and diverse tumor microenvironmental features [1,6,37,3]. Although postoperative radiotherapy and concurrent chemoradiotherapy improve locoregional control in selected patients [3], intrinsic or acquired radioresistance remains common [38,39,12]. Radioresistance involves DNA repair, checkpoint activation, apoptosis resistance, hypoxia, EMT, cancer stemness, metabolic reprogramming, extracellular vesicle-mediated communication, and immune escape [9,[13], [14], [15],40]. Here, we identified a radiotherapy-associated four-gene signature, KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B, with prognostic relevance and potential links to OSCC radioresistance. Although the model performed well in the TCGA-OSCC training cohort, its predictive accuracy was moderate in the external GSE41613 cohort, which may be related to cohort heterogeneity, sample size, platform differences, incomplete treatment information, and differences in follow-up. Therefore, further validation in larger, well-annotated OSCC cohorts is needed before clinical application.

The integration of bulk RNA-seq and single-cell RNA-seq allowed us to identify candidate genes with both clinical relevance and cellular specificity. Bulk transcriptomic analysis is useful for identifying differentially expressed genes, prognostic signatures, and radiotherapy-related biomarkers, but it mainly reflects averaged signals from mixed cellular populations [[17], [18], [19]]. In contrast, single-cell RNA sequencing provides cell-type-resolved information and enables the characterization of malignant cells, immune cells, fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and other stromal components within the tumor ecosystem [22,23]. In our analysis, malignant cells contained the largest number of Scissor+ cells associated with the radiotherapy phenotype, suggesting that intrinsic malignant-cell transcriptional states may be central to OSCC radioresistance. This finding is consistent with the value of integrative computational approaches such as Seurat, Harmony, Scissor, hdWGCNA, WGCNA, and DESeq2 for batch correction, phenotype-associated cell identification, co-expression module discovery, and differential expression analysis [[24], [25], [26]]. By intersecting OSCC-related DEGs, Scissor-defined radiotherapy-associated DEGs, and malignant cell-associated module genes, our study prioritized genes that were simultaneously linked to OSCC progression, radiotherapy phenotype, and malignant-cell biology.

The four-gene signature was closely associated with immune remodeling, malignant-cell heterogeneity, and stress-related pathways. As the tumor microenvironment critically shapes tumor progression, therapeutic resistance, and immunotherapy response [20], our immune infiltration and checkpoint analyses suggest that radioresistant malignant-cell states may interact with the immune microenvironment. The association between risk genes and immune checkpoints, particularly CD276-related patterns, is biologically relevant because CD276 promotes immune evasion in squamous cell carcinoma stem-like cells [41]. In addition, the high-risk group showed broad immune-cell alterations, with most significantly changed immune populations displaying lower infiltration scores, suggesting a relatively weakened antitumor immune context. The upregulation of multiple immune checkpoint genes in OSCC, together with the positive correlations of BTN2A1 and CD276 with several risk model genes, further indicates that the four-gene signature may be associated with checkpoint-related immune suppression. Because radiotherapy response is influenced by both tumor-intrinsic radiosensitivity and antitumor immunity, these findings suggest that high-risk patients may represent a subgroup with radioresistant and immunologically remodeled tumor features [42]. This provides a rationale for further exploring combined radiotherapy and immunotherapy strategies in OSCC, although this possibility requires prospective clinical validation.

Moreover, malignant cell subclustering, CytoTRACE, and pseudotime analyses revealed distinct malignant cell states and differentiation trajectories, supporting the concept that less differentiated or dynamically evolving malignant subpopulations may contribute to therapy resistance [43,44]. CellChat and virtual knockout analyses further indicated that these genes may affect ligand–receptor communication and functional pathways related to extracellular matrix remodeling, oxidative stress, glutathione metabolism, ferroptosis, apoptosis, and ECM–receptor interaction [[45], [46], [47], [48]]. These pathways are highly relevant to radiation response, as radiotherapy can induce oxidative stress, DNA damage, apoptosis, ferroptosis, and matrix-associated adaptive responses [49,50]. Thus, CellChat and virtual knockout analyses provided mechanistic clues suggesting that the four genes may influence OSCC radioresistance through altered cell communication and stress-related malignant-cell programs. The subsequent siRNA-based assays further supported these computational predictions by showing that knockdown of these genes enhanced irradiation-induced inhibition of metabolic activity, colony formation, and invasion. Together, these findings suggest that the four-gene signature may reflect a multi-dimensional radioresistance-related malignant-cell state rather than a single pathway. Radiation-induced oxidative stress may interact with ferroptosis through redox imbalance and lipid peroxidation [51]. Meanwhile, metabolic reprogramming and extracellular matrix remodeling may support antioxidant defense, matrix-dependent survival, invasion, and stress adaptation [52]. Therefore, KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B may jointly mark a radioresistant OSCC cell state involving stress adaptation, ferroptosis-related redox regulation, metabolic plasticity, ECM remodeling, and enhanced cell survival.

Among the four genes, KPNA2 may contribute to radioresistance through nuclear transport, p53 signaling, autophagy, and DNA damage-related stress adaptation. Previous studies reported that KPNA2 knockdown can inhibit autophagy in OSCC cells by blocking p53 nuclear translocation, and KPNA2 has also been associated with oncogenic features and immune infiltration in tongue squamous cell carcinoma [28,29]. P4HA1, a collagen prolyl 4-hydroxylase subunit, may participate in radioresistance by promoting extracellular matrix remodeling, collagen deposition, hypoxia-related adaptation, and invasion [30,31]. ARL6IP1 is less studied in OSCC, but it has been linked to apoptosis regulation, endoplasmic reticulum stress, autophagy, and tumor cell survival, suggesting that it may help tumor cells tolerate irradiation-induced stress [32,33]. TUBA1B, a microtubule-related cytoskeletal gene, may support cell division, structural plasticity, invasion, immune modulation, and therapy resistance [53,34,35]. Therefore, the four genes may represent different but complementary biological dimensions of OSCC radioresistance, involving nuclear transport, extracellular matrix remodeling, stress survival, and cytoskeletal adaptation.

Experimental validation further strengthened the biological relevance of the computational findings. Immunohistochemical staining showed that KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B were upregulated in OSCC tissues, particularly in radioresistant OSCC tissues. In vitro, HSC-3 and SCC9 cells showed relatively stronger radioresistance than CAL-27 and SCC25 cells, and SCC9 was selected for functional validation. Knockdown of KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, or TUBA1B in SCC9 cells enhanced irradiation-induced suppression of cellular metabolic activity, colony formation, and invasion, indicating that these genes may not only serve as prognostic biomarkers but also functionally contribute to the radioresistant phenotype of OSCC cells. Importantly, these validation results showed a coherent pattern across tissue, cell-line, and functional levels. At the tissue level, the four genes were more highly expressed in OSCC tissues, especially in radioresistant OSCC tissues. At the cell-line level, their expression was elevated in OSCC cells and was relatively higher in HSC-3 and SCC9 cells, which showed stronger radioresistant characteristics. At the functional level, silencing these genes enhanced irradiation-induced inhibition of metabolic activity, clonogenic survival, and invasion in SCC9 cells. This cross-level consistency supports the biological relevance of the four-gene signature and suggests that these genes may participate in maintaining radioresistant malignant behavior rather than merely serving as passive expression markers.

However, the present findings should be interpreted cautiously with regard to therapeutic targeting. Our results support KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B as radiotherapy-associated prognostic biomarkers and candidate functional mediators, rather than as fully validated direct therapeutic targets. Previous studies have linked KPNA2 and ARL6IP1 to autophagy-related stress adaptation and malignant-cell survival [29], supporting the biological plausibility of their involvement in resistant phenotypes. Nevertheless, defining these genes as direct therapeutic targets requires further evidence, including rescue experiments, pathway-level mechanistic validation, pharmacological inhibition, in vivo radiosensitization studies, and clinically relevant predictive validation [54]. Therefore, we describe these genes as prognostic biomarkers and putative radiosensitivity-related functional candidates in the present study. Several limitations should be acknowledged. The study relied partly on public datasets, a limited IHC cohort, and mainly SCC9-based in vitro validation. Some mechanistic findings were computationally inferred, and the risk-score cutoff requires further standardization. Moreover, although gene knockdown enhanced irradiation-induced suppression of malignant phenotypes, direct regulation of radiotherapy resistance by these four genes remains to be fully proven. Overall, KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B may serve as radiotherapy-associated prognostic biomarkers and candidate functional mediators in OSCC.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified a radiotherapy-associated four-gene signature comprising KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B in OSCC through integrated single-cell and bulk transcriptomic analyses. This signature showed prognostic value and was closely associated with malignant cell heterogeneity, immune remodeling, intercellular communication, and stress-related pathways. Tissue and cellular validation further confirmed that these genes were upregulated in radioresistant OSCC and that their knockdown enhanced the radiosensitivity of SCC9 cells. These findings suggest that KPNA2, P4HA1, ARL6IP1, and TUBA1B may serve as potential biomarkers and therapeutic targets for overcoming OSCC radioresistance.
