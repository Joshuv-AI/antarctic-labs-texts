Glycerol 3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase Catalyzed Hydride Transfer: Enzyme Activation by Cofactor Pieces
Authors: Rania Hegazy, Judith R. Cristobal, John P. Richard
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Glycerol 3-phosphate dehydrogenase catalyzes reversible hydride transfer from glycerol 3-phosphate (G3P) to NAD+ to form dihydroxyacetone phosphate; from the truncated substrate ethylene glycol to NAD+ in a reaction activated by the phosphite dianion substrate fragment; and from G3P to the truncated nicotinamide riboside cofactor in a reaction activated by adenosine 5′-diphosphate, adenosine 5′-monophosphate, and ribose 5-phosphate cofactor fragments. The sum of the stabilization of the transition state for GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer reactions of the whole substrates by the phosphodianion fragment of G3P and the ADP fragment of NAD+ is 25 kcal/mol. Fourteen kcal/mol of this transition state stabilization is recovered as phosphite dianion and AMP activation of the reactions of the substrate and cofactor fragments. X-ray crystal structures for unliganded GPDH, for a binary GPDH·NAD+ complex, and for a nonproductive ternary GPDH·NAD+·DHAP complex show that the ligand binding energy is utilized to drive an extensive protein conformational change that creates a caged complex for these ligands. The phosphite dianion and AMP fragments are proposed to activate GPDH for the catalysis of hydride transfer by stabilization of this active caged complex. The closure of a conserved loop [292-LNGQKL-297] during substrate binding stabilizes the G3P and NAD+ complexes by interactions, respectively, with the Q295 and K296 loop side chains. The appearance and apparent conservation of two side chains that interact with the hydride donor and acceptor to stabilize the active closed enzyme are proposed to represent a significant improvement in the catalytic performance of GPDH.

Introduction

Pauling proposed that enzymatic rate accelerations are the result of stabilization of the transition state for the nonenzymatic reaction by interactions with the protein catalyst.1,2 There is abundant evidence to support this proposal and no clear examples of enzymes for which it fails to account for an observed rate acceleration. Enzymes such as orotidine 5′-monophosphate decarboxylase (OMPDC) show very large rate accelerations in catalysis of reactions of relatively low molecular weight substrates and have been proposed as examples where the transition state stabilization exceeds the potential stabilization from protein–ligand interactions.3,4 It was shown for OMPDC and other enzymes that their very large rate accelerations are enabled by binding interactions between the protein and nonreacting substrate fragments.5−9 These interactions are not expressed at the ground-state Michaelis complex but develop at the enzymatic transition state.5

Nonreacting fragments at substrates have the potential to provide large stabilizations of enzymatic transition states. For example, the phosphodianion fragment at phosphate monoesters provides a total 12 kcal/mol stabilization of the transition states for a variety of enzyme-catalyzed reactions, including proton transfer,10,11 hydride transfer,10,12 decarboxylation,10,13 and phosphoryl transfer14 reactions. In all cases, ca. 6 kcal/mol of this total binding energy is expressed specifically at the enzymatic reaction transition states.10−13 Such specificity in transition state binding gives rise to spectacular ca. 10,000-fold rate accelerations from phosphite dianion activation of enzyme-catalyzed reactions phosphodianion truncated substrates.10−13 The phosphite dianion fragment does not interact directly with the transition state, as envisioned in early formulations of the Pauling model, but rather interacts with the protein catalyst and provides the binding energy required to mold catalysts into their active forms, as envisioned by Koshland in developing the induced fit model for enzyme catalysis.15,16

We are now working to identify and characterize other modular substrate fragments that provide transition state stabilizations similar to or greater than those for the phosphodianion. We have shown that the nonreacting adenosyl fragments of the whole substrate AMP activates adenylate kinase for catalysis of phosphoryl transfer from ATP to the truncated substrate phosphite dianion17,18 and that nonreacting fragments of the whole NAD+ cofactor activate formate dehydrogenase (Candida boidinii, CbFDH) for catalysis of hydride transfer to the truncated cofactor nicotinamide riboside (NR, Scheme 1).19

(A) Hydride Transfer Reaction Catalyzed by CbFDH. (B) Comparison of the Whole NAD+ Cofactor and the NR and ADP Cofactor Pieces

There is much scope for expanding studies on adenylate kinase and FDH to other enzyme-catalyzed reactions because of the many enzymes that utilize either AMP or NAD+ as substrates. Glycerol 3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GPDH) catalyzes hydride transfer from (R)-glycerol 3-phosphate (G3P) to NAD+ to form dihydroxyacetone phosphate (DHAP) and NADH (Scheme 2A). We recently reported that the transition state for GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer reaction of G3P is stabilized by 12 kcal/mol by the substrate phosphodianion and that roughly 50% of this total dianion binding energy is recovered in activation of GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer from ethylene glycol (EtG, Scheme 2B) to NAD+.12,20 GPDH is a logical target for a study of the reaction of NAD+ in pieces because of the exciting possibility that the activity of this enzyme for catalysis of hydride transfer might be captured in catalysis of the reaction of pieces derived from either the organic substrate G3P or the cofactor NAD+.

(A) Glycerol 3-Phosphate Dehydrogenase Catalyzed Hydride Transfer from (R)-Glycerol 3-Phosphate (G3P) to NAD+. (B) The Reactivity of the Whole Substrate G3P is Captured in Enzymatic Catalysis of the Reaction of the Ethylene Glycol and Phosphite Dianion Pieces

We report that the ADP fragment of the NAD+ cofactor (Scheme 1B) provides a second large reservoir of 13 kcal/mol binding energy for stabilization of the transition state for GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer so that the phosphodianion and ADP fragments provide a total transition state stabilization of 25 kcal/mol. The AMP fragment of the NAD+ cofactor provides a 7 kcal/mol stabilization of the transition state for GPDH-catalyzed transfer of hydride from G3P to the truncated NR cofactor. These results are remarkably similar to those from our recent study of the hydride transfer reaction catalyzed by CbFDH.19

GPDH undergoes a global ligand-driven conformational change that has been characterized by X-ray crystallographic analyses.21,22 This induced fit15,16 conformational change is driven by binding interactions of the protein with both the phosphite dianion fragment of the G3P hydride donor and the AMP fragment of the NAD+ hydride acceptor.23,24 The large intrinsic binding energy of the phosphite dianion and ADP or AMP substrate fragments is utilized to drive this energetically unfavorable conformational change in the inactive open enzyme to generate the active closed enzyme. A part of this binding energy is utilized to activate GPDH for catalysis of hydride transfer.25

Experimental Section

Materials

Water was obtained from a Milli-Q Academic purification system. The following reagent grade chemicals were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich: triethanolamine (TEA) hydrochloride, l-glycerol 3-phosphate, adenosine 5′-monophosphate (AMP, monosodium salt), adenosine 5′-diphosphate (ADP, monosodium salt), β-nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+), nicotinamide riboside chloride (NR), ribose 5′-phosphate (R5P), and adenosine (Ado). Sodium chloride and ethylene glycol (EtG) were obtained from Fisher. Dithiothreitol was purchased from GoldBio. Bovine serum albumin (BSA) was purchased from Roche Diagnostics. Wild-type glycerol 3-phosphate dehydrogenase from human liver (GPDH) was expressed and purified by following a published procedure.20 Solution pH was determined at 25 °C using an Orion Model 720A pH meter equipped with a Radiometer pHC4006–9 combination electrode that was standardized at pH 4.00, 7.00, and 10.00 at 25 °C. The pH of stock solutions of AMP and ADP was adjusted to 7.5 prior to use. Stock solutions of NAD+ were prepared by dissolving the coenzyme in water, and then stored at −20 °C. The concentration of NAD+ in these solutions was determined from the absorbance at 260 nm using the extinction coefficient of ε = 17,450 M–1 cm–1. The concentration of GPDH was calculated from the absorbance at 280 nm using the extinction coefficient of ε = 18,450 M–1 cm–1 and a subunit molecular weight of 37,500 Da that was determined using the ProtParam tool available on the ExPASy server.26,27 The concentration of ADP was determined as the concentration of NADH reduced in the coupled pyruvate kinase-catalyzed phosphorylation of ADP by phosphoenolpyruvate and lactate dehydrogenase-catalyzed reduction of pyruvate by NADH. The concentration of AMP was determined in a similar coupled enzyme assay, but with the addition of adenylate kinase to catalyze phosphorylation of AMP by ATP.18

Enzyme Assays

All reactions were performed at 25 °C, pH 7.5 (20 mM TEA), and I = 0.12 (NaCl). The assay for GPDH-catalyzed oxidation of L-G3P by NAD+ was monitored by a published procedure.20 The oxidation of L-G3P by NR to form the reduced cofactor was monitored by ultraviolet–visible (UV–vis) spectrometry at 340 nm and using the molar extinction coefficient of 4900 M–1 cm–1 for the reduced cofactor fragment.28

GPDH-Catalyzed Reduction of NR to Form NRH

The assay mixtures contained 20 mM TEA at pH 7.5 (I = 0.12, NaCl), 0.1 mg/mL BSA, L-G3P (0.75–10 mM), NR (20–40 mM), and 1.1 × 10–4 M GPDH. The oxidation of L-G3P was followed by monitoring the formation of NRH. Initial velocities vE (M/s) for the reduction of NR over ≪10% reaction were calculated from the increase in the cofactor absorbance at 340 nm using the molar extinction coefficient of 4900 M–1 cm–1.28

GPDH-Catalyzed Reduction of NR to NRH in the Presence of Activators

The assay mixtures contained 20 mM TEA at pH 7.5 (I = 0.12, NaCl), 0.1 mg/mL BSA, L-G3P (0.25–3 mM), NR (5–15 mM), and GPDH (5 × 10–6–3 × 10–5 M). The following ranges of activator concentrations were used: AMP, 0.2–2 mM; ADP, 1–9 mM; 1–11 mM R5P. The oxidation of L-G3P was followed by monitoring the formation of NRH at 340 nm. Initial velocities vE (M/s) for the reduction of NR over ≪10% reaction were calculated from the increase in absorbance at 340 nm using the molar extinction coefficient of 4900 M–1 cm–1.28

The initial experimental reaction velocities vE are typically reproducible to ±5%. The nonlinear least-squares fit of kinetic data to the appropriate kinetic equation was obtained using the Prism 8 program for MacOS from GraphPad software.

Discussion

GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer from G3P to NAD+ proceeds by an ordered reaction mechanism, with NAD+ binding before G3P.30,31 In contrast to the reaction of the whole cofactor NAD+ (Km = 2.1 × 10–4 M) there is no detectable approach to saturation for GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer from G3P to NR for reactions at up to 40 mM NR and 10 mM G3P (Figure 1). We were therefore unable to determine whether there is an enforced order for the binding of NR and G3P to GPDH. We assume in Scheme 3 that the binding of the NR and nucleotide pieces, like the binding of NAD+, occurs before the binding of G3P. This assumption does not affect the conclusions from this work. There is no detectable approach to saturation for reactions at 2.0 mM AMP (Figures 2 and S1–S4) so that (Km)app ≫ 2 mM for AMP. The fourth-order rate constant for AMP-activation of GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer was determined from the full set of data shown in Figures S1–S4 and 2–4. The corresponding fourth-order rate constants for activation by ADP and R5P are based on more limited sets of data and are calculated with the assumption that the activator reactivity relative to AMP determined for reactions at a single [G3P] = 3 mM holds for all [G3P] (Table 1). Figures 5A and 6A show the approach to saturation for reactions at ≈10 mM ADP or R5P, but we have not further characterized the weak ground-state affinity of substrate fragments for GPDH.

GPDH catalyzes the slow hydride transfer from G3P to NR to form the reduced cofactor fragment NRH. A comparison between the third-order rate constants (Figure 1 and Table 1) for GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer from G3P to NAD+ (4.2 × 108 M–2 s–1) and from G3P to NR (0.058 M–2 s–1) shows that the ADP fragment of NAD+ activates GPDH by 7.2 × 109-fold for catalysis of hydride transfer. This corresponds to a 13.4 kcal/mol stabilization of the hydride transfer transition state by interactions between GPDH and the ADP cofactor fragment (Figure 7). By comparison, the third-order rate constants of (1.1 × 106 M–2 s–1) and (0.0026 M–2 s–1), respectively, for formate dehydrogenase (FDH)-catalyzed hydride transfer from formate to NAD+ and to NR corresponds to a 4.2 × 108-fold activation and an 11.7 kcal/mol transition state stabilization by the ADP fragment of NAD+ (Figure 7).19

Comparison of the stabilization of the transition states for GPDH-catalyzed [Table 1] and FDH-catalyzed hydride transfer to NR by the ADP moiety at the whole NAD+ cofactor, and the transition state stabilization from binding of exogenous activator (1.0 M, Scheme 4).19

GPDH is activated for catalysis of hydride transfer from G3P to the truncated NR cofactor by the addition of AMP, ADP, or R5P fragments of the NAD+ cofactor. A value of k′cat/KNRKActKG3P = 5100 M–3 s–1 (Scheme 3) for enzyme activation was obtained by sequentially determining the effect of increasing [AMP] (Figures 2 and S1–S4), [NR] (Figure 3) and [G3P] (Figure 4) on the velocity of the GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer from G3P to NR. Approximate values of k′cat/KNRKG3PKAct for activation of GPDH by ADP and R5P reported in Table 1 were obtained starting with values for the observed third-order rate constants (k′cat/KNRKAct)obs = k′cat[G3P]/KNRKG3PKAct (eq 2) for reactions of ADP (Figure 5) and R5P (Figure 6) and with substitution of [G3P] = 3 mM.

We conclude that the transition state for GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer from G3P to NR is stabilized by the binding of ADP, AMP, and R5P fragments of the whole NAD+ cofactor. The transition state stabilization by a standard state of 1.0 M activator is defined by KAct† (Scheme 4) for dissociation of the activator fragment from the quaternary enzyme-transition state complex (Scheme 4).19 Values of KAct† (eq 3) were calculated from the ratio of the fourth-order rate constant (k′cat/KNRKActKG3P) for activated and the third-order rate constant (kcat/KNRKG3P) for unactivated GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer reactions. The values of ΔGact† (Figure 7) for transition state stabilization by interactions with 1.0 M ADP, AMP, and R5P activators were calculated by using eq 4. The weaker transition state stabilization by ADP (5.2 kcal/mol) compared to AMP (6.7 kcal/mol) suggests that the former transition state is destabilized by steric interactions between the β-phosphate of ADP and the 4′-hydroxymethylene group at NR.5,19,32

GPDH and FDH-Catalyzed Hydride Transfer

The free energy reaction profiles for the GPDH- and FDH-catalyzed hydride transfer reactions of the truncated NR cofactor shown in Figure 8 were prepared using the values for the third-order reaction rate constants, ΔGact† for activation of GPDH and FDH by AMP (Figure 7), and assuming that ΔG° for hydride transfer from G3P or formate to NR, respectively, is the same as for hydride transfer from G3P to NAD+ (4.3 kcal/mol)29,33 or from formate to NAD+ (−7.9 kcal/mol).34,35 The ratio of the third-order rate constants for GPDH- (Table 1) and FDH-catalyzed19 hydride transfer to NR, (0.058)/(0.0026 M–2 s–1) = 22, gives a 1.8 kcal/mol lower activation barrier for GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer (Figure 8). FDH-catalyzed hydride transfer to the NR is effectively irreversible, and there is a large 14 kcal/mol difference in the activation barriers for the GPDH and FDH-catalyzed reactions in the reverse direction (Figure 8).

Free energy reaction profiles for unactivated and AMP-activated GPDH- (left) and FDH-(right)19 catalyzed hydride transfer to the truncated NR cofactor. The AMP activator transition state binding energies ΔGAMP† were calculated as described in the text.

The robust transition state binding energies determined for activation of FDH- and GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer reactions of the truncated NR cofactor by ADP, AMP, and R5P (Figure 7) suggest that fragment activation of enzyme-catalyzed reactions of NR will be observed for a broad range of enzymes. We first examined FDH-catalyzed hydride transfer because the small hydride donor formate provides minimal binding energy for transition state stabilization, with the result that there is a large demand for stabilization from interactions with the NAD+ cofactor.19Figure 8 shows that despite the large driving force that favors FDH- compared to GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer to NR, there is a 2 kcal/mol smaller activation barrier to the latter thermodynamically unfavorable reaction.36 This reflects the larger total transition state stabilization for GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer from utilization of the intrinsic substrate binding energy of both the ADP fragment of NAD+ (13.4 kcal/mol) and the phosphodianion fragment of G3P (12 kcal/mol).29 The total 25 kcal/mol binding energy of these fragments is greater than 10 kcal/mol, which is the sum of the NAD+ and G3P binding energies calculated from the substrate disassociation constants. This is a testament to the enormous power of nonreacting substrate fragments to provide stabilization of enzymatic transition states.25

Model for Enzyme Activation

There are now many examples of enzymes that show effective catalysis of the reactions of both their whole substrates and structurally related substrates in pieces. GPDH is the first example of an enzyme-catalyzed bimolecular reaction in which catalysis of the reaction of the two whole substrates, NAD+ or G3P, is mimicked by structurally related substrate pieces. These results may be rationalized by a substrate-induced fit mechanism, where the binding energy of the two small molecule activator pieces is utilized to drive a protein conformational change that activates GPDH for catalysis.23,24,43 This is shown by Scheme 7 where GPDH is activated by an uphill protein conformational change from EO to EC that is driven by protein interactions with either the substrates G3P and NAD+ or the phosphite dianion and ADP fragments. There is evidence from both kinetic and structural studies that substrate binding to GPDH drives an activating protein conformational change.

Induced-Fit Mechanism for GPDH Where the Binding Energy of Fragments from Both Substrates is Utilized to Drive the Thermodynamically Unfavorable Change in Protein Conformation from the Open Unliganded Enzyme to the Closed Structure for the Ternary Complex

Kinetic Evidence for a Substrate-Induced Fit

The ordered kinetic mechanism for substrate binding to GPDH, with NAD+ binding before G3P,30,31,44 shows that NAD+ binding causes a change in protein structure that results in an increase in the affinity of GPDH for binding to G3P. The value of Km (G3P) = 1.9 × 10–4 M for GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer to NAD+ by this ordered mechanism is much smaller than Ki > 0.01 M for G3P substrate inhibition.29−31 This shows that G3P binds only weakly to free GPDH to form a dead end complex that occludes the productive binding of NAD+. The linked binding of G3P and NAD+ to form the active ternary E·NAD+·G3P complex requires that any effect of NAD+ binding on the affinity for G3P be matched by an effect of G3P binding on the affinity for the NAD+. The observed ordered reaction mechanism, with NAD+ binding first, must therefore reflect the large kinetic barrier to binding of NAD+ to the binary E·G3P complex

There is no detectable approach to saturation for GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer to the truncated NR cofactor for a reaction at 0.010 M G3P (Figure 1), in contrast to the value of Kd ≈ 0.2 mM determined for G3P.29 This shows that the ADP fragment of the NAD+ cofactor enhances the affinity of G3P for binding to the binary E·NAD+ complex. We propose that this represents stabilization of a protein conformation that shows a high affinity for G3P. Part or all of the ADP-activation of GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer may then be due to a similar interaction of the ADP fragment, which increases the fraction of GPDH present in the active conformation that shows specificity for binding to G3P.

Structural Evidence for a Substrate-Induced Fit

There are two sets of published crystal structures for unliganded GPDH; the binary E•NAD+ complex and the nonproductive ternary E·NAD+·DHAP complex.21,22 Both sets of crystal structures provide evidence for the substrate-driven conformational change (Scheme 7) from an inactive open conformation to an active closed conformation. The striking differences in these two sets of structures provide additional insights into the role of this substrate-driven conformational change in catalysis.

In one set of structures, unliganded GPDH [PDB 6E8Y] and the E·NAD+ complex [PDB 6E8Z], with the [292-LNGQKL-297] loop shaded red, are both in the open conformation. This is shown in Figure 9A for the binary E·NAD+ complex. The change in protein conformation to the closed structure occurs upon DHAP binding to form the nonproductive ternary complex [Figure 9B, PDB 6E90].21 This results in the movement of the N-terminal NAD+-binding and C-terminal sugar phosphate binding domains and closure of the [292-LNGQKL-297] loop over the enzyme active site.

Ligand-Driven Conformational Change

Enzyme Activation

GPDH and other enzymes are activated for catalysis by utilization of fragment binding interactions to stabilize the active closed enzyme EC (Scheme 7) relative to the inactive open form EO.23,24 The catalytic roles of the following side chains at GPDH have been characterized in extensive mutagenesis studies: Q295,41 R269,39,40,45 N270,46 K204,47 D260,21 and K120.21,48 The X-ray crystal structure for the binary E·NAD+ complex (Figure 10A) shows that these side chains are scattered across the active site cleft at the inactive open form of GPDH. The binding of DHAP (Figure 10B) then drives the formation of a stunning network of hydrogen bonds. This activates GPDH for catalysis by locking the catalytic side chains into their active positions around the substrate.21,29,47 The hydrogen-bonded side chain network runs from Q295, which is close to the phosphodianion of DHAP, to K120 which is close to the DHAP carbonyl oxygen,29 and includes the N270, N205, K204, and D260 side chains. All of the side chains in this network except Q295 are highly conserved.49

Figure 10 shows interactions of the DHAP phosphodianion and the AMP fragment of NAD+ that develop with the DHAP driven conformational change. These interactions activate GPDH for catalysis by stabilizing the active closed enzyme. The strongest interaction is the ion pair between the R269 side chain and the phosphodianion. The stability of this ion-pair is significantly enhanced by the hydrogen bond that forms between the R269 and the Q295 side chains (Figure 10B).41

The closed form of GPDH is stabilized by interactions with the cofactor that develop during the conformational change (Figure 11): (1) The K296 side chain cation moves to within 5 Å of the purine ring. This is in the range of distances observed for lysine cation-pi interactions at proteins,50,51 whose strength is estimated to range from 1 to 4 kcal/mol depending upon the effective protein dielectric constant.52 (2) The backbone amide of K296 is hydrogen bonded to the α-ribosyl phosphate of NAD+. (3) A stacking interaction occurs between the purine ring of NAD+ and the conserved aromatic side chain of F41. The binding of DHAP to the binary E·NAD+ complex reduces the separation of these aromatic rings from 4.7 to 3.3 Å, and this may stabilize the closed enzyme (EC).21 (4) Hydrogen bonds from K120 and K296 backbone amides, respectively, to the 3-OH of the nicotinamide ribosyl ring and the α-ribosyl phosphate of NAD+. This expands the network of hydrogen bonds discussed above to include the NAD+ cofactor.

[292-LNGQKL-297] Loop

Figure 9 shows that this loop folds over the mouth of the active site upon binding of DHAP to the E·NAD+ complex. Loop closure positions the Q295 and R269 side chains to interact with one another (Figure 10) and drives the K296 side chain toward the purine ring of NAD+ (Figures 10 and 11). The Q295-R269 side chain interaction promotes efficient phosphite dianion activation of GPDH-catalyzed reduction of glycolaldehyde.41 We propose that interactions between the K296 side chain and the purine ring of AMP promote activation of GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer from G3P to NR and account for part or all of the difference between the 6.7 and 4.8 kcal/mol activator binding energies determined for AMP and ribose 5-phosphate (Figure 7).

The [292-LNGQKL-297] loop sequence is conserved at GPDH from human (accession code, P21695), rabbit (P08507), rat (Q8K4T7), mouse (P13707), fish (A0A672MWR6), and Drosophila melanogaster (P13706). There is considerable loop sequence variation for GPDH from organisms at lower positions on the evolutionary tree.49 For example, the sequences for GPDH from Schizosaccharomyces pombe (P21696) and Leishmania mexicana (P90551), respectively, are [292-LGGQLL-297] and [292-TSAAVA-297].49 We propose that the conserved loop sequence from GPDH from higher organisms provides optimal stabilizing interactions for the Q295 and K296 side chains at the closed enzyme (Figure 11). For example, the backbone amide from V296 for GPDH from L. mexicana forms a hydrogen bond to the α-ribosyl phosphate of NAD+, similar to that for the backbone amide of K296 (Figure 11), but the other stabilizing interactions observed for the Q295 and K296 side chains are absent.49 A phylogenetic sequence analysis for GPDH, and an examination of the effect of differences in the loop sequence for GPDH from H. sapiens and L. mexicana on the enzyme kinetic parameters for activated and unactivated GPDH-catalyzed reactions of whole and truncated substrates, should provide additional mechanistic insights.

Energetics of Activating Ligand-Driven Enzyme Conformational Changes

GPDH, OMPDC, and triosephosphate isomerase (TIM) show very low activities for catalysis of reactions of phosphodianion truncated substrates because the catalytic side chains at the free enzymes are poorly placed to carry out their assigned functions. These enzymes undergo complex conformational changes that do not appear to be thermoneutral processes. However, the possibility that these are thermodynamically unfavorable was not considered until our repeated observations of enzyme activation of the reactions of truncated substrates by small, truncated substrate fragments.23,24,55

The total 14 kcal/mol stabilization of the transition states for GPDH-catalyzed reactions of truncated substrates by the nonreacting phosphite dianion and AMP activator pieces (Scheme 6) is too large to be rationalized by direct stabilizing through-space interactions between the pieces and the transition state that are expressed specifically at the transition state.56,57 This is because of the large distances separating these species and the relatively small difference in the structures for the reaction ground and transition states.29Scheme 7 was developed to rationalize this and many other observations of fragment activation in cases where an activator piece [and now pieces] drive a protein conformational change.5 This model requires that the 14 kcal/mol activator stabilization of the transition states for GPDH-catalyzed hydride transfer reactions reflect the utilization of the binding energy of the two activator pieces to drive the thermodynamically unfavorable conversion of inactive unliganded EO to the active closed form EC.43

We are not aware of computational studies to model the energetics for substrate-driven conformational changes, such as occur upon binding of substrates to TIM, OMPDC, and GPDH. Summary

The transition state for the hydride transfer reaction catalyzed by GPDH is stabilized by 25 kcal/mol by binding interactions with the nonreacting phosphodianion fragment of G3P and the ADP fragment of NAD+. More than 50% of this substrate binding energy (ca. 14 kcal/mol, Scheme 6) is utilized to activate GPDH for the catalysis of hydride transfer. GPDH is the first example of an enzyme-catalyzed bimolecular reaction where catalysis of the reaction of either substrate piece (NR or GA) is activated by the second truncated piece (HPi or ADP). This is the second example of an enzyme-catalyzed transfer of hydride to the truncated NR cofactor that is activated by cofactor pieces. The sample size is too small to broadly generalize these results to other NAD+ dehydrogenases. However, we are unlikely to have stumbled across the only two dehydrogenases that show this behavior and predict that similar activation by NAD+ cofactor fragments will be observed for other enzymes.

X-ray crystallographic structural analyses have identified a global substrate-driven protein conformational change for GPDH,19,20 that is similar to those observed for other NAD+ dehydrogenases.49,62−65 Evolution has favored unliganded conformations for dehydrogenases that are inactive, and the utilization of substrate binding to generate catalytically active enzymes.23 We propose that a large intrinsic substrate binding energy is required to mold the structure of these inactive protein catalysts into their active closed forms.23 The utilization of substrate binding energy to drive these protein conformational changes has the effect of weakening the observed substrate binding affinity as is sometimes required to avoid tight and irreversible substrate binding.24,25 The active closed form of GPDH is stabilized relative to the inactive open form by interactions of the protein with both the activating phosphite dianion and AMP substrate fragments. The interactions that develop with the Q295 and K296 loop side chains during this protein conformational change are proposed to stabilize the enzyme complexes to phosphite dianion and AMP activators. The appearance of the Q295 and K296 side chains and the conservation of this loop at GPDH from higher organisms are proposed to reflect an increase in the enzyme catalytic efficiency.

These results reveal gaps in our understanding of protein structure–function relationships. For example, computational studies to model enzyme catalytic activity have focused on modeling the activation barrier to reactions that occur at the active closed forms of protein catalysts obtained from X-ray crystallographic analyses.24 These studies do a good job in modeling kcat for turnover of enzyme-bound substrate, and the roles of catalytic side chains in stabilizing enzyme-bound transition states.66,67 There has been less attention directed toward modeling the roles of catalytic side chains that function in part or entirely to hold the protein in the active closed conformation. Our studies on the mechanism of action of TIM,68−70 OMPDC,6,8,9,70,71 and GPDH have identified and characterized the amino acid side chains that interact with the bound substrate to stabilize the active closed enzyme. These side chains function during turnover, in concert with the side chains that interact directly with the reaction transition state, to enable proficient catalysis. We suggest that the failure to appreciate the importance of protein side chains that play partly or mainly structural roles in the stabilization of high energy active forms of enzyme catalysts and to characterize their function during turnover has stalled progress from studies on enzymatic reaction mechanisms.
