Microplastics in Human Bronchoalveolar Lavage Fluid
Authors: Takatomo Tokito, Takashi Kido, Osamu Nagafuchi, Koichi Tomoshige, Koyomi Nakazawa, Ken'ichi Shinozuka, Shuntaro Sato, Kumiko Kido, Yasuko Noguchi, Takamune Matsumoto, Satoshi Mizoguchi, Ritsuko Murakami, Hirokazu Yura, Hiroshi Ishimoto, Takahiro Takazono, Noriho Sakamoto, Yuji Ishimatsu, Yoshimasa Tanaka, Keitaro Matsumoto, Takeshi Nagayasu, Hiroshi Mukae
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and ObjectiveHealth impacts of inhaling airborne microplastics have been actively investigated. Although microplastics have been detected in lung tissues and bronchoalveolar lavage fluids, their detection requires substantial effort and is technically challenging, with existing studies demonstrating various limitations. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid is a less invasive sampling method than surgical lung biopsy. This study aimed to establish a detection method for microplastics from bronchoalveolar lavage fluid and evaluate the relevant health impacts.MethodsPatients undergoing bronchoscopy for diffuse lung disease diagnosis were included. Microplastics were detected using Nile Red staining and fluorescence microscopy. Particle size, shape, and concentration were assessed through image analysis, and plastic types were identified via Raman spectroscopy. Correlations between microplastic findings and clinical laboratory data were evaluated.ResultsMicroplastics were detected in the bronchoalveolar lavage fluid of all 30 patients. The median concentration of Nile Red‐stained particles was 684.7 particles/mL, and over 80.0% of the particles were smaller than 10 μm, with 93.5% being particulate and 6.5% fibre. The most frequently identified microplastics were polyvinyl chloride, polystyrene, and polyethylene terephthalate. Microplastic concentration positively correlated with blood C‐reactive protein levels (rs = 0.39) and was higher in areas showing consolidation shadows (p = 0.024).ConclusionsWe established a method for detecting microplastics in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid. Microplastics were found in all patients, with a potential link to inflammation. In addition, Nile Red staining was applied for the first time to detect plastics in the lung and appeared to be useful for screening and quantification.

Introduction

Environmental plastic pollution has become a global issue [1]. Plastics degrade into smaller particles through natural forces like wind and waves, persisting in the environment for centuries [2, 3]. These particles, measuring less than 5 mm in diameter, are referred to as microplastics. Microplastics have been found in various environmental media, including air. Observation of airborne microplastics has been reported since 2016, confirming their presence not only in urban areas with high plastic consumption, such as Shanghai, Tokyo, and Paris, but also in remote mountain areas with little human activity, such as the Alps and the Pyrenees [4, 5]. Several studies have shown that particles smaller than 10 μm account for more than 50% of all ambient microplastics, and the proportion increases as size decreases [6, 7].

Although the impact of microplastic inhalation has been actively investigated, many aspects remain unclear. In vitro studies show plastic exposure increases inflammatory cytokines, cytotoxicity, and cell death in human lung epithelial cells [8]. Daily inhalation of microplastics in rats has been linked to lung inflammation, suggesting a potential association with chronic lung diseases such as obstructive pulmonary disease and interstitial lung diseases [9]. Additionally, occupational exposure to nylon, a type of plastic, has been associated with lung inflammation and interstitial lung disease in workers at five facilities across the United States and Canada [10]. Microplastics were detected in lung tissues of 11/13 (84.6%) and 13/20 (65%) patients in previous studies [11, 12]. However, both studies had limitations—small sample sizes, use of tissues from lung cancer surgeries or autopsies, and limited analysis of disease association and health effects. To address these limitations and better understand the health implications, it is essential to develop a system for collecting a larger and more varied sample set. Bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) offers a relatively non‐invasive sampling method in a clinical setting and is commonly used for diagnosing diffuse and environmentally induced lung diseases like asbestosis [13, 14, 15]. This method has already been employed for the detection of microplastics [16, 17, 18, 19, 20]. However, detecting microplastics smaller than 10 μm remains technically challenging and requires substantial effort [21]. Conventional methods using lung tissues or BALF are often limited in both the quantitative and qualitative analyses of such fine particles. Several techniques are available for quantifying, screening, and identifying plastics, each with distinct strengths and limitations. Therefore, combining two or more complementary methods is generally recommended [21]. Nile Red staining has shown promise as an efficient and effective tool for screening and quantifying microplastics in biological samples, offering a significant advantage in overcoming the technical barriers. However, its application to lung tissues and BALF has not been reported. Further, although Raman spectroscopy is time‐consuming, it offers an advantage in identifying microplastics as small as 10 μm.

In this study, we aimed to develop a detection method for microplastics in BALF by combining Nile Red staining and Raman spectroscopy; and to explore, as far as possible, their potential health impacts.

Methods

Participants and Ethics

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Nagasaki University Hospital and conducted between April 2021 and March 2024. Further details are provided in Appendix S1.

Patient Background Information

The patients' background information and clinical characteristics were collected from the electronic medical records of Nagasaki University Hospital prior to bronchoscopy. Additional information can be found in Appendix S1.

Sample Collection

BALF sampling was performed as described in our previous report [13]. A total of 10 mL saline was injected through the biopsy channel five times before bronchoscopy and collected as controls. Lung tissue samples were obtained from adult patients who underwent lobectomy for the treatment of lung cancer. Air samples were collected by continuous filtration for 24 h on the rooftop of the Nagasaki University Hospital. The air sample collection followed the sampling protocol described in previous studies [4, 22]. Further details are provided in Appendix S1.

Sample Preprocessing

Each sample underwent the methodology outlined in a previous study [23] to ensure the removal of organic particles while preserving the integrity of the microplastics. The samples were then filtered through polytetrafluoroethylene filter membranes (Advantec, Tokyo, Japan) using a glass vacuum filtration system (Advantec, Tokyo, Japan). Further details are provided in Appendix S1.

Nile Red Staining and Quantification

The filtered samples were fixed onto polytetrafluoroethylene filters and stained with Nile Red (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) [1]. The filters were observed under a microscope and imaged in green fluorescence (excitation wavelength: 460 nm, emission wavelength: 525 nm) and bright field (Figure 1A). Automatic particle recognition and quantification based on fluorescent images were performed using ImageJ 1.5i (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, MD, USA) (Figure 1B). Particles with a major‐to‐minor axis ratio ≥ 3 were classified as fibrous, and those with a ratio < 3 as particulate. This procedure was conducted at the Institute of Environmental Science at Fukuoka Institute of Technology or Nagasaki University Hospital. See Appendix S1 for additional details.

Green fluorescence and bright field images of polytetrafluoroethylene filter paper stained with Nile Red and quantification using ImageJ. (A) Green fluorescence images of Nile Red‐stained filter paper (100× magnification) and bright‐field images of Nile Red‐stained filter paper (100× magnification). (B) Images of fibre and particulate fluorescent particles along with analysis results using ImageJ.

Micro‐Raman Spectroscopy

Material identification was performed using micro‐Raman spectroscopy (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) at the Fukuoka Institute of Technology's Research Organisation. Particles were focused with a ×50 objective on a micro‐Raman microscope, photobleached using a 532 nm laser, and Raman spectra were obtained. The spectra were then analysed through library searches for plastic identification. Obtaining Raman spectra from a single fine particle approximately 10 μm in size typically took about 1 h. Owing to time constraints, more than five randomly selected particles from each sample were examined. To avoid bias due to the choice of colour or appearance, the person responsible for capturing fluorescence and bright‐field images randomly selected around five particles, while another researcher conducted the Raman spectroscopic analysis. See Appendix S1 for further details.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using a paired t‐test, Mann–Whitney U test, and Kruskal–Wallis test. Spearman's rank correlation coefficient (rs) was calculated to find the association between the concentration of fluorescent particles in BALF and various background factors. Stratified analyses were also performed based on clinical characteristics. All data analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism version 10.2.0 for Windows (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). Given the exploratory nature of this study, no adjustments were made for multiple comparisons. P values and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated for exploratory purposes only and should be interpreted with caution.

Results

Background Information on Collected Samples

Thirty BALF samples from patients with diffuse lung diseases, along with corresponding saline samples, three lung tissue samples from patients with lung cancer, and 3 days' worth of air samples were collected. Tables [Link], [Link], S3 and S4 present the background and clinical information of the patients. Air samples were collected from the rooftop of Nagasaki University Hospital on November 8, 9, and 10, 2022, and all days were clear and sunny.

Concentration, Particle Shape, and Size Distribution of Microplastics in Collected Samples

As described in the methods, to avoid potential plastic contamination within the bronchoscope, 10 mL of saline was injected five times through the biopsy channel prior to the procedure, and the 1st through 5th control samples were collected. The BALF samples from the first three patients were analysed along with these five control samples. No significant differences in particle concentration were observed among the 1st to 5th control samples (Figure 2). Therefore, the 5th saline sample was chosen as the control for comparison with BALF. In BALF samples, a median concentration of 684.7 particles/mL (interquartile range [IQR] 407.7–1163) of fluorescently stained particles was observed. In control samples, 95.9 particles/mL (IQR 32.7–133.5) of particles were observed, with significantly higher concentrations in BALF (Figure 3A). In lung tissue, fluorescently stained particles were found at concentrations of 7.6 particles/mg, 4.3 particles/mg, and 4.7 particles/mg, with an average (median) of 5.5 (4.7) particles/mg (Figure 3B). In the air samples, fluorescently stained particles were found at concentrations of 15.5 particles/m3, 21.4 particles/m3, and 64.5 particles/m3, with an average (median) of 33.8 (21.4) particles/m3 (Figure 3C). The shapes of microparticles in BALF were predominantly particulate, accounting for 93.5%, with only 6.5% being fibres (Figure 4A). The median size of microplastics in BALF determined by Nile Red staining was 3.29 μm (range 1.21–261.7), and size distributions were 1–2.5 μm (35.5%), 2.5–5 μm (29%), 5–10 μm (17%), 10–15 μm (7.2%), 15–20 μm (3.6%), and larger than 20 μm (7.7%), with particles with a diameter smaller than 10 μm accounting for 81.5% (Figure 4B). The proportion of particulates/fibres and size distribution were relatively similar between BALF, lung tissues, and air samples.

Identification of Fluorescent Stained Particles in Samples

Material identification of the particles fluorescently stained with Nile Red was performed using Raman spectroscopy. Owing to time constraints, the first 14 consecutive BALF samples, along with their paired 14 control samples, three lung tissue samples, and three air samples, were analysed. Eight types of microplastics were detected: polyethylene, polypropylene, polystyrene, polyvinyl chloride, polyethylene terephthalate, polymethyl methacrylate, cellulose, and nylon. Representative waveforms of microplastics detected by Raman spectroscopy are shown in Figure 4C. The BALF samples were analysed for 94 particles from 14 specimens, of which 69 (73.4%) were identified as microplastics (Figure 4D). The microplastics included polyvinyl chloride (25.5%), polystyrene (19.1%), polyethylene terephthalate (12.8%), polypropylene (8.5%), polymethyl methacrylate (3.2%), cellulose (3.2%), and polyethylene (1.1%). The 18.1% classified as ‘unidentifiabl’ could not be matched with the library data. From the 14 control specimens, 79 particles were analysed, of which 48 (60.8%) were identified as microplastics. From the three samples of lung tissues, 15 particles were analysed, of which 11 (73.3%) were identified as microplastics. From the three air samples, 16 particles were analysed, of which 12 (75.0%) were identified as microplastics. Details of the plastics detected by Raman spectroscopy are shown in Table S5.

Association Between Particle Concentration in BALF Samples and Patients' Characteristics

The association between particle concentration in BALF samples and various patient characteristics was evaluated, including sex, age, smoking status, comorbidities, possible occupational exposure to plastics, BALF lavage section and imaging findings, recovery rate, and findings from BALF and blood tests (Figures 5, 6, S1 and S2). When the BALF washing area showed consolidation, the particle concentration was significantly higher compared with that in non‐consolidation areas (p = 0.024). Correlation analysis showed that C‐reactive protein (CRP) was positively correlated with particle concentration (rs = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.02 to 0.66, p = 0.035). No differences were observed in other parameters.

Discussion

Several studies have reported microplastics in BALF [16, 17, 18, 19, 20], but limitations remain in both qualitative and quantitative analyses.

The first report using a micro‐Fourier Transform Interferometer identified microplastics in all samples tested, but the detected particles were relatively large, with a mean size of 1.73 ± 0.15 mm [16]. Studies using laser direct infrared spectroscopy focused on smoking‐related exposure, but detection was limited to particles between 20 and 100 μm [17, 18]. An electron microscope‐based study visualised nanoscale plastic‐like particles; however, the particles were not identified or quantified [19]. Another study using Raman spectroscopy, similar to ours, detected microplastics as small as 5 μm in patients with chronic lung disease, but this study was limited by the absence of a screening protocol and the low particle count (mean 1.6/sample) [20].

In this study, we developed a two‐step detection method combining Nile red staining for initial screening and quantification with Raman spectroscopy for material identification. Using Nile Red staining, we were able to detect presumed microplastics as small as 10 μm with high sensitivity and efficiency, enabling quantification as well as analysis of size distribution and morphology. Particles suspected to be microplastics were detected in all BALF samples stained with Nile Red, with a median concentration of 684.7 particles/mL (IQR 407.7–1163), and over 80.0% of the particles were smaller than 10 μm. Furthermore, Raman spectroscopy confirmed that at least 73.4% of the Nile Red‐stained particles were plastic materials. The size and morphology of microplastics in BALF were comparable to those of particles found in lung tissue and air samples, supporting the hypothesis that airborne microplastics may penetrate the lungs and distribute similarly within the tissue. Furthermore, we observed that microplastic concentrations in BALF were positively correlated with serum CRP levels and associated with radiographic shadows at the irrigation site, although these are exploratory findings.

Based on these results, we believe our study provides new insights into the detection of PM10‐level microplastics in the human respiratory tract.

Nile Red staining is an inexpensive and simple method for detecting plastics and has been widely used. Although Nile Red staining identifies plastics with 70%–90% efficiency, non‐plastic particles may also be stained, while some plastic types may not be stained [24, 25, 26]. When analysing organic matter, such as human tissue, it is important to remove organic contaminants using agents like hydrogen peroxide or potassium hydroxide [23]. To improve identification accuracy, combining this method with Raman spectroscopy or micro‐Fourier Transform Interferometer analysis is recommended [1, 24]. Although micro‐Fourier Transform Interferometer analysis is widely used in marine plastic research because it can analyse multiple relatively large particles simultaneously, it is not suitable for detecting small particles around 10 μm. In contrast, Raman spectroscopy, although limited to one particle at a time, can detect microplastics around 10 μm [27, 28]. Raman spectroscopy has been used to detect microplastics in human tissue and fluids, including lung tissue, kidneys, and urine [28, 29, 30, 31]. Consequently, we chose Raman spectroscopy to detect microplastics around 10 μm, which may be absorbed into the lungs.

The median size of microplastics determined by Raman spectroscopy was 14.7 μm (range 3.1–117.9) in BALF and 34.1 μm (range 7.9–58.3) in lung tissues, which is generally larger than those detected by Nile Red staining. We believe that this discrepancy is primarily due to the limitations of Raman spectroscopy in identifying smaller particles. As the size decreases, particle volume becomes smaller, increasing susceptibility to laser‐induced thermal degradation. Smaller particles may also fragment or degrade, hindering identification, and the laser may fail to accurately focus on such small targets. Consequently, we could not identify plastics smaller than approximately 3 μm, particularly in the 1–3 μm range detected with Nile Red. This discrepancy might affect the overall interpretation of our findings. However, considering our results regarding particles smaller than 10 μm, 22/30 (73%) particles identified by Raman spectroscopy were plastic, seven were unknown, and one was a mineral. This identification rate is comparable to that of the overall analysis, suggesting that the reliability of plastic identification within this size range remained consistent.

In our study, Raman spectroscopy identified 73.4% of the Nile Red‐stained particles, with 8.5% identified as minerals and 18.1% classified as ‘unidentifiabl’. The classification of ‘unidentifiabl’ might be due to issues like plastic degradation or damage during photobleaching, which prevented matching with reference library spectra. The Raman spectra of old microplastics differ significantly from those of fresh standard samples, making them harder to detect [27]. Furthermore, heat‐sensitive plastics might be damaged during photobleaching, which may lead to differences in detection rates. Some particles initially showed plastic‐like Raman spectra but were degraded due to combustion during photobleaching. This was not observed in mineral particles, suggesting that many ‘unidentifiabl’ particles were likely heat‐sensitive materials. Especially, particles smaller than 5 μm in diameter are difficult to identify because the heat concentrates in a small area, causing them to degrade easily. Previous studies that detected microplastics in the human body using Raman spectroscopy also showed an identification rate of approximately 40%–80%, with the average size of microplastics ranging from 5 to 15 μm [28, 30, 31]. These findings are consistent with our results and support the validity of microplastic detection in BALF samples. It is expected that future improvements in equipment will allow for more detailed analysis in this regard.

This study showed that polyvinyl chloride, polystyrene, and polyethylene terephthalate were the most commonly found microplastics in BALF. Previous studies have similarly identified these microplastics in the human body and various fluids [11, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 32, 33]. These plastics are frequently used in everyday items such as food packaging and clothing and have also been detected in the air [34]. It has been reported that the types and concentrations of airborne microplastics differ between indoor and outdoor environments and by season [5, 6, 35, 36]. This might have influenced our results, as our samples were collected only from the outdoors within a single season. For a more detailed analysis, air samples from various locations and seasons, including both indoor and outdoor environments, should be analysed.

Contamination control is critical in microplastic research owing to their pervasive presence. In this study, strict protocols were followed based on prior reports [1, 11, 12], and reagent‐only controls showed minimal signals, suggesting negligible procedural contamination. All BALF samples had higher microplastic counts than controls, indicating sample‐derived presence. However, background contamination remains unavoidable and should be considered when interpreting these results. Continuous refinement of detection and control methods is essential for accurate microplastic analysis using human samples.

Microplastics have been found in various human tissues and body fluids, but their effects on human health remain largely unclear [11, 32, 33]. In our study, a weak positive correlation was observed between the number of microplastics in BALF and both CRP levels and peripheral blood monocyte counts. Furthermore, when the imaging findings at the lavage site showed consolidation, suggesting the presence of relatively acute inflammatory lesions, particle concentration was higher than that observed in other types of opacities.

Stratified analyses based on steroid use, smoking, and inflammatory diseases showed higher correlation coefficients than unstratified analysis. A moderate correlation was found in patients with inflammatory diseases (rs = 0.67, 95% CI: 0.25–0.88, p = 0.0056) and those not using steroids (rs = 0.43, 95% CI: 0.04–0.71, p = 0.03) (Figure S3). These findings suggest a possible association between microplastics and inflammation and that the use of steroids might mask this inflammatory response.

Given the exploratory nature of this study and the lack of correction for multiple comparisons, these results should be interpreted with caution. Nevertheless, our findings suggest a potential association between respiratory exposure to microplastics and pulmonary as well as systemic inflammation.

Similar effects are known for particulate matter (PM), a major air pollutant, which induces systemic inflammation, including elevated CRP and monocyte levels [37, 38]. This may result from inflammatory mediators released via alveolar macrophages after phagocytosing PM10 [39, 40]. This process induces the production of CRP from the liver and white blood cells from the bone marrow [41, 42]. Systemic inflammation induced by inhaled PM10 has been reported as a risk factor for cardiovascular diseases [43]. The inflammatory response can cause dysfunction of endothelial cells, which are crucial for maintaining vascular health, thereby potentially increasing the risk of atherosclerosis and thrombosis. In addition, smaller particles (such as PM0.1) may cross the alveolar blood‐air barrier, enter the bloodstream, and accumulate in organs [29, 44]. Recent reports indicate that the presence of microplastics in vascular plaques is associated with poor prognosis of cardiovascular events [45]. Microplastics may affect health through mechanisms similar to those of PM. However, high microplastic exposure often coincides with elevated PM levels; therefore, our findings may not reflect microplastics alone.

Similar to previous studies, this study has some limitations. First, because microplastics are ubiquitous in the environment, background contamination occurs even after applying strict quality control measures [6, 11, 12]. As in previous studies, control samples were included for comparison, and efforts to minimise plastic contamination during processing were made in collaboration with environmental specialists. Second, because bronchoalveolar lavage is generally performed in the middle lobe or lingula, our results do not represent the distribution of microplastics across the entire lung. Additionally, it is unclear whether microplastics detected in BALF represent accumulation in the lung over time or are subject to turnover. Third, there are temporal and technical limitations associated with Nile red staining and Raman spectroscopy. Not all Nile red‐stained particles can be qualitatively identified as microplastics. Among the 14 BALF and control samples, only around five particles per sample were randomly selected and qualitatively identified. The smallest identified particles were approximately 3 μm in size. Particles smaller than this were presumed to be microplastics based on Nile red staining, but the possibility of false positives cannot be ruled out. Furthermore, the identification rate of Raman spectroscopy was 73.4%, indicating that over 25% of the particles may be false positives, although these results are consistent with those of previous reports. Overcoming this limitation will require advancements in analytical techniques, which may become feasible in the future. Fourth, the small number of patients and heterogeneity in patient backgrounds might have influenced the analysis results. Although a weak but statistically significant correlation was observed between CRP levels and particle concentration (rs = 0.39, p = 0.035), this finding should be interpreted with caution given the exploratory nature of the study and the lack of correction for multiple comparisons.

In conclusion, in this study, we proposed a method for detecting microplastics using BALF, combining Nile red staining for particle screening with Raman spectroscopy for material identification. Microplastics were detected in all BALF samples, with a particularly high proportion of microplastics under 10 μm, suggesting they can be inhaled and reach the lungs. We also demonstrated an association between microplastics in BALF and lung and systemic inflammation. Nevertheless, large‐scale prospective studies are warranted to further investigate the health effects of microplastic exposure, alongside the necessary advancements in analytical techniques.

Ethics Statement

The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board at Nagasaki University Hospital (approval numbers 20122117 and 21041919) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, from April 2021 to March 2024. For patients with diffuse lung diseases receiving bronchoalveolar lavage (BAL) for diagnostic purposes, verbal consent was obtained using a descriptive text in combination with an opt‐out form available on the website of Nagasaki University. A signature was waived because residual samples of BAL fluid (BALF) were used. Patients from whom surplus BALF could not be obtained or who declined its use after verbal explanation were excluded from this study. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients who underwent lung lobectomy for lung cancer.
