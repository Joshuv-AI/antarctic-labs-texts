Modeling Oxygen Saturation Targets, Mortality, and Retinopathy Among Extremely Preterm Infants
Authors: Brian C. King, John A. F. Zupancic, Erika M. Edwards, Danielle E. Y. Ehret, Jeffrey D. Horbar, Roger F. Soll, Barbara K. Schmidt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key PointsQuestionWhat are the trade-offs between mortality and treated retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) with universal adoption of a higher vs lower oxygen saturation target range in extremely preterm (gestational age 23-27 weeks) infants?FindingsIn this decision analytical modeling study in 19 050 simulated infants based on US national birth data, local baseline risks were associated with different trade-offs between mortality and treated ROP. Universal adoption of a higher oxygen saturation target range may be preferable if more than 4 cases of treated ROP per 1 death averted were acceptable.MeaningThese findings suggest that decisions about oxygen saturation target ranges may vary and depend on how stakeholders value the trade-off between reduced mortality and an increased risk of treated ROP.

Introduction

In neonatal intensive care units, administration of supplemental oxygen is common but challenging because clinicians must balance the risks and benefits that are associated with higher vs lower oxygen saturation target ranges.1 The Neonatal Oxygenation Prospective Meta-Analysis (NeOProM) Collaboration performed 5 separate randomized clinical trials of oxygen saturation targeting in extremely preterm infants and combined the data in an individual patient data meta-analysis.2 The primary composite outcome of death or disability at a corrected age of 18 to 24 months did not differ between infants who had been randomly assigned to a higher (91%-95%) or lower (85%-89%) oxygen saturation as measured by pulse oximetry (Spo2) target range. However, important differences were observed for several secondary outcomes. The higher Spo2 target range was associated with decreased risks of surgical necrotizing enterocolitis and mortality, while the lower Spo2 target range was associated with a decreased risk of treatment for retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) and chronic lung disease at 36 weeks postmenstrual age.2

Trade-offs between risks and benefits of different Spo2 targets should be informed by individual patient and center risks. Baseline risks differing from the NeOProM trials could change the expected absolute treatment effects.3 Gestational age (GA) may be an important risk factor and modifier of the treatment effect at the individual patient level.4 Within the NeOProM Collaboration, risks of adverse outcomes differed considerably among the 5 trials.3 More generally, risks for important neonatal outcomes vary greatly between centers and over time.5 The objectives of this study were to estimate the incremental effectiveness of a national policy of higher vs lower Spo2 target ranges for extremely preterm (GA 23-27 weeks) infants in a US birth cohort and to evaluate how much local baseline risks of death and treated ROP at each clinical center may influence the potential risk-benefit trade-off.

Methods

Model Description and Structure

In this decision analytical modeling study, we developed an individual patient microsimulation to estimate the incremental effectiveness of high vs low Spo2 targets among an annual US birth cohort of extremely preterm infants by using data from the NeOProM Collaboration and Vermont Oxford Network (VON) (eTable 1 in Supplement 1). The microsimulation model was constructed following the standards of published guidelines.6,7 The Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center Institutional Review Board deemed this study as exempt from review and informed consent as there was no greater than minimal risk to human participants. The VON research repository is a deidentified, Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act–compliant dataset with WCG Institutional Review Board approval for queries. This study followed the ISPOR–The Professional Society for Health Economics and Outcomes Research Good Research Practices for Comparative Effectiveness Research (ISPOR) reporting guideline.6

The microsimulation model structure is provided in eFigure 1 in Supplement 1. Simulated infants were assigned a GA based on a population distribution from national data. Subsequently, infants were distributed into 16 different performance subgroups based on center-based risks of mortality and treated ROP using data from the VON database. Following these assignments, simulated infants followed 2 pathways corresponding to the high and low Spo2 target ranges used in the NeOProM trials. Each pathway included a GA-specific risk of key outcomes that were chosen based on differences in the treatment effects reported by the NeOProM Collaboration. The time horizon of the model was limited to hospital discharge, as there were no significant differences in neurodevelopmental outcomes at 18 to 24 months in the NeOProM trials, and 95% of all deaths had occurred by hospital discharge.2 Face validity of the model was assessed using interviews with 5 independent academic neonatologists. Internal validity was assessed by varying data values to extremes to assess the impact on model outcomes, and external validity was assessed by comparing outcomes generated from the model with the source data and an external source for outcome data from the Neonatal Research Network (eTable 2 in Supplement 1).8

Model Outcomes

Outcomes included treated ROP, surgical necrotizing enterocolitis, chronic lung disease at 36 weeks, and mortality before discharge, all secondary outcomes with significant associations with treatment in NeOPrOM.2 The primary outcome for the model was the incremental effectiveness of Spo2 target ranges, defined as a risk trade-off ratio (RTOR) of additional cases of treated ROP for every case of death averted with adoption of the high Spo2 target (ROP/death RTOR). For example, an RTOR of 2 suggests that adoption of the high Spo2 target may result, on average, in 2 additional cases of treated ROP for every death averted.

To match the outcome definitions by the NeOProM Collaboration, outcomes in the VON database were defined as mortality before discharge, ROP requiring treatment (either surgery or anti–vascular endothelial growth factor therapy), necrotizing enterocolitis requiring surgery, and chronic lung disease as administration of supplemental oxygen at 36 weeks postmenstrual age. Baseline event probabilities for mortality before discharge and treated ROP were stratified by GA and performance subgroup, while baseline event probabilities for surgical necrotizing enterocolitis and chronic lung disease were stratified by GA only (eTable 3 in Supplement 1). As in the NeOProM analysis and the VON database, mortality before discharge and surgical necrotizing enterocolitis were counted among the entire cohort. Treated ROP and chronic lung disease were counted among survivors only, as most deaths in this population occur before ascertainment of treated ROP and chronic lung disease. Baseline event probabilities were assigned to the high Spo2 target group, while rates in the low Spo2 target group were derived by multiplying these baseline rates by the relative risk effect estimates from NeOProM. Since the Spo2 targets used among VON centers were not known, we chose this approach because the NeOProM Collaboration presented relative risks associated with adopting a lower target range compared with a higher target range. A methodological sensitivity analysis was performed in which baseline probabilities were assigned to the lower target range.

Data Sources

Model inputs included population distributions, baseline event probabilities, and treatment effect estimates (eTable 4 in Supplement 1). US National Center for Health Statistics data estimated a national cohort of infants born at GA 23 0/7 weeks to 27 6/7 weeks in 2023, with GA-specific proportions.9 This GA range was chosen to match eligibility among the NeOProM trials. The VON database was used to derive baseline event probabilities included in the model. To approximate the population and study settings in the NeOProM trials, we included all births in the same GA range between 2019 and 2023 among type B2 and type C centers in the US. These types of centers have no restrictions on ventilation and perform major surgeries. There was no evidence of subgroup heterogeneity by race and ethnicity reported in the NeOProM Collaboration, so we did not request or receive race and ethnicity data from the VON.

To model performance subgroups, we grouped VON centers included in this analysis into 1 of 16 groups using 2 quartile assignments based on infants’ baseline risk of mortality before discharge and of ROP requiring treatment. These assignments were generated using all infants born between GA 22 and 29 weeks within each center to match benchmarking data available to each center from the VON database. The distribution of infants born at each week of GA included in the analysis (23-27 weeks) among each performance subgroup was then used to distribute the birth cohort within the model and assign baseline event probabilities.

Statistical Analysis

The data analysis was performed between January and August 2025 using TreeAge Pro Healthcare, version 25.2.0 (TreeAge Software, LLC). Estimates of outcomes comparing universal adoption of the high or low Spo2 targets were summarized using results of a probabilistic sensitivity analysis in which the simulated cohort was repeated in 1000 iterations. Within each iteration, the relative risks for the 4 included outcomes were varied within their 95% CIs, and outcomes were reported as means with their 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). In lieu of a well-established threshold for a trade-off between mortality and treated ROP, the percentage of iterations in which the ROP/death RTOR was greater than 1, 2, and 3 are reported. One-way sensitivity analyses were used to explore the impact of individual data inputs on the analysis (eFigure 2 in Supplement 1). Subgroup analyses from the probabilistic sensitivity analysis included calculating the ROP/death RTOR for infants born into each of the 16 performance subgroups, as well as by week of GA.

To explore the impact of centers adopting 1 of the 2 Spo2 targets based on their baseline risk of mortality and treated ROP, we developed 3 scenarios in which performance subgroups were assigned 1 of the 2 targets based on each subgroup’s calculated ROP/death RTOR. In scenarios 1, 2, and 3, performance subgroups were modeled to adopt a low Spo2 target if their RTOR was greater than 1.0, 1.5, and 2.0, respectively, reflecting an increasing willingness to tolerate additional cases of treated ROP for every death averted. In each scenario, performance subgroups below those cutoff values were assumed to adopt the higher Spo2 target. Finally, to further explore center-based variation in outcomes beyond the 16 performance subgroups, we used a 2-way sensitivity analysis in which the risk of mortality and treated ROP for the entire GA range was varied across a plausible range of outcomes (0%-50%).

Results

Universal Adoption of High vs Low Spo2 Target Range

The modeled annual US birth cohort included 19 050 infants, of whom 2372 (12.5%) were born at GA 23 weeks, 3253 (17.1%) at GA 24 weeks, 3809 (20.0%) at GA 25 weeks, 4357 (22.9%) at GA 26 weeks, and 5259 (27.6%) at GA 27 weeks. A universal policy of targeting a high Spo2 was associated with means of 551 additional cases (95% UI, 435-659 additional cases) of treated ROP and 638 fewer deaths (95% UI, −363 to −934 deaths) before discharge compared with a universal policy of targeting a low Spo2 (Table). The mean ROP/death RTOR with the high Spo2 target was 0.91 (95% UI, 0.61-1.43). In addition, a mean of 301 fewer cases (95% UI, 170-425 cases) of surgical necrotizing enterocolitis and 1947 additional cases (95% UI, 1573-2313 additional cases) of chronic lung disease occurred with the high Spo2 target range (Table). Selective Adoption of High vs Low Spo2 Target Range

The results of subgroup analyses according to center-specific baseline risk quartiles for mortality and treated ROP are summarized in Figure 2 and eTable 7 in Supplement 1. Infants treated in centers with the highest risk of mortality (4th quartile) and lowest risk of treated ROP (1st quartile) had the lowest mean ROP/death RTOR at 0.16 (95% UI, 0.00-0.40). Conversely, infants born in centers with the highest risk of mortality (1st quartile) and the lowest risk of treated ROP (4th quartile) had the highest mean ROP/death RTOR at 2.52 (95% UI, 1.39-4.72). Ten of the 16 performance subgroups had mean RTORs less than 1, and only 1 had a mean RTOR greater than 2.

Acceptability of Universal vs Selective Spo2 Target Range Adoption

Figure 3 shows the probabilities that either universal or selective adoption of Spo2 targets would be preferable, depending on an acceptable threshold number of ROP cases per death averted. For example, if up to 2 additional cases of ROP were acceptable for every death averted, a selective choice to adopt the high Spo2 target range for centers with RTORs greater than 2 was preferable in 50% of iterations. Universal adoption of the high Spo2 target became the preferable approach in more than 90% of iterations when the ROP/death RTOR threshold was greater than or equal to 4. Discussion

The NeOProM Collaboration reported risk differences with 95% CIs for mortality and treated ROP when comparing higher vs lower Spo2 target ranges.2 The corresponding reciprocals of the risk differences are the numbers needed to treat for benefit or harm.10,11 According to NeOProM, a mean of 36 infants (95% CI, 20-167 infants) must receive the higher target range to prevent 1 death, while 1 additional case of treated ROP occurs per a mean of 25 infants (95% CI, 16-50 infants) exposed to the higher target range. However, such absolute measures of treatment effects cannot be applied to patients whose baseline risks for experiencing the outcomes of interest differ from the risks of the average patient in the NeOProM trials.

This microsimulation decision analytical modeling study used a novel approach to express the risks and benefits of the NeOProM intervention, the RTOR. The RTOR mirrors incremental cost-effectiveness ratios from cost-effectiveness analyses but allows clinicians and families to decide what is an acceptable trade-off between risks and benefits. In the absence of a universally accepted threshold for the trade-off between ROP and death, we provide estimates of ROP/death RTORs for different baseline risks of treated ROP and mortality in a modeled annual US birth cohort of extremely preterm infants. Our data suggest that a universal policy of adopting the higher Spo2 target range remains preferable, with a greater than 90% probability if 4 or more additional cases of treated ROP were considered acceptable for each death averted. Individual centers could use their own baseline mortality and treated ROP rates to estimate their RTOR and determine whether adoption of a higher Spo2 target range aligns with the values and preferences of their patient population and family representatives.

To make an informed choice about a trade-off that is acceptable for them, stakeholders require accurate information about the prognosis of treated ROP. Although the risks of severe visual impairment at age 2 years were similar in the higher and lower Spo2 target ranges of the NeOProM analysis, treated ROP is associated with both visual and nonvisual disabilities later in life.12,13,14,15 The association between treated ROP and neurodevelopmental impairment in childhood is not surprising because, as mention in Helström et al,16 “treatment warranting ROP is a sign of impaired neurovascular development in the central nervous system.”

One approach to quantifying a risk trade-off threshold could be to compare the incremental impact of treated ROP on quality of life relative to the overall quality of life among extremely preterm infants.17,18,19,20 Risk trade-off ratio thresholds could then be estimated by the ratio of quality of life loss from 1 death to the incremental quality of life loss per treated ROP case. However, many limitations to objective measures of health-related quality of life have been noted in the literature, specifically those related to pediatrics.21,22,23 In addition, the impact of a chronic morbidity, such as visual impairment, would vary based on the resources available, which may lead to differences in trade-offs between countries and regions, similar to differences in traditional willingness-to-pay thresholds.24 Given these uncertainties, we chose to provide a range of threshold values to allow stakeholders to consider preferences they deem suitable for their own population and context.

While changes toward a higher Spo2 target range have occurred over the past decade, there may still be wide variation in target ranges used. The most recent survey in the US suggested that while 90% to 95% is now the most frequently used range, the majority (71.7%) of centers still used some alternative range, including more than one-third of centers incorporating a lower Spo2 threshold of less than 90%.25 Observational studies have reported outcomes associated with intermediate target ranges, but the certainty of evidence is not well established compared with the 2 ranges studied in the NeOProM trials.26 Given the unknown impact of an intermediate target range, we opted to not include them in our model.

Strengths and Limitations

Strengths of this work included the use of US national data derived from more than 400 neonatal intensive care units and 60 000 infants from VON to derive probabilities for the microsimulation. Consequently, our model incorporated clinical variation in the baseline risks of important neonatal morbidities. This US birth cohort model derived all outcome data from the same population source, minimizing assumptions related to validity and generalizability that often occur with decision models.27 Another unique feature of our study was the adaptation of traditional methods of economic evaluation to generate RTORs (equivalent to an incremental cost-effectiveness ratio) that could inform stakeholders, such as parents and neonatal clinicians, about the trade-offs between the benefits and harms of a specific and evidence-based intervention.

Our study also had some limitations. First, the model included assignment of baseline data to the high target range due to lack of knowledge about targets used among the centers that contributed data to baseline probabilities. Incremental differences reported may not reflect actual changes that would be expected to occur after a national policy of a high Spo2 target range. We explored this assumption in a sensitivity analysis presented in eTable 5 in Supplemental 1. Risks within each performance subgroup reflect overall proportions and may not capture outlier centers. Second, the model assumed effect homogeneity, applying the same relative risks across baseline risks. While NeOProM included multicenter trials from several regions, baseline risks were derived from US centers, limiting generalizability to other settings. Finally, the model focused primarily on the risk trade-off between treated ROP and mortality, though incremental differences have been included for chronic lung disease and necrotizing enterocolitis. We focused on the trade-off between mortality and treated ROP because this has been the most emphasized clinical trade-off following the NeOProM Collaboration, despite the formal primary end point of death or major neurodevelopmental impairment at age 18 to 24 months.28 While other important outcomes (eg, chronic lung disease, surgical necrotizing enterocolitis) were not included in our ratio, they were included in the model and reported in absolute numbers. Importantly, the model derived relative risks from published data and, therefore, could not incorporate co-occurrence of included morbidities, which would lead to overcounting events if reporting a composite outcome in the risk trade-off. Preference thresholds would differ if those outcomes were incorporated into the trade-off framework.

Conclusions

This microsimulation decision analytical modeling study of an annual US birth cohort using national data found that universal adoption of a higher Spo2 target range among infants GA 23 to 27 weeks was associated with 638 fewer deaths (33 per 1000) in exchange for 551 additional cases of treated ROP (29 per 1000) compared with universal adoption of a lower Spo2 target range. The risk trade-off between mortality and treated ROP varied according to baseline risks. We estimated a more than 90% probability that universal adoption of a higher Spo2 target range (91%-95%) would be preferable if 4 or more additional cases of treated ROP were acceptable for each death averted, but selective adoption tailored to center-specific risks may be more appropriate if fewer cases were acceptable.
