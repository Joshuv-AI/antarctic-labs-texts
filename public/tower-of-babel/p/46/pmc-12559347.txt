Assessment of the water and energy nexus in the energy supply subsystem of water stressed countries like Iran
Authors: Masoud Vahabzadeh, Amir Molajou, Hossein Akbari Variani, Abbas Afshar
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Iran, situated in the Middle East, is recognized as a prominent energy hub, with its economy heavily reliant on the exportation of energy. Iran currently faces significant water stress, underscoring the importance of examining its Water–Energy (WE) nexus. Hence, it is crucial to examine the Water–Energy (WE) nexus in this nation. This study evaluates Iran’s WE nexus from upstream to downstream in its energy subsystem (2007–2017) through an integrated framework combining water footprint analysis, water consumption methodologies, and nexus system modeling. This study assessed the WE nexus from upstream to downstream in Iran from 2007 to 2017. Key findings reveal that steam turbine power plants, particularly Ramin and Neka, exhibit the highest water consumption intensities, approximately 2.04 and 2.65 m3/MWh respectively, making them critical targets for efficiency improvements or retirement. Conversely, combined-cycle plants with dry cooling technology show significantly lower water intensity (0.18 m3/MWh), presenting viable alternatives. The study recommends shifting energy infrastructure towards combined-cycle and gas turbine plants to mitigate water stress, thus providing actionable insights for sustainable energy and water resource management in water-stressed regions.

Introduction

Energy and water are inextricably linked: water is vital for fuel extraction, thermal‐plant cooling, and hydropower, while energy drives water supply, treatment, and distribution1,2. Rapid energy demand growth and mounting water scarcity in arid regions like Iran make their nexus central to sustainable resource management3,4. Simultaneously, a major proportion of the global populace is encountering water scarcity as a consequence of climate change5,6. Consequently, the condition of water resources will deteriorate, leading to heightened water demands3,7,8. Energy and water are strongly interdependent due to water’s critical role in energy production, transportation, and consumption processes. Conversely, the various stages of water management, encompassing withdrawal, supply, delivery, consumption, drainage, and treatment, necessitate a substantial allocation of energy resources9,10. There is a high-water footprint associated with many forms of energy production. For instance, large amounts of water from nearby bodies of water are required in thermal power plants for cooling purposes11,12. The ‘Water–Energy Nexus’ (WE nexus) describes the interaction between energy and water13,14. The implementation of management schemes and policies segregates the consideration of either water or energy resources. It has the potential to lead to unexpected, undesirable, and less than optimal results for either one or both of these crucial resources15,16. A comprehensive and thorough examination of the WE nexus system is imperative to attain sustainable management of water and energy resources17,18. Gaining a comprehensive perception of the interconnectedness between energy and water is crucial to effectively mitigate the consumption of energy and water. Also, it is vital to diminish environmental emissions19.

Iran, hosting significant global fossil fuel reserves, relies heavily on fossil fuels, constituting 83% of installed power capacity, while renewable sources like solar and wind account for less than 1%20. Rapid urbanization, industrial growth, and increasing population have driven substantial energy demand increases, primarily from fossil fuels21. Given the finite nature of fossil fuels, continued reliance poses notable risks to long-term energy security22. In general, economies are closely interconnected with their energy systems, particularly with regard to the oil export revenues in the case of countries that export oil22.

Iran’s economy has been severely impacted by heavy sanctions under the current conditions. As a result, the country is leveraging its position as an energy hub in the region to drive economic growth. As a result of this policy, Iran is compelled to increase the production of additional energy carriers in order to cope with the sanctions. Moreover, the agricultural sector of this nation is progressively shifting towards achieving self-sufficiency in the production of essential commodities23. On the other hand, Iran is a region that experiences water stress and inadequate water resource management24.

The problem of water scarcity in Iran is a complicated issue that involves several different aspects, including social and ecological concerns25. An ineffective water management system, agriculture, urbanization, climate change, population increase, and land-use change are only a few of the many elements that contribute to this issue in different ways26. The Falkenmark water stress index categorizes the water situation in a region by considering the amount of water available per person on a yearly basis. The categories are as follows: no stress (more than 1700 m3 per capita), stress (1000–1700 m3 per capita), scarcity (500–1000 m3 per capita), and severe scarcity (less than 500 m3 per capita)24. Since Iran has a renewable water supply of 1560 cubic meters per capita, the country is currently experiencing water stress27. The water stress index in specific regions of the country is significantly lower than the overall national annual average. The main reason for this is the widespread occurrence of arid and semi-arid climates in Iran, which are characterized by low rainfall and high rates of evaporation28. Furthermore, the water stress condition is expected to increase24. Reduced surface water and runoff, caused by low precipitation and high evaporation, have increased reliance on alternative sources such as groundwater. This has led to a concerning reduction in the country’s groundwater reserves29. Consequently, in light of the water stress predicament in Iran and the nation’s economic reliance on energy generation and exports to various nations, the significance of investigating the nexus between water and energy has been amplified.

Within the scope of this study, a novel paradigm that was initially presented by a research group in the year 2021 was utilized in order to investigate the nexus between water and energy. Molajou et al.14 proposed a systems approach as a new paradigm to investigate feasible spaces in a scenario-based manner within the nexus framework. In the following, Afshar et al.30 presented a new framework for modeling the nexus system that was based on new paradigm. Interrelation, interaction, and nexus variables were the most important concepts that were investigated in this study. The interrelation within the nexus system is referred to as intra-system connection. The interconnection between different components of a system occurs in a bidirectional manner, as exemplified by the interrelation between surface water and groundwater in the water subsystem. Interaction within the nexus system is referred to as inter-system connections. This indicates a bidirectional interaction between subsystems—such as water and energy—as exemplified by their interdependence in hydropower production. Nexus variables are also variables that are intermediate variables in nature. Stated differently, the nexus variables provide a system task to every component of the subsystems, such energy and water, forming a system-of-systems (nexus system), and link these systems to function as an integrated system30,31. The nexus system simulation is based on a complex network of interactions and interrelations32. The meaningfulness of a comprehensive nexus simulation is contingent upon the inclusion and consideration of nexus variables. In addition, according to the new nexus paradigm, interrelation influences interaction. In other words, the intra-system connection can alter the inter-system connection, as illustrated in Fig. 1.

As shown in Fig. 1, surface water recharges groundwater through the process of infiltration flow, while groundwater recharges surface water through the process of exfiltration flow. The connection between the surface water and groundwater components of the water subsystem is referred to as interrelation. Also, the variables of infiltrated and exfiltrated flow are known as nexus variables. According to the existing interrelation between surface water and groundwater, the hydraulic head of the groundwater fluctuates, as indicated by the variables h1, h2, and h3. Subsequently, stakeholders must utilize a pumping motor to extract the groundwater (energy subsystem). In order to determine the amount of energy consumption for pumping groundwater, two essential factors must be considered: hydraulic head and discharge volume. Both of the variables mentioned are referred to as nexus variables, and the connection between groundwater (water subsystem) and pumping motor (energy subsystem) is known as an interaction.

Multiple studies have been carried out in the field of nexus system modeling utilizing the aforementioned new paradigm. In a separate study conducted by Raver et al. utilizing the system dynamic methodology, their primary objective was to examine the energy consumption associated with crops and pumping, specifically on the energy demand side. However, they were unable to incorporate the nexus between water and energy on the energy supply side into their model33. Akbari Variani et al. incorporated the energy and water sectors into the modeling of the nexus system. However, the primary emphasis of this study was on the energy consumption of crops, specifically the energy demand side, and did not investigate the energy production sector, or the energy supply side34. In their study, Soleimanian et al. focused solely on modeling the connection between groundwater pumping and energy demand. They did not investigate the aspect concerning the generation of energy carriers and their water consumption35. Recent WE nexus studies in other arid Middle Eastern countries offer valuable comparative insights. For instance, Al-Zahrani and Abo-Monasar investigated the water demand of electricity generation in Saudi Arabia, highlighting the growing pressure on non-renewable groundwater due to once-through cooling systems. Their study also emphasized the importance of transitioning to dry cooling and solar PV technologies to reduce water stress36. Similarly, Al-Saidi and Hussein examined the UAE’s integration of desalination, energy planning, and water reuse under a nexus lens, noting the role of technological innovation in mitigating cross-sectoral tradeoffs. These regional examples reinforce the applicability of integrated planning frameworks under extreme climatic constraints and support the relevance of Iran adopting similar strategic approaches37.

None of the above studies that utilized the new paradigm to simulate the nexus system were able to accurately simulate the nexus between water and energy in the energy generation sector. The energy subsystem within the energy supply and generation sector holds significant importance as one of the primary water consumption subsystems in Iran. However, several studies have utilized various methodologies to model the energy generation sector or supply side and water consumption38–40. Meldrum et al.41 have carried out research to assess the water consumption of electricity generation, where it is clearly evident that the research can be used for modeling the water and energy subsystems. The efficiency of power plants and the type of cooling system they employ were not taken into account in this investigation. Ali et al. created a standard for the water demand coefficients throughout the entire life cycle of power generation from natural gas-fired plants. The water demand coefficients encompass both water consumption and water withdrawals at different stages of natural gas production, as well as during power generation from natural gas. This study was unable to assess the water consumption of thermal power plants by considering thermal efficiency and cooling type, using a new approach called the nexus system approach42. In an important study, Lee et al.3 conducted an estimation of the regional water consumption factors pertaining to hydro and thermal power plants’ electricity generation within the United States. This study, could not assess the water consumption factors based on thermal efficiency using nexus approach. Miglietta et al. conducted an analysis on the generation of electricity, examining it from both an economic and technical standpoint. This analysis was carried out by employing the two methodologies, footprint of water and productivity of economic water43. In this study, the water consumption methods using nexus paradigm for thermal power plants have not been used.

In research conducted by Gao et al.44, an analysis was performed on the operational water consumption and withdrawal factors associated with generation of power technology in China. This study could not evaluate the water usage for energy subsystem holistically, such as fuel extraction and processing unit. Pan et al. did research to investigate and evaluate the water consumption of thermal power plants. This study examined the power plant’s water consumption by analyzing the cooling system employed. The study investigated the primary factors influencing the quality of water discharged from cooling towers, with a specific focus on their implications for the design and operation of thermal power plants45. This study could not evaluate hydropower water footprint and thermal water consumption using nexus system approach. Sun et al. conducted an estimation of water consumption in refineries for the petroleum fuels production. This estimation took into account three common configurations of refinery, namely heavy coking, light coking, and cracking46. Mentioned study was not a comprehensive method to assess water consumption and water footprint of thermal power plants using nexus approach. Chai et al. measured the extent of both water depletion and degradation throughout the entire life cycle of power generation at coal-fired power plants in China. They accomplished this by employing a mixed-unit input–output model. This model focuses on the life cycle of water usage in thermal power plants, specifically emphasizing the significance of operational water consumption47. In a research work, Larsen et al. examined the efficacy of utilizing a collection of factor sets that represent energy generated per liter of water by various sources of energy. They also conducted a thorough analysis regarding the cooling systems and the water supplies employed by individual thermoelectric plants48.

The study conducted by Nouri et al. investigated the water consumption associated with different types of electricity generation sources, encompassing both renewable and non-renewable options. A comprehensive model has been formulated to ascertain the quantity of water withdrawal and water consumption associated with different scenarios of electrical energy generation38. This study used data-based model for hydropower water footprint which is not suitable for nexus study because the nexus variables are neglected. Li et al.49 employed an extensive dataset that revealed that electricity generation is associated with significant water consumption, with cooling water being identified as the primary category of water usage. Ghodrati et al. assessed the carbon, energy, and water in combined cycle power plants. The method of Life Cycle Assessment was utilized to estimate carbon and water footprints40. Jin et al. conducted a study to determine the water consumption of thermal power plants in each province of China. They took into account the water stress index to make their estimates. An inherent limitation of this study was that it did not take into account the specific type of power plant and cooling method used in each province when calculating the total water consumption50.

According to the literature reviews, the studies have not adequately addressed the nexus variables, as defined by the new paradigm in this study which are determining factor to nexus system. Furthermore, a comprehensive investigation of the energy supply sector in Iran, which is heavily reliant on energy production and facing water stress, has not yet been conducted to effectively address the nexus of water and energy. However, a study was conducted in 2010 by Beheshti Tabar et al.51 to analyze the energy demand sector in Iran, but in terms of supply sector nothing has been done. Previous studies primarily emphasize electricity generation, neglecting types of cooling tower which can impact water consumption of thermal power plants significantly. Also, the water footprint of hydropower is crucial for Iran in order to manage reservoirs effectively for multi-objective purposes. Additionally, water consumption intensity related to fossil fuel extraction, such as oil and gas, is critical for Iran because of its significant energy exports worldwide.

Iran is experiencing a significant decline in its renewable water resources, which poses a serious threat to its energy production and overall economic stability. Iran’s renewable water resources have decreased from approximately 130 billion cubic meters to less than 100 billion cubic meters due to anthropogenic and climatic changes52. This substantial reduction underscores the urgent need for integrated water and energy management strategies to ensure the country’s sustainable development. In this work, we adopt a novel paradigm for the WE nexus that centers on three core nexus variables—water withdrawal, water consumption, and water footprint—mapped systematically across the upstream, midstream, and downstream segments of the energy supply chain. Unlike previous approaches, which largely remain qualitative or use Life-Cycle Assessment metrics in a standalone fashion, our paradigm treats these variables as quantitative linking parameters. This permits straightforward incorporation into modular energy system models and paves the way for future optimization and dynamic simulation studies. We then apply this framework to Iran’s energy supply sectors—upstream extraction of coal, oil, and gas; midstream refining and processing; and downstream electricity generation—calculating each nexus variable for different fuel types and cooling technologies (once-through, recirculating, and dry). By classifying the energy supply side into three distinct flows and explicitly quantifying cooling-tower impacts on thermal power plants (steam turbines, combined cycles, and gas turbines), our study fills a gap in existing WE nexus literature and provides a transferable modeling foundation for integrated resource management.

Materials and methods

Operationally, our paradigm is characterized by (1) explicit identification of nexus variables at each supply-side stage, (2) definition of these variables as continuous model inputs rather than post-hoc indicators, and (3) a structured framework that enables direct parameterization in mathematical and simulation models (e.g., system-of-systems networks or mixed-integer programming). While this manuscript focuses on establishing the database of nexus variables, the framework itself introduces a mathematical novelty by converting water–energy linkages into scenario-ready parameters, thus moving beyond purely semantic or descriptive treatments.

As mentioned earlier, the energy subsystem exhibits interdependencies with the water subsystem in diverse energy‐carrier production processes, such that nexus variables and interactions determine the Water–Energy (WE) nexus system. Hence, to construct a robust WE nexus model, it is imperative to ascertain all relevant nexus variables—namely water withdrawal, consumption, and footprint—across the supply‐side stages of upstream extraction, midstream processing, and downstream generation. Operationally, our paradigm is characterized by (1) explicit identification of these nexus variables at each stage, (2) their definition as continuous model inputs rather than post‐hoc indicators, and (3) a structured framework enabling direct parameterization in mathematical and simulation models (e.g., system‐of‐systems networks or mixed‐integer programming). While this study focuses on establishing the database of nexus variables, the framework itself introduces a mathematical novelty by converting water–energy linkages into scenario‐ready parameters, moving beyond purely descriptive treatments. For Iran, we therefore estimate water withdrawal and consumption intensities for each segment—upstream fossil‐fuel extraction, midstream refining/processing, and downstream electricity generation—recognizing that conventional strategies such as dams, inter‐basin transfers, and groundwater extraction are proving insufficient amid rising evaporation, dust storms, falling water tables, and intense competition over scarce supplies, making water security a top national priority.

The net power generation by Iran in 2019 was 306 TWh, with 88% coming from fossil fuel sources11. Iran mostly relies on natural gas as its main fuel source for generating power, making up approximately 73% of the total generation. Most of Iran’s electricity output comes from thermal power facilities that rely on water11. Figure 2 highlights the importance of providing initial definitions pertaining to the energy supply side. The first stage of the energy production process involves the acquisition and extraction of both renewable and non-renewable fuels in their raw and unrefined state. This stage is called upstream. The output generated during this stage is subsequently utilized as the input for the processing sector as primary energy. Secondary energy, also known as secondary energy carriers, refers to the stage of energy production that encompasses the processing and refining of extracted crude fuels. This stage called midstream. The output generated by this particular sector (secondary energy carriers) functions as the input for the subsequent conversion sector, which is responsible for the operation of power plants. Final energy, also known as final energy carriers, refers to a range of energy carriers that are generated by power plants. This carrier is called electricity as final energy carrier. This stage is also called downstream. The energy generated (electricity) at this particular level functions as the input for the technology used in transmission and distribution as useful energy such as lighting, cooling, etc.11,32.

In the context of energy subsystem with the nexus approach, two frequently employed terms are utilized to denote water utilization—water withdrawal and water consumption. Water withdrawal is defined as the quantity of water that is taken from either a surface water body or an underground aquifer. Water consumption, in this context, pertains to the quantity of water that is rendered inaccessible for alternative purposes within a given water source in a particular geographic area38. Water discharged from facilities is generally not considered consumed, as it is treated and reused within the same region. In general, water consumption can be attributed to three primary factors: evaporation, utilization in the production of goods, and degradation leading to a diminished quality unsuitable for subsequent utilization3.

Energy carrier extraction from upstream to downstream stages requires substantial water resources. In this study, the nexus variables specifically focus on water withdrawal, excluding returned flows. Given the complexity and computational demands of process-based simulations, water consumption estimates at each stage utilize an integrated framework comprising water footprint, water consumption methodology, and the new nexus paradigm.

To facilitate the evaluation of the effect of energy generation on water supplies, it is beneficial to employ the established concept referred to as the water footprint. The concept of water footprint refers to the quantification of the quantity of water needed to produce goods and services that a country’s population consumes53. Different forms of water use leave distinct imprints on the water footprint. When surface and groundwater are consumed, the term “blue water” is used. What we call “green water” is actually water that seeps into the ground from sources like rain-fed farms. Lastly, the term “gray water” refers to the amount of water required to alleviate the effects of pollutant flows into the environment54. Both green and gray water footprints are involved in energy production, but these types of water usage are not taken into account in this study.

Water withdrawal and consumption of upstream and midstream flow of energy as nexus variable

The extraction of fossil fuel resources is associated with the upstream flow of energy. Petroleum, natural gas, and coal are examples of non-renewable energy sources which require water for the extraction process. Conversely, the extraction of water is not required by renewable energy sources like solar and wind power. The processing and refining of fossil fuels are classified as a midstream flow within the energy subsystem. Within this particular industry, petroleum products, including gasoline and diesel, are derived from the refining processes conducted in oil refineries. Gas refineries produce liquefied natural gas (LNG), compressed natural gas (CNG), and liquefied petroleum gas (LPG), among other gas products. Additionally, unprocessed coal is generated within coal washing facilities to serve as the necessary fuel for thermal power plants. In this study, each nexus variable (e.g., water‐consumption intensity of a given power plant) is operationalized as a static, literature‐ and data–driven lookup factor rather than as a state variable in a dynamic simulation. Specifically, we compile heat rate, cooling coefficients, and reservoir evaporation rates that directly feeds related equation. This approach allows us to quantify water use across the entire supply chain (upstream, midstream, downstream) with minimal complexity. However, that true water–energy feedback—whereby water scarcity dynamically constrains energy production—would require coupling with a water‐subsystem model (e.g., a hydrological or system‐dynamics framework) to simulate how declining water availability modulates generation capacity over time. Such an integrated system‐of‐systems model is a valuable direction for future research but lies outside the scope of the present national‐level assessment.

Water withdrawal and consumption of downstream flow of energy as nexus variable

Electricity is generated by thermal, hydro, and renewable power plants, which are all part of the downstream flow of energy. It is important to acknowledge that renewable power plants, with the exception of hydropower plants, such as wind and solar power plants, have minimal water consumption. Consequently, this prevents the exchange of nexus variables between the water and energy subsystems, resulting in the absence of any interaction. One of the benefits associated with renewable energy is their ability to mitigate the tradeoff within the nexus system. In the context of hydropower plants, one important point to make is that the water consumption associated with these facilities is relatively minimal, while the water withdrawal is considerably substantial. The quantity of water extracted by power plants for the purpose of turbine rotation is subsequently restored to the downstream river in an equivalent amount. Hence, the quantity of water consumed is not substantial. Both the withdrawal water and the consumption water are used by thermal power plants. In thermal power plants, the cooling segment is where most of the water is used. Therefore, this research aims to quantify the water footprints of thermal power plants based on their water consumption. We first describe the cooling technologies used in thermal power plants.

Cooling system classification

Thermal power plants’ cooling systems are responsible for dissipating the heat that is released into the atmosphere from the steam that is expelled by the turbines. A number of variables, including the power plant’s efficiency, the technology used, and the amount of power generated determine the magnitude of this heat. The most common cooling systems’ types in thermal power plants are once-through systems and closed-loop or recirculating systems.

The system extracts a significant amount of water from water bodies such as lake, river, etc. The water, circulated by pumps through condenser tubes, gets heated as it passes through the hot tubes. The cooling system water, extracted from the water body, absorbs the waste heat generated in the power plant. A small portion of the water extracted from water bodies is consumed in the open-cycle cooling system due to evaporation. Besides being simple and cost-effective, these systems have other advantages. If sufficient and cold water is available, compared to other cooling methods, it is the most efficient and effective. However, since the amount of water extracted by these types of systems is substantial, their environmental impacts can be detrimental. It might be possible that due to increased extraction from water bodies, the aquatic life may be endangered. Water discharged at high temperatures also poses environmental risks and can alter local ecosystems.

Recirculating cooling (closed-loop) system

Cooling systems can be categorized into two main types, namely cooling towers and swamp (artificial) cooling. Both these cooling systems operate on a similar principle and have analogous functionalities. Essentially, they utilize circulating water within a closed loop. In cooling towers, water is directed into the steam condensers to dissipate the waste heat output from the turbine. After being heated to a high temperature, the water is pumped to the cooling tower, where it is cooled by releasing air from the base of the tower. The water entering the cooling tower is heated by the airflow, which transfers that heat to the atmosphere. Evaporation is the primary factor in cooling the hot water. At the base of the cooling tower, the leftover cooled water collects and is later recycled for use in the power plant’s steam condenser.

While cooling towers utilize less water withdrawal from water bodies compared to once-through cooling systems, they have a higher water consumption rate. In the process of dispersing warm water into the ambient air within cooling towers for cooling purposes, the water, upon being cooled, is stored in the lower section of the tower due to evaporation. A portion of the warm water inside the tower evaporates and exits as water vapor. To compensate for the evaporated water in cooling towers, it is essential that water shortage for the cooling process be continually replenished from natural water bodies. Moreover, a small amount of water circulating within the cooling tower must be treated to prevent the formation of harmful contaminants. This process is commonly referred to as blowdown in cooling towers, with its focus on suspended and insoluble solids in the water. The blowdown water, before being discharged into a natural water body, must be purified and refined to eliminate the associated contaminants. Therefore, despite the relatively low water withdrawal of cooling towers, they possess some environmental impacts. However, their higher water consumption entails more cost and complexity compared to once-through cooling systems.

Dry cooling system

Air is used to cool the dry cooling system within a power plant, eliminating the necessity for water. In contrast to alternative cooling methods, it possesses lesser detrimental consequences on the environment. Nonetheless, air does not serve as an efficient fluid for heat transfer as water does. Therefore, dry cooling necessitates a larger surface area for the dissipation of wasted heat. Compared to similar wet cooling towers, the price of this cooling system is three to four times higher due to this characteristic. Dry cooling systems are commonly employed in arid regions due to the scarcity of water resources.

Hydropower plants

Using the nexus system approach, hydropower plants are one of the primary components of energy generation in the energy subsystem. Hydropower facilities produce energy by harnessing the relationship between the reservoir head and the water flowing through the turbine. This process occurs on a spatial scale that encompasses the watershed and river. Hydropower facilities generate varying amounts of energy depending on the reservoir head and the volume of water that passes through the turbine. For large-scale scenarios such as the national level, where simulation is time-consuming, it is essential to compute a database for hydroelectric water use. Thus, in this analysis, the nexus variable is simplified to a single factor, namely that amount of water needed to generate one unit of energy. This research aimed to discover the water footprint linked to the chosen hydropower plants’ electricity generation by collecting data on average yearly evaporation volume and electricity generation. Managing water resources and their uses is the primary focus of the water footprint index’s guiding principles. In particular, they can provide valuable insight for regional or national water resource and usage planning. Furthermore, the tools established through this index have the capability to regulate the global water flow, whether in real or virtual form, or by modifying the water consumption habits of a society, with the aim of minimizing the quantity of water consumed by that community in line with sustainable development principles. Hoekstra and Hong introduced the water footprint index to offer valuable insights on people’s consumption patterns, the amount of water that is consumed in the manufacture of different goods, global water exchanges, and water resources management.

The water footprint is comprised of three components: blue water, green water, and gray water. The term “blue water” refers to water that is obtained from both surface and subsurface water sources. What we call “green water” is actually just the parts of rainwater that is absorbed by the soil and subsequently evaporates or is released through transpiration before the next rainfall. Water that has been contaminated during the production process is referred to as gray water. Within the production chain, it is possible for any or all of these varieties of water to be utilized. A power plant’s “water footprint” is the total quantity of water used and contaminated by the plant in producing electricity. This study pertains to the blue water footprint.

Results

To effectively implement the WE nexus system framework for integrated water–energy planning, it is crucial to identify the key nexus variables that underpin the relationship between the energy and water subsystems. This will enable the proper management of interactions between these two subsystems. The initial phase involved gathering data and information pertaining to thermal and hydropower facilities, including HR, cooling system type, efficiency, and reservoir evaporation, from relevant organizations. The second phase involved utilizing the acquired data to ascertain the necessary parameters for conducting thermal and hydropower plant calculations. The third stage involved calculating the values of the nexus variables, namely the water consumption intensity and water footprint, utilizing the applicable relationships for thermal and hydropower plants. The water consumption of thermal and water footprint of hydropower plants in Iran, were determined by utilizing the energy data values generated from each power plant. The water footprint and water consumption of each step of energy subsystem as nexus variables in the supply-side namely upstream, midstream, and downstream is shown.

For gas turbine power plants, the sensitivity analysis revealed that the water consumption intensity remained constant at 0.25 m3/MWh across all facilities, regardless of ± 10% variations in HR. This is because gas turbine power plants do not use water-based cooling systems, and their water use is primarily limited to auxiliary operations that are not significantly influenced by thermal performance.

The sensitivity analysis performed in this study evaluated how a ± 10% variation in the HR influences water consumption intensity across various thermal power plant technologies. The results indicated that steam turbine power plants with closed-loop and once-through cooling systems exhibit considerable sensitivity. For instance, Ramin and Neka plants showed water consumption intensity variations of approximately ± 23% and ± 9.4%, respectively. Similarly, combined cycle power plants employing wet cooling towers, such as Guilan and Montazer Ghaem, displayed moderate sensitivity, with variations near ± 8.7%. Conversely, dry-cooled steam and combined cycle plants, alongside gas turbine power plants, demonstrated no sensitivity to HR variations, maintaining stable water intensity values.

These findings highlight important implications for managing water resources in regions experiencing water scarcity, such as Iran. Plants exhibiting higher sensitivity require precise operational control to minimize fluctuations in water usage. The stability observed in dry-cooled combined cycle and gas turbine plants suggests these configurations offer substantial advantages in terms of predictability and resource management efficiency. Hence, policymakers should consider transitioning toward these less-sensitive plant technologies to enhance resilience against water-related uncertainties and support sustainable energy planning under conditions of increasing water stress.

Discussion

Given Iran’s current water stress and the likelihood of a future water crisis, the significance of water resources management is greatly amplified. Thus, the energy subsystem within the WE nexus system is crucial due to its high-water consumption, making it one of the most significant subsystems. Thermal and hydropower plants are the primary sources of energy generation in Iran. The findings showed that the water consumption of steam turbine power plants has decreased, whereas the water consumption of combined cycle and gas turbine power plants has increased between 2007 and 2017. Consequently, there has been a decline in the output capacity of steam turbine power plants, while combined cycle and gas turbine power plants have experienced a rise. Additionally, the country’s decision makers have become aware of the water stress situations. This observed pattern aligns with national energy development policies aimed at mitigating water-related risks60. The Ministry of Energy of Iran has, in recent years, advocated for the transition from water-intensive steam turbine plants to more water-efficient combined cycle units60. For instance, the shift away from steam-based plants such as Ramin and Neka coincides with the implementation of water resource management directives outlined in national energy and water strategies (e.g., 5-Year Development Plans). While direct causal documentation is limited, the temporal correlation between capacity reduction in high-consumption plants and national prioritization of low-water-use technologies supports the plausibility of this interpretation. The rationale behind this decision is founded on the idea that steam turbine power plants have a water consumption intensity of 2.5 L per kilowatt hour, whereas combined cycle and gas turbine power plants have water consumption intensities of 0.25 and 1.2 L per kilowatt hour, respectively. For instance, the Neka and Jahrom power plants, which are classified as steam turbine and gas turbine power plants, respectively. The Neka thermal power plant, which utilizes steam turbine technology, has experienced a 15% reduction in its generation capacity. Conversely, the Jahrom thermal power plant, which employs gas turbine technology, has seen a 27% increase in its production capacity. Consequently, the gas power plants are experiencing a steady growth in their generation capacity in comparison to thermal power plants. This development is particularly advantageous for Iran, a country grappling with water scarcity.

The findings of this study were compared to those of research conducted in 2019 by Nouri et al.38. The study conducted by Nouri et al. (2019) on thermal power plants utilizing steam turbine technology in California, USA, revealed that the water consumption intensity for these plants, employing closed-loop cooling towers, falls within the range of 2.51–4.43 m3/MWh. Likewise, our study found that the corresponding interval for Iran is 1.85–2.95 m3/MWh. Furthermore, we compared the water consumption of Isfahan and Bistoon thermal power plants in 2017 with the data on water consumption of steam turbine technology in 2017, which was provided in the report of Tavanir Company of the Islamic Republic of Iran. The data for Tavanir company’s Isfahan and Bistoon power plants in 2017 were 7.7 MCM and 7.6 MCM, respectively. Subsequently, our study revealed that the water consumption for these power plants is 7.9 MCM and 8 MCM, respectively. The comparisons demonstrated that our study estimated the quantity of water consumed by thermal power plants suitably.

Based on Fig. 9, the thermal power plants Ramin and Neka, which use steam turbine technology, have the highest water consumption among all steam turbine power plants. Ramin consumes an average of 22.4 MCM per year, while Neka consumes an average of 26.6 MCM per year. As it is evident, their water usage per generation of one kilowatt hour of energy is very high, ranging from 2.04 to 2.65. These power plants, which consume a significant amount of water, can be retired and decommissioned. They can be replaced with combined cycle power plants, whose capacity is currently expanding in the country. This study demonstrated the feasibility of enhancing the capacity of Neishabour, Abadan, Rajaei, Kerman, Damavand, Fars, and Kazeroun combined cycle power plants. These power plants, with a significantly reduced water consumption intensity of 0.18 L per kilowatt hour, can serve as replacements for retired power plants. If the total annual average energy generation of the Ramin and Neka power plants is generated by the combined cycle power plants stated, the water consumption will fall from 48,978,227.13 to 3,782,634.5 resulting in an average reduction of approximately 16 times the water consumption in the power plants.

The energy generation of hydropower plants is contingent upon two variables: the reservoir’s net head and the power plant’s turbine flow rate. The amount of energy that can be stored in a reservoir is directly proportional to its height. The water gains kinetic energy as it is released from the reservoir’s back end and flows towards the turbine of the power plant. By reducing the volume of water passing through the turbine, more energy is generated. The reason why hydropower plants with a bigger water footprint produce less energy is due to their lower reservoir head. Take, for instance, the Aras and Masjed Soleyman power plants. Figure 16 clearly indicates that the Aras power plant had the lowest amount of energy output, while the Masjed Soleyman power plant had the highest amount of energy production between 2007 and 2017. Figure 18 clearly demonstrates that the Aras hydropower plant has a significantly larger water footprint compared to the Masjed Soleyman power plant. Table 5 reveals that the head of Aras reservoir measures 42 m, whereas the height of Masjed Soleyman stands at 177 m, making it 4 times taller than the Aras reservoir. Due to its high head, the Masjed Soleyman power plant is able to generate more energy with less flow going through the turbine. In contrast, the Aras power plant generates less energy as more flow passes through.

Furthermore, as stated earlier, the average energy output from hydropower plants in 2017 has plummeted by 70% compared to the initial years. Concurrently, there has been a 49% rise in the average water footprint. This implies that we have augmented the rate at which water is being channeled through the turbine in power plants with a reservoir with low head, while diminishing it in power plants with a high reservoir head. As an illustration, let’s take a look at the Aras power plants, which now have the smallest reservoir level measuring 42 m, and the Karun-3 power plant, which has the greatest reservoir level at 205 m. The water footprint of Aras Power Plant is on average 301 thousand cubic meters, representing a 39% increase compared to the earlier years when it was around 200 thousand cubic meters. Concurrently, the Karun-3 power plant has achieved a 50% reduction in its average water footprint in comparison to the initial years of electricity generation.

It is recommended that the policy makers and decision makers in Iran’s hydropower sector reduce capacity of power plants that have a low ratio of energy generated to water vaporized, or a high-water footprint. They should also focus on increasing the generation capacity of power plants that have a lower water footprint compared to the energy produced. For example, the Aras power plant has an energy-to-water vaporization ratio of 0.71, whereas the Karkheh power plant has a ratio of 2.06. Comparatively, the Masjed Soleyman power plant has an energy-to-water vaporization ratio of 223, whereas the Karun-1 power plant has a ratio of 37. Consequently, according to the results of this study, it is evident that the Aras and Karkheh power plants exhibit lower efficiency in electricity generation. Therefore, it would be more advantageous to decommission these plants and instead focus on enhancing the production capacity of the Karun-4 power plants. The Karun-4 power plants are exclusively electricity-based and possess a greater reservoir head, making them more favorable for electricity generation. Considering the unfavorable state of Iran’s water resources, it is advisable for policymakers and decision-makers to refrain from relying on thermal power plants that use steam turbine technology. Instead, they should focus on enhancing the production capacity of combined cycle and gas turbine power plants.

Our findings underscore critical implications for Iran’s Ministry of Energy, particularly in light of the country’s escalating water stress. The Ministry should prioritize transitioning away from water-intensive steam turbine power plants—such as Ramin and Neka—and invest in expanding dry-cooled combined-cycle and gas turbine technologies, which exhibit significantly lower water consumption intensities. Furthermore, modernizing existing plants through retrofitting cooling systems and adopting dry cooling where feasible would yield substantial water savings. An integrated water–energy planning framework should be institutionalized to guide future infrastructure decisions by explicitly considering water constraints as part of energy development policy.

In addition, the Ministry should increase support for non-hydro renewable energy, particularly solar and wind, which offer near-zero water consumption. Hydropower operations should also be optimized based on water footprint efficiency, with reduced reliance on plants such as Aras and Karkheh that consume disproportionate volumes of water per unit energy. To support evidence-based policymaking, we recommend implementing standardized monitoring of water use in power generation and encouraging public–private partnerships to scale up adoption of water-efficient technologies. These strategies will enhance the resilience of Iran’s energy sector while safeguarding its increasingly scarce water resources.

Conclusion

This study examined the nexus between the energy subsystem in the energy supply and generation sector and the water subsystem, considering the significance of water resources management in Iran and the challenges posed by water stress. In this study, the novel paradigm of the nexus system approach was introduced to address WE nexus system better. Examining the connection between these two subsystems can assist in the comprehensive energy and water management using the novel nexus system approach. By analyzing the correlation factors between the energy and water subsystems and determining nexus variables, the WE nexus approach allows for the modeling of the holistic WE nexus system. This technique concentrates on determining the appropriate water consumption and footprint for energy generation. This study assessed the water footprint of hydroelectric and water consumption of thermal power plants generating facilities as nexus variables during 2007–2017. This study evaluated three types of thermal power plants, namely those utilizing gas turbine, combined cycle, steam turbine technologies and their cooling types, in terms of their water usage. This research investigated the water footprint nexus variable in hydropower plants and found that both the Aras and Karkheh power plants in Iran are inefficient in energy production and contribute to water wastage during water stress conditions. Furthermore, after examining thermal power plants and the nexus variable of water consumption in the energy production process, it was concluded that power plants utilizing steam turbine technology ought to be relocated away from other power plants employing the same technology. This is due to the substantial water consumption in the cooling system. Instead, it is recommended that these power plants be relocated towards combined cycle and gas turbine power plants. Based on the findings of this study, we recommend that Iran’s Ministry of Energy prioritize the retirement or retrofitting of high water-consuming steam turbine power plants, particularly those using once-through or wet cooling technologies. Investment should be directed toward expanding combined cycle and gas turbine plants with dry cooling systems to reduce water intensity in power generation. Additionally, optimization of hydropower operations based on water footprint efficiency should be pursued, favoring high-head plants with lower evaporation losses. Establishing an integrated water–energy planning framework, incorporating standardized monitoring of water use, will be essential to support evidence-based decision-making under Iran’s increasing water stress.

Limitation of the study

This study, while comprehensive in scope, is subject to several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, thermal power plant efficiencies were assumed constant across the study period (2007–2017), which may not reflect real-world operational variations due to aging infrastructure or maintenance improvements. Similarly, for hydropower plants, annual evaporation rates were treated as temporally static, without accounting for interannual climatic variability that may affect reservoir water loss.

Moreover, while the nexus framework integrates upstream, midstream, and downstream subsystems, we excluded returned flows and energy consumption during extraction and refining stages due to data unavailability. This may slightly underestimate the water footprint of fossil fuel supply chains. In addition, while our plant-level data is robust, national extrapolation assumes representativeness across different geographic and technological contexts, which may introduce some generalization error. Lastly, our analysis did not model socio-political factors or behavioral responses to policy changes, which may affect the practical implementation of the recommendations. Future work could integrate dynamic system modeling or remote sensing data to address these dimensions.

Pathway for the future integration

To maintain analytical focus and methodological rigor, this national-scale study concentrated solely on how water supports energy generation, deliberately excluding the reverse linkage—how energy availability and costs constrain water services such as pumping, treatment, and distribution. Quantifying these dependencies requires high-resolution, plant-level energy use and tariff data, which are not available nationwide, and including them would necessitate shifting toward a fully coupled demand–supply model with significantly increased complexity. Our staged approach aligns with best practices in nexus research, which recommend progressive integration of interdependencies as data quality improves. Additionally, our study follows the blue-water footprint methodology, which, in line with ISO standards, accounts only for net water removal via evaporation or incorporation and excludes non-consumptive return flows unless irreversibly degraded. In Iran, return flows from once-through and closed-loop cooling systems generally rejoin their source basins, and consistent national-level data on their quality and timing remain unavailable, making speculative estimates methodologically inappropriate. By focusing on withdrawal and consumption, we deliver transparent, reproducible supply-side nexus variables (Tables 6, 7, 8, 9, 10 and 11) that align with international guidelines and reflect operational water losses. Recognizing that thermal discharges may carry ecological risks, we suggest that future studies integrate return-flow degradation coefficients once robust environmental monitoring data become available. To advance this research, we recommend three specific pathways: (1) assessing thermal plant return flows in terms of temperature and contaminant loads using hydro-thermal modeling; (2) using remote sensing to correlate drought conditions with reduced energy output over time; and (3) evaluating catchment-level renewable water availability and its relation to hydropower and thermal plant footprints. These steps would significantly enhance the scientific and practical utility of Iran’s water–energy nexus assessments.
