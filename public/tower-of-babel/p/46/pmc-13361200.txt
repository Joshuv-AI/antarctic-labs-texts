Epidemiology and Characteristics of Antibiotic Resistance of ESKAPE and E. coli Bloodstream Infections in Patients with Hematological Malignancies: High ESBL and Carbapenem Resistance in a 5-Year Single-Center Retrospective Study
Authors: Chunyan Gao, Guangjuan Gao, Feifei Kong, Panfei Hou
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo analyze epidemiology and characteristics of antibiotic resistance of ESKAPE and Escherichia coli bloodstream infections (BSI) in hematological malignancy patients.MethodsRetrospective study of ESKAPE and Escherichia coli BSI cases for 5 years at Shanxi Bethune Hospital. Species identification used MALDI-TOF MS, and susceptibility testing followed CLSI guidelines. Carbapenemase phenotype was confirmed by mCIM/eCIM. Chi-square tests compared resistance and mortality used SPSS27.0.ResultsAmong 180 isolates, Gram-negative bacilli were predominated (162/180, 90.0%), including 74 Escherichia coli, 49 Klebsiella pneumoniae and 22 Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Among Gram-positive cocci, Enterococcus faecium accounted for 12 isolates and Staphylococcus aureus for 6 isolates. ESBL rate was 44.1% in Escherichia coli significantly higher than in Klebsiella pneumoniae (36.8%, p = 0.466). Carbapenems showed higher activity against Escherichia coli overall (meropenem 87.5%), but susceptibility was lower in Klebsiella pneumoniae (66.7%) and Pseudomonas aeruginosa (68%). CRKP mortality was 36.4% vs 10.5% for CSKP (p = 0.063). MRSA rate was 50% (3/6); one VREfm isolate was detected.ConclusionMultidrug-resistant Enterobacterales remain the most significant threat to hematological tumor patients, with CRKP requiring particular attention. Vancomycin and linezolid maintain high activity against Enterococcus faecium and Staphylococcus aureus.

Introduction

Bloodstream infection (BSI) represents a common and frequently fatal complication in patients with hematological diseases, particularly those who develop profound neutropenia following chemotherapy or hematopoietic stem cell transplantation.1 In 2008, Rice LB coined the acronym “ESKAPE” to encompass six bacterial groups (Enterococcus faecium, E. faecium; Staphylococcus aureus, S. aureus; Klebsiella pneumoniae, K. pneumoniae; Acinetobacter baumannii, A. baumannii; Pseudomonas aeruginosa, P. aeruginosa; and Enterobacter species) that are prevalent in hospital environments and associated with antimicrobial resistance.2 Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) has become a major threat to global health. Xu3 study reveals that by 2021, bacterial AMR was a significant contributor to mortality in China, with bloodstream infections being particularly lethal. Given that patients with hematological malignancies constitute a high-risk population for ESKAPE and Escherichia coli (E. coli) bloodstream infections, understanding the epidemiological distribution and resistance characteristics of these pathogens in this specific patient cohort is indispensable for guiding initial empirical antimicrobial therapy, improving clinical outcomes, and formulating effective infection prevention and control strategies. At our institution, historical surveillance data indicate ESBL rates among E. coli of approximately 48.6%, though specific data in hematological patients have not been previously reported. This study specifically examines pathogen distribution, underlying hematological diagnoses, and antimicrobial resistance profiles. The 2021–2025 time frame was selected to capture post-pandemic resistance trends following updated institutional antibiotic stewardship protocols implemented in late 2020. While ESKAPE encompasses both Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens, previous studies suggest Gram-negative bacilli predominate in hematological BSI, based on the data from this study, we focus on analyzing the situation of Gram-negative bacilli. Thereby providing scientific evidence for rational antibiotic utilization and hospital infection control.

Materials and Methods

Study Population

We retrospectively reviewed data from all patients in the Department of Hematology at Shanxi Bethune Hospital (Taiyuan, Shanxi, China) with positive blood cultures for ESKAPE and E. coli pathogens between January 2021 and December 2025. Inclusion criteria were: (1) confirmed diagnosis of a hematological disorder; (2) isolation of a definitive pathogen from blood culture specimens; and (3) complete antimicrobial susceptibility testing results. Duplicate isolates from the same patient (same species) were excluded, with only the first isolate included in the resistance analysis. A total of 180 positive blood culture specimens were ultimately enrolled, with patient demographic information, underlying diseases, Absolute Neutrophil Count (ANC), pathogen identification, and antimicrobial susceptibility results extracted from the laboratory information system. This retrospective study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Shanxi Bethune Hospital (Approval No. YXLL-2024-156) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The study utilized laboratory test results (blood culture identification and antimicrobial susceptibility data) and medical records obtained during routine clinical care from January 2021 to December 2025. No additional biological specimens were collected, and no interventional procedures were performed. Given the retrospective nature of the study and the use of anonymized clinical data, the requirement for individual informed consent was waived by the ethics committee. Patients had provided broad consent for the use of de-identified medical data for research purposes upon hospital admission, and no patient had explicitly opted out of research use during the study period.

Strain Identification and Antimicrobial Susceptibility Testing

Positive blood culture specimens were subcultured and isolated using standard microbiological procedures. Species identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS, Bruker Daltonics, Bremen, Germany). Antimicrobial susceptibility testing was conducted using the VITEK 2 Compact system (bioMérieux, France) and the Kirby-Bauer disk diffusion method (Oxoid, UK), with results interpreted according to the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines.4 Tigecycline adopts the discount point recommended by the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA).5 Carbapenemase production was confirmed using the modified carbapenem inactivation method (mCIM) and EDTA-modified carbapenem inactivation method (eCIM) according to CLSI guidelines. Quality control strains, including Klebsiella pneumoniae ATCC 700603, ATCC BAA-1705, ATCC BAA-2146, Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27853, Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25913, Escherichia coli ATCC 25922, and Staphylococcus aureus ATCC 25923, were obtained from the China National Health Inspection Center.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistical methods were employed for analysis. The distribution of pathogens across different underlying hematological diseases (eg, acute myeloid leukemia, myelodysplastic syndromes) and associated clinical characteristics were analyzed Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare resistance rates and mortality between groups, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 27.0. Data were compiled using WHONET 2023.

Results

Overall Pathogen Distribution

A total of 180 ESKAPE and E. coli pathogens were isolated. Gram-negative bacilli predominated, accounting for 90% (162/180), with E. coli being the most frequently identified group. The specific distribution included: E. coli (74, 41.1%), K. pneumoniae (49, 27.2%), Enterobacter species (16, 8.9%), P. aeruginosa (22, 12.1%), and A. baumannii (1, 0.6%). Among Gram-positive cocci, E. faecium accounted for 12 isolates (6.7%) and S. aureus for 6 isolates (3.3%). Clinical Characteristics of Infected Patients

A total of 180 patients, all of them had confirmed hematological disorders, with ages ranging from 18 to 88 years (median 51 years). Male patients (103, 57.2%) were predominated. Acute Myeloid Leukemia (AML) was the most common underlying disease (101, 56.1%), representing the primary disease association across all pathogen categories. This was followed by Acute Lymphoblastic Leukemia (ALL) (27, 15.0%), Multiple Myeloma (MM) (20, 11.1%), Myelodysplastic Syndromes (MDS) (12, 6.7%), Aplastic Anemia (AA) (11, 6.1%), and other hematological malignancies (9, 5.0%). The majority of infections occurred during active disease phases or chemotherapy-induced neutropenia. Discussion

Patients with hematological malignancies are profoundly immunocompromised due to the underlying disease itself and intensive therapeutic interventions, including aggressive chemotherapy and hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, rendering them highly susceptible to bloodstream infections6 that often progress rapidly and are associated with substantial mortality.7 ESKAPE and E. coli are not only important pathogens of hospital acquired infections but also a focus of attention for bloodstream infections. As a collection of multidrug-resistant organisms, ESKAPE pathogens not only complicate anti-infective therapy but are also closely associated with adverse clinical outcomes. Therefore, systematic analysis of the clinical and microbiological characteristics of ESKAPE bloodstream infections in this patient population is of paramount importance for guiding precision antimicrobial therapy and optimizing infection prevention and control strategies. Retrospective analysis of ESKAPE and E. coli resistance phenotype of patients with hematological malignancies has a certain guiding role in the empirical medication.

E. coli, K. pneumoniae, and P. aeruginosa are the most common pathogenic bacteria for bloodstream infections. Our findings demonstrate that Gram-negative bacilli represent the absolute predominant group (90.0%) of ESKAPE and E. coli bloodstream infections in hematological malignancy patients at our institution, particularly E. coli and K. pneumoniae, being the most frequently isolated pathogens, followed by P. aeruginosa.

The underlying mechanisms are multifactorial.: first, Hematological patients, particularly those receiving high-intensity chemotherapy or transplantation, frequently develop severe neutropenia and intestinal mucosal barrier disruption (mucositis), facilitating the translocation of enteric commensal organisms into the bloodstream.8,9 The widespread use of central venous catheters provides a portal of entry for skin and environmental pathogens such as P. aeruginosa. We also identified acute myeloid leukemia (AML) as the highest-risk population for ESKAPE bloodstream infections (56%), which correlates with the intensive chemotherapy regimens, prolonged neutropenia, extended hospitalization, and frequent invasive procedures characteristic of this disease.10 These epidemiological features suggest that enhanced intestinal microecological management, rigorous central venous catheter maintenance, and strict aseptic protocols are critical preventive measures for ESKAPE bloodstream infections in hematological patients, particularly those with AML.

Our antimicrobial susceptibility data reveal a concerning resistance landscape, particularly among Gram-negative bacilli. The ESBL detection rate of 44.1% in E. coli is consistent with national CHINET and CARSS surveillance data and regional reports from Shanxi province,11,12 while the notably lower rate of 36.8% in K. pneumoniae aligns with the global trend of lower K. pneumoniae ESBL rates compared to E. coli.13 In this five-year study of bloodstream infections in patients with hematological malignancies, we observed marked annual fluctuations in carbapenem resistance among E. coli isolates: ertapenem resistance peaked at 40% in 2023 but declined to 0% by 2025. Although concerning, this temporary increase did not persist, and the very low carbapenem resistance observed in 2024–2025 supports their continued use as empirical therapy in this high-risk population. Resistance rates to ceftriaxone and ciprofloxacin exceeded 75% in 2023 and subsequently decreased to approximately 33% and 50%, respectively, suggesting a potential impact of antimicrobial stewardship or changes in epidemiology. Importantly, piperacillin-tazobactam (resistance rate 7–22%), amikacin (0–9%), and tigecycline (0%) maintained consistently low resistance rates throughout the study period, positioning them as reliable alternative therapeutic options, particularly when carbapenem-sparing strategies are desired. For K. pneumoniae, resistance rates also showed annual fluctuations, peaking in 2023 (50% for ertapenem/meropenem) and declining to 0% by 2025. Tigecycline maintained high activity (94% susceptible; 0% resistance in four of five years), supporting its use against CRKP infections, though its low serum concentrations warrant caution in bloodstream infections. Amikacin (86% susceptible) and piperacillin-tazobactam (74% susceptible) remain useful but should be guided by susceptibility testing. Resistance to ceftriaxone (51%), fluoroquinolones (43–46%), gentamicin (42%), and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole (49%) was persistently high, precluding their empirical use. Given the small annual sample sizes (as low as n = 7 in 2023), these fluctuations should be interpreted cautiously. The emergence of CRKP poses a substantial threat. Of greater concern is the detection of carbapenemase-producing isolates (including metallo-β-lactamases and serine carbapenemases) in K. pneumoniae, resulting in reduced susceptibility to imipenem (77.6%) and meropenem (66.7%). As this study employed phenotypic carbapenemase confirmatory testing, definitive resistance mechanisms could not be determined and warrant further investigation.

The emergence and dissemination of CRKP pose a substantial threat to clinical management, often resulting in limited therapeutic options and increased patient mortality.14 In this study, the mortality rate in the CRKP group was 36.4% (4/11), versus 10.5% (4/38) in the CSKP group. Although the Fisher’s exact test showed no statistically significant difference (P = 0.063)—likely due to the small sample size—the substantially higher mortality in the CRKP group underscores the need for heightened clinical vigilance when CRKP is identified, given the greater therapeutic challenges it presents.

P. aeruginosa maintained high susceptibility (>95%) to multiple antipseudomonal β-lactams (piperacillin-tazobactam, ceftazidime, cefepime) and aminoglycosides (amikacin, gentamicin), offering effective options for empirical therapy. However, carbapenem resistance rates exceeding 30% indicate increasing selection pressure for carbapenem-resistant P. aeruginosa (CRPA), consistent with findings by Falcone et al15 and Tenover et al.16 Carbapenems using for P. aeruginosa requires attention to drug resistance.

Enterobacter species extracted from clinical samples are usually reported as E. cloacae, and sometimes E. asburiae, E. hormaechei, or E. kobei. They are all members of E. cloacae complex (ECC), among the 16 strains of Enterobacter species we isolated, 15 strains belonged to the ECC. This distribution pattern is highly consistent with previous reports from both domestic and international studies.17–20 Ceftriaxone resistance was 50% and meropenem resistance approximately 20%, indicating the emergence of carbapenem resistance within this group. Notably, all Enterobacter isolates remained 100% susceptible to amikacin and 93.8% susceptible to tigecycline, suggesting these agents as potential therapeutic options. The emergence of carbapenem resistance in Enterobacter species has been increasingly recognized as a global concern. Recent genomic surveillance in Japan21 revealed that high-risk clonal groups ST78 and ST133 account for over 60% of carbapenemase-producing ECC isolates, yet most retained susceptibility to amikacin (99.5%) and tigecycline (88.3%), findings consistent with our observations.

Among the S. aureus isolates in this study, the MRSA detection rate of 50.0% is consistent with previous domestic and international reports.22,23 Notably, all S. aureus isolates retained 100% susceptibility to vancomycin, linezolid, and tigecycline, ensuring that glycopeptides and oxazolidinones remain reliable cornerstone therapies for suspected or confirmed MRSA bloodstream infections. In contrast, E. faecium presented a more alarming resistance profile. Near-universal resistance to fluoroquinolones and macrolides was observed. The high prevalence of high-level gentamicin resistance (41.7%) and High-level streptomycin resistance 45.5% indicates that synergistic combination therapy with cell wall-active agents (ampicillin or vancomycin) and aminoglycosides would be ineffective for serious infections caused by these strains. Most critically, we detected one vancomycin-resistant E. faecium (VREfm) isolate. Although the current VREfm detection rate remains low (8.3%) and all E. faecium isolates retained 100% susceptibility to linezolid and tigecycline, this finding serves as a critical warning signal. The emergence of VREfm is often associated with selective pressure from glycopeptide use, and its genetic determinants can undergo horizontal transfer within the enterococcal genus and potentially to other Gram-positive pathogens such as S. aureus,24 posing substantial public health risks. These findings underscore the necessity for intensified antimicrobial resistance surveillance of enterococci, particularly E. faecium, and stringent stewardship of glycopeptide antibiotics.

Based on our single-center findings, the following empirical antimicrobial considerations may be cautiously suggested for this high-risk population, recognizing that therapeutic decisions must be individualized and guided by local susceptibility patterns, patient-specific factors, and ongoing culture results: (1) fluoroquinolones should generally be avoided empirically due to >50% resistance in E. coli and K. pneumoniae; (2) tigecycline or amikacin may represent alternative options for suspected Gram-negative infections, given their high susceptibility rates in E. coli (100% and 91.9%, respectively) and Enterobacter species (93.8% and 100%, respectively); (3) vancomycin or linezolid remain reasonable choices for suspected Gram-positive infections, as all S. aureus and E. faecium isolates in this cohort remained susceptible; and (4) carbapenems should be used judiciously, particularly when CRKP or carbapenem-resistant Enterobacter species is suspected. Given the retrospective, single-center design of this study and the relatively small sample sizes in subgroup analyses, these recommendations should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than definitive practice guidelines. Multi-center prospective studies with larger cohorts and molecular characterization of resistance mechanisms are urgently needed to validate these findings and refine therapeutic strategies for this vulnerable patient population.

This study has several limitations. First, it is a single-center retrospective study, which limits generalizability. Second, small subgroup sizes (eg, A. baumannii, VREfm) precluded robust statistical analysis. Third, we did not collect data on neutropenia severity, prior antibiotic exposure, or central line use, which are important confounders. Fourth, carbapenemase typing was phenotypic only; molecular characterization was not performed. These limitations should be considered when interpreting our findings and formulating therapeutic recommendations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, ESKAPE and E. coli bloodstream infections in patients with hematological malignancies at our institution are predominantly caused by Gram-negative bacilli, with E. coli and K. pneumoniae being the most frequent pathogens. Antimicrobial resistance is highly prevalent, characterized by high ESBL production rate in E. coli and K. pneumoniae. Carbapenem resistance has emerged as a serious concern, particularly in K. pneumoniae and Enterobacter species, and was associated with increased mortality in CRKP cases.
