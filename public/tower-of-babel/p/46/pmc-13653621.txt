Directional Second Sphere Effect on CO2 Activation and Reduction by Iron Porphyrin in Aprotic and Protic Media
Authors: Ashutosh Vishwakarma, Sami Essid, Sarah Bouery, Régis Guillot, Bernard Boitrel, Philipp Gotico, Marie Sircoglou, Zakaria Halime
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe topological directionality of functional groups in the secondary coordination sphere (SCS) plays a decisive role in enzymatic small‐molecule activation but remains underexplored in synthetic systems. Here, we investigate how symmetry remodeling of a urea‐based SCS modulates electrocatalytic CO2‐to‐CO reduction using two atropisomeric iron porphyrins, αβαβ‐UrFe and α2β2 ‐UrFe, which differ solely in the spatial arrangement of identical hydrogen‐bond donor groups. Switching from a face‐to‐face to an adjacent urea‐arms configuration enables unprecedented CO2‐to‐CO conversion under strictly aprotic conditions, in the absence of Brønsted or Lewis acids. Combined electrochemical, spectroscopic, kinetic, and DFT studies reveal that the adjacent positioning of the urea arms weakens the stabilization of the initial [Fe─CO2] intermediate but creates a more open and dynamically reorganizable SCS that permits insertion of a second CO2 molecule as an oxygen‐atom acceptor, to yield CO and CO3 2ˉ. Under protic conditions, this improved active‐site accessibility, combined with a dissymmetrization of the activated CO2 molecule, accelerates proton transfer, with CO2‐to‐CO reduction reaching turnover frequency of up to 2.72 × 107 s−1, among the highest reported for homogeneous catalysis. These results establish directional interactions within the SCS as powerful design strategy for boosting molecular catalysis.Keywords: carbon dioxide, directionality, electrocatalysis, hydrogen‐bond, second coordination sphere

Introduction

The activation and redox transformation of small, relatively inert atmospheric molecules such as CO2, O2, and N2 under mild conditions represents one of the most fundamental and challenging goals in both biological and energy‐related synthetic chemistry [1]. Metalloenzymes have evolved highly efficient strategies to activate these molecules under ambient conditions by leveraging both the primary coordination environment and the surrounding protein matrix [2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9]. In particular, the second coordination sphere (SCS) plays a critical role in modulating reactivity through non‐covalent interactions, hydrogen bonding networks, and electrostatic fields. In contrast to synthetic catalysts, which often adopt idealized high‐symmetry geometries when incorporating multiple SCS functional groups due to synthetic constraints [10, 11, 12, 13], metalloenzymes feature lower‐symmetry SCS architectures that play an important role in modulating electronic structure, redox properties, and substrate binding affinities. Enzymes such as cytochrome P450s [14, 15], carbon monoxide dehydrogenases [2, 5, 16, 17], and nitrogenases [8, 9] exemplify the functional advantages of low‐symmetry active sites. These configurations favor directional interactions with highly symmetric substrates such as CO2, O2, and N2, allowing their controlled activation by dissymmetrization to favor the formation of polar intermediates through dynamic interactions [18].

Several recent studies have systematically examined how the topological positioning of SCS functional groups influences the electrocatalytic CO2 reduction reaction (CO2RR) by synthesizing distinct atropisomers within the porphyrin catalyst family (Scheme 1). In 2020, our group reported that the αα and αβ atropisomers of an iron porphyrin bearing two urea groups (Scheme 1a) catalyze CO2RR at comparable overpotentials, yet with markedly higher catalytic rates for the αβ atropisomer [19]. Experimental and computational evidences suggest that although CO2 binding is more favored in the αα configuration, the αβ allows a more efficient proton access to the distal oxygen of the mono‐urea activated CO2, facilitating C─O bond cleavage. In contrast, Zhang and Cao later reported a catalytic superiority for the αα atropisomer of iron porphyrins bearing trimethylbenzylamine substituents compared to the αβ analog (Scheme 1b). DFT calculations indicate that this arrangement allows one group to interact with the CO2 adduct while the other engages the proton source via electrostatic interactions. Likewise, Jiang and co‐workers observed enhanced catalytic activity for the αα atropisomer of zinc porphyrins featuring triazole units (Scheme 1c), which are proposed to facilitate proton transfer from the hydrophilic network of the bulk solvent to the CO2‐bound intermediate [20]. In 2021, Mayer and co‐workers reported that the orientation of cationic trimethylanilinium groups in iron porphyrins is optimal in the αααα atropisomer (Scheme 1d) which achieves the highest CO2RR rate but at the cost of the highest overpotential [21]. The correlation between turnover frequency (TOF) and overpotential points to an electronic scaling effect driven by the electron‐withdrawing nature of the cationic substituents [22], while the enhanced catalytic rates likely arise from favorable through‐space electrostatic interaction, consistent with previous observations for imidazolium‐based cationic moieties [23]. In heterogeneous catalysis, the group of Lu, Peng, and Zhang exploited Cu porphyrin atropisomers bearing salicylimine groups (Scheme 1e), where the αααα atropisomer favors CO formation whereas the αβαβ atropisomer favors CH4 production when heterogenized on a carbon‐based electrode [24]. Collectively, these studies highlight the potential of atropisomerism as a design principle for tuning the electrocatalytic activity and selectivity of porphyrin complexes by modulating substrate binding, activation, and proton co‐substrate delivery [25]. Furthermore, studies involving hydrogen‐bond donors typically compare atropisomers featuring varying numbers of functionalities within each SCS site (e.g., αααα vs αβαβ, αα vs αβ). A systematic approach would thus be essential to disentangle the effects of positioning and directionality from those related to the number of SCS functionalities involved in the reaction.

In this work, we leverage a unique opportunity arising from the geometric differences between the α2β2 and αβαβ atropisomers of a urea‐functionalized iron porphyrin catalyst to investigate the impact of SCS symmetry on the catalytic performances in the electrochemical CO2‐to‐CO reduction. We previously reported a D2d symmetric αβαβ‐UrFe catalyst [26], with two urea groups acting as multipoint H‐bond donors positioned in a face‐to‐face configuration on each side of the porphyrin macrocycle (Scheme 2). In contrast, the α2β2 ‐UrFe catalyst was designed with a lower C2h symmetry, where the two urea groups on each side of the porphyrin are located at adjacent meso positions. These systems present a rare and controlled case in which both catalysts bear identical numbers and types of functional groups in the SCS, differing only in their spatial arrangement, with each exhibiting identical SCS on both faces of the porphyrin ring. This structural configuration provides an ideal platform to probe how SCS directionality modulates the balance between CO2 capture, stabilization, and reactivity, ultimately influencing the catalytic activity for CO2 reduction. Remarkably, the α2β2 ‐UrFe catalyst demonstrates an unprecedented ability to catalyze this reaction under aprotic media, without any external proton source or Lewis acid, producing CO and CO3 2ˉ. Under protic conditions, switching from the αβαβ to the α2β2 configuration boosts turnover frequency (TOF) by three orders of magnitude, achieving, to the best or our knowledge, one of the highest reported rates for homogeneous CO2‐to‐CO electrocatalysis (k cat = 2.72 × 107 s−1) by an iron‐porphyrin‐based catalyst. Below, we present comprehensive electrochemical, kinetic, spectroscopic, and computational studies that reveal how this subtle tuning of SCS directionality modulates CO2 activation while dynamically reorganizing the SCS to accommodate co‐substrates such as water and a second CO2 molecule as oxide acceptor, thereby dictating the observed reactivity differences.

Results and Discussion

The α2β2‐UrFe catalyst was synthesized by adapting our previously reported synthetic strategy for αβαβ‐UrFe [26]. All synthetic procedures (Figure S1) and characterizations (Figures S24–S30) are gathered in the Supporting Information (SI). The retention of the initial porphyrin geometry throughout the multistep synthesis of the ligand was first confirmed by nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy (Figures S26–S28), which indicated the exclusive presence of a single atropisomer. Further confirmation of the three‐dimensional structure of α2β2 ‐UrFe was obtained via single‐crystal X‐ray diffraction, using crystals grown by slow evaporation of a saturated solution in a mixture of acetone, methanol, and water (Scheme 2 and Figure S23; Table S8 provides detailed crystallographic parameters). All catalysts studied here were used in their Fe(III)─Cl state with a chloride axial ligand. However, during the crystallization process for single‐crystal X‐ray diffraction analysis, the Clˉ ligand was displaced by methoxide (from methanol) in the case αβαβ‐UrFe and hydroxide (from water) in the case of α2β2 ‐UrFe. Comparison of the solid‐state structures of α2β2 ‐UrFe with that of αβαβ‐UrFe highlights the influence of porphyrin symmetry on the spatial arrangement of the urea groups in the SCS. In αβαβ‐UrFe, the two urea moieties are positioned in an almost linear arrangement with an angle of 162° between the carbon atoms of the urea groups and the axial oxygen atom bound to the iron center. In contrast, α2β2 ‐UrFe exhibits a significantly more acute angle of 106° between the equivalent atoms. This geometric divergence shows that the symmetry of the porphyrin core significantly modulates the spatial orientation of the SCS functional groups, potentially leading to different interaction modes with the linear CO2 substrate.

To investigate the effect of this orientation difference on the electrocatalytic properties of the urea modified iron–porphyrin for CO2‐to‐CO reduction, the cyclic voltammogram (CV) of α2β2 ‐UrFe (1 mM) was first recorded in argon (Ar)‐purged, anhydrous dimethylformamide (DMF) containing 0.1 M of tetra‐N‐butylammonium hexafluorophosphate ([Bu4N]PF6) as the supporting electrolyte. As expected for an iron porphyrin, three reversible redox waves corresponding to the formal Fe(III/II), Fe(II/I), and Fe(I/0) redox events were observed (Figure 1a), with the latter two more precisely assigned as FeIIPor/FeIIPor‒ and FeIIPor/FeIIPor2‒, respectively. While the metal‐centered Fe(III/II) reduction exhibits minimal variation between the two atropisomers, the ligand‐based reductions show more pronounced differences, with α2β2 ‐UrFe displaying a 60 mV cathodic shift in the formal Fe(II/I) couple and 90 mV cathodic shift in the formal Fe(I/0) couple relative to αβαβ‐UrFe (Table S1). This shift is surprising, as both catalysts experience the same inductive electron‐withdrawing influence from the four urea arms. The calculated diffusion coefficients (Table S3) indicate a smaller hydrodynamic size of αβαβ‐UrFe (D = 1.40 × 10−6 cm2 s−1) compared to α2β2 ‐UrFe (D = 0.69 × 10−6 cm2 s−1), which could result from a more compact arrangement of the urea arms in solution (Figure S33) thereby facilitating its reduction on the electrode surface.

UV–vis spectroscopy was employed to probe potential electronic differences between the two atropisomers that could account for the observed variations in redox potentials. In their ground‐state Fe(III)–Cl form (Figure 1b), the iron porphyrin catalysts display different spectral features. Notably, the Soret band shifts from 436 to 424 nm and the Q band from 550 to 580 nm when comparing αβαβ‐UrFe to α2β2 ‐UrFe. At this oxidation state, these differences likely arise from distinct interaction modes with the axial chloride ligand, depending on the relative positioning of the urea groups, which may induce subtle distortions of the porphyrin macrocycle. Upon reduction to the Fe(II) state, accompanied by chloride dissociation, and further to the Fe(I) and Fe(0) states, no significant spectral differences are observed between the atropisomers (Figure 1c–e). These findings further suggest that the electrochemical discrepancies are unlikely to originate from intrinsic electronic differences, but rather from electrode–molecule interfacial interactions that are not captured under homogeneous spectroscopic conditions.

When the reaction atmosphere was switched from Ar to CO2, a more striking difference between the two catalysts emerged. As previously reported for αβαβ‐UrFe, this transition induces an anodic shift in the Fe(I/0) redox wave (Figure 2a), attributed to an early CO2 binding to the Fe(I) species facilitated by hydrogen‐bonding interactions within the SCS [27]. A similar but more pronounced anodic shift (120 mV) is also observed here for α2β2 ‐UrFe; however, in this case, the redox wave is now immediately followed by a marked increase in the current intensity (Figure 2b). Because such a behavior is unprecedented for iron porphyrins in absence of a proton source or a Lewis acid, bulk electrolysis (BE) experiment coupled to gas chromatography (GC) was performed to determine whether this current increase corresponds to the electrocatalytic CO2 reduction (Figures S14, Table S4). These experiments unambiguously confirm the generation of CO, albeit with a modest Faradaic efficiency (FE) of 29%, upon applying a potential of −1.86 V vs Fc+ /0.

Beyond the classical proton‐assisted CO2‐to‐CO electroreduction pathway (CO2 + 2H+ + 2e– →CO + H2O), Savéant and co‐workers have reported an alternative mechanism operating under aprotic conditions. In this case, two molecules of CO2 are involved, one being reduced into CO while the second serves as an oxygen atom acceptor (2 CO2 + 2e– → CO + CO3 2–) in the presence of an activating exogenous Lewis acid (Mg2+, Ca2+, Ba2+, Li+ or Na+) [28, 29]. Foot‐of‐the‐wave (FOW) analyses of the CVs [30, 31, 32, 33] under varying CO2 concentrations (Figure 2c) reveals a rate dependence consistent with second‐order kinetics, supporting the involvement of two CO2 molecules in the catalytic cycle. A similar mechanism therefore likely operates here, but driven by the topological configuration of the urea arms in the α2β2 ‐UrFe. UV‐Visible spectroelectrochemistry (UV–vis SEC) indicates CO2 already binds at the Fe(I) state for both atropisomers forming [FeIII‐CO2]– , with distinct spectral features. For αβαβ‐UrFe (Figure 2d), new bands appear at 424, 442, and 568 nm, while α2β2 ‐UrFe exhibits features at 390, 442, and 570 nm (Figure 2e), reflecting differences in their electronic environments. Baseline drift during measurements at more negative potential unfortunately limited the characterization of the [FeII‐CO2]2– species by UV–Vis SEC. Infrared spectroelectrochemistry (IR SEC) at the catalytic potential of –2.05 V vs Fc+/0 in dry deuterated acetonitrile (Figure 2f) additionally revealed new IR bands for α2β2 ‐UrFe at 1678, 1957, 1543 and 1323 cm−1, that were computationally assigned to an [FeII‐CO…CO3Ur]2– and [FeI‐CO3] 3−) intermediates (vide infra and Table S14).

To account for such a reactivity, the mechanism of the aprotic reduction of CO2 was investigated computationally using density functional theory (DFT). Calculations employed simplified models denoted m αβαβ‐UrFe and m α2β2 ‐UrFe in which the two urea arms on one face of the porphyrin were replaced with protons. The geometry of each intermediate was optimized at the CPCM(DMF)‐D3‐OLYP/def2svp level of theory and its energy was refined at SMD(DMF)‐D3(BJ)‐OLYP/def2tzvpp (see Supporting Information for detailed procedures). The coordination of the first CO2 molecule was confirmed to be accessible from the formal oxidation state Fe(I) and is calculated to be more favored for m αβαβ‐UrFe(I) compared to m α2β2 ‐UrFe(I) (–6.6 vs + 2.5 kcal.mol−1). However, a further one‐electron reduction of these species is required prior to reaction with a second CO2 molecule (Table S11). Structural analysis of the resulting Fe─CO2 intermediates reveals the formation of hydrogen bonds between the urea arms and the bound CO2 in both atropisomers (Figure 3a, b). As previously described for m αβαβ‐UrFe, the CO2 is symmetrically clamped by two sets of hydrogen bonds between each O atom and one urea group (Figure 3a, c). In contrast, in m α2β2 ‐UrFe, the two urea groups interact with the same oxygen atom via three H‐bonds in a dissymmetrical fashion (Figure 3b, d). The binding of CO2 is less stabilizing in this intermediate compared to the previous example (–11.8 vs –20.8 kcal.mol−1), but this could be mainly due to the removal of one H‐bond. Interestingly, these mono‐directional interactions activate the CO2 fragment to a comparable extent to that in m αβαβ‐UrFe, according to calculated CO2 bending (Figure S33) and neat charge (Table S12). This suggests that the redesign of the SCS in m α2β2 ‐UrFe does not alter significantly the intrinsic basicity of the Fe─CO2 intermediate. However, a marked difference is found in the accessibility of the distal O atom which is much higher in the case of m α2β2‐UrFe. The buried volume analysis indeed reveals significantly lower Vbur values for m α2β2 ‐UrFe than m αβαβ‐UrFe, both in the accessible quadrant (32.4% vs 60.0%) and octant (6.4% vs 33.6%) decompositions (Figure 3e, f).

As a result, the [m α2β2 ‐UrFe─CO2]2– intermediate enables the insertion of a second CO2 molecule to form the bis‐CO2 adduct (Figure S36) via a moderately endergonic but kinetically accessible step (∆G = +6.2 kcal.mol−1, ∆G‡ = +7.5 kcal.mol−1). Notably, the O─C bond formation between the two CO2 fragments is accompanied by a rearrangement of the hydrogen‐bonding network, with the singly H‐bonded urea shifting from the Fe‐coordinated CO2 to the incoming CO2 molecule (Figure 4). In contrast, the insertion of a second CO2 molecule onto the symmetrically clamped mono CO2 adduct within m αβαβ‐UrFe is thermodynamically unfavorable (∆G = +17.4 kcal.mol−1), and would yield a substantially distorted Fe─(CO2)2 (Figure S34, Table S13). In addition, its formation would require the decoordination of a tightly bound urea arm and the insertion of the second CO2 molecule in a more sterically buried Fe─CO2 site (Figure 3e) and no transition state could be found to account for this transformation. Surprisingly, the second CO2 insertion to the non‐functionalized [TPPFe─CO2]2– where the lack of SCS interactions is offset by a greater Fe─CO2 accessibility require a comparable cost to those of m α2β2 ‐UrFe (barrierless process, ∆G = +7.3 kcal.mol−1, Figure S34). However, subsequent C─O bond cleavage to carbonate is prohibitively endergonic without a Lewis acid (+30.1 kcal.mol−1). In contrast, in m α2β2 ‐UrFe, hydrogen‐bonding transiently stabilizes the departing carbonate, lowering the activation barrier to 16.0 kcal.mol−1 (Figures 4, S35 and S36). The resulting [FeII ─CO…CO3‐Ur]2– intermediate exhibits calculated vibrational signatures at 1948 and 1680 cm−1 (Figure 4) corresponding to the C─O stretches at the Fe‐bound carbon monoxide and the urea‐bound carbonate, respectively, in excellent agreement with the experimental IR SEC observations (Figure 2f).

The unprecedented ability of α2β2 ‐UrFe to catalyze CO2 electroreduction without exogenous Brønsted or Lewis acids distinguish it from the αβαβ‐UrFe atropisomer. The elements brought by our mechanistic study indicates that the SCS of α2β2 ‐UrFe is optimally positioned to participate in each step of the aprotic CO2RR mechanism, from CO2 binding and activation, to the functionalization of the distal O atom, and its abstraction to form CO. This optimal design also extends to protic CO2RR as shown by a direct comparison of the catalytic performances of αβαβ‐UrFe (Figure 5a) and α2β2 ‐UrFe (Figure 5b) in the presence of 5.55 M of water as a proton source. Under these conditions, both catalysts exhibit important catalytic waves; however, α2β2 ‐UrFe shows significantly higher current intensity, and a more pronounced anodic shift of the onset potential (210 mV shift compared to 150 mV) indicative of higher activity at lower overpotential. CO was identified as the exclusive product, with a FE of 84%, as determined by BE coupled to GC (Figure S14, Table S4), and no liquid products were detected. The corresponding log(TOFmax) versus overpotential (η) plots obtained from FOW analysis for these two catalysts is shown in Figure 5c, together with benchmark data for the non‐functionalized iron tetraphenylporphyrin (TPPFe), its perfluorinated analogues (F8Fe, F20Fe), the four trimethylanilinium‐modified iron porphyrin atropisomers ( αααα ‐, α3β ‐, α2β2‐ , and αβαβ‐FeTMA) reported by Mayer, and our previously reported αα‐ and αβ ‐UrFe (see Table S7 and Figure S22 for full comparison with the state‐of‐the‐art) [21, 26, 34]. This comparison clearly illustrates that the incorporation of urea groups as hydrogen‐bond‐donating SCS motifs in α2β2 ‐UrFe and αβαβ‐UrFe enables these catalysts to break away from the linear scaling trend imposed by purely through‐bond electron‐withdrawing effect of the fluorine substituents, which typically enforce a trade‐off between lower overpotentials and reduced TOFs. Moreover, the atropisomeric effect appears to exert a much stronger influence with urea‐based H‐bond‐donating groups than with previously studied cationic substituents. This trend underscores that beyond CO2 binding and activation, site accessibility and hydrogen‐bond donor directionality are critical design elements.

In our prior study on an iron porphyrin bearing two face‐to‐face urea arms ( αα‐UrFe), we showed protonation is rate‐determining [19, 26]. Kinetic isotope effect (KIE) measurements and DFT calculations revealed hindered proton (water) access to the active site by the urea arms. This interpretation was supported by a marked increase in the reaction rate upon removal of one urea arm in αβ‐UrFe, albeit at the expense of a reduced CO2 binding affinity. For α2β2 ‐UrFe, the second urea arm is repositioned adjacently rather than removed. As shown by the calculated buried volume (Figure 3f), this modification improves accessibility to the active site, and could also increase the local proton/water concentration by interaction with the partially bound urea moiety (Figure S37). This synergy is evidenced by pronounced upward trend in the log TOF with water (carbonic acid) as external proton source (Figure 4d). An exceptionally high KIE of 32 suggest that protons are involved in the rate determining steps (Figure 4e), consistent with the second‐order dependence on water concentration (Figure 5f), and distinct from the αβαβ‐, αα‐, and αβ‐UrFe atropisomers. This configuration results in a significantly enhanced TOFs (7.78 × 106 s−1) at just 500 mV overpotential. This value can even reach 2.72 × 107 s−1 at 7.22 M water, although some catalyst precipitation can be observed at such high‐water content. These results underscore once again the critical role of SCS topology and symmetry in fine‐tuning catalytic efficiency.

On the basis of the results described above, α2β2 ‐UrFe triggers the CO2‐to‐CO reduction from the Fe(I) species, identified as the catalytically active state to form the [FeIII─CO2]ˉ adduct [27]. From this common intermediate, two distinct mechanisms operate, depending on the reaction conditions (Scheme 3). Under protic conditions, it follows the conventional two‐electron/two‐proton pathway. Sequential or coupled proton and electron transfer generates [FeII─CO2H]ˉ, whose further protonation cleaves the C─O bond (releasing H2O), followed by one‐electron reduction of [FeII─CO] to produce CO and regenerate Fe(I). In contrast, in aprotic conditions the unique SCS configuration of α2β2 ‐UrFe enables insertion of a second CO2 molecule after one‐electron reduction of [FeIII─CO2]ˉ, leading in our calculation to a [FeII─(CO2)2]2ˉ intermediate. This process is accompanied by a rearrangement of the urea groups that facilitates C─O bond cleavage and carbonate departure toward a distal urea arm, as evidenced by IR spectroscopy.

Conclusion

We reported the first example of a modified iron porphyrin catalyst capable of promoting electrocatalytic CO2‐to‐CO reduction in the absence of both exogenous Brønsted and Lewis acids. This unprecedented reactivity arises from deliberate symmetry remodeling, which induces changes in directionality of the hydrogen‐bonding network within a urea‐based SCS. Our investigation demonstrates that positioning two urea arms within the same SCS at adjacent positions in α2β2 ‐UrFe fundamentally alters the interaction mode with the substrate coordinated to the metal center, compared to the face‐to‐face arrangement found in the previously studied αβαβ‐UrFe analogue. Mechanistic studies combining electrochemistry, spectroscopy, and DFT calculations reveal that, although the α2β2 configuration provides comparatively weaker activation of the [FeIII─CO2]ˉ intermediate than the αβαβ arrangement, it uniquely enables insertion of a second CO2 molecule. This second CO2 acts as an oxygen‐atom acceptor, a process facilitated by reorganization of the urea arms and a more open steric environment (larger accessible buried volume) around the metal center. Under protic conditions, the enhanced accessibility of the active site in α2β2 ‐UrFe is also crucial, as it facilitates access of proton sources and thereby promotes proton transfer in the rate‐determining step. This effect contributes to a three‐order‐of‐magnitude increase in the reaction rate, reaching a TOF as high as 2.72 × 107 s−1. More broadly, this study highlights an important aspect of secondary coordination sphere design: optimal catalysis not only result from the fine tune between stabilization and activation of key intermediates, but can also benefit from directional and adaptive non‐covalent interactions that can facilitate protonation and/or co‐substrate insertion. Beyond the simple proximity of functional groups to the active site, the preorganization and orientation of these interactions play a central role in controlling reactivity. In enzymes, such features are intrinsically imposed by the protein scaffold and likely contribute to the remarkable efficiency of metalloenzymes. In addition to recent developments in hybrid materials capable of achieving current densities more compatible with industrial applications [35, 36, 37, 38, 39], our approach opens up new perspectives for the design of efficient, enzyme‐inspired catalytic systems.
