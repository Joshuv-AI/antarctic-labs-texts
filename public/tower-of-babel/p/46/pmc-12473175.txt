Genetic Variants Associated With Neurodegenerative Disorders in Patients With Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis and Phenotypic Variability
Authors: Maurizio Grassano, Emanuele Koumantakis, Francesca F. Palumbo, Maura Brunetti, Giovanni De Marco, Ilaria Merulla, Claudia Paolantonio, Barbara Iazzolino, Marcella Testa, Umberto Manera, Antonio Canosa, Rosario Vasta, Giuseppe Fuda, Paolina Salamone, Giulia Marchese, Federico Casale, Salvatore Gallone, Gabriele Mora, Cristina Moglia, Andrea Calvo, Adriano Chiò
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and ObjectivesThe genetic contribution to clinical heterogeneity in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) remains poorly understood, particularly regarding the role of genes associated with other neurodegenerative disorders. This study aimed to determine whether rare variants in neurodegeneration-associated genes influence ALS risk and clinical phenotype.MethodsThis case-control study included patients with ALS without pathogenic variants in major ALS genes from the population-based Piemonte and Valle d'Aosta Register for ALS and matched controls. High-impact rare variants (minor allele frequency <0.01% or novel) in 151 genes associated with neurodegenerative disorders were identified through whole-genome sequencing. Main outcomes included ALS risk, motor phenotype, cognitive status, and survival. Findings were replicated in the Project MinE dataset (6,596 ALS cases, 2,454 controls).ResultsThe study population consisted of 791 ALS patients (median age 67.9 years; 46.3% women) and 747 matched controls. One hundred twenty-seven ALS cases (16.1%) carried at least one rare variant in neurodegeneration-associated genes compared with 91 (12.2%) controls (odds ratio [OR] 1.37; 95% CI 1.04–1.80; p = 0.027). In particular, novel variants in Parkinson disease–associated genes (OR 3.62; 95% CI 1.33–9.80; p = 0.01) and hereditary neuropathy genes (OR 3.30; 95% CI 1.55–7.03; p = 0.002) conferred a 3-fold increased risk. These findings were independently replicated in the Project MinE dataset (OR 1.43; 95% CI 1.30–1.57; p < 0.001). Stratified analysis also confirmed the enrichment of rare variants in Parkinson disease and hereditary neuropathy-associated genes. Variant carriers were more likely to present with the flail arm phenotype (OR 2.05; 95% CI 1.05–3.98; p = 0.03) and showed increased risk of frontotemporal dementia (OR 1.85; 95% CI 1.04–3.21; p = 0.03) and shorter survival (hazard ratio 1.99; 95% CI 1.26–3.14; p = 0.003).DiscussionRare variants in genes associated with other neurodegenerative disorders influence both ALS susceptibility and clinical features, highlighting genetic pleiotropy across neurodegenerative diseases. Our findings support a model in which ALS arises from a complex and heterogeneous genetic landscape involving multiple variants, often shared with other neurodegenerative conditions, that shape disease expression. Although these findings suggest potential future expansion of genetic screening beyond classical ALS genes, such variants should be interpreted cautiously in clinical practice pending further functional validation.

Introduction

Substantial genetic overlap exists between neurodegenerative disorders, including amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS). Variants in genes primarily associated with one neurodegenerative disorder can influence risk and presentation of others. The C9orf72 repeat expansion and TARDBP mutations cause frontotemporal dementia and contribute to parkinsonism.1,2 Similarly, variants in KIF5A3 and SPTLC14 have been implicated across multiple neurodegenerative conditions.

As whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing become more common in ALS clinical practice, variants in genes previously associated with other neurodegenerative disorders are increasingly detected. However, whether these variants contribute to clinical presentation in ALS or should be considered in genetic counseling remains unknown.

This study aimed to characterize rare variants in genes associated with various neurodegenerative disorders among patients with ALS and to determine whether these variants contribute to ALS risk or modify disease presentation, potentially explaining phenotypic variability.

Methods

Study Population

The study cohort consisted of 1,324 patients with ALS from the population-based Piemonte and Valle d'Aosta Register for ALS (PARALS)5 and 744 healthy controls of matched ancestry. To minimize misdiagnosis risks, particularly of ALS cases that could be confounded for other neurodegenerative disorders, we included only patients with definite or probable/laboratory-supported ALS according to El Escorial criteria.6 Patients were also excluded if they carried a pathogenic variant in a known ALS-associated gene. Detailed selection criteria and cohort characteristics are provided in the Supplementary Materials.

Clinical Measures

Participants were classified into established motor phenotype based on the region of onset, the propagation of motor symptoms, and the degree of upper and lower motor neuron dysfunction7: classic ALS phenotype (mixed upper and lower motor neuron signs in the upper or lower limbs), bulbar phenotype (dysarthria and/or dysphagia as initial symptoms and no spinal involvement for the first 6 months after symptoms onset), flail arm syndrome (progressive weakness in upper limbs confined to the flail limbs for at least 12 months after symptom onset), flail leg syndrome (progressive distal onset of weakness and wasting in the lower limbs with minimal upper limb involvement), pyramidal variant (clinical manifestations dominated by upper motor neuron signs), and respiratory onset (initial respiratory symptoms with only mild spinal or bulbar signs in the first 6 months after onset). Clinical data, including survival, were retrieved from the epidemiologic register data. At the time of diagnosis, patients also underwent a standardized neuropsychological assessment, including a comprehensive battery of cognitive and behavioral tests. Based on these evaluations, patients were classified according to the Consensus Criteria for frontotemporal cognitive and behavioral syndromes in ALS8 into 5 categories: ALS with normal cognition (ALS-CN), ALS with cognitive impairment (ALSci), ALS with behavioral impairment (ALSbi), ALS with cognitive-behavioral impairment (ALScbi), and ALS with frontotemporal dementia (ALS-FTD). Complete assessment protocols are described in the Supplementary Materials.

Whole-Genome Sequencing and Variant Filtering

Whole-genome sequencing analysis and screening for the C9orf72 intronic expansion was performed on all participants.9,10 We defined high-impact variants as ultra-rare (minor allele frequency <0.1% in Genome Aggregation Database) or novel variants with either loss-of-function consequences or missense variants with Combined Annotation-Dependent Depletion score >20. We analyzed 145 genes associated with neurodegenerative disorders based on Ensembl annotations (eTable 1). Sequencing procedures, quality control, and variant filtering criteria are detailed in the eMethods.

Statistical Analysis

We examined differences in variant burden between cases and controls using logistic regression, adjusted for sex, site of onset, 5 ancestry-defining principal components, and total genetic load. To test whether ALS cases carrying rare variants in non-ALS neurodegeneration-associated genes have different phenotypic features, we fitted multiple regression models. Linear regression models were used to evaluate associations with continuous clinical measures, including age at disease onset, diagnostic delay, Revised Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis Functional Rating Scale (ALSFRS-R) score at diagnosis, and ALSFRS-R rate of decline. For categorical phenotypes, logistic regression models were fitted with mutational burden (presence of rare variants) as the outcome variable. For motor phenotype analysis, classic ALS served as the reference category, with bulbar onset, flail arm syndrome, flail leg syndrome, pyramidal variant, and respiratory onset included as predictor variables in a single multinomial model. For cognitive phenotype analysis, patients were dichotomized into 2 groups: those with frontotemporal dementia (ALS-FTD) vs those without full dementia, which included patients with normal cognition (ALS-CN) and those with intermediate cognitive-behavioral impairment (ALSci, ALSbi, and ALScbi combined). All models were adjusted for sex and age at onset as covariates. Survival was analyzed using Kaplan-Meier curves with long-rank tests and Cox regression models adjusted for sex and age at onset. Complete statistical methods are available in the Supplementary Material.

We performed separate analyses grouping variants by their associated neurodegenerative disease category: Alzheimer disease and related dementia, Parkinson disease and related movement disorders, hereditary spastic paresis, hereditary neuropathies, and spinal muscular atrophies. The identified genes and their associated phenotypes are shown in eMethods.

All statistical tests were 2-sided, and statistical significance was set at 0.05. Analyses were performed using R version 4.3.0.

Replication Analysis

For replication, we analyzed summary data from Project MinE (6,596 ALS cases and 2,454 controls) using identical filtering criteria for variants in the same neurodegeneration-associated genes identified in our discovery cohort. Associations were assessed using Fisher exact tests to estimate odds ratios (ORs), 95% CIs, and p values. Because only aggregated data were available, adjustments for population stratification or polygenic load could not be performed in the replication cohort.

Standard Protocol Approvals, Registrations, and Patient Consents

This study was approved by the Comitato Etico Azienda Ospedaliero-Universitaria Città della Salute e della Scienza, Torino (protocol number 0038876). All participants provided written informed consent before enrollment.

Data Availability

Individual-level whole-genome sequencing data from the PARALS cohort are available on the dbGaP web portal (accession number phs001963.3). Clinical data are available on reasonable request from qualified researchers. Project MinE data are accessible at databrowser.projectmine.com.

Results

Study Cohort Description

After excluding 284 cases with variants in established ALS genes and 206 cases that did not meet the criteria for a definite or probable/laboratory-supported diagnosis, a total of 791 ALS individuals were included in the final study cohort. Among the 791 ALS patients analyzed, 425 (53.7%) were male, with median age at diagnosis of 67.9 years. Motor phenotypes included: bulbar (32.2%), classic (31.4%), flail arm (6.3%), flail leg (18.7%), pyramidal (9.6%), and respiratory (1.8%). Five hundred fifty-seven patients (70.4%) underwent neuropsychological testing: cognitive and behavioral impairment was observed in 31.4% of them, while an additional 14.2% presented a full blown FTD (eTable 2).

Disease Risk

Overall, 127 of 791 (16.1%) ALS patients carried at least one rare variant compared with 91 of 744 (12.2%) controls (eTable 3). Logistic regression analysis confirmed that the presence of rare variants moderately increased ALS risk (OR 1.37; 95% CI 1.04–1.80; p = 0.027), with a stronger association detected for novel variants (OR 1.75; 95% CI 1.22–2.52; p = 0.003) (Figure 1, eTable 4A).

Genetic Overlap Between ALS and Other Neurodegenerative Disorders

When grouping genes by their associated neurodegenerative diseases, novel variants in Parkinson disease–associated genes increased ALS risk more than 3-fold (OR 3.62; 95% CI 1.33–9.80; p = 0.01). Similar increases were seen with novel variants in hereditary neuropathy genes (OR 3.30; 95% CI 1.55–7.03; p = 0.002) (Figure 1, eTable 4B).

Disease Phenotype and Progression

The flail arm phenotype showed significant enrichment of variants in neurodegeneration-associated genes (OR 2.05; 95% CI 1.05–3.98; p = 0.03) (eTable 5A), particularly in Parkinson disease–associated genes (OR 3.52; 95% CI 1.29–9.63; p = 0.01). No other motor or cognitive phenotype demonstrated significant enrichment in rare variants (eTable 5B). The presence of a neurodegeneration-related variant did not significantly influence age at disease onset (mean difference 0.07 years; 95% CI −0.11 to 0.25; p = 0.41).

Similarly, no significant differences were observed in time from onset to diagnosis (mean difference −1.10 months; 95% CI −3.07 to 0.88; p = 0.28) or ALSFRS-R score at diagnosis (mean difference −0.21 points; 95% CI −1.32 to 0.89; p = 0.71). The ALSFRS-R progression rate at diagnosis was slightly higher in variant carriers compared with noncarriers, but this difference was not statistically significant (mean difference 0.29 points per month; 95% CI −0.05 to 0.62; p = 0.09). A more aggressive disease course was observed in carriers of novel variants, especially in Parkinson disease–associated genes, that showed significantly shorter survival (hazard ratio 1.99; 95% CI 1.26–3.14; p = 0.003) (Figure 2, eTables 6 and 7). Among the 557 patients who underwent neuropsychological testing, variant carriers also showed increased risk of frontotemporal dementia (OR 1.85; 95% CI 1.04–3.21; p = 0.03) (eTable 6).

Replication Analysis

The findings from the PARALS cohort were independently replicated in the Project MinE dataset, comprising 6,596 ALS patients and 2,454 controls. Overall, variant burden across neurodegeneration-related genes was significantly enriched in ALS patients for rare (OR 1.43; 95% CI 1.30–1.57; p = 1.41 × 10−11) and novel variants (OR 1.40; 95% CI 1.27–1.55; p = 1.32 × 10−10). Stratified analysis confirmed the enrichment of novel variants in Parkinson disease–associated genes (OR 1.39; 95% CI 1.14–1.70; p = 0.001) and hereditary neuropathy genes (OR 1.45; 95% CI 1.22–1.73; p = 1.56 × 10−5), in line with results from the primary cohort. Ultra-rare variant burden in these gene groups also replicated significantly (Figure 3, eTable 8).

Discussion

This study demonstrates that rare variants in genes associated with other neurodegenerative disorders increase ALS risk in 2 independent cohorts of European ancestry, supporting the significant role these genetic variations play in ALS pathogenesis.11 These variants may account for a portion of the missing heritability in ALS that has not been explained by common risk variants or by known ALS-related genes. Our data support a complex genetic disease model with contributions from many disease-causing or disease-modifying gene variants which encompass not only known ALS genes but also other genes from the neurodegenerative disease spectrum. Moreover, rare variants also influence disease progression, suggesting that they are not incidental but contribute to ALS pathogenesis and progression.

These findings extend evidence for shared genetic architecture across neurodegenerative disorders, particularly between ALS and Parkinson disease12,13 and genetic neuropathies. While approximately one-third of patients with ALS exhibit extrapyramidal signs,14 the association between Parkinson disease–related variants and the flail arm phenotype—characterized by predominant lower motor neuron symptoms distinct from parkinsonian features—demonstrates genuine pleiotropic effects rather than variant enrichment in cases with mixed phenotypes.15 Similarly, while hereditary motor neuropathies are known ALS mimics16 and may also present with upper motor neuron features, we observed that neuropathy-associated variants are enriched in patients with typical ALS presentations and not just in those with distal limb-onset forms. Importantly, our analysis was restricted to patients with a definite or probable ALS diagnosis, reducing the likelihood that these overlaps reflect clinical misdiagnosis or disease mimics (eTable 9). This finding strengthens evidence for shared pathogenic mechanisms across neurodegenerative disorders with distinct clinical presentations.17,18 Our results also suggest that specific ALS phenotypes may have distinct genetic backgrounds, indicating that different molecular mechanisms may underlie various patterns of motor neuron degeneration. Notably, the association of Parkinson disease–associated gene variants with both the flail-arm phenotype—typically associated with longer survival—and a more aggressive disease highlights how genetic profiling may uncover biologically meaningful ALS subtypes that are not fully captured by traditional phenotype-based definitions.

In our large Italian cohort, approximately 1 in 6 individuals with sporadic ALS without known ALS-related variants carried at least one rare variant in genes linked to other neurodegenerative disorders (eTable 10). This observation should not be considered a definitive estimate, as the frequency of identified variants will likely vary according to population ancestry, gene sets analyzed, and criteria used for variant filtering. Nevertheless, our findings have important clinical implications. Our results indicate that rare variants in neurodegeneration-associated genes contribute significantly to the genetic landscape of ALS and are likely involved in its pathogenesis. However, it is important to note that the aggregate effect size is relatively small. As such, causality should not be attributed to individual variants at this stage, particularly in a clinical context. The precise mechanistic role—whether they act primarily as modest risk factors or whether a subset represents high-impact, pleiotropic variants—remains to be determined. This distinction is crucial for clinical interpretation and application. While our data support the potential future expansion of genetic screening panels to include neurodegeneration-associated genes beyond classical ALS genes, we emphasize that these variants should be interpreted cautiously in clinical practice. Additional functional studies and larger, diverse cohorts are needed to determine their pathogenicity and clinical relevance for patients and family members. Notably, the observed association of these variants with shorter survival and frontotemporal dementia may still help clinicians anticipate disease progression and develop tailored management strategies.

A key limitation of this study is the inability to disentangle the individual effects of rare variants. It is possible that certain variants increase ALS risk, while others may influence disease progression or phenotype. By analyzing variants in aggregate, we may obscure opposing or modifying effects. Moreover, population-specific associations cannot be excluded. Nonetheless, replication of our findings in the large, independent Project MinE cohort supports their robustness and encourages further investigation in diverse populations.

Our findings contribute to a growing body of evidence suggesting that neurodegenerative disorders arise within a highly complex and variable genetic landscape. As with other ageing-associated conditions, ALS likely emerges from the interplay of multiple genetic risk variants, some of which are shared across the neurodegenerative disease spectrum. This broader genetic context may not only shape individual susceptibility but also influence clinical expression, progression, and response to therapies.

In conclusion, our study reveals that rare variants in neurodegeneration-associated genes influence both ALS susceptibility and clinical features, further highlighting genetic pleiotropy across neurodegenerative disorders and potentially explaining some of the clinical heterogeneity observed in ALS.
