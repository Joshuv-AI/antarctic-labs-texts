Magnetic hydrogel-mediated periodic stimulation enhances mesenchymal stem cell small extracellular vesicle release
Authors: Chen Wang, Ye Chen, Tianhao Yan, Shuxin Zhang, Xiaoru Li, Shangquan Wu, Qingchuan Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Small extracellular vesicles (sEVs) derived from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) are regarded as important “cell-free” therapeutic carriers due to their potential for immune regulation and tissue repair. However, their large-scale application is constrained by bottlenecks such as low secretion yields under conventional culture conditions. This study established a magnetically controlled dynamic mechanical stimulation platform to enhance MSC-sEVs production. Superparamagnetic Fe3O4 nanoparticles were embedded into polyacrylamide to form magnetic composite hydrogels. An variable magnetic field induced reversible deformation of the hydrogel, thereby applying periodic mechanical loading to adherent cells. Results demonstrate that, compared to conventional plastic substrates, dynamic loading on MSCs significantly increases sEVs secretion (up to approximately 5-fold) without altering typical vesicle morphology or size distribution. Mechanistic studies indicate that this stimulus induces upregulation of the mechanosensitive channel Piezo1 and the nuclear YAP signaling pathway, thereby promoting sEVs release. Inhibition of Piezo1 or YAP attenuated the enhanced production effect, supporting a dual-pathway synergistic model where “Piezo1–Ca2+ promotes release, while YAP promotes multivesicular bodies (MVB) generation.” This platform offers an economical, simple, and promising solution for efficient and controllable sEVs production.

Introduction

Regenerative medicine provides an important framework for addressing the global health challenge of tissue injury and organ failure. Therapeutic strategies based on mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs), leveraging their multipotent differentiation potential, immunomodulatory properties, and paracrine effects, have been extensively investigated as an important approach for tissue repair and regeneration [1]. In recent years, increasing attention has been directed toward their secretome, particularly small extracellular vesicles (sEVs), which are recognized as key mediators of intercellular communication. sEVs are membrane-bound vesicles with approximately 30–200 nm in diameter, containing bioactive substances such as proteins, nucleic acids (e.g., mRNA, miRNA), and lipids. They deliver their contents to recipient cells, thereby regulating a range of physiological and pathological processes including cell proliferation, migration, differentiation, and immune responses, partially reproducing or mimicking the therapeutic effects of their parent MSCs [2]. Owing to their high tissue penetrability at the nanoscale, low immunogenicity, ease of storage, and the potential for engineering their contents to enhance targeting and efficacy, sEVs are regarded as promising “cell-free” therapeutic agents and drug delivery vehicles [3,4].

However, translating the exceptional therapeutic potential of MSCs-derived sEVs into clinical reality faces a fundamental bottleneck: the efficient production of sEVs at scale with high purity and consistent biological activity. Traditional sEVs acquisition relies on in vitro expansion of MSCs, however, stem cell culture itself is costly, cumbersome, and prone to batch-to-batch variability. More critically, MSCs exhibit limited baseline sEVs secretion under conventional culture conditions. Therefore, developing novel strategies to efficiently enhance MSC sEVs secretion is crucial for advancing their clinical translation.

Currently, strategies to enhance MSCs-sEVs production primarily focus on microenvironmental regulation, which can be broadly categorized into biochemical stimulation and physical stimulation. Biochemical methods, such as adding specific growth factors (e.g., TGF-β, bFGF), cytokines, or small molecules, can moderately upregulate sEVs secretion. However, these approaches often come with limitations including high costs and the potential introduction of exogenous contaminants. Their safety and controllability require further evaluation [5]. In contrast, physical stimulation offers unique application prospects due to its non-invasive nature, precise parameter controllability, ease of integration into biomanufacturing processes, and avoidance of chemical additives. The physical microenvironment, such as mechanical and electrical stimulation, is widely recognized as an important regulator of cellular behavior, including the biogenesis and release of sEVs. Studies indicate that substrate stiffness perceived by cells significantly influences sEVs secretion: Culturing on softer substrates accelerates the transport rate of multivesicular bodies (MVBs) within cells and increases their fusion frequency with the plasma membrane, thereby promoting sEVs release [6]. The electroactive hydrogel developed in our earlier work enables “cell self-stimulation” by converting cellular traction forces into self-generated ionic electrical signals, thereby stimulating cells to secrete increased amounts of sEVs. [7]. Beyond the inherent properties of the growth substrate itself, direct application of external mechanical loads has also been demonstrated as an effective secretion enhancer. For instance, fluid shear stress [8,9] has been shown to significantly elevate cellular sEVs production. These findings collectively point to a clear pathway: applying controlled mechanical stimulation to MSCs serves as a “physical key” to unlocking their high-yield sEVs secretion.

A critical technical challenge is to deliver dynamic mechanical stimuli to mesenchymal stem cells in an efficient, wireless, and dynamically controllable manner that better mimics the in vivo microenvironment and thereby enhances sEVs secretion. We envisioned the integration of magnetic nanomaterials with smart biomaterials. Magnetic nanoparticles (MNPs), have played significant roles in targeted drug delivery, nanosensing, magnetothermal therapy, and resonance imaging due to their excellent biocompatibility, superparamagnetism (avoiding hysteresis and aggregation), ease of functionalization, and ability to generate force, heat, or displacement in response to external magnetic fields [[10], [11], [12], [13]]. Furthermore, the superparamagnetism, minute size, and economical preparation costs of magnetic nanoparticles have broadened their applications in biomedical engineering. Hydrogels, as high-water-content three-dimensional network polymers, have emerged as ideal cell carriers and culture platforms in tissue engineering and regenerative medicine due to their physicochemical properties similar to extracellular matrices, excellent biocompatibility, tunable mechanical properties, and ease of loading cells and bioactive factors. Previous studies have combined magnetic nanoparticles with hydrogels to form novel nanocomposites, enabling remote dynamic regulation of gel mechanical properties via externally applied magnetic fields [14]. MNPs undergo polarization and directional motion under magnetic fields. We incorporated MNPs into hydrogels and drove their movement via magnetic fields, thereby inducing deformation of the gel network. This approach enabled the application of controlled mechanical stimuli to cells cultured on the gel surface, and we investigated the effects of such mechanical stimulation on the secretion of sEVs by MSCs.

In this study, we established a novel system that promotes the secretion of sEVs from mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) by applying periodic mechanical loading through the deformation of magnetic hydrogels in a dynamic magnetic field. Specifically, a permanent magnet was driven in a reciprocating motion above the culture dish to generate a periodically varying magnetic field. The embedded Fe3O4 nanoparticles responded to this dynamic field and induced reversible deformation of the hydrogel network, thereby transmitting cyclic mechanical stimulation to the adherent cells. We prepared a composite magnetic hydrogel suitable for MSC culture under the tested conditions and constructed an external magnetic field platform to enable dynamic mechanical loading. We further investigated the influence of magnetic hydrogel properties on MSC-derived sEVs secretion capacity and explored the biomechanical mechanisms underlying this platform's influences of sEVs production. Unlike our previous piezoionic hydrogel-based self-stimulation strategy, this platform delivers externally imposed and dynamically tunable mechanical cues. This distinction is important because it enables us to directly investigate how periodic mechanical loading, regulates sEVs secretion and related mechanotransduction pathways. This controllable mechanical activation-biomechanical feedback system provides a viable solution for boosting sEVs yield.

Result

In this work, Fe3O4 MNPs serve as the core component for inducing an externally applied dynamic magnetic field and as the primary vehicle for delivering mechanical forces to cells cultured on a gel substrate. Here, we employed an improved Double-Solvent Solvothermal Method to synthesize 100- and 160-nm-sized Fe3O4 MNPs [15].

TEM observations (Fig. 1A) showed that the obtained particles were relatively uniform and nearly spherical, with relatively relatively concentrated size distributions centered at approximately 100 and 160 nm (Fig. S1). These size values correspond to the overall diameters of the particles observed in TEM. Closer inspection of the particle morphology (Fig. S2) suggests that the larger particles are likely secondary structures assembled from smaller primary nanograins, rather than compact single-crystalline particles. To further characterize their crystal structure, X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was performed (Fig. 1C). The diffraction patterns exhibited characteristic peaks at 2θ ≈ 30.1°, 35.5°, 43.1°, 53.4°, 57.0°, and 62.6°, corresponding to the (220), (311), (400), (422), (511), and (440) crystal planes of Fe3O4, respectively, confirming the crystalline Fe3O4 phase of the synthesized particles. This structural feature is consistent with their magnetic behavior. Their hysteresis loops measured at room temperature (Fig. 1B) showed high saturation magnetization values of 76 and 79 emu/g, respectively, together with negligible coercivity and remanence, which can be more clearly seen in the enlarged view near zero field (Fig. 1B, inset). These results indicate superparamagnetic-like behavior at room temperature, rather than the clear ferromagnetic hysteresis expected for large compact single-domain particles. The presence of multi-core or polycrystalline-like secondary particles may help explain why particles with overall diameters of 100–160 nm still showed near-zero coercivity and remanence.

Based on the FTIR spectrum of MNPs (Fig. 1D), the absorption peak at 581 cm−1 is attributed to the stretching vibration of Fe–O. The symmetric and asymmetric vibration peaks of COO− appear at ∼1350–1450 cm−1 and 1550–1650 cm−1, respectively, indicating that numerous carboxylate groups interact with iron cations [16]. The broad peak within 2500–3500 cm−1 originates from the O–H stretching vibration in carboxyl groups and potentially adsorbed water. A very weak free C=O peak near ∼1720 cm−1 indicates successful coordination of carboxyl groups to the MNPs surface. Due to the presence of carboxyl groups, Fe3O4 MNPs disperse in water to form stable dispersions suitable for further preparation of composite hydrogel materials with uniformly dispersed MNPs.

The composite magnetic hydrogel is formed by embedding MNPs into an acrylamide hydrogel matrix. When we employ a crank-connecting rod mechanism to drive a periodically oscillating permanent magnet (Fig. S3), generating a dynamically changing magnetic field, this field acts upon the MNPs-acrylamide composite magnetic hydrogel. The magnetic force exerted on the embedded MNPs is oriented along the local magnetic field lines, and both its direction and magnitude vary dynamically with the reciprocating motion of the magnet. This induced mechanical motion in the MNPs, which is transmitted to the surrounding polymer chains, thereby mediating localized stretching and remodeling of the entire crosslinked network (Fig. 1E). For SEM characterization, the gel samples were rapidly frozen under the corresponding magnetic-field condition before freeze-drying. Under the magnetic field, the hydrogel exhibited a noticeable local reduction in pore size in the surface/near-surface region (Fig. 1F–S4). Quantitative analysis of the SEM images using ImageJ further supported this trend. The mean pore size decreased from 15.64 ± 16.16 μm in the untreated group to 11.03 ± 7.86 μm under magnetic-field treatment, and the corresponding 95% confidence interval plot is provided in Fig. S5 to illustrate the distribution of the measurements. In addition, EDS analysis showed an increased Fe signal in the magnet-facing near-surface region under magnetic-field application (Fig. 1G–S6). Considering that SEM-EDS reflects elemental information from a finite near-surface sampling volume, these results support magnetic-field-induced near-surface redistribution/enrichment of MNPs and accompanying local structural remodeling, rather than serving as a direct measurement of particle migration throughout the full gel thickness. Although some local enrichment of MNPs may occur within the hydrogel network, no evident large-scale sedimentation or particle leakage was observed in the prepared gels. The overall magnetic response and deformation behavior remained reproducible across samples, indicating that such microscale heterogeneity did not dominate the gel-level actuation behavior under our experimental conditions. After magnetic field removal, the Young's modulus of the magnetic hydrogel showed no significant change (Fig. 1H and I), and the magnetic particles returned to their original positions (Fig. S7), indicating that the magnetic particles did not detach from the magnetic gel system under the dynamic magnetic field and that the deformation of the magnetic gel was reversible. Here, the magnetic and non-magnetic hydrogels exhibit similar Young's moduli, achieved by adjusting the composition of monomers and crosslinking agents during hydrogel preparation to minimize the impact of substrate stiffness differences on cell behavior. Therefore, similar modulus values do not imply that the introduction of magnetic nanoparticles themselves has no mechanical effect.

We used COMSOL to establish a three-dimensional model of an N52 neodymium-iron-boron permanent magnet that perfectly matches the experimental setup (Fig. 1J). Simulation results visually demonstrate the spatial decay pattern of the high-gradient magnetic field surrounding the magnet (Fig. 1K–Video S1).

By recording the motion of the magnetic hydrogel under the dynamic magnetic field (Fig. 1L and M, Video S2-7), we obtained representative motion trajectories of hydrogels with different MNP loadings. From these, we estimated the relative temporal variation trend of magnetic actuation based on the change in distance between the magnet and the gel center (Fig. 1N–S8), which was mainly used to compare the presence and magnitude of magnetically induced responses among different hydrogel formulations rather than to provide an absolute quantification of the microscopic stresses experienced by cells. Besides, the measured gel motion may also be influenced by interfacial factors such as friction and dish surface condition; therefore, under the standardized experimental setup used here, these measurements are more suitable for relative comparison among groups than for absolute force determination. AO/EB staining (Fig. S9) showed no obvious acute cytotoxicity under the tested conditions, supporting the feasibility of subsequent in vitro experiments. This is crucial for subsequent cell culture experiments and for translating magnetic force into mechanical stimulation for MSCs.

We cultured cells on plastic culture plates and pre-prepared magnetic hydrogels, then applied a cyclic variable magnetic field (0.17 Hz) (Fig. 2A). After 48 h, we collected sEVs. Results showed significantly fewer sEVs collected on non-magnetic hydrogels (∼1 × 105 per cell) compared to magnetic hydrogels (∼2.5 × 105 per cell) (Fig. 2B), indicating that magnetic field-mediated wireless mechanical stimulation acts on cells to promote increased sEVs secretion. sEVs collected on plastic substrates (∼4 × 104 per cell) was lower than that collected on acrylamide hydrogels, attributed to substrate stiffness [6]. Nanoparticle analysis (NTA) revealed similar particle size distributions across substrates (Fig. 2C). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis showed nanoparticles exhibiting cup-shaped or disc-shaped transparent membrane structures (Fig. 2D), confirming the collected nanoparticles were sEVs. Based on TEM image analysis, sEVs exhibited an average diameter of approximately 80 nm (Fig. 2E) and a circularity of about 0.8 (Fig. 2F), with no significant differences. Thus, periodic mechanical stimulation significantly influences MSC sEVs secretion but does not affect vesicle size or morphology. To exclude potential effects from magnetic particle leakage, we added MNPs separately to the culture medium in plastic culture plates. The amount of free MNPs used in this control was 3 mg, corresponding to the MNP content in 1 mL of prepolymer solution for the 0.3 wt% magnetic hydrogel. Considering that 3 mL of prepolymer solution was used to prepare the gels for sEVs collection, this control represented a deliberately conservative condition assuming substantial particle leakage. The results (Fig. S11) indicate that the previously observed increase in sEVs was unrelated to the MNPs themselves. To exclude potential effects from altered surface properties of the magnetic hydrogel itself, we cultured cells under standard conditions on different substrates. Results (Fig. S12A) indicate that material surface properties have negligible impact on sEVs secretion. To assess whether a constant magnetic field was sufficient to explain the observed increase in sEVs secretion, we further conducted a control experiment under a constant magnetic field. Under this condition, although there were some visual differences between replicates, there was no statistically significant difference in sEVs production between AM and MG (Fig. S12B). Applying a constant magnetic field alone was insufficient to reproduce the significant enhancement observed under dynamic loading. Under these experimental conditions, there is a possibility that the constant magnetic field may induce local microstructural changes in the magnetic hydrogel, and may also have some effect on the cells grown on it, but this effect was not as significant as under a dynamic magnetic field. In summary, none of the aforementioned factors resulted in significant changes in sEVs quantity or particle size. These findings indicate that magnetic field-mediated wireless periodic mechanical stimulation acts upon cellular structures as a key factor influencing sEVs production.

Furthermore, we varied the particle size and mass fraction of MNPs in the hydrogel and found that the highest efficiency of sEVs secretion by MSCs was achieved at a MNPs particle size of 100 nm and a mass fraction of 1.2 wt%, reaching ∼3 × 105 per cell (Fig. 2G–J).

Previous studies have demonstrated that cyclic loading induces cellular reorientation [17,18]. As the permanent magnet reciprocates above the hydrogel, the transmitted magnetic force induces periodic deformation in both horizontal and vertical directions within the gel network. In this study, the horizontal reciprocating direction of the permanent magnet above the hydrogel was defined as the loading direction, and this direction was assigned as 90° in the cell orientation analysis. We therefore hypothesize that this regular mechanical deformation transmits periodic mechanical signals to cells growing on its surface. Fluorescent staining images (Fig. 3B–S13) reveal that on the surface of magnetic hydrogels, after 1 h of dynamic magnetic field loading, the growth direction of stress fibers and focal adhesions undergoes adaptive changes (Fig. 3D and E). These two panels are derived from the same source data but highlight different features of the orientation response. To respond to periodic mechanical loading, the cytoskeletal system is reinforced and stabilized perpendicular to the loading direction (Fig. 3C).In contrast, cells on non-magnetic polyacrylamide hydrogels exhibited uniform distribution of cell orientation across all angles, regardless of magnetic field application. Furthermore, Fourier transform analysis of fluorescence images of the actin cytoskeleton in MSCs grown on magnetic hydrogels containing 3 wt% MNPs revealed conformed that cells under unloaded conditions exhibited high anisotropy (Fig. S14). This quantitatively confirmed the ordered reorientation of the cell population along the direction perpendicular to the magnet movement. Our simulation results (Fig. S15) also confirm that under identical substrate strain conditions, cell membranes oriented perpendicular to the strain direction experience lower surface stress.

These phenomena align with the classical biomechanical principle that “cells tend to align in the direction that minimizes the strain they experience on the substrate” [19]. Specifically, focal adhesions act as mechanosensors, converting external stress into intracellular biochemical signals that drive dynamic reorganization of the cytoskeleton. Under force, cytoskeletal components like actin filaments undergo restructuring and realignment, a process accompanied by the sequential transmission and amplification of intracellular mechanical signals. Sustained periodic mechanical stimulation guides cells to adaptively adjust their cytoskeletal structure and adhesion sites, ultimately driving the cell as a whole to undergo a polarity change and rearrange and grow along a specific mechanical direction.

Intracellular calcium ions (Ca2+) serve as a crucial second messenger within cells, playing a key role in various physiological processes such as neurotransmitter release and muscle contraction. Previous studies have demonstrated that elevated intracellular Ca2+ concentrations promote the formation of MVBs while accelerating the release of sEVs [[20], [21], [22]]. When cells undergo external mechanical stimulation, mechanosensitive ion channels on the cell membrane surface detect deformation and open, leading to the influx of ions such as Ca2+ and consequently increasing intracellular ion concentrations [23].

Here, we hypothesize that periodic mechanical loading triggers cells to open Ca2+ influx channels, promoting Ca2+ influx, which then serves as an initiation signal to enhance sEVs secretion. We labeled intracellular Ca2+ using the Fluo-4 AM fluorescent probe, followed by rapid real-time dynamic imaging with confocal microscopy. Results demonstrate that (Fig. 4A), compared to non-magnetic substrates, cells on magnetic hydrogels exhibited showed higher intracellular Ca2+ fluorescence levels (Fig. 4B) and distinct time-dependent fluctuations (Fig. 4C). These results demonstrate that magnetically controlled periodic mechanical loading effectively activates intracellular calcium signaling pathways. Sustained elevations and transient fluctuations in Ca2+ levels likely constitute early critical events in the conversion of mechanical signals to vesicle secretion regulation, providing direct evidence for elucidating the molecular mechanisms by which mechanical stimuli regulate sEVs secretion.

To further elucidate the molecular mechanisms of mechanical signal transduction, we focused on the key mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 [24,25], which triggers Ca2+ influx by sensing changes in membrane mechanical forces [26]. We validated its expression changes under both cyclic mechanical loading and non-loading conditions. Results indicate that mechanical loading treatment significantly upregulates cellular Piezo1 protein expression levels (Fig. 5A and B). Since GsMTx4 is a functional inhibitor of mechanosensitive ion channels and does not directly inhibit Piezo1 expression, changes in Piezo1 immunomarkers after GsMTx4 treatment may reflect alterations in channel status, membrane localization, or antibody accessibility. This finding aligns closely with the previously observed significant increase in secreted sEVs numbers and elevated Ca2+ concentrations, suggesting that Piezo1 may serve as an initiator of mechanical sensing, regulating sEVs release by mediating calcium influx. Furthermore, intervention with the Piezo1-specific inhibitor GsMTx4 prevented mechanical loading from inducing an increase in sEVs numbers (Fig. 5C). This inhibitory effect functionally confirmed that cyclic mechanical loading activates Piezo1 channels, thereby initiating downstream calcium signaling and the sEVs secretion pathway. This reveals the pathway “mechanical loading → Piezo1 activation → calcium influx → sEVs release,” providing a mechanistic basis for understanding how mechanobiology regulates sEVs secretion. The immunofluorescence data here reflect the intensity and distribution of Piezo1-related cellular signals, but they cannot distinguish between changes in total protein abundance and changes in channel localization or antibody accessibility. After identifying the Piezo1-mediated mechanochemical transduction pathway, we further investigated the role of another core mechanosensitive factor, Yes-associated protein (YAP). As a key nuclear mechanosignal transducer, YAP has been extensively demonstrated to respond to mechanical stimuli and regulate diverse cellular behaviors [9,27]. To explore the role of YAP-driven sEVs secretion in our magnetic loading system, we examined intracellular YAP expression and corresponding sEVs secretion with and without periodic mechanical loading. As shown by the experimental results (Fig. 5A, Control), mechanical loading significantly enhanced nuclear YAP signaling, correlating with higher sEVs production in the previously treated group (Fig. 2B). Moreover, this increased sEVs generation was substantially suppressed by veriporfin (a YAP inhibitor) (Fig. 5C, D, S16). This altered sEVs production pattern indicates that the YAP expression elevation induced by magnetically controlled mechanical stimulation plays a crucial role in sEVs generation.

Based on existing literature and the aforementioned experimental results, we propose the following integrated mechanistic framework (Fig. 5E): When cells undergo periodic mechanical loading on a magnetic hydrogel surface, force signals are perceived and transmitted via membrane proteins (such as integrins), triggering cytoskeletal reorganization that drives YAP nuclear translocation and activation. Concurrently, the mechanosensitive channel Piezo1 is directly activated, mediating calcium ion influx. It is known that YAP activation upregulates MVB biogenesis [28], while calcium signaling promotes MVB fusion with the plasma membrane and sEVs release. Therefore, the Piezo1-Ca2+ and YAP pathways likely synergize at two levels, “release regulation” and “generation regulation” to jointly enhance sEVs secretion.

However, stronger mechanical stimulation does not necessarily lead to increased sEVs secretion (Fig. 2G–I), and existing studies may offer explanations for this phenomenon. Past studies have demonstrated that dynamic mechanical loading promotes the accumulation of the signaling protein pFAK and stimulates the generation of new actin filaments [29,30], while the enhanced cytoskeleton impedes the release of sEVs [6]. Therefore, the regulation of sEVs secretion by magnetically controlled loading may actually involve dual effects of both promotion and inhibition, with the final outcome determined by the dynamic equilibrium and crosstalk between different signaling pathways (Fig. 5F). This complexity suggests that cells integrate multi-level regulatory networks in response to their mechanical microenvironment to achieve precise modulation of their secretory behavior.

In short, this study preliminarily reveals a dual-axis mechanism by which mechanical stimulation precisely regulates sEVs secretion through parallel and potentially converging signaling nodes (Piezo1 and YAP), providing a new theoretical perspective for understanding the mechanobiological regulation of sEVs.

Conclusion

This study successfully developed and validated an innovative platform based on magnetically controlled mechanical loading to efficiently promote the secretion of small extracellular vesicles by mesenchymal stem cells. We first prepared a biocompatible magnetic composite hydrogel substrate. Driven by an external dynamic magnetic field, this platform enables the remote, conversion of magnetic energy into periodic mechanical deformations acting upon the hydrogel and adherent cells, exhibiting high reversibility. Experimental results demonstrate that, compared to static culture or non-magnetic substrates, this dynamic mechanical stimulation significantly enhances MSC-derived sEVs secretion, reaching up to 5 times the levels under conventional conditions, without affecting the typical morphology and size distribution of the vesicles. Through a series of control experiments, we confirm that this enhancing effect originates from the magnetic field-mediated periodic mechanical stimulation itself, rather than indirect influences from the material's magnetic properties or surface characteristics.

The significance of this work lies not only in providing a remotely controllable physical strategy for enhancing sEVs production, offering novel insights to overcome bottlenecks in large-scale manufacturing, but more importantly, in establishing a new theoretical perspective from a biomechanical standpoint for understanding the force-based biological regulation of sEVs secretion. Of course, this study still has certain limitations. For example, in-depth analysis of changes in sEVs subpopulations and content lineages has not yet been conducted, the spatial distribution of MNPs within the hydrogel is not perfectly homogeneous, and local particle aggregation may exist in composite magnetic soft materials. Although our current results indicate that this does not alter the overall magnetic actuation trend or the main biological conclusions, more quantitative characterization of particle distribution homogeneity and batch-to-batch variation will be valuable in future studies. Besides, the biosafety of in vivo loading requires further evaluation, and the therapeutic efficacy of the obtained sEVs needs to be validated in more disease models. Looking ahead, this magnetic control platform can be further optimized for stimulation parameters and extended to other therapeutically valuable cell types. Precise programming of mechanical regulation to tailor sEVs cargo could enable the “customized” production of functionally enhanced vesicle formulations, propelling cell-free therapeutic strategies toward more robust clinical applications.

Experimental procedures

Preparation of Fe3O4 MNPs

Diethylene glycol (DEG, Sinopharm, CHN) and ethylene glycol (EG, Sinopharm, CHN) were mixed in a volume ratio of 3:1 corresponding to 100 nm MNPs and 5:3 corresponding to 160 nm MNPs. 2.7% w/v ferric chloride hexahydrate (FeCl3·6H2O, Sinopharm, CHN), 10% w/v anhydrous sodium acetate (Sinopharm, CHN), and 0.25% w/v polyacrylic acid (PAA, Sinopharm, CHN) were mixed under ultrasonic stirring conditions. The above solution was added into a high temperature reactor and continuously heated at 200 °C for 12 h, followed by natural cooling at room temperature. The precipitate was washed alternately with water and ethanol (Macklin, CHN).

Characterization of Fe3O4 MNPs

The morphology of the prepared Fe3O4 MNPs samples was observed using a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Zeiss, DEU). Room temperature hysteresis lines of Fe3O4 MNPs samples were measured using a vibrating sample magnetometer (VSM, Quantum Design, USA). Sample composition of Fe3O4 MNPs was measured using Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Bruker, DEU).

Preparation of magnetic hydrogels

Glass Petri dishes (NEST, CHN) were sequentially trimmed using 4% APTES (Sigma, USA) solution, 15 min, and 2% glutaraldehyde (Sinopharm, CHN)/PBS (Gibco, USA) solution, 30 min, to allow the bottom of the gel to attach to the bottom of the dish. To minimize the effect of substrate stiffness differences between magnetic and non-magnetic hydrogels, different monomer and crosslinker compositions were used to obtain comparable initial mechanical properties. For non-magnetic hydrogels, 7.5 wt% acrylamide (Sinopharm, CHN), N,N′-methylenebisacrylamide (BIS, Sinopharm, CHN) at 0.08 wt% relative to the acrylamide content, 0.1% TEMED (Sinopharm, CHN), and 0.1% ammonium persulfate (Sinopharm, CHN) were used. For magnetic hydrogels, 5 wt% acrylamide (Sinopharm, CHN), BIS (Sinopharm, CHN) at 0.1 wt% relative to the acrylamide content, 0.1% TEMED (Sinopharm, CHN), and 0.1% ammonium persulfate (Sinopharm, CHN) were used. For magnetic hydrogels, 5 wt% acrylamide (Sinopharm, CHN), BIS (Sinopharm, CHN) at 0.1 wt% relative to the acrylamide content, 0.1% TEMED (Sinopharm, CHN), 0.1% ammonium persulfate (Sinopharm, CHN), and a certain amount of Fe3O4 MNPs were mixed thoroughly by vortexing and ultrasonic dispersion immediately before gelation to minimize particle aggregation and obtain a uniform prepolymer solution as much as possible, which was polymerized at room temperature in the absence of air for approximately 30 min. After polymerization, the gels were rinsed with deionized water to remove unpolymerized solution and free Fe3O4 MNPs.

Mechanical characterization of magnetic hydrogels

Mechanical properties of magnetic hydrogels were measured using a JPK NanoWizard Sense+ (Bruker, DEU) Bio-Atomic Force Microscope. The modulus information was obtained by analyzing the force-displacement curves of the retraction phase in force curve mode using an NPO-10-B probe (sping const: 0.12 N/m) with a tip modified with 20 μm diameter polyvinyl chloride spheres. The Young's modulus (E) of the samples was calculated using the Hertz model:(1)F=43ErRδ3/2where F is the indentation force, Er is the indentation force, the R is the radius of the probe, δ is the indentation depth.

Characterization of magnetic hydrogels

For SEM observation, gel samples were rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen under their corresponding treatment conditions and then freeze-dried. For the magnetic-field group, the magnet was maintained in place during the freezing step so that the instantaneous field-induced structural state could be preserved as much as possible before drying. After the surface was sprayed with gold, a scanning electron microscope (SEM, Zeiss, DEU) was used to obtain the morphology and an energy spectrometer was used to obtain the elemental distribution (Thermo, USA).

Cell culture

Cells were cultured at 37 °C and 5% CO2. Human umbilical cord mesenchymal stem cells (hMSCs) were generously provided by Anhui Medical University. hMSCs were cultured using mesenchymal stem cell medium (ScienCell, USA). The above medium contained 5% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 1% MSC growth supplement (MSC GS), and 1% penicillin/streptomycin solution (Pen Strep). Cells were passaged after reaching 70% to 80% confluence in cell division. Cells were routinely tested for mycoplasma contamination and were used only in the absence of contamination.

Cell inoculation on magnetic hydrogels

Type I collagen (Solarbio, CHN) dissolved at 0.6% w/v in 6 mM acetic acid (Sinopharm, CHN) was encapsulated on the hydrogel to promote cell adhesion on the hydrogel, and the protein was bonded to the hydrogel using a 1 mg/mL anisotropic and bifunctional protein cross-linking agent, sulfosuccinimide 6-(4′-azido-2′-nitrophenylamino) hexanoate (Sulfo-SANPAH, Thermo Fisher, USA) was used to bond the proteins to the hydrogels. Afterwards, the collagen-modified hydrogels were stored in PBS (Gibco, USA) at 4 °C until further cell culture.

Prior to cell inoculation, the prepared collagen-modified hydrogels were incubated in PBS overnight, then rinsed with PBS and the hydrogels were sterilized with 254 nm UV light for at least 1 h. Cells were rinsed with PBS and digested with 0.25% trypsin-EDTA solution (Gibco, USA) in culture flasks for 3-5 min. The digested cells were centrifuged at 300 g for 3-5 min. Finally, the cells were re-suspended and inoculated onto the prepared collagen-modified hydrogels. In order to avoid the potential effects of intercellular connections and interactions on the ability of cells to secrete sEVs, the inoculation density of hMSC was low (about 50 cells/mm2). The cells were cultured in complete medium for 24 h, and the supernatant rich in sEVs was collected after cultured in MSC serum-free medium (ScienCell, USA) for 48 h. After elution of the medium, the number of live adherent cells was counted using a cell counting plate (WASTON, JPN).

Cytocompatibility assessment of magnetic hydrogels

24 h after cell inoculation, the cytocompatibility of the hydrogels was assessed using AO/EB stains (KeyGEN, CHN), in which the concentrations of both Acridine Orange (AO) and Ethidium Bromide (EB) were 100 μg/mL, and the staining was visualized using a fluorescence microscope (DMi8, Leica, GEN) after 5-10 min.

Magnetic field platform construction

Permanent magnets N52 (2×2×2 cm3) were fixed to crank–connecting rod device for reciprocating motion to provide a periodically varying magnetic field. During loading, the magnet moved horizontally above the culture dish, while the dish remained stationary in a live-cell workstation (Tokai Hit, JPN). This setup generated a dynamic magnetic field whose magnitude at the hydrogel position varied periodically with the motion of the magnet. The loading frequency was controlled by the motor speed and was set to 0.17 Hz unless otherwise specified. The maximum magnetic field strength applied to the magnetic hydrogel was adjusted by changing the distance between the permanent magnet and the Petri dish. Through this configuration, the embedded Fe3O4 nanoparticles in the hydrogel responded to the changing magnetic field and induced reversible deformation of the gel, thereby delivering cyclic mechanical stimulation to the adherent cells.

Extraction and purification of sEVs

The cell culture supernatant was centrifuged at 4 °C for 10 min (300g), 10 min (2,000g), and 10 min (10,000g) to remove cells, dead cells, and membrane debris, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at an ultracentrifuge of 100,000g for 70 min to collect the sEVs pellet with impurities. The precipitate was resuspended in PBS and then centrifuged at 100,000g for 70 min to collect the crude sEVs, and the precipitate was resuspended in PBS. The crude sEVs were passed through a 220 nm filter membrane (Merck, USA).

The ExoPura® Basic Exosome Purification Column Kit (Umibio, CHN) was used according to the manufacturer's instructions for sEVs purification using the size exclusion method. The column was washed with PBS, 0.5 mL of room temperature equilibrated sample was added, the sample was eluted using 2.37 mL of PBS, 0.5 mL of PBS was added in batches, and the purified sEVs sample was collected in a volume of 1.5 mL. The sEVs were passed through a 220 nm filter membrane (Merck, USA) and stored at −80 °C.

Characterization of particle size and number of sEVs

Nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA) was performed using NanoSight NS300 (Malvern Panalytical, UK) and the corresponding NTA 3.4 Build 3.4.4 software to measure the particle size and concentration of sEVs. Isolated sEVs samples were diluted appropriately with PBS buffer to measure particle size and concentration. The temperature was maintained around 20 °C and 30 °C.

Transmission electron microscopy characterization

Transmission electron microscopy (TECNAI T12 120 KV, FEI, USA) was used to observe the morphology and size magnitude of sEVs. Particle diameter and roundness were determined manually from the images using imageJ software. sEVs roundness was calculated using the formula:(2)Circularity=4π(Area)Perimeter2

Cytoskeleton and adhesion spot staining

Cells were incubated on samples for 23 h, loaded with a dynamic magnetic field for 1 h. Cells were fixed using 4% paraformaldehyde (Biosharp, CHN) for 10 min at room temperature and washed three times with ice PBS. Cells were permeabilized with 0.25% Triton-X (Biosharp, CHN) for 10 min at room temperature and washed 3 times with PBS. Cells were incubated with 3% bovine serum protein (BSA, Solarbio, CHN) for 1 h at room temperature to seal the specific binding of the antibody. Samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C using Paxillin primary antibody (proteintech, CHN) and washed 3 times with PBS. Samples were incubated for 1 h using secondary antibody (Abcam, USA) protected from light and washed 3 times with PBS. The samples were incubated with 100 nM ghost pen cyclic peptide (Solarbio, CHN) solution for 30 min and 100 ng/mL DAPI (Yeasen, CHN) solution for 2 min at 4 °C. The samples were washed 3 times with PBS and stored at 4 °C away from light. Fluorescence images were obtained by confocal microscopy (Leica, GER). Radial intensity analysis and cell orientation and fluorescence area were obtained using ImageJ software.

Intracellular calcium measurements

Cells were incubated on samples for 23 h, loaded with a dynamic magnetic field for 1 h, incubated with 1 μM Fluo-4 (MCE, USA) in PBS for 30 min at room temperature, and further incubated in complete medium for 30 min. Time-lapse intracellular calcium imaging was performed using a confocal microscope (Leica, GER). Images were acquired at equal time intervals over 15 min (30 frames in total). Regions of interest (ROIs) were selected at the single-cell level, and fluorescence intensity was extracted frame by frame using ImageJ. Background fluorescence was subtracted for each frame. The mean fluorescence intensity of each cell over the full recording period was used for statistical analysis.

Piezo1 and YAP mediated release of sEVs

Cells were cultured on the samples for 24 h, during which the kinetic magnetic field was loaded twice for 1 h. Controls were not treated with any drugs, and cells treated with 5 μM GsMTx4 (MCE, USA), 2 μM Verteporfin (KKL, CHN) were used as the experimental group. Immunofluorescence staining steps were as above. Images were acquired using confocal microscopy (Leica, GER), and the fluorescence intensity was counted by ImageJ. In addition, EVs from all groups in the medium were collected by ultracentrifugation and detected with NTA.

Finite element analysis

Finite element analysis was performed using the commercial software COMSOL on square hydrogel substrate cells (side length: 100 μm, height 15 μm) with an elliptical cell of 15 μm in height, 40 μm in length and 25 μm in width. The maximum strain of the hydrogel substrate was set to 20% along the x and -x directions, respectively. This model was used to evaluate how cell orientation affects stress distribution under a prescribed substrate strain state. It did not explicitly incorporate magnetic field-particle interactions, particle heterogeneity within the gel, interfacial friction, fluid effects, or the time-dependent viscoelastic behavior of the hydrogel. Therefore, the simulation should be regarded as a simplified mechanistic analysis rather than a full reconstruction of the magnetic loading process.

Modeling of cells using a hyperelastic material model (Neo-Hookean model)(3)Ψ=μ2(I‾1−3)+κ2(Jel−1)2where μ is the shear modulus, J is the elastic volume ratio, κ is the bulk modulus, and I is the isotropic first invariant. The Young's modulus of the hydrogel was set to 8 kPa and Poisson's ratio was set to 0.45.

Statistical analysis

Statistics data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three independent replicates. Statistical analysis was performed with one-way ANOVA with Tukey post hoc test (Origin 2022 software) for multiple comparison. The statistical significance, high statistical significance, and no statistical significance are defined as P < 0.05, ∗, P < 0.01, ∗∗, P < 0.001, ∗∗∗, and NS, respectively.
