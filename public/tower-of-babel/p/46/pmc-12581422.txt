Thrombotic microangiopathy associated with mantle cell lymphoma: a case report
Authors: Yuri Tonouchi, Koji Hashimoto, Ryohei Iwabuchi, Hitoshi Sakai, Takayuki Nimura, Kosuke Yamaka, Marina Nishikawa, Akinori Yamaguchi, Kosuke Sonoda, Yuji Kamijo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is a syndrome characterized by vascular endothelial cell damage, with anemia, thrombocytopenia, and thrombus-related organ damage. Secondary TMA is caused by various factors, including hematological malignancy. Case reports on TMA with mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) are extremely rare. Immunostaining of the kidney biopsy specimen revealed MCL; chemotherapy was initiated, which improved the thrombocytopenia and hemolysis.ConclusionsTo our best knowledge, this is a very rare case of secondary TMA owing to MCL wherein chemotherapy was effective, suggesting a causal relationship. Background

Thrombotic microangiopathy (TMA) is a syndrome characterized by vascular endothelial cell damage, with the three main features of hemolytic anemia, thrombocytopenia, and thrombus-related organ damage, including kidney injury [1]. TMA is classified into four major categories according to the causative disease: Shiga toxin-induced hemolytic uremic syndrome (STEC-HUS), thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura (TTP), atypical hemolytic uremic syndrome (aHUS), and secondary TMA. A differential diagnosis is essential because treatment strategies differ depending on the causative disease [2]. Secondary TMA is caused by various factors, including autoimmune diseases, drugs, and malignancy. Therefore, treating the underlying cause is crucial for improving patient’s condition. Secondary TMA caused by hematological malignancies is generally less common than that caused by solid tumors; lymphoma is the most common cause of TMA among hematological malignancies [3]. Although Mantle cell lymphoma (MCL) accounts for 3% of all malignant lymphomas [4], case reports on TMA with MCL are extremely rare. Thus, its pathogenic relationship remains unknown.

Herein, we report a very rare case of secondary TMA wherein MCL was diagnosed based on the same kidney biopsy specimen and treated with chemotherapy, resulting in improvement in TMA.

Case presentation

A 60-year-old man receiving treatment of hypertension and dyslipidemia visited his family doctor due to neck stiffness, coldness, and color changes in his fingers over a period of two months. He was diagnosed with peripheral circulatory failure owing to a smoking history of 10 cigarettes per day. The following month, his serum creatinine level worsened from 0.7 to 2.5 mg/dL without any subjective symptoms. It further worsened to 4.0 mg/dL over the next three weeks. Rapid progressive glomerulonephritis was suspected because of worsening kidney function, proteinuria, and microscopic hematuria. Consequently, he was referred to the nephrology department of a general hospital and underwent a kidney biopsy. Light microscopy revealed 23 glomeruli, of which five had global glomerular sclerosis. Some glomeruli also showed edematous lesions in the mesangial area (Fig. 1a) and foamy changes in the subendothelial lumen (Fig. 1b). Additionally, subendothelial edema of the interlobular artery was observed (Fig. 1c), which resulted in the narrowing of the vessel lumen and subsequent glomerular collapse (Fig. 1d). No duplication (double contour formation) of the glomerular basement membrane was observed. The presence of simplified epithelium and interstitial edema in 30% of the tubules was suggestive of acute tubular injury” for more readability. Additionally, interstitial fibrosis and tubular atrophy were observed in 20% of the tubules. Electron microscopy revealed an enlarged subendothelial lumen but no high-electron-density deposits; however, immunofluorescence staining for immunoglobulins and complement revealed negative results (see Additional file 1). Furthermore, focal interstitial lymphocytic infiltration was detected in the kidney tissue (Fig. 2a) and aggregates of pathologically homologous lymphocytes were observed in the interstitial area, suggesting monoclonal proliferation (Fig. 2b). However, these lymphocytes did not infiltrate the glomerular capillaries and remained localized outside the glomeruli. Three months prior to the kidney biopsy, his platelet count was 19.9 × 10⁴, which had decreased to 12.5 × 10⁴/µL at the time of the biopsy along with low hemoglobin and haptoglobin levels. Moreover, fundus examination revealed cotton wool spots and petechiae in both eyes. These findings were thought to be caused by temporary impairment in ocular blood flow. These symptoms and kidney biopsy findings suggested a rapid change in systemic hemodynamics, possibly owing to systemic TMA. At that time, the blood pressure was 120/80 mmHg, thereby ruling out malignant hypertension.

Consequently, the patient was referred to our hospital for advanced medical care. At the initial visit to our hospital, the patient exhibited clear consciousness and no fever. Moreover, the patient’s vital signs were within the normal range: blood pressure, 119/68 mmHg; pulse rate, 95 beats per minute; and oxygen saturation rate, 98% on room air. Respiratory sounds showed no rales. Furthermore, his abdomen was soft without tenderness. However, both hands felt cold, fingers were dark purple (Fig. 3a); Raynaud’s symptoms induced by cooling were observed (Fig. 3b). Additionally, urinary tests at admission to our hospital showed moderately elevated protein excretion, a marked increase in tubular damage markers, and various cellular casts in the urine sediment, suggesting kidney cell injury (Table 1). Blood examination revealed a slightly low hemoglobin and high lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) level, elevated schistocyte count, and low haptoglobin level. Although the platelet level was within normal limits (19.8 × 10⁴/µL) at the time of admission to our hospital, it decreased to 12.1 × 10⁴/µL in the following 10 days. Serum levels of creatinine, urea nitrogen, and potassium were also markedly high, indicating rapid progressive kidney dysfunction requiring renal replacement therapy.

Notably, the patient did not experience any episode of bloody stools or abdominal pain suggestive of STEC-HUS. Additionally, the activity of disintegrin-like metalloproteinase with thrombospondin type 1 motif 13 (ADAMTS13) was normal and autoantibodies, including ADAMTS13 inhibitors, were not detected. Moreover, the patient had not been administered any drug recognized for its association with TMA for a few months prior to this event. Although soluble interleukin (IL)-2 receptor (sIL-2R) was elevated, the changes were mild. Thus, the patient’s medical history and negative autoantibody test results did not indicate any common causative diseases of secondary TMA. In addition, although chest radiography showed no abnormalities, abdominal contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) showed bilateral mild kidney atrophy with diffuse spotty areas and poor enhancement (Fig. 4a). The CT image also showed an enlarged left inguinal lymph node; however, no other lymph node enlargements or neoplastic lesions were observed (Fig. 4b). As the kidney biopsy findings indicated marked TMA and worsening kidney function, we considered the possibility of TMA exacerbation, which required immediate treatment. Since the possibility of aHUS or TTP could not be ruled out at the time of hospitalization, plasma exchange, intravenous methylprednisolone steroid therapy (1000 mg/day for 3 days), and oral prednisolone (50 mg/day) were initiated as the initial treatments of TMA. Hemodialysis (HD) was also introduced to treat the kidney dysfunction; however, the disease condition responded poorly to initial therapy and platelets count decreased to 0.6 × 10⁴/µL. In addition, Hb decreased to 8.6 g/dL, LDH increased to 476 U/L, and the percentage of schistocyte worsened to 1.1%. These findings indicated progression of hemolytic anemia; we determined that the TMA had worsened.

Since focal interstitial lymphocytic infiltration was obvious in the kidney biopsy specimens, we suspected monoclonal lymphocyte proliferation and performed immunostaining, identifying CD20-positive and Cyclin D1-positive monoclonal lymphocyte infiltration (Fig. 2c and d). Further analysis using fluorescence in situ hybridization detected (11;14) translocations in the same kidney specimens. Based on these findings, the patient was diagnosed with MCL. Additionally, flow cytometric examination of a bone marrow sample showed the possibility of bone marrow infiltration by the MCL. Although a lymph node biopsy could not be performed, an enlarged lymph node limited to the inguinal region was suspected to be MCL. Thus, the MCL was determined to be stage IV. Based on these detailed examinations, the patient was diagnosed with secondary TMA associated with MCL, wherein chemotherapy with VR-CAP therapy (21days) of bortezomib (1.3 mg/m² hypodermic injection day 1, 4, 8, 11), rituximab (375 mg/m² intravenous drip day 1), cyclophosphamide (750 mg/m² intravenous drip day 1), doxorubicin (50 mg/m² intravenous drip day 1), and prednisolone (100 mg oral medication day 1–5) was initiated. We adjusted the regimen based on previous reports [5].

After 1 cycle of VR-CAP therapy, the platelet count increased to 15.0 × 10⁴/µL and the serum LDH level decreased, suggesting an improvement in the TMA findings. Subsequently, thrombocytopenia was observed once again after chemotherapy. However, the patient exhibited no recurrence of hemolytic anemia, wherein haptoglobin levels were elevated. After six cycles of VR-CAP therapy, the MCL reached complete remission. At the same time, there were no findings of thrombocytopenia or hemolytic anemia (Plt, 13.4 × 10⁴/µL; Hb, 12.0 g/dL; LDH, 432 U/L); TMA remission was maintained. In addition, haptoglobin levels also increased (see Additional file 2). However, kidney function did not improve; therefore, maintenance HD therapy was continued.

Discussion and conclusions

Herein, we report a rare case of secondary TMA associated with MCL that was treated with chemotherapy. Secondary TMA can be caused by various diseases including autoimmune and infectious diseases, malignant tumors, and malignant hypertension along with drugs and pregnancy [2]. Among these, malignancy-associated TMA is frequently reported, with the majority being solid organ malignant diseases, including gastric (26%), breast (21%), and prostate cancer (14%). Although hematologic tumors are less frequently reported than solid tumors, lymphomas are common (8%), including intravascular and Burkitt lymphoma [2, 3]. However, TMA associated with MCL is exceedingly rare. Therefore, our case is clinically instructive in several respects, particularly in terms of diagnosis and treatment.

First, pathological findings indicating TMA and MCL were detected in the same organ specimen. In general, various examinations are required to determine the cause of TMA. For instance, an interview regarding the history of prior infection and use of new drugs and blood tests for ADAMTS13 activity, various autoantibodies, tumor markers, and antibodies against various infectious diseases are necessary. Additionally, confirming the presence or absence of neoplastic lesions using CT and other imaging modalities is important. However, in this case, since STEC-HUS and TTP were ruled out, secondary TMA was suspected. The patient had no history of new drug use, and antibody screening tests for autoimmune diseases and various infections revealed negative results along with most tumor markers; only sIL-2R levels were elevated. CT also did not reveal any obvious neoplastic lesions. These negative examinations highlight the difficulty in identifying the primary disease of TMA. Nonetheless, the kidney biopsy revealed findings indicative of MCL, leading to the primary diagnosis of TMA. Thus, a kidney biopsy might help diagnose the underlying disease in TMA and should be performed aggressively, particularly when the underlying disease is unknown. As in this case, scattered poor enhancing areas on renal contrast-enhanced CT imaging suggest the possibility of invasive lesions such as lymphoma. A renal biopsy might be warranted in such cases as part of a more proactive diagnostic strategy.

However, the high risk of bleeding is a serious concern because TMA can cause thrombocytopenia. Hematoma is also most likely to occur in patients with platelet counts below 100,000/µL [6]; therefore, kidney biopsies must be performed with caution in TMA cases. Additionally, confirming the absence of anemia and progression or enlargement of hematoma formation around the kidney using blood tests and ultrasonography after kidney biopsy is essential. Moreover, kidney biopsies should be performed at a facility capable of handling emergencies, such as catheter-based kidney artery embolization or nephrectomy for bleeding complications. Although specific criteria for platelet counts required for kidney biopsies have not been established, many centers refer to the Japanese guidelines for platelet transfusions for procedures and surgery in patients presenting with thrombocytopenia [7]. The Japanese guideline recommend kidney biopsy or platelet transfusion prior to biopsy when platelets are below 50,000/µL [7]. Transvenous kidney biopsy is also reported to be relatively safe in patients at high risk of bleeding, such as those with thrombocytopenia [8]; therefore, this may be a useful method at centers that can perform it.

Second, the clinical course of MCL after chemotherapy in this case suggests a possible relationship between TMA and MCL. The standard treatments of TTP include plasma exchange and steroid therapy [9]. In the case of TMA, plasma exchange should be initiated on the presumption of TTP unless evidence strongly suggests another etiology [10]. Therefore, in this case, the patient was initially treated with plasma exchange and steroids; however, the response was poor. Secondary TMA is often refractory to conventional therapies such as plasma exchange, but treatment of the causative disease is effective. Consequently, VR-CAP therapy for MCL promptly increased platelet counts and decreased serum LDH levels, suggesting an improvement in the TMA condition. This phenomenon indirectly suggests a causal relationship between TMA and MCL.

Two major mechanisms of kidney injury in MCL are membranous proliferative glomerulonephritis, associated with immune complexes or complement, and kidney injury, owing to direct lymphoma infiltration. Previously reported cases of MCL with kidney injury, in which kidney biopsy was conducted, were systematically summarized in a table (Table 2) [11–30]. The incidence of MPGN and renal infiltration of lymphoma in patients with MCL and kidney dysfunction is 27% and 27%, respectively. Additionally, membranous proliferative glomerulonephritis is thought to be caused by dysregulation of systemic immunity, complemented by MCL, rather than direct deposition of tumor-produced proteins [31]. In these cases, C3 and IgG are deposited in the glomeruli and complement is reduced. In contrast, we did not find immune complex deposition in the glomeruli in the present case. Fluorescent immunostaining was also negative for IgG, C3, and all other stains, and serum complement was not depleted. The lymphoma cells infiltrated the stroma, but the tubular structures were preserved in the area. Thus, we concluded that renal dysfunction was not caused by tubular dysfunction due to lymphoma infiltration. On the other hand, we diagnosed the patient with renal injury due to TMA based on findings characteristic of TMA, such as subendothelial edema of the interlobular arteries and narrowing of the lumen of the vessels. No report of renal TMA in patients with MCL is available; therefore, our case is rare.

Nonetheless, the mechanisms underlying the development of TMA associated with MCL remain unclear. A previous study suggested that cytokines produced by tumors may influence the mechanism of malignancy-associated TMA [32]; however, specific cytokines have not yet been identified. In contrast, experiments using umbilical vein blood suggested that IL-8, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and IL-6 affect the process of von Willebrand factor (vWF) formation. IL-8, TNF-α, and IL-6 receptor complexes promote the production of Unusually large VWF multimers (UL-vWFM) in vascular endothelial cells and IL-6 inhibits the cleavage of UL-vWFM by ADAMTS13, resulting in increased UL-vWFM and decreased vWF [33]. Patients with increased UL-vWFM are more likely to develop thrombosis. In addition, complement C3 is activated in UL-vWFM moored on the surface of vascular endothelial cells, and C5b-9, the final product of the complement-activating cascade, causes vascular endothelial cell damage [34]. Previous studies suggested that these cytokines may be involved in the mechanisms underlying TMA caused by hematopoietic stem cell transplantation and COVID-19 [34, 35]. A previous study indicated that MCL cells secrete IL-6 [36] and another study showed that patients newly diagnosed with MCL had significantly elevated IL-8 levels compared to controls [37]. Thus, malignancy-associated TMA may be associated with cytokine levels.

Reportedly, plasma exchange is not effective in malignancy-associated TMA, probably because of uncontrolled cytokine production, whereas treatment of the underlying malignancy is effective because of the reduction in cytokines from the tumor [32]. Thus, in the current case, cytokines produced by renal-infiltrating MCL cells might have influenced TMA development. The speed of cytokine production by MCL may be greater than the speed of cytokine removal by plasma exchange; therefore the patient was unable to respond to the initial treatment. However, several cycles of chemotherapy for MCL may reduce the number of tumor cells and decrease cytokine production, leading to TMA improvement, as observed in the current case.

Third, although TMA improved following lymphoma treatment, the lack of renal function recovery underscores the importance for early diagnosis and intervention. In this case, the patient presented to the previous physician when renal dysfunction had already progressed, showing a serum creatinine level of 2.5 mg/dL. The absence of renal recovery, despite resolution of TMA manifestations, was possibly due to irreversible kidney damage by the time of diagnosis, which could have developed advanced through a subacute course. Therefore, earlier recognition and initiation of treatment are critical.

In this case, Raynaud’s phenomenon preceded the development of significant renal dysfunction. Vascular endothelial cell injury is considered a contributing factor to Raynaud’s phenomenon [38]; thus this symptom may have been related to TMA. When Raynaud’s symptoms suddenly occur and tests for collagen vascular disease reveal negative results, TMA should be considered as a possible underlying cause. Furthermore, previous reports have described cotton-wool exudates as an early manifestation of TMA [39]. The ocular lesions observed in this patient might likewise represent an early sign of the disease. Encountering such findings, careful monitoring and investigation for underlying causes are warranted, with consideration of TMA as a potential initial presentation.

Conclusion

We encountered a rare case of secondary TMA associated with MCL. Thus, malignancies may coexist in patients with treatment-resistant TMA and detailed evaluation of the renal biopsy tissue could lead to MCL diagnosis, which allowed us to identify the primary disease of TMA and provide effective treatment in this case.
