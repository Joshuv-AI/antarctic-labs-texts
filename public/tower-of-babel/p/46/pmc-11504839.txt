How sleep duration impacts cognitive status among Indian population aged ≥45 years: Evidence from Longitudinal Aging Study India (LASI)
Authors: Pritam Halder, Srinath Kathirvel, Anshul Mamgai, Saumyarup Pal, Sayani Bisoi, Ankita Chattopadhyay, Abin K. Rajan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroduction:An aging population is associated with an increased risk of cognitive decline. Sleep duration, both excess and deficiency, is a risk factor for cognitive impairment. Therefore, this study was conducted to assess the relationship between sleep duration and cognition in the Indian population.Methods:This study was a secondary analysis of the first wave of the Longitudinal Aging Study in India (LASI) conducted across India in all 34 states and union territories. Sleep duration was calculated using sleep and wake times, whereas cognition was measured as a composite cognitive index. Both univariable and multivariable linear regressions were performed on the composite cognitive score. Subgroup analyses were conducted based on sex and age groups (45-59 years and ≥60 years).Results:The median (IQR) cognition score was 25 (20-30). A sleep duration of ≤4 hours was associated with a decrease in cognition status of 1.15 points (1.57-0.72), and a sleep duration of ≥9 hours was associated with a decrease of 0.93 points (1.25-0.60). Subgroup analysis revealed that this relationship remained consistent across both genders and both age groups.Conclusion:Both long and short durations of sleep were seen to be associated with a decrease in cognition scores among Indian adults, in both sexes and both age groups. Thus, proper sleep duration can help in the prevention of declining cognition as one ages and the development of dementia.

Introduction

Cognitive impairment is a growing public health problem across the world because of increased longevity. Cognitive impairment can lead to physical, mental, functional, and emotional impairment, thus affecting one’s ability to do work, decision-making, and social interactions. Dementia is currently the seventh leading cause of death globally and one of the major causes of disability among elderly people.[1] The estimated prevalence of dementia in India is 7.4%, affecting 8.8 million individuals aged 60 years or older.[2] Around 340 DALYs are lost globally every year because of dementia.[3] Dementia had cost 1.3 trillion US dollars globally.[1] With aging population on the rise, changing lifestyle practices, increasing metabolic risk factors, and increasing mental health illnesses, dementia has the potential to slowly become the next silent epidemic all over the world. Hence, it is essential to understand thoroughly the epidemiology of dementia and to identify all major risk factors of dementia.

One of such key risk factors that share a complex relationship with dementia is sleep. Sleep is an important aspect of human life because human beings spend around one-third of their entire lifetime in sleep. Several physiological events, important to maintain normal functionality of the human brain, such as memory consolidation, cortical and subcortical arousals, and awakenings, happen during sleep.[4] Deprivation of such physiological activities leads to several psychosocial consequences such as poor performance at work, fatigue, memory difficulties, difficulty in concentration, and increased risk of road traffic accidents. Apart from cognitive impairment, it also leads to other mental health problems such as depression, anxiety conditions, alcohol, and other substance use and medical problems such as metabolic disorders and disorders of cardiovascular, respiratory, renal, gastrointestinal, musculoskeletal, and immune systems. Sleep disturbance can increase the overall risk of mortality.[45]

Unfortunately, sleep deficiency and sleep disorders are still “an unmet public health problem” globally.[6] Around every 1 in 10 people aged ≥45 years (12.7%) have sleep problems in India.[7] This is expected to rise further because of an anticipated increase in aging population, changing work culture, increasing trends in the consumption of tea and coffee, late-evening light exposure, television viewing, video-gaming, social media usage, texting, smartphone use, and increasing mental health illnesses.[5]

The relationship between sleep disturbance and cognition is complex and varies contextually. Both increased and short sleep durations have been found to be associated with cognitive impairment. There is mixed evidence to comment on the direction of this relationship, i.e. whether sleep duration affects cognitive impairment or vice versa. Also, the relationship between sleep duration and cognition varies with respect to age group, domain of cognition, and context being assessed.[8] Although Study on Global Aging and Adult Health (SAGE) had been conducted in multiple countries including India, it found that patterns of both sleep duration and cognitive impairment were significantly different in India compared with other countries. The average sleep duration was also lower in India compared with some middle-income countries.[9] This necessitates a clear understanding of relationship between sleep duration and cognition among Indian adults in a country-specific context.

Longitudinal Ageing Survey of India (LASI) may provide a reliable opportunity to understand this relationship. LASI is a nationally representative study funded by the National Institute on Aging, the Government of India’s Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, and the United Nations Population Fund and contributed by The University of Southern California, the International Institute for Population Sciences, and the Harvard T.H. Chan School of Public Health. The survey intends to assess psychological, social, economic, and health aspects of aging and collects information on demographics, household economic status, chronic health conditions, symptom-based health conditions, functional health, mental health (cognition and depression), biomarkers, healthcare utilisation, family and social networks, social welfare programmes, employment, retirement, satisfaction, and life expectations.[7] Such nationally representative data with multi-dimensional information would be an ideal opportunity to look for the valid association between sleep and cognition among Indian adults. Therefore, we conducted this study with an objective to determine the association between sleep duration and cognitive status among Indian population aged ≥45 years under the LASI study, and furthermore, we aimed to assess the effect of gender and age over this association.

Methods

Study site

LASI Wave 1 survey was conducted across India in all 34 states and union territories (UT) of India, including both urban and rural areas under each state/UT.

Study participants

The target population for LASI Wave 1 included all Indian adults and elderly men and women aged 45 years and older, and their spouses who reside in the same household, irrespective of age. Among them, we included those who were aged at least 45 years for our study.

Study duration

The LASI field survey was conducted from April 2017 to December 2018.

Sample size

The survey intends to follow a representative sample of the older adult population every two years for the following 25 years, with a revised sample size to account for attrition because of death, migration, non-reachable, and non-responsive. More than 73,000 adult Indians were a part of round 1 of LASI survey.[7] After exclusion of missing data by complete case analysis with row-wise deletion, which was needed to study our study’s objective, a total of 14,241 participants aged ≥45 years were included in this study. Sampling technique

LASI adopted a multistage stratified area probability cluster sampling design. Within each state, LASI Wave 1 adopted a three-stage sampling design in rural areas and a four-stage sampling design in urban areas. In each state/UT, the first stage involved the selection of primary sampling units (PSUs), that is, sub-districts (Tehsils/Talukas), and the second stage involved the selection of 5 villages in rural areas and 5 wards in urban areas under each selected PSUs. In rural areas, 32 households were selected from selected villages in the third stage. However, sampling in urban areas involved an additional stage. Specifically, in the third stage, one Census Enumeration Block (CEB) was randomly selected in each urban area. In the fourth stage, 35 households were selected from this CEB.

Data collection

The LASI survey instrument comprised three pre-tested, survey schedules (household-, individual-, and community-level schedules). The following socio-demographic variables from individual schedules were considered in our study as explanatory variables: age, gender, educational status, residence (rural or urban), marital status, occupation, and monthly per capita expenditure (MPCE) quintile. Other variables included health-related behaviours such as physical activity, history of tobacco use, and history of alcohol use. History of being under coverage of health insurance, self-rated health, and history of multi-morbidity were assessed. Outcome variable considered in our study was cognition, which was measured in terms of the composite cognitive index. Data collectors in LASI were trained, and survey was monitored through a three-tiered supervision and monitoring mechanism to ensure data quality. Based on data collected under LASI survey on the time of onset of sleep at night and time of waking up in the morning, sleep duration was estimated for each participant by this study’s investigator, and it was used as an explanatory variable.

Operational definitions

Outcome variables

Cognition was measured in terms of the composite cognitive index, which was a composite measure of cognitive performance of adults under 6 different domains (memory, orientation, retrieval fluency, arithmetic function, executive function, and object naming). The total composite cognitive index score ranged from 0 to 43. [Supplementary Table 1]. For this study, both composite cognitive score and scores of each of the 6 different domains were used as outcome variables.

Explanatory variable

Sleep duration was considered as an explanatory variable of choice. Under sleep duration, participants were categorised into the following groups, with sleep duration of ≤4, 5, 6, 7, 8, and ≥9 hours per night.

Covariates

Age group (45-59 and ≥60 years), gender (male and female), minimum education (illiterate, less than primary. primary completed, middle completed, secondary school, higher secondary, and diploma/graduate), residence (rural and urban), marital status (unmarried, married/in live-in, and widow/separated/divorced), MPCE (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest) quintile, health insurance (no or yes), occupation (unemployed, professional and semi-professional- ”legislators and senior officials, professionals, technicians, and associate professionals,” clerical and skilled “clerks, service workers and shopkeepers, skilled agriculture and fishery workers, craft and related trade worker, plant and machine operator,” unskilled), physical activity (everyday, once per week, 1-3 times per week, once per month, or never), self-rated health (excellent, very good, good, fair, or poor), tobacco abuse (have you ever consumed tobacco? no or yes), alcohol abuse (have you ever consumed alcohol? no, yes), and multimorbidity were taken as other explanatory variables. Following chronic morbidities were included—hypertension, diabetes, cancer, chronic lung diseases (e.g., chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, asthma, chronic bronchitis, and other chronic lung problems), chronic heart disease (e.g., congestive heart failure, myocardial infarction, heart attack, and other chronic heart diseases), stroke, musculoskeletal disorder (MSD e.g., rheumatism, arthritis, osteoporosis, other chronic joint, or bone disorders), dyslipidaemia (high cholesterol), thyroid disorders, chronic renal failure, visual impairment, and hearing impairment. The interviewer asked a related question about chronic health conditions/morbidities with dichotomous answers (no/yes)—”Has any health professional ever diagnosed you with the following chronic conditions or diseases?” Participants having at least two chronic health conditions were described as multimorbidity. All these covariates were taken into consideration to adjust the potential confounders during regression.

Statistical analysis

Data were analysed in Stata version 17 (StataCorp. 2017. Stata Statistical Software: Release 17. College Station, TX: StataCorp LP.). Characteristics of participants were described as mean (standard deviation)/median (interquartile range) for continuous variables and frequencies and percentages for categorical variables (age group, occupation, education).

Sleep duration was calculated from following STATA command:

“gen double sleep_ = clock (tu002, “hm”)

gen double wake = clock (tu001, “hm”)

gen time_diff2 = cond (sleep_>wake, (wake + 86400000-sleep_)/60000, (wake-sleep_)/60000)”

“time_diff2” was then divided by 60 to obtain sleep duration in hours.

Univariable linear regression was conducted between an outcome variable and each explanatory variable. To avoid multicollinearity among explanatory variables, VIF (Variance inflation factor) was applied. VIF >5 indicates high correlation between a given explanatory variable and other covariates in the model. Self-related health and marital status had VIF >5. [Supplementary Table 2]. Hence, all the explanatory variables except these two were included for the final association. P value < 0.2 was taken for further multivariable regression, and regression coefficient (β) was plotted against sleep duration categories keeping 7 hours as reference. Subgroup regression analyses were performed with each domain under composite cognitive index as outcome (memory, orientation, arithmetic function, executive function, and object naming). P value < 0.05 was considered as statistically significant. Sub-group regression analyses were performed for age (45-59/older adults and ≥60 years/elderly) and gender sub-groups. All the above analyses were conducted under 2 separate models, one adjusting for age and/or gender (model 1), and another one adjusting for age, gender, education, residence, MPCE quintile, health insurance, occupation, physical activity, alcohol use, tobacco use, and multimorbidity (model 2).

Ethical clearance

The Indian Council of Medical Research’s (ICMR) Central Ethics Committee on Human Research (CECHR) gave its ethical clearance for the LASI survey’s execution.[7]

Results

The mean (SD) age of the overall participants was 58.95 (10.12). The median (IQR) cognition score was 25 (20-30), which was higher in males (28 (23-31)) than females (23 (19-28)). The median (IQR) cognition score was higher in older adults (27 (22-31)) than in elderly (24 (19-29)) participants. Overall, around one-third were users of tobacco, and one-sixth were consumers of alcohol. Mean (SD) sleep duration was 9.34 (5.02) hours per night, which was almost equal among gender and age groups. Of the total participants, three-fifths had slept for a longer duration (>8 hours), and around one-fifth had slept for a shorter duration (<7 hours) [Table 1].

Discussion

Roughly, three-fifths of the participants (60%) had slept for a longer duration (>8 hours) and around 17% had a shorter duration of sleep (<7 hours). Mean (SD) sleep duration of Indian adults aged at least 45 years was 9.34 (5.02) hours, which was higher than the recommended number of hours of sleep (7-8 hours). This was not similar to what was observed in The Study on Global Ageing and Adult Health (SAGE) survey among Indian adults.[9]

Both short and long sleep durations were associated with a significant decline in composite cognitive score and the results being almost similar across all subgroups. This V-shaped relationship between sleep duration and cognition has been observed by various studies in the previous literature from other countries.[81011]

Biological mechanisms behind shorter sleep duration and cognitive decline have been well understood by studies. When sleep is shortened or disrupted, cognitive performance on a range of activities including short-term memory, attention, and processing speed suffers.

Results from this study are also in harmony with two meta-analyses by Fan et al. and Liang et al. another study comprising of 2457 older participants from the Framingham cohort showed doubling of risk of all-cause dementia over 10 years of follow-up in individuals who reported long sleep duration.[101112] However, pathophysiology behind longer sleep duration and cognitive decline was not well understood. Various postulations have been put forward including early neurodegeneration corroborating with smaller total cerebral blood volume, a proxy imaging marker for global brain atrophy.[12] Circadian dysfunction, misalignment, and the altered production and secretion of melatonin can be attributed to neurodegenerative changes in projections between the suprachiasmatic nucleus, pineal gland, and retina that are involved in the regulation of sleep and wakefulness.[1314] Inflammaging can be held as a possible mechanism with increased Interleukin-6 and C-reactive protein levels in individuals with prolonged sleep duration, Alzheimer’s disease, severity of white matter hyperintensities, and global markers of brain atrophy.[10151617] The other possibilities are the presence of confounding variables such as depression, and underlying health problems. which could be associated with both long sleep duration and cognitive decline.[18]

There were numerous strengths to this study. The data were collected through community-based sampling through multi-staged stratified cluster random sampling. A large number of participants were included in the study. Although many participants of LASI survey were excluded from this study due to missing data, baseline characteristics were similar between included participants and overall participants who were included in the original LASI survey. This may indicate absence of potential selection bias because of missing data. Quality assurance measures such as training of data collectors and formulating standard operating procedure were followed during data collection. Cognition was comprehensively assessed through the composite cognitive index. Multivariable regression analyses were used to address measured and residual confounding. Sub-group analyses and sensitivity analyses were performed to check on robustness of the study’s findings.

Despite these, there were some limitations. Because of its cross-sectional nature, temporality could not be established as residual confounding could not be completely ignored. Because of self-reporting type of documentation of data, the actual prevalence of cognitive impairment might differ. Because of the self-reporting style, there was higher probability of recall bias and social desirability bias, which could not be eliminated.

However, the possibility of reverse causality that poor cognitive function leads to improper sleep duration cannot be ruled out as such. Temporality of the association between sleep duration and cognition might not be established through this study. Still, a sensitivity analysis excluding those with known cognitive or mental health disorders and those who belong to the lowest 10 percentile of cognitive function in the LASI survey yielded similar results. This may support the fact that improper sleep duration may lead to cognitive decline.

Also, sleep duration was assessed for a single day which may not be sufficient to comment on individual’s sleeping pattern. An individual’s sleep might be altered at some days because of various factors such as personal stressors, diet, caffeine intake, environmental factors, medications, history of daytime nap, and shift of working, and this could be a limitation of this study. Further research with objective methods including polysomnography, biomarkers, and studies aimed at interventions to improve sleep in terms of quality and duration aiming to prevent cognitive impairment may strengthen the findings of this study.

Conclusion

Overall, sleep duration of Indian adults was higher than the recommended number of hours of sleep (7-8 hours). Both short and long sleep durations were significantly associated with cognitive impairment among Indian adults. Hence, early attention on sleep disorders and health education regarding sleep hygiene can be helpful in preventing emergence of dementia as a new epidemic.
