Sphingolipids in human disease: organ-specific pathologies, chain-length-dependent effects, and translational implications
Authors: Lili Kong, Jiaxin Shi, Siyuan Wang, Jiaqi Huang, Yidong Ge, Yvxuan Li, KaiLang Li, Mengxiang Zhao, Zhiyou Li, Xiaofeng Jin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSphingolipids, particularly ceramide (Cer) and sphingomyelin (SM), are critical for cellular homeostasis, serving as both essential membrane components and key signaling molecules. This review systematically classifies sphingolipid disorders by organ system, emphasizing chain-length-dependent effects such as the pro-inflammatory role of C16-Cer versus the protective functions of C24-Cer in disease.Main bodyThe review details sphingolipid involvement across seven major organ systems. In cardiovascular disease, SM(d18:1/16:1) contributes to atherosclerotic plaque instability, while Cer promotes NLRP3 inflammasome activation. In neurodegenerative diseases, an altered Cer/SM ratio disrupts lipid raft integrity and accelerates pathogenic protein aggregation. In oncology, cancer progression is facilitated through mechanisms like nSMase-mediated exosome release and CerS6-dependent metastasis. Translational applications are emerging, including Cer-based biomarkers with high diagnostic accuracy for early stroke and investigational aSMase inhibitors for atherosclerosis.ConclusionModulating sphingolipid metabolism presents a promising therapeutic strategy for precision medicine. However, challenges remain in deciphering tissue-specific regulatory networks and standardizing lipidomic methodologies.

Introduction

Sphingolipids consitute a diverse class of bioactive lipids with a complex metabolic network involving tightly regulated biosynthetic and catabolic pathways. These molecules play critical roles in numerous physiological and pathological processes [1]. Ceramide (Cer) biosynthesis and homeostasis are centrally regulated through three major interconnected pathways, as depicted in the schematic [2]. The ​de novo synthesis pathway​ initiates in the endoplasmic reticulum, where L-serine and palmitoyl-CoA are condensed by serine palmitoyltransferase (SPT) to form 3-ketodihydrosphingosine (KDS). This intermediate is subsequently reduced by 3-ketodihydrosphingosine reductase (KDR/KDSR) to dihydrosphingosine (DHS), which is then directly acylated by ceramide synthase (CerS) to generate Cer [3, 4]. CerS1–6 subsequently conjugate the dihydrosphingosine with acyl-CoAs of varying chain lengths (C14–C26) to produce dihydroceramide (DHCer), which is finally desaturated to Cer by dihydroceramide desaturase (DES). Tthe CerS family exhibit strict acyl chain length specificity: CerS5/6 preferentially generate C16–C18 Cer, CerS2 produces long-chain Cer(C22–C24), and CerS1 specifically synthesizes C18 Cer [1, 5, 6]. This specificity underlies the tissue-specific distribution of Cer species. For instance, CerS6 expression enriches Cer16:0 in skeletal muscle, whereas CerS2 dominance in the liver favors C24:0 Cer production [6, 7]. The ​SMase-mediated hydrolysis pathway​ provides a rapid mechanism for Cer generation, whereby sphingomyelin (SM) is hydrolyzed by sphingomyelinase (SMase) at the plasma membrane to release Cer [8, 9]. Based on their optimal pH, SMases are categorized by their pH optima into acid SMase (aSMase), membrane-associated neutral SMases (nSMase1-3), and alkaline SMase (alk-SMase). aSMase degrades endocytosed SM in lysosomes, and its deficiency leads to Niemann-Pick disease (NPD) [10, 11]. nSMase2, localized at the plasma membrane, is activated by stimuli such as TNFα and modulates inflammation and apoptosis via Cer generation [12, 13]. Notably, aSMase is implicated in acute injuries like myocardial ischemia, whereas nSMase2 contributes to chronic conditions such as atherosclerosis [14, 15]. In parallel, the ​salvage pathway​ reclaims sphingoid bases: complex sphingolipids such as glycosphingolipids (GSLs) and glucosylceramide (GlcCer) are degraded to sphingosine, which can be phosphorylated by sphingosine kinase (SphK) to sphingosine-1-phosphate (S1P). S1P may then be either irreversibly cleaved by S1P lyase (SPL/SGPL1) to hexadecenal and phosphoethanolamine or dephosphorylated by S1P phosphatase (SPP) to regenerate sphingosine, which CerS reacylates to reform Cer, thus completing the recycling loop. Furthermore, Cer can be glycosylated by GlcCer synthase (GCS) to form GlcCer, phosphorylated by ceramide kinase (CERK) to ceramide-1-phosphate (C1P)—which may be dephosphorylated by C1P phosphatase (C1PP)—or utilized in the synthesis of SM and more complex sphingolipids [4, 16]. These pathways are tightly integrated, underscoring the dynamic regulation of Cer levels and its pivotal role in cellular signaling and metabolic fate [17] (Fig. 1). Beyond their metabolic interconversions, sphingolipids, particularly the Cer/SM balance, exert profound effects on cell fate and tissue homeostasis by modulating key signaling pathways. Cer acts as a central signaling molecule that can activate protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A), leading to the dephosphorylation and inhibition of pro-survival kinases like Akt [18]. It also promotes the assembly and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome, driving the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β [19]. Furthermore, Cer accumulation at mitochondrial membranes facilitates permeabilization, triggering the intrinsic apoptotic pathway [20]. The hydrolysis of SM to Cer within plasma membrane lipid rafts can disrupt raft integrity, affecting the signaling of various receptors [17, 21]. Understanding these core mechanisms provides a framework for interpreting the disease-specific pathologies discussed herein.

It is noteworthy that SM, a major metabolic derivative of Cer, represents the most abundant sphingolipid in mammalian cell membranes, accounting for 10–20% of membrane lipids. Its synthesis is catalyzed by members of the sphingomyelin synthase (SMS) family [22, 23]. SMS1 localizes primarily to the Golgi apparatus, while SMS2 is found at the plasma membrane; both isoforms produce SM by transferring a phosphocholine group from phosphatidylcholine to Cer [24, 25]. In cotrast, the SMS-related protein (SMSr) does not exhibit synthase activity but instead functions as a phosphatidylethanolamine phospholipase C activity [23, 24, 26]. The dynamic equilibrium between SM synthesis and degradation is essential for preserving membrane integrity and function. For instance, SMS2 knockout mice display a 50% reduction in plasma membrane SM content, which correlates with decreased stability of the LDL receptor and attenuated development of atherosclerosis [27]. Similarly, SMS1 deficiency results in depletion of SM in the Golgi apparatus and leads to impaired protein secretion [25]. These findings underscore the isoform-specific roles of SMS enzymes in maintaining membrane lipid architecture and cellular homeostasis.

Furthermore, sphingolipids serve not only as structural elements of cell membranes but also as critical signaling hubs that regulate a wide range of physiological and pathological processes. In metabolic regulation, Cer promotes insulin resistance by inhibiting the insulin signaling pathway, whereas SM enhances insulin receptor activity by stabilizing lipid raft structure [17, 28, 29]. For example, elevated Cer(d18:1/16:0)​ levels in the skeletal muscle of type 2 diabetic patients suppress AKT phosphorylation and impair glucose uptake [7]. Within immune and inflammatory responses, Cer and SM display dual roles: Cer can activate NADPH oxidase and the NLRP3 inflammasome to stimulate pro-inflammatory cytokines production, whereas sphingosine-1-phosphate(S1P) exerts anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing NF-κB signaling via S1PR1 [30, 31]. In cell fate determination, the Cer/SM ratio critically influences apoptosis, autophagy, and senescence. Mitochondria Cer accumulation induces mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP), thereby triggering apoptosis [6], whereas SM inhibits autophagy through mTORC1 activation [32]. The biological functions of sphingolipids are further modulated by acyl chain specificit. SM, as a key lipid raft constituent, helps modulate membrane fluidity and facilitates receptor signaling, whereas Cer generation often disrupts lipid raft stability, leading to receptor internalization [17, 21]. These mechanisms exhibit tissue-specific manifestations in disease context: Cer(d18:1/16:0)​ has been identified as a biomarker in heart failure, while ​Cer(d18:1/24:1)​ is associated with the progression of neurodegenerative disorders [33, 34].

It is important to emphasize that sphingolipid metabolism is subject to multifaceted regulation by nutritional status, hormonal signals, and oxidative stress. For instance, insulin suppresses SPT activity through the AKT/mTORC1 pathway, whereas inflammatory cytokines such as TNFα activate nSMase2 to promote Cer generation [6, 29]. Under pathological conditions, disruptions in sphingolipid metabolism leads to an altered Cer/SM ratio, which in turn impairs cellular functions and accelerates disease progression [1, 32]. Owing to its complexity and pleiotropic effects, sphingolipid metabolism has garnered significant interest as a therapeutic target. This is exemplified by the development of pharmacological agents such as aSMase inhibitors (e.g., amitriptyline) for atherosclerosis and S1PR modulators (e.g., fingolimod) for multiple sclerosis [12, 35]. Future research should further focus on elucidating the tissue-specific and microenvironment-dependent regulation of sphingolipid metabolism to facilitate the development of more precise and effective intervention [1, 36].

In summary, dysregulation of sphingolipid metabolism is closely implicated in the pathogenesis and progression of a wide range of major diseases. A deeper understanding of the aberrant changes in SM and Cer offers considerable theoretical and clinical promise. As key structural and signaling lipids, alterations in SM and Cer metabolism often occur prior to the onset of clinical symptoms. For instance, elevated Cer(d18:1/16:0)​ levels in the skeletal muscle can be detected in individuals with type 2 diabetes before hyperglycemia becomes apparent, highlighting its potential as an early biomarker [7, 28] Notably, different diseases exhibit distinct and tissue-specific perturbations in SM/Cer profiles: prostate cancer is associated with accumulation of long-chain Cer, which correlates with tumor aggressiveness, while cardiovascular diseases show increased aSMase activity leading to Cer-induced mitochondrial dysfunction [14, 37]. These findings not only underscore the central role of sphingolipid imbalance in disease mechanisms but also reveal promising molecular targets for diagnostic and therapeutic innovation. Substantial progress has already been made in developing drugs that modulate sphingolipid pathways, such as the aSMase inhibitor amitriptyline and the S1PR modulator fingolimod for multiple sclerosis, respectively [35, 38]. As precision medicine advances, further elucidation of SM/Cer metabolic dysregulation is expected to open new avenues for disease prevention and mangement, positioning sphingolipid biology as an increasingly critical component of future medical practice (Fig. 2) (Tables 1 and 2).

Diseases

Cardiovascular and cerebrovascular diseases

ACVD

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ACVD) is a chronic, multifactorial condition characterized by lipid metabolism disorders, chronic inflammation, vulnerable plaques, and a tendency to form vascular thrombi, all of which are risk factors for the disease [39]. Recent advances in lipidomics have shown that specific lipids, including oxidized lipids, SM with long saturated fatty acyl chains, glycerophosphocholines (PCs) with saturated and monounsaturated fatty acyl chains, Cer, and hexosyl ceramides (HexCer, a subclass of glucosyl ceramides), are associated with the clinical classification and characteristics of coronary artery disease [40–44]. Furthermore, numerous studies have demonstrated a strong link between the gut microbiota and the development of atherosclerosis. LPS, known as an integrin of Gram-negative bacteria, is known to regulate the progression of atherosclerosis [40]. High-fat diets (HFD) are well-documented to induce gut microbiota dysbiosis, characterized by increased colonization of dermatophytes, facultative anaerobes, and opportunistic pathogens [45]. Additionally, short-chain fatty acids (SCFA), as bacterial metabolic products, can combat atherosclerosis. According to the report, SCFA can significantly inhibit the induction of oxidized low-density lipoprotein (Ox-LDL) by modulating the NOD-, LRR, and NLRP3 caspase-1 signaling pathway. These findings position exogenous SCFA supplementation as a promising therapeutic strategy for atherosclerosis, operating through dual modulation of immunometabolic pathways [19]. Furthermore, SCFA upregulates PPARγ and PPARα, which are involved in the differentiation of adipocytes and energy consumption. Vulnerable plaques, a type of late-stage atherosclerotic plaque, are prone to rupture, leading to acute coronary syndrome (ACS) or sudden cardiac death [46, 47]. The phenotypic features of vulnerable plaques include thin-cap fibrous atherosclerotic lesions (TCFA), lipophilic plaques with necrotic cores, macrophages, microvessels, and cholesterol crystals [48, 49]. The morphological and pathological features of vulnerable atherosclerotic plaques typically include increased necrosis, inflammation, matrix degradation, increased cell apoptosis, and a reduced fibrous cap [50]. Changes in lipid metabolism can lead to a thinner fibrous cap and plaque instability [51]. Increasing evidence suggests that plaque stability may be influenced by the complex interactions between HexCer, PPAR signaling, SCFA metabolism, and fat tissue dysfunction [52, 53].

A clinical study utilized optical coherence tomography (OCT) to categorize pathological types among 245 coronary plaques obtained from 99 patients, while simultaneously employing targeted lipidomics to quantify plasma sphingolipid levels. The study found that patients with TCFA had significantly higher levels of long-chain SM(d18:1/16:1) compared to non-TCFA patients (P < 0.001). Moreover, these levels were positively correlated with thinner fiber caps (FCT) and cholesterol crystallization (CC) (OR = 0.42, P = 0.003) [54]. Further research revealed that SM enhances lipoprotein oxidation sensitivity (OR = 1.82, P = 0.001), promoting cholesterol crystallization and mechanical stress in the fiber cap. Animal models confirmed these findings, showing that CerS2 dysfunction leads to reduced synthesis of ultra-long-chain Cer (e.g., Cer(d18:1/22:0)) and increased levels of pro-inflammatory long-chain Cer (e.g., Cer(d18:1/16:0)), which exacerbate plaque instability [30, 54, 55]. Furthermore, in the ApoE−/− mouse model simulating the entire process of human plaque rupture, the accumulation of Cer leads to an increase in necrotic core area and plaque instability [52]. Mechanistically, Cer activates the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, exacerbating vascular wall inflammation [19]. High levels of Cer promote foam cell formation [56] and increase the necrotic core within plaques, ultimately leading to a thinner fibrous cap and abnormal positive remodeling of the vascular wall, causing vulnerable plaque rupture and resulting in atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [57]. Notably, high levels of HexCer (monosaccharide Cer) interfere with the transcriptional activity of PPARγ, inhibiting the normal differentiation of adipocytes and promoting adipose tissue inflammation, indirectly driving the progression of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease [58]. Recent studies indicate that a HFD alters the gut microbiota, reducing the production of SCFA-producing bacteria, leading to SCFA deficiency and weakening its inhibitory effect on Cer synthesis, thus forming a vicious cycle [59, 60]. The inflammatory microenvironment inhibits SMase activity, hindering the conversion of SM to Cer thus leading to pathological SM accumulation. Elevated SM levels can enhance inflammatory signals, amplifying the inflammatory response to pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) such as LPS [61]. In summary, SM and Cer offer new potential therapeutic targets for treating atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (Fig. 3).

AIS

Acute ischemic stroke (AIS) represents a prevalent condition characterized by high incidence, disability, and recurrence rates [62]. The pathogenesis of AIS progresses through three distinct phases: During the acute phase, excitotoxicity (excessive glutamate release leading to calcium influx), oxidative damage from reactive oxygen species (ROS) burst, and blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption collectively contribute to rapid neuronal death in the ischemic core [63, 64]. The subacute phase is primarily driven by neuroinflammation, manifested by microglial activation, release of pro-inflammatory cytokines, and immune cell infiltration, which exacerbate secondary brain injury [65]. In the reparative phase, angiogenesis and neurogenesis are impaired by metabolic disturbances, particularly sphingolipid dysregulation. Cer accumulation promotes neuronal apoptosis through activation of mitochondrial apoptotic pathways, including increased membrane permeability and caspase cascade initiation [66–68]. Mesenchymal stem cells (MSCs) have emerged as promising therapeutic candidates due to their low immunogenicity, multipotent differentiation capacity, and ease of isolation and expansion [69, 70]. Emerging evidence suggests that the therapeutic benefits of MSCs may be partly mediated through the modulation of sphingolipid metabolism. For instance, MSCs have been shown to attenuate the accumulation of pro-apoptotic ceramides in the ischemic brain and may suppress nSMase activity, thereby reducing sphingomyelin hydrolysis and Cer generation, which contributes to their neuroprotective and anti-inflammatory effects [71]. Preclinical studies have substantiated he therapeutic potential of these interventions in stroke models, including infarct size reduction and functional recovery [72, 73], mediated through multiple mechanisms such as neuroprotection, immunomodulation, angiogenesis, and neural circuit reconstruction [74–79].

Clinical investigations employing UPLC-MS/MS-based serum sphingolipid metabolomics in 112 subjects (56 AIS patients versus 56 healthy controls) revealed significant upregulation of 31 sphingolipid metabolites (VIP > 1, FC ≥ 1.5) in stroke patients. LASSO regression and SVM analyses identified six core biomarkers: CerP (d18:1/20:3), CerP (d18:1/18:1), CerP (d18:1/18:0), CerP (d18:1/16:0), SM (d18:1/d26:1), and Cer (d18:0/d20:0) [80]. These biomarkers exhibited superior diagnostic performance in cohort validation, with individual AUCs ranging from 0.717 to 0.827 and a combined model AUC of 0.908 (sensitivity 86.7%, specificity 84.2%), significantly outperforming conventional biomarkers such as phenylacetylglutamine (AUC = 0.69) [78, 81]. These findings establish serum sphingolipid dysregulation as a high-precision noninvasive diagnostic tool for AIS, while suggesting its potential involvement in disease pathogenesis through apoptotic mechanisms (Fig. 3).

STEMI

The core pathogenesis of ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) involves the rupture or erosion of unstable atherosclerotic plaques within the coronary arteries, which exposes plaque contents (such as OX-LDL and Cer) and triggers platelet activation and a coagulation cascade reaction, leading to complete occlusion of the coronary artery by thrombus [82, 83]. The interruption of blood flow results in sustained myocardial ischemia and hypoxia; if reperfusion is not promptly achieved, myocardial cells will undergo irreversible necrosis [84, 85]. During this process, high concentrations of saturated fatty acids (SAFA) activate TLR4/NF-κB pathway, inducing endothelial cells to express inflammatory factors such as IL-6 and IL-1β [86, 87]. Additionally, cholesterol crystals and FA activate NLRP3, promoting the release of IL-1β, which exacerbates local inflammation and increases endothelial permeability, accelerating plaque rupture [88–90]. The therapy aimed at lowering LDL-C has significantly altered the natural course of CVD Lipid-lowering therapies [82], such as statins combined with intestinal cholesterol absorption inhibitors (simvastatin + ezetimibe), can partially reverse many inflammatory biomarkers [88–90]. A lipidomics study following rosuvastatin treatment showed a significant decrease in SM, triglycerides (TG), phosphatidylinositol (PI), and phosphatidylethanolamine (PE) [91]. Although using weaker statins combined with ezetimibe can effectively target LDL-C and non-HDL-C, their effects on other lipids are less well documented.

Through a prospective randomized open-label study, 20 patients with STEMI were randomly assigned to either the rosuvastatin 20 mg monotherapy group (n = 10) or the simvastatin 40 mg + ezetimibe 10 mg combination therapy group (n = 10). After 30 days of treatment, plasma lipid profiles were analyzed using a high-precision lipidomics platform. The results showed that Cer levels in the rosuvastatin group decreased by 37% (q = 0.02), while there was no change in the combination therapy group (q > 0.05). SM levels decreased in both groups, but the reduction was more significant in the rosuvastatin group (-36% vs-27%, q < 0.001) [92]. Although the improvements in classic lipid profiles were similar, lipidomics revealed that rosuvastatin uniquely reduced pro-atherogenic lipids (Cer/SM) [92, 93]. Cer, as an independent predictor of cardiovascular events, its selective reduction may explain the potential advantage of rosuvastatin in residual risk control. This study is the first to systematically confirm the deep metabolic differences between two potent lipid-lowering strategies through lipidomics, providing a molecular basis for optimizing lipid-lowering therapy after myocardial infarction (Fig. 3).

HF

Heart failure (HF) is a multifaceted clinical syndrome characterized by inadequate cardiac output and/or elevated intracardiac pressure, resulting from impaired ventricular pumping function, reduced filling capacity, or structural and functional abnormalities of the heart [94]. HF is classified into three categories based on left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF): high ejection fraction heart failure (HFpEF; LVEF ≥ 50%), mild ejection fraction reduction heart failure (HFmrEF; LVEF 41–49%), and low ejection fraction heart failure (HFrEF; LVEF ≤ 40%) [94, 95].

Clinically, patients with HF exhibit significantly elevated total Cer levels in plasma, particularly Cer(d18:1/16:0) and Cer(d18:1/18:0). Population studies indicate a non-linear relationship between S1P and outcomes. A study on sphingolipid metabolism-related genes revealed that plasma S1P levels in HF patients follow a U-shaped mortality curve, where both S1P < 0.8 µM and > 2.2 µM significantly increase mortality risk by HR = 1.8–2.3 [96]. For the HFpEF subtype, plasma Cer(d18:1/16:0) concentration nearly doubles compared to healthy controls [33], with a Cer(d18:1/16:0)/Cer(d18:1/24:1) ratio exceeding 0.25 correlating with a 50% increased mortality risk [97]. Furthermore, population-wide analyses demonstrate positive correlation between elevated plasma SM levels and sudden cardiac death risk (HR = 1.32 during 10-year follow-up) [98]. However, a study on HF etiology reported that serum SM levels in HFpEF patients decrease by approximately 30% compared to healthy individuals, potentially serving as a key biomarker for HF identification [99]. In animal models, the dyslipidemia of HF was further confirmed. In the HFrEF mouse model after myocardial infarction, Cer clearly increased in myocardial tissue, accompanied by SM decreased [100]. In addition, SPT overexpression further accumulated Cer, further increasing mitochondrial respiratory function and myocardial apoptosis [101]. In the HFpEF mouse model induced by high-fat diet, Cer accumulation in myocardial tissue [102] caused deterioration of myocardial contractile function [103]. In the HFpEF mouse model induced by diabetes, dhCer and GlcCer were significantly increased, which inhibited autophagy and aggravated insulin resistance [104, 105]. In terms of molecular mechanisms, the core disorder in HF’s sphingolipid metabolism manifests as a vicious cycle driven by Cer-mediated mitochondrial apoptotic pathways and SM hydrolysis (as described in the Introduction) [20, 106]. Inflammatory factors like TNF-α activate aSMase, which hydrolyzes SM to regenerate Cer [12]. Additionally, SM deficiency disrupts lipid raft structures, causing β1-adrenergic receptor signaling dysfunction [107]. Notably, Cer inhibits Akt/PKB phosphorylation, blocking GLUT4 translocation and reducing myocardial glucose uptake capacity [108]. Concurrently, it activates PP2A and suppresses the PPARα/PGC-1α pathway, decreasing fatty acid β-oxidation efficiency [109]. These dual pathways collectively contribute to energy metabolism collapse (Fig. 3).

VaD

Dementia is a progressive neurological condition characterized by gradual deterioration of cognitive functions, with an estimated global prevalence affecting 47.5 million individuals worldwide [110]. Vascular dementia (VaD) is the second most common cause of dementia [111], and it is characterized by cerebrovascular diseases such as infarction, microbleeds, and cerebral amyloid angiopathy [112, 113]. The pathogenesis of the VaD is mainly caused by vascular risk factors leading to cerebral vascular endothelial injury, atherosclerosis and small vessel lesions [114]. Ischemia and hypoxia in brain tissue directly result in neuronal death and synapse loss, while also activating microglia to release pro-inflammatory factors (such as IL-1β and TNF-α), leading to neuroinflammation and oxidative stress, which exacerbate neural damage. Sphingolipid metabolism disorders, a key biomarker, play a crucial role in this process, reflecting the secondary impact of vascular dysfunction on lipid metabolism [115, 116]. These pathological interactions result in the destruction of cortical-subcortical neural circuits, causing patients to experience progressive decline in executive function, attention, and other cognitive domains.

A clinical study (N = 526) employing the ‌NINDS-AIREN diagnostic criteria‌ for VaD conducted comprehensive serum sphingolipid profiling via ‌LC-MS/MS, indicating that the d16:1 backbone of sphingolipids (including Cer and SM) was generally reduced in plasma [117, 118], while d18:1 sphingolipids showed a slight increase or no change, with the most significant reduction observed in Cer(d16:1/24:0) [114]. Mechanistically, the reduction in d16:1 sphingolipids is closely linked to cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension and coronary heart disease, possibly due to vascular damage. Further, after adjusting for covariates such as hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia and cardiovascular disease, the association between all sphingolipids and VaD disappeared [119, 120], suggesting that the sphingolipid disorder of VaD is a metabolic manifestation of cardiovascular disease, and its combination can be used as a highly specific biomarker (AUC > 0.94), providing a new basis for the molecular classification of VaD [114] (Fig. 3).

Neurological disorders

AD

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is a complex and multifactorial disorder, with its pathogenesis still not fully understood [121]. The prevailing etiological theory suggests that the accumulation of amyloid protein β-peptide (Aβ) and phosphorylated tau (p-Tau) proteins in the brain leads to neuronal loss through pathological changes, resulting in memory impairment and cognitive decline [122]. Clinically, the AD spectrum progresses through three distinct stages: preclinical phase, mild cognitive impairment (MCI), and advanced dementia [123].

At the clinical level, recent study has demonstrated that Cer levels (d18:1/16:0) in plasma are significantly elevated by 1.5-fold compared to age matched cognitively normal individuals (P < 0.001) [114]. Additionally, a cerebrospinal fluid lipidomics analysis of 209 subjects (91 AD patients, 92 MCI patients, and 26 controls) revealed that increased Cer levels (d18:1/20:3) raised the risk of Aβ42 positivity by 3.3-fold (OR = 0.302, p = 0.007), indicating that Cer metabolic disorders directly drive amyloid plaque formation [124]. Notably, this study also showed that elevated SM levels (d18:1/12:0) increased pathological tau protein risk by 5.3-fold (OR = 5.349, p = 0.01), suggesting that SM imbalance exacerbates neurofibrillary tangles [124]. In animal models, we further investigated the dysregulation of SM and Cer in AD. The ApoE gene is widely recognized as the most significant genetic risk factor for AD, affecting over half of cases [125]. In a female 5xFAD transgenic mouse model, mice with the APOE4 genotype exhibited significantly elevated plasma SM levels and markedly reduced Cer levels [126]. This SM/Cer imbalance emerged during early disease stages (2–6 months) and remained unaffected by AD mutations, indicating that APOE4 itself drives systemic sphingolipid metabolic disorders [126]. APOE4 may inhibit SMase activity, leading to SM accumulation and reduced Cer production, thereby disrupting the balance of the Cer/SM metabolic axis [126]. From a molecular mechanism perspective, Alterations in the Cer/SM metabolic axis disrupt lipid raft integrity (see Introduction), thereby affecting the localization and processing of amyloid precursor protein (APP) and accelerating Aβ production [127, 128]. Furthermore, Aβ activates nSMase to catalyze SM hydrolysis into Cer, leading to Cer accumulation [71]. Cer can be converted into Sph, elevating Sph levels [129] while maintaining S1P levels unchanged, indicating an imbalance between survival-promoting (S1P) and apoptosis-promoting (Cer/Sph) pathways in AD. Simultaneously, Cer activates the NF-κB/IL-1β pathway, amplifying neuroinflammation [130]. Notably, depletion of SM reduces membrane orderliness, impairing tau protein-microtubule binding and exacerbating its abnormal phosphorylation [131]. Conversely, Cer induces mitochondrial ROS release, activating tau kinases (e.g., GSK-3β) and promoting p-Tau accumulation [132] (Fig. 4).

PD

Parkinson’s disease (PD), a chronic progressive neurodegenerative disorder. Symptoms develop gradually over several years and primarily include cognitive impairment, behavioral changes, muscle rigidity, postural instability, and resting tremors [133]. These manifestations stem from the degeneration of dopaminergic neurons in the brainstem. Current research indicates that Lewy bodies and neurotubules—structures composed of α-synuclein (α-syn) aggregates in neuronal cytoplasm—also appear in affected brain regions [134]. A study by Leftin, et al. using model membranes revealed that α-synuclein aggregates preferentially accumulate in SM-enriched regions, leading to membrane structural disruption [135]. Such alterations are likely to impair membrane fusion processes and may thereby ignificantly affect neurotransmitter exocytosis [135].

In clinical studies, lipid profile analysis of 52 PD patients (26 with normal cognition and 26 with cognitive impairment) and 5 healthy controls revealed widespread sphingolipid metabolic disorders in PD patients. Compared to controls, PD patients exhibited significantly elevated levels of various chain-length molecules including Cer [e.g., Cer(d18:1/16:0), Cer d18:1/24:1)], monohexosaccharide Cer (containing glucose Cer), and lactose Cer (P < 0.05) [136]. Cognitive impairment in PD patients further showed increased specific lipid levels, particularly Cer(d18:1/16:0), Cer(d18:1/18:0), Cer(d18:1/20:0), Cer(d18:1/22:0), Cer(d18:1/24:1), and monohexosaccharide Cers [Cer(d18:1/16:0), Cer(d18:1/20:0), Cer (d18:1/24:0)] (P < 0.05). Notably, each unit increase in Cer(d18:1/16:0) was associated with a 7.8-fold higher risk of cognitive impairment (OR = 7.81, P = 0.084) [136]. This phenomenon exists independently of lipid changes such as cholesterol and is closely associated with the pathological mechanism of α-syn deposition. The accumulation of glucose Cer may inhibit lysosomal function (e.g., decreased GCase activity), impeding the degradation of α-syn and forming a pathological cycle that accelerates neurodegeneration [137, 138]. Emerging evidence revealed that SM levels in PD patients may vary across brain regions. Specifically, SM levels are significantly reduced in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) affected by PD, while SM levels show no significant changes in the occipital cortex (OCC) without signs of neurodegenerative lesions [139]. From a molecular perspective, in PD, the nuclear translocation of NF-κB in dopaminergic neurons triggers inflammatory responses [140], leading to increased ROS production. This activates n-SMase, causing Cer accumulation and activating apoptosis-related kinases. Through the JNK/p38 MAPK pathway, caspase is activated, further inducing dopaminergic neuronal death [141]. Additionally, α-syn inserts into lipid raft structures (rich in Cer/SM) via its N-terminal lipid-binding domain, inducing membrane disorder [135]. This directly interferes with Cer-degrading enzymes, resulting in regional Cer imbalance and impaired dopamine release. These dual mechanisms disrupt dopamine regulation, ultimately influencing the development and progression of PD (Fig. 4).

Gastrointestinal and hepatic diseases

IBD

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) imposes a significant global health burden, affecting millions of people [142]. The pathogenesis involves multifactorial interactions including genetic susceptibility, gut microbiota composition, environmental exposures, and immune dysregulation [143]. Clinically, IBD is classified into Crohn’s disease (CD) and ulcerative colitis (UC) [144]. A recent clinical study involving 75 IBD patients (18 with CD and 57 with UC) revealed that SM and Cer may serve as promising therapeutic targets for both diagnosis and treatment of IBD.

In terms of the research significance of IBD subtype identification, SM and Cer in UC patients are generally lower than CD, especially SM (d18:1/16:0) and Cer (18:1; 2O/24:1), which may be caused by the impairment of intestinal mucosal barrier function due to the decrease of SM and Cer, which is consistent with the pathological characteristics of diffuse mucosal damage and clinical manifestations of extensive superficial ulcers in UC [145]. In patients with IBD, disease activity is associated with distinct alterations in sphingolipid metabolism. Levels of active SM was significantly decreased, compromising the integrity of the intestinal barrier and increasing risk of bacterial translocation. Concurrently, elevated levels of Cer activate the NF-κB pathway, leading to enhanced production of pro-inflammator cytokine such as IL-6 and TNF-α [145]. Interestingly, fecal calnexin, an inflammatory biomarker, has emerged as a therapeutic and diagnostic target for IBD [146, 147]. Mechanistically, the dysregulation of non-apoptotic epithelial cell death, oxidative stress, and homeostatic pathways leads to excessive calnexin release, thereby exacerbating inflammation [148]. Recent studies have revealed that elevated fecal calnexin levels correlate with increased SM, which may promote the generation of pro-inflammatory S1P and amplify Th17 immune responses. Furthermore, in patient models with elevated fecal calnexin, strong correlations between Cer and glutamate/creatinine suggest cellular stress, providing new insights for developing calnexin-targeted therapies for IBD [145] (Fig. 4).

NAFLD

At the molecular level, sphingolipid dysregulation intersects with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) pathogenesis through spatially and temporally distinct mechanisms. Cer-enriched lipid rafts facilitate pathological clustering of death receptors (e.g., TNF-α receptor) on hepatocyte membranes, promoting caspase-8-mediated necroptosis that underlies non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) -related hepatocellular loss [149]. Simultaneously, SM hydrolysis-derived Cer activates protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A) in hepatic stellate cells, triggering a transcriptional switch from quiescent vitamin A-storing phenotypes to collagen-secreting myofibroblasts—a hallmark of fibrotic remodeling [150]. This sphingolipid-centric paradigm not only explains NAFLD’s multisystemic manifestations but also reveals therapeutic vulnerabilities. Pharmacological strategies targeting sphingolipid flux, such as myriocin-mediated SPT inhibition or synthetic Cer that competitively block PP2A activation, have demonstrated efficacy in preclinical models, reducing steatosis scores by 40–60% and fibrosis markers by 30–50% [149, 150]. Emerging clinical evidence further supports the therapeutic potential of sphingolipid modulation, with phase II trials showing improved hepatic insulin sensitivity and attenuated inflammation following Cer synthase inhibition. These advances position sphingolipid metabolism as both a biomarker panel and druggable target axis for NAFLD management, bridging molecular pathogenesis to precision therapeutics.

Clinical investigations in NAFLD have elucidated the pivotal role of sphingolipid dysregulation, with phase II trials demonstrating that inhibition of CerS enhances hepatic insulin sensitivity and attenuates inflammation in patients, thereby supporting the therapeutic potential of targeting sphingolipid pathways. Preclinical studies robustly validate these findings: interventions such as myriocin-mediated serine palmitoyltransferase (SPT) inhibition to reduce sphingolipid synthesis, or synthetic Cer analogs competitively blocking protein phosphatase 2 A (PP2A) activation, significantly reduced steatosis scores (40%–60%) and fibrosis markers (30%–50%) in animal models [149, 150], confirming the centrality and druggability of sphingolipid metabolism. Emerging mechanistic evidence indicates that sphingolipids, particularly Cer, mediate fundamental pathological processes in hepatocytes, Cer-enriched lipid rafts facilitate pathological clustering of death receptors (e.g., TNF-α receptor), triggering caspase-8-mediated necroptosis and subsequent hepatocellular loss in NASH [149]; concurrently, in hepatic stellate cells (HSCs), Cer generated from SM hydrolysis activates PP2A, driving phenotypic transition from quiescent vitamin A-storing cells to collagen-secreting myofibroblasts—a pivotal step in fibrotic remodeling [150]. Thus, sphingolipid dysregulation constitutes a central node in NAFLD’s multisystemic manifestations, with its study not only delineating molecular mechanisms linking hepatocyte injury to fibrosis but also establishing it as a biomarker-defined and therapeutically actionable axis, bridging clinical phenotypes to precision therapeutics (Fig. 4).

Disorders of the endocrine and metabolic systems

Diabetes

The pathogenesis of diabetes involves a complex interplay between insulin resistance and β -cell dysfunction, driven by systemic metabolic disturbances. In insulin target tissues such as skeletal muscle, Cer accumulation inhibits insulin signaling, primarily through PP2A-mediated dephosphorylation of Akt (reduction of 80%, p < 0.001) [151–154], thereby blocking GLUT4 translocation [150]. In contrast, in the liver, diacylglycerol (DAG) accumulation activates protein kinase C0 (PKC0) to impair insulin receptor substrate1 (IRS-1) tyrosine phosphorylation [155–159]. Hepatic gluconeogenesis is exacerbated by FoxO1 activation through DAG-PKCε signaling, increasing phosphoenolpyruvate carboxykinase (PEPCK) expression 3.5-fold [160, 161]. Similarly, lipotoxicity from high - fat diets cause lipid ectopic deposition in myocardium and adipose tissue. Cer activates PP2A to suppress insulin receptor signaling [162], and elevated FFA release triggers chronic inflammation via the TLR4/NF-κB pathway [163, 164]. β-cell function decline is closely tied to lipotoxicity. Palmitic acid, a saturated fatty acid, acts by upregulating Cer synthase, particularly CerS6, leading to the production of Cer. This, in turn, activates mitochondrial apoptosis pathways [165]. Simultaneously, endoplasmic reticulum stress emerges, which interferes with proinsulin processing through the PERK/eIF2αpathway [166, 167]. Specifically, lipotoxicity drives a 2.8-fold increase in mitochondrial fission, accompanied by a 4.2-fold upsurge in CHOP expression [168–170]. Moreover, lipidomics analysis unveils the myocardial accumulation of Cer. This Cer species activates caspase-3, propelling cardiomyocyte apoptosis to increase by 2.3-fold [152, 171, 172]. In addition, the decrease in acylcarnitines by 52% reflects the impaired mitochondrial β - oxidation [171, 173]. Furthermore, chronic hyperglycemia promotes retinal VEGF expression and enhances the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, notably tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), which are upregulated approximately 3.1-fold and 2.7-fold, respectively, via the AGEs-RAGE-NF-κB signaling axis [158, 174–176]. Cer-mediated mitochondrial fragmentation worsens corneal nerve degeneration [177]. Furthermore, metabolic endotoxemia (LPS leakage) synergistically activates the NLRP3 inflammasome with palmitic acid [164], creating a vicious cycle of insulin resistance [163, 178].

Clinically, elevated serum ​Cer​ levels are strongly associated with insulin resistance and β-cell apoptosis in type 2 diabetes (T2DM) [29, 170], while reduced Cer​ pools in type 1 diabetes (T1DM) reflect β-cell lipotoxicity and dysfunction [179]. Diabetic complications further manifest in ocular tissues, where Cer​ overload in meibomian glands disrupts cell cycle progression (via CDK/cyclin downregulation) and impairs meibum rheology, exacerbating tear film instability and dry eye pathology [180–182]. ​Experimental studies in animal models​ corroborate these clinical observations: in metabolically sensitive C57BL/6 mice, accumulation of long-chain ​Cer ​analogs exacerbates hyperglycemia and insulin resistance [29, 183–186], whereas in insulin-resistant BALB/c mice, enrichment of ultra-long-chain Cer derivatives enhances insulin receptor clustering and improves glucose homeostasis, highlighting tissue-specific adaptive roles [187]. ​Mechanistically, sphingolipid imbalance—particularly disrupted SM-Cer homeostasis—drives diabetic pathophysiology through dual pathways. In T2DM, dyslipidemia-induced remodeling of the HDL lipidome, marked by SM depletion and ​Cer​ enrichment, destabilizes cellular membranes and amplifies insulin resistance via PP2A-mediated IRS-1 dephosphorylation [173, 188] and ER stress activation [187]. Chain length-specific effects of ​Cer​ are further delineated: long-chain species suppress Akt signaling and upregulate hepatic gluconeogenesis, while ultra-long-chain variants promote insulin receptor spatial reorganization [29, 183–187]. The dynamic interplay between Cer​ and S1P additionally regulates apoptotic balance, with palmitate-LPS crosstalk underscoring therapeutic opportunities in sphingolipid pathway modulation [174]. Collectively, these findings emphasize the context-dependent duality of Cer​ subspecies, advocating for stratified interventions that integrate sphingolipid profiling and genetic susceptibility in diabetes management [179, 187, 189, 190] (Fig. 5).

Infectious diseases

CHB

Chronic hepatitis B (CHB) is an inflammatory liver disease caused by persistent hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection, with its pathogenesis involving complex crosstalk mechanisms between the virus and the host immune system. HBV persists within infected hepatocytes primarily through covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) located in the nucleus, enabling continuous replication and production of viral antigens (e.g., HBsAg, HBeAg), which contribute to immune tolerance dysregulation [191, 192]. The host immune response, while attempting viral clearance, also mediates hepatocyte damage. CD8+ T cells recognize viral antigens presented on the surface of infected hepatocytes, subsequently inducing hepatocyte apoptosis and necrosis through the release of cytokines (such as TNF-α and IFN-γ) and direct cytolytic activity, leading to hepatic inflammation [193]. If inflammation persists for more than six months, it can progress to chronic hepatitis and subsequently advance to liver fibrosis, cirrhosis, and even HCC [194].

In CHB, hepatic necroinflammation is closely linked to dysregulated sphingolipid metabolism. Clinical studies utilizing liver biopsy histopathology with the Scheuer scoring system for necroinflammation grading (G), coupled with ultra-high performance liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (UHPLC-MS/MS) analysis of serum sphingolipids in 126 biopsy patients, revealed that individuals with significant inflammation(G ≥ 2) exhibited significantly elevated levels of long-chain sphingomyelin SM(d16:0/16:1) (5.61 ± 1.50 vs. 4.77µmol/L, P = 0.002) and reduced levels of very-long-chain Cer(d18:2/22:0) (0.24 ± 0.10 vs. 0.31µmol/L, P = 0.002) compared to those with milder inflammation(G < 2) [195]. This pattern suggests that hepatic inflammation suppresses the activity of SMase, impairing the conversion of SM to Cer and leading to SM accumulation [195]. Longitudinal analysis in these patients further demonstrated that baseline G ≥ 2 patients achieving anti-viral therapy response showed a significant decline in SM(d16:0/16:1) level after 5 years of treatment (Δ=-0.84 µmol/L, P < 0.05), confirming inflammation resolution coincides with restored SMase activity [195]. Notably, sub-group analysis indicated that Cer(d18:2/22:0) levels remained significantly lower in G ≥ 2 patients even when alanine aminotransferase (ALT) was normal (0.22 ± 0.10 vs. 0.32 µmol/L, P = 0.007), hinting at its potential role beyond conventional markers [195]. ​Animal model investigations​ substantiated the clinical findings, revealing that deficiency of CerS2, which synthesizes C22-C24 Cer species including Cer(d18:1/22:0), leads to a decrease in these protective Cer and a concomitant activation of pro-apoptotic C16 Cer [196]. ​Mechanistically, the reduction in Cer(d18:0/22:0) is closely tied to CerS2/CerS4 dysfunction induced by inflammation [195, 196]. Importantly, sphingolipids exert chain-length-specific effects: long-chain SM(d16:0/16:1) promotes hepatocyte injury by activating the mitochondrial apoptotic pathway (Bax/Bcl-2 imbalance; OR = 1.552, P = 0.004), whereas very-long-chain Cer(d18:2/22:0) exhibits anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting NF-κB signaling(OR = 0.003, P = 0.006) [195]. The diagnostic value of Cer(d18:2/22:0) as a potential inflammation biomarker independent of ALT was highlighted by its superior performance in predicting G ≥ 2 necroinflammation (ROC AUC = 0.621) compared to ALT(AUC = 0.549) [195]. Collectively, SM/Cer metabolic imbalance, driven by dysregulation of the SMase-CerS axis and mediated through chain-length-specific modulation of apoptosis and inflammation networks, critically drives CHB liver injury progression [195, 196] (Fig. 6).

Ctr

According to data from the WHO, 128.5 million cases of Chlamydia trachomatis (Ctr) infection were reported in 2020, making it a leading cause of globally transmitted diseases. In most cases, Ctr infections remain asymptomatic, which facilitates disease transmission and chronic progression [197]. Recent studies have identified metabolic disorders involving SM and Cer as playing significant roles in Ctr infection.

During Ctr infection, the bacterium recruits host Cer transporters (CERT) via its envelope protein IncD, forming membrane contact sites (MCSs) between the ER and inclusion bodies. This process directly hijacks Cer synthesized by the ER for transport into inclusion bodies [198, 199]. After infecting M2 macrophages, total Cer levels rise markedly, supplying essential lipids for Chlamydia membrane assembly. The appropriated Cer is incorporated into the pathogen’s membranes, promoting inclusion body stability and enhancing viral replication [200]. Additionally, Chlamydia recruits host sphingosamine synthase (SMA) to convert Cer into Sph [198], or synthesizes Sph internally [201] to complete membrane remodeling. Simultaneously, infection activates SphK1, which upregulates Sph phosphatase (SGPP1/2) and lysin (SGPL1), converting Sph back to sphingosamine [202]. This leads to Sph accumulation in M2 macrophages. Translocated Sph is transported into inclusion bodies and inserted into Chlamydia membranes, directly disrupting pathogen integrity and significantly reducing viral infectivity for antibacterial effects [200]. In summary, Ctr obtains lipid resources through hijacking Cer/SM metabolic pathways, while the host forms a defense barrier via Sph accumulation mediated by SGPP2. This reveals the dual role of Cer/SM metabolism disruption: supporting pathogen proliferation while potentially triggering host defense mechanisms (Fig. 6).

Autoimmune diseases

LN

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is an autoimmune disease in which the immune system attacks its own tissues, causing widespread inflammation and tissue damage in affected organs. Lupus nephritis (LN), seen in 50% of SLE patients, is a major cause of morbidity and mortality [203].

In LN, renal inflammation is closely associated with sphingolipid metabolism disorders. A clinical study involving 116 participants (21 healthy individuals, 52 SLE patients, and 43 LN patients) confirmed this relationship. The serum SM(d18:0/16:2) levels in LN patients were significantly elevated by 2.89-fold (P < 0.001), while Cer(d18:1/16:3) decreased by 43% (P < 0.001). These biomarkers demonstrated excellent diagnostic efficacy in distinguishing LN from SLE: SM(d18:0/16:2) showed good diagnostic efficacy (AUC = 0.798, sensitivity 80%, specificity 69%), while Cer(d18:1/16:3) achieved similar performance (AUC = 0.758, specificity 86%)—both surpassing traditional indicators like serum creatinine (AUC = 0.677) [204]. Additionally, Cer(d18:1/16:3) was positively correlated with estimated eGFR (r = 0.254, P = 0.020), suggesting a potential role in the regulation of renal function. Conversely, SM(d18:0/16:2) exhibited a positive correlation with urea levels (r = 0.230, P = 0.035), reflecting the severity of azotemia [204]. At the molecular level, SM accumulation and Cer depletion stem from SMase dysfunction, which impedes SM-to-Cer conversion [204]. This imbalance directly contributes to renal vascular dysfunction: Cer inhibits RhoA/Rho kinase-mediated vasodilation, with reduced Cer levels impairing this effect [205, 206]. Simultaneously, SM buildup increases S1P/SPC metabolites that activate G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs), promoting renal vasoconstriction [207, 208]. Furthermore, elevated ROS disrupt lipid metabolism homeostasis through lipid peroxidation, activating Cer-dependent apoptosis pathways (e.g., mitochondrial pathway) that exacerbate renal cell damage [209, 210]. These combined mechanisms ultimately lead to impaired renal perfusion and cellular death, driving the progression of LN [204] (Fig. 6).

MS

Multiple Sclerosis (MS), an immune-mediated neurodegenerative demyelinating chronic disorder with an unknown etiology, may involve genetic susceptibility and environmental factors [211, 212]. This neurological condition manifests diverse symptoms including severe motor impairment, paralysis, visual disturbances, and pelvic organ dysfunction [213, 214]. Recent studies highlight the potential value of lipid biomarkers in predicting disease progression and monitoring MS outcomes [215].

A recent study revealed that patients with MS exhibit significantly elevated aSMase activity in their plasma, leading to extensive hydrolysis of SM and subsequent SM depletion coupled with Cer accumulation [216]. As an intrinsic antioxidant in cell membranes, reduced SM levels render PUFAs more susceptible to ROS-mediated damage [217]. Meanwhile, Cer accumulation dramatically stimulates ROS release from glial cells, activating mitochondrial apoptosis pathway that induce oligodendrocyte and neuronal death [218–220]. Additionally, Cer positively regulates phospholipase A2 (PLA2) activity, promoting arachidonic acid (AA) release [221]. AA subsequently generates potent pro-inflammatory mediators like prostaglandins and leukotrienes through the epoxyxygenase and lipoxygenase pathways, exacerbating neuroinflammation [222]. Notably, increased ACER activity in MS patients’ plasma further hydrolyzes accumulated Cer into Sph [216, 223]. Sph can be phosphorylated by SphK to produce S1P or may directly contribute to oligodendrocyte apoptosis and demyelination. Notably, elevated levels of lipid peroxides (LPO) and F2-epidermal hyperkeratin (F2-ehK), specific markers of oxidative stress, in the plasma of MS patients not only cause direct damage to cell membranes and myelin sheaths [224, 225], but also activate aSMase, thereby establishing a self-reinforcing vicious cycle (Fig. 6).

Neoplasms

Breast cancer

Breast cancer is one of the the most common malignant tumors worldwide and remains a leading cause of cancer-related mortality among women [226, 227]. Alterations in sphingolipid metabolism have been increasingly implicated in breast cancer pathogenesis. Notably, human breast cancer tissues exhibit elevated levels of several Cer species compared to normal or benign tissues [228]. Specifically, levels of Cer(d18:1/16:0)​, Cer(d18:1/24:1), and ​Cer(d18:1/24:0)​ are significantly higher in malignant tissues than in benign or normal tissues, and ​Cer(d18:1/16:0)​ is associated with positive lymph node status [229]. Furthermore, Cer(d18:1/18:0)​ and ​Cer(d18:1/20:0)​ levels are significantly higher in estrogen receptor (ER)-positive tumor tissues compared to ER-negative tumors [229]. Consistent with these observation, the expression of CerS, including CerS2, CerS 4, and CerS6, is elevated in malignant breast tissues [230]. Studies have shown that overexpression of CerS2, which synthesizes very long-chain Cer, can inhibit the proliferation and migration of breast cancer cells [231, 232]. However, the Luminal B-specific splicing variant of CerS2 reduces very long-chain Cer levels, thereby enhancing cancer cell proliferation and migration thus leading to poorer patient survival prognosis [231].

In contrast, sustained overexpression of CerS4 promotes breast cancer progression and invasiveness by activating multiple oncogenic signaling pathways, including Akt/mTOR, NF-κB, and β-catenin, inducing epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) [233]. N-acylsphingosine amidohydrolase 1 (ASAH1), which converts Cer into sphingosine and free fatty acids, is overexpressed in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cells and regulated by the p53 and PI3K-AKT signaling pathways [234]. Notably, high ASAH1 expression is associated with both ER-positive breast cancer and better prognosis, which appears paradoxical [235]. This apparent contradiction may be explained by the high complexity and interconnectivity of sphingolipid metabolism. In addition to ASAH1, multiple enzymes, including various Cer synthases are involved in regulating cellular Cer levels, and the expression of these enzymes also differs significantly between ER-positive and ER-negative breast tumors [234, 235]. Although deficiency of neutral ceramidase (NcDase) can promote tumor growth by modulating lipid storage, lipolysis, and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) in tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs), and by inducing cytotoxic T cell exhaustion [236].

In breast cancer, disregulation of SM and Cer metabolism is closely linked to disease progression. Clinical studies have revealed that CERK levels are significantly elevated in tumor tissues compared to adjacent normal tissues [237], and CERK promotes tumor cell growth, survival, and breast cancer recurrence [238, 239]. In patients with metastatic breast cancer, elevated SGMS2 expression contributes to tumor progression by upregulating SM, which in turn enhances TGF-β1 expression and promotes EMT in breast cancer cells, and enhancing their migratory and invasive capabilities [237] In terms of treatment, increasing Cer levels in tumors can enhance their sensitivity to chemotherapy [228, 240]. After chemotherapy, pro-apoptotic Cer levels are significantly reduced in studied cohorts of patients, indicating that Cer downregulation is associated with the response to chemotherapy in breast cancer patients [241]. Mechanistically, chemotherapy suppresses Cer levels by increasing the mRNA and protein expression of UDP-glucose ceramide glucosyltransferase (UGCG) [241]. Therefore, stimulating Cer generation or reducing UGCG may contribute to breast cancer treatment [242, 243]. In triple-negative breast cancer treatment, ceramide nanoliposomes (CNLs) have emerged as potential chemotherapeutic agents with considerable clinical promise [244–246]. Notably, MLKL mRNA expression is higher in TNBC tumors than in non-TNBC subtypes, rendering TNBC cells more susceptible to CNL-induced necroptosis [247]. Tamoxifen, a cornerstone in the treatment of hormone receptor-positive (HR+) breast cancer, enhances chemosensitivity through multiple mechanisms by activating cell death markers, reducing Cer glycosylation, and inhibiting acid ceramidase [244, 248]. Combination regimens that modulate Cer levels alongside tamoxifen have been shown to significantly enhance antitumor effect [244, 249] (Fig. 7).

HCC

Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) constitutes a severe global health challenge and poses substantial clinical burdens, ranking as the fourth leading cause of cancer-related mortality worldwide [250]. Its incidence and mortality rates exhibit a steady upward trajectory [251]. And earlier predictive models indicated that the global incidence of primary liver cancer would surpass one million annual cases by 2025 [252]. Accounting for approximately 90% of primary liver cancer cases, HCC predominates. Exosomes, extracellular vesicles measuring 40–160 nm in diameter, originate from the endosomal system. Their biogenesis initiates with membrane invagination forming early sorting endosomes (ESEs), which mature into late sorting endosomes (LSEs). Intraluminal vesicles (ILVs) are then generated through inward budding of the endosomal membrane. Subsequently, multivesicular bodies (MVBs) fuse with the plasma membrane, releasing ILVs as exosomes [253–255]. Exosome generation relies on two primary mechanisms: the ESCRT (endosomal sorting complex required for transport)-dependent pathway and the ESCRT-independent pathway, the latter involving lipid metabolism regulation. Specifically, nSMase and exosome release [255, 256]. In HCC, exosome release is markedly enhanced, a phenomenon intricately linked to tumor microenvironmental cues such as hypoxia. Under hypoxic conditions, tumor cells exploit exosome-mediated intercellular communication to disseminate oncogenic signals (e.g., miRNAs, proteins), thereby promoting angiogenesis, immune evasion, and microenvironmental remodeling to support tumor progression and metastasis [257–259]. HCC cells enhance exosome secretion by upregulating sphingolipid metabolism pathways. Hypoxic stress activates nSMase, which converts SMs to Cers, promoting multivesicular body (MVB) formation and ESCRT-independent exosome release [256, 260–262]. This metabolic reprogramming not only escalates exosome quantity but also accelerates disease progression through the cargo content (e.g., pro-angiogenic factors).

Aberrant lipid metabolism, particularly reprogramming of sphingolipid metabolism, is a key feature intricately linked to hepatocarcinogenesis and the aggressive nature of HCC. Clinically, pseudo-targeted lipidomic analysis of serum exosomes from healthy individuals, HCC patients, and intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma(ICC) patients revealed significant elevations in ​Cer(d18:1/18:0)​​ and ​Cer(d18:1/16:0)​, alongside substantial reductions in 55 sphingolipids within HCC patient exosomes-including 40 SMs such as SM(d18:1/16:0) and SM(d18:1/24:0) [263]. Key sphingolipid ratios, notably SM/Cer (e.g., SM/Cer(d18:1/16:0)), were significantly lower in the HCC group [263], indicating augmented n-SMase activity and accelerated SM-to-Cer conversion [256, 264]. This metabolic imbalance directly correlated with elevated exosome release in cancer patients, signifying ESCRT-independent pathway activation in HCC. Experimentally, treatment of hepatocytes with the n-SMase inhibitor GW4869 resulted in increased SM/Cer ratios within both cells and derived exosomes, accompanied by a significant decrease in the exosomal marker protein CD81 expression [263]. This demonstrates that inhibiting SM hydrolysis suppresses exosome secretion. Conversely, activating nSMase using ATRA further decreased the SM/Cer ratio and significantly augmented exosome release [265, 266]. Correlation analysis confirmed that reduced exosomal SM/Cer(d18:1/24:0) strongly and negatively correlated with increased exosome secretion [256, 264, 267], validating that nSMase directly drives exosome biogenesis via sphingolipid metabolism regulation. These findings collectively confirm that upregulated nSMase activity in HCC enhances SM hydrolysis. The resultant Cer accumulation promotes ILV generation within MVBs through the formation of membrane microdomains, ultimately increasing exosome secretion [263]. Mechanistically, beyond mediating exosome release, this pathway also holds diagnostic and therapeutic significance. In HCC, alterations in SM include elevated SM(d18:1/18:2/24:1) levels, potentially linked to dietary factors or tumor-specific metabolic adaptations [268, 269]. The mTORC2 pathway contributes to enhanced tumorigenesis by promoting glucosylceramide accumulation [270]. Clinically observed upregulation of Cer16 (C16-ceramide, structurally related to ​Cer(d18:1/16:0)​) in serum and tumor tissues of HCC patients promotes C16-ceramide binding to p53, inhibiting its MDM2-mediated degradation and activating p53-dependent apoptosis; however, this pro-apoptotic effect is often counteracted by oncogenic signaling within tumors [269, 271, 272]. Additionally, Cer regulates the RAS-RAF-MAPK pathway to promote tumor cell proliferation49. These findings reveal how sphingolipid metabolism dysregulation (particularly SM-to-Cer conversion via nSMase) fuels both exosome overproduction and oncogenic signaling, positioning SM/Cer ratios as diagnostic biomarkers and nSMase inhibition as a promising HCC therapeutic target [256, 266] (Fig. 7).

Lung cancer

According to Global Cancer Statistics 2020, lung cancer accounts for 11.4% of the 19.3 million newly diagnosed cancer cases worldwide and remains the leading cause of cancer-related mortality [273]. Active smoking is responsible for approximately 90% of all lung cancer cases [274]. Recent novel findings have demonstrated that cigarette smoke not only aberrantly stimulates EGFR to induce hyperplasia but also activates nSMase2 to generate Cer and induce apoptosis, thereby resulting in tissue injury. Meanwhile, nSMase2 is critical for the generation of exosomes, which may act as vehicles for disseminating activated oncogenes (such as EGFR) and miRNAs; nSMase2/Cer may also directly affect aberrant EGFR/Src activation and their mutual interaction, leading to enhanced proliferation/tumorigenic signaling and ultimately tumorigenesis [275–278]. Dysregulation of specific CerS enzymes is associated with lung cancer progression. CerS1, which is responsible for the biosynthesis of C18-Cer, is significantly downregulated in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) cell lines and brain metastases, correlating with poor prognosis. Overexpression of CerS1 inhibited cell migration, invasion, and brain metastasis formation in vivo, suggesting a tumor-suppressive role [279, 280]. In contrast, CerS6 is overexpressed in NSCLC tissues and correlates with poor prognosis and lymph node metastasis. Elevated CerS6 expression promotes migration and metastasis through increasing C16-Cer levels and Rac family small GTPase 1 (RAC1)-positive lamellipodia formation, indicating that CerS6 acts as a metastasis promoter in NSCLC [280]. Unlike Cer, C1P and S1P support tumor progression by enhancing cell survival, proliferation, angiogenesis, and metastasis [280]. CERK regulates cell fate by modulating the balance between pro-apoptotic Cer and anti-apoptotic C1P [280–282].

At the clinical level, SM and Cer show promise as biomarkers for lung cancer diagnosis and disease assessment. For instance, higher plasma concentrations of total Cer are associated with an increased future risk of lung cancer, and elevated levels of Cer(d18:1/24:1) and SM (d18:1/16) have also been observed in the plasma of NSCLC patients [278, 283, 284]. In animal models, exogenous Cer effectively promotes apoptosis of lung cancer cells [285, 286]. Therapeutic strategies targeting the downstream pathways further demonstrate significant suppression of lung cancer progression. For example, inhibition of Cer synergizes with first-line cisplatin treatment in NSCLC by reducing cell survival and in vivo tumor growth through modulation of VDAC, while administration of a CDase inhibitor rescues the response of chemoresistant lung cancer cells to chemotherapy by reactivating pyroptosis both in vitro and in vivo [287, 288]. At the molecular mechanism level, however, further experimental studies and evidence-based data are still needed to identify specific SM and Cer species with optimal biomarkers potential for lung cancer [280] (Fig. 7).

PC

Prostate cancer (PC) is the second most common cancer and the fifth leading cause of cancer-related deaths among men worldwide [250]. A key metabolic feature of PC progression is the enhanced de novo fatty acid synthesis driven by the overexpression of fatty acid synthase (FASN), which leads to reduced intracellular levels of Cer and SM [289]. Specifically, the pathway for Cer synthesis via hydrolysis of SM is inhibited [290]. In this process, prostate cancer tissues exhibit reduced expression of nSMase1 and nSMase2, with lower expression levels correlating with older patients [290]. Moreover, pharmacological inhibition of nSMase2 in PC cells markedly remodels the extracellular vesicle secretome and strongly enhances cell migration [290]. Concurrently, the Cer-metabolizing enzyme acid CDase (AC) is up-regulated in tumor tissues [291]. Accumulating evidence indicates that these alterations result in a dysfunctional Cer pathway, which promotes tumor progression and confers resistance to chemotherapy and radiotherapy [291–293]. On the other hand, elevated levels of circulating Cer species are associated with poorer clinical outcomes throughout the natural history of PC [37, 294, 295]. From a metabolic perspective, the conversion of Cer to S1P requires Cer to be first converted to sphingosine, which is then phosphorylated by sphingosine kinase (SPHK) into S1P. Compared to normal tissues, various cancer types including PC show higher expression of SPHK1 [296]. Thus, elevated circulating Cer in patients with advanced disease may be taken up by tumor cells and converted into S1P, a pro-survival signaling molecule, thereby promoting resistance to androgen receptor signaling inhibitors (ARSI) and driving tumor progression [297].

At the clinical level, elevated circulating Cer species are associated with adverse outcomes throughout the natural history of PC. Specifically, lipid profiles characterized by increased Cer levels are significantly associated with metastatic relapse in localized PC (HR 5.80, 95% CI 3.04–11.1, P = 1 × 10⁻⁶), earlier testosterone suppression failure in metastatic hormone-sensitive PC (HR 3.70, 95% CI 1.37–10.0, P = 0.01), and shorter overall survival in metastatic castration-resistant PC (HR 2.54, 95% CI 1.73–3.72, P = 1 × 10⁻⁶) [37, 298]. Based on these findings, these clinically actionable lipid profiles may serve as potential therapeutic targets in prospective clinical trials to improve outcomes in PC. At the experimental level, this concept has been validated in animal models. For example, intradermal or intraperitoneal injection of a Cer analog in BALB/c-Nude or NOD-SCID mice bearing human prostate tumors resulted significant tumor regression [299]. At the molecular mechanism level, Cer acts as a lipid second messenger that mediates cell death in PC cells [300]. It has been demonstrated that treatment with exogenous Cer (C6 and C16) and Cer analogs delays tumor cell growth and induces apoptosis, supporting the strategy of elevating Cer levels as a novel chemotherapeutic approach [301–304]. Furthermore, Cer regulation can synergize with other interventions to optimize PC treatment efficacy. For instance, Cer modulates the anti-tumor mechanisms of erianin in both androgen-sensitive and castration-resistant PC and enhances the cytotoxicity of the viral protein apoptin in PC cells [305, 306] (Fig. 7).

Translational implications and therapeutic perspectives

The compelling evidence linking sphingolipid dysregulation to a wide spectrum of diseases underscores their significant translational potential, both as biomarkers and therapeutic targets (Table 3).

Sphingolipids as diagnostic and prognostic biomarkers

Sphingolipid profiles, particularly of Cer and SM species, have emerged as promising biomarkers due to their disease-specific alterations and detectability in accessible biofluids like plasma and serum. For instance, specific Cer species demonstrate high diagnostic accuracy for early stroke (AUC > 0.9) [80], while distinct SM/Cer ratios show promise in distinguishing lupus nephritis from systemic lupus erythematosus [204]. In oncology, exosomal sphingolipid signatures offer potential for non-invasive cancer detection and monitoring [263]. However, the translation of these findings into clinical practice faces challenges, including the need for standardized lipidomic methodologies across laboratories and the establishment of validated reference ranges. Future efforts should focus on large-scale, multi-center validation studies to confirm the clinical utility of these sphingolipid biomarkers.

Therapeutic targeting of sphingolipid metabolism

Pharmacological modulation of sphingolipid metabolism represents a burgeoning therapeutic strategy. Several approaches are under investigation:

Enzyme inhibitors

Inhibitors of key enzymes, such as acid sphingomyelinase (e.g., Amitriptyline) [307] and serine palmitoyltransferase (e.g., Myriocin) [149], aim to reduce the production of pathogenic Cer. While showing efficacy in preclinical models, challenges include achieving tissue specificity and managing potential on-target side effects due to the ubiquitous role of sphingolipids.

S1P receptor modulators

Drugs like Fingolimod, which targets S1P receptors, are already approved for multiple sclerosis [35, 216]. Their primary advantage is immunomodulation, but limitations include cardiovascular side effects (e.g., bradycardia) and increased infection risk.

Cer-mimetics and nanoliposomes

Strategies to elevate Cer levels directly, such as using Cer analogs or ceramide-loaded nanoliposomes (CNLs), can induce apoptosis in cancer cells and sensitize them to chemotherapy [244, 247]. A key advantage is the direct targeting of the pro-apoptotic molecule, but drug delivery and stability remain hurdles.

The future of sphingolipid-based therapeutics lies in developing isoform- and chain-length-specific modulators, optimizing delivery systems to target specific tissues, and combining these agents with existing treatments to enhance efficacy and overcome resistance.

Discussion

Although dysregulated sphingolipid metabolism, particularly involving SM and Cer, has been exensively implicated in disease pathogenesis, several fundamental challenges remain unresolved. First, while the tissue-specific nature of SM/Cer imbalance is well-documented [7, 309, 310], the dynamic spatiotemporal regulation of sphingolipid metabolism across different microenvironments remains poorly systematized. For instance, elevated SMase activity enhances insulin sensitivity in skeletal muscle [28], yet the same change in cardiac tissue aggravates dysfunction [14], highlighting a regulatory paradox that demands mechanistic clarification. Moreover, temporal variations in enzyme activation, such as the differential activation of aSMase in the acute phase and nSMase in the chronic phase following traumatic brain injury (TBI) [311, 312]—further complicate research efforts, necessitating the development of spatiotemporally resolved detection technologies to address this issue.

Second, although distinct functional roles have been proposed for specific Cer species (e.g., pro-inflammatory C16:0 and protective C24:1), the molecular mechanisms underlying these differences remain largely correlative [37, 313]. Most evidence stems from association studies, such as between plasma Cer(d18:1/16:0)​​ and HFpEF [33], while direct genetic or functional validation of Cer chain length-specific effects is scarce. Moreover, the subcellular compartmentalization of sphingolipid enzymes, such as Golgi-localized SMS1 and plasma membrane-bound SMS2 [26], suggests that Cer chain length may determine functional specificity, a hypothesis yet to be confirmed through targeted gene-editing models. Although sphingolipids hold promise as biomarkers [102, 314], their clinical translation is impeded by methodological inconsistencies in detction prorocols and the dual roles of many interventions. Discrepancies in detection methods and sample types across studies hinder cross-comparison of data [315–317], and while inhibition of aSMase or SMS may alleviate certain symptoms [27, 38], it could simultaneously disrupt other physiological processes [11, 18].

Finally, crosstalk between the SM-Cer metabolic axis and other lipid signaling pathways remains underexplored. While enhanced Cer-S1P conversion has been linked to drug-resistant PC [297], the distinct contributions of S1P receptor subtypes are unclear. Non-canonical pathways, such as deoxysphingolipid synthesis induced by serine deficiency [2], are also often overlooked. Moreover, translational relevance is limited by discrepancies between animal models and human diseases—such as consistent improvements in insulin sensitivity upon SM reduction in mice models [318] versus variable SM changes in skeletal human muscle SM [7, 28]. Overcoming these barriers will require interdisciplinary strategies, including the development of chain-length-specific molecular probes, standardized lipidomic protocols and tissue-targeted delivery system. Despite these challenges, targeting sphingolipid metabolism offers unique and promising opportunities for therapeutic interventions across a spectrum of diseases.
