Prognostic Significance of Blood-Based Multicancer Detection in Circulating Tumor DNA: Five-Year Outcomes Analysis
Authors: Charles Swanton, Alan Bryce, Allen L Cohn, Matthew Margolis, Earl Hubbell, April Sagan, Oliver Venn, Michael Seiden
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPURPOSEA multicancer early detection (MCED) test was developed and validated in the case-control Circulating Cell-free Genome Atlas (CCGA) study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02889978). Previous analysis of the second (cross-validation) CCGA substudy identified prognostic value of cancer signal detection by the MCED test with 3-year follow-up. Here, we evaluated the prognostic value of a cancer signal detected (CSD) result in the third (validation) CCGA substudy (CCGA3) using an updated statistical methodology with 5-year follow-up.METHODSCCGA3 participants with confirmed cancer were followed for up to 5 years; overall survival was stratified by MCED test result (CSD or no CSD [NCSD]). Observed survival was compared with the expected survival of a reference population calculated from Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results data matched to clinical characteristics (age, sex, cancer type, and stage) in each signal detection group.RESULTSOf 2,513 participants with stageable, invasive cancer, 792 (31.5%) died and 1,683 (67.0%) were confirmed alive at follow-up, with 38 (1.5%) lost to follow-up. CSD rates were higher in participants who died during follow-up compared with those alive (85% v 34%). Overall observed survival versus expected survival was similar for CSD (43% observed v 40% expected) and NCSD (88% observed v 81% expected) groups. For NCSD, hazard ratios (HRs) were more favorable (<1) relative to the matched reference population at all stages (P < .0001); for CSD, HRs were <1 at stages III to IV (P < .0001) and ≅1 at stages I to II (P > .4).CONCLUSIONCSD cancers had long-term survival similar to expectations, even in early stages, indicating that CSD early-stage cancers are unlikely to be more micrometastatic and lethal than cancers detected by conventional means. The MCED test was likely to find clinically significant cancers without contributing to overdiagnosis.

INTRODUCTION

Multi-cancer early detection (MCED) tests are an emerging screening technology that uses next-generation sequencing to detect cancer signals within blood analytes such as circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA), ie, fragments of genomic DNA shed into the bloodstream by tumor cells.1 MCED tests can screen for multiple cancer types simultaneously, including those without recommended screening and low-incidence cancer types for which single-cancer screening programs would be costly or impractical, allowing for the detection of cancers that would otherwise likely only present through symptoms at late stages.2-4 These attributes enable the diagnosis of cancers at earlier stages, when treatment is more effective, and thus contribute to the potential mortality benefit of population-level MCED screening programs.5,6

The case-control Circulating Cell-free Genome Atlas (CCGA) study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02889978) was designed to develop and assess the performance of an MCED test that detects cancer-associated methylation patterns in ctDNA.7,8 The MCED test uses targeted methylation sequencing of ctDNA from plasma and machine learning classifiers to identify a cancer signal shared across many different cancer types. The classifier was designed to have high (≥99.5%) specificity for screening purposes in a large asymptomatic population. The MCED test returns a result of cancer signal detected (CSD) or no cancer signal detected (NCSD). If a cancer signal is detected, the test also returns the likely cancer or tissue type where the tumor arose (ie, cancer signal origin).7-9

Assessment of ctDNA can provide useful information on the underlying biology and aggressiveness of the cancer.1,10 Lower levels of ctDNA have also been associated with better prognoses for several cancer types, including lung,11,12 liver,13,14 pancreatic,15 and colorectal.16 High preoperative blood concentrations of ctDNA have been linked with metastasis and poor survival in early-stage lung cancer, while a lack of ctDNA detection corresponded to a subgroup of indolent cancers with better survival.11,12 Among colorectal cancers, asymptomatic patients have observed lower ctDNA levels and recurrence than symptomatic patients, suggesting an indolent subset of colorectal cancers that do not shed readily detectable levels ctDNA.17 Cancers typically associated with poor prognosis, namely those at later stages and aggressive cancer types, are also associated with increased ctDNA shedding.8,10

Given this association between prognosis and ctDNA levels, it is important to understand the prognostic implications of new screening tests that use ctDNA to detect cancer signals.18-20 Although the relative hazard ratios (HRs) between ctDNA-detectable cancers and those that are not detectable have been examined, the question of absolute survival differences at early stages must be addressed to understand the potential for survival improvement by presymptomatic detection via ctDNA analysis.

Previous analysis of survival outcomes from the second (cross-validation) CCGA substudy in participants with known cancer status evaluated the prognostic value of cancer signal detection by an early version of this MCED test with 3-year longitudinal follow-up data.21 In participants with cancer, those who had an NCSD MCED test result were found to have better survival than those with a CSD MCED test result. This relative survival advantage was seen even after adjusting for clinical covariates, including clinical stage and method of clinical diagnosis. Importantly, despite a relative disadvantage in survival over the NCSD group, the observed survival for those with a CSD result was still better than the modeled overall cancer survival for matched cases from SEER data.21 This difference between CSD and SEER cases was apparent even at early stages, which already had better absolute survival than later stages.

In the present analysis, we examine observed and expected survival using data from the third (validation) CCGA substudy, in which blood samples from participants with known cancer status were tested using a refined version of the MCED test. We assessed the prognostic value of cancer signal detection by the MCED test by comparing participant survival outcomes with up to 5 years of follow-up to matched reference populations using an updated statistical methodology to account for confounding variation between groups.

METHODS

Study Design

The CCGA study (ClinicalTrials.gov identifier: NCT02889978) was a prospective, multicenter, case-control, observational study with longitudinal follow-up in 15,254 participants with known cancer status.7,8 Participants age 20 years and older with and without cancer were recruited from 142 sites in North America between August 2016 and February 2019. All participants were required to provide written informed consent. Participants with cancer were diagnosed through usual care before study entry. An overview of the three CCGA substudies is depicted in Appendix Figure A1.

The third CCGA substudy included 4,077 participants for independent validation of the MCED test, 2,823 with and 1,254 without cancer. Deidentified plasma samples were collected from each participant in a single blood draw and were then processed as previously described8 for MCED testing using a refined version of the MCED test. Results of the MCED test were not conveyed to the participant or health care provider. Participants with cancer were then followed for up to 5 years. Information on vital status, cancer treatment, current cancer status, and new cancer diagnoses was collected through electronic medical records and/or phone contact with participants. This report includes data collected up to December 12, 2023, for participants diagnosed with cancer.

The study was approved by the institutional review board or an independent ethics committee at each participating trial site and conducted in accordance with the International Conference on Harmonisation for Good Clinical Practice guidelines and the Declaration of Helsinki.

Statistical Analyses

Using 5-year longitudinal follow-up data for participants with cancer in the third CCGA substudy, overall survival (OS) was calculated and stratified by cancer signal detection status (CSD or NCSD). OS was calculated using the Kaplan-Meier (K-M) estimator based on the time from the date of blood sample collection to the date of death or the last date the participant was confirmed alive (censored).

Because MCED tests can detect multiple cancer types that are each associated with varying levels of ctDNA shedding, it can be misleading to directly compare cancer signal detection with survival risks to estimate HRs. To address this, the observed survival of each signal detection group was compared with the expected survival, which was calculated based on a US reference population generated using data from the National Cancer Institute's SEER Program and related SEER*Stat program (version 8.4.1). The reference population included patients with cancer diagnosed between 2006 and 2015, matched to the distribution of age, sex, cancer type, and cancer stage at diagnosis of CCGA participants in each signal detection group. Cancers were categorized as stage I, II, III, IV, or unknown according to American Joint Committee on Cancer 6th edition guidelines. Plasma cell neoplasms, nonstaged cancers, and cases with multiple primary cancers were excluded from the analysis. The comparison of the observed survival curve to the expected survival curve was done to isolate the effects of signal detection beyond the matched clinical factors and avoid issues with collinearity among known predictors of survival.22

A one-sample proportional hazards model was used to assess the significance of the difference between observed and expected OS. We adapted the log-likelihood survival framework from previous literature and implemented HRs in two ways.23 First, HRs and 95% CI were calculated for each group and the SEER reference survival distribution using a one-sample test. Second, we computed the maximum log-likelihood as a point estimate and used profile likelihood chi-square to obtain a 95% CI for the single variable. When comparing HRs between groups of different references, we applied Markov chain Monte Carlo sampling to estimate the HR and variability using the RStan package.24

The total contribution of the clinically predictive factors to differential survival between groups was examined through the comparison of HRs to obtain a relative HR. Kaplan-Meier plots were generated using the R software package (version 4.3.2).25

RESULTS

A Greater Proportion of Participants Who Died During the 5-Year Follow-Up Period Had Been Diagnosed With Late-Stage Cancer

Median length of follow-up was 53.8 months (IQR, 22.7-58.9 months). Of the 2,823 CCGA3 participants with cancer, 310 were diagnosed with plasma cell neoplasms, nonstaged cancers, or multiple primary cancers, and were excluded from the analysis. Of the 2,513 participants with stageable, invasive cancer, 1.5% (38/2,513) were lost during follow-up while 31.5% (792/2,513) were confirmed to have died by the end of the follow-up period (Fig 1). Participants who died during follow-up lived an average age of 66.0 years and were 42.2% female; those alive at the end of follow-up (67.0%; 1,683/2,513) lived to an average age of 60.6 years and were 54.5% female (Table 1). As expected, a larger proportion of participants confirmed to have died were diagnosed with stage III and IV cancers (75.2%) than those who were confirmed alive (26.7%) at 5 years (Table 1).

Survival Probability Was Higher for Participants With NCSD Versus CSD MCED Test Results Across Cancer Stages

When stratified by signal detection status, the clinical characteristics and distribution of cancer types and stages varied between the two groups (Appendix Fig A2 and Table A1). There was a greater proportion of late-stage cancers among those with a CSD compared with NCSD (70.5% v 13.2%). In addition, the CSD group had a greater proportion of deaths during the follow-up period compared with the NCSD group (85.0% v 34.4%, respectively; Table 1). Because the raw odds ratio for survival with a CSD may be misleading, we used SEER-based reference comparators matched for age, sex, cancer type, and stage to unmask the isolated prognostic impact of cancer signal detection that is obscured by differences in these clinically relevant factors.

When comparing the Kaplan-Meier curves of the observed and expected OS, survival estimates for both CSD and NCSD groups followed generally similar trends to matched expectations, although with the observed NCSD curve showing better-than-expected survival (Fig 2). The observed 5-year survival rates were 43% (95% CI, 40 to 45) for CSD and 88% (95% CI, 86 to 90) for NCSD, while the expected 5-year survival of the SEER-reference population matched for age, sex, cancer type, and stage at diagnosis was 40% for CSD and 81% for NCSD. The difference between observed and expected survival was significant (P < .0001) for both CSD and NCSD.

Among CSD Participants, Absolute Survival at Early Stages Was Significantly Better Than at Late Stages

In the CSD group, observed survival was similar to SEER-based expectations at early stages. The K-M estimator for 5-year survival for CSD stage I and II cancers was 66% (95% CI, 57 to 75) and 64% (95% CI, 57 to 70), respectively, compared with a SEER-expected survival of 71% and 67%, respectively (Fig 3, top panels). At later stages, observed survival was slightly more favorable compared with expected (48% [95% CI, 43 to 54] v 42% for stage III and 22% [95% CI, 18 to 26] v 16% for stage IV).

In the NCSD group, observed survival was slightly more favorable than SEER-based expectations at stage I (90% [95% CI, 87 to 93] v 85%) and stage II (92% [95% CI, 90 to 95] v 83%) but much more favorable at stage III (79% [95% CI, 71 to 87] v 66%) and stage IV (56% [95% CI, 41 to 70] v 32%; Fig 3, bottom panels). HRs were calculated to compare the risk of death for CCGA3 participants versus the matched SEER populations (Table 2 and Fig 4). For the NCSD group, HRs were <1 and statistically significant at all stages, indicating that those with NCSD had better survival than expected, regardless of stage at diagnosis. For the CSD group, HRs were <1 and statistically significant at stages III and IV, indicating that those with CSD and later-stage diagnoses had better survival than expected. At stages I and II, the HRs were close to 1 and not statistically significant (Table 2), suggesting that those with a CSD and early-stage diagnosis had survival risks similar to what would be expected in the matched SEER reference population.

DISCUSSION

The prognostic value of cancer signal detection by an MCED test was assessed in this 5-year follow-up analysis by comparing the observed survival of participants with cancer to the survival expected after accounting for variation in clinical factors known to affect survival outcomes. In the third CCGA substudy, participants with cancer who had an NCSD MCED test result had better survival across all stages compared with the CSD group, which can be explained by a combination of clinical factors, such as cancer type and stage, and the additional prognostic effect attributable to cancer signal detection. This pattern was most evident for cancers diagnosed at stages III and IV, where the survival probability of participants with NCSD was higher than expected and higher than the observed survival of participants with CSD. The present analysis includes multiple aspects that improve and expand upon previous analyses of survival outcomes in the CCGA study. In a previous publication,21 participants in the second CCGA substudy were tested using an early version of the MCED test and followed for 3 years. Covariates assessed included a grouping of cancer types based on expected 5-year survival as a predictor in a Cox proportional hazards model. The current analysis covers a longer, ie, 5-year, follow-up period after blood draw for participants in the third CCGA substudy, reaching a frequently used landmark for long-term cancer survival.

The OS probability observed in either signal detection group was close to the expected survival probability. This closeness to expectations suggests that the matched clinical factors, comprising age, sex, cancer type, and clinical stage, explain most of the variation in survival between signal detection groups. The remaining differences in HRs observed between CSD and NCSD groups not attributable to the addressed clinical factors may be explained by two nonexclusive possibilities. One possibility is that the NCSD group may have contained participants with unusually indolent cancers or cancer types that are more responsive to treatment. Consistent with this possibility, NCSD individuals were estimated to have over 90% 5-year survival for stage I and II, with the expected survival from SEER being below 85%. An alternative possibility is the CSD group may have contained participants with unusually hard-to-treat or aggressive cancers, even after accounting for their cancer type and any healthy volunteer bias improving overall cancer survival. However, this possibility is less likely as the observed 5-year survival probability and risk of death for early-stage CSD cancers is similar to what would be expected based on matched SEER data (stage I: 66% [95% CI, 57 to 75] observed v 71% expected; HR, 1.15, P = .415; stage II: 64% [95% CI, 57 to 70] observed v 67% expected; HR, 1.00, P = .917).

It has been suggested that by the time a case of early-stage cancer has begun to shed enough ctDNA to be detected by an MCED test, it has already begun to spread via micrometastases and is set to develop to an advanced stage, regardless of the timing of diagnosis or treatment.16,26-29 Our results do not support this assertion, as approximately two thirds of participants with an early-stage cancer diagnosis and a CSD test result were observed to survive up to 5 years. In our study, survival for those with early-stage CSD cancers is similar to that for matched individuals captured in SEER who would have been diagnosed by conventional means.

Although the underlying biology of ctDNA shedding does reinforce the link between ctDNA blood concentration and survival outcomes,30,31 our results demonstrate that premature mortality is not a foregone conclusion if cancer is detected by an MCED test. These early-stage CSD cancers are serious, yet still potentially treatable and likely have better outcomes compared with the same cancer diagnosed at a later stage. Overdiagnosis of indolent cancers is a concern for any cancer detection test.32 However, in this analysis, the observed survival curves are also not consistent with large numbers of indolent cancers shedding detectable ctDNA. Cancers that do not shed ctDNA, and thus less likely to be detected by an MCED test, are correlated with extremely favorable survival; following this logic, cancers that do have a CSD at an earlier stage may indicate a more clinically relevant cancer than an undetected cancer of the same type and stage.

Correlations between cancer type and ctDNA shedding and other clinical predictors can lead to overinterpretation of the prognostic effect of signal detection. Two detected and undetected cancers of the same type and stage may differ in underlying biology and thus clinical outcome. Therefore, heterogeneity between participant groups limits direct survival comparisons. This study population included participants diagnosed with a broad range of cancer types and stages from across multiple health care systems and, consequently, includes great variation in preexisting clinical characteristics. By using matched SEER reference data, we controlled for variation in survival due to known clinical predictors and are able to isolate the explanatory effect of signal detection, strengthening the validity of the results. By including the entire survival curve based on individual age, sex, cancer type, and stage as a predictor in our analysis, we avoided issues with less frequent cancer types and stages having limited observations.

A limitation of this analysis is that complex cancer type and stage information was summarized across multiple cancer types. CCGA cancer cases were matched to SEER data via general cancer type and not via distinct histologic and molecular subtypes (eg, tumor biology, histology, and grade), which provide prognostic information on their own. As cancer subtypes were not controlled for in this analysis, the known prevalence and differential survival rates of certain cancer subtypes, particularly those for breast, lung, and prostate, may have influenced the differences in observed and expected survival for NCSD and CSD cancers. Indeed, we see a large proportion of prostate and breast cancers in the stage I NCSD group (Appendix Fig A2). Low ctDNA-shedding indolent cancer subtypes may contribute to the markedly better survival seen for stage I NCSD cancers (HR, 0.51, P < .0001) in this analysis. Similar results were seen in previously published work specifically examining prostate cancers from CCGA3, which found that the MCED test did not detect any low-grade (Gleason grade 1) indolent prostate cancers, preferentially detecting high-grade, clinically significant prostate cancers.32

It is important to consider the evidence of healthy volunteer bias when observed survival is compared with the expected survival. Both CSD and NCSD groups showed improved average survival relative to the SEER-reference population, suggesting the third CCGA substudy cohort may possess better access to health care, such as curative-intent treatment, and potentially better overall health. We did not control for any variables related to health care access in this analysis. Under the assumption that this volunteer bias is identical between CSD and NCSD groups, this bias may cancel out in relative hazard analysis. However, we cannot rule out the possibility that this volunteer bias differs between groups.

This study has other limitations. First, CCGA was a noninterventional study, and therefore, the analysis includes only observational data. Participants' cancers were first diagnosed through usual care and therefore not representative of an asymptomatic screening population. Second, OS was used instead of cancer-specific causes of death. Third, this analysis focused on controlling for a limited number of clinical covariates. The influence of treatment modality or patient smoking history on survival rates was not controlled for because of matching information not being available in the SEER database. Finally, HRs may not be optimal for modeling this type of data, where a mixture of differing factors may affect the outcomes as well as time-varying hazards.

In summary, this extended follow-up of cancer outcomes supports and extends previous work showing that, although much of the difference between observed and expected survival for signal detection groups is explainable by known clinical factors, detection of a cancer signal by an MCED test has prognostic value. Cancers that were not detected by the MCED test tended to follow a more indolent course than detected cancers. Although cancer detected by the MCED test did carry a meaningful risk of death, the rate of survival for these detected cancers was no worse than cancers detected by conventional means in the general SEER population. This suggests that, on average, early-stage MCED test–detected cancers respond to treatment, are cured at the same rate, and are not more likely to be predominantly micrometastatic than cancers detected during routine care. Implementation of MCED testing for screening at a population level has the potential to improve survival outcomes by intercepting cancers while they are treatable at an early stage.
