A generative AI–enhanced intelligent service system with contextual retrieval and adaptive interaction for hospital use
Authors: Telung Pan, Yufang Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study presents the design and implementation of an intelligent service system that integrates generative AI, contextual retrieval, and adaptive interaction mechanisms to support frontline consultation in hospital environments. Motivated by the challenges faced in HIV and infectious-disease counseling—such as limited manpower, varying patient needs, and the demand for timely and standardized information—this research proposes a modular AI service architecture capable of delivering context-aware and empathetic responses. The system combines multi-stage speech interaction, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), and domain-adapted prompting strategies to support contextually consistent and retrieval-grounded responses in medical consultation scenarios. A distance-sensing trigger module initiates the interaction, while Voice Activity Detection (VAD) and speech-to-text (STT) modules capture user speech input with stability. The RAG module searches a curated medical knowledge base to provide domain-relevant contextual information, and the large language model (GPT-4o-mini) generates contextually grounded and comprehensible responses. The system’s multimodal output—consisting of synchronized text, speech, and animated visual feedback—supports user engagement and interaction fluency. Experimental evaluation conducted in collaboration with a regional hospital demonstrated positive results in language-generation quality and exploratory usability assessment under controlled or semi-controlled evaluation conditions (BLEU-4 = 0.71; ROUGE-L = 0.83; fluency score = 4.6/5). Scenario-based evaluations further suggest that the prototype can support retrieval-grounded consultation workflows and provide traceable interaction records under the evaluated conditions. Overall, the proposed system demonstrates the feasibility of integrating retrieval-grounded language generation, multimodal interaction, and embedded sensing into a human-centered consultation-support framework for healthcare service environments.Keywords: Intelligent service system, Retrieval-Augmented generation (RAG), Human-Computer interaction, Healthcare consultation support, Multimodal interactionSubject terms: Computational biology and bioinformatics, Engineering, Health care, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

In recent years, artificial intelligence (AI) technologies—particularly large language models (LLMs), conversational AI, and digital human systems—have advanced rapidly, opening new possibilities for automated interaction services. Within enterprise and healthcare settings, frontline staff are often required to handle highly repetitive and routine tasks, which may lead to inefficient use of human resources and inconsistent service quality. In hospitals, reception and triage nurses often respond to large volumes of basic inquiries and consultations over extended periods, resulting in increased workload, emotional fatigue, and operational pressure. Moreover, hospitals are high-risk environments in which frontline personnel may face elevated exposure to infectious diseases. These challenges highlight the need for AI systems that can support frontline consultation workflows by providing consistent and context-aware informational assistance. Importantly, the system proposed in this study is intended as informational support rather than as a diagnostic or triage decision-making tool.

When designing AI-based customer service systems for real-world physical environments, recent studies emphasize three essential technological foundations: data-driven service design, AI integration within service systems, and effective human–computer interaction (HCI)1–3. These elements are critical for developing service solutions that are efficient, adaptive, and user centered. However, traditional Product–Service Systems (PSS) — which integrate physical products with service processes to deliver holistic value—often struggle to incorporate user-generated data and AI-driven decision mechanisms into a coherent, practical system architecture1.

Therefore, a successful AI-based digital service robot must simultaneously integrate (1) data-driven design principles, (2) robust AI model utilization, and (3) human-centered interaction mechanisms to create an intelligent service system capable of responsive and context-aware operation. To ensure that physical products and associated service processes function as a sustainable solution, this study adopts a Smart Product–Service System (Smart PSS) perspective, integrating AI modules, embedded hardware, and interaction workflows into a unified framework that supports practical deployment.

Despite recent advances in conversational AI, retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and multimodal service systems, several research gaps remain in healthcare-oriented intelligent service design. First, prior studies often focus on isolated components—such as dialogue models, retrieval modules, or human–computer interaction mechanisms—rather than integrated deployable architectures suitable for real-world hospital environments. Second, relatively limited research has examined how retrieval grounding, adaptive multimodal interaction, and edge-oriented deployment can be combined into a coherent intelligent service framework under healthcare privacy and operational constraints. Third, empirical evidence regarding the practical feasibility of such systems in consultation-oriented service scenarios remains limited.

To address these gaps, this study proposes an intelligent service system that integrates contextual retrieval, adaptive multimodal interaction, and edge-oriented deployment for hospital consultation support. Unlike studies focused primarily on algorithmic innovation, the contribution of this work lies in system-level architectural integration and deployable service design for applied healthcare settings. Literature review

In recent years, the application of artificial intelligence (AI) in service system design has shifted from isolated functional modules toward fully integrated architectures. Prior studies have shown that service systems integrating data-driven decision support, multimodal AI, and human–computer interaction (HCI) can substantially improve service quality and user experience1,3. This section reviews three major foundations that inform the system design of the present study: data-driven service design, AI integration in service systems, and deep reinforcement learning (DRL) and human–AI interaction.

Data-Driven service design

In intelligent service systems, data-driven design plays a central role. It emphasizes real-time data collection, analysis, and feedback, enabling systems to dynamically adapt service processes and decision mechanisms according to actual user contexts. Chiu et al., demonstrated that through systematic frameworks and text analytics, Product–Service Systems (PSS) can capture implicit information embedded in user interactions and continuously improve service quality based on contextual variations and behavioral patterns1. The core of PSS lies in integrating products, services, and data flows, allowing real-time, user-centered adaptation.

Data-driven design (DDD) has been widely adopted in manufacturing, healthcare, transportation, and intelligent customer service. For instance, Lee et al., proposed the Cyber–Physical Systems (CPS) model in smart manufacturing, showing how sensor data and cloud analytics can predict failures and optimize maintenance strategies—transforming passive maintenance into proactive service4. In healthcare, Teoh et al., utilized multi-source data (e.g., medical records, speech logs, and physiological sensors) to develop clinical decision-support systems, enhancing safety and efficiency in clinical workflows5. Sidaoui introduced the “data-driven customer experience gestalt,” demonstrating how behavior logs and conversational data can reveal latent emotional needs across the user journey6. This allows designers to optimize interactions using empirical data rather than subjective assumptions.

In sum, data-driven service design is not merely a technical process but a methodology that places user experience and continuous learning at its core. It stresses learning from data, decision-making based on evidence, and iterative improvement through a continuous data–feedback loop. For this study, transforming multi-source data—including speech input, distance sensing, and interaction logs—into actionable knowledge is essential for enabling the Magic Mirror AI Service Desk to perform real-time understanding and self-adjustment.

Integration of artificial intelligence in service systems

With the rapid development of AI, its integration into service systems has expanded, especially in multimodal processing, semantic understanding, and decision support. Baltrušaitis, Ahuja, and Morency proposed a Multimodal Machine Learning Framework emphasizing fusion, alignment, and co-learning, allowing systems to process speech, text, and visual data simultaneously and achieve deeper semantic understanding. This foundational multimodal theory plays an essential role in modern intelligent service design7. Tsai et al., further introduced multimodal Transformers capable of dynamically weighting non-aligned speech and text sequences—an ability crucial for real-time semantic interpretation in customer service and healthcare dialogue systems operating under high uncertainty8.

In affective computing, Akçay and Oğuz showed that deep learning models combining acoustic features (e.g., pitch, tempo, energy) with semantic features significantly improve emotion recognition9. Zhao et al., demonstrated that CNN-based models using spectrograms can effectively learn emotional representations from speech10. Poria et al., found that considering both conversational context and emotional shifts enables more natural and empathetic multi-turn dialogues11. In healthcare service applications, Chen et al., confirmed that multimodal chatbots combining speech recognition, semantic analysis, and medical knowledge retrieval can enhance patient satisfaction and improve response accuracy12.

Overall, the integration of AI in service systems can be conceptualized as a continuous perception–understanding–response process. Multimodal learning techniques enable intelligent systems to interpret contextual information from multiple sensory channels, while emotion-aware and semantic reasoning models contribute to more natural, adaptive, and humanlike interactions. These technological foundations highlight how modern AI-based service systems can merge speech processing, semantic retrieval, and interactive feedback mechanisms to provide responsive and contextually appropriate services in complex real-world environments.

Hybrid multimodal and edge-deployable intelligent service systems

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), together with adaptive decision mechanisms informed by reinforcement-learning research, has been increasingly explored for intelligent service systems. Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) enables an agent to improve its behavior through trial-and-error learning and reward-based feedback, allowing it to adapt its decision-making strategies dynamically in response to user inputs and changing environmental conditions. In parallel, RAG offers a hybrid mechanism that integrates generative language models with external knowledge retrieval, thereby reducing hallucination and enhancing factual accuracy through the identification of semantically relevant information from structured knowledge bases13.

In healthcare-oriented conversational applications, these technologies have demonstrated notable advantages. Domain knowledge—such as screening procedures, risk factors, clinical definitions, and follow-up guidelines—is often encoded as vector embeddings and stored within searchable databases. Semantic retrieval enables AI systems to provide medically aligned and contextually accurate responses to user queries, leading to improved reliability in automated counseling and triage tasks. Prior studies suggest retrieval-enhanced models may improve factual alignment relative to purely generative models in producing clinically precise explanations, particularly when users seek information related to diagnostic timelines, treatment protocols, or medication effects.

Beyond retrieval, model adaptation techniques such as domain-specific prompting and response refinement contribute further to domain-specific consistency and contextual alignment. Research using clinical dialogues, patient–provider communication logs, and medical education transcripts indicates that domain-adapted small-to-medium language models can more effectively interpret medical terminology, patient expressions, and ethical communication norms. Domain-adapted prompting strategies help maintain consistent tone and reasoning patterns, while retrieval modules supplement the model with updated and verifiable knowledge. Together, these mechanisms form a dual-layer architecture that balances linguistic fluency from generative models with factual grounding from semantic retrieval.

From the perspective of interaction learning, multimodal and affective modeling research provides additional insights for human–AI interaction. Zhao, Mao, and Chen introduced a CNN–LSTM architecture capable of jointly analyzing acoustic patterns and emotional cues, thereby enhancing a system’s ability to infer user affective states10. Similarly, Su et al., demonstrated that reinforcement-learning-based dialogue management allows conversational agents to maintain stable and coherent responses even under ambiguous or incomplete user input14. These findings collectively suggest that integrating DRL-based decision optimization with RAG-supported knowledge grounding can enable the development of hybrid intelligent agents that are more adaptive, context-aware, and trustworthy in real-world service environments.

Recent studies have further highlighted the importance of hybrid multimodal architectures and lightweight edge-deployable intelligent systems in healthcare-related applications. For example, Hugar and Kagalkar15, proposed a hybrid dual-stream deep learning architecture for real-time assistive healthcare interaction, demonstrating the feasibility of multimodal inference under resource-constrained deployment environments. Similarly, Hugar et al.,16 conducted a comparative analysis of hybrid deep learning models and showed that multimodal model integration can improve robustness and scalability in healthcare-oriented intelligent interaction tasks. These studies reinforce the relevance of multimodal fusion, comparative evaluation, and lightweight deployment strategies, which are aligned with the system design rationale of the present study15,16.

Human–Computer interaction and smart service integration

Effective Human–Computer Interaction (HCI) is a decisive factor in whether intelligent service systems are accepted and continuously used. Recent studies suggest that HCI encompasses not only interface design but also cognitive psychology, emotional feedback, and trust formation17. Under human–AI collaboration, AI is no longer a passive assistant but an active partner capable of sensing, predicting, and responding to user needs.

Multimodal interaction enriched with emotional intelligence significantly enhances user engagement and allows AI to adjust tone and nonverbal behavior more naturally. In information systems, Kummer emphasized that knowledge management and cognitive adaptation are central to meaningful interaction18, requiring AI systems to learn behavioral patterns and adjust strategies dynamically—particularly critical in medical and public service environments where uncertainty and emotional sensitivity are high19. Therefore, trust-building, explainability, and safe interaction have become the three primary challenges in smart service design.

The rise of Smart Product–Service Systems (Smart PSS) offers a new framework for integrating HCI and AI. Traditional PSS emphasizes product–service combinations but is often limited to static processes20. In contrast, Smart PSS leverages sensors, cloud data, and AI to enable real-time information exchange and self-optimization (Zheng et al., 2018). In this framework, AI becomes a cognitive core that dynamically adjusts interaction strategies and service content. In medical applications, these integrations are especially crucial. By combining speech recognition, semantic understanding, real-time TTS/STT, and emotion detection, AI service robots can guide users proactively rather than merely reacting—achieving the “from passive response to active guidance” interaction paradigm.

Experimental system design and architecture

This section presents the system design and overall architecture of the Magic Mirror AI Service Desk, developed for this research. The system integrates Artificial Intelligence (AI), voice-based interaction, Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG), and Human–Computer Interaction (HCI) technologies to support natural-language healthcare consultation support and anonymous screening assistance. The primary application scenario targets hospital and clinic environments, particularly for HIV and infectious-disease education, pre-screening consultation, and follow-up navigation. Users interact verbally with the system and receive instant, context-relevant medical responses through a digital nurse avatar shown on the display.

Grounded in the theoretical foundations discussed in Sect. 2, the following subsection provides a detailed explanation of the system architecture, modular workflow, and semantic retrieval mechanism.

System workflow design

The interaction workflow of the Magic Mirror AI Service Desk, illustrated in Fig. 1, adopts a Smart Product–Service System (Smart PSS) design approach. Through modular connections between sensing, speech recognition, semantic retrieval, and language generation components, the system enables real-time adaptation and human-AI interaction.

System workflow of the Magic Mirror AI Service Desk based on a hybrid local-cloud architecture.

The workflow consists of six primary stages, described below:

Trigger by proximity detection

A distance-sensing module continuously monitors the user’s distance from the device. When the distance remains below a defined threshold for over two seconds, the system automatically activates and enters ready mode. This corresponds to the situational awareness principle in Smart PSS and ensures that interaction begins only when user intention is evident.

Voice input and detection

The Voice Activity Detection (VAD) module is activated to detect the start and end of utterances through silence-based segmentation. This ensures that the captured audio is complete and accurately framed, forming the input basis for subsequent semantic processing.

Speech recognition and semantic retrieval

Speech content is transcribed into text using a Speech-to-Text (STT) engine, then passed to the RAG pipeline for semantic matching. Through vector embedding and similarity search, the system retrieves medical knowledge fragments from an internal vector database (including HIV prevention, anonymous screening, and clinical follow-up guidelines). If similarity falls below a confidence threshold, a clarification prompt is issued to maintain response integrity and traceability.

Response generation and knowledge integration

A LLM generates responses based on the user query, retrieved knowledge fragments, and contextual alignment. A prompt-layering framework and streaming generation enable fast, coherent output. Domain-specific prompt adaptation maintains a medically appropriate tone, ensures consistent clinical terminology, and adheres to ethical communication standards—supporting trust and explainability in HCI.

Speech output and multimodal display

Generated text is converted into speech using a Text-to-Speech (TTS) module, then played through the audio system while the interface displays synchronized subtitles, character animation, and conversation status using Tkinter + VLC. Multimodal feedback enhances user clarity and creates a more immersive consultation experience.

Feedback and continuous learning

After each session, conversation summaries, response latency, and retrieval scores are logged anonymously. These data contribute to incremental updates of the vector database and future prompt-refinement and knowledge-base update cycles, forming a data-feedback loop that enables continual system improvement.

As shown in Fig. 1, the Magic Mirror AI Service Desk processes user interaction through a pipeline beginning with proximity-triggered activation, followed by speech recognition, semantic retrieval via RAG, knowledge-grounded response generation using LLM, and multimodal output with continuous learning feedback mechanisms.

Although proximity sensing, voice activity detection, interaction orchestration, and semantic retrieval using a local vector database were executed locally, external API services were used for GPT-4o-mini inference for response generation. Accordingly, the present system should be understood as a hybrid local-cloud architecture rather than a fully on-device architecture. In the prototype evaluation setting, only query text required for language inference was transmitted to the external model service, while domain knowledge retrieval remained local and raw personal identifiers were not used or stored. This architectural arrangement is intended to balance model capability, privacy considerations, and practical deployability.

Hardware architecture and sensor integration

The Magic Mirror AI Service Desk is built upon an embedded AI-interaction hardware framework, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The system is powered by a Raspberry Pi 5 (8 GB RAM) serving as the central controller, selected for its low power consumption, modular scalability, and edge-computing capability—enabling long-term deployment in hospital consultation desks or semi-public health service areas. Peripheral modules, including sensors, microphones, speakers, and a full-HD display, are interfaced via USB, I²C, GPIO, and HDMI connections.

Sensor-integrated hardware and edge-centric AI interaction framework of the Magic Mirror AI Service Desk.

A VL53L1X Time-of-Flight sensor forms the proximity-trigger mechanism for system activation. Distances are sampled via the PiicoDev I²C library and noise-reduced using a sliding-window average. When a user is detected within 1 m for > 2 s, an activation event is generated—representing context-aware interaction onset, a core property of Smart PSS.

Speech input is captured using a directional USB microphone (16-bit/44.1 kHz) with WebRTC-based Voice Activity Detection (VAD). Output audio is played through a high-fidelity external speaker, coordinated by a synchronized playback queue to maintain alignment with subtitles and animations. Visual feedback is presented through a 15.6-inch HDMI display using a Tkinter + VLC UI, showing system state, captions, and animated digital-nurse responses.

To meet healthcare privacy constraints, the system operates on an edge-AI model, where all speech, transcription, and embeddings are processed locally. Only anonymized statistical summaries are retained for model improvement, supporting privacy-aware deployment considerations in healthcare environments.

Software module design

The software architecture follows a layered modular design spanning four logic tiers:

(1) sensing and trigger input → (2) semantic understanding → (3) language generation → (4) multimodal output feedback, as shown in Fig. 3.

Software module architecture.

The View Module manages visual interaction, synchronizing system prompts, captions, and animated avatar expressions to enhance immersion and dialogue transparency. The Distance Module provides event-driven activation signals through distance polling and threshold logic consistent with context-aware Smart PSS design. VAD detects speech onset/termination through energy thresholds, ensuring natural input segmentation even in noisy environments. The Speech-to-Text (STT) Module handles streaming transcription with timeout-safe recovery: empty results automatically trigger user guidance prompts. The Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) Module embeds transcripts, performs cosine-similarity knowledge retrieval, and returns Top-k medical knowledge fragments for grounding. The LLM generates responses and multi-layer prompting to maintain clinical ethics, tone, and contextual relevance. Text-to-Speech synthesis and audio playback form the final multimodal delivery stage, supporting queued sentence streaming to minimize latency.

Operational logic and error handling

The main runtime loop is governed by a centralized ChatController, which operates as an asynchronous event-driven state machine (Fig. 4). The controller continuously cycles through a sequence of well-defined states, including IDLE, LISTENING, PROCESSING, GENERATING, RESPONDING, and COMPLETE, enabling robust and non-blocking interaction control.

Asynchronous event-driven state machine of the ChatController.

As illustrated in Fig. 4, the system remains in the IDLE state until user presence or voice activity is detected, at which point it transitions to LISTENING to capture speech input. The captured input is then forwarded to the PROCESSING state for speech recognition and semantic retrieval, followed by GENERATING, where the large language model produces a response. The system subsequently enters the RESPONDING state to deliver synthesized speech and visual feedback, before completing the interaction cycle and safely returning to IDLE.

To ensure system stability under real-world deployment conditions, timeout protection mechanisms are applied to critical components, including speech-to-text (STT), retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), and LLM inference. In the event of module failure, excessive latency, or invalid output, the system performs automatic recovery by resetting the state machine to IDLE, thereby preventing deadlock or system crashes and ensuring continuous operation in hospital environments.

Additional exception-handling strategies include silence-timeout recovery, module-level restart, and user-interrupt override. For example, if a new user approaches during an ongoing response, the current dialogue is gracefully terminated and a fresh interaction session is initiated. Collectively, these mechanisms enhance system resilience against environmental noise, unpredictable user behavior, and extended runtime operation, which are common challenges in real-world medical service settings.

Language generation and TTS performance

Beyond speech recognition and knowledge retrieval, a critical indicator of overall system usability lies in the clarity, coherence, and contextual appropriateness of the generated responses. To evaluate this aspect, the GPT-4o-mini model was assessed using a set of representative medical inquiry prompts related to HIV screening, risk-behavior counseling, and follow-up care, which reflect common use cases in clinical consultation and public health education scenarios.

Generation quality was quantitatively evaluated using BLEU-4 and ROUGE-L metrics to measure semantic alignment with reference responses. In addition, a fluency assessment was conducted using a 5-point Likert scale, focusing on conversational naturalness, readability, and patient-friendliness. This combined evaluation framework enables assessment not only of lexical and semantic alignment, but also of the system’s ability to communicate medical information in a conversational, accessible, and user-centered manner.

For language-generation evaluation, a shared set of 30 representative consultation prompts was used across all three system configurations. Each response was generated once using fixed decoding settings across all configurations to ensure consistent comparative evaluation conditions. These prompts were designed to cover common scenarios in HIV-related consultation, including screening procedures, risk assessment, symptom interpretation, and follow-up guidance.

Fluency was independently rated by three evaluators with backgrounds in information management and healthcare-related domains, and scores were reported as mean values across prompts and evaluators. Each response was rated using a 5-point Likert scale based on conversational naturalness, readability, and patient-friendliness.

Reference responses were constructed based on the curated domain knowledge base and reviewed for consistency with established medical information. BLEU-4 and ROUGE-L were used as preliminary proxy measures of lexical and semantic alignment between generated and reference responses. While these metrics do not fully capture dialogue quality, factual correctness, or response safety, they were adopted as complementary indicators and interpreted together with fluency evaluation.

The GPT-4o-mini model was adapted using domain-specific prompt design and response refinement strategies based on curated medical knowledge, rather than full parameter fine-tuning. This approach supports consistent response style and alignment with domain knowledge while maintaining deployment feasibility under resource constraints.

User study: interaction fluency & trust

To evaluate interaction fluency and perceived user trust, a controlled usability study was conducted with 15 participants, who freely interacted with the system under controlled usage conditions designed to approximate realistic interaction scenarios. Participants were encouraged to engage in natural, open-ended dialogue scenarios representative of real-world digital health service interactions. The evaluation was conducted under controlled or semi-controlled conditions using representative consultation scenarios, rather than through real-world clinical deployment. Given the exploratory nature of the study and limited sample size (n = 15), findings are interpreted descriptively rather than as formal statistical evidence or clinical validation. Participants consisted of adult volunteers, including undergraduate students in information management, with general familiarity with conversational systems. The participants were not clinical patients or healthcare professionals.

Despite the overall strong system performance, several failure modes were observed during pilot deployment and user interaction testing. These cases, along with their underlying causes and corresponding system-level mitigation strategies, are summarized in Table 5.

The most frequently observed issue involved premature voice activity detection (VAD) cutoff under noisy environmental conditions, primarily caused by ambient sound fluctuations in semi-open clinical spaces. This issue was mitigated through adaptive VAD threshold tuning, enabling the system to dynamically adjust sensitivity based on real-time noise profiles.

Another failure mode was associated with ambiguous user queries, which occasionally led to overly broad semantic retrieval results. This behavior was traced to unclear or underspecified user phrasing rather than retrieval model errors. To address this limitation, multi-turn clarification prompts were introduced, allowing the system to explicitly request additional context before executing retrieval and response generation.

A third issue involved approximately 0.5-second streaming jitter during audio playback, caused by temporary overflow in the text-to-speech (TTS) output queue under high concurrency. This was resolved through the implementation of audio pre-buffering and pre-loading mechanisms, ensuring smoother streaming output and improved temporal stability.

Future work will further enhance system robustness by incorporating ambiguity detection, longer-term conversational context tracking, and adaptive speech monitoring mechanisms, with the goal of stabilizing interaction quality in high-traffic clinical environments.

Conclusion and discussion

This study presents the Magic Mirror AI Service Desk as a practical demonstration of how data-driven design, AI system integration, and human–computer interaction optimization can converge to support a new class of intelligent healthcare services. Implemented on a Raspberry Pi–based edge computing platform, the system integrates speech recognition (STT), retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), large language models (LLMs), text-to-speech (TTS), and visual interaction modules to provide real-time, context-aware digital assistance in medical environments.

The preliminary findings and contributions of this research can be summarized as follows. First, this work proposes a modular AI interaction architecture coordinated by a centralized ChatController, which enables asynchronous orchestration across input sensing, semantic retrieval, response generation, and multimodal output rendering. This architecture supports scalability and flexibility, allowing dialogue logic, prosody, and behavioral patterns to be adapted for diverse service scenarios such as medical education, outpatient consultation counters, and public health information desks.

Second, the integration of retrieval-augmented generation with a domain-adapted LLM configuration suggests that retrieval grounding and model adaptation may contribute to improved semantic consistency and response reliability under the evaluated conditions. As reported in the evaluation results, the system achieved low-latency responses and strong retrieval grounding, indicating a tendency toward alignment between retrieved medical knowledge and generated conversational outputs.

Third, the system emphasizes human-centered multimodal interaction by combining speech, subtitles, and animated visual responses. User study results indicate positive perceptions regarding fluency, clarity, and overall interaction experience, suggesting that multimodal feedback may support communication quality and user engagement in healthcare interaction contexts.

Fourth, this work extends the concept of Smart Product–Service Systems (Smart PSS) into AI-driven service design by tightly integrating hardware infrastructure, interaction workflows, and domain knowledge bases. The proposed model illustrates how intelligent services may be deployed as cohesive service ecosystems rather than isolated AI components, providing a foundation for future AI-augmented service deployments in hospitals, long-term care institutions, and educational facilities.

From a clinical perspective, the present results suggest that AI systems of this type are more appropriately positioned as assistive extensions to consultation-support workflows rather than substitutes for professional medical staff. The conversational agent is intended to support early-stage informational inquiry by delivering structured and empathetic guidance, while allowing healthcare professionals to remain responsible for clinical judgment, complex decision-making, and psychological support. Importantly, the system is intended as informational support only and is not designed to make autonomous diagnostic, screening, or triage decisions.

At the same time, system trust is closely linked to transparency and predictability. While multimodal interaction may improve conversational naturalness, additional mechanisms for explaining reasoning processes and information sources remain necessary to meet medical ethics and informed-use standards. Ensuring high-quality standardized medical knowledge sources and continuous expert-audited model updates is therefore critical to reducing semantic drift and supporting safe deployment.

Despite the advantages of Raspberry Pi–based edge deployment in terms of cost efficiency and portability, computational capacity and model size remain constrained. Hybrid cloud–edge architectures and further optimization through model quantization or lightweight inference strategies are recommended to support broader scaling and improved latency performance in future implementations.

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the participant sample size was limited (n = 15), which may not fully represent diverse user populations. Second, the evaluated knowledge domain focused primarily on HIV counseling, requiring further validation across additional clinical topics. Third, system evaluation was conducted in simulated consultation environments rather than full hospital-scale deployment. Fourth, the current evaluation should be interpreted as exploratory prototype validation and does not constitute formal clinical validation.

In addition, formal statistical significance testing was not performed due to the exploratory nature and limited sample size of the present prototype evaluation. Furthermore, retrieval evaluation primarily focused on descriptive retrieval relevance and Top-k matching performance; more comprehensive retrieval-module analysis and large-scale benchmarking remain important directions for future research.

These limitations define natural directions for future work. Subsequent research may explore multilingual adaptation for international deployment, integration of medical knowledge graphs into the RAG pipeline to enhance reasoning transparency, and multimodal emotion recognition to dynamically adjust conversational tone and depth. Long-term field deployment in real hospital environments will also be essential for evaluating trust dynamics, workflow efficiency, privacy and security implications, and safety performance.

Overall, the development and preliminary evaluation of the Magic Mirror AI Service Desk suggest the feasibility of integrating retrieval-grounded language generation, multimodal interaction, and embedded sensing under controlled prototype evaluation conditions. While the present results should be interpreted within the scope of exploratory prototype evaluation rather than full clinical validation, the findings indicate that such systems may support human-centered service workflows and provide a foundation for future investigation. Further large-scale field studies will be needed to evaluate long-term trust, workflow impact, safety performance, and generalizability in real-world deployment settings.
