Chitosan-based hydrogel facilitates DC-SIGN-mediated monocyte-to-dendritic cell transformation in vivo for antigen-specific antitumor therapy
Authors: Jiake Lin, Benke Li, Xun Wang, Yuemin Zhou, Jinyuan Song, Jianghui Tang, Junlei Zhang, Haibin Hao, Yongtao Ji, Zhenyu Liu, Xingcan Shen, Xiao Chen, Jianpeng Sheng, Ruikang Tang, Xiaoyu Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn vivo-generated monocyte-derived dendritic cells (moDCs) play a pivotal role in inducing effective immune responses against infections and cancer due to their exceptional cross-presentation capabilities. However, the in vivo generation of moDCs during immunotherapy is constrained by the absence of safe and efficient in vivo strategies. Here, we propose a chitosan oligosaccharide-based CaCO3 nanoparticle-loaded hydrogel (CCH) to facilitate effective in vivo monocyte-to-moDC conversion by leveraging its in-situ spatiotemporal regulation capabilities. The CCH promotes the secretion of chemokines to recruit monocytes and then targets a dendritic cell-specific intercellular adhesion molecule-3-grabbing non-integrin (DC-SIGN)-pathway to facilitate the differentiation of monocytes into moDCs within a subcutaneous immune niche formed by CCH. After the removal of DC-SIGN+ cells in DC-SIGN-DTR mice, the proportion of CCH-induced moDCs significantly decreased, indicating the DC-SIGN-dependent conversion of moDCs. Accordingly, the tumor cell loaded-CCH (TCH)-induced moDCs facilitate cross-presentation to prime T cells and enhance robust antitumor T cell memory responses. Thus, subcutaneous injection of TCH effectively prevent the growth of primary liver cancer and patient-derived xenograft (PDX) colorectal cancer. Moreover, TCH inhibits postoperative tumor recurrence, providing promising advancements in immunotherapy. Our CCH-based strategy provided important insights on how to in vivo induce moDCs to harness cross-presentation for anti-tumor therapy.Keywords: Chitosan hydrogel, Monocyte-to-moDCs conversion, DC-SIGN, Primary liver cancer model

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy has emerged as a promising approach to treating malignancies by leveraging the body's natural immune system to target and eliminate tumor cells [1,2]. However, one of the critical challenges in this field lies in enabling effective cross-presentation—the process by which tumor-associated antigens are presented on MHC-I molecules to prime cytotoxic CD8+ T cell responses. In this context, monocyte-derived dendritic cells (moDCs) have been identified as key players in overcoming this challenge [3,4]. Unlike conventional type 1 dendritic cells (cDC1), which primarily perform cross-presentation in lymphoid organs, moDCs are uniquely equipped to execute this function directly in peripheral tissues under inflammatory conditions [5]. The differentiation and activation of moDC involves the recruitment of monocytes into inflamed tissues, followed by their local maturation into functional moDCs capable of cross-presenting antigens. This process enables moDCs to effectively prime CD8+ T cells. Therefore, harnessing the cross-presentation capacity of moDCs presents a promising therapeutic avenue for cancer immunotherapy. By directly engaging effector T cells in the tumor microenvironment, moDCs can help overcome the limitations of traditional antigen presentation pathways and drive robust antitumor immune responses. While early research on moDCs primarily focused on their role in infections, their growing recognition as key contributors to tumor immunity highlights their potential as therapeutic targets in cancer immunotherapy [[6], [7], [8]]. LPS and LPS-expressing bacteria can effectively drive the differentiation of monocytes into DC-SIGN/CD209a-expressing monocyte-derived dendritic cells (moDCs) in vivo [9]. However, bacteria or LPS pose significant risks when used systemically, as it can trigger severe inflammatory responses. These limitations highlight the need for safer, more targeted alternatives to induce moDC differentiation, such as biomaterials designed to mimic its beneficial effects while minimizing toxicity [10].

In recent years, hydrogels are emerging as a versatile material for spatiotemporal immune modulation, particularly in immunotherapy, due to their unique ability to provide dynamic control over the release and presentation of immune signals [11,12]. Hydrogels can serve as platforms to deliver immune-modulating agents, enabling the recruitment of specific immune cells such as T cells, dendritic cells (DCs), and macrophages to the tumor site. Hydrogels offer a promising strategy to manipulate the immune microenvironment in a controlled, localized manner, thereby boosting the effectiveness of cancer immunotherapies [13,14]. Therefore, we propose using hydrogels to create a localized inflammatory environment that recruits monocytes and, on this basis, activates the pathways related to the differentiation of monocytes into moDCs, achieving efficient local conversion from monocytes to monocyte-derived dendritic cells. The key aspect of this design is to continuously recruit monocytes, while efficiently promote their differentiation into moDCs to locally process antigens at the site of action. Accordingly, we suggest the use of chitosan-based hydrogel. Chitosan is a biocompatible and versatile polysaccharide, widely recognized for its potential in biomedical applications, particularly in immunology [15]. It is a linear polysaccharide made of N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) and glucosamine (GlcN) units, linked by β-1,4-glycosidic bonds [16,17]. Of note, bacterial polysaccharides, especially those with insoluble structures, enhance their interaction with C-type lectin receptors (CLRs) on immune cells, thereby activating moDC transformation. Chitosan can also trigger immune activation via CLR binding [18,19]. To enhance CLR activation, we propose using a chitosan-based hydrogel loaded with calcium carbonate (CaCO3), which increases the crosslinking of chitosan molecules, thereby improving their interaction with CTLs and providing calcium ions necessary for CLR activation [20]. The combined effect of these features in combination of chitosan-based hydrogel may promote local modulation, boosts immune cell recruitment, and enhances the overall immune response.

Here, we present a chitosan-based complex hydrogel, termed CCH, which incorporates CaCO3 nanoparticles and is capable of loading tumor whole-cell as antigens. The CCH recruited monocytes at the injection site and effectively induced the differentiation of these monocytes into dendritic cells (moDCs) through the regulation of the pattern recognition receptor DC-SIGN. As such, after immunization with tumor cell-loaded CCH (TCH), the antigens were efficiently cross-presented, leading to the activation of antigen-specific T cell immune responses and the generation of antigen-specific immune memory. We also tested the anti-tumor immune response of TCH using a primary liver cancer model and PDX colorectal cancer model, confirming its potential to generate robust antitumor responses with a favorable safety profile. In a postoperative treatment model, TCH significantly inhibited the recovery of tumor growth, demonstrating its ability to elicit a specific immune response (Fig. 1). The platform marks a significant advancement in overcoming the challenges of monocyte-to-moDC conversion and offers a promising route for developing targeted whole-cell tumor vaccines.

Upon efficient presentation of tumor whole-cell antigens by moDCs, T-cell responses in both the lymph nodes and liver are enhanced, leading to tumor prevention.

Results

Preparation, characterization and safety assessment of TCH

moDCs can cross-present antigens and directly stimulate effector CD8 T cells in situ within inflamed tissues but not lymph nodes like other DCs do [21,22]. Therefore, we utilized a complex hydrogel to create a localized tumor cell-loaden microenvironment that enables in situ monocyte cell recruitment and transforming, facilitating further antigen processing. Accordingly, we utilized positively charged chitosan to form a hydrogel capable of efficiently encapsulating B16-OVA tumor cells (1 × 106 cells/100 μl) as whole-cell antigens. Sodium polyaspartate-stabilized nano-CaCO3 was added as a crosslinker as the nano-CaCO3 reduced the pH of chitosan-tumor cell mixture, facilitating intermolecular crosslinking of chitosan. This approach resulted in a stable, temperature-responsive composite hydrogel with effective cell encapsulation. As depicted in Fig. S1A–B (Supporting Information), TCH maintained in the sol state at room temperature to ensure injectability, but hydrogel formed at 37 °C, which ensures the gel formation after subcutaneous injection. Characterization of TCH by confocal microscopy revealed DAPI-labeled nuclei and green fluorescent-labeled nano-calcium carbonate are uniformly dispersed within the red fluorescent-labeled chitosan hydrogel (Fig. 2A), indicating the complexed hydrogel structure. Cryo-electron microscopy results showed a porous structure of TCH, with a porosity of approximately 45% (Fig. 2B). Nano-CaCO3 can be evenly distributed in the hydrogel because of its small size (Fig. S1C). Under acidic conditions, calcium ions were released more rapidly to exert their effect (Fig. S1D). Next, we evaluated the cytotoxicity of CCH using cell counting kit-8 (CCK-8) assay under with various concentrations of TCH. The results showed that TCH displayed no toxicity, indicating the biocompatibility of the TCH platform (Fig. 2C).

To ensure the immunogenicity of the whole-tumor cells, untreated tumor cells was loaded into CCH, and their cell viability was approximately 90% (Fig. S2A). Thus, we first evaluate, from a safety perspective, whether the tumor cell-loaded hydrogel complex can effectively restrict tumor growth in vitro and in vivo. In vitro, TCH is stable and does not degrade in buffers of different pH values (Fig. S2B). We performed flow cytometry analysis to examine the cells released into the culture supernatant after incubating TCH in the culture medium for 48 h. The results revealed no tumor cell leakage compared to the tumor cell group (Fig. S2C–E). Next, we examined the in vivo formation of melanoma following the subcutaneous injection. We observed that noticeable subcutaneous nodules formed in the mice starting from the second day after injection, and by the 7th to 14th day, the nodules had reduced in size, which could indicate either the migration of recruited cells or the degradation of the hydrogel (Fig. S3). For mice directly inoculated with B16-OVA melanoma cells, tumor developed significantly within 14 days. In contrast, mice injected with TCH loaded with B16-OVA showed no signs of tumor growth during the same period (Fig. 2D). These results highlighted the biosafety profile of TCH. However, the absence of tumor growth cannot be entirely attributed to electrostatic interactions between tumor cells and chitosan, as it is challenging to restrict tumor cells solely through electrostatic effects. The reasons are likely related to phagocytosis by macrophages and the rapid activation of a specific immune response. Our previous work [23] have verified that whole antigen loading hydrogel achieves antigen fixation and digestion through both electrostatic interactions and local immune modulation, ensuring the safety of this strategy. We also harvested the major organs of mice injected with TCH for hematoxylin–eosin (HE) staining, and found no obvious tissue damages (Fig. 2E). Hemolysis experiments help assess the biocompatibility of CCH by measuring their potential to damage red blood cells. The CCH showed a low level of hemolysis (Fig. S4), suggesting low toxicity and biocompatibility.

In vivo immune cells recruitment and transformation

Next, we explored the recruitment of immune cells by CCH. We first examined the endotoxin levels in the prepared CCH and found them to be very low (0.01099 EU/mL), ruling out the possibility of immune activation caused by LPS contamination (Fig. S5). The in vivo formed nodules following CCH injection were extracted and subjected to immunohistochemical (IHC) staining to identify monocytes and moDCs. Compared to normal dermal tissue, the CCH group exhibited an increased recruitment of dendritic cells (CD11c+) and monocytes (CD14+) within the dermal tissue at the injection site (Fig. 3A–D). Then, we isolated the CCH recruited cells from digested dermal tissue and analyzed them using flow cytometry at 1- and 3-day post-injection to assess the dynamic transformation of monocytes into moDCs. On the first day after CCH injection, we observed a dramatic increase in the proportion of monocytes compared to the control group (Fig. 3E), indicating that the CCH effectively recruited a large number of migratory monocytes at an early stage. However, on day 1, the number of corresponding moDCs showed only a slight increase compared to the Blank group, with no significant elevation in their proportion (Fig. 3F). On the third day following subcutaneous injection of CCH, flow cytometry analysis revealed a significant decrease in the proportion of monocytes (Fig. 3G), while the proportion of moDCs increased by 50% (Fig. 3H). The gating strategies for monocytes and moDCs are shown in Fig. 3I. We selected F4/80+CD11b+ cells, excluded CD326+ Langerhans cells, and then defined moDCs as Ly6C−MHC-II+ cells and monocytes as Ly6C+MHC-II- cells within the resulting population. These data showed effective monocyte-to-moDCs transformation in vivo in the presence of CCH.

The CCL family of chemokines plays a crucial role in monocyte recruitment, participating in immune modulation by binding to specific receptors on the surface of monocytes and directing their movement in vivo [24]. Accordingly, we used transcriptome sequencing (RNA-seq) to determine chemokine expression in nodules at the site of CCH inoculation. In a three-group experimental design consisting of Blank, CCH without Ca (CCH/Ca−), and CCH, distinct chemokine expression profiles were observed 1 day after inoculation. Both the CCH/Ca− and CCH group exhibited higher levels of chemokines than the Blank group. Notably, the CCH group exhibited significantly higher expression levels of the CCL family of chemokines than did the CCH/Ca− group (Fig. 3J). This indicated that the presence of nano-CaCO3 enhanced the ability of the hydrogel to recruit immune cells. Additionally, we analyzed the most differentially expressed genes across the three groups. In the CCH group, the majority of the enriched pathways for the highly expressed genes were related to immune cell migration. (Fig. S6). The CCH upregulated chemokines and high expression of cell migration-related genes, which facilitates the recruitment of monocytes and the subsequent generation of moDCs. The results are in line with previous studies that reported low-molecular-weight chitosan promote the innate immune response to activate dendritic cells [25,26].

DC-SIGN-mediated monocyte-to-dendritic Cell transformation

DC-SIGN, a prototype C-type lectin, also known as CD209, recognizes mannose and fucose repeating units present on various pathogens, including mycobacteria, fungi, viruses, and parasites [16,17]. DC-SIGN functions as a pathogen recognition receptor, playing a crucial role in detecting and sensing a wide range of pathogens in a multivalent and calcium-dependent manner [27,28]. CD209 are frequently used to identify moDCs. Thus, we first performed molecular docking of the chitosan monomer with the DC-SIGN receptor protein (PDB ID: 1SL4) to evaluate the strength of their interaction. The molecular docking score indicated that the chitosan oligosaccharide monomer exhibited the strongest interaction energy with the 1SL4 protein in the presence of Ca2+ ions, with a binding energy of −5.782 kcal/mol (Fig. 4A). The stable binding of the chitosan oligosaccharide monomer within the active site of the 1SL4 protein was primarily attributed to two factors. First, the chitosan monomer contained abundant hydrogen bond donor atoms, enabling multiple hydrogen bond interactions with the surrounding amino acids in the binding pocket. Second, the coordination between the oxygen atoms in the chitosan monomer and the Ca2+ ion significantly enhances the binding strength between the chitosan monomer and the protein. In the absence of calcium ions, the chitosan oligosaccharide monomer bonding with the 1SL4 protein was significantly weakened, preventing the monomer from fully entering the active cavity, which likely resulted in reduced or lost activity (Fig. S7). The molecular docking results indicated that CCH activated the DC-SIGN pathway through the synergistic effects of chitosan-based hydrogel and CaCO3 nanoparticles, which released Ca2+ upon entering the lysosome. In addition, we co-incubated CCH with RAW264.7 cells and detected the mRNA level of DC-SIGN on the surface of RAW264.7 cells by qPCR. The results showed that CCH significantly activated the DC-SIGN receptor. (Fig. 4B).

To evaluate whether CCH activates dendritic cells through the DC-SIGN signaling pathway in vivo, we established a DC-SIGN-DTR (diphtheria toxin receptor) mouse model to selectively deplete DC-SIGN-positive dendritic cells and verify the mechanism of action and immunological effects of CCH. Three days following CCH injection, we administered diphtheria toxin (DT) to DC-SIGN-DTR mice, as described in the literature (Fig. 4C), to transiently deplete a subset of DC-SIGN+ cells, achieving targeted clearance [29]. Compared to the untreated group, DC-SIGN+ cells in the CCH+DT group were eliminated. Similarly, without the clearance of DC-SIGN+ cells, moDCs were approximately six times more abundant than monocytes three days after CCH injection (Fig. 4D and E). However, upon the clearance of DC-SIGN, monocytes predominated, and the conversion efficiency to moDCs significantly decreased (Fig. 4F). These data confirmed that CCH targets the DC-SIGN pathway to facilitate the subsequent transformation of recruited monocytes into moDCs in vivo.

In general, the activation of DC-SIGN on monocyte-derived DCs can contribute to the induction of pro-inflammatory cytokines and type I interferons through a Toll/IL-1R domain-containing adaptor-inducing IFN-β (TRIF)-dependent mechanism. Once transformed into moDCs upon DC-SIGN activation, they can activate TRIF signaling, turning them into highly efficient antigen-capturing and presenting cells, with the ability to cross-present Gram-negative bacteria and captured antigens [30]. In contrast to previous reports, our results show that CCH activates DC-SIGN, leading to efficient conversion of monocytes to moDCs. Therefore, we further investigate whether CCH can effectively trigger downstream activation signals of the TRIF signaling pathway, enabling antigen presentation and T cell activation. We conducted a Western blot analysis to assess the expression of TRIF protein in skin tissues collected after CCH inoculation. As shown in Fig. 4G and Fig. S8, the CCH-treated group exhibited a significant upregulation of TRIF protein compared to the Blank group. This suggests that CCH activates moDCs through TRIF signaling, as TRIF, is essential for moDC mobilization.

Antigen cross-presentation by moDCs

moDCs are potent antigen-capturing and presenting cells capable of cross-presenting engulfed antigens [31]. Activation of the DC-SIGN pathway by TCH drives monocyte differentiation into moDCs in vivo, prompting us to investigate their potential to enhance antigen internalization and T-cell activation [32]. To demonstrate the versatility of the CCH strategy in loading tumor cells as antigens, we isolated primary hepatocellular carcinoma cells (pHCCs) from mice and incorporated them into the hydrogel, highlighting the plug-and-play potential of gel-based tumor vaccines. We constructed a primary hepatocellular carcinoma model by combining hydrodynamic transfection with Sleeping Beauty transposon-mediated somatic cell integration to obtain pHCCs [33]. Then, we isolated pHCCs to serve as homologous tumor whole-cell antigens for TCH (Fig. S9). To assess antigen presentation, pHCCs were loaded into CCH to make TCH and then labeled with CellTracker™ Green CMFDA (Fig. 5A). Three days post-inoculation, it was observed that 99.6% of the monocyte-derived dendritic cells (moDCs) from the TCH-injected group exhibited a CMFDA signal, demonstrating that the whole tumor cell antigens from pHCCs were efficiently taken up by the moDCs recruited by the TCH (Fig. 5B and C). This suggests that the TCH effectively facilitates the uptake of tumor antigens by moDCs, enhancing antigen presentation for immune activation. In addition, we measured the fluorescence signals of pHCCs in the draining lymph nodes (dLNs) to assess if antigen-uptake moDCs is capable of migrating to dLNs. The presence of moDCs in the draining lymph nodes is linked to the effective antitumor immune response [34]. Accordingly, in TCH-injection group, flow cytometry results showed that more than 60% of the moDCs in the dLNs is CMFDA-positive, suggesting that subcutaneously injected pHCCs-loading TCH was taken up by moDCs and then efficiently migrated to the lymph nodes (Fig. 5D–F).

moDCs is capable of cross-presentation to CD8 T cells [35]. To evaluate the regulation of TCH on cross-presentation, we loaded peptide OVA257-264 (SIINFEKL) to mimic antigen during TCH preparation (Fig. 5A). Three days after TCH treatment, dLN-moDCs were isolated and then cocultured with CFSE-labeled OT-1 T cells, which specifically recognize OVA-related antigens presented by dLN-moDCs, at a 1:1 ratio for 48 h. Flow cytometry analysis revealed significant multigenerational proliferation of OT-1 T cells stimulated by TCH-stimulated moDCs, demonstrating the dLN-moDCs effectively cross-presented OVA-antigen to T cells and activated the proliferation of T cells (Fig. 5G).

Memory T cell responses by antigen-loading moDCs

Given the significant reduction in the size of the TCH-formed nodules 14 days after inoculation, the long-term modulation of TCH may have a positive effect on immune memory responses. Tissue-resident memory T cells (TRMs) play an important role in immune memory due to their unique functions in the local immune response, long-term immunoprotection and adaptation to various pathogens [[36], [37], [38]]. Therefore, we administered two injections of TCH (OVA) at 14-day intervals to achieve a durable immune stimulus, and we collected the dLNs and livers of the mice on Days 7 and 14 after the second inoculation to determine TRM enrichment. The canonical markers CD69 and CD103 have been identified as key markers for TRM cells, and their positive expression in CD8+ T cells is important for tumor control [39,40]. In both the dLNs and livers, we observed that the CCH group had the highest percentage of CD69 and CD103 double-positive CD8+ T cells among the three groups. Furthermore, the percentage of CD8+ T cells in the TCH group was significantly higher than that in the control group that incubated with OVA only. In addition, the proportion of Tetramer+ CD69 and CD103 double-positive cells remained highest in the TCH group, which indicates antigen-specific, activated, and resident TRM within tissues. These markers help differentiate TRM cells from circulating T cells and provide insights into their role in local immune responses and tissue immunity (Fig. 5H–I and Fig. S10). TRMs can be mobilized immediately to prevent tumor growth.

TCH prevents tumor growth

To more accurately simulate clinical tumorigenesis, we employed a hydrodynamic plasmid transfection technique to establish a primary hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) model. In this model, normal hepatocytes were directly induced to transform into cancerous cells in situ through the introduction of plasmids carrying specific oncogenes, which triggers malignant transformation. This method closely mimics the natural process of liver tumorigenesis, allowing us to evaluate the tumor-preventive effect of CCH in a more clinically relevant context [41].

We administered two doses of TCH 14 days apart to elicit an immune response, followed by tail vein injection of plasmid DNA containing oncogenes and transposons on the 14th day after the second vaccination to establish the tumor model (Fig. 6A). Forty days after the plasmid injection, we autopsied the mice to harvest livers and evaluate the preventive effect of TCH on primary HCC. The liver results showed that the TCH group exhibited significant prevention of HCC development, while the CCH group, which did not receive the HCC load, showed reduced prevention compared to the TCH group but was still more effective than the Blank group (Fig. 6B), suggesting tumor prevention effect. Weights of mouse livers also revealed that, compared to the very severe tumor burden in the Blank group, both the CCH and TCH groups showed a reduction in liver cancer lesions (Fig. S11).

The survival experiment showed that in the blank group, mice began to die 33 days after plasmid injection, and all mice in this group were dead by day 65. In contrast, no mice from the CCH or TCH groups had died by day 65 (n = 8). In the CCH group, which did not receive primary tumor cells, only two mice remained alive after 75 days, whereas in the TCH group, the survival rate was 62.5%, with three mice still alive at 90 days (Fig. 6C).

We speculate that the anti-tumor effect of CCH may be related to non-specific immunity, so we investigated the levels of NK cells in the paracancerous tissues of the liver, and flow cytometry showed that the proportions of NK cells in both the CCH and TCH groups were significantly higher than those in the Blank group (Fig. 6D). Since the anti-tumor effect of CCH is weaker compared to TCH, we believe that the specific T cell immune response is the main reason for the anti-tumor effect. Therefore, we investigated the levels of CD8+ T cells in the tumor lesions. The proportion of CD8+ T cells in the CCH group was significantly higher than that in the Blank group and CCH group (Fig. 6E), suggesting the tumor preventive effect of TCH was attributed to the increase antigen-specific T-cell response. CCH-induced innate immunity is mainly reflected by the activation of moDCs in vivo, and thus we used the DC-SIGN-DTR mouse model to validate the effect of moDC clearance on tumor prevention efficacy. With the same tumor challenge design, we used a combination of short-term depletion and long-term depletion to achieve the removal of DC-SIGN+ moDCs during the experimental cycle (Fig. S12). The autopsy results showed that the protective effect of CCH on primary liver tumor growth was abolished after the removal of moDCs, and the growth of primary liver cancer was even more pronounced than that in the original Blank group (Fig. 6F). The results highlight the crucial role of moDCs in inhibiting tumor growth.

We also utilized a patient-derived xenograft (PDX) colorectal model to more accurately replicate the pathogenesis observed in the clinical related tumor model and further confirm the inhibitory effect of CCH on tumor growth following the establishment of a primary tumor model. By digesting and processing clinically extracted colorectal cancer tumors as antigens, we obtained primary tumor cell-loading CCH. Then, we vaccinated CCH loaded with different numbers of tumor cells (5 × 105 and 2 × 106 primary tumor cells per CCH), and then performed tumor challenge experiments by implanting 2 × 2 × 2 mm3 tumor blocks. The anatomical results showed the anti-tumor growth effect of CCH in severely immune-deficient (NSG) mice (Fig. 7A and B). Within the tumors, the proportions of dendritic cells (Fig. 7C) and naïve helper T lymphocytes (Th naïve, Fig. 7D) were significantly increased in the TCH group, while naïve cytotoxic T lymphocytes (Tc naïve, Fig. 7E) and NK cells (Fig. 7F) were also elevated compared to the control groups. These findings indicate that TCH inoculation promoted immune cell infiltration within the tumors and substantially inhibited tumor growth. We evaluated the inhibitory effect of CCH on the primary liver cancer model and the clinically derived PDX tumor model, and found that CCH exhibited good efficacy in both of these challenging tumor models, suggesting that moDCs can generate antigen-specific immune protective responses.

TCH inhibits postoperative tumor recurrence

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of the TCH, we explored whether postoperatively embedded the 4T1 cell-loaded TCH could inhibit 4T1 tumor recurrence. Mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 106 4T1-Luc tumor cells on day 14, followed by surgical resection of tumors on day 0. Subsequently, TCH was embedded into the partially excised tumor in situ, and bioluminescence imaging was performed on days −1, 0, 7, 14, and 21 to monitor tumor recurrence (Fig. 8A). After surgical implantation, the bioluminescence signal in the PBS group returned to levels comparable to those observed before the surgery. In contrast, within 20 days, mice in the TCH group demonstrated lower tumor bioluminescence intensity (Fig. 8B and C) and reduced tumor volume compared to the PBS group (Fig. 8D and E). Moreover, survival analysis (Fig. 8F) showed significantly prolonged survival in TCH-treated mice relative to PBS-treated mice, highlighting the therapeutic advantages of TCH. These results collectively confirmed that CCH treatment effectively inhibited tumor growth and improved survival in the 4T1 breast cancer model, suggesting that CCH has potential as a whole-cell tumor vaccine for the treatment of tumor recurrence after surgery.

Conclusions

To tackle the challenge of insufficient antigen-presenting cells in cancer immunotherapy, we propose a chitosan-based hydrogel complex as a platform for transforming monocytes into moDCs. This innovative approach addresses the current lack of effective and precise in vivo strategies for moDC transformation. The highlight of using TCH lies in its immune-modulating properties, which recruit monocytes through chemokine secretion and activate the calcium-dependent DC-SIGN pathway, facilitating the transformation of hydrogel-recruited monocytes into immune-active, antigen-presenting moDCs within the immune niche formed by TCH. As a result of effective whole-cell antigen processing and antigen cross-presentation by moDCs, the antigen-specific response, including T-cell priming and antigen-specific immune memory, was significantly enhanced. Leveraging these advantages, TCH demonstrated effective prophylactic efficacy against primary liver cancer and clinically extracted colorectal cancer. In the post-surgical tumor model, TCH was able to recruit moDCs to the resected area and significantly inhibited tumor recurrence without the addition of any therapeutic drugs, indicating the induction of a tumor-specific immune response. Our strategy offers an effective hydrogel-based platform for generating moDCs with a strong ability to cross-present antigens and stimulate an antigen-specific T-cell response, providing a promising approach for whole-cell vaccine-based cancer immunotherapy.

Methods

Preparation of TCH

First, we cultured B16-OVA cells in a cell culture incubator (Thermo Fisher) at 37 °C with 5% CO2, ensuring optimal conditions, with a concentration of 1.2 × 107 cells per 100 μL saline. Following this, the centrifuge tube containing quantitative ddH2O and COS powder was vigorously shocked to completely dissolve COS, and 25 mg/mL of COS aqueous solution was obtained and set aside. Meanwhile, the CaCO3 nanoparticles was pre-prepared by sequentially adding CaCl2 (111 mg/mL), PASP-Na (200 mg/mL), and NaHCO3 (84 mg/mL) in a 1:1:1 ratio to a small glass vial under magnetic stirring. Finally, the TCH was prepared by mixing the COS aqueous solution, CaCO3, and B16-OVA cell suspension in a volume ratio of 8:3:1 with stirring. For CCH/Ca−, COS, ddH2O, NaHCO3 and PASP-Na were mixed in a ratio of 8:2:1:1 and then stirred.

Characterization of TCH

A Nikon confocal microscope (model: N-STORM/A1R) was used to observe the spatial distribution of TCH components. We label COS, CaCO3, and B16-OVA cells with Alexa Fluor™555 NHS (Invitrogen™, A20009), calcein (MCE, 1461-15-0), and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Sigma-Aldrich, D9542), respectively. Then, the prepared TCH was pipetted and placed in a confocal petri dish in an appropriate amount as described above. Under a 40x objective lens, excitation with 561 nm, 488 nm and 405 nm lasers were used for imaging. All reagents were used with reference to the manufacturer's instructions. The microstructure of the TCH was then characterized using cryogenic scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The TCH samples are first loaded with clamps and dropped directly into a liquid nitrogen slurry for cryofixation, then the samples are transferred from the liquid nitrogen to a frozen sample stage loading device and then the stage containing the samples is transferred to an EM ACE600 sputterometer using a vacuum cryotransfer. A knife in the sputterometer was applied to freeze fracture the sample to expose a fresh cross section of the sample. Finally, a platinum spray treatment is applied to avoid discharge during SEM observation. The viscoelastic properties of the TCH were characterized using an Anton Paar MCR 302 rheometer. The storage modulus (G′) and loss modulus (G″) were measured as under a constant strain of 1.0% and an angular frequency of 6.28 rad/s.

Domain-limiting assay of TCH on tumor cells in vitro

100 μl of TCH was transferred into a 12-well plate (Corning), after which 1 ml of RPMI-1640 medium was gently added to completely cover the TCH. Supernatant medium was collected for flow cytometry assay (Beckman, CytoFLEX LX) at four time points, 6 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h after incubation. The medium was centrifuged at 300 g for 3 min, the supernatant was discarded, and the precipitate was resuspended in 200 μl of Hoechst 33342 (Beyotime, C1022) and stained for 30 min at 4 °C with light protection. At the end of the staining process, 500 μl of Phosphate-Buffered Saline (PBS) containing 2% FBS was added for centrifugation to remove the excess dye, and the final resuspension was done in 200 μl of PBS containing 2% FBS for subsequent flow cytometry analyses. To accurately gate the target cell population in TCH supernatant samples, Hoechst-negative cells were added as an internal control.

TCH in vitro toxicity assay

To assess the proliferative activity of B16-OVA cells under varying concentrations of TCH, the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was employed. Briefly, B16-OVA cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and allowed to adhere overnight. Subsequently, gradient dilution was used to obtain different concentrations of TCH to treat the cells. Six parallel wells were set for each concentration and untreated normal wells were set. After incubation for 24 h, 10 μL of CCK-8 solution (MCE, HY-K0301) was added to each well and incubated for an additional 2 h at 37 °C. The absorbance was measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

TCH in vivo safety assessment

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the Guidelines for the Care and Use of Animals for Scientific Purposes issued by Zhejiang University, which are in compliance with the ARRIVE guidelines and implemented in accordance with the U.K. Animals (Scientific Procedures) Act, 1986 and associated guidelines. All experiments were approved by the Laboratory Animal Welfare and Ethics Committee of Zhejiang University under the ethical approval number ZJU20240276, and the operator number was 17730. TCHs, dosed at 100 μl containing 1 × 106 B16-OVA cells per mouse (C57BL/6JGpt, 6-8 weeks old, male), were subcutaneously implanted, and the growth of subcutaneous tumors in mice was observed over a period of 14 days, and on the 14th day, the hearts, livers, spleens, lungs, and kidneys of the mice were harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution, ten times the organ volume, for subsequent HE staining. In addition, TCH was inoculated in BALB/c-Nude (6-8 weeks old, male) mice, and the self-degradation of TCH in vivo was observed over 14 days.

Experimental study of immune cell recruitment and activation by TCH in vivo

After TCH inoculation, mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation to harvest samples for immune cell recruitment analysis. The hair around the injection site in the right lower abdomen was removed, and the skin in direct contact with the TCH was carefully peeled away. This skin was either soaked directly in tissue fixative for subsequent IHC experiments or meticulously scraped clean and cut into strips measuring 2-3 mm in width. These strips were then submerged in a solution containing 0.2% type I dispase and placed within a 37 °C incubator for a digestion period of 2 h.

Upon completion of the digestion process, the skin was cautiously separated into its epidermal and dermal layers using delicate tweezers. The dermal tissue was collected and washed thrice with PBS. Subsequently, the dermal tissue was sectioned into smaller pieces and subjected to a 30-min digestion in a solution containing 0.2% type IV collagenase, all while being maintained at a temperature of 37 °C. Finally, the dermal tissue was passed through a 70 μm cell sieve to effectively remove any excess tissue, resulting in the acquisition of a single cell suspension comprised of dermal cells for flow cytometry analysis. Similarly, immune cell activation was analyzed in mice harvested after cervical dislocation on day three.

Molecular docking simulation

The initial structure of the DC-SIGN protein was downloaded from the RCSB Protein Data Bank (PDB) with the serial number 1SL4. It was processed using AutoDock Tools 1.5.6 to maintain the original charge of the protein and to generate a pdbqt file for docking with the small molecule COS monomer (C12H24N2O9). Literature research indicates that the active pocket of the small molecule in the protein is located near the Ca2+ ion in the protein structure.

Molecular docking was performed using the AutoDock 4.2.6 software package. The center coordinates of the docking box were set to the center of the potentially active pockets in the protein. The number of grid points in each direction of XYZ was set to 60 × 60 × 60, and the number of docking runs was set to 50. Default values were used for the remaining parameters.

The results of molecular docking may contain unreasonable atomic contacts in the spatial structure, which can be alleviated through energy optimization to achieve a more stable structure. Energy optimization was conducted using the Amber14 force field. The optimization process consisted of two steps: first, the Steepest Descent Method (SDM) was employed for 1000 steps, followed by the use of the Conjugate Gradient Method (CGM) for a further 500 steps to refine the structure.

Detection of DC-SIGN mRNA

The mRNA expressions of DC-SIGN in cells were determined by qPCR. Total RNA was extracted from cells using the RNeasy Mini Kit (Qiagen). According to the manufacturers' protocols, cDNA was synthesized from the RNA using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (TaKaRa). Quantitative real-time PCR was performed on an Applied Biosystems Real-Time PCR system using SYBR Premix Ex Taq (TaKaRa), 2 μL of cDNA, and gene-specific primers (for sequences, see table). The relative expression of target genes was calculated using the comparative 2^−ΔΔCt method, with GAPDH serving as the endogenous reference for normalization. Primers were forward 5′-CTGCACAGTCTTCCTCTCCC-3′ and reverse 5′-TGGTACTGGGTAGATGGTTC A-3′,DC-SIGN; forward 5′-CACCATCTTCCAGGAGCGAG-3′ and reverse 5′-CCTTCTCCATGGTGGTGAAGA-3′, GAPDH.

Short-term depletion experiment of DC-SIGN+ cell subsets

To ascertain whether the transformation of monocytes into moDCs relies on the activation of the DC-SIGN pathway, we employed a strategy involving the injection of DT into DC-SIGN-DTR mice, thereby inducing a short-term elimination of the DC-SIGN+ cell subset. The specific administration protocol is illustrated in Fig. 5G.

First, DC-SIGN-DTR mice aged 6-8 weeks were randomly assigned to three groups: Vehicle, CCH, and DT. In the Vehicle group, only the solvent physiological saline used to dissolve DT was subcutaneously inoculated. The CCH group received the same treatment as in the previous experiment. The TCH and DT groups, which were vaccinated with uncomplexed tumor whole cell antigen, received intraperitoneal injections of DT at a dose of 20 ng per gram of mouse body weight, based on the CCH group. The timing for the three injections was as follows: the first dose of DT was administered on day 0, concurrently with the inoculation of Vehicle and CCH. The second injection of DT was administered on the 1st day, followed by the final injection of DT 6 h before harvesting the dermal tissue from the DC-SIGN-DTR mice on the 3rd day, achieving a transient depletion of the DC-SIGN+ cell subset.

On the 3rd day (consistent with the previous experimental design, 3 days after CCH inoculation), dermal tissue cells from the Vehicle and CCH injection sites were collected for flow cytometric analysis. Data analysis and graphing were performed using FlowJo and GraphPad Prism software, respectively.

Construction of primary liver cancer model and acquisition of pHCC antigens

We used a combination of hydrodynamic transfection technology and Sleeping Beauty (SB) transposon-mediated somatic cell integration to construct a primary hepatocellular carcinoma model to obtain primary tumor cells to act as high-quality tumor whole-cell antigens for TCH, the specific process is shown in Fig. S5. By rapidly injecting a plasmid DNA (encoding the oncogenes N-Ras and c-Myc) dilution equivalent to 10% of the body weight into the tail vein of 6- to 8-week-old C57 mice (within 5-9 s), we anticipated the induction of primary HCC formation, provided that the N-Ras, c-Myc, and SB ratio was maintained at 10:2:2 (5, 1, 1 μg/mL). Forty days after plasmid injection, once the tumor had taken shape, it was extracted from the liver and subjected to shearing in a digestive solution (containing type I DNase and type IV collagenase). The tumor blocks after removal and shearing were incubated in a constant temperature shaker at 37 °C, 170 rpm for 45 min, and then the erythrocytes were lysed, ground and filtered through a 70-μm cell sieve to obtain a single-cell suspension (pHCCs) for subsequent TCH preparation.

Uptake and presentation of antigens by TCH

pHCCs were labeled and stained with CellTracker™ Green CMFDA (5-chloromethylfluorescein diacetate) at a concentration of 10 μM for 30 min. After labeling, the cells were washed with PBS containing 2% FBS before being utilized for TCH preparation. It's important to note that for subsequent verification of adaptive immune activation, we incorporated 20 mg of high-quality simulated antigen ovalbumin peptide OVA257-264 into the pHCCs. The prepared TCH (100 μL) was then injected subcutaneously into mice in the same manner. A control group was established by directly injecting 1 × 106 labeled pHCCs (containing OVA257-264 20 mg per mouse). Due to the short local retention of the antigen at the injection site, we used a higher concentration of OVA peptide to ensure that antigen cross-presentation could also be detected in the control group. Three days after inoculation, dermal tissue and draining lymph nodes were collected from the mice for flow cytometry analysis. The "FITC" channel was utilized to detect fluorescence signals.

Magnetic bead sorting of OT-1 mouse T cells

Under sterile conditions, the spleens were extracted from OT-1 mice and placed in culture dishes containing RPMI 1640 medium. The spleen tissues were gently ground into a single-cell suspension through a 70 μm cell strainer, and the suspension was filtered through a 40 μm cell strainer to remove tissue debris. Red blood cells were lysed using red blood cell lysis buffer, followed by washing the cells with PBS containing 2% FBS, and centrifugation at 600g for 3 min to remove the supernatant. Cells were then counted under a microscope, and the cell density was adjusted to 2 × 107 cells/mL using RPMI 1640 medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and placed in a 15 mL centrifuge tube for culture.

Subsequently, magnetic bead washing was performed by vortexing Dynabeads™ Mouse T Activator CD3/CD28 (4 × 107 Beads/mL) to ensure uniform mixing. Beads were then extracted at a ratio of 1:1 with cells into new sterile 15 mL centrifuge tubes. After adding 2 mL of sterile PBS solution for washing, centrifugation was performed at 300 g at room temperature for 5 min, and the supernatant was discarded. Cells that were counted were then mixed with antibody-coated magnetic beads at a 1:1 ratio. The mixture was gently shaken every 10 min for 1 h at 4 °C or room temperature to ensure sufficient binding of the beads to the target cell surface molecules.

Finally, magnetic separation was performed by transferring the cell-bead mixture to sterile 5 mL flow tubes and placing them in a MojoSort™ Magnet for 5 min. Under the influence of the magnetic field, CD3-positive T cells labeled with magnetic beads were attracted and retained in the column or magnetic surface, while unbound cells were washed away.

In vivo specific immune activation verification experiment

CD3+ T cells sorted from the spleens of OT-1 mice were labeled with CFSE (Invitrogen™). Simultaneously, three days after TCH injection, the DC-SIGN+ cell population (moDC) was obtained by FACS sorting from the draining lymph nodes of mice. Subsequently, CFSE-labeled T cells were co-cultured with DC-SIGN+ cells, and flow cytometry was employed to observe the proliferation of T cells after 48 h. The wild-type here refers to the normal mice. The T cells do not proliferate in response to the stimulation with the OVA peptide segment.

Effect of moDCs on TCH in preventing primary tumor growth

Unlike when investigating the mechanism of moDCs (by short-term elimination of the DC-SIGN+ cell subset), to validate the effect of moDCs on preventing primary tumor growth by TCH, we employed a combined approach of short-term and long-term depletion of the DC-SIGN+ cell subset. In the short-term elimination phase, we administered three doses of DT with a 1-day interval followed by a 2-day interval, totaling three doses within a short period (at a dosage of 20 ng DT/g mouse body weight). Subsequently, over the next 35 days, mice received one dose of DT every 7 days, totaling five doses, to achieve long-term elimination of the DC-SIGN+ cell subset in DC-SIGN-DTR mice. For the prevention experiment, we continued to use the CCH system directly with antigen-only pHCCs for validation. The relevant dosing regimen is shown in Fig. S9, and experiments were conducted with the same timing intervals as described above: inoculation of TCH and injection of plasmid DNA, followed by sample collection 40 days after plasmid injection to obtain liver tissue for photography.

Experiments on tumor growth prevention by TCH in a PDX model

Clinical colorectal cancer tumor blocks were used with informed signed consent from patients and their families, and the principles of GCP and The Code of Ethics of the World Medical Association (Declaration of Helsinki) were fully complied with in the study, and all procedures were carried out in accordance with the relevant laws and institutional guidelines, and were reported to the Second Affiliated Hospital of Zhejiang University School of Medicine, Human Research Ethics Committee for approval, with the review date of April 16th, 2024, and the event acceptance number of I2024419, and the project acceptance number is Research 2024-0272.

Tumor volumes were measured using calipers, and volume was calculated using the formula: Volume = (Length x Width2)/2. Tumor growth rates were compared between the PBS and TCH-treated groups over the study period. Survival time was recorded for each mouse, defined as the time from tumor inoculation to humane endpoint or natural death. Humane endpoint criteria included a tumor volume exceeding 1500 mm3, significant weight loss (>20%), or signs of distress or pain. Survival curves were generated, and statistical analysis was performed using the log-rank test.

TCH therapeutic experiment in 4T1 mouse breast cancer model

To evaluate the therapeutic efficacy of TCH in the 4T1 breast cancer model, female BALB/c mice (6-8 weeks old) were used, following approval from the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) and adhering to institutional guidelines for animal care. Mice were subcutaneously inoculated with 1 × 106 4T1-Luc (luciferase-expressing 4T1) tumor cells into the right flank on day −14. Tumors were allowed to establish over a period of 14 days, after which they were surgically resected on day 0 under aseptic conditions. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane, and tumors were carefully excised, ensuring minimal impact on surrounding tissues.

Post-surgery, mice were randomly assigned into two groups: the Vehicle group (PBS) and the TCH-treated group. TCH was administered according to the experimental design, while the Vehicle group received an equivalent volume of PBS. The administration route, dosage, and frequency of TCH treatment were based on optimized protocols established in preliminary studies.

Bioluminescent imaging was performed on days −1, 0, 7, 14, and 21 to monitor tumor development and recurrence. Mice were injected intraperitoneally with D-luciferin (150 mg/kg) and anesthetized with isoflurane. Bioluminescent signals were captured using the IVIS Spectrum Imaging System (PerkinElmer). The bioluminescence radiance (photons/sec/cm2/sr) was quantified using Living Image software (PerkinElmer).

Statistical analysis

All quantitative data were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD). Statistical significance was determined using Student's t-test or one-way ANOVA followed by post hoc tests for multiple comparisons. A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
