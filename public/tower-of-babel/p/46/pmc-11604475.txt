Exaggerated Pressure Recovery during Pregnancy: A Multimodal Approach to Assessment of Prosthetic Aortic Stenosis Severity
Authors: Brynn Connor, Makoto Takei, Jennifer Woo, Desireé Conrad, Rahul Sharma, George Lui
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Aortic stenosis, Pregnancy, Pressure recovery, Patient-prosthesis mismatch, Aortic valve replacement

Introduction

Most patients with mild to moderate aortic stenosis (AS) tolerate pregnancy well. Those with severe AS, however, are at an increased risk for maternal and fetal complications, including heart failure, prematurity, and low birth weight.1 Pregnancy represents a high cardiac output state, with aortic valve (AV) gradients expected to increase nearly 2-fold by the third trimester.2 When measured AV gradients exceed that expected from pregnancy, potential considerations include progressive AV disease, pressure recovery, and altered aortic biomechanics following prosthetic aortic graft replacement. Pressure recovery is more commonly observed with moderate or worse AS and small aortic root dimensions, and additionally may be more pronounced following prosthetic aortic graft replacement.3, 4, 5 We present a case of exaggerated pressure recovery in a pregnant woman with a history of bioprosthetic AV and ascending aorta graft replacement, and highlight the potential limitations of Doppler-based measurement of transaortic gradients under high cardiac output states and with altered aortic biomechanics following surgical aortic graft replacement.

Case Presentation

A 43-year-old G3P2 woman with a history of mosaic Turner syndrome and prior AV replacement with a 23-mm Edwards pericardial bioprosthesis and ascending aorta replacement with a 26-mm Hemashield vascular graft for management of bicuspid AV disease was followed by cardiology for management during pregnancy. Following initial AV surgery 13 years prior, the patient's prosthetic valve function remained stable on serial echocardiographic evaluation, with a mean transaortic Doppler gradient of 10 to 15 mm Hg and indexed effective orifice area (EOAi) of 0.87 cm2/m2. The patient's first pregnancy 2 years prior was overall uncomplicated, with ultimate elective cesarean delivery at 37 weeks gestation. However, there was a notable increase in the mean transaortic Doppler gradients (30-40 mm Hg) during the first pregnancy, which persisted on postpartum follow-up. This progressive rise in transaortic Doppler gradients was ascribed to structural valve deterioration, with low overall clinical suspicion for valve thrombosis. However, apart from repeat magnetic resonance angiography performed for aortic graft surveillance, no additional advanced imaging was obtained prior to the second pregnancy to definitively evaluate for valve thrombus.

On initial evaluation prior to the second pregnancy, the patient remained asymptomatic and without clinical evidence of heart failure. Physical examination was notable for a late peaking, III/VI, systolic ejection murmur at the left upper sternal border, with radiation to the bilateral carotid arteries. Repeat Doppler echocardiographic assessment demonstrated continued evidence of prosthetic AV dysfunction (mean prosthetic AV gradient: 30 mm Hg, dimensionless index [DI], 0.28; acceleration time, 93 msec; and EOAi, 0.5 cm2/m2), albeit with good overall left ventricular (LV) contractile reserve, a low estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure (30 mm Hg at peak exercise), and normal blood pressure response documented on exercise stress echocardiography. On follow-up during the second and third trimesters of pregnancy, however, there was a progressive increase in the mean transaortic Doppler gradients, reaching 50 mm Hg at 28 weeks gestation and ultimately 100 mm Hg at 32 weeks gestation (DI, 0.19; acceleration time, 120 msec; EOAi, 0.3 cm2/m2; energy loss index [ELI], 0.4 cm2/m2). Although the LV systolic function remained preserved, the patient developed progressive dyspnea on exertion with evidence of symptomatic volume overload (N-terminal pro-B-type natriuretic peptide, 445 pg/mL). The patient was therefore hospitalized for intravenous diuresis, and following further medical heart failure optimization ultimately underwent a cesarean section at 32 weeks gestation, without complication.

The patient was initially monitored in the cardiac intensive care unit following delivery and ultimately transferred to the floor after 24 hours of observation, with stable hemodynamics. However, on a repeat postpartum echocardiogram obtained prior to hospital discharge, the mean transaortic Doppler gradient remained persistently elevated at 120 mm Hg (Figure 1, Video 1).

(A) Two-dimensional transthoracic echocardiogram, parasternal short-axis diastolic view acquired in the early postpartum period demonstrates a well-seated aortic bioprosthesis with thickened and calcified prosthetic valve leaflets; (B) continuous-wave Doppler obtained from the suprasternal notch acquisition window using a nonimaging probe demonstrates a peak systolic velocity of 6.8 m/sec (mean gradient >100 mm Hg), consistent with severe prosthetic AS.

Given concern for potential adverse maternal cardiac outcomes while awaiting later definitive surgical intervention, the patient was referred for invasive hemodynamic assessment and consideration of transcatheter balloon valvuloplasty and/or transcatheter AV replacement. However, on catheter-based evaluation, the mean calculated pressure gradient, as assessed on direct pullback measurement while under moderate sedation, was only 60 mm Hg (Figure 2).

Invasive hemodynamic assessment of AS severity via direct pullback measurement from the ascending aorta to the LV outflow tract acquired in the early postpartum period. The peak-to-peak systolic AV gradient and mean transaortic gradient (area under the curve, shown in gray) were both approximately 60 mm Hg.

Balloon valvuloplasty was therefore deferred, with planned surgical AV replacement following additional postpartum recovery. On postpartum follow-up, the mean transaortic Doppler gradient gradually declined, reaching a nadir of 60 mm Hg at 8 weeks postpartum. At 3 months postpartum, the patient ultimately underwent mechanical AV and ascending aorta replacement with a 21 mm composite valved graft. The intraoperative transesophageal echocardiogram (Videos 2 and 3) and preoperative computed tomography (Figure 3, Video 4) demonstrated severely thickened and calcified bioprosthetic valve leaflets, with no evident valve thrombus. Significant subannular pannus formation was additionally noted on operative inspection.

Contrast-enhanced multidetector computed tomography of the aortic bioprosthesis, transcatheter AV implantation protocol using retrospective electrocardiographic-gating, volume-rendered, short-axis “en-face” display in systole (A) and diastole (B), demonstrates severely thickened and calcified prosthetic valve leaflets (white arrows) with resultant reduced systolic excursion; the dropout artifact along the body of the leftward-nonfacing and rightward-facing leaflets are from leaflet calcification.

The patient's postoperative course was complicated by complete heart block, for which a transvenous permanent pacemaker was placed prior to hospital discharge. A repeat transthoracic echocardiogram at hospital discharge demonstrated stable function of the mechanical aortic prosthesis (mean transaortic Doppler gradient of 8 mm Hg) and preserved LV systolic function.

Discussion

This case highlights an impressive example of pressure recovery leading to falsely elevated prosthetic AV gradients by Doppler echocardiography. Without invasive measurement of the AV gradient, this asymptomatic woman may have undergone unnecessary transcatheter intervention with small, but serious, risks of aortic regurgitation and heart failure. In patients with prosthetic AS and/or small aortic root dimensions, poor agreement between Doppler-derived gradients and catheterization-based measurements of AS severity has been documented in upward of 10% to 15% of patients.3 The reason for this discrepancy is likely multifactorial, encompassing the effects of technical measurement errors (i.e., poor acoustic windows, use of single imaging planes, suboptimal Doppler alignment), systematic measurement errors (i.e., improper application of the simplified Bernoulli equation with concomitant subvalvar obstruction or high cardiac output states), and variable physiologic conditions at the time of assessment (i.e., sedative/analgesic use during cardiac catheterization).6

Pressure recovery is another important phenomenon that can result in significant overestimation of catheter-based gradients by Doppler echocardiography.3 In the setting of a stenotic AV, potential energy is converted to kinetic energy at the vena contracta, which represents the point of maximal velocity and lowest pressure. As the velocity again decreases distal to the stenosis, a portion of this kinetic energy is reconverted back to potential energy, resulting in pressure “recovery” more distally in the aorta.7 Continuous-wave Doppler echocardiography samples the peak velocity at the vena contracta and thus captures the highest transvalvular pressure drop. Conversely, catheter-based techniques measure the aortic pressure distal to the vena contracta, resulting in lower transvalvular gradient estimates.7

Why was the magnitude of pressure recovery so significant in this case? The magnitude of pressure recovery is largely determined by the ratio of the effective valve orifice area to the cross-sectional area of the distal aorta, as these 2 factors predominantly dictate the degree of flow separation and turbulence.3,5 Therefore, pressure recovery was likely more pronounced due to prior prosthetic aortic graft replacement and significant baseline prosthetic valve stenosis. Prosthetic aortic graft replacement can significantly alter distal aortic biomechanics due to decreased aortic graft distensibility.5 Mechanistically, the smaller, nondistensible aortic graft minimizes the loss of “recoverable” kinetic energy that is otherwise dissipated distally in more compliant or dilated aortas.5 Therefore, for a given valve EOAi, the magnitude of pressure recovery observed with prosthetic aortic grafts will be higher, yielding more pronounced differences between transvalvular Doppler and catheter-based gradients. With concomitant prosthetic valve stenosis, proximal turbulent kinetic energy is amplified, similarly yielding a higher transvalvular pressure drop.3,7 Thus, the altered aortic flow dynamics incurred with a reduced prosthetic valve EOAi and prosthetic aortic graft replacement can exaggerate pressure recovery and consequentially impact the accuracy of transaortic Doppler measurements.3, 4, 5

As current recommendations from the 2020 American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association guidelines for management of valvular heart disease still largely rely on Doppler echocardiographic measures of AS severity,8 increasing attention has been given to the development and implementation of Doppler parameters adjusted for pressure recovery. One commonly utilized parameter is the ELI, a well-validated adjustment of the Doppler-derived AV area (AVA) for the size of the ascending aorta {ELI = [AVA × Aa/(Aa – AVA)]/body surface area, where Aa is the cross-sectional area of the ascending aorta}.9 The ELI-based classification of AS severity utilizes the same cutoff values as standard Doppler derived parameters (severe AS = ELI ≤ 0.6 cm2/m2), with an ELI that is discordant and larger than the unadjusted AVA thereby suggesting nonsevere AS.10 In addition to reclassifying AS severity, use of the ELI has been shown to have incremental prognostic value in the prediction of adverse cardiovascular outcomes when compared to conventional Doppler estimates of AS severity.10

Pregnancy represents another important altered hemodynamic state that can potentially impact the interpretation of Doppler-based measures of AS severity. In order to meet the metabolic demands of the mother and fetus, significant cardiovascular physiologic changes are incurred during pregnancy, with typical peak effects observed at the beginning of the third trimester.11 This includes a 35% to 40% drop in systemic vascular resistance, 20% to 25% increase in heart rate, 45% increase in circulating blood volume, and a corresponding 45% increase in cardiac output.11 Pregnancy therefore fundamentally represents a high cardiac output state, with a nearly 2-fold physiologic increase in transaortic Doppler gradients observed early in the third trimester.2 Given this expected increase in flow-dependent parameters, conventional Doppler gradients should be interpreted with caution during pregnancy, and use of flow-independent echocardiographic parameters (i.e., ELI, DI) and complementary diagnostic modalities, including invasive catheter-based measurements, may be required to guide management of severe AS.

Although this observation of more pronounced pressure recovery during pregnancy has not been previously documented in the literature, pregnancy is a known risk factor for accelerated bioprosthetic valve degeneration.12 In the present case, although the transaortic Doppler gradients were exaggerated when compared to invasive hemodynamic assessment, there was additional evidence of progressive prosthetic valve dysfunction during both pregnancies, as demonstrated by the serial change in several flow-independent echocardiographic parameters (EOAi, ELI, and DI). Accelerated bioprosthetic AV deterioration is not uncommon during pregnancy, with a documented incidence of upward of 50% and corresponding requirement for reoperation in 25% to 50% of affected patients during the peripartum period.12 Proposed pathophysiologic mechanisms include valve thrombosis, given the inherent hypercoagulability associated with pregnancy, as well as accelerated valve degeneration attributable to pregnancy-related hemodynamic changes.11 However, more recent studies have challenged this notion, specifically finding similar rates of structural valve deterioration in women of childbearing age with or without prior pregnancy.12,13 While the association between pregnancy and bioprosthetic valve degeneration may be partially explained by age, with accelerated rates of tissue valve deterioration commonly observed in younger patients, further investigation is warranted to definitively establish the causal mechanisms behind this association.

Women with mild to moderate AS typically do well during pregnancy. Even those with severe AS have a low overall maternal mortality rate (<1%).2 However, substantial maternal and fetal morbidity is still incurred, with upward of 15% of pregnant women with severe AS developing symptomatic heart failure.1 Management of severe AS during pregnancy is typically supportive, although transcatheter balloon valvuloplasty and/or transcatheter AV replacement can be safely performed in the setting of severe, symptomatic AS refractory to medical management.14 Surgical AV replacement can alternatively be considered, although is often deferred to the postpartum period given the high rates of fetal complications, including prematurity and fetal demise (35%).15

Conclusion

Pressure recovery is a well-documented phenomenon in prosthetic AS and can account for discrepancies in transaortic Doppler gradients and direct catheter-based pressure measurements. In the presence of concurrent prosthetic valve stenosis, prior aortic graft replacement, and/or altered physiologic hemodynamic states, the magnitude of pressure recovery is even more pronounced, with resultant significant overestimation of AS severity by conventional Doppler measurements. In these clinical scenarios, complementary use of multiple diagnostic modalities, including invasive hemodynamic assessment, may be required to inform medical decision-making and minimize downstream adverse maternal and fetal health outcomes.
