Imaging the mechanical properties of a developing embryonic tendon using Brillouin microscopy
Authors: Divya Sivalingam, Kamryn Henderson, Aniket Jana, Phong Nguyen, Giuliano Scarcelli, Catherine K Kuo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTendon mechanical properties are critical for proper musculoskeletal movement. Yet, current understanding of how tendons develop their mechanical properties remains incomplete. Atomic force microscopy and tensile testing are used to characterize early-stage and late-stage embryonic tendon mechanical properties respectively, but both require contact with the tissue, which can alter or destroy tissue structure or integrity and render the tissue unsuitable for subsequent assays. Brillouin confocal microscopy is an emerging tool used to measure mechanical properties of tissues in an all-optical, contact-free, label-free manner. Here, Brillouin confocal microscopy was successfully implemented to measure chick embryo Achilles tendon mechanical properties, which were then validated by comparisons to previously published tensile modulus data. Significant changes in mechanical properties that occur with development and due to experimentally induced changes in extracellular matrix crosslinking were quantifiable. Brillouin microscopy also detected increasing mechanical heterogeneity of developing tendons. The potential to map the spatial distribution of the mechanical properties of developing extracellular matrix using Brillouin microscopy was demonstrated. Measurement of mechanical properties of the tendon within the limb, and sensitivity to changes in tendon strain imposed by changing ankle flexion demonstrated the potential for Brillouin microscopy to quantify tendon properties in vivo. Collectively, these results provide an exciting first study using Brillouin microscopy for quantitative and spatial mapping of embryonic tendon mechanical properties in an all-optical, label-free manner, which could lead to mechanistic discoveries not possible with conventional mechanical testing methodologies.Keywords: Brillouin microscopy, Tendon, Embryo, Mechanical properties, Tensile testing

Introduction

Tendons are highly collagenous tissues that enable skeletal movement by transferring muscle-generated forces to bone. Despite their important function, tendons experience ruptures at a relatively high frequency, are prone to chronic injuries (tendinopathy), and are involved in common birth abnormalities such as clubfoot and hip dysplasia [1–4]. Unfortunately, tendons lack the ability to regenerate and there are no effective treatments that regenerate the tendon. The primary function of tendon is mechanical in nature, thus elucidating how tendon mechanical properties develop could enable the development of therapeutic approaches to treat tendon injuries, tendinopathies, and birth abnormalities.

Studies with the chick embryo have demonstrated that the Achilles tendon evolves from a highly cellular tissue into a collagen-rich tissue with highly organized collagen fibers [5,6], during which time the elastic modulus increases non-linearly [5,7]. We performed perturbation studies to discover that lysyl oxidase (LOX)-mediated crosslinking of collagen imparts tendon with its mechanical properties during collagen deposition and remodeling [5,8]. As reflected by these studies, the chick embryo is a well-established model to study musculoskeletal tissue development. Distinct advantages of the chick embryo model include its significant structural and molecular overlap with mammalian musculoskeletal tissue development, compact size, rapid development in only 21 days, and that it develops in its own vessel [5,9–12]. The latter enables direct access through the eggshell for perturbations and imaging, in contrast to the complex surgery required to gain access to an embryo or fetus in utero, such as with rodent and other commonly used models in tendon development research.

For the studies described above, we developed methodology specific for the chick embryo Achilles tendon to reliably identify, grip, and uniaxially tensile test the tendon from embryonic day (D) 14, which corresponds to mid-development, until right before hatching [7,13,14]. Uniaxial tensile testing is the conventional “gold standard” technique to mechanically test adult tendons, which involves ramp-to-failure testing to quantify ultimate strength and tensile modulus [15]. However, at stages of development earlier than D14, the chick embryo Achilles tendon is too fragile and short to grip for tensile testing. To overcome this challenge, we adapted force-volume atomic force microscopy (AFM) methodology to quantify the nano- and microscale indentation elastic moduli of the developing Achilles tendon from early to late chick embryo stages [5]. Notably, elastic modulus exhibited a similar non-linear trend over the same developmental stages with both tensile testing and AFM [5,7].

We have focused primarily on the Achilles tendon in our studies, as the Achilles tendon is highly susceptible to acute and chronic injuries postnatally, and its abnormal development is involved in clubfoot and other common birth abnormalities [1–3]. In contrast, others have focused on longer hindlimb tendons such as flexor, extensor, and metatarsal tendons [11,12,16–18]. Embryonic flexor and extensor tendons are substantially longer than Achilles tendon – for example, the chick embryo hindlimb extensor tendon is 15 to 20 mm long from D14 to D17 of development, whereas the Achilles tendon is 1 to 3 mm long at the same stages [7,17]. Thus, the longer flexor and extensor tendons are relatively easy to dissect and grip for tensile testing and can provide material for multiple assays. In contrast, the shorter chick embryo Achilles tendon is more difficult to dissect reliably, and requires pooling from multiple embryos to obtain sufficient sample mass for molecular and protein biology assays. A study that includes both mechanical testing and biological assays essentially doubles or triples the number of embryos, labor, and time needed for the study. The ability to use the same Achilles tendon for multiple assays, such as mechanical testing followed by a biological assay on the same tendon, would dramatically reduce the number of embryos for a study. AFM and tensile testing require contact with the tissue and alter or destroy tissue structure or integrity, rendering the tissue unsuitable for subsequent assays that require preservation of tissue sterility or microstructure. Further, longitudinal studies to monitor how the same tissue’s mechanical properties change over time is not possible with AFM or tensile testing. To overcome these limitations, we propose to use Brillouin microscopy, an all-optical technique, to measure chick embryo Achilles tendon mechanical properties.

Brillouin microscopy is an all-optical, contact-free method that can determine tissue mechanical properties via light scattering spectroscopy. Confocal Brillouin microscopy uses a focused laser beam and a highly sensitive spectrometer (based on a virtually imaged phased array, VIPA etalon) that allows for Brillouin imaging at various depths within tissues [19]. The underlying principles of Brillouin microscopy are rooted in Brillouin scattering of light, which occurs due to the inelastic scattering of incident light with inherent thermodynamic density or pressure fluctuations in materials (acoustic phonons) [19]. The inelastic interaction within the material leads to a frequency shift in the scattered light, due to the exchange in energy between light and acoustic phonons. The frequency shift of the scattered light, also referred to as the Brillouin shift, is proportional to the longitudinal elastic modulus of the material [20]. Longitudinal elastic modulus is a measure of elastic modulus wherein a material undergoes a purely uniaxial deformation in response to a purely uniaxial force, unlike tensile modulus wherein the material can deform in directions different from the applied stress. In addition, Brillouin microscopy measures the longitudinal elastic modulus at a high frequency (GHz), since it acts as a mechanical test performed at the frequency of the measured Brillouin shift. In terms of intensity, for a water-based material, the scattered light is ten orders of magnitude lower than the incident light. In contrast to AFM and tensile testing, confocal Brillouin microscopy can image mechanical properties at various depths into the tissue without the requirement of physical tissue sectioning and preserve the tissue for subsequent assays [19]. The preservation of precious, delicate samples with Brillouin microscopy enables robust paired analyses to compare and correlate mechanical properties with other tendon properties. Furthermore, this reduces animal numbers, which is non-trivial as tiny embryo tissues typically require pooling from multiple animals to comprise one biological replicate. The all-optical and non-contact nature of Brillouin microscopy also enables longitudinal studies with the same tissue or animal.

Embryonic tissues that are highly cellular are optically transparent, which makes them conducive to optical measurements like those made by Brillouin microscopy. Brillouin microscopy has been utilized to study the biomechanics of early-stage embryonic tissues, including the neural tube, neural folds, and early-stage heart in murine embryos [21,22]. Additionally, Brillouin microscopy has been used to perform longitudinal studies for tissue mechanical characterization during neural tube closure in early development of chick embryos [23]. Brillouin microscopy has also been employed to perform longitudinal mechanical characterizations of Drosophila embryos during tissue folding events associated with gastrulation, early stages of ascidian embryo development, and mouse embryos during blastocyst formation [24].

Here, we developed methodology using Brillouin microscopy to mechanically characterize developing chick embryonic Achilles tendons. We examined the ability for Brillouin microscopy to optically quantify the mechanical properties of developing chick embryo tendons at a high spatial resolution and validated these results against tensile modulus. We also characterized the utility of Brillouin microscopy to resolve mechanically distinct microstructures and detect changes in tendon mechanical properties associated with collagen extracellular matrix (ECM) elaboration as well as experimentally perturbed ECM crosslinking. Finally, we examined the potential for Brillouin microscopy to probe mechanical properties of the Achilles tendon attached to the limb and evaluated the sensitivity of Brillouin measurements to mechanical tension associated with different ankle conformations. Collectively, our observations establish Brillouin microscopy as an innovative technique for contact-free and label-free measurement of developing embryonic tendon mechanical properties.

Materials and methods

Chick embryo culture and tissue harvest

Fertilized White Leghorn chicken eggs were obtained from the Department of Animal and Avian sciences at University of Maryland and cultured in a humidified rocking incubator maintained at 37.5 °C and 55 % humidity. All animal procedures with day 17 and older chick embryos received prior approval from the University of Maryland Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee Board under protocol 2261546–2 and are in compliance with NIH guidelines.

Chick embryos were euthanized by decapitation at stages-of-interest, from Hamburger Hamilton (HH) 35 to HH45 (day 9 to 19 of development; the chick embryo hatches at approximately day 21). Hindlimbs were separated from the body above the knee joint, and one limb was allocated for Brillouin microscopy, tensile testing, or staining, unless otherwise noted. Embryos were staged based on the length of the third toe [25]. Hindlimbs were harvested and skinned carefully, to avoid damage to the tendon. The Achilles tendon was identified and marked on each end using Verhoeff stain, as previously described [7]. The tendon was dissected out using a pair of sharp microscissors by transecting at the Verhoeff markers, near the myotendinous junction on one end and the enthesis on the other end. Dissections were performed while submerging the limb in physiological saline (0.9 % w/v sodium chloride, Sigma-Aldrich S9888) to maintain tissue hydration. For tensile testing, intact, whole tendons were harvested with excess muscle and cartilage on the ends to enable gripping within custom clamps, as we previously described [7,13,14]. For Brillouin and fluorescence microscopy, individual bundles from the tendon were extracted using a pair of fine tweezers, as described in our previous study [13]. Since HH35 tendons are too fragile to dissect out, they were imaged while still attached to the limb. To study the effects of ankle flexion on tendon mechanical properties, HH41 tendons were imaged while still attached to the limb using Brillouin microscopy.

Inhibition of collagen crosslinking

HH42 chick embryos were treated with β-aminopropionitrile (BAPN, Sigma-Aldrich Cat# A3134) to inhibit lysyl oxidase (LOX) activity for 24 h and inhibit new collagen crosslink formation during development, as previously described [5,8,26]. A small hole was made over the aircell, through which 200 μL of 5mg/g embryo weight BAPN or saline (vehicle) was injected in ovo. Chick embryos were euthanized 24 h after treatment. Whole tendons were tensile tested and tendon bundles were imaged by Brillouin microscopy.

Photoactivated collagen crosslinking

HH40 chick embryos were euthanized, and either whole tendons or tendon bundles were extracted. Tendons or tendon bundles were soaked for 20 min in a 0.1 % solution of riboflavin in phosphate-buffered saline (without Calcium or Magnesium) supplemented with 10 % dextran, and then exposed to UVA light (365 nm wavelength, 9 mW/cm2) for 10 min while still soaking in the riboflavin solution, as previously described [27]. Tissues were then washed in fresh saline to remove excess riboflavin solution. Untreated tendons served as controls. Treated and contralateral untreated whole tendons from the same embryo were tensile tested. Tendon bundles were imaged by Brillouin microscopy before (untreated control) and after UVA-riboflavin treatment.

Brillouin confocal microscopy

A confocal Brillouin microscope [28,29] with a single mode 660 nm continuous wave laser with power of ~ 30 mW, and an objective lens (Olympus, 20 × /0.4 NA) installed on an inverted microscope (Olympus, IX81) was used to image samples. Backscattered Brillouin signal was collected by the objective and analyzed using a two-stage VIPA (Light Machinery, 15 GHz FSR) based spectrometer. The Brillouin spectrum was recorded by an EMCCD camera (Andor, iXon 897) with an exposure time of 50 ms. Region of interest (ROI), ROI size, and pixel size were entered into a custom program (LabView). The scan time per pixel was approximately 0.05 s. Scan times for Brillouin scans presented in this manuscript ranged from 20 s to 33.33 min, dependent on pixel size and ROI size. Scan times for each experiment are specified in the supplement (Tables S1, S2, S3). Tendons were scanned at a pixel size of 0.5 μm × 0.5 μm to 2 μm × 2 μm, with pixel size for each experiment specified in the corresponding figure caption, as well as in the supplement (Tables S1, S2, S3).

Tendon bundles were laid on the glass surface of a petri dish and submerged in physiological saline. A small metal ring with 5 mm inner diameter was laid carefully on top of the bundle to restrict it from floating during Brillouin measurements. The sample was oriented such that incident light was perpendicular to the longitudinal axis of the tendon (Fig. 1). When imaging with the confocal Brillouin microscope, the major axis of the cross-sectional area (CSA) of the tendon was considered the x-axis, the minor axis of the CSA the z-axis, and the length of the tendon the y-axis (Fig. 1A and B). For maximum depth determination studies, the tendon was imaged in the x-z plane (Fig. 1C–E), and for all other experiments the tendon was imaged along its axis of tension, in the x-y plane (Fig. 1F).

For ankle flexion studies, HH41 limbs were separated from the body at the hip joint and skinned to expose the Achilles tendon. Two vertical supports were glued onto a glass dish and the limb was positioned to hold the ankle in either dorsiflexion (20° fixed angle) or plantarflexion (140° fixed angle). The Achilles tendon faced the glass surface with the objective lens perpendicular to the long axis of the tendon. Legs were submerged in saline solution to ensure tissue hydration during imaging.

Brillouin shift calculation

Brillouin microscopy is based on the principle of an incident photon interacting with the acoustic phonons of a material causing a frequency shift (vB) of the scattered light. This frequency shift is called the Brillouin shift, and the measured intensity of the scattered light is called the Brillouin signal intensity. where n is the refractive index, λ is the incident wavelength, ρ is the density of the material, M is the longitudinal elastic modulus of the material, and θ is the angle of incident light [19,23,30]. Since we used a backscattering configuration with θ = 180°, λ = 660 nm, and if the n/ρ ratio can be approximated as uniform in tissues [31], then Brillouin shift directly relates to high frequency (GHz) longitudinal elastic modulus [19,32]. Longitudinal elastic modulus is defined as the ratio of the uniaxial stress to the uniaxial strain of a material when there is no deformation in the direction perpendicular to the stress applied [33].

The Brillouin shift map for each ROI within a tendon was color-coded, with warmer colors indicating a higher Brillouin shift, and thus higher longitudinal elastic modulus. The average Brillouin shift (reported in GHz) of a biological replicate was calculated by first averaging the Brillouin shifts of all the ROIs in a tendon bundle, and then averaging the Brillouin shifts for all the tendon bundles within a biological replicate. In cases where the whole tendon was imaged, the average Brillouin shift for a tendon was determined by averaging all scans per biological replicate.

Maximum scanning depth determination for Brillouin microscopy

One representative x-z scan (Fig. 1C–E) per biological replicate was selected for maximum scanning depth determination. For each scan, an ROI with a width (in the x-direction) of 20 μm, and depth (in the z-direction, with z = 0 being the glass-tissue interface) that covered the entire tendon bundle cross-section was selected. ROI width (in the x-direction) of 20 μm was chosen to ensure analysis was performed only within the tendon, to minimize potential artifacts that could result from including the tendon-saline interface. The standard deviation of the Brillouin shift and the average Brillouin intensity were determined at depths from 0 to 100 μm in increments of 10 μm. The average intensity at which the Brillouin shift standard deviation began to increase substantially, called the “minimum intensity”, was determined for each scan. The median minimum intensity was then determined for each stage, and the greatest median minimum intensity across all stages analyzed was set as the required threshold for a stable Brillouin shift map. The maximum scanning depth was then determined by locating the corresponding depth for each stage.

Tensile testing

Non-tendon tissues on each end of the marked whole tendon were glued (Loctite, Connecticut, USA) to sandpaper using our previously reported protocol [7] and submerged in saline until tensile testing. For testing, sandpaper-covered ends were mounted between custom grips in an Instron 5542 tester (Instron corporation, Norwood, USA). Images were taken of the tendon major axis (widest portion of the tendon) and minor axis to determine CSA of the mounted tendon. To calculate the CSA, ImageJ was used to measure the major and minor axes at 25 %, 50 %, and 75 % along the tendon length. The tendon was preloaded to 0.01 N to establish gauge length, and tensile tested by uniaxial stretching at a rate of 1 % strain per second to failure. Initial gauge length was recorded. Force and displacement data were converted to stress and strain, respectively, based on the initial tendon CSA, and gauge length, as previously described [7,13,14]. Tensile modulus, in this paper, was calculated as the slope of the linear region of the stress-strain curve, after the toe region, as previously described [7].

Collagen and nuclear staining

Tendon bundles isolated from HH40, HH41, HH43, and HH44 chick embryos were stained for collagen using 10 μM Col-F (MyBioSource Cat# MBS258089) and cell nuclei using vendor-recommended concentration of Hoechst 33342 (Thermofisher Cat# R37605) in saline, for 1 h with gentle rocking in a dark chamber. Samples were then washed for 5 min in fresh saline with gentle rocking and then imaged with confocal fluorescence microscopy at the midpoint of the tissue long axis.

Confocal fluorescence microscopy

Confocal fluorescence microscopy of Col-F and Hoechst-stained tissues was performed at a depth of 30 μm into the tissue, at the midpoint of the long axis of the tendon. Tendons were imaged on an Olympus FV3000 confocal microscope with a 20×0.75 NA air objective using the 405 nm and 488 nm laser lines to detect cell nuclei (Hoechst) and collagen fibers (Col-F), with a resolution of 0.27 μm and 0.32 μm respectively. Brillouin imaging was subsequently performed on the same tendon bundle at the same tissue depth and similar location along the bundle length.

Confocal fluorescence and Brillouin shift line scans

To reduce noise in the Brillouin shift map, the 3-D median filter function in MATLAB was applied to the Brillouin shift maps (Image Processing Toolbox, The MathWorks Inc., Natick, Massachusetts, USA). A code was generated using MATLAB software to plot the Brillouin shift along a single horizontal line at 50 % of the height of a Brillouin shift map, with a reading taken every 0.5 μm along the line. For confocal fluorescence maps, the same procedure as for the Brillouin shift maps was followed to produce a line scan of the intensity of the collagen fibers.

Statistical analysis

A biological replicate (N) was a tendon or tendon bundle from a single embryo, with both tendons from one embryo belonging to the same biological replicate. Numbers of biological and technical replicates for each Brillouin microscopy experiment are listed in the supplement (Tables S1, S2, S3). Statistical comparisons were performed using GraphPad prism 10.0.2 (GraphPad Software, Boston, USA), unless otherwise specified. A one-way ANOVA with Holm-Sidak’s multiple comparisons was performed to compare the average Brillouin shift across developmental stages. An F-test was performed, using R Statistical Software (v4.3.2; R Core Team 2023) to compare the variances of Brillouin shift spread across developmental stages. Strength of the correlation between average Brillouin shift and our previously published tensile modulus [7] across developmental stages was determined by Pearson’s correlation coefficient. Unpaired t-test was used to compare the average Brillouin shift or tensile moduli of BAPN-treated and control tendon bundles, and to compare the tensile moduli of photocrosslinked and control tendons. A paired t-test was used to compare the average Brillouin shifts of photocrosslinked and control tendon bundles, and of tendon ankle flexion experimental groups. Error bars represent standard deviations. A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Measured Brillouin signal strength is independent of developmental stage

We first determined the maximum scanning depth of the tendon at which the Brillouin signal can be reliably detected. Brillouin shift becomes harder to detect at higher depths in a tissue due to the progressive decay of the signal deeper within tissues, caused by scattering and absorption of the light by cells and ECM [32]. Because tendons become increasingly collagen-rich over the course of development [5,8], we characterized the Brillouin signal decay profile of embryonic tendons from early to late developmental stages. We generated x-z scans of tendon bundles to capture the Brillouin shifts and signal intensities throughout cross-sections of tendons at developmental stages HH38, HH40, HH41, HH42, HH43, HH44, and HH45 (Fig. 2A). The length of the minor axis of tendon cross-section was found to be approximately 100 to 200 μm, with later stage tendons growing larger, which agreed with previously published characterizations of tendon bundles [34]. The images showed a rapid decrease in Brillouin shift deeper within the bundles, which we attributed to the emergence of imaging artifacts with increasing depth (Fig. 2A), thus we restricted our analyses to the first 100 μm. Across developmental stages, tendon bundles exhibited similar exponential decays in Brillouin signal intensity with increasing scan depth (Fig. 2B). The similar decay rate for tendons of all developmental stages characterized suggests that tissue opacity does not change substantially between HH38 and HH45. Interestingly, the rate of Brillouin signal intensity decay was lower for HH42 compared to other stages, which will require further investigation. The x-z scans of HH38, HH41, and HH45 tendons, representing early, middle, and late stage tendons, were used to analyze the degradation of Brillouin shift precision with increasing tissue depth, which was then used to determine the minimum scanning intensity and maximum scanning depth for our subsequent x-y scans. Brillouin shift intensity was reported in photon counts, where higher photon counts correspond to a more intense signal. Based on this analysis, we found that a Brillouin signal intensity of approximately 45, 000 photon counts (around 0.09 times the intensity at the tendon surface) is the minimum scanning intensity to maintain high measurement precision; below this threshold, the precision of the Brillouin shift of the tendon decreased substantially compared to higher depths. This minimum scanning intensity of 45,000 photon counts corresponded with a maximum scanning depth of approximately 65 μm across developmental stages (Fig. 2B and C). We also examined whether Brillouin spectral shape was affected when imaging at different depths within the tendon. Others have shown that increasing imaging depth in an optically non-transparent, or “turbid”, sample can cause changes in Brillouin spectral shape due to multiple scattering effects [35]. As embryonic tendons increase in collagen density during development, they become increasingly turbid samples. Here, we examined the Brillouin spectral shape at depths up to 100 μm in HH38, HH41, and HH45 tendons (Fig. S1). Changes in Brillouin spectral shape with increasing imaging depth were random and not ascribable to a specific effect, suggesting that turbidity of developing embryonic tendon may not increase multiple scattering effects at depths up till at least 100 μm. Brillouin spectral shapes were also only randomly different between stages, suggesting that optical heterogeneity does not increase sufficiently during embryonic tendon development to significantly impact tendon turbidity. For subsequent experiments, a scanning depth of 50 μm or lower was selected to ensure that scanning occurred well within the tendon bundle core while still maintaining sufficient Brillouin signal intensity. All subsequent measurements of Brillouin shift were performed in the x-y plane of the tendon bundle or whole tendon, along the plane of collagen fiber alignment (Fig. 1B and F).

Brillouin microscopy detects increases in Brillouin shift of embryo tendons during development

Brillouin microscopy was performed on the Achilles tendon at various stages of chick embryo development from early stages until prior to hatch (HH35 to HH45) (Fig. 3A) by imaging the core of the tendon bundle (whole tendon for HH35) at the midpoint of the long axis of the bundle at a depth of 50 μm. Brillouin shifts were similar between HH35 and HH38 and then increased significantly from HH38 to HH45 (Fig. 3B and C). Previously acquired data with both AFM and tensile testing also show no significant changes in tendon elastic modulus until HH38, after which elastic modulus increases steadily with development [5,7]. We validated Brillouin shift measurements of the developing Achilles tendons by plotting against previously reported tensile moduli [7] (Fig. 3D). Average tensile moduli and Brillouin shifts exhibited a log-linear relationship across developmental stages, with a statistically significant strong correlation (r = 0.9951, p = 0.0001). The strong agreement between average Brillouin shifts and tensile modulus across development supports that the properties measured by Brillouin microscopy in this study are of tendon elastic properties. Using linear regression analysis, we established, for the first time for embryonic tissue, an equation describing the log-linear relationship between Brillouin shift and tensile modulus that can be used as a calibration curve to determine the tensile modulus based on the Brillouin shift. These data compellingly demonstrate the potential to use Brillouin microscopy to measure chick embryo tendon elastic modulus in an all-optical, non-contact manner.

Brillouin microscopy is sensitive to changes in ECM crosslinking

The development of embryo tendon mechanical properties is highly dependent on the crosslinking of collagen, which is mediated by the enzyme lysyl oxidase (LOX) [5,8]. In previous studies with the chick embryo, BAPN treatment to inhibit LOX activity in turn inhibited LOX-mediated crosslinking of collagen and resulted in lower Achilles tendon elastic modulus compared to normally developing tendons [5,8, 26]. Here, we performed Brillouin microscopy and tensile testing on HH43 tendons after 24 h treatment with either LOX inhibitor (BAPN) or vehicle (saline). Both tensile modulus and Brillouin shift of BAPN-treated samples were significantly lower than that of vehicle-treated controls (Fig. 4), demonstrating a similar capacity for Brillouin microscopy as tensile testing to detect differences in mechanical properties due to differences in LOX-mediated collagen crosslinking.

We also tested the ability of Brillouin microscopy to detect increases in embryonic tendon mechanical properties due to increases in collagen crosslinking induced by UVA-photoactivated riboflavin treatment, which has been used previously to induce stiffening of cornea and tendon [27,36,37]. HH40 tendon samples were subjected to Brillouin microscopy immediately before and after UVA-photoactivated riboflavin treatments, or tensile testing with or without UVA-photoactivated riboflavin treatment. Both tensile modulus and Brillouin shift of photocrosslinked samples were significantly higher than that of untreated controls (Fig. 5). Taken together, these data (Figs. 4 and 5) demonstrate that Brillouin microscopy can detect changes in embryonic tendon mechanical properties due to different types of covalent crosslinking of the ECM.

Brillouin microscopy detects increasing mechanical heterogeneity of embryo tendons from early to late developmental stage

In previous work, force-volume AFM detected increasing structural and spatial mechanical heterogeneity of Achilles tendon over development and with increases in LOX-mediated collagen crosslinking [5,13]. Here, the variance of Brillouin shifts at each developmental stage was analyzed. At each stage, for each scan, the distribution of Brillouin shift occurrence was plotted (Fig. 6A). At the earlier stages (HH35 to 40) the occurrences within an embryo stage were more narrowly distributed and centered around lower Brillouin shifts (Fig. 6A). In contrast, at later stages of development (HH43 to 45) the occurrences were more broadly distributed and centered around higher Brillouin shifts (Fig. 6A). To analyze the heterogeneity of Brillouin shifts at each developmental stage, we plotted the average spread in Brillouin shift for each biological replicate per stage (Fig. 6B). Though the mean of the average Brillouin shift spread did not change significantly during development (Fig. 6B), the variance of the average Brillouin shift spread was significantly higher at later developmental stages (Fig. 6C). These results illustrate the capability of Brillouin microscopy to detect changes in heterogeneity of mechanical properties between stages, as reported in previous studies with AFM [5,13].

Each data point (red circles) represents the average Brillouin shift spread of one N. Means were compared using a one-way ANOVA with Holm-Sidak post-hoc, with no significant differences found between any developmental stages. Brillouin microscopy resolves spatial patterns in mechanical properties of later stage tendons

To investigate the potential for Brillouin microscopy to quantify mechanical properties of tendons spatially, and to image tendon microstructure, HH40, HH41, HH43, and HH44 Achilles tendon bundles were stained for collagen with Col-F and for nuclei with Hoechst 33342, respectively, after which they were imaged using a confocal fluorescence microscope as well as Brillouin microscopy (Fig. 7A and B). At each stage studied, Col-F and Hoechst 33342 staining highlighted collagen fibers arranged parallel to each other with their characteristic crimp pattern, with cells distributed throughout the tissue (Fig. 7A). Brillouin microscopy revealed distinct regions of lower and higher Brillouin shifts. By HH41, the regions of higher Brillouin shifts appeared to possess similar morphology and dimensions as Col-F-stained collagen fibers (Fig. 7B).

To compare regions of higher Brillouin shifts with regions of Col-F staining, we generated 1-dimensional line profiles for both Brillouin maps and confocal fluorescence images (Fig. 7C and D). By later stages (HH43, HH44) the line profiles of Brillouin shifts showed more distinct peaks and troughs. It appears that regions of higher Brillouin shift correlate with regions of collagen fibers at later stages of tendon development, which could be confirmed in future studies by imaging the same tissue region for Brillouin shifts and Col-F staining. Waveform analysis was attempted, but determination of peaks was difficult due to the noise of the scans. An attempt to create a minimum threshold for a peak was performed, but due to the inconsistency of absorbance values in a Col-F-stained collagen fiber, a reliable minimum could not be determined. Furthermore, it was not possible to correlate these peaks with Brillouin shifts since the imaging of Brillouin shifts and Col-F staining were not of the same regions.

LOX-mediated collagen crosslink density, a major contributor to embryonic tendon mechanical properties, increases substantially after HH40 in the chick embryo [5,8]. In previous work, second harmonic generation imaging of fibrillar collagen and AFM-mapping of the elastic modulus distribution exhibited spatial correlations in the embryonic tendon that were lost when LOX-mediated crosslinking was inhibited by BAPN treatment [5]. Here, Brillouin microscopy may be detecting collagen fibers with sufficiently high mechanical properties at later developmental stages due to higher levels of LOX-mediated collagen crosslinking at these stages.

Based on these data, Brillouin microscopy can characterize spatial mechanically distinct microstructures of developing tendons in an all-optical manner. This also illustrates the possibility for Brillouin microscopy to distinguish tendon microstructures based on mechanical properties, as has been demonstrated with other tissues such as adult cartilage, skeletal muscle, skin, and brain tissues [38].

Brillouin microscopy detects mechanical properties of Achilles tendon when attached to the limb and is sensitive to changes in tendon strain associated with ankle flexion angle

We examined the potential to use Brillouin microscopy to perform measurements of embryo tendons while still attached to the leg by imaging the Achilles tendon of HH41 limbs (with the skin removed). Previous studies using ultrasound elastography have shown that higher angles of ankle dorsiflexion result in higher transverse compressive strain on adult human and mouse Achilles tendon [39,40]. We performed Brillouin microscopy on the tendon, at a depth of 50 μm, with the ankle plantarflexed to a 140° flexion angle and then dorsiflexed to a 20° flexion angle, to determine whether Brillouin microscopy could detect the effect of varying tendon strain associated with different limb conformations (Fig. 8A). During passive plantarflexion the embryo tendon experiences very little strain, whereas at the dorsiflexion angle that we tested the tendon experiences strain within the linear range of the tendon’s stress-strain curve. To ensure the tissue was in a relaxed state for each conformation, the ankles were held in plantarflexion or dorsiflexion for 10 min before performing Brillouin microscopy, as this duration was previously used to achieve relaxation of rat Achilles tendon in a deformed state [41]. Average Brillouin shift of the Achilles tendon under plantarflexion (Fig. 8B and C) was similar to that of the Achilles tendon bundle dissected from the limb (Fig. 3C). These data reflect the potential to use Brillouin microscopy to measure the mechanical properties of Achilles tendons in vivo, provided that the ankle remains in the same flexion angle for each measurement. It is worth noting that average Brillouin shift was similar at a depth of 50 μm into a whole tendon as well as tendon bundle, indicating homogeneity of average mechanical properties throughout the tendon. When the ankle was dorsiflexed, average Brillouin shift increased significantly compared to when plantarflexed (Fig. 8B and C). Differences in Brillouin shift with ankle flexion were attributed to differences in tendon elastic modulus associated with the toe and linear regions of the stress-strain curve. However, it is also important to consider whether changes in Brillouin shift during ankle flexion could be due to changing turbidity [35] of the tissue caused by changes in local tissue density as the collagen uncrimps. To consider this, we examined the effect of ankle flexion on Brillouin spectral shape and found that ankle flexion had little influence on spectral shape (Fig. S2). Thus, changes in turbidity did not seem to have a significant effect on changes in Brillouin shift with ankle flexion, at least at the level of sensitivity enabled by our instrument linewidth. Collectively these data (Fig. 8 and Fig. S2) support that Brillouin microscopy can detect changes in elastic modulus of embryonic tendon with changing strain on the tendon, which is similar to previous findings with adult nerves [42]. These results confirm sensitivity of Brillouin microscopy to changes in mechanical properties that occur with limb conformation-associated changes in tendon strain in vivo.

Discussion

We demonstrate Brillouin microscopy as an innovative approach to mechanically characterize developing tendons in an all-optical, contact-free, and non-destructive manner. We optimized Brillouin microscopy for the chick embryo Achilles tendon. We established that Brillouin microscopy can scan as deep as 65 μm into a chick embryo tendon bundle to quantify its mechanical properties, and showed that this applies to early through late developmental stage tendons (Fig. 2). Brillouin shifts increased non-linearly over the course of development with significant increases during the later embryo stages (Fig. 3B and C). These Brillouin shifts correlated with our previously published study’s tensile testing data [7], thereby validating Brillouin microscopy as a feasible approach to measure chick embryo tendon mechanical properties (Fig. 3D). Brillouin microscopy also detected changes in tissue elastic modulus due to perturbations in ECM crosslink density (Figs. 4 and 5). Brillouin shift maps reflected the increasing heterogeneity of developing tendons (Fig. 6) that appeared to be associated with collagen fiber organization [5], highlighting the potential to characterize individual collagen fiber mechanical properties and structure (Fig. 7). Lastly, we demonstrated the potential for Brillouin microscopy to directly probe the mechanical properties of tendons in vivo, with sensitivity to changes induced by different joint angles (Fig. 8). These data support the potential to use Brillouin microscopy to quantitatively and spatiotemporally assess changes in embryonic tendon mechanical properties during development. This work also demonstrates that Brillouin microscopy can be used to measure mechanical properties of short tendons, overcoming the difficulty of preparation of such tendons for tensile testing. We anticipate this methodology can be similarly extended to developing ligaments and other collagen-rich connective tissues such as embryonic or fetal cartilage, meniscus, bone, and skin.

The ability to use Brillouin microscopy to measure Achilles tendon elastic modulus in vivo suggests an exciting possibility to use Brillouin microscopy to longitudinally monitor changes in mechanical properties of the same tendon as it develops over time or in response to developmental or experimental perturbations. Our previous studies have shown that chick embryos are amenable to in ovo manipulations, without effects on viability, by performing in ovo mechanical loading of chick embryo limb for a similar length of time as required for Brillouin imaging and observing no effect on viability 24 h later [43]. While earlier stage embryo skin may be sufficiently transparent for Brillouin imaging to detect the Achilles tendon underneath, optimization will be required to address the increasing opacity of skin with later developmental stages. Currently, ultrasound elastography, a non-invasive imaging technique, is used to measure in vivo tendon mechanical properties [39,40,44]. Strain elastography tracks deformation of tendon during movement in response to a known load to infer Young’s modulus of the tendon, while shear-wave elastography subjects a tissue to a short ultrasound burst to locally deform the tissue, after which the speed of sound wave propagation through tissue is estimated as an indirect measurement of Young’s modulus or shear modulus [39,40,44,45]. We attempted to use ultrasound imaging to quantitatively assess embryonic tendon elastic modulus in vivo, however there was insufficient signal to distinguish the tendon from surrounding structures in the leg (unpublished), potentially due to substantially lower collagen content than in adult tendon. Using Brillouin microscopy to perform in vivo and longitudinal evaluation of embryonic tendon mechanical properties could lead to new insights into tendon development.

There are a few additional points to consider. Viscoelastic properties of adult tendons and embryonic flexor tendon have been characterized to assess the effects of injury or experimental perturbations on tendon [12,16,46–53]. An exciting possibility is to use Brillouin microscopy to evaluate viscous properties of delicate embryonic Achilles tendons - Brillouin linewidth is evaluated as a measure of viscous properties of hydrogels and various tissues [42,54–59]. While this would require validation against other techniques for tendon, using Brillouin microscopy to characterize embryo tendon viscoelastic properties could lead to new insights into tendon development. Another point is that the Brillouin shift obtained through this approach is not a direct metric of tissue elastic modulus. Here, we generated a calibration curve to convert Brillouin shift values of chick embryonic Achilles tendon to the respective tensile modulus. However, for small or delicate tissues that cannot be tested with conventional mechanical testing methods, the generation of a calibration curve to convert Brillouin shift to tensile modulus is not possible. Alternatively, Brillouin shift can be converted to longitudinal elastic modulus, but such conversion requires knowledge of the local refractive index (n) and density of the sample (ρ) [19,27]. Such measurements are challenging to obtain for tissues; measurements of refractive index require advanced techniques like tomographic phase microscopy or digital holographic microscopy to understand its spatial variations within tissue, while techniques to measure spatial variations of density have not been established [60,61]. Without knowledge of these parameters, Brillouin shift measurements are limited to relative comparison of tissue mechanical properties between differing conditions. There is also a concern of Brillouin shifts being incomparable between tissues at different developmental stages, since their n/√ρ ratio could change with development. However, we do not expect the n/√ρ ratio to vary significantly between different stages of development, since previous studies have shown that the ratio is mainly dependent on lipid content of a tissue [61], and in our extensive characterizations of embryonic tendon, we have not observed significant lipid content [5,8,34,62,63]. This lack of optical heterogeneity between tendons at different developmental stages is further confirmed by the strong correlation of Brillouin shift with tensile modulus (Fig. 3D) and similar Brillouin spectral shape between different developmental stages (Fig. S1). However, it is possible that tissue turbidity affected our findings [35]. As an embryonic tendon develops into a more collagen-rich tissue [5,34,62], it will become a more turbid and optically heterogeneous material. Based on similar Brillouin spectral shapes, we found that tissue turbidity effects were minimal with increasing imaging depth (Fig. S1), developmental stage (Fig. S1), and ankle flexion (Fig. S2). However, our current Brillouin spectrometer has limited spectral resolution, with a limited ability to discern subtle spectral changes, which may have prevented detection of turbidity-induced errors. Another concern is the influence of turbidity-induced errors on our determination of maximum scanning depth. When we determined maximum scanning depth, we averaged Brillouin signal intensity across the x-axis of x-z scans, at a particular tissue depth. However, due to the cylindrical shape of the tendon, it is possible that there may have been differences in turbidity-induced signal decay across the x-axis of the tendon, since different points on the x-axis could have been at different depths from the tendon surface (Fig. 1). To minimize these errors, we performed the analysis within the center of the tendon where it is relatively flat. However, we acknowledge that small errors may have resulted from turbidity-induced artifacts due to unaccounted tendon curvature within our region of analysis. Another consideration is that the longitudinal elastic modulus acquired with Brillouin microscopy differs from tensile (Young’s) modulus. Previous studies have demonstrated that the relative change in the longitudinal elastic modulus is significantly lower as compared to the corresponding change in Young’s modulus [20,31,32], which should be considered when using Brillouin microscopy. While there have been significant advancements in Brillouin microscopy to optimize imaging acquisition times, characterizing large areas within the tendon tissue will require longer imaging times, with imaging times longer than one hour potentially inducing phototoxicity and damage cells within the tissue [29]. Since, in our study, imaging times did not exceed 34 min, induced phototoxicity was not a major concern, but it is something to consider for future experiments requiring longer acquisition times. Lastly, a potential drawback is the loss of Brillouin signal at moderate tissue depths in non-transparent tissues. Transparent adult tissues such as cornea, and crystalline lens can be imaged by Brillouin microscopy without concerns of signal decay [19,20,27,36,64]. Here, Brillouin microscopy was capable of imaging embryonic tissue that evolves into collagen-rich tissues till a maximum determined depth. Brillouin microscopy might also be applicable to injured and healing adult tendons while collagen matrix is less dense, but imaging ECM-rich healthy adult tendons would likely be a challenge because of their higher opacity due to lower cellularity and higher extracellular matrix content. Adult tendon mechanical properties could potentially be measured by using Brillouin microscopy on sectioned tissue, however this would not have much advantage over AFM. In this study, the tendon was amenable to confocal Brillouin microscopy through D19 (HH45) of chick embryonic development. It is possible that postnatal tendons may still be characterized by Brillouin microscopy, before developing into a fully opaque, highly collagen-rich tissue. Future studies could establish the latest possible stage of postnatal tendon development at which confocal Brillouin microscopy could be used to measure tendon mechanical properties. Future studies should also attempt to image tendons within limbs without skin removal. Despite these few limitations, we anticipate application of Brillouin microscopy will open new avenues for the study of embryonic tendon development, leading to new insights that would not be gained with current tools.

Conclusions

The results presented here demonstrate the innovative application of Brillouin microscopy to quantitatively and spatially map collagen-rich tissues as they develop in the embryo in an all-optical, contact-free, label-free manner. The application of Brillouin microscopy to measure embryonic tendon mechanical properties followed by additional biochemical assays or immunohistochemical staining on the same tissue, offers a powerful approach to pursue studies that previously have been intractable due to the large number of embryos needed for multiple assays. This advantage, coupled with the ability to longitudinally monitor changes in mechanical properties in vivo, could lead to mechanistic discoveries of tissue development and mechanobiology that have not been possible with conventional mechanical testing methodologies.

Statement of significance:

Tendon injuries and disorders are problematic, as tendons lack the ability to regenerate, and current clinical solutions are insufficient. Knowledge gained from elucidating how embryonic tendons develop their mechanical properties could be used to inform the development of therapeutics to treat injured or abnormal tendons. However, conventional mechanical testing methods require challenging manipulations of small tissues and render tissues unusable for additional assays. Brillouin microscopy is a powerful technique that can image mechanical properties of tissues in a non-contact and label-free manner, which enables in vivo analysis of mechanical properties. In this study, we show that Brillouin microscopy can image mechanical properties of tendon in developing embryos, thus opening new avenues for investigation into the development of tendon mechanical properties.
