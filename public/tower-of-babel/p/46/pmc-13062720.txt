Invisible Scars: A Population‐Based Study Examining Mental and Physical Health, Suicide Risk, and Family Well‐Being Among Women Survivors of Intimate Partner Violence
Authors: Margherita Cameranesi, Caroline C Piotrowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis research study used linked population‐based administrative data to identify and analyze similarities and differences in several indicators of mental and physical health, suicide risk, and family well‐being between two cohorts of women living in Central Canada, one including intimate partner violence (IPV) survivors (IPV cohort) and one including women who had never experienced IPV (non‐IPV cohort). A population‐based retrospective cohort study design was used to create the two study cohorts using linked administrative data on all residents of the Canadian Province of Manitoba over a 12‐year period. Mental health, including suicide risk, physical health, and family well‐being were compared in the two cohorts using Chi‐square test, T‐test, and Wilcoxon Mann‐Whiney U‐test analyses. The women survivors of IPV showed significantly higher rates of depression, anxiety, and post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) diagnoses. Suicide risk was significantly elevated in women IPV survivors, with 3% of the IPV cohort having attempted suicide 1–7 times and 0.7% having died by suicide during the study period. All poor physical health indicators were significantly prominent in the IPV cohort, except for asthma diagnosis. Additionally, the IPV cohort showed poorer family well‐being, as indicated by a significantly larger proportion of single‐parent families (vs. dual‐parent families) and contacts with the child welfare system. This investigation highlights the profound and lasting effects that IPV has on the mental and physical health of women in Manitoba, with significant implications for suicide risk and family well‐being. There is a critical need for integrated, trauma‐informed, and contextually sensitive support services tailored to the needs of IPV survivors, particularly in more socioeconomic disadvantaged communities.

Introduction

Intimate partner violence (IPV) is the most common form of violence against women worldwide, representing a significant global public health issue, with profound implications for the health and well‐being of survivors and their families (Grigaitė et al. 2024; Omar et al. 2022; WHO, 2022; WHO, 2024). The effects of IPV on women extend far beyond immediate physical harm as IPV has a significant negative lasting impact on both the mental and physical health of women, in addition to negatively affecting family well‐being (Omar et al. 2022; Wathen et al. 2025). This paper presents a population‐based study investigating similarities and differences in mental and physical health, suicide risk, and family well‐being between two cohorts of Canadian women with school‐age children, including a cohort of IPV survivors and one of women with no history of IPV.

Mental Health in Women IPV Survivors

Compared to women who have never experienced IPV, IPV survivors are at a significantly higher risk of developing mental health problems, including mood disorders, anxiety disorders, post‐traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and substance use disorders (Grigaitė et al. 2024; Mengo et al. 2021; Omar et al. 2022; Rowther et al. 2023; Walker‐Descartes et al. 2021; White et al. 2024). Women who endure IPV are more likely to experience symptoms of depression, including feelings of hopelessness, worthlessness, and a loss of interest in activities they once enjoyed. Women IPV survivors may develop depressive symptoms due to the cumulative stress of prolonged abuse, fear for personal safety and the safety of their children, and feelings of powerlessness that the abusive relationship causes (Molding et al., 2021). Anxiety disorders are also common among IPV survivors, with many women experiencing heightened levels of fear, worry, and panic (Mengo et al. 2021; Omar et al. 2022; Rowther et al. 2023; Walker‐Descartes et al. 2021). This can manifest as generalized anxiety disorder, panic disorder, or specific phobias. IPV survivors may develop anxiety due to fear for their personal safety and the safety of their children, or fear of the perpetrator's threats of further violence (Cordier et al. 2024). In these circumstances, exposure to multiple adverse factors, including psychological trauma, decreased sense of self‐worth, and loss of income, work, housing, and social participation may undermine recovery and lead to chronic mental health problems in these women (Cordier et al. 2024; Molding et al., 2021). In severe cases, the mental health problems women experience following IPV may become debilitating and contribute to functioning impairments involving difficulty performing daily activities, maintaining relationships, or functioning at work, home, or in social settings (Walker‐Descartes et al. 2021; White et al. 2024).

Arguably the most severe consequence women IPV survivors face is an increased risk for suicide ideation and suicide attempt (K. Devries et al. 2011; Potter et al., 2021; Rowther et al. 2023). Women who are subjected to IPV often experience a profound sense of despair, hopelessness, and helplessness, all of which increase the risk of suicidal ideation and self‐harm behavior. Studies have shown that women who experience IPV are significantly more likely to contemplate suicide and engage in self‐harm or suicidal behavior than women who have not been exposed to IPV (Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and National Center for Injury Prevention and Control 2023; K. M. Devries et al. 2013; K. Devries et al. 2011; Kafka et al. 2022; White et al. 2024). The relationship between IPV and suicide is complex and multifactorial. In many cases, psychological trauma and PTSD from the violence cause erosion of self‐esteem and self‐worth that can lead to suicide as a means of escaping suffering (Rasmussen et al. 2025). However, suicide behavior is not solely the result of IPV exposure, as it is often precipitated by the combination of multiple additional risk factors, such as financial hardship; food, employment, and shelter insecurity; substance use; social isolation; and lack of support systems (Johnson 2023). Additionally, women experiencing IPV may be afraid to seek help due to fear of further abuse or stigmatization, further exacerbating their mental health issues and increasing the likelihood of suicide (Cordier et al. 2024; Malihi et al. 2021).

Physical Health in Women IPV Survivors

In addition to its mental health consequences, IPV can have a devastating effect on women's physical health, leading to acute injuries that require medical attention and, in extreme cases, hospitalization, as well as chronic physical health conditions (Bosch et al. 2017; Cordier et al. 2024; Stubbs and Szoeke 2022). Physical injury is one of the most immediate and visible consequences of IPV. Women victims of IPV often sustain injuries that require medical treatment, including traumatic brain injury (TBI) (Haag et al. 2019; Nesca et al. 2021; Thomas et al. 2021). In extreme cases, injuries from IPV may lead to death, with intimate partner homicide being one of the leading causes of death for women worldwide (Nesca et al. 2021). The physical injuries sustained by IPV survivors are not limited to the immediate aftermath of an assault; they can also result in chronic health issues and long‐term disabilities requiring ongoing medical care (Ballan et al. 2022). Women who experience IPV are at an increased risk of developing chronic physical health conditions, such as asthma, diabetes, and cardiovascular diseases (Bosch et al. 2017; Wong et al., 2022). Chronic stress caused by lasting IPV can trigger or worsen existing health problems by multiple pathways. Chronic stress activates the hypothalamic‐pituitary‐adrenal (HPA) axis and the sympathetic nervous system, leading to dysregulation in cortisol levels, increased insulin resistance, elevated blood pressure, and elevated inflammatory markers (Alhalal and Falatah 2020; Mata‐Gil et al. 2021; Shibuya et al. 2024). These physiological changes impair immune function and promote systemic inflammation, thereby contributing to the development and progression of conditions such as asthma, autoimmune diseases, cardiovascular disease, and type 2 diabetes. These health conditions are not only debilitating but can also significantly reduce women's quality of life and contribute to long‐term disability (Campos‐Tinajero et al. 2024; Pebole, Iverson, et al. 2024).

In some cases, women IPV survivors use psychotropic substances, like alcohol and illicit drugs, to cope with the trauma of experiencing abuse in a romantic relationship (Forkus et al. 2024; Wright et al. 2019). Additionally, women IPV survivors often experience physical injury or trauma leading to hospitalization and chronic pain (Wuest et al. 2008). Chronic pain, which is often persistent and difficult to manage, may cause women to rely on pain medications and psychotropic drugs, which, if used long term, may increase the risk of developing substance use disorders and intensify the cycle of abuse and victimization, further complicating women's physical and mental health (Mehr et al. 2023; Romans et al. 2008; Woolhouse et al. 2019; Wuest et al. 2008). The mental and physical health problems experienced by women IPV survivors contribute to the large number of physician and hospital visits seen in this population (Daoud et al. 2020; Mellar et al. 2023).

IPV and Family Well‐Being

The effects of IPV are not limited to the victim alone; they extend to the entire family system. IPV often causes instability in the household, resulting in economic hardship, employment and housing insecurity, and increased reliance on social services (C. S. Chan et al. 2021; Kulkarni and Notario 2024; Marçal et al. 2022; Showalter et al. 2023). The trauma caused by IPV can significantly hinder parenting quality by making it difficult for women to care for their children adequately, leading to potential child abuse or neglect (Chiesa et al. 2018; Cuccì et al. 2025). Consequently, women IPV survivors are more likely to be involved with the child welfare system, either because of their own victimization or due to concerns for their children's safety (Janczewski et al. 2023).

Despite growing recognition of the widespread impact of IPV, significant gaps remain in the literature regarding the intersection of women's health, suicide risk, and family well‐being. Much of the existing research is based on self‐report data on small, non‐representative samples, limiting generalizability and overlooking the broader population‐level patterns that shape health outcomes for women and families affected by IPV. Some health consequences of IPV, including suicide risk, medication use, and physician or hospital visits, are understudied and often underexplored in investigations that use self‐report assessments. The population‐based study described here offers a powerful approach to addressing these gaps by capturing a large, diverse cohort and using linked administrative data, that enable the examination of objective indicators of health and family well‐being. Such research can provide critical insights into the cumulative burden of IPV and inform more effective, coordinated service and policy responses.

Current Study

In this study, de‐identified data from multiple population‐based administrative datasets were used to identify and analyze similarities and differences in several indicators of individual health and family well‐being between two cohorts of Canadian women, one including women IPV survivors (IPV cohort) and one including women who had never experienced IPV (non‐IPV cohort). Given the large body of evidence showing that women survivors of IPV are at significant increased risk of experiencing chronic health conditions and poor family well‐being, it was expected that the women in the two cohorts would differ significantly on all indicators assessed in the study. Specifically, we hypothesized that, compared to the non‐IPV cohort, the women IPV survivors experience significantly more mental and physical health problems, as indicated by one or more diagnosis of chronic health condition and recorded suicide attempts and death by suicide. We also expected the mothers in the IPV cohort to experience significantly poorer family well‐being than those in the non‐IPV cohort, as indicated by heightened levels of socioeconomic disadvantage in the form of a single‐parent household and involvement with the child welfare system.

Methods

Study Design

Within a retrospective cohort study design, population‐based data on all residents of the Canadian province of Manitoba over a 12‐year period (January 2006‐December 2017) were used to create a cohort of mothers with school‐aged children who experienced IPV as well as a matching cohort of mothers with school‐age children who did not face IPV, and to extract data on all outcome variables from administrative datasets. The two cohorts were matched using a ratio of 1:1 based on child age, child biological sex, and family socioeconomic status. Matching was restricted to variables that were consistently available and reliably measured across datasets and that were directly relevant to family context and child development.

Study Setting

Manitoba is a province in central Canada with a population of approximately 1.5 million people (Manitoba Bureau of Statistics, 2023). Publicly funded universal health care is available to all Canadian residents. All residents of Manitoba are registered with the provincial department of health, Manitoba Health, by receiving a unique identifier called Personal Health Identification Number (PHIN) that qualifies them to receive health care services anywhere in the province. Through this unique identifier assigned to each resident of Manitoba, any healthcare service receives by each resident is recorded and this information is stored in a database contained in the Manitoba Population Research Data Repository (hereafter referred to as “the Repository”), which is housed and maintained by the Manitoba Centre for Health Policy (MCHP).

Data Sources

The Repository contains a broad collection of anonymized, but linkable, health and non‐health administrative databases covering all Manitoba residents and uses an encrypted version of each person's PHIN to store each individual's information within the Repository. Table 1 provides a summary of the data that were used in this study and the multiple administrative population‐based health, social, and justice Repository databases from which they were extracted. For the purposes of this investigation, anonymized data were linked across the specified datasets using an encrypted version of each person's PHIN. The use of these unique identifiers enabled individuals to be tracked across the specified datasets and over the study period while ensuring their anonymity.

Study Population

The target population of the present study included mothers with school‐aged children between the ages of 6 and 11 at the end of the study period (calendar year 2017); mothers of younger or older children were excluded from the study. These families were identified using 12 calendar years of data from January 2006 to December 2017 to include all mothers of children who were born between the years 2006 and 2011, and who were therefore aged 6–11 years at the end of the study period in 2017. Indicators of IPV occurred between the years 2006 and 2016 were created to distinguish women survivors of IPV from those who did not face IPV. The criteria for inclusion in this study are outlined in the next sections.

The IPV Cohort

Mothers who experienced IPV at any time between January 2006 and December 2016 (11‐year period) were identified by linking information recorded in five different administrative datasets, including the Hospital Discharge Abstracts, Physician Claims, Midwifery Summary Reports, PRISM, and Family First Screen datasets. This linkage across datasets allowed the investigators to identify families who experienced different types and severity of IPV. The IPV cohort included 3886 women who were survivors of IPV, had children aged 6–11 years at the end of the study period, were living in Manitoba, and had Manitoba Health coverage for the entire study period (January 2006 – December 2017).

The Non‐IPV Cohort

After creating the IPV cohort, the non‐IPV cohort was generated by matching children in the IPV cohort using a ratio of 1:1 by age, biological sex, and socioeconomic status. The absence of any type of IPV over the 1‐year period 2006–2016 was identified linking the same five administrative databases used for the IPV‐cohort (i.e., Hospital Discharge Abstracts, Physician Claims, Midwifery Summary Reports, PRISM, and Family First Screen datasets). The non‐IPV cohort included 3886 women who were not survivors of IPV, had children aged 6–11 years at the end of the study period, were living in Manitoba, and had Manitoba Health coverage for the entire study period (January 2006 – December 2017).

Study Measures

This analysis used several standardized measures developed by the MCHP and based on linked administrative datasets, which are described in the following sections. Health, service‐use, and family well‐being outcomes were assessed in the 2017 calendar year, which served as a standardized index year for all study participants. Selection of a single index year allowed for consistent measurement of outcomes across cohorts once IPV exposure status had been established during the preceding observation window (2006–2016) and ensured complete and comparable data availability across linked administrative datasets.

Demographic Information

Children's age in 2017 and their biological sex, as well as mother's age at the birth of the child included in this study were extracted from the Manitoba Population Registry, which includes demographic information on all residents of the province registered for health care with Manitoba Health. Maternal age at childbirth was derived from the Manitoba Population Registry, a validated administrative database that captures all registered births in the province. Recorded maternal ages reflect age at the time of delivery and include rare cases of childbirth among very young adolescents. These values were reviewed in consultation with data documentation standards and were determined to represent valid records rather than data‐entry or linkage errors.

Mood and Anxiety Disorders

Given the overlap in symptomatic presentation of these conditions and prescription medications used to treat them, the current MCHP approach for identifying adults with a diagnosis of mood disorders or anxiety disorders is to combine these two classes of disorders into the condition. For the purposes of this investigation, it was assessed whether in a calendar year 2017, the women in the study cohorts were diagnosed with at least one mood disorder (e.g., depression, mania, or bipolar disorder) or anxiety disorder (e.g., anxiety state, phobia, or obsessive‐compulsive disorder [OCD]). An algorithm linking data in the Hospital Abstracts, Physician Claims, and DPIN databases was used to identify children and mothers who experienced any of these disorders in 2017.

Post‐Traumatic Stress Disorder

An algorithm linking data in the Hospital Abstracts, Physician Claims, and DPIN databases was used to identify mothers who in the calendar year 2017were diagnosed with PTSD or PTSD symptoms.

Suicide Attempt and Death by Suicide

Attempted suicide was identified from specific International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes in the Hospital Abstracts and Physician Claims datasets. Additionally, women who died by suicide at any time during the study period were identified directly from the cause of death in the Vital Statistics Mortality dataset. This information was recorded into two variables, one describing the number of times each woman had attempted suicide (never or one or more times) and one identifying the women who died by suicide.

Chronic Physical Health Conditions

Using information recorded in the Hospital Abstracts, Physician Claims, and DPIN datasets, two indicators of physical health in the calendar year 2017 were created for both women to indicate the presence of a diagnosis of asthma and diabetes, respectively. Asthma and diabetes diagnoses were identified using operational definitions developed by the MCHP, which include indicators over a 2‐year period (2016–2017).

Ambulatory Physician Visits

The number of ambulatory physician's visits that women completed in the calendar year 2017 were extracted by analyzing the physician claims dataset, which includes records of all contacts with physicians (i.e., general practitioners or family doctors and specialists, such as gynecologists and paediatricians). The ambulatory physician's visits recorded in the physician claims dataset include office visits, walk‐in clinics, home visits, personal care home or nursing home visits, and visits to outpatient departments. The dataset also includes a tariff code that defines the type of service provided. However, for the purposes of this investigation, the number of visits per woman was recorded regardless of the type of service received.

Prescription Medications

The number of any class of prescription medications prescribed to women in the calendar year 2017 was extracted from the DPIN dataset. Medications that are purchased without a prescription (over–the–counter medications) are not captured in this DPIN dataset and therefore, were not included in this analysis.

Maternal Inpatient Hospital Episodes and Hospital Episodes Length of Stay

The number of inpatient hospital episodes that study participants required in the calendar year 2017 and their duration (length of stay [LOS]) in days were extracted from the Hospital Discharge Abstracts dataset by examining hospital episodes between 1 January 2017 and 31 December 2017. If a woman was hospitalized in one hospital and then transferred to a different hospital, it was counted as a single episode. All hospitalizations for any reasons were included in the record except for birth hospitalizations, which were excluded because nearly all children are born in hospital. From the Hospital Discharge Abstracts dataset, we also extracted the number of inpatient hospitalizations women required in the calendar year 2017 that were due to unintentional injury.

Family Type

Information on the presence of a living spouse or common‐law partner was extracted from the Manitoba Population Registry, which includes demographic information on all residents of the province of registered for health care with Manitoba Health. We used this information to create a variable that indicated whether the family was a single‐parent or dual‐parent family in 2017.

Contact With the Child Welfare System

The number of contacts that each family had with Child and Family Services (CFS) for any reason after the birth of the child included in this study were calculated using information recorded in the Child and Family Services: Application and Intake dataset. In this administrative dataset involvement with the child welfare system identifies: (i) children in families receiving child welfare services (whose health or emotional well‐being is thought to be endangered), or (ii) children in care of the child welfare system who were removed from their family of origin and placed in the care of another adult(s) (not a parent or guardian) due to concerns about the proper provision of care in the family of origin. It is reasonable to expect that parents and children who are involved with the child welfare system, especially in cases of multiple involvements, are experiencing lower levels of family well‐being than families who never require these services. Therefore, we used this variable as an indicator of family well‐being.

Statistical Analysis

First, preliminary descriptive analyses involving univariate analysis of all study variables to describe both children and mothers in the non‐IPV and IPV cohorts were performed. Next, group differences (i.e., non‐IPV cohort vs. IPV cohort) on all study variables were examined using cross‐tabulations with Chi‐square tests of association for discrete variables, T‐test for continuous variables that were normally distributed, and Wilcoxon Mann‐Whiney U‐test for continuous variables that were non‐normally distributed. Additionally, based on the differences identified through the cohort comparisons between the IPV cohort and the non‐IPV cohort, a post‐hoc analysis was performed to investigate associations among indicators in the IPV cohort. These associations were examined and tested using Pearson product moment correlation coefficient for continuous variables and Spearman correlation coefficient for binomial variables.

Given the population‐based nature of the linked administrative data and the primary objective of the present study, data analysis involved on descriptive and comparative approaches rather than multivariable causal modeling. This analytic strategy is consistent with established uses of administrative health and social data to identify population‐level disparities, service‐use patterns, and clustering of adverse outcomes across groups (Karakurt et al. 2017; Nesca et al. 2021). The use of univariate and bivariate analyses allowed for transparent comparison of health and family well‐being indicators between IPV‐exposed and non‐exposed cohorts while minimizing assumptions that cannot be fully tested using observational administrative datasets, such as unmeasured confounding, timing of exposure relative to outcomes, and pathway‐specific mediation. Correlational analyses within the IPV cohort were conducted to explore patterns of co‐occurrence among mental health, physical health, and service‐use indicators, with the goal of informing interpretation and hypothesis generation rather than causal inference.

Although multivariate regression models are often used to address confounding, their validity depends on the availability and accurate measurement of relevant covariates. In studies using routinely collected administrative data, key variables may be unavailable, inconsistently recorded, or subject to misclassification, increasing the risk of residual confounding or biased estimates when used for regression adjustment (Benchimol et al. 2015). Several factors known to influence both IPV exposure and health outcome, including, for instance, ethnicity and immigration or refugee status are either unavailable or incompletely captured within administrative datasets. In this context, the analytic approach prioritized transparent descriptive and comparative analyses to characterize population‐level disparities rather than producing adjusted estimates that could be misleading given data limitations.

Ethical Considerations

The present study involved the secondary analysis of population‐based administrative data and was conducted in accordance with established ethical and privacy frameworks governing the use of linked health and social data in Canada. Approval was obtained from an institutional Research Ethics Board (REB) and the Manitoba Health Information Privacy Committee (HIPC), along with additional data access approvals from relevant provincial authorities.

To protect confidentiality and personal privacy, all data were fully de‐identified prior to researcher access and linked using encrypted personal health identification numbers (PHINs). No direct identifiers were available to the research team at any stage of the study. Data were accessed and analyzed within a secure research environment at the MCHP, which employs strict physical, technical, and administrative safeguards, including controlled access, auditing, and disclosure review procedures, to prevent unauthorized use or re‐identification.

Ethical considerations specific to research involving IPV were addressed through the exclusive use of retrospective administrative records, eliminating the need for direct contact with women or families and thereby avoiding potential risks such as re‐traumatization, distress, or inadvertent disclosure of IPV experiences. Results are reported only in aggregate form, and small cell sizes were suppressed in accordance with MCHP disclosure guidelines to further reduce the risk of identification. Taken together, these measures ensured that the study adhered to core ethical principles of respect for persons, concern for welfare, and justice, while enabling the examination of population‐level health and family well‐being patterns among women exposed to IPV.

Results

Cohort Comparison

Cohort comparison analysis showed that women IPV survivors were significantly different from their counterparts who did not experience IPV in several ways (see Table 2 for details). Although both cohorts included a small number of very young mothers, such cases were rare and reflect documented instances of early adolescent childbirth within the population rather than anomalies in the data. At the birth of the target child, the youngest mother in the exposed cohort was aged 9 years while the youngest mother in the nonexposed cohort was aged 14 years; mothers in the IPV cohort were significantly younger on average than mothers in the non‐IPV cohort (t(7,770) = 25.63, p < 0.001).

Descriptive statistics and cohort comparison.

As expected, women who faced IPV were significantly more likely to experience poor mental health compared to mothers in the non‐IPV cohort; they had almost double the rates of mood and anxiety disorders (X 2(1) = 585.30, p < 0.001) and more than three times the rates of PTSD (X 2(1) = 195.69, p < 0.001). Remarkably, mothers who had faced IPV were also significantly more likely to die by suicide (X 2(1) = 26.09, p < 0.001) than mothers in the non‐IPV cohort and were more than three times more likely to have attempted suicide than mothers who had not faced IPV (X 2(1) = 40.82, p < 0.001).

In line with our hypotheses, women with a history of IPV had significantly poorer physical health on most dimensions assessed, compared to women without such a history. The women in the IPV cohort were significantly more likely to be diagnosed with diabetes (X 2(1) = 32.41, p < 0.001) and require inpatient hospital care for any reasons (X 2(1) = 84.27, p < 0.001), including unintentional injury (X 2(1) = 16.23, p < 0.001) with significantly longer stays in the hospital (X 2(1) = 84.27, p < 0.001). Women in the IP‐V cohort visited a physician significantly more often (Z = −15.64, p < 0.001) and were prescribed significantly more medications (Z = −20.60, p < 0.001) than women in the non‐IPV cohort. In fact, women IPV survivors were prescribed more than 7 times the number of medications than women in the non‐IPV cohort. No significant differences were found in the rates of asthma experienced by the two cohorts of women.

As hypothesized, contact with the child welfare system since the birth of the target child in the present study was significantly more common in the IPV cohort than in the non‐IPV cohort (Z = −3.96, p < 0.001). During the study period, 317 families (8.15%) in the non‐IPV cohort had one to seven contacts with the child welfare system, while 1618 (41.63%) families in the IPV cohort had 1‐14 contacts during the same period. Thus, as hypothesized, not only the families in the IPV cohort experienced significantly more contacts with the child welfare system than the families in the non‐IPV cohort, but there was also a significantly larger number of families in the IPV‐cohort who had one or more contacts with the child welfare system during the study period (X 2(1) = 1164.71, p < 0.001).

Post‐Hoc Analysis: Correlations in the IPV Cohort

Table 3 includes a correlation matrix displaying the Pearson's and Spearman's correlation coefficients between indicators of health and family well‐being in the IPV cohort. This analysis showed some significant strong associations deserving attention. The number of ambulatory visits and prescription medications required by mothers in 2017 was significantly and positively associated with most negative physical health outcomes, including inpatient hospital episodes and inpatient hospital LOS, as well as a diagnosis of asthma and diabetes. Spearman correlation analysis also revealed a strong positive correlation between the number of ambulatory visits that mothers required in 2017 and the number of prescription medications that they required that year. The number of prescription medications mothers required in 2017 was also significantly positively associated with the number of inpatient hospital episodes due to involuntary injury they required that year. Taken together these associations are indicative of poor physical health. Mother's diagnoses of mood and anxiety disorders and PTSD were significantly and positively associated. These mental health disorders in mothers were also significantly and positively associated with the number of ambulatory visits and prescription medications they required in 2017. Maternal diagnoses of mood and anxiety disorders were also significantly and positively associated with asthma and inpatient hospital episodes and LOS. PTSD in mothers was also significantly and positively associated with suicide attempts, indicating that, as expected, women with a diagnosis of PTSD were at greater risk for attempting suicide. There was a significant and negative association between death by suicide and ambulatory visits, indicating that women who completed suicide had less contact with the healthcare system.

Discussion

Given the substantial lack of population‐based studies using objective administrative indicators of health and well‐being in families exposed to IPV, the present study makes an important contribution to the scholarly literature in several ways. Firs, this study is among the limited investigations to date that have examined health outcomes in families exposed to IPV taking a population‐based approach. This approach allows investigators to draw conclusions that have a higher degree of generalizability to the specific context in which the study is being conducted, and therefore have more value for policymaking and clinical practice. The present study is also among the few investigations to identify families impacted by IPV by linking several population‐based datasets including health, social welfare, and justice records. We found striking differences between the mothers in the IPV cohort and their counterparts who did not experience IPV. In line with our hypotheses, both mothers in the IPV cohort demonstrated significantly poorer mental and physical health as compared to those in the non‐IPV cohort.

The families in the IPV cohort experienced substantially higher levels of social adversity, as reflected in markedly greater contact with the child welfare system and a significantly higher prevalence of single‐parent households. Consistent with the study hypotheses, women in the IPV cohort demonstrated significantly poorer mental health than women in the non‐IPV cohort, including substantially higher rates of mood and anxiety disorders and PTSD. Suicide risk was also markedly elevated among women exposed to IPV, with higher rates of suicide attempts and death by suicide observed during the study period.

In addition to adverse mental health outcomes, women in the IPV cohort experienced significantly poorer physical health and more intensive health‐care utilization. Compared to women who had not experienced IPV, they were more likely to be diagnosed with chronic physical health conditions such as diabetes, to require inpatient hospital care (including for unintentional injury), to experience longer hospital stays, to attend more frequent ambulatory physician visits, and to be prescribed substantially more medications. Taken together, these findings align with a growing body of evidence demonstrating that IPV is associated with enduring and cumulative impacts on women's mental health, physical health, suicide risk, and reliance on health and social service systems (Cordier et al. 2024; Jenkins et al. 2025; Mellar et al. 2023; Omar et al. 2022; Rodrigues et al. 2025; White et al. 2024).

Interpreted through a trauma‐ and violence‐informed lens, the findings of the present study highlight the cumulative and embodied burden associated with prolonged exposure to IPV on women's health and family well‐being. Trauma‐ and violence‐informed frameworks emphasize that health outcomes among survivors are shaped not only by discrete violent events but also by sustained psychosocial stress, fear, loss of control, and repeated system interactions across the life course (Omar et al. 2022; Wathen et al. 2025). The pronounced clustering of mental health diagnoses, chronic physical health conditions, and intensive health service use observed among women in the IPV cohort is consistent with models of toxic stress, which posit that chronic exposure to uncontrollable adversity is associated with dysregulation of neuroendocrine, immune, and metabolic systems (Alhalal and Falatah 2020; Hughes et al. 2017; Shibuya et al. 2024; Sonu et al. 2019). Within this framework, these physiological and psychosocial processes offer a plausible explanatory context for the elevated prevalence of mood and anxiety disorders, PTSD, diabetes, hospitalizations, and prescription medication use observed in this population‐based cohort. Prior scholarship also suggests that poor mental health is frequently associated with adverse physical health outcomes through both direct and indirect pathways, including reduced self‐care, sedentary behavior, unhealthy eating patterns, and medication side effects (Pebole, Iverson, et al. 2024; Wright et al. 2019; Zoie and Digal 2025).

An intersectional and social determinants of mental health perspective further underscores that the disparities observed in this study are unlikely to be attributable to IPV exposure in isolation. Women in the IPV cohort experienced markedly greater social disadvantage, as reflected in substantially higher rates of child welfare system involvement and single‐parent family status, indicating the convergence of multiple structural stressors alongside documented violence exposure. Previous research demonstrates that socioeconomic marginalization, housing and employment instability, and intensive system involvement are associated with compounded health vulnerability and constrained access to timely, preventive, and culturally responsive care (C. S. Chan et al. 2021; Kulkarni and Notario 2024; Marçal et al. 2022). From an intersectional standpoint, these findings suggest that gendered experiences of violence co‐occur with broader structural inequities in ways that shape cumulative health risk across the life course. Population‐level interventions aimed at supporting IPV survivors therefore need to extend beyond individual‐level treatment to address structural determinants of health, including poverty, housing insecurity, and fragmented service systems, in order to reduce persistent health inequities among women and families affected by IPV.

During the 11 years of the study period (2006–2017), 26 women who had experienced IPV died by suicide, and over 100 women attempted suicide, with some engaging in up to seven multiple attempts. These findings are highly concerning and call for immediate actions, particularly given the young average age of mothers in the cohort, and underscore a strong association between IPV exposure and elevated suicide risk. However, given the observational nature of the data and the timing of outcome measurement, these results should be interpreted as reflecting co‐occurring vulnerability and cumulative risk, rather than a unidirectional causal pathway.

The growing recognition of the adverse health outcomes experienced by women who have been exposed to IPV has contributed to increased public awareness of the far‐reaching impact of this form of intra‐familial trauma. This awareness has informed the development of population‐based interventions aimed at addressing mental and physical health needs among IPV‐affected women, particularly younger women. More recently, the adoption of strength‐based frameworks (Davidson et al. 2025) has advanced the field by emphasizing personal strengths, contextual resources, and resilience processes that are associated with positive adjustment and well‐being following IPV exposure. This emerging focus on resilience offers important opportunities to inform public health promotion initiatives – particularly universal or selective programs provided at no cost to participants – which may play a critical role in supporting health and well‐being while strengthening individual‐ and family‐level protective factors (Callaghan et al. 2019).

Evidence suggests that individuals and families who participate in strength‐based interventions often report better health, well‐being, and quality of life compared to those engaged in approaches focused solely on mitigating adverse outcomes (Andisheh et al. 2024; Hoeven et al. 2023). The development and application of effective coping strategies associated with resilience may support women IPV survivors in navigating challenges across multiple life domains (Yoon et al. 2021).

Clinical and Policy Implications

Given the high prevalence of suicide attempts and deaths by suicide observed among women with documented IPV exposure in this study, these findings highlight the need for targeted prevention and intervention strategies. Suicide remains a leading cause of death among young people in Canada, including in Manitoba (Manitoba Advocate for Children and Youth 2020; Public Health Agency of Canada 2024). Consistent with recommendations from the Manitoba Advocate for Children and Youth (2020), training on trauma, toxic stress, and trauma‐informed practice should be widely available to service providers across sectors serving women and families. In addition, the development of inpatient and community‐based long‐term support services may be warranted for families experiencing elevated risk of self‐harm, particularly where less intensive interventions are insufficient.

Intervention programs for IPV‐affected families should aim to address co‐occurring mental and physical health challenges while also supporting family relationships and positive parenting practices. One such program, the Moms' Empowerment Program (MEP), has been shown across multiple contexts to be associated with improvements in maternal mental and physical health and parenting outcomes among women who have experienced IPV (Clark et al. 2018; Graham‐Bermann and Miller‐Graff 2015; Howell et al. 2015; Martinez‐Torteya et al. 2025). Given that the MEP has not yet been implemented in Canada, future research is needed to examine its feasibility and effectiveness within the Canadian context.

Limitations

The present analysis was not designed to exclude cases based on the sequencing of IPV exposure and health events, nor to test causal directionality. Rather, the study aimed to characterize population‐level disparities in health and family well‐being between women with and without documented IPV exposure. Accordingly, findings should be interpreted as descriptive associations that reflect cumulative burden rather than causal effects of IPV. While IPV exposure was identified over an extended period (2006–2016), several health outcomes were measured in a single index year (2017). This design does not permit fine‐grained examination of health trajectories prior to, during, or following IPV exposure, nor does it allow definitive determination of temporal ordering between IPV and subsequent health conditions. As a result, bidirectional relationships are possible; for example, pre‐existing physical or mental health problems may increase vulnerability to IPV, while IPV exposure may in turn exacerbate or contribute to worsening health outcomes. The cross‐sectional nature of our study did not allow to test change over time in the health trajectories of the two study cohorts. Given the scarce research on health trajectories in families exposed to IPV, future research should use longitudinal research designs to better describe changes in health trajectories in families impacted by IPV over time and identify the factors and processes that promote their health and well‐being over time. Future longitudinal analyses leveraging repeated annual measures could further disentangle temporal sequencing and bidirectional relationships between IPV exposure and health outcomes.

Despite matching on child age, child biological sex, and family socioeconomic status, residual confounding remains a limitation of this study. IPV exposure and subsequent health outcomes are influenced by a range of individual, social, and structural factors that could not be fully accounted for using administrative data, including immigration and refugee status, ethnicity, rural versus urban residence, disability status, substance use, and pre‐existing mental health conditions. The absence or incomplete measurement of these variables limits the ability to disentangle their independent contributions to observed disparities between cohorts.

An additional limitation of the present study is the inability to disaggregate IPV exposure by specific type (e.g., psychological, economic, physical, or sexual violence). IPV status was identified using indicators drawn from multiple linked administrative health, justice, and social service datasets, which are designed primarily for service delivery and surveillance rather than for nuanced characterization of violence experiences. As a result, these data do not consistently or comprehensively capture the type, severity, or chronicity of IPV exposure across individuals. This limitation precludes examination of whether different forms of IPV are differentially associated with specific mental health, physical health, or family well‐being outcomes. Future research using mixed methods designs, survey‐based measures, or qualitative approaches could complement population‐based administrative data by enabling more fine‐grained analyses of IPV typologies and their distinct health consequences, while retaining the strengths of large‐scale linkage for examining cumulative and long‐term outcomes.

A further consideration is the presence of a small number of mothers who gave birth at very young ages, including early adolescence. While these records represent valid population‐level data, such extreme age values likely reflect underlying circumstances of sexual exploitation, abuse, or profound social vulnerability that are not fully captured within administrative datasets. Maternal age is closely intertwined with socioeconomic disadvantage, health risk, and exposure to violence, and these early childbearing experiences may therefore contribute to the disparities observed between cohorts. Consistent with this interpretation, population‐based research demonstrates that very young maternal age is associated with extreme social disadvantage, elevated physical and mental health risks, and coercive or abusive contexts, indicating that these cases represent substantively meaningful vulnerability rather than anomalous observations (Chen et al. 2007; Harden et al. 2009; Mollborn and Morningstar 2009). More recent evidence similarly shows that very young maternal age is associated with heightened risk of adverse pregnancy and neonatal outcomes and functions as a marker of underlying socioeconomic disadvantage and structural vulnerability, rather than a simple demographic artifact (Diabelková et al. 2023). Given the population‐based nature of the present study and the rarity of these observations, the primary findings reflect overall cohort‐level patterns rather than being driven by extreme values. Excluding these cases would risk underrepresenting some of the most vulnerable individuals affected by violence and adversity. Future research could build on these findings through age‐stratified analyses or complementary qualitative approaches to more fully elucidate the distinct needs, contexts, and trajectories of very young mothers.

Fathers were excluded from the present investigation due to limited information available in the administrative data. Future research on IPV should include fathers or father figures as they often represent an ambiguous influence on women's health and well‐being (K. L. Chan 2021). Additionally, this analysis did not distinguish between type 1 and type 2 diabetes or between childhood‐ and adult‐onset asthma, resulting in the inability to discriminate between genetic and environmental influences when considering the adjustment of the two IPV cohorts. Future research that uses genetically sensitive designs is needed to clarify the proportion of variance in the negative health outcomes experienced by women IPV survivors that is due to genetic versus environmental influences.

Conclusions

IPV is a pervasive public health issue that is strongly associated with substantial disparities in women's physical and mental health, as well as family well‐being. Findings from this population‐based study indicate that women with documented IPV exposure experience markedly poorer mental health, including higher prevalence of mood and anxiety disorders and substantially elevated suicide risk, compared to women without documented IPV exposure. IPV exposure was also associated with greater physical health burden, including higher rates of injury‐related hospitalization, chronic health conditions, and more intensive health‐care utilization. Beyond individual health outcomes, IPV exposure was associated with indicators of family instability, including increased contact with the child welfare system. Together, these findings highlight the cumulative and co‐occurring vulnerabilities experienced by women and families affected by IPV, rather than discrete or isolated outcomes.

Despite the magnitude of suicide risk observed among women with documented IPV exposure, research examining suicide behavior in this population remains limited. This gap constrains efforts to fully understand the pathways through which IPV exposure and broader structural vulnerabilities intersect with mental health and suicide risk. Continued population‐based and longitudinal research, alongside strengthened health and social service responses, is needed to better characterize these associations and to inform the development of coordinated, trauma‐informed interventions that support women and families affected by IPV.
