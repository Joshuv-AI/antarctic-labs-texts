Comparison of interpatient and intrapatient variability in doxorubicin exposure using a validated limited sampling model in dogs with cancer
Authors: Sridhar Madan Veluvolu, Robert B. Rebhun, Jaeyoung Kim, Luke Anthony Wittenburg
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDoxorubicin (DOX) is an anthracycline chemotherapeutic used for many canine malignancies, and its adverse event (AE) profile has been well-described in dogs. A limited sampling (LS) pharmacokinetic model was recently developed in canine patients to predict hematologic exposure to DOX. The primary goal of this study was to evaluate within-patient and between-patient variability in DOX exposure over three consecutive doses using the LS model. A secondary goal was to determine if there is a correlation between DOX exposure and gastrointestinal (GI) AEs utilizing a standardized owner questionnaire.MethodsWe performed a prospective evaluation of DOX exposure in seven tumor-bearing dogs across three cycles of treatment and compared the coefficient of variation (%CV) of the between- and within-patient exposures.ResultsThis data set corroborated the ability of the DOX LS model to predict absolute neutrophil count for patients whose absolute neutrophil counts are lower at seven days compared to baseline. Dose normalized within-patient variability (4.7%) was significantly lower (p < 0.001) than between-patient variability (25.4%) in DOX exposure. Decreased appetite (p = 0.005) and increased nausea (p = 0.02) were significantly correlated with DOX exposure.ConclusionsTogether, these data suggest wide interpatient variability in dose-normalized DOX exposure but much more consistent exposure within an individual patient across treatment cycles. Future studies can attempt to target specific DOX exposures and absolute neutrophil counts to assess if DOX dosing can be personalized to increase efficacy and decrease GI AEs.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s44356-025-00038-z.

Background

Doxorubicin (DOX) is one of the most commonly used chemotherapeutics in veterinary oncology. It is an anthracycline antitumor antibiotic used in the treatment of a variety of canine malignancies, with pleiotropic mechanisms targeting cancer cells. Reported cytotoxic effects of DOX include DNA intercalation, inhibition of RNA and DNA polymerases, inhibition of topoisomerase II, alkylation of DNA, and creation of reactive oxygen species among others [1]. Its versatility is well recognized by both the veterinary and human medical communities, but an ability to “personalize” its use in cancer therapy to optimize treatment outcomes has not yet been realized.

Dosing of DOX, and many other chemotherapies, is based on patient body surface area (BSA). The use of BSA was initially implemented to normalize the dose administered across multiple species on the basis that BSA is more representative of an animal’s basal metabolic rate [2]. BSA was thought to be more representative of physiologic processes and drug metabolism as compared to weight. Although this may be partially true, it has been suggested that BSA based dosing cannot account for several key factors including drug distribution, metabolism and excretion among others [3, 4]. It is also known that smaller dogs experience more severe adverse events when the dose administered is based on BSA [5]and dogs less than 15 kg (kg) are typically treated with a 1 milligram (mg)/kg dose. This provides evidence that the current dosing scheme may not fully represent exposure to antineoplastic compounds and suggests that the established formula for BSA is imperfect.

As such, newer dosing schemes are needed to better predict drug exposure and limit patient toxicity. The adverse event (AE) profile for doxorubicin is significant compared to other intravenous chemotherapeutics, with gastrointestinal (GI) AEs and myelosuppression comprising the dose limiting toxicities [6]. A higher percentage of patients experience GI AEs when they receive DOX as a single agent compared to other chemotherapeutics. Furthermore, DOX is commonly used in multi agent protocols which are associated with more significant AE profiles compared to single agent protocols [7].

A previous study [8] has correlated exposure (measured as the predicted area-under-the drug concentration-time curve [AUC]) to DOX with neutropenia in canine patients. This same study also correlated exposure to the total number of white blood cells, total number of neutrophils, and surviving fraction of neutrophils at the nadir. These data mirror similar data collected in human medicine which support the notion of pharmacokinetic (PK) guided dosing [9, 10]. PK-guided dosing has been shown to reduce inter-individual variability in the degree of bone marrow suppression following docetaxel therapy by up to 50%9. With respect to fluorouracil therapy in patients with metastatic colorectal cancer, PK-guided dosing led to significantly improved objective response rates [10]. Thus, there is evidence that patient drug exposure correlates better with effect than the dose administered. However, the use of PK-guided dosing has not been universally adopted in human oncology because the relationship between exposure and effect has not been elucidated for all drugs and tumor types. These relationships are even less well understood in veterinary cancer therapy.

Indeed, there is need to completely characterize how DOX behaves within a patient to continue the effort to personalize its dosing. A limited sampling (LS) model to predict DOX PK was previously developed and validated in canine cancer patients [8]. This model was shown to accurately predict AUC. Baseline neutrophil counts of the patients in this study could also be used to predict the neutrophil count at the seven-day nadir [5]. However, this model has previously been utilized after only one dose of DOX was administered to each patient. The consistency of predictions across serial doses needs to be established to appropriately use the LS model to prospectively personalize DOX. The purpose of this study was to compare the within- and between-patient variability in DOX exposure after three consecutive doses of DOX. Secondary objectives were to validate the ability of the previously developed LS model to predict nadir neutrophil count and investigate potential correlations between DOX exposure and GI AEs.

Methods

Animals

This was a prospective study conducted at the University of California – Davis Veterinary Medical Teaching Hospital from June 2021 – August 2022. The protocol and owner informed consent form were approved by the UC Davis IACUC (IACUC # 22149) and the UC Davis Veterinary Medical Teaching Hospital Clinical Trials Review Board. Data collected upon enrollment included age, sex, neuter status, breed, weight (in kg), body condition score (on a nine-point scale), tumor type, tumor location, and significant comorbidities. Inclusion criteria were a signed owner consent form, any patient with a cytologic or histologic diagnosis of cancer susceptible to doxorubicin chemotherapy, patient had a favorable performance score defined as VCOG-CTCAE [11] 0–2, patient weight of at least 15 kg, and appropriate patient mentation and cooperativity for the planned study. Exclusion criteria included absolute neutrophil count less than or equal to 2,000 per microliter, platelet count less than or equal to 100,000 per microliter, bilirubin greater than or equal to the upper limit of reference, serum creatinine greater than or equal to the upper limit of reference, any patient with known or suspected cardiac systolic dysfunction or any other significant underlying cardiac disease, and any breed predisposed to MDR-1 gene mutations. No specific drugs (i.e. steroids, NSAIDs, etc.) affected patient eligibility. No specific DOX schedule (q2 weeks, q3 weeks, multi-agent, etc.) was required for patient enrollment.

Patients were considered on study for three doses of DOX with their respective seven-day nadir complete blood counts (CBCs). Total dose (milligrams) received, [2] square meters (m2) of the patient at each dose, and mg/m2 per dose were recorded. CBCs were performed before each dose of DOX and seven days following each dose on a Siemens Advia 120 Hematology System. Hematocrit, total white blood cell count, total neutrophil count, and platelet count were collected. Surviving fractions were calculated by dividing the measured number of respective cells at seven days by the measured number of cells at baseline. Bone marrow toxicity was evaluated using established criteria [11]. A chemistry profile was performed on a Roche Cobas 501 analyzer before the first dose only. A quality-of-life form was provided to owners to fill out to assess GI toxicity after each dose of doxorubicin (Supplemental Materials). The form was the same used in several previously published studies [12, 13]. During the study, owners were encouraged to maintain their pets on a consistent diet (without additional treats or diet changes) to standardize the observation of gastrointestinal side effects as best as possible. Metronidazole and ondansetron were prescribed and sent home with owners in the event that diarrhea and/or nausea/vomiting were observed. The dose of metronidazole was as close to 10 mg/kg as possible, given every 12 h for three days. Ondansetron was prescribed as close to 0.5 mg/kg as possible, given by mouth every 12 h until appetite returned to normal or nausea/vomiting ceased.

Prediction of doxorubicin exposure (AUC)

All dogs initially received a standard dose of DOX (30 mg/m2) by 20-minute intravenous infusion. No premedication was administered in hospital to any patient prior to DOX. Blood samples were obtained at five, 45, and 60 min post-infusion. These time points represent those that were previously validated to predict AUC0−6 h, providing an accurate estimation of total DOX exposure in dogs [14]. Three milliliters of blood was collected in a red top tube, placed on ice for 10 to 15 min, and centrifuged at 2000×g for 15 min at room temperature. Doxorubicin analysis

Doxorubicin was measured in canine serum by a validated liquid chromatography tandem-mass spectrometry assay [8]. Positive ion electrospray ionization mass spectra were obtained with a 6500 + QTRAP triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (Applied Biosystems, Inc., Foster City, California) with a turbo ionspray source interfaced to a Sciex Exion UPLC system (Columbia, Maryland). Samples were chromatographed with a Kinetex, 2.6 μm, 2.1 × 50 mm column (Phenomenex, Torrence, CA) with a Phenomenex C18 filter frit guard cartridge. Calibration curves were linear between 10 and 750 ng/mL (r > 0.999) and accuracy of curves was within 15% except at the lower limit of quantitation (10 ng/mL based on signal to noise ratio of 10) where accuracy was within 20%. Precision of calibration curves and quality control samples (50 and 500 ng/mL) was within 5%.

Statistical analysis

The target number of patients enrolled in this study was seven. This was based on a previously published study which evaluated inter- versus intrapatient variability of chemotherapy kinetics in humans [15]. Available funding also limited enrollment of the number of subjects calculated by a standard power calculation. Statistical analyses were performed using Prism Version 10 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, California). The average coefficient of variation (CV) for AUC between treatment cycles was calculated for each patient. The degree of intrapatient and interpatient variability (%CV) were compared using a two-tailed Welch’s t-test following determination of normal distribution of data by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Least squares linear regression analysis was performed on predicted AUC against measured hematologic parameters. Spearman correlation was performed on predicted AUC versus GI toxicity scores. Correlation and linear regression analysis was additionally performed only for the subset of dogs whose measured nadir neutrophil counts were lower than baseline (“true nadir”). For all analyses, p-values less than 0.05 were considered significant.

Results

Ten patients met inclusion criteria and patient demographics are outlined in Table 1. Protocol deviations were permitted to enroll two small dogs (patients #3 and #8) who were prescribed a starting DOX dose of 1 mg/kg rather than the intended 30 mg/m2. Three patients only received one dose of doxorubicin while on trial, and were withdrawn due to owner-perceived constitutional and GI AEs. Since these patients only received one dose of DOX, data from these patients were only included when analyzing interpatient variability in DOX exposure of dose one. Seven patients received all three intended doses of doxorubicin, subsequent CBCs and GI toxicity questionnaires. These data were subsequently used in all analyses.

Hematologic parameters were evaluated for relationships with predicted AUC. No significant relationship was found between predicted AUC and absolute lymphocyte count, surviving fraction of lymphocytes, absolute platelet count, surviving fraction of platelets, absolute neutrophil count (Fig. 5A), absolute neutrophil count (nadir only) (Fig. 5B), or surviving fraction of neutrophils (Fig. 5C). It is important to note that although 7 days post-DOX is commonly reported as the nadir, this can be variable across dogs, and it is possible that for dogs whose day 7 neutrophil count was higher than baseline, the true nadir was missed. When two data points were removed (with a surviving fraction of neutrophils > 1), a significant relationship was found between AUC and surviving fraction of neutrophils for only those patients with day 7 neutrophil counts lower than baseline (nadir only) (Fig. 5D). The patients in this clinical study served as an external validation set for those on which the LS model was initially developed. Indeed, the model was again able to utilize the baseline neutrophil count and predicted AUC to predict the absolute nadir neutrophil count for data points with a surviving fraction of neutrophils < 1 with a mean prediction error of 3.3% and mean absolute prediction error of 41% (Fig. 6).

The patients who completed all three doses on study all started at a standard dose of either 30 mg/m2 or 1 mg/kg. Most had only minor dose adjustments due to normal fluctuation in weight or BSA, with only one patient receiving a > 20% dose reduction due to owner perceived AEs. Notably, the degree of reduction in dose in this patient (25%) was consistent with the subsequent reduction in exposure (25–32%).

Discussion

DOX continues to be one of the most commonly used injectable chemotherapeutics in dogs. Its side effect profile is well established and it has utility in both single and multi-agent chemotherapy protocols. However, there is a paucity of information to adapt its dosing based on appropriate PK parameters as compared to traditional BSA-based methods. For the first time in dogs, this study showed that the variability in intrapatient exposure to DOX across three cycles is significantly lower when compared to the variability between patients. Increased exposure was correlated to increased owner reporting of nausea and reduced appetite. Increased exposure to DOX was significantly associated with decreased surviving fraction of neutrophils in patients who had a decreased absolute neutrophil count at seven days compared to baseline. Lastly, it validated a previously established LS model of DOX AUC to predict absolute neutrophil count at the nadir. These data taken together suggest that the LS model might be used to personalize DOX dosing in dogs.

PK-guided dosing has been shown to be beneficial in single agent protocols, multi agent protocols, and in several tumor contexts in human cancer care. PK-guided dosing of fluorouracil in humans with colorectal cancer led to significantly improved objective response rates, a trend towards higher survival rates, and less severe toxicity than BSA-based dosing even though mean fluorouracil doses were higher in the PK-guided group [10]. Similarly, PK-guided dosing of paclitaxel in combination with cisplatin or carboplatin resulted in similar response rates and progression free survivals in a cohort of 365 patients with non-small cell lung cancer. The patients in this study who received PK-guided dosing of paclitaxel had significantly fewer high grade neuropathies compared to those who received standard dosing at 200mg/m2, suggesting PK-guided dosing can be used to ameliorate high grade AEs without mitigating response [16]. These are just a few examples of how strategies aside from BSA based dosing can be implemented to improve patient outcome. Since DOX is used in a wide variety of multi-agent protocols in addition to its use as a single agent, its implementation will need to be carefully considered based on its combination with other chemotherapies.

In this study, the %CV was calculated both before and after dose normalization to account for one patient who received a substantial dose reduction due to owner perceived AEs and another patient that was < 15 kg. Interestingly, the within patient variability in DOX exposure we found in our patient population (2.8%−9%) is lower than that seen in humans (6%−59%)17. Similarly, the between-patient variability in humans (37%−93%) is higher than what was seen in our patient population (24.6%−26%). It is important to note that the required inclusion/exclusion criteria and the limited number of dogs recruited to this study may not accurately represent the entire population of tumor bearing canines receiving DOX. The DOX metabolic pathway involves a multitude of genes including influx transporters (SLC family members), efflux transporters (ABC family members), and drug metabolizing enzymes (CBR family members) [18] among others. Breed associations have been made with ABC family member mutants [19]but the role of other genetic variants involved in DOX metabolism have not been fully described in dogs.

The significant AE profile of DOX has understandably resulted in its cautious clinical application. Specifically, its dose-limiting GI toxicity compared to other commonly used injectable chemotherapies [7] and a cumulative cardiotoxicity represent opportunities to optimize its dosing to minimize side effects. Owners were given questionnaires to objectify GI and constitutional side effects at home. Statistical correlations on these scores demonstrated increased AE scores with regard to appetite and nausea, but no significant relationship between average GI AE score, and total GI AE scores were observed in patients with higher predicted AUC. While statistical analysis was performed on the relationship between predicted AUC and GI AEs, it is important to note that the values used for GI AEs can be quite variable due to inherent owner bias in scoring subjective signs like appetite and nausea.

An obvious knowledge gap in implementing PK-guided DOX dosing is a clear understanding of what the target AUC should be to optimize patient outcome while minimizing AEs, and this likely varies from patient to patient. AUC guided dose adjustments dramatically improved the percent of patients within the therapeutic window from 28.3 to 68.9% and improved the disease control rate from 72 to 85% for human patients with solid tumors receiving docetaxel [20]. Additional information is needed to determine what the target AUC should be for patients receiving DOX. It then needs to be investigated if achieving the target AUC improves outcome and minimizes owner perceived AEs.

Alternative pharmacodynamic markers should also be assessed to validate their use as surrogate biomarkers of response. Neutropenia has been reported as a predictive marker in dogs and is associated with significantly prolonged first remission (812 versus 219 days) and survival times (952 versus 282 days) in patients receiving multi-agent chemotherapy protocols for high-grade lymphoma [21]. Circulating absolute neutrophil counts may serve as a simultaneous indicator of clinical efficacy and toxicity. In humans, chemotherapy induced neutropenia has been linked with improved outcomes in several tumor types [22, 23]. Importantly, PK guided dosing has also led to improved response rates in solid tumors as well [24]. Given DOX’s common use in treating both hematopoietic and solid tumors in veterinary medicine, it is crucial to ensure its dosage is optimized to provide the best possible clinical response.

Due to the limited number of patients in this study, it is essential to conduct further research involving a larger, more diverse population with varying tumor types. It will also be important to characterize the impact of dose escalation and reduction on predicted AUC and subsequent observed neutrophil count at the nadir using the LS model. Our experience with one patient that received a > 20% dose reduction suggests that a similar reduction in exposure can be expected. However, this observation must be validated in a larger population across a range of reductions and escalations. These next steps could then be applied to personalize dosing schemes for DOX as a single agent and in combination with other drugs.

Conclusions

The small degree of intrapatient variability seen in this study is a promising finding to implement strategies to reach target exposures following the first treatment cycle of DOX. We established that the LS model can be applied in a prospective manner to characterize within- and between-patient variability in AUC which holds promise for future efforts to personalize DOX dosing in dogs.
