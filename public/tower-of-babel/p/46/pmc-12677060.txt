Synthesis of corn-derived carbon quantum dots and their application in intracellular pH sensing and multicolor cellular imaging
Authors: Yue-Fang Hu, Yu-Xin Chen, Jin-Fang Li, Wen-Wang Wei, Xiu-Ning Xu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBiomass corn was utilized as both carbon and nitrogen precursors to synthesize nitrogen self-doped carbon quantum dots (N-CQDs) via a hydrothermal method. The N-CQDs were further used as a nano-fluorescent probe for multicolor cellular imaging and real-time intracellular pH sensing. Experimental results showed that the N-CQDs exhibited a quantum yield of 18.13%, along with excellent biocompatibility, outstanding photoelectrochemical properties, and pH-responsive fluorescence sensing capabilities. The fluorescence intensity of the N-CQDs nanoprobes showed a strong linear correlation with pH values in the range of 4.2–8.4. In live HeLa cells, the N-CQDs nanoprobes displayed high-performance multicolor imaging, and their blue fluorescence intensity also maintained a good linear relationship with pH (4.2–8.4). These findings indicate that the synthesized N-CQDs nanoprobes can be used for the semi-quantitative measurement of intracellular pH, holding great potential for biomedical applications such as cellular imaging and intracellular pH sensing.

Introduction

The pH value within lysosomes typically ranges from 4.5 to 5.5, while in mitochondria, it is approximately 8.0. Thus, the overall intracellular pH generally falls between 4.5 and 8.0.1 Intracellular pH takes a significant role in cellular metabolism, including processes such as apoptosis and proliferation. Unusual pH levels can lead to organelle dysfunction or indicate alterations in cellular functions, serving as a hallmark of various diseases.2 Therefore, measuring the pH and its fluctuations in living cells is crucial for biological research. To date, various methods for detecting intracellular pH have been developed. Among them, fluorescent probes with high resolution have garnered significant attention for live-cell pH sensing due to their non-invasiveness, high sensitivity, and excellent fluorescence imaging performance.3 So far, different kinds of fluorescent probes, including organic dye-based probes, fluorescent protein-based probes, and nanomaterial-based fluorescent probes, have been widely used for intracellular pH detection.4,5,6 However, organic dyes and fluorescent proteins suffer from drawbacks such as complicated procedures, rapid photobleaching, and the need for complex extraction and purification steps.7 Meanwhile, nanomaterial-based pH sensors also face limitations – semiconductor quantum dots exhibit toxicity,8 while upconversion nanoparticles,9 gold nanoparticles10 and silver nanoclusters,11 involve intricate synthesis processes and large particle sizes, restricting their broad application.

Carbon quantum dots (CQDs) are carbon-based fluorescent nanomaterials with simple preparation methods, a particle diameter of less than 10 nm, and numerous advantages such as excellent fluorescence properties, water solubility, and biocompatibility.12 In recent years, they have been successfully applied in fluorescence probes, pH sensing, live-cell imaging, and drug delivery.13 Under the concept of green chemistry, more researchers favor the green and low-cost synthesis of CQDs. Natural biomass, which is rich in carbon and nitrogen elements and widely available, is one of the most competitive and ideal carbon sources for CQDs preparation.14 As a result, various types of waste biomass have been utilized for CQDs synthesis.15 However, most CQDs synthesized from biomass suffer from low fluorescence quantum yield (QY), weak fluorescence intensity, and poor photoelectric performance, limiting their applications in analytical chemistry and life sciences. It has been reported that heteroatom doping (N, S, P) can enhance the fluorescence stability, quantum yield, and fluorescence intensity of CQDs while improving their photoelectric properties.14,16 Additionally, the introduction of N atoms can not only significantly enhance the QY and increase luminescence intensity by providing additional electronic states (n-π transitions) but also modulate the bandgap structure, enabling emission tuning from blue to red light, which is suitable for multicolor imaging and sensor design.12,17 In particular, N-CQDs with their unique surface chemistry, stable optical properties, and versatile modification strategies have achieved high sensitivity, a wide dynamic range, and excellent environmental adaptability in pH sensing. They serve as a powerful complement to traditional pH indicators and glass electrodes, particularly demonstrating significant advantages in miniaturization, biocompatibility, and multifunctional sensing applications.

Therefore, this study adopted biomass corn, rich in carbon and nitrogen elements, as a raw material to explore the optimal synthesis time and temperature for preparing N-CQDs via a one step hydrothermal synthesis. Morphology, surface functional groups, elemental composition, optical properties, and pH-sensing characteristics of the N-CQDs were investigated. Additionally, to evaluate the biotoxicity, optical properties, multicolor cellular imaging, and intracellular pH-sensing characteristics of the prepared biomass corn-derived N-CQDs, it has been further applied to the research into pH sensing within living cells.

Optimization of nitrogen self-doped carbon quantum dots synthesis conditions

To obtain N-CQDs with QY and small particle size, we optimized the synthesis conditions, including reaction time, temperature, and the mass concentration of corn solution. Optical properties of corn nitrogen self-doped carbon quantum dot fluorescence probe

To investigate the optical properties of the N-CQDs fluorescence probe, the experiment analyzed the UV-Vis absorption spectrum and fluorescence spectrum of N-CQDs.24 Figure 4A shows the UV-Vis spectrum of N-CQDs, revealing that the peak that appears at 273 nm is due to π-electron transition, which is caused by the π-π∗ transition of the C=C bond structure of N-CQDs. Figure 4B shows the maximum excitation wavelength (Ex) and the maximum emission wavelength (Em) fluorescence spectrum of N-CQDs probe. Analysis reveals that the Ex of N-CQDs is 360 nm, while the Em is 463 nm (Em1 and Em2 are the fluorescence spectra of N-CQDs at different concentrations after determining the optimal excitation (Ex = 360 nm)). The inset of Figure 4B shows photos of the corn N-CQDs fluorescence probe. It can be observed that the N-CQDs fluorescence probe appears brown (left) when exposed to daylight, while they emit intense blue fluorescence (right) when illuminated by a 365 nm ultraviolet lamp, indicating that the biomass-derived N-CQDs prepared from corn possess strong fluorescence intensity and a higher fluorescence quantum yield. 365 nm lies in the near-ultraviolet region and can effectively excite electron transitions (such as π-π∗ or n-π∗ transitions) in most CQDs, thereby producing visible blue-green fluorescence. If the excitation wavelength is too long, it may not provide sufficient energy to excite the luminescent centers of the CQDs; if it is too short, it may lead to photodegradation or non-radiative transitions. Additionally, 365 nm is a common wavelength for UV lamps, making the equipment readily available and cost-effective. Therefore, 365 nm is a “compromise choice” that balances excitation efficiency, universality, and operational convenience. As seen in Figure 4C and as the excitation wavelength increases from 320 nm to 480 nm, the maximum emission wavelength of the N-CQDs fluorescence probe gradually red-shifts, indicating an excitation-dependent luminescence behavior (The emission wavelength shifts with varying excitation wavelengths). This excitation-dependent property of N-CQDs fluorescence probes originates from their polydisperse small-size structures and the selective activation of specific emission centers by different excitation wavelengths, governed by their surface states.25 This unique characteristic enables the N-CQDs fluorescence probe to emit fluorescence of different colors (blue, green, and even red) under varying excitation wavelengths without the need for synthesizing multiple materials, demonstrating exceptional flexibility and broad potential.Figure 4UV-Vis and FL spectra of N-CQDs and CQDs(A) UV-Vis spectrum of N-CQDs.(B) Ex and Em spectra of N-CQDs, inset: photos under daylight and 365 nm UV light irradiation.(C) Emission wavelengths of N-CQDs at different excitation wavelengths.(D) UV-Vis spectrum of CQDs.(E) Ex and Em spectra of CQDs. inset: photos under daylight and 365 nm UV light irradiation.(F) Emission wavelengths of CQDs at different excitation wavelengths.

Analysis of the stability of the nitrogen self-doped carbon quantum dot fluorescence probe

In practical applications, CQDs with superior fluorescence stability are crucial for widespread utilization across various fields. In this experiment, N-CQDs, CQDs, and the commercial dye fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) fluorescence probe were exposed to xenon lamp irradiation (intensity: 100 mW/cm2), with their fluorescence intensity recorded at 5-min intervals to assess photostability. As depicted in Figure 5A, the fluorescence emission wavelengths of both N-CQDs and CQDs fluorescence probes remained nearly constant over time. After 1 h of irradiation, the fluorescence intensities of N-CQDs and CQDs fluorescence probe retained over 90% of their initial values, demonstrating excellent fluorescence stability. In contrast, as shown in Figure 5A, the FITC exhibited significant photobleaching, with fluorescence intensity dropping to nearly zero after 3 min of irradiation. It indicates that N-CQDs and CQDs fluorescence probes exhibit significantly better photostability than commercial dyes and organic fluorophores, along with remarkable fluorescence durability and resistance to photobleaching.25Figure 5The influence of different xenon lamp irradiation times and NaCl concentration on the fluorescence intensities of N-CQDs, CQDs, and FITC(A) The influence of irradiation times on the fluorescence intensities of N-CQDs, CQDs, and FITC.(B) Effects of different NaCl concentrations on the fluorescence intensity of N-CQDs and CQDs.

The electrochemical performance of the nitrogen self-doped carbon quantum dot fluorescence probe

CQDs with excellent electrochemical performance typically possess abundant surface functional groups (-COOH, -OH, -NH2). These groups can undergo protonation/deprotonation reactions with H+/OH− and enhance electron transfer efficiency, thereby significantly improving the sensitivity and response speed of pH sensing.26 Generally, the more outstanding the electrochemical performance of a fluorescent probe, the more capable it is of detecting minute pH variations. In this study, the electrochemical performance of N-CQDs and CQDs fluorescence probes was evaluated through cyclic voltammetry (CV) and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS). As shown in Figure 6A, the cyclic voltammograms of both CQDs and N-CQDs modified glassy carbon electrodes (GCE) exhibit closed curves, each with a pair of redox peaks. Notably, the reduction and oxidation peaks of the N-CQDs-modified GCE appear more symmetrical, indicating that after N-CQDs are deposited on the electrode surface, their abundant surface functional groups enhance electrocatalytic activity and improve conductivity. Consequently, a fast and reversible reaction occurs.27 In contrast, the conductivity of the CQDs-modified GCE shows negligible improvement, resulting in a slower, irreversible reaction.17 The more symmetrical and pronounced redox peaks of the N-CQDs-modified GCE demonstrate that the N-CQDs probe exhibits superior electrochemical performance compared to CQDs.Figure 6CV and EIS diagram of CQDs and N-CQDs modified electrode(A) CV diagram of CQDs and N-CQDs modified electrode.(B) EIS diagram of N-CQDs/GCE, CQDs/GCE, and bare electrode.

This study also used EIS to analyze the electrochemical performance of the N-CQDs probe.16 Figure 6B displays the Nyquist plots of bare/GCE, CQDs/GCE, and N-CQDs/GCE. As observed in the figure, bare/GCE exhibits the smallest impedance among the three, corresponding to the red curve.

When the bare/GCE surface is modified with CQDs, the impedance increases significantly, appearing as a distinct semicircle (black curve). Further modification with N-CQDs results in an even greater impedance, represented by a larger semicircle (blue curve), indicating higher resistance after N-CQDs modification. This phenomenon occurs because both CQDs and N-CQDs carry negative charges on their surfaces, and N-CQDs possess more negative charges, leading to stronger electrostatic repulsion with the negatively charged Fe(CN)63−/4-, thus resulting in a more pronounced increase in resistance.27 Both CV and EIS results demonstrate that N-CQDs exhibit superior electrochemical performance, higher sensitivity for pH sensing, and greater potential for application in intracellular and extracellular pH sensing research.

Analysis of the pH sensing performance of nitrogen self-doped carbon quantum dot fluorescent probes

It has been reported that nitrogen doping enhances the pH sensitivity of N-CQDs fluorescent probes by modulating surface chemistry, electronic structure, and defect states. In order to verify the sensitivity of the corn N-CQDs probe for pH sensing, the fluorescence intensity of the N-CQDs probe was detected at different pH values (3.0, 3.5, 4.2, 4.8, 5.3, 6.0, 6.5, 7.0, 7.5, 8.0, 8.4, and 9.5), and the corresponding spectral profiles were obtained. As shown in Figures 7A and 7B, the intensity of the N-CQDs probe gradually increased as the pH rose from 3.0 to 9.5. However, when the pH exceeded 8.4, the increasing trend of fluorescence intensity began to plateau. The inset of Figure 7B reveals a strong linear correlation between fluorescence intensity and pH within the range of 4.2–8.4. The linear regression equation was determined as F = 105.81pH-169.11, with a correlation coefficient (R2) of 0.9985. The linear fluorescence-pH relationship suggests the protonation/deprotonation of the surface functional groups -COOH and -NH2. Based on the XPS data, it can be inferred that within the acidic range, the primary pH-sensitive group is -COOH. At pH 4.0–5.0, -COOH predominantly remains in its protonated form, while under neutral to alkaline conditions, it deprotonates to -COO-, increasing the surface negative charge, which may influence fluorescence through electrostatic interactions. In the pH 6.0–8.0 range, both-COOH and -NH2 groups contribute to pH sensitivity, exhibiting a synergistic effect where successive deprotonation/protonation leads to significant fluorescence changes.Figure 7Fluorescence spectra and pH-dependent fluorescence intensity changes in N-CQDs and CQDs under various pH values(A) Fluorescence spectra of N-CQDs.(B) Fluorescence intensity changes in N-CQDs.(C) Fluorescence spectra of CQDs.(D) Fluorescence intensity changes in CQDs.

The results indicate that the corn N-CQDs pH-sensing probe features a wide linear detection range and requires no chemical modification, making it suitable for full-range intracellular pH sensing. Monitoring intracellular pH in the range holds significant physiological and pathological importance in clinical medicine. Tumor cells often exhibit an alkaline intracellular pH alongside an acidic extracellular environment. This pH gradient is associated with cancer aggressiveness, metastasis, and drug resistance, making pH monitoring crucial for assessing tumor metabolic status and treatment response. Additionally, metabolic disorders such as diabetes can disrupt intracellular pH homeostasis. For instance, insulin resistance has been linked to acidosis in skeletal muscle cells. Intracellular pH monitoring provides key insights into disease mechanisms, evaluates therapeutic efficacy, and supports the development of treatments, demonstrating particular promise in oncology and metabolic disorder research. Cytotoxicity and interference test

Cytotoxicity assays serve as a critical bridge between basic research and clinical applications, significantly reducing late-stage development risks and safeguarding human health and environmental safety. The cytotoxicity of corn-derived N-CQDs fluorescent probes was investigated using the MTT. As shown in Figure 8A, no change in the cell viability of HeLa cells until it reaches the cytotoxic inhibitory concentration (IC50 = 481.3 ± 15.7 μg/mL),and maintained excellent viability (91.5%) even at high N-CQDs concentrations up to 600 μg/mL, demonstrating robust cell growth. The potential interference of cellular molecules or ions with the fluorescence intensity of N-CQDs probes is critical for their pH-sensing performance in cells. To evaluate this, we added 30 μL of 20 mmol/L solutions of common cellular molecules or ions to 170 μL of N-CQDs aqueous solution (containing 20 μL PBS buffer at pH 7.4). After 30 min of incubation, the fluorescence intensity of each mixture was measured three times. As shown in Figure 8B, at pH 7.4, the fluorescence intensities of all mixtures containing potential interferents remained nearly identical to that of the pure N-CQDs solution. These results clearly demonstrate that common intracellular molecules and ions do not interfere with the pH-sensing capability of the N-CQDs probes in cellular environments.

In this study, we developed N-CQDs derived from biomass corn as a highly sensitive pH-sensing probe for monitoring the full dynamic range of intracellular pH and multicolor cellular imaging. This work presents a green synthesis strategy for corn-based N-CQDs and demonstrates their application in pH-responsive fluorescence imaging, offering some insights and technical references for intracellular biosensing. Experimental results have verified that the as-prepared N-CQDs probe exhibits excellent stability, better optical performance, minimal cytotoxicity, high-performance multicolor imaging, and outstanding pH-sensitive detection capability. It is suggested that corn-derived N-CQDs probes hold great promise for applications in cytology and broader biomedical research.

Limitations of the study

The response to minor pH changes (such as less than 0.5 units) may not be sensitive enough, which affects the accurate detection of subcellular environments (such as lysosomes and mitochondria).

Synthesis of N-CQDs

Corn was dried using a vacuum drying oven and then ground into fine powder using a pulverizer to serve as carbon and nitrogen sources. The synthesis conditions were optimized, with the best reaction time determined to be 8 h and the optimal temperature at 200 °C. The detailed procedure is as follows: 10 g of corn flour was placed in a beaker with 40 mL of ultrapure water, oscillated to form a homogeneous mixture, and then was placed in a 50 mL reaction still, then was put into an oven at 200 °C for hydrothermal reaction for 8 h. The resulting yellowish-brown liquid was centrifuged at 4000 r/min for 20 min, and the supernatant was collected. The supernatant was dialyzed in a dialysis bag for 72 h to obtain yellowish-brown N-doped carbon quantum dots (N-CQDs) solution, which was stored at 4 °C for further use. For comparison, a set of undoped pure carbon quantum dots (CQDs) was synthesized under the same conditions using watermelon peel powder.

Study on the fluorescence quantum yield and stability of N-CQDs

Fluorescence quantum yield (QY) refers to the ratio of the number of emitted photons to the number of absorbed photons (usually expressed as a percentage) when a fluorescent material absorbs sufficient light. This value reflects the luminescent properties of the material. In this experiment, quinine sulfate was selected as a standard to ensure the accuracy of the measurement results. Furthermore, a reference method was used to determine the quantum yield of the fluorescent carbon quantum dots (CQDs). This method calculates the quantum yield by measuring the relationship between the fluorescence intensity and the fluorescence emission wavelength of the CQDs. The fluorescence quantum yields of N-CQDs and CQDs were calculated using the following formula:Q1 = Q2×A2/A1×I1/I2×(μ1/μ2) 2

In the above equation: A represents absorbance, Q represents fluorescence quantum yield, μ represents refractive index, I represents integrated fluorescence intensity, the subscript 1 denotes the sample N-CQDs (or CQDs), and the subscript 2 denotes quinine sulfate.

NaCl solutions with concentrations of 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, and 210 mmol/L were prepared separately. Then, 2 mL of N-CQDs or CQDs was mixed with 2 mL of each NaCl solution at different concentrations. The mixtures were sonicated to ensure thorough homogenization and then transferred into 4 mL fluorescence cuvettes. The fluorescence intensity was measured using a fluorescence spectrophotometer.

Study on fluorescence sensing performance of pH value

In a set of centrifuge tubes, add 100 mL of N-CQDs solution (15 mg/mL) into 5.00 mL of buffer solutions with different pH values (3.0, 3.5, 4.2, 4.8, 5.3, 6.0, 6.5, 7.0, 7.5, 8.0, 8.4, 9.5), respectively. This will yield N-CQDs mixed solutions at different pH levels. Then, scan their corresponding fluorescence spectra under 330 nm excitation.

Study on cytotoxicity

The biotoxicity of corn-derived N-CQDs was investigated using the MTT (Thiazolyl Blue Tetrazolium Bromide) assay to assess cell viability and growth. N-CQDs solutions with mass concentrations of 0, 100, 200, 300, 400, 500, 600 μg/mL were prepared and added to six culture dishes containing HeLa cells, respectively. Then, 20 μL of MTT solution (20%) was added, followed by incubation for 24 h. Subsequently, 100 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) was added and incubated for 15 min. Optical measurements were performed on the cells, and the survival rate of HeLa cells was calculated to evaluate the biotoxicity of nitrogen self-doped carbon quantum dots derived from corn.

Study on cell multi-color imaging and intracellular pH value sensing

After digesting 4T1 cells with trypsin, the cell suspension was seeded into cell culture dishes and cultured in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C for 24 h. The 4T1 cells were washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then incubated with 10 μg/mL N-CQDs and cell culture medium at 37°C for 40 min. After washing three times with PBS (pH 7.4), the cells were observed under a confocal microscope using different excitation wavelengths.

After digestion with trypsin, HeLa cells were cultured in an incubator with 5% CO2 at 37°C for 24 h. The cells were then washed three times with PBS buffer, followed by the addition of 15 mg/mL nitrogen self-doped carbon quantum dots (N-CQDs) derived from corn and fresh cell culture medium. The cells were further incubated at the same temperature for 4 h and washed three times. The N-CQDs-loaded HeLa cells were incubated in different pH buffers (4.2, 5.3, 6.0, 7.0, 8.4) for 30 min (the control group was treated with an equal volume of buffer solution instead of N-CQDs and incubated under the same pH conditions). Subsequently, the cells were observed and imaged using a confocal laser scanning microscope under a 405 nm excitation wavelength.
