Solid phosphate buffers boost CO2 capture performance and enable energy-lean operation in amine-functionalized adsorbents
Authors: Shichao Zhang, Yang Liu, Yingping Huang, Chenxu Wang, Junju Mu, Chaofeng Zhang, Di Huang, Chuncheng Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAmine-functionalized solid adsorbents represent promising next-generation materials for efficient CO2 capture. However, their practical deployment is constrained by a fundamental trilemma balancing CO2 adsorption capacity, adsorption kinetics and regeneration energy efficiency. Herein, we resolve this challenge through catalytic proton shuttle engineering by incorporating sodium dihydrogen phosphate into tetraethylenepentamine-functionalized mesoporous silica gel (HP-TEPA/MSG). The phosphate modifier exhibits dual functionality which not only enhances TEPA dispersion within mesopores to improve mass transfer efficiency, but also establishes atomic-scale proton transfer networks through buffer microdomains that accelerate proton shuttling during adsorption-desorption cycles. Compared to unmodified TEPA/MSG, the optimized 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbent achieves 18.7% higher CO2 capacity, 28% faster adsorption kinetics (the time required to reach 90% of the saturated adsorption capacity) and 27% lower regeneration energy. This work resolves the persistent capacity-kinetics-energy trilemma through proton-coupled reaction engineering, establishing a new paradigm for designing energy-lean carbon capture materials. Subject terms: Sustainability, Carbon capture and storage

Introduction

Anthropogenic climate change has emerged as a defining challenge of the 21st century, one of the main causes of which is the massive emissions of CO2 and other greenhouse gases. According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), global CO2 emissions reached 37.8 billion tonnes in 2024, reflecting a 0.8% year-on-year increase, underscoring the urgent imperative for decarbonization1. Carbon capture, utilization, and storage (CCUS) technologies constitute a critical pathway toward achieving deep decarbonization and net-zero emissions targets under the Paris Agreement, recognized as essential components of global climate mitigation strategies2–4. However, a fundamental barrier persists: the capture stage consumes 60-80% of total energy input in typical CCUS projects; therefore, developing energy-efficient capture technologies represents a pivotal prerequisite for large-scale CCUS deployment5.

While monoethanolamine (MEA) absorption remains the most mature CO2 capture technology, its inherent limitations, including equipment corrosion, solvent management challenges and high regeneration energy, motivate the development of advanced alternatives. Solid amine-functionalization adsorbents have emerged as next-generation candidates, offering compelling advantages: corrosion-resistant operation, good equipment compatibility, and lower energy consumption for CO2 regeneration6–8. The CO2 capture performance of solid amine adsorbents is critically governed by both amine loadings and dispersion uniformity within the pore structure of supports. While high amine loadings could provide increased CO2 adsorption sites to enhance capacity, it simultaneously introduces critical trade-offs: (i) such as high adsorption capacity but low adsorption rate, in which the excessive amine aggregation obstructs mesoporous channels, compromising mass transfer and reducing adsorption kinetics; (ii) higher energy requirements, where these dense amine-sites may strengthen CO2 binding affinity, elevating regeneration energy consumption9–12. Consequently, resolving the fundamental trilemma among “CO2 adsorption capacity-adsorption kinetics-regeneration energy efficiency” constitutes a critical challenge that must be overcome for the industrial implementation of solid amine-functionalization adsorbents.

The CO2 capture kinetics of amine-based adsorbents are predominantly governed by mass transfer limitations when the adsorption temperature is below 75 °C, directly correlated with the spatial dispersion uniformity of amines within the porous structure of the support13. Enhancing amine distribution homogeneity in porous structures facilitates improved diffusion pathways, thereby accelerating adsorption rates. The Chen group developed a PEI-modified ordered “pocket structure” solid amine CO2 adsorbent, which could maximize the accessibility of amine sites by confining PEI chains within the exposed nanostructured domains on the surface, achieving a 24% increase in CO2 capacity and a 13.2% improvement in cycling stability14. Our prior work demonstrated that single-site Ni anchoring effectively suppresses TEPA aggregation through coordination interactions, boosting kinetic performance by 1.58-fold. However, this strategy incurred an inherent trade-off: Ni-amine coordination occupied part of the active sites, reducing theoretical CO2 capacity15. The regeneration kinetics and energy consumption of solid amine CO2 adsorbents could be optimized by leveraging insights from liquid amine systems: introducing solid acid catalysts enhances proton transfer efficiency to thermodynamically accelerate desorption and reduce regeneration energy demands16–19. However, a critical divergence exists: for the MEA solution absorption system, dynamic contact between carbamate and solid acid catalysts enables efficient proton donation, and mobile phases facilitate full catalyst accessibility, which is beneficial for promoting CO2 desorption. For a solid amine-functionalization adsorbent system, spatially constrained catalyst accessibility and limited proton diffusion pathways could compromise proton transfer efficiency. To effectively resolve the fundamental trilemma of “CO2 adsorption capacity-adsorption kinetics-regeneration energy efficiency” in solid amine-functionalization adsorbents, a dual-optimization strategy is essential: (i) Enhance amine dispersion within the support mesoporous structure to maximize accessible adsorption sites and facilitate mass transfer. (ii) Establish efficient proton transfer networks among loaded amines to catalyze carbamate decomposition and reduce CO2 regeneration energy consumption.

In this work, we engineer atomic-scale proton transfer networks through strategically incorporated phosphate buffer microdomains, significantly enhancing proton shuttling efficiency during CO2 adsorption-desorption cycles (Scheme 1), thereby improving the CO2 adsorption performance of the adsorbent. Concurrently, phosphate mediation optimizes TEPA dispersion uniformity within the mesoporous structure. These dual innovations are materialized in a series of phosphate-modified TEPA/MSG adsorbents, whose CO2 capture performance, “encompassing adsorption capacity, sorption kinetics, regeneration energy efficiency and cyclic stability”, is systematically investigated to elucidate the underlying enhancement mechanisms. This study establishes a new paradigm for designing energy-lean carbon capture materials through proton-coupled reaction engineering.

Results and discussion

Comparison of the CO2 adsorption–desorption performance of various adsorbents prepared

The adsorption-desorption performance and regeneration energy consumption of the prepared CO2 adsorbents were investigated and evaluated using a fixed-bed system. Figure 1a demonstrates the CO2 adsorption performance of the different adsorbents, and the CO2 capacity of the adsorbents modified with phosphates was significantly improved. Compared to the TEPA/MSG adsorbent, the 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbent exhibited an increased CO2 adsorption capacity from 2.03 mmol/g to 2.41 mmol/g, representing an 18.7% enhancement (Fig. S1). It is noteworthy that the CO2 adsorption curve of all HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbents became steeper compared to that of the TEPA/MSG, suggesting enhanced mass transfer in HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbents. To elucidate the adsorption rate differences, normalized CO2 adsorption performance curves of TEPA/MSG and 3HP-TEPA/MSG were systematically analyzed as shown in Fig. 1b. Compared to TEPA/MSG, the 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbent demonstrated accelerated adsorption kinetics: the time required to reach 50% CO2 saturation capacity (t50) decreased from 2.4 min to 1.9 min (20.8% reduction), meanwhile achieving 90% saturation (t90) took only 9.8 min versus 13.6 min (28% faster), demonstrating phosphate modification-induced adsorption rate improvement. Furthermore, the CO2 adsorption performance was also evaluated through breakthrough curve analysis, where the breakthrough point was defined as the moment when the outlet CO2 concentration reached 10% of the inlet concentration (Fig. 1c)20–22. The breakthrough time of 3HP-TEPA/MSG has been extended from 143 s to 166 s with a 16.1% prolongation compared to TEPA/MSG. Combined with its accelerated adsorption kinetics and enhanced CO2 uptake capacity, these results demonstrate that 3HP-TEPA/MSG could be beneficial for improving the adsorption efficiency within the specified working time.

The desorption rate and energy consumption are also crucial for a complete CO2 capture process; the desorption performance of various adsorbents has also been further evaluated. According to Fig. 1d, it can be observed that the CO2 desorption rates of the phosphate-modified adsorbents were also improved, with 3HP-TEPA/MSG exhibiting the optimal CO2 desorption performance, where the CO2 desorption rate could reach up to 15 mmol/min under a nitrogen flow rate of 50 mL/min. For solid amine CO2 adsorbents, the enhancement of the adsorption-desorption rate is often related to the mass transfer and activation energy during CO2 capture. We hypothesize that the introduction of sodium dihydrogen phosphate could, on the one hand, improve dispersion of TEPA within the mesoporous support structure via phosphate-TEPA interactions, and thus enhance the mass transfer. More importantly, on the other hand, the HPO42-/PO43- buffer system could act as a proton shuttle mediator, facilitating the transfer of protons between carbamate zwitterions (RNH2+COO-) and ammonium carbamate (RNHCOO-), thereby reducing the activation energy during adsorption and desorption processes23. The activation energies (Ea) for CO2 adsorption of 3HP-TEPA/MSG and TEPA/MSG were evaluated based on the Arrhenius curves (Fig. S2) and determined using the Arrhenius equation (Equation S1). As shown in Fig.1e, the Ea value of 3HP-TEPA/MSG (14.8 kJ/mol) was significantly lower than that of TEPA/MSG (20.5 kJ/mol), indicating enhanced adsorption kinetics due to phosphate modification. A lower Ea value may be conducive to improving the desorption efficiency of CO2 and reducing energy consumption24. As shown in Fig. S3, TEPA/MSG exhibits a heat release of 68.5 kJ/mol, while 3HP-TEPA/MSG exhibits 62.6 kJ/mol. This reduction indicates that the modified adsorbent exhibits weaker CO2 binding affinity due to the decreased basicity of the amine groups, which thermodynamically favors desorption. Therefore, the energy consumption of various HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbents for CO2 desorption relative to TEPA/MSG was investigated, and the relative heat duty was calculated based on Equation S225. As shown in Fig. 1f, all HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbents exhibited lower CO2 regeneration heat duty compared to pristine TEPA/MSG, with the 3HP-TEPA/MSG demonstrating the most significant 27% reduction (73% of TEPA/MSG’s baseline). The reduction in CO2 regeneration heat absorption of 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbent was further verified through the differential scanning calorimetry result (Fig. S4). These findings substantiate the crucial role of the phosphate-enabled proton shuttle mechanism, which could synergistically enhance both adsorption-desorption kinetics and energy-efficient regeneration.

Dispersion of TEPA in pore structure and proton transfer ability of adsorbents characterization

The dispersion state of TEPA within the support pore structure critically governs CO2 adsorption-desorption kinetics by modulating mass transfer efficiency. Therefore, N2 physisorption analysis was conducted to explore the TEPA distribution patterns before and after phosphate modification within the support structure. The N2 adsorption-desorption isotherms and pore size distribution of MSG support and all adsorbents are shown in Fig. 2a, b and Fig. S5a and summarizes the pore-structural information in Table S1. Both TEPA/MSG and HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbents exhibited significantly reduced N2 adsorption capacities compared to the MSG support (Fig. 2a), attributed to partial pore volume occupation by the loaded TEPA. However, both adsorbents retain their mesoporous structure, with a large hysteresis loop at 0.45 < P/P0 < 0.9, which facilitates enhanced mass transfer and improved adsorption-desorption kinetics during CO2 capture processes15. Notably, despite identical 30 wt% TEPA loadings, the two adsorbents exhibited significant differences in pore structural properties. The TEPA/MSG adsorbent displayed a specific surface area of 90 m2/g and a pore volume of 0.36 cm3/g, both lower than those of the 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbent (128 m2/g and 0.46 cm3/g, respectively), as summarized in Table 1. In addition, differential pore volume (dV/dD) analysis of the pore size distribution profiles revealed critical structural modifications: both TEPA/MSG and 3HP-TEPA/MSG exhibited markedly reduced dV/dD increments in the pore size range of 2–12 nm compared to the MSG support; the dV/dD pore volume increment in the pore size range of 11-13 nm was gradually decreasing until disappeared (Fig. 2b), indicating partial pore occupation by loaded TEPA. The N2 adsorption-desorption characterization results showed that 3HP-TEPA/MSG exhibited superior structural retention compared to TEPA/MSG under the same amine loading conditions, indicating that the phosphate modification enhanced the dispersion uniformity of TEPA and alleviated the pore blockage in the MSG pore structure.

The loading of TEPA in the support was explored by FT-IR, as shown in Fig. 2c and Fig. S5b. The characteristic absorption peaks at 1105 , 808 , and 466 cm-1 observed in the FT-IR spectra of MSG, TEPA/MSG and 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbents are assigned to the asymmetric stretching vibration, symmetric stretching vibration, and bending vibration of Si-O-Si bonds in the support framework, respectively4,26. The TEPA/MSG and 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbents exhibited new characteristic absorption peaks corresponding to organic functional groups after TEPA loading: the absorption peaks at 3200–3500 cm-1 were attributed to the stretching vibration of primary amine and the variable angle vibration; the absorption peak at 1314 cm-1 was attributed to the C-N stretching vibration, and the absorption peaks at 2941 cm-1and 2836 cm-1 were attributed to the antisymmetric and symmetric stretching vibration of CH227,28. These spectroscopic evidences confirm the successful immobilization of TEPA within the MSG framework. However, it is noteworthy that the 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbent exhibited two distinct N-H stretching vibrations at 3200–3500 cm-1 region (3452 and 3275 cm-1) while TEPA/MSG displayed a single broadened absorption peak (Fig. 2c and Fig. S6), which may be due to the agglomeration of TEPA molecules induced by hydrogen bonding interactions between the amine groups15,29. This result is consistent with the characterization results of N2 physical adsorption.

The enhanced adsorption-desorption rates of the 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbent are related to enhanced mass transfer on the one hand, and more importantly, the possible facilitation of the interconversion between carbamate zwitterions (RNH2+COO-) and ammonium carbamate (RNHCOO- & RNH3+) via a proton shuttle mechanism on the other hand. Since proton transfer and electron transfer are related features, the proton transfer capability of the synthesized adsorbents was evaluated via electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements (Fig. 2d). Compared to the TEPA/MSG adsorbent, HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbents after phosphate-modified TEPA exhibited a smaller radius of the Nyquist curve, indicating better proton transfer efficiency18. This enhanced proton transfer efficiency could originate from the phosphate-mediated Grotthuss-like proton shuttle mechanism, which facilitates rapid H+ hopping along HPO42-/PO43-&RNH2+COO- networks.

The formation of a proton shuttle network was validated by XPS, 31P NMR, and Raman spectroscopy, as shown in Fig. S7 and Fig. 3. The 31P NMR spectrum of individual HP shows a single peak at 2.66 ppm (Fig. 3a). Upon incorporation into the adsorbent, this peak shifts to 2.55 ppm in 3HP‑TEPA/MSG. This clear upfield shift confirms a specific electronic interaction between the phosphate species and the amine groups of the TEPA molecule, which indicates the formation of a phosphate buffer microenvironment conducive to proton shuttling. In addition, from the XPS characterization results (Fig. S7), it can be seen that the proportion of amine peaks attributed to protonation significantly increased in the N 1 s high-resolution of 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbent, and the presence of P-element peaks further confirmed the interaction between HP and TEPA.

Raman spectroscopic analysis is also further employed to validate the proton shuttle mechanism facilitated by the phosphate buffer microenvironment during CO2 adsorption, as presented in Fig. 3b. Prior to CO2 exposure, the 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbent exhibited characteristic Raman peaks of phosphate species, with distinct peaks at 935 cm-1 and 440 cm-1, assigned to the symmetric P-O stretching and O-P-O bending vibrations, respectively. Following CO2 adsorption, the original peak at 935 cm-1 disappeared, while a new emergent peak at 976 cm-1 was observed, corresponding to the P-OH stretching vibration of hydrogen phosphate (HPO42-), where this transformation provides direct evidence of the conversion between PO43- and HPO42- species. Furthermore, the O-P-O bending vibration originally located at 440 cm-1 underwent a discernible blue shift to 451 cm-1 after CO2 adsorption (Fig. 3c). This shift is attributed to the introduction of protons (H+), which disrupts the symmetry of P-O bonds and increases bond strength, resulting in a higher vibrational frequency. Collectively, these results confirm that the phosphate buffer pair serves as an efficient proton carrier, enabling enhanced proton transfer during the CO2 adsorption process.

The morphological and structural evolution of MSG adsorbents after TEPA or HP-TEPA functionalized were observed by transmission electron microscopy (TEM), as shown in Fig. 4. The pristine MSG support exhibited an amorphous morphology (Fig. S8), and the distinct TEPA coating layers were visually confirmed on both adsorbents. Notably, the 3HP-EPA/MSG adsorbent (Fig. 4d, e) displayed a remarkably homogeneous and smooth surface compared to TEPA/MSG (Fig. 4a, b), suggesting better dispersion of TEPA under the same loadings (Fig. S9). Moreover, the atomic ratio of (C + N)/(Si+O) derived from the semi-quantitative analysis of elemental mapping (Fig. 4c, f) revealed distinct differences in surface TEPA coverage: the ratio of 3.05 for TEPA/MSG is significantly higher than that of 3HP-TEPA/MSG (2.11). About the 31% reduction (Table S2) in organic-to-inorganic elemental ratio conclusively demonstrates the excellent dispersion of TEPA in 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbent, as the lower ratio indicates reduced surface aggregation and deeper penetration of TEPA into the mesoporous framework. This result is also in agreement with the results of BET and FT-IR.

Catalytic acceleration mechanism of CO2 adsorption-desorption

The CO2 adsorption and desorption mechanism of TEPA/MSG and 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbents were investigated by In-situ FT-IR spectroscopy, 13C NMR and DFT, as shown in Fig. S10 and Fig. 5. The 13C NMR spectra of both adsorbents before and after CO2 capture are presented in Fig. S10. The signal between 35-60 ppm labeled as 1–3 is assigned to alkyl carbons signals within the TEPA molecular framework30. Upon CO2 adsorption, new resonances emerged at 164.6 ppm (C4) and 162.2 ppm (C5), corresponding to carbonyl carbons formed through carbamate species generation with primary or secondary amines, respectively31–33. Notable chemical shift differences were observed between the adsorbents, which could be due to the phosphate-modified adsorbent enhanced amine protonation states mediated by the HPO42-/PO43- buffer system. During CO₂ adsorption, characteristic infrared absorption peaks emerged at specific wavenumbers: the symmetric and asymmetric stretching vibrations of COO- groups were observed at 1416 cm-1 and 1572 cm-1, respectively34,35. The absorption peaks at 1489 cm-1 and 1630 cm-1 were assigned to the symmetric and asymmetric bending vibrations of protonated amine groups (-NH3+); while the stretching vibration of N-COO- species appeared within the 1320–1330 cm-1 range (Fig. S11)34,35. The progressive intensification of these spectral signatures over time quantitatively corroborated the dynamic accumulation of CO2 adducts during the adsorption process. More importantly, the rapid growth rate of the FT-IR signal of 3HP-TEPA/MSG is much larger compared to that of TEPA/MSG, indicating that the adsorbent 3HP-TEPA/MSG has a faster adsorption rate.

In addition, the temporal evolution of carbamate species during thermal regeneration was monitored via time-resolved FT-IR spectroscopy. For the TEPA/MSG adsorbent (Fig. 5a, b), the peak intensities of COO- (1572 cm-1) and N-COO- (1325 cm-1) exhibited gradual attenuation, decreasing by 70% and 83% at 3 min and 5 min (Fig. 5e), respectively. In stark contrast, the 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbent (Fig. 5c, d) demonstrated accelerated carbamate decomposition kinetics, with about 90% reduction in COO- peak intensity within 3 min (Fig. 5e)36. This marked divergence in the FT-IR peaks intensity of CO2 adsorption and desorption species indicates that phosphate modification accelerates proton transfer, greatly improves the adsorption and desorption dynamics, and could reduce the energy consumption of CO2 regeneration. Further, complementary density functional theory (DFT) calculations revealed further thermodynamic enhancements: the transition-state energy barrier for CO₂ desorption in 3HP-TEPA/MSG was reduced to 0.91 eV, representing an 18.8% decrease compared to TEPA/MSG (1.12 eV). This dual reduction in kinetic and thermodynamic barriers could originate from phosphate-mediated proton shuttle acceleration effects: HPO42-/PO43- buffer facilitates Grotthuss-like proton relay. Therefore, we propose a phosphate-mediated proton shuttle-accelerated CO2 adsorption-desorption mechanism in the 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbent, as schematically illustrated in Fig. 5g. For the CO2 adsorption process: CO2 initially reacts with amine groups (-NH2) in TEPA, forming metastable zwitterionic intermediates (RNH2+COO-); The labile proton on the zwitterion is rapidly transferred to adjacent phosphate species (PO43-), generating HPO42-; The HPO42- subsequently donates a proton to neighboring amine groups, yielding stabilized -NH3+ species. This phosphate-buffered proton transfer circumvents the energy-intensive direct proton migration between amines. For the desorption process of CO2: RNH-COO- retrieves protons from HPO42-, generating RNH-COOH intermediates; Thermal activation induces zwitterion reconfiguration of RNH-COOH and subsequently releases CO2. This bidirectional proton shuttle system achieves a 28% increase in adsorption kinetics rate and a 27% reduction in CO2 regeneration heat duty compared to conventional pathways. In addition to rapid adsorption-desorption kinetics and low regeneration energy, cyclic stability proves critical for practical CO2 capture applications. As shown in Fig. S12, the CO2 adsorption capacity of the TEPA/MSG decreased by 9.8% from 2.03 mmol/g to 1.84 mmol/g after 5 cycles, while the CO2 adsorption capacity of the 3HP-TEPA/MSG declined marginally from 2.43 mmol/g to 2.38 mmol/g (2.1% loss), indicating that the 3HP-TEPA/MSG adsorbent has better stability. What’s more, we conducted a ten‑fold scale‑up preparation of the adsorbent and evaluated its CO2 adsorption performance. The normalized CO2 adsorption curves are presented in Fig. S13. The results demonstrate that the scaled‑up sample retains similarly fast adsorption kinetics, while the cost increase is less than 3‰ (Table S3). The above results demonstrated that HPO42-/PO43- buffer could serve as a “dynamic proton relay” not only synergistically optimizing the reaction thermodynamics and mass transfer efficiency, but also enhancing the cyclic stability of the adsorbent. These findings indicate that the proposed phosphate‑modification strategy holds promising potential for industrial scalability.

Conclusions

In summary, this work resolves the fundamental capacity-kinetics-energy trilemma in amine-functionalized adsorbents through a phosphate-mediated dual-engineering strategy. The improvement of TEPA dispersibility enhances mass transfer and maintains the accessibility of CO2 and amine adsorption sites, accelerating CO2 diffusion and boosting adsorption kinetics by 28% (the time required to reach 90% of the saturated adsorption capacity). More importantly, atomic-scale proton transfer networks via HPO42-/PO43- buffer microdomains catalyze zwitterionic intermediates transformation, synergistically increasing CO2 capacity by 18.7% while reducing regeneration energy demand by 27%. By strategically orchestrating proton management and nanoscale amine distribution, this work overcomes traditional trade-offs between adsorption performance metrics, establishing a blueprint for energy-lean carbon capture materials.

Methods

The preparation of various adsorbents

Amine-functionalized solid CO2 adsorbents were prepared using the physical impregnation method15. Typically, NaH2PO4 at varying concentrations (0–4.75 wt%) was dissolved in 4 mL of deionized water, respectively, followed by continuous stirring for 30 min. Then, add 0.3 g TEPA and continue stirring for 1 h. After that, the 0.7 g support of MSG was added into the HP-TEPA aqueous solution, and the flask was placed on the shaking table for dynamic impregnation at 30 °C and 150 r min-1 for 12 h. Then the aqueous solution was removed by using a rotary evaporator, and subsequently dried the samples in a vacuum drying oven at 80 °C for 4 h. The prepared adsorbents were referred to as xHP-TEPA/MSG, where x represents the weight percentage of NaH2PO4 loading.

Characterization

The changes of specific surface area, pore volume and pore size distribution of adsorbents before and after amine-functionalization were measured by N2 physical adsorption on Kubo-SSA-7000. The adsorbents need to be degassed at 100 °C for 4 h before testing. The total surface area was calculated by the Brunauer-Emmett-Teller (BET) equation, and the pore volume and pore size distribution were calculated according to the BJH method. Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy results were collected on the Thermo Scientific Nicolet iS50. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) images of the samples were obtained on a JEM-F200, equipped with an energy dispersive spectrometer (EDS). X-ray powder diffraction was performed on SmartLab. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was measured on the AXIS Supra. Raman spectra were acquired using the Thermo Fisher DXR3 instrument. The 13C NMR and 31P NMR spectra of the samples with deuteroxide (D2O) as the solvent were measured on a Bruker Avance III 600 MHz spectrometer (13C: 150 MHz, 31P: 243 MHz).

Theoretical calculation

The geometric optimizations for all adsorbed, desorbed, and transition states were performed at the ωB97X-D4/ma-def2-SVP level of theory37,38 using the ORCA 6.0 software39. Two different models were used. For the absence of phosphate, simulate the reaction using two TEPA molecules and one CO2 molecule. When phosphate is introduced, one TEPA molecule in the model is replaced by a phosphate molecule. For these two cases, geometric optimization was performed on the adsorption state, desorption state, and transition state between them. Additionally, Gibbs free energy corrections at 298.15 K and 1 atm were computed. Subsequently, high-accuracy single-point energy calculations were carried out on all optimized geometries using the revDSD-PBEP86-D4/2021/ma-def2-TZVP level of theory to obtain precise electronic energies40. These energies were then combined with the Gibbs free energy corrections to yield high-accuracy Gibbs free energies.

CO2 adsorption performance test

CO2 adsorption performance was carried out using a fixed-bed adsorption device. Typically, about 100-300 mg of adsorbent was placed in a quartz reaction tube and pretreated at 110 °C for 30 min with 50 mL min-1 of N2 to remove moisture and other residual species, before the measurements. Then the temperature was cooled to a desired adsorption test temperature, and the gas was adjusted to a mixture of 15% CO2 (balanced with N2) to start adsorption with a total flow rate of 50 mL min-1 for 35 min. For cycle performance measurement, after the adsorption of the adsorbent was completed, the temperature was increased to 110 °C and kept at a constant temperature for 20 min for desorption and the gas was switched to N2. Then repeat the above adsorption and desorption steps to test the cycle performance of the adsorbents.
