Discovery of a potent covalent inhibitor for the treatment of sepsis targeting DNAJC9: Identification of a druggable target for inflammation
Authors: Xingjun Li, Tao Yang, Hui Huang, Yalan Ma, Lu Liu, Qingqing Li, Naixia Zhang, Zhizhen Hui, Yazhou Li, Fang Bai, Qiangqiang Chen, Jiang Wang, Chen Zhou, Hu Zhou, Wei Tang, Hong Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSepsis is a leading cause of mortality among patients in intensive care units (ICUs), with no specific treatments for sepsis available in clinical practice. In this study, we report the discovery of compound A12, a tanshinone derivative featuring a covalent warhead, which demonstrates potent anti-inflammatory effects both in vitro and in vivo. In a lipopolysaccharide-induced mouse model of endotoxemia and acute systemic inflammation, A12 significantly reduces levels of TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-12. Distinct from current anti-inflammatory small molecules, A12 presents a unique mechanism, covalently binding to DNAJC9, thereby modulating the HSP complex and inhibiting the downstream inflammatory response. This work represents the first application of covalent drug design principles to the structural modification of the natural product tanshinone, and reveals the previously unrecognized role of DNAJC9 in inflammatory diseases, offering a novel therapeutic target for management of sepsis-associated acute systemic inflammation.Key words: Tanshinone derivatives, Covalent inhibitor, Proteomic analysis, Target identification, DNAJC9, HSP complex, Anti-inflammatory, Sepsis

Introduction

Sepsis is characterized by organ dysfunction caused by a dysregulated host response to infection1. Globally, approximately 18 million new cases of sepsis are reported each year, with a mortality rate ranging from 28% to 40%2,3. For patients experiencing septic shock, the mortality rate is even higher, between 50% and 85%, making sepsis one of the leading causes of death in intensive care units (ICUs) worldwide4,5. Despite its high incidence and poor prognosis, there are no specific pharmacological treatments available for sepsis. The pathogenesis of sepsis remains insufficiently understood, with excessive inflammation being a major driver of disease progression, leading to multi-organ dysfunction and injury6, 7, 8, 9. Timely and appropriate regulation of the inflammatory response could improve outcomes for sepsis patients10,11. Therefore, understanding the inflammatory status and developing anti-sepsis drugs based on inflammation therapy is of critical importance and could help impact the treatment landscape for this life-threatening condition.

Tanshinone IIA, the most abundant lipophilic component of the traditional Chinese medicine Salvia miltiorrhiza, exhibits a range of pharmacological activities, including anti-inflammatory, anti-oxidant, anti-cancer, and anti-atherosclerotic effects, making it a promising lead compound12,13. Our research team has been dedicated to the structural modification and pharmacological study of natural products, successfully constructing a library of tanshinone derivatives with diverse scaffold structures. Through phenotype screening of this compound library in LPS-induced RAW264.7 cells, we identified a series of anti-inflammatory tanshinone small molecules that effectively reduce TNF-α and IL-6 secretion levels. Further exploration of the structure–activity relationship led to the discovery of compound A12, an anti-inflammatory tanshinone small molecule whose weak anti-inflammatory activity was enhanced approximately 100-fold to submicromolar levels by the introduction of a covalent warhead. Additionally, oral administration of compound A12 significantly decreases pro-inflammatory cytokines TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-12 in an LPS-induced mouse model of septic systemic acute inflammation, effectively suppressing the inflammatory response.

Target exploration and identification remain a challenging aspect of drug discovery for natural products. Given the promising in vivo and in vitro anti-inflammatory activity of compound A12, we pursued further target discovery studies. Employing a rational molecular probe design strategy, we designed and synthesized two positive probes and one negative probe, leading to the rapid identification of the target protein DNAJC9 (Dnaj Heat Shock Protein Family (HSP40) Member C9), and elucidated the unique anti-inflammatory mechanism of A12. Furthermore, we validated the role of DNAJC9 in inflammation, and it is hypothesized that A12 exerts its anti-inflammatory effects through covalent interaction with DNAJC9, thereby influencing HSP complexes and inhibiting downstream inflammatory responses.

Given that DNAJC9 is a key co-chaperone involved in the regulation of HSP70, its interaction with A12 may influence the broader HSP70 chaperone system, which is known for its critical role in cellular protein homeostasis and its involvement in modulating inflammatory responses. HSP40 (DNAJ) is a crucial component of the HSP70 chaperone system, regulating HSP70 protein function by stimulating ATP hydrolysis14, 15, 16, 17. HSP70s, produced abundantly under stress conditions, play a pivotal role in maintaining protein homeostasis18,19. Additionally, intracellular HSP70 exhibits anti-inflammatory properties by inhibiting the production and pro-inflammatory effects of cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-12, and IL-120, 21, 22, 23, 24.

Here, we report the discovery of A12, a novel anti-inflammatory small molecule covalent inhibitor identified through phenotype-based drug screening. Through a covalent drug design strategy, we have significantly enhanced the modest anti-inflammatory activity of tanshinone IIA by approximately 100 times with the introduction of covalent warheads in the design of A12. A12 demonstrates remarkable efficacy in inhibiting the occurrence of cytokine storm and significantly reducing the expression of TNF-α and IL-6 in both in vitro LPS-induced inflammation models and in vivo sepsis mice models of systemic acute inflammation. Importantly, through the design and synthesis of chemical probes, and chemoproteomic approaches, we discovered that A12 exerts its anti-inflammatory effects by inhibiting DNAJC9. Genomic and proteomic studies have identified DNAJC9 as a novel anti-inflammatory target. Molecular biological experiments further elucidated its role in inflammation regulation, offering a novel avenue for the development of anti-inflammatory drugs. This novel mechanism, unveiled through positive and negative probes, illustrated a previously unexplored pathway for anti-inflammatory drug development. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first report of the role of DNAJC9 in inflammation pathogenesis and regulation, providing a novel target for anti-inflammatory drug development.

Materials and methods

Mice

Animal experiments utilized inbred 6 to 8-week-old male C57BL/6 mice procured from Shanghai Lingchang Biotechnology Co., Ltd. All mice were housed under specific pathogen-free conditions in a controlled environment (12 h light/dark cycle, 60%−80% humidity, 22 ± 1 °C). Mice underwent a one-week acclimatization period before the commencement of experiments. All procedures adhered to the guidelines outlined in the National Institutes of Health Guides for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and were approved by the Bioethics Committee of the Shanghai Institute of Materia Medica, Chinese Academy of Sciences (IACUC: 2022-07-TW-109). For the LPS-induced inflammation model, mice were orally administered compound A12 0.5 h before or after intraperitoneal injection with 5 mg/kg LPS. Serum samples were collected to assess cytokine levels.

Cell culture

The RAW264.7, THP1 and U937 cell lines were purchased from the American Type Culture Collection (ATCC, Manassas, VA, USA). Cells were cultured in DMEM or RPMI 1640 medium (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (HyClone, Logan, UT, USA), 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 μg/mL streptomycin. Cells were maintained in a 5% CO2 humidified incubator at 37 °C.

Bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) were harvested from the femur and tibia bones of C57BL/6 mice, following previously established protocols25. The BMDMs were cultured for 7 days in Iscove’s Modified Dulbecco’s Medium (IMDM; Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 10 ng/mL of mouse colony-stimulating factor (M-CSF, Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA).

Anti-inflammatory activity assay

For the evaluation of anti-inflammatory activity, mouse macrophage cell line RAW264.7 cells (1 × 105 cells) were cultured in DMEM medium. Subsequently, the cells were treated with the compounds and LPS (1 μg/mL, Sigma‒Aldrich, MO, USA) for 24 h. Compound A12 was incubated simultaneously with LPS stimulation. The levels of TNF-α and IL-6 in the cell culture supernatants were measured using mouse-specific ELISA kits.

Reactive oxygen species assay

Cells were collected and rinsed in PBS. Fresh FBS-free medium containing H2DCFDA (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) was added and incubated at 37 °C for 20 min. Subsequently, the cells were detached in PBS, washed twice, and resuspended in PBS. ROS production was detected by flow cytometric analysis.

siRNA transfection

To knock down DNAJC9 in BMDMs and RAW264.7 cells, cells were transfected with siRNA (Ribobio, Guangzhou, China) and mixed with Lipofectamine® RNAiMAX Reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) in serum-free opti-MEM medium. Cells were collected and assayed after 48 h of transfection.

RNA isolation and real-time quantitative PCR

Total RNA was isolated from cell samples utilizing the Total RNA Extraction Purification Kit (Tiangen, Beijing, China), adhering strictly to the manufacturer’s protocol. Following this, cDNA was produced from the isolated RNA using the cDNA Synthesis SuperMix Reverse Transcription Kit (Yeasen, Shanghai, China). For real-time quantitative PCR analysis, SYBR Green Realtime PCR Master Mix (Yeasen) was employed in conjunction with the 7500 Fast Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems, Foster City, CA, USA) using gene-specific primers. The primer sequences used for RT-qPCR are listed below.

Western blotting

The cells underwent lysis through repeated pipetting with SDS lysis buffer. Subsequently, protein samples were boiled for 10 min, separated via SDS-PAGE gel electrophoresis, and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). These membranes were then blocked using SuperBlock™ T20 blocking buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) and exposed to appropriately diluted primary antibodies. Signal detection was performed using the ChemiDoc™ MP Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

Immunofluorescent staining

BMDMs cultured on coverslips were fixed in a fixing solution (Beyotime) for 10 min, followed by treatment with blocking buffer (Beyotime). Subsequently, the cells were incubated with rabbit anti-HSP70 overnight. Following washing with washing buffer (Beyotime), AF647-conjugated anti-rabbit antibody was applied. After a 1 h incubation at room temperature, the cells were washed and counterstained with DAPI. Imaging was performed using a Leica TCS SPS CFSMP microscope (Wetzlar, Germany).

RNA-seq and analysis

The RAW264.7 cells were extracted to obtain the total RNA. The RNA sequencing and analysis were conducted by Annoroad Gene Technology Corporation (Beijing, China). RNA integrity and concentration were assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). A total amount of 2 μg RNA per sample was used as input material for the RNA sample preparations. Sequencing libraries were generated using NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (#E7530L, NEB, USA) following the manufacturer’s recommendations and index codes were added to attribute sequences to each sample. The clustering of the index-coded samples was performed on a cBot cluster generation system using HiSeq PE Cluster Kit v4-cBot-HS (Illumina) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. After cluster generation, the libraries were sequenced on an Illumina platform and 150 bp paired-end reads were generated. Then, the libraries were constructed and sequenced on an Illumina platform. Raw data (raw reads) of fastq format were firstly processed using Trimmomatic and the low-quality reads were removed to obtain the clean reads. The clean reads were mapped to the mouse genome. Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq. P adjust value < 0.05, |log2FC| >2 was set as the threshold for significant differential expression. Hierarchical cluster analysis of differentially expressed genes (DEGs) was performed to demonstrate the expression pattern of genes in different groups and samples. GO functional enrichment and KEGG pathway analysis were carried out by Goatools and Python scipy, respectively.

Plasmid transfection

U937 cells were cultured with 50 ng/mL PMA for 24 h. The PMA-U937 cells were then transfected with pCDNA3.0_DNAJC9 using the Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (Thermo Fisher Scientific) for 48 h.

Pull-down assay

Cell lysate was extracted from RAW264.7 cells using lysis buffer containing 1% NP-40 and protease inhibitor cocktail, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. Biotin-tagged probes and biotin as a control were coupled with streptavidin magnetic beads (Thermo) for 2 h at room temperature. Cell lysate was then incubated with probe-coupled beads overnight at 4 °C. Beads were washed with the wash buffer (PBST containing 0.05% Tween20 and 0.1% SDS) for 10 times and PBS for 5 times. Binding proteins were eluted by 2 × loading buffer and separated by 10% SDS-PAGE gels followed by staining with Coomassie brilliant blue (CBB) and in-gel digestion as described previously26.

LC‒MS/MS analysis

Recombinant protein samples for molecular weight detection were loaded on an Agilent PLRP-S column (1.0 mm × 50 mm, 5 μm) and separated using a 12 min gradient (hold at 5% B for 4 min, 5%‒90% B for 6 min, 90%‒2% B for 2 min) at a flow rate of 0.300 mL/min. Mobile Phase A was 0.1% formic acid (FA) in water, while Mobile Phase B was 0.1% FA in acetonitrile (ACN). The protein was detected with an Agilent 6545 Q-TOF mass spectrometer (Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA) equipped with an electrospray ionization ion (ESI) source. The parameters of mass spectrometry instrument were set as the follows: the dry gas flow rate was set 10.0 L/min at 325 °C, the nebulizer was set at 50 psig, the capillary voltage was set at 4.5 kV and the scan range was from 500 to 1700 m/z.

The peptides from in-gel digestion experiment were resolved in 0.1% FA (phase A) and analyzed on a nanoElute LC system coupled with a timsTOF Pro mass spectrometer (Bruker, Germany). The peptides were loaded on a custom column (75 μm × 20 cm, 1.5 μm ReproSil-Pur C18 resin, Dr. Maisch GmbH, Germany) and separated with a gradient for 60 min as follows: 2%‒4% B (0.1% FA in ACN) in 1 min; 4%‒26% B in 47 min; 26%‒32% B in 5 min; 32%‒90% B in 2 min at a flow rate of 250 nL/min, followed by 90% B for 5 min at a flow rate of 300 nL/min. Source capillary voltage was set to 1500 V in positive ion mode and dry gas flow to 3 L/min at 180 °C. The MS was operated in data-dependent mode for the ion mobility-enhanced spectral library generation. The scanning range of MS1 full scan was 100‒1700 m/z. The ramp time was set to 100 ms and the collision energy was ramped linearly as a function of the mobility from 59 eV at//K0 = 1.6 Vs/cm2 to 20 eV at 1/K0 ‒0.6 Vs/cm2. The overall acquisition cycle of 1.17 s comprised one full TIMS-MS scan and 10 parallel accumulation-serial fragmentation (PASEF) MS/MS scans.

MS data analysis

Raw MS data for recombinant protein mass analysis were processed using MassHunter BioConfirm software (Version 10.0, Agilent, Santa Clara, CA, USA). Raw MS data from timsTOF was analyzed by MaxQuant software (version 1.6.7.0) by searching against the UniProt Mus musculus database downloaded from Uniprot (http://www.uniprot.org). The FDR was set to 1% for both peptide and protein identification. Carbamidomethyl cysteine was searched as a fixed modification, oxidized methionine and protein N-term acetylation as variable modifications. Trypsin was chosen as the digestive enzyme with two missing cleavages. The LFQ intensity values were used for the following data analysis. The mass spectrometry data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium via the PRIDE partner repository with the dataset identifier (project accession No: PXD053467). The protein groups were filtered to eliminate the identifications from the common contaminants and reverse database. Only proteins that contain at least 1 unique peptide were used for subsequent analysis. To calculate the fold change of protein abundance between groups, the intensity of proteins without quantitative value was set to 100.

Protein sample preparation

The gene encoding human DNAJC9 was cloned into the pGEX-6P-1 and pET28a expression vectors. Site-directed mutagenesis was performed on the pET28a-DNAJC9 construct to generate the single mutants (C7A, C150A, and C243A) and the triple mutant (C7A/C150A/C243A). For protein expression, the plasmids were transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) pLysS competent cells. The transformants were cultured at 37 °C until the OD600 reached 0.6-0.8, followed by induction with 0.5 mmol/L isopropyl β-D-1-thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG). After overnight incubation at 20 °C, cells were harvested by centrifugation and resuspended in PBS. GST-tagged DNAJC9 was purified using Glutathione Sepharose 4B resin (Cytiva). The bound protein was eluted with glutathione-containing buffer and exchanged into cleavage buffer to remove glutathione. After overnight digestion with human rhinovirus (HRV) 3C protease at 4 °C, the cleaved GST tag and uncleaved fusion protein were removed by reverse glutathione affinity chromatography. The tag-free protein was further purified by size-exclusion chromatography (SEC) on an ÄKTA FPLC system equilibrated with buffer containing 500 mmol/L NaCl, 2.7 mmol/L KCl, 10 mmol/L Na2HPO4 and 2 mmol/L KH2PO4 (pH 7.4). His-tagged DNAJC9 proteins, including the wild-type and mutant proteins, were purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography followed by SEC using the same buffer. Human HSP70 was cloned into the pET28a-SUMO vector and the resulting plasmid was transformed into E. coli BL21 (DE3) pLysS cells. The transformants were cultured at 37 °C to an OD600 of 0.6-0.8, induced with 0.2 mmol/L IPTG, and incubated overnight at 18 °C. After harvesting by centrifugation, cell pellets were resuspended in 50 mmol/L Tris-HCl, 500 mmol/L NaCl (pH 8.0). SUMO-tagged HSP70 was purified by Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. After elution with imidazole-containing buffer, the protein was exchanged into imidazole-free buffer and digested overnight with ULP1 protease at 4 °C. The cleaved SUMO tag, uncleaved fusion protein, and His-tagged ULP1 protease were removed by reverse Ni-NTA affinity chromatography. The tag-free HSP70 was further purified by SEC on an ÄKTA FPLC system using a buffer containing 20 mmol/L KH2PO4, 20 mmol/L KOH, 75 mmol/L KCl, 2 mmol/L DTT (pH 7.0).

Thermal shift assay

Thermal shift experiments were performed using QuantStudioTM 5 (ABI, USA). Each reaction system contains 10 × SYPRO Orange, 10 μmol/L protein sample and A12 at different concentrations (0.1, 0.5 and 1 mmol/L) dissolved in 20 μL of thermal shift assay buffer (500 mmol/L NaCl, 2.7 mmol/L KCl, 10 mmol/L Na2HPO4, 2 mmol/L KH2PO4, pH 7.4). The mixture samples were heated from 25 to 99 °C at 0.05 °C/s Ramp rate, and the melting curves were processed by using Graphpad Prism 5.0.

DARTS assay

The assay was performed as previously described27. In short, cell lysate extracted from RAW264.7 cells was diluted to 5 μg/μL with TNC buffer (50 mmol/L Tris‒HCl, 50 mmol/L NaCl, 10 mmol/L CaCl2) followed by treatment with indicated concentrations of A12 or DMSO. After incubation for 1 h at room temperature, 5 μg/mL pronase was added to degrade cell lysate for 30 min at room temperature. Finally, 5 × loading buffer was added to terminate the reaction, followed with analysis by Western blotting.

NMR spectroscopy

The ligand observed CPMG (Carr-Purcell-Meiboom-Gill) and STD (saturation transfer difference) experiments were acquired at 25 °C on Bruker 600 MHz NMR spectrometer equipped with a cryogenically cooled probe with the utilization of the standard Bruker pulse sequences cpmgpr1d and stddiffgp19.3. Experimental samples contain 200 μmol/L A12 and 5 μmol/L DNAJC9 protein in NMR buffer (20 mmol/L Na2HPO4/NaH2PO4, 100 mmol/L NaCl, 2% DMSO, pH 7.4). The spectra were processed by using MestReNova x64.

Histopathological examination

Mouse tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned. To conduct histopathological analysis, the paraffin sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). Histopathology was evaluated using a semi-quantitative scoring system as previously described28,29.

NADH-coupled ATPase assay

The ATPase activity of HSP70–DNAJC9 system was measured by tracking the decrease in the absorbance at 340 nm for 90 min at an interval of 60 s using a regenerating enzyme-coupled assay. The reaction was carried out in the 96-well plate with a 100 μL volume and the reaction buffer consisted of 100 mmol/L Tris (pH 7.4), 20 mmol/L KCl, 1 mmol/L phosphoenol pyruvate (Sigma‒Aldrich), 0.6 mmol/L NADH, 2 μL pyruvate kinase/lactate dehydrogenase enzymes (Sigma‒Aldrich), 6 mmol/L MgCl2, 1 mmol/L DTT, 2 mmol/L ATP, and 2% (v/v) DMSO. The reaction mixture contained 2 μmol/L HSP70 and 10 μmol/L DNAJC9, either in the absence or presence of A12 (100 or 200 μmol/L). DNAJC9 was pre-incubated with A12 for 0, 2, or 4 h prior to initiation of the ATPase reaction. Measurements were initiated by adding the HSP70-DNAJC9 mixture, with or without A12, and carried out at 37 °C using a SpectraMax M5 plate reader. Raw data were analyzed as previously described30. ATPase rates were calculated and expressed as turnover per μmol/L HSP70 (1/min). All experiments were performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as means ± SEM at least 3 independent experiments. GraphPad Prism software was applied for statistical analysis. The significance of differences was evaluated by Student’s t-test between groups or by one-way ANOVA followed by the Tukey’s test in multiple groups. P < 0.05 was defined as statistically significant.

Results

Enhancing the anti-inflammatory activity of tanshinone IIA by introducing covalent warhead

Tanshinone IIA exhibits some anti-inflammatory activity (IL-6 IC50 > 30 μmol/L, TNF-α IC50 > 50 μmol/L), however, its activity is relatively weak. To enhance the anti-inflammatory activity of tanshinone IIA, we introduced covalent warhead at the C1 position of tanshinone IIA, to improve its anti-inflammatory activity, and designed and synthesized a series of novel tanshinone IIA derivatives. These compounds were screened for in vitro anti-inflammatory activity using an LPS-induced RAW264.7 cell model. The covalently modified derivatives A1‒A12 exhibited significant inhibition of IL-6 and TNF-α secretion, with IC50 values ranging from 0.67 to 4.51 and 0.87 to 13.21 μmol/L, respectively (Supporting Information Table S1), greatly surpassing the activity of Tanshinone IIA. Among them, the derivative A12, featuring a (E)-4-(dimethyl amino) but-2-enamide terminal covalent warhead, demonstrated the most potent in vitro anti-inflammatory activity, with IC50 values of 0.87 μmol/L for IL-6 and 0.67 μmol/L for TNF-α (Fig. 1a and Supporting Information Table S1). A12 demonstrated superior potency compared to the reference controls Tanshinone IIA and apremilast, validating the reliability of our screening platform (Supporting Information Fig. S1).

Encouraged by the potent in vitro activity, we extensively evaluated the translational potential of A12. Pharmacokinetic analysis revealed that A12 possesses favorable oral bioavailability (10.7%) and systemic exposure (Supporting Information Table S2). To verify whether the structural modification translated into superior therapeutic efficacy, we performed a head-to-head comparison between A12 and its parent compound, Tanshinone IIA. Remarkably, oral administration of A12 exhibited significantly stronger suppression of serum cytokines than Tanshinone IIA in both prophylactic and therapeutic dosing regimens (Fig. 1b, Supporting Information Fig. S2a, b). Histological examination further confirmed that A12 remarkably alleviated LPS-induced alveolar thickening and intestinal mucosal injury (Supporting Information Fig. S2c). At a dose of 10 mg/kg, the oral administration of A12 exhibited an inhibitory effect comparable to that of the positive control dexamethasone, administered intraperitoneally at 5 mg/kg, demonstrating good in vivo anti-inflammatory effects (Fig. 1b). Moreover, A12 effectively restored systemic homeostasis, as evidenced by the prevention of sepsis-associated hypothermia and the blockade of late-stage inflammatory mediators (e.g., iNos) at 24 h (Supporting Information Fig. S3). In the clinically relevant CLP model, A12 treatment significantly extended the survival window of septic mice (Supporting Information Fig. S4). These comprehensive in vivo data justify A12 as a robust lead candidate for further mechanistic investigation.

RNA-Seq analysis of compound A12 in RAW264.7 cells

To elucidate the mechanisms underlying the therapeutic potential of compound A12 in inflammatory diseases, RNA-Seq analysis was conducted. In LPS-stimulated RAW264.7 cells, 253 genes were significantly upregulated, while 987 genes were downregulated compared to untreated RAW264.7 cells (Fig. 2a). Heatmap visualizations revealed distinct expression patterns among the sample groups, highlighting significant differences between them (Fig. 2a). Subsequent Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis identified several perturbed signaling pathways, with notable enrichment in the NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 2b). Gene Ontology biological process (GOBP) and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis indicated that the cellular functions notably impacted by compound A12 were primarily associated with the inflammatory response (Fig. 2b). Notably down-regulated genes included Il-6, Il-1β, and Nos2, all recognized as pivotal pro-inflammatory genes, a finding corroborated through qPCR validation (Fig. 2c). Furthermore, our investigations unveiled that A12 markedly suppressed the generation of reactive oxygen species in macrophages under inflammatory conditions (Fig. 2d). Comparable anti-inflammatory activity of A12 was also confirmed in primary mouse macrophages (BMDM) and human macrophages (PMA-THP1) (Fig. 2e and f).

Identification of DNAJC9 as a direct target of A12

The potent inhibitory effect of A12 on macrophages prompted us to identify its molecular target. To avoid affecting the specific binding between A12 and the target, and also considering the feasibility of synthesis, we chose to introduce a biotin molecule into the D ring for the design of the probe molecule. Three biotinylated probes (B1, B2, and B3) were designed and synthesized based on the A12 structure (Fig. 3a). Among them, B1 and B2 retained significant anti-inflammatory activity, whereas B3 was largely inactive. Consequently, B1 and B2 were designated as active probes, while B3 served as the negative probe (Supporting Information Table S3). A pull-down assay was then conducted to isolate potential protein targets of A12 from RAW264.7 cell lysates. Briefly, the probes were immobilized on streptavidin-coated beads and incubated with the lysates. The bound proteins were subsequently digested with trypsin and analyzed by liquid chromatography‒tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS). Relative protein abundance across the four experimental groups was compared using a label-free quantitative approach (Supporting Information Table S4).

(c) Venn diagrams illustrate the overlap of significantly enriched protein targets between experimental groups. The top diagram compares B1 and B2 against the biotin control (B), while the bottom diagram further excludes targets also detected in the negative probe (B3) group. The 964 proteins in group B3 are those not enriched (no quantitative data available). (d) GO biological process enrichment analysis of potential targets overlapped in B1 and B2 groups. The numeric labels beside the columns represent the count of genes enriched per biological process. (f) List of four candidate targets which were enriched in both B1 and B2 groups but not in B and B3 groups. (g) Western blot analysis of biotinylated A12 probes pull-down experiments (left). Compared to the biotin-treated control group (B), 103 and 113 proteins were significantly enriched in the positive probe groups B1 and B2, respectively (Fig. 3b). Between the two positive probe groups, 77 proteins overlapped (Fig. 3c). These overlapping proteins are primarily involved in biological processes such as RNA processing and immune response (Fig. 3d). To enable statistical analysis of protein abundance, protein intensities that were not quantified in the control group were uniformly assigned a value of 100. Among these, 30 proteins showed significant enrichment in both B1 and B2 compared to the control (B1 vs B and B2 vs B, P < 0.05; Fig. 3e). To further narrow the scope of target identification, a joint analysis was performed using the negative probe (B3). This analysis identified only four proteins that were significantly enriched in both B1 and B2 but were not detected in B3 as potential targets (Fig. 3b). Among these, DNAJC9 exhibited the highest fold-change values in both B1 and B2, with P-values < 0.05 in both group comparisons (Fig. 3f), suggesting that DNAJC9 may be a key potential target for the anti-inflammatory effect of A12.

Next, pull-down analysis validated the interaction between A12 and native DNAJC9 through Coomassie Blue staining and immunoblotting (Fig. 3g, Supporting Information Fig. S5b). To rigorously exclude non-specific binding, we confirmed that control fragments containing only the linker and biotin moieties failed to enrich DNAJC9 or inhibit cytokine release (Supporting Information Fig. S5a‒c). To validate the direct physical engagement between A12 and DNAJC9, a drug affinity responsive target stability (DARTS) assay was performed. This assay is based on the biophysical principle that ligand binding can thermodynamically stabilize the target protein structure, thereby reducing protease accessibility and increasing resistance to enzymatic degradation. As shown in Fig. 3g, treatment with A12 markedly enhanced the proteolytic tolerance of DNAJC9 compared with the vehicle control. Notably, these results observation reflects direct binding and structural engagement of A12 with DNAJC9, rather than stabilization of DNAJC9 expression levels or biological activity.

Characterization of the interaction between DNAJC9 and compound A12

To investigate the interaction between the target DNAJC9 and compound A12, thermal shift assay was first applied to determine whether A12 could directly interact with DNAJC9. A negative Tm shift for DNAJC9 was observed upon the addition of the compound A12, suggesting that the presence of the compound A12 could lead to the decreased thermal stability of DNAJC9. These results indicate that A12 can bind to DNAJC9 (Fig. 4a). In line with the thermal shift assay data, positive results were observed for ligand observed CPMG and STD spectra of DNAJC9 : A12 mixture. Upon the addition of DNAJC9, the NMR resonances of A12 underwent significant line broadening and attenuation as revealed by the CPMG spectra, which indicates that A12 could interact with DNAJC9. Additionally, positive STD signals were also detected for A12 in response to the presence of DNAJC9, which further supports the interaction between the compound A12 and DNAJC9 (Fig. 4b). In addition, LC‒MS analysis was used to detect the difference between mass spectra of DNAJC9 treated with or without A12, the results showed that the protein molecular weight of DNAJC9 increased stably by 492 Da and indicated that one A12 molecule covalently bound to one DNAJC9 monomer (Fig. 4c). To identify the specific residue modified by A12 through its cysteine-targeting acrylamide warhead, we performed site-directed mutagenesis on the three cysteine residues of DNAJC9 (C7, C150, and C243). Single mutants (C7A, C150A, and C243A) and a triple mutant (C7A/C150A/C243A) were generated. A molecular weight shift indicative of A12 modification was observed in the C7A and C150A mutants, but not in the C243A or the triple mutant (Supporting Information Fig. S6a‒d). These results demonstrate that A12 specifically and covalently modifies the C243 residue of DNAJC9. Further surface plasmon resonance (SPR) experiments measured a Kd value of 12.8 μmol/L (Fig. 4d). These results indicate that compound A12 can covalently bind to the target protein DNAJC9.

Target validation of A12

HSP40/DNAJ proteins represent the largest subfamily within the HSP family, playing a crucial role in modulating the activities of HSP70 and HSP90 proteins31, 32, 33. Among these, DNAJC9 stands out for its function in integrating ATP-resourced protein HSP70 and influencing HSP70-mediated inflammatory response34, 35, 36. Studies have linked the DNAJC9 gene to carcinogenesis, suggesting its potential utility as a candidate biomarker for breast cancer survival, while few studies have been concerned with its role in inflammatory responses37,38. To investigate the involvement of DNAJC9 in inflammation, a siRNA-mediated DNAJC9 knockdown system was introduced in RAW264.7 cells or primary macrophages. Upon DNAJC9 knockdown, there was a dramatic decline in key mediator release, such as TNF-α, IL-6 and IL-12, suggesting its pivotal role in modulating the inflammatory process (Fig. 5a and b and Supporting Information Fig. S8a and b). Crucially, the addition of A12 to DNAJC9-knockdown BMDMs failed to result in significant further reduction in inflammation compared to the siRNA-alone group (Fig. 5b). Moreover, RNA-seq analysis outlined some noteworthy pathways in the anti-inflammatory properties of A12, such as the NF-κB pathway. A certain association between the NF-κB pathway, especially IκBα regulation and DNAJC9-mediated biological effects was further highlighted by a significant inhibition in IκBα phosphorylation upon A12 treatment or DNAJC9 knockdown (Fig. 5c). Since HSP70 is generally recognized as the key protein integrates with DNAJC9, further investigation was designed to focus on intracellular events involving HSP70. Intriguingly, our data revealed a marked promotion of HSP70 aggregation in the nucleus upon A12 treatment (Fig. 5d and Supporting Information Fig. S9). Previous literature extensively supports the notion that nuclear HSP70 aggregation, particularly under conditions of intense external stimuli like heat stress and inflammation, plays a crucial role in preserving nucleolar stability. A similar promotion of HSP70 aggregation in the nucleus was observed using siRNA knockdown of DNAJC9 (Fig. 5e). At the same time, we overexpressed DNAJC9 in macrophages and found that it could promote the transcription levels of inflammatory cytokines, whereas A12 could still reduce their expression (Fig. 5f and Supporting Information Fig. S8c). Notably, biochemical assays showed that A12 did not directly inhibit the ATPase activity of the HSP70‒DNAJC9 complex, suggesting a mechanism based on conformational steric hindrance rather than enzymatic inhibition (Supporting Information Fig. S7). This implies a significant relevance of the compound A12 anti-inflammatory mechanisms to the HSP family.

Discussion

Sepsis remains a life-threatening condition with no effective pharmacological treatments available, underscoring the critical need for the development of novel therapeutic strategies. The covalent warhead-modified compound A12 demonstrates significant promise as a novel sepsis drug candidate, offering new mechanistic insights and therapeutic potential. By covalently modifying Tanshinone IIA at the C1 position, we markedly enhanced its anti-inflammatory potency, as evidenced by its superior IC50 values for IL-6 and TNF-α inhibition in vitro and its robust efficacy in vivo, comparable to dexamethasone (Fig. 1). RNA-Seq analysis revealed that A12 downregulates pro-inflammatory genes, including IL-6, IL-1β, and Nos2, and targets the NF-κB signaling pathway (Fig. 2). Mechanistically, A12 binds covalently to DNAJC9, a critical member of the HSP40/DNAJ family that modulates HSP70 activity, with evidence of a 492 Da increase in the molecular weight of DNAJC9 upon binding (Figure 3, Figure 4).

DNAJC9, a member of the HSP40 family, has not been thoroughly studied regarding its biological functions, particularly in the context of inflammatory responses. To determine whether DNAJC9 influences inflammation, we employed gene knockdown and overexpression approaches. Our findings reveal that DNAJC9 indeed exhibits a pro-inflammatory function. Given that this is the first discovery of the role of DNAJC9 in inflammation and considering the primary biological function of the HSP40 family is closely related to HSP70, we further investigated the impact of DNAJC9 modulation on HSP70. The results indicate that both compound A12 treatment and DNAJC9 knockdown promote the intracellular aggregation of HSP70 under inflammatory conditions (Fig. 5). These findings establish DNAJC9 as a pivotal target in inflammation and suggest that the targeted mechanism of A12 may address inflammatory diseases resistant to conventional therapies. Previous studies have extensively reported that HSP70 plays a critical role in suppressing inflammatory responses, mainly by inhibiting the phosphorylation of IκBα in the NF-κB pathway19,22,39. Consistent with these findings, our experiments demonstrated that compound A12 significantly suppresses IκBα phosphorylation, suggesting that the DNAJC9/HSP70 complex plays a pivotal role in modulating inflammation. However, our current work only suggests that the anti-inflammatory effect of compound A12 targeting DNAJC9 is associated with the HSP complex. A more detailed regulatory mechanism requires further investigation through interdisciplinary approaches.

While previous studies have demonstrated that Tanshinone IIA alleviates sepsis-related injury primarily through antioxidant pathways (e.g., the SIRT1/Sestrin2/HO-1 axis), our findings reveal that the covalent derivative A12 operates via a fundamentally different and more specific molecular mode of action40. First, regarding potency, the introduction of the acrylamide warhead transforms the reversible ligand into a potent covalent inhibitor, enabling A12 to achieve superior anti-inflammatory efficacy at significantly lower doses (10 mg/kg) compared to the typical high doses required for Tanshinone IIA. Second, regarding the target, we identified DNAJC9 as the direct physical target of A12. Unlike the general antioxidant effects of the parent compound, A12 specifically engages DNAJC9 to disrupt the HSP70-NF-κB signaling axis. This strike on a specific chaperone complex explains the robust efficacy of A12 in suppressing the initial cytokine storm, representing a first-in-class strategy distinct from the multi-target effects of traditional natural products.

Although the full complexity of the molecular events linking DNAJC9 engagement to inflammatory suppression warrants further investigation, our study aims to delineate a plausible mechanism supported by structural and functional evidence. By mapping the covalent attachment site to Cys[243], we established the physical basis of A12 specificity. Unlike classical inhibitors that target the active sites of enzymes, A12 modifies DNAJC9, a non-catalytic J-domain co-chaperone. Consistent with our biochemical data showing that A12 does not directly inhibit the ATPase activity of the chaperone complex, we propose that the covalent occupancy of Cys[243] likely induces steric hindrance or conformational rigidity. This structural perturbation impairs the dynamic cycling of the HSP70-DNAJC9 machinery, resulting in the trapping of HSP70 within the nucleus—a phenomenon we observed as nuclear aggregation. Given that nuclear HSP70 is a well-characterized negative regulator of IKK activation, A12 effectively hijacks this endogenous regulatory circuit to blunt NF-κB signaling. The strict dependence of A12’s efficacy on DNAJC9, as confirmed by our knockdown experiments, validates this covalent disruption model, suggesting that targeting co-chaperone dynamics offers a viable therapeutic strategy for sepsis-associated acute systemic inflammation.

A potential concern regarding the inhibition of chaperone-associated proteins is the risk of inducing broad proteostatic stress or cytotoxicity. However, unlike housekeeping HSP40s that are universally essential for general protein folding, DNAJC9 has been characterized as a specialized histone chaperone. This functional specialization implies that other members of the extensive HSP40 family likely maintain basal proteostasis through functional redundancy, thereby preventing catastrophic cytotoxicity when DNAJC9 is selectively inhibited. Empirically, A12 exhibited a favorable therapeutic window in vitro, with a cytotoxicity concentration significantly exceeding its effective anti-inflammatory dose. Furthermore, in a preliminary repeated-dose toxicity assessment, mice administered A12 at a high dose of 100 mg/kg for 5 consecutive days showed no significant body weight loss or behavioral abnormalities (data not shown). These findings suggest that pharmacological modulation of DNAJC9 is well-tolerated in vivo and represents a viable therapeutic strategy with a manageable safety profile.

Collectively, our findings demonstrate that A12 acts as a potent pharmacological modulator of the DNAJC9-HSP70-NF-κB signaling axis. Although clinical sepsis is a highly complex syndrome involving pathogen proliferation and immune dysregulation, and multi-organ dysfunction, our results from the LPS-induced endotoxemia model highlight the specific efficacy of A12 in attenuating the early hyper-inflammatory cytokine response. Although A12 showed significant protective effects during the acute phase, it functions primarily as an anti-inflammatory agent rather than an antimicrobial intervention. Accordingly, A12 is not intended as a standalone monotherapy for all stages of sepsis, but rather as a specialized therapeutic candidate for managing sepsis-associated acute systemic inflammation, potentially in combination with antibiotics to mitigate early mortality driven by excessive immune activation.

Conclusions

In summary, we discovered A12, a small molecule covalent tanshinone derivative, as an anti-inflammatory inhibitor that enhances the weak anti-inflammatory activity of tanshinone IIA by approximately 100-fold through the introduction of a covalent warhead, achieving sub-micromolar potency. Moreover, considering the limited availability of effective clinical treatments for sepsis, A12 demonstrates significant therapeutic potential, as it can be administered orally and exhibits comparable efficacy to the positive control, dexamethasone. By rationally designing positive and negative probes, the key signaling pathway perturbed by A12 was screened out, and protein DNAJC9 as the target was locked with sufficient evidence. We discovered the new role of DNAJC9 in the occurrence and regulation of inflammation through affecting the HSP complex and following downstream responses. Furthermore, we inferred the anti-inflammatory effects of A12 by covalently binding to DNAJC9. Our findings validate DNAJC9 as a druggable target and developed the establish A12 as a promising drug candidate, providing a comprehensive strategy for design, target exploration, and drug development of novel treatments for sepsis and related inflammatory conditions.
