Late Outcomes of Thoracic Endovascular Aneurysm Repair Outside Versus Inside Instruction for Use
Authors: Lorenzo Gibello, Michele Piazza, Andrea Gaggiano, Massimo Maione, Andrea Discalzi, Francesco Squizzato, Simone Quaglino, Edoardo Frola, Gianfranco Varetto, Marianna Scevola, Michela Parise, Fabio Verzini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesTo evaluate results of thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) for thoracic aortic aneurysm (TAA) and to investigate outcomes based on adherence to manufacturer’s instructions for use (IFUs).MethodsPatients treated with TEVAR in 4 tertiary hospitals between 2010 and 2021 were collected. Patients with connective tissue disorders, post-dissection TAAs, aortic coarctation and intramural haematomas were excluded. Both elective and urgent procedures were considered. Primary outcome was long-term survival. Secondary outcomes were: aortic-related reinterventions and late TEVAR failure.ResultsOut of 192 patients, 147 (76%) had endografts deployed inside IFU and 45 (24%) did not. Early outcomes were similar between groups with mortality rates of 6% inside IFU and 8.8% outside. Median follow-up was 36 months (interquartile range 20-63 months). Survival rate at 1, 3, and 5 years were 90%, 75%, and 61% for IFU group and 97%, 87%, and 66% for outside-IFU group (P = .55). Freedom from aortic-related reintervention at 1, 3, and 5 years were 94%, 87%, and 80% for IFU group and 88%, 78%, and 74% for outside IFU group (P = .04). TEVAR failure at 5 years occurred in 18 (13%) of inside IFU and 9 (22%) of outside IFU (P = .13).ConclusionsTEVAR is safe and effective in the long-term. Performing TEVAR outside IFU guidelines can still be acceptable but poses a higher risk of reintervention, especially for proximal landing zone violations. Close follow-up is essential to detect late failures.

INTRODUCTION

Thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) is considered the primary therapeutic option for descending thoracic aortic disease, particularly in high-risk patients.1–4 The endovascular approach for atherosclerotic thoracic aortic aneurysms (TAAs) is linked to reduced perioperative morbidity, shorter hospital stays, and improved freedom from aneurysm-related mortality compared to open surgical repair.5,6 These benefits, combined with the advancements in endograft devices, have enabled the treatment of more fragile patients, relegating open surgery to a select group of patients in highly specialized hospitals.7,8 To mitigate the risks of early and late failures, strict adherence to the endograft instructions for use (IFUs) is recommended by the manufacturers. However, with the expanding of the knowledge of this technique, vascular surgeons have been encouraged to apply this approach to aneurysms with challenging anatomies, even when they fall outside the traditional IFU.9–11 While the long-term effects of adherence to IFU are well established for the endovascular repair of infrarenal aneurysms,12,13 there remains a lack of evidence regarding TEVAR outcomes. This study aims to explore this critical issue by analysing the multicentre experience of four tertiary vascular centres.

PATIENTS AND METHODS

Study design

A multicentric retrospective study was conducted, encompassing all patients with degenerative aneurysms of the descending thoracic aorta who were consecutively treated with TEVAR in 4 tertiary hospitals between 2010 and 2021. Both elective and urgent cases were included. Patients with connective tissue disorders, type B dissections, post-dissection TAA, aortic coarctation, and intramural haematomas were excluded. Custom-made and physician-modified devices were also excluded. The study population was stratified based on adherence to the IFU for the specific endograft used (see Table S1). Classification and extent of thoracic aortic pathology, procedural data, and treatment effects are presented according to the Society for Vascular Surgery (SVS) reporting standards.14

The primary outcome was long-term survival at 1, 3, and 5 years. Secondary outcomes, defined according to SVS reporting standards,14 included: technical success, early major systemic complications, late aortic-related death, and reinterventions. Specifically, an aortic-related reintervention is defined as any unplanned subsequent surgical procedure (endovascular or open) performed after the index procedure, to address complications related to the aneurysm or the device itself. A death is considered aortic-related if it occurs as a direct result of the aortic disease process, complications, or within 30 days from the treatment (index procedure or reinterventions). Spinal cord ischaemia (SCI) is defined as the new-onset motor or sensory deficits attributable to spinal cord injury after aortic intervention.

Additionally, late TEVAR failure—a composite end-point including late aortic death, aortic rupture, aortic reintervention, aneurysm growth >5 mm at follow-up, or open surgical conversion—was evaluated.

TEVAR was considered the first therapeutic option for favourable anatomies, with treatment indications following international guidelines.1–4

Follow-up was concluded in June 2023 and follow-up integrity was assessed using the follow-up index.15 This study was designed as a retrospective analysis of prospectively collected databases on patients treated with TEVAR. This study followed the ethical standards of the Helsinki Declaration. Ethical committee approval was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study according to the local rules.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 (R-foundation for statistical computing, Wien, Austria). Categorical variables are presented as number and percentages, while continuous variables are expressed as means with standard deviation or as medians with interquartile range (IQR), depending on their distribution as assessed by the Shapiro-Wilk test. Categorical variables were compared using Chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. For continuous variables, the Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon test was utilized, contingent upon the normality of the data. Kaplan-Meier survival estimates were computed to evaluate overall mortality with the log-rank test assessing statistical significance. Aortic-related mortality and freedom from reintervention were analysed using cumulative incidence functions with death as competing events, and Gray’s test was applied for statistical significance. Survival curves are displayed as solid lines up to a standard error (SE) of <.10. Statistical significance was set at P < .05. Preoperative characteristics (age and sex), cardiovascular risk factors, comorbidities (cardiac, renal, pulmonary, cerebrovascular), clinical presentation (symptoms, rupture), anatomical features (max aortic diameter, proximal and distal aortic neck length, proximal and distal sealing zones according to Ishimaru, and IFU violations), and endograft type were considered in univariate and multivariate analyses for outcomes including overall survival, aortic-related survival, and aortic-related reinterventions. Multivariate logistic regression was employed to assess the relationships between predictors and outcomes, calculating odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) to determine effect size. To minimize overfitting, penalized logistic regression models, such as LASSO (Least Absolute Shrinkage and Selection Operator) and Elastic Net, were used to identify the most influential variables. The model’s discriminative power was evaluated using the area under the curve (AUC).

RESULTS

Out of 278 primary TEVAR procedures, 192 (69%) met the study inclusion criteria (see Figure S1): 147 (76%) were conducted within IFU, while 45 (24%) were outside IFU. The demographics and baseline clinical features of the study population are detailed in Table 1. The mean maximum aortic diameter was similar between the 2 groups (59 ± 19 for those inside IFU vs 63 ± 17 for those outside IFU; P = .2). Urgent procedures adhered to IFU in 47 (32%) and were outside IFU in 12 (26%), P = .6. Among urgent cases, aortic rupture accounted for 21 cases (14%) of inside IFU and 10 cases (22%) of outside IFU (P = .2). The distribution of aortic arch types was similar between the groups, with type I, II, and III observed in 40%, 25%, and 35% inside IFU patients and 43%, 19%, and 36% of outside IFU group, respectively (P = .3). Bovine arch configuration was noted in 11% of inside IFU group and 10% of outside IFU (P = .9). An aberrant left subclavian artery was found in one outside IFU case, and 5 cases of left vertebral arteries arising directly from the aortic arch were identified within the IFU group. TEVAR procedures were classified as outside IFU due to proximal landing zone violation in 32 cases (71%): 23 (72%) due to proximal landing zone location and 9 (28%) due to short neck length. In this group, concomitant open surgery of supra-aortic trunks was required in 13 cases (40%), chimney technique in 5 cases (15%), and physician-modified endograft in 1 case (3%). Distal IFU violations occurred in 13 cases (29%) due to distal landing zone location in 6 (46%), short neck length in 7 (53%), and presence of severe circumferential thrombosis/calcifications in 2 (15%). Intentional coverage of the coeliac trunk was required in 4 cases (38%), endovascular sandwich technique for coeliac trunk/superior mesenteric artery in 2 cases (15%), and Heli-FX EndoAnchor System (Medtronic, Santa Rosa, CA, USA) in 1 case (7%). Antegrade TEVAR was performed in 6 patients (13%). Two patients (4%) had 2 violations (the first proximal neck location and antegrade deployment; the second patient had proximal neck location and distal severe thrombotic apposition). A single patient (2%) had 3 violations and was performed in urgent setting (proximal neck location, antegrade TEVAR, and severe circumferential calcification of distal sealing zone). The proximal landing zone was planned in Ishimaru’s zone Z1: 3 (2%); Z2: 26 (18%); Z3: 52 (35%); Z4: 66 (45%) for inside IFU procedures and Z0: 13 (29%); Z1: 11 (24%); Z2: 9 (20%); Z3: 6 (13%); Z4: 6 (13%) for outside IFU TEVARs. The mean covered aortic length was similar between groups (189 ± 60 mm vs 173 ± 64 mm for IFU and outside IFU, respectively; P = .1). The distal landing zone was achieved in Ishimaru’s zone Z4: 47 (32%); Z5: 94 (64%); Z6: 6 (4%) for patients treated within IFU and Z4: 14 (31%); Z5: 19 (42%); Z6: 12 (27%) for those treated outside IFU. No statistical difference was found in terms of landing zone areas. A prosthetic conduit was required in 8 (18%) of outside IFU procedures, and in none of inside IFU patients. A single endograft was used in 98 (67%) of IFU procedures and 35 (77%) of outside IFU procedures (P = .1). Two endografts were used in 45 (30%) of IFU and 8 (18%) of outside IFU procedures (P = .09). Three endografts were used in 4 (3%) and 2 (5%), respectively (P = .5).

Early outcomes

The early outcomes are summarized in Table 2. Technical success was achieved in all cases. No surgical conversions occurred, and no type I or III endoleaks (ELs) were detected at completion angiography. There were 9 access site complications (4.7%), such as pseudoaneurysm or thrombosis, that required reintervention; these were evenly distributed between the groups (3.4% inside IFU and 8.8% for outside IFU; P = .1). Early mortality was observed in 9 cases (6%) in the inside IFU group (all urgent TEVARs, exception case due to post-operative bowel ischaemia and multiorgan failure) and 4 (8.8%) in outside IFU procedures (all urgent TEVARs). The early mortality rate among octogenarians was similar between the groups (7% IFU; 6% outside IFU, P = .9).

Cerebrovascular ischaemic complications occurred in 7 subjects (3.6%), with stroke occurring in 5 (2.6%) and no significant differences between groups (P = .8). The postoperative SCI rate was 4%, equally distributed between the groups. Two severe SCI occurred in the inside IFU group, both during elective procedures. The first patient underwent TEVAR with preventive cerebrospinal fluid drainage, which was removed on postoperative day 2. On day 6, the patient developed paraplegia and urinary incontinence. The cerebrospinal fluid drainage was reinserted but it was complicated by an epidural haematoma. Symptoms persisted despite a T12-L2 laminectomy. The second case involved a patient with a distal thoracic descending aneurysm with significant wall calcification; cerebrospinal fluid drainage was only initiated at the onset of neurological symptoms, without recovery. In outside IFU group, only one case of severe SCI occurred in a patient treated for ruptured aorta in an urgent setting.

Late outcomes

Follow-up data were available for 138 patients treated inside IFU and 41 patients treated outside IFU, as summarized in Table 3. The median follow-up was 36 months (IQR 17-92 months) for the inside IFU group and 37 months (IQR 17-62 months) for the outside IFU group (P = .6). The follow-up index was similar between the groups (0.81 ± 0.33 IFU vs 0.74 ± 0.37 outside IFU; P = .2). The rate of loss to follow-up was 21% in IFU and 27% in the outside IFU (P = .4). Overall survival rates at 1, 3, and 5 years were 90%, 75%, and 61% for the inside IFU group and 97%, 87%, and 66% for outside IFU group (P = .55). Survival curves (Figures 1 and 2) show no significant differences in overall survival and aortic-related death. Aortic-related deaths occurred in 4 patients (3%) treated inside IFU: one due to a type Ia EL with postoperative cardiac complications, 2 cases due to type Ib EL and distal aneurysm progression. Both were treated with redo TEVAR; the first patient died in the postoperative due to pulmonary complications and the second died for visceral malperfusion after intentional occlusion of coeliac trunk and reverse chimney on superior mesenteric artery. A forth patient was admitted in urgent setting in critical conditions with aortic rupture due to a type Ib EL and was palliated. In the outside IFU group, 3 patients experienced aortic-related death: one case due to type Ia EL that was unsuccessfully managed firstly with re-TEVAR and then with surgical conversion (the patient died in the post-operative hospitalization). A second case presenting with aorto-oesophageal fistula and aortic rupture underwent redo TEVAR and died 1 month after the procedure. The last case was due to the consequences of visceral embolization after the endovascular treatment of a type Ib EL.

DISCUSSION

This study demonstrates the safety and efficacy of TEVAR in treating TAAs. The reduced invasiveness and faster postoperative recovery associated with TEVAR, compared to open surgery, encourage vascular surgeons to consider this approach for patients with multiple comorbidities. This trend was highlighted by Hellgren et al16 in a VASCUNET Registry analysis involving 4436 patients treated with TEVAR for TAA. Their real-world data indicated that one-quarter of the population had a preoperative maximum aortic diameter of < 60 mm and 18% of TEVAR procedures were performed on patients aged ≥ 80 years, with a perioperative mortality rate of 7.2%. Our study’s octogenarian percentage aligns with Hellgren et al and was predominant in the outside IFU group. The results suggest that TEVAR outside IFU may be a feasible alternative for selected patients to avoid more invasive procedures.

Contrasting evidence exists regarding the advantage of TEVAR vs open repair for TAA in fit patients. A retrospective evaluation of the Medicare database reported longer mean survival for TEVAR at 9 years compared to open repair, albeit with an increased risk of late reintervention rate in the endovascular group.17 Conversely, a propensity score analysis by Iyanna et al18 found a survival advantage with open surgical repair after 2 years compared to the endovascular technique. The impact of IFU adherence on late survival remains underexplored. Our study reports 1- and 5-year survival rate of 90% and 61%, respectively, similar to findings by Karaolanis et al19 and slightly better than Ho et al20 (78% and 46%, respectively), whose study population was older at baseline.

Predictors of late TEVAR failure have been extensively investigated.21–23 Proximal sealing zone length is deemed the most significant factor influencing TEVAR’s long-term.21,22 While a sealing length of at least 25 mm is generally recommended, the benefits of an adequate proximal landing zone must be weighed against the technical challenges of rerouting supra-aortic trunks and the increased risk of neurological complications.24,25 Dieleman et al26 noted that surgical debranching of the left subclavian artery adds valuable potential sealing length in zone 2, though with relatively short apposition length due to endograft tilting. Kanaoka et al27 reported that proximal sealing zone location also affects early type I EL rates after TEVAR: in zone 0-2 TEVAR, type Ia EL rate was 13% compared to zone 4 (1.4%). In our study, proximal landing zone IFU violations emerged as the most predictive factor for late aortic reinterventions. TEVAR with antegrade deployment showed a higher risk for ELs and late aortic-related reinterventions.21 Our study observed a higher incidence of ELs in patients with antegrade deployment, though not reaching statistical significance, possibly due to the limited sample size. Notably, type I EL was twice as common in patients treated outside IFU revealing the lower stability of these treatments, with a potential increased risk of treatment failure.

Freedom from aortic-related reintervention rates for inside IFU patients aligns with a recent meta-analysis of “on-label” TEVAR studies but is considerably lower compared to our outside IFU group.19 As described by Fairman et al,28 most of reinterventions were managed with re-TEVAR or more complex endovascular or hybrid procedures, with surgical open conversion primarily reserved for endograft infections. Despite the higher failure rate of TEVAR outside IFU, no increased incidence of aortic rupture or death was observed in our experience, likely due to prompt failure management and high follow-up adherence, as indicated by a lower follow-up loss percentage than reported in the literature.29 Our findings may not be generalizable to all patients with outside IFU anatomical characteristics. Careful selection at high-volume tertiary centres with a strong aortic disease management background may help exclude high risk outside IFU patients.

In 2021, preliminary imaging findings from the Valiant Evo Global Clinical Trial revealed late structural failures in the Navion stent graft platform (Medtronic Inc, Santa Rosa, Calif.), suggesting the need for close monitoring of these patients.30–32 In our study, Navion stent graft was used in 29 patients with 2 cases of late reintervention due to a type Ia and a type Ib EL, respectively. However, multivariate analysis of this small cohort failed to identify the Navion stent graft as a predictor of late TEVAR failure, likely due to the limited number of events and the follow-up period.

Study limitations

The retrospective nature of this study presents a limitation, as does the relatively small sample size, particularly within the outside IFU cohort. Results concerning variables with low event rates should also be interpreted with caution. Additionally, the decision to treat patients outside IFU was based on a case-by-case evaluation of the risks and benefits for each potential therapeutic strategy, including the option to abstain from treatment. This approach may have introduced selection bias, potentially leading to an overestimation of the performances of TEVAR outside IFU.

CONCLUSION

This study confirms that thoracic endovascular repair is a safe and effective therapeutic option for the treatment of thoracic aneurysms, even over the long-term. The selective use of TEVAR outside IFU can yield acceptable results in carefully chosen cases, with an increased risk of reintervention, especially in instances of proximal IFU violations. A strict follow-up protocol is essential, especially for patients treated outside IFU, to promptly identify and manage late TEVAR failures.
