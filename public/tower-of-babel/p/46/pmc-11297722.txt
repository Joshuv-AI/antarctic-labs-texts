Unraveling the effects of DIP payment reform on inpatient healthcare: insights into impacts and challenges
Authors: Ying Zhang, Shu-yi Xu, Guang-ming Tan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Diagnosis-Intervention Packet (DIP) payment system, initiated by China's National Healthcare Security Administration, is designed to enhance healthcare efficiency and manage rising healthcare costs. This study aims to evaluate the impact of the DIP payment reform on inpatient care in a specialized obstetrics and gynecology hospital, with a focus on its implications for various patient groups.MethodsTo assess the DIP policy's effects, we employed the Difference-in-Differences (DID) approach. This method was used to analyze changes in total hospital costs and Length of Stay (LOS) across different patient groups, particularly within select DIP categories. The study involved a comprehensive examination of the DIP policy's influence pre- and post-implementation.ResultsOur findings indicate that the implementation of the DIP policy led to a significant increase in both total costs and LOS for the insured group relative to the self-paying group. The study further identified variations within DIP groups both before and after the reform. In-depth analysis of specific disease groups revealed that the insured group experienced notably higher total costs and LOS compared to the self-paying group.ConclusionsThe DIP reform has led to several challenges, including upcoding and diagnostic ambiguity, because of the pursuit of higher reimbursements. These findings underscore the necessity for continuous improvement of the DIP payment system to effectively tackle these challenges and optimize healthcare delivery and cost management.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-024-11363-8.

Introduction

In 1983, the United States first adopted the Diagnosis-Related Groups (DRGs) system, a prospective case-based reimbursement model for healthcare. Since then, numerous adaptations of the DRG model have been implemented worldwide to manage inpatient care [1]. The system categorizes patients into groups based on similar clinical characteristics, such as age, gender, severity, complications, and comorbidities, as well as resource consumption [2, 3].

DRG is a globally acclaimed methodology that assures quality of medical care while ensuring better cost control. Empirical studies on the effects of the DRG payment system are abundant. The prevailing literature suggests that the overall impact of DRG has been limited but has somewhat improved efficiency and contained costs [4]. Although there's evidence suggesting that DRG may marginally boost efficiency, concerns remain regarding the potential compromise in equity and healthcare quality [5, 6]. A systematic review of the impact of the DRG payment system on hospital healthcare in China found that it moderately improved healthcare efficiency by reducing the LOS [7], the meta-analysis assessed the effectiveness of the DRG system, which implied cost-saving benefits by reducing LOS but did little to curtail readmission rates [8, 9], which is consistent with the policy expectations and is theoretically plausible.

However, the system also has potential side effects. The DRG model may incentivize physicians to perform more frequent diagnoses and treatments to boost revenue [3], Moreover, it might inadvertently squeeze out patients with high-cost diseases from the healthcare system, as physicians could prefer dealing with easier and more profitable cases [10, 11]. Hence, debates continue about the impacts and potential side effects of the DRG system.

The Chinese government launched a DRG payment system reform in 2019 to control the expansion of health expenditure [12]. In the process of shaping the reforms, it was recognized through a variety of studies that the DRG payment system could not encompass all healthcare service forms [13, 14]. Moreover, the operation of the DRG payment system was found to necessitate high administrative costs and an advanced information system [15]. As a response to these challenges, the National Healthcare Security Administration independently conceived and implemented the Diagnosis-Intervention Packet (DIP) payment method, borrowing from the principles underpinning the DRGs system [16].

The DIP payment reform in Guangzhou has attracted nationwide attention since its initiation in 2017 and subsequent official implementation in January 2019. The DIP, grounded in China's unique context and conceived from clinical practice, offers a bottom-up approach to payment reform, proving less challenging to implement due to its operational simplicity compared to the DRG system. The system applies a classification approach that progresses from 'coarse to fine', providing granularity in the categorization of medical cases. Given the DIP system's modest requirements for medical record supervision and informatization level, it is particularly suitable for implementation in hospitals located in second and third-tier cities [17].

The DIP system is still in the early stages of policy implementation, with a unified agreement about its role yet to be formed. Furthermore, there has been a relative dearth of attention given to the exploration and control of confounding factors, as well as an in-depth analysis of the shifts in disease groups. This highlights the necessity for further comprehensive research in order to more accurately discern the impacts of the DIP system.

This study intends to utilize the Difference-in-Differences (DID) method to analyze the impact of the DIP payment reform on total costs and the LOS in a specific Women and Children health hospital. Additionally, through scrutinizing the alterations in DIP disease groups pre- and post-Reform implementation, we aim to explore the DIP policy's influence on the diagnostic and therapeutic behaviors of healthcare institutions. This investigation is expected to generate recommendations for improving the management of DIP policy and the quality of healthcare information.

In the following sections, the methods for implementing the DIP policy will be outlined, including the data sources and analytical strategy. The results of the analysis will focus on both the overall and subgroup impacts of the DIP payment reform on hospital expenses and LOS. Finally, the implications of the DIP payment reform, its actual impact, and potential countermeasures will be discussed.

Methods

Diagnosis-intervention packet (DIP) payment method

In 2019, Guangzhou introduced the DIP payment method, applicable to inpatient expenses covered by the Employee Basic Medical Insurance and Urban–Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance. DIP is a unique product developed in China with no application experience in other countries. The grouping of DIP is determined by the prevalence of actual treatment plans in a region, based on big data. By collecting a large number of real-world cases, cases are grouped according to the "diagnosis + procedure" rule. In DIP version 1.0, all disease groups with 15 or more cases in the region are classified as core groups, while treatment plans with fewer than 15 cases are grouped into composite groups based on disease similarity and resource usage. This approach uses natural clustering combinations, fine-tuning of groups, low within-group variation coefficients, and stable inter-group differences [18, 19].

Compared to DRG, which requires high standards for data quality, information system modifications, management, and technical level, DIP is simple, quick, and highly operable, representing a preliminary version of prospective payment. Each year, modifications were made to the DIP Groups and grouping rules. For instance, in Guangzhou, 11,359 DIP groups were released in 2019, which increased to 13,515 in 2021 and then decreased to 7,876 in 2022. Guided by the requirements of the national healthcare insurance system reform, DIP Groups are divided into Core and Comprehensive disease Groups. Cases exceeding a predetermined threshold are encompassed in the Core disease groups, with the residual cases, predicated on disease characteristics, further amalgamated and categorized as Comprehensive disease groups. The regional Point Value (PV) is determined by exploiting historical case data and conducting a weighted calculation of the average cost per disease. The Assigned Point Volume (APV) for DIP groups is computed by taking the ratio of the average medical cost per disease to the benchmark average medical cost per disease, and multiplying by 1000. The Medical Institution Coefficient, comprised of the benchmark coefficient and additional coefficients, are holistically determined by a range of factors, include the insurance settlement level of the medical institution, its functional positioning, medical technical capability, specialty characteristics, and health insurance management et.al. Data

We conducted a comprehensive extraction of data from the Patient Record Statistics Systems within a specialized Women and Children's Hospital located in Guangdong Province. This included essential patient demographics, hospitalization expenses, Length of Stay (LOS), diagnoses as categorized under ICD-10 codes (International Classification of Diseases, 10th Revision), and procedures categorized under ICD-9-CM3 codes (International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision, Clinical Modification) and other relevant information.

Our study incorporated patient records across two phases of discharge, between 2016–2017 and 2021–2022. After the exclusion of records associated with other payment methodologies, totaling 8,541 records, we analyzed a substantial dataset of 229,306 inpatient records. Of these, 108,628 records were linked to insured patients and were thus assigned to the treatment group, while the remaining 120,678 records pertained to self-paying patients and were allocated to the control group. This research is a retrospective analysis utilizing pre-existing case data, with approval procured from the Health Research Ethics Committee of the authors institute. The necessity for informed consent was waived by the hospital's Ethics Committee.

Python was utilized for data sanitization, ensuring patient privacy through comprehensive de-identification and desensitization of the dataset. Python's statsmodels library was then leveraged for DID estimation and robustness testing.

Analytical strategy

This study proposes to use the Difference-in-Differences (DID) method to estimate the effect of DIP payments on the natural logarithm of hospital expenses and the LOS within certain specialties of this institution. The DID method [20] is a quasi-experimental design used to estimate causal relationships by comparing the changes in outcomes over time between a treatment group and a control group. This method helps control for confounding factors and isolate the effect of the intervention by assuming that, in the absence of the intervention, the difference between the treatment and control groups would have remained constant over time. By applying DID, we can more accurately attribute changes in hospital expenses and LOS to the implementation of the DIP payment reform, rather than to other external factors.

The intervention group includes inpatient cases covered by the Employee Basic Medical Insurance and Urban–Rural Resident Basic Medical Insurance, while the control group includes self-paying inpatient cases. Given the pilot rollout of the DIP health insurance payment policy in 2019, we designated cases from 2016–2017 as the pre-reform period, a time when the fee-for-service system was predominantly used for payments, and cases from 2021–2022 were classified as the post-reform period. Although the numbers of the intervention and control groups do not perfectly match in this study, key characteristics such as age and gender remain consistent.

The immediate changes in total costs and LOS across groups following the reform add credibility to the identification strategy used. The introduction of this reform heightened the need for healthcare providers to reduce expenses for the insured group, potentially leading to a strategic shift towards the self-paying segment to maintain financial balance. Simultaneously, patients in pursuit of higher-quality healthcare services may consider forgoing insurance in favor of direct payment options.

To ascertain whether these observed variations were a direct consequence of the DIP reform, we refined our OLS model for a detailed subgroup analysis of selected DIP groups. This method is intended to clarify the reform's effects on hospital management and financial governance. Results

The study analyzed a total of 229,306 inpatient cases, with 109,067 cases recorded from 2016–2017 and 120,239 cases from 2021–2022. The analysis revealed that the DIP payment reform was associated with an increase in total costs and length of stay (LOS). Heterogeneity analysis identified changes that prompted further subgroup analysis. This subsequent analysis revealed that changes in total costs and LOS occurred in certain subgroups, which were associated with the APV allocated to these DIP groups.

Descriptive statistics

The subjects from both pre-reform and post-reform periods were divided into intervention and control groups based on the method of settlement. An analysis was carried out on the distribution of age groups, gender, hospital expenses, and LOS within these four categories, the results of which are presented in Table 1. The analysis elucidated that there were no statistically significant differences in the distribution of age categories, gender, hospital expenses, and LOS across the various groups.

The distribution of hospital expenses manifests a positively skewed distribution (skewness test value = 502.40; P-value = 0.00), with a mean of 10,234.11 ± 33.28, and a median of 6393.16. Furthermore, the distribution of the Length of Stay (LOS) also presents a positive skew (skewness test value = 483.23; P-value = 0.00), averaging at 5.63 ± 0.01, and a median of 4.0. Similarly, both the hospital expenses and LOS for all four groups exhibit a positively skewed distribution. Heterogeneity analysis in DIP groups

Based on the primary diagnoses (PDX) subcategories within each specialty, we conducted a heterogeneity analysis to assess how the DIP policy differentially impacted various groups. The results reveal heterogeneity effects in certain groups before and after the reform, associated with APV allocated to DIP groups.

In obstetrics, due to the implementation of the DIP, providers have intensified their management of coding quality and medical record documentation. This has led to an increase in the number of cases in categories such as O36.3 and O68.0. In pediatrics, due to the higher APV for the J18.9 group under the DIP policy, there was a notable reduction in J15.7 and J18.0 cases, while the incidence of J18.9 cases increased. In pediatric surgery, the category G47.3, related to surgical removal procedures for sleep apnea disorders and valued at 1024, experienced a rise in cases post-reform, in contrast to the J35.2 category for adenoid hypertrophy surgical removal, with an APV of 849.

These strategic adjustments by healthcare providers, aimed at optimizing medical resource allocation and securing higher reimbursement points, have indirectly influenced total costs and the length of stay (LOS).

Subgroup analysis for selected DIP groups

To investigate the potential differential impacts of the DIP policy on various clinical conditions, we conducted an exhaustive subgroup analysis on several selected DIP diagnostic groups. The criteria for selecting these specific diagnostic groups included: 1) Ensuring that each chosen subgroup had a sufficient sample size for statistically reliable analysis; 2) Selecting subgroups that are characteristic and representative within their respective specialties; 3) Choosing subgroups that either showed significant changes or relative stability before and after the reform, to enable a comparison that aids in developing more robust and credible research conclusions.

Appendix G presents the distribution of age group, gender, total costs, and LOS both before and after the reform for selected DIP Groups. These groups show no significant differences in age and gender distributions. However, some groups exhibit imbalanced sample sizes. For example, the D25.9:68.29/67.37 and D24.X:85.21 groups have relatively fewer self-pay cases post-reform, while the F84.0 Group has fewer cases pre-reform. Figure 5A and B display the yearly trends of median total costs and LOS for each selected DIP Group. Across these disease groups, the total costs for self-pay groups are higher than those for insured groups before the DIP reform, with both cohorts exhibiting similar median total cost trends. However, the parallel trends in LOS pre-reform are not clear.

Discussion

Based on China's national conditions, the domestically developed Diagnosis-Intervention Packet (DIP) system draws on massive data to merge the point method with the total regional budget, thereby establishing effective payment management along with incentive and restraint mechanisms for medical institutions. Both administrators and policy-makers are keenly observing the effects of DIP's implementation, to modify grouping rules and requirements based on outcomes.

This study employs data from a Women and Children Hospital, using the Difference-in-Differences (DID) method to assess the impact of the DIP payment reform on various aspects of medical resource consumption, including hospitalization expenses, Length of Stay (LOS), and diagnostic choices.

The results indicate that implementation of the DIP reform, this institution experienced limited control over total costs and LOS, particularly in terms of total costs for insured group, which showed a noticeable upward trend. We conducted a heterogeneity analysis of the Principal Diagnosis (PDX) across major specialties of this institution, uncovering significant variations in the frequency of certain disease groups before and after the DIP reform. While shifts in disease spectrum, specialty development, and health education might contribute to these heterogeneous outcomes, our expertise leads us to consider the possible impact of the DIP reform on specific disease groups.

Consequently, we undertook a subgroup analysis to scrutinize selected disease groups. Our findings reveal that changes in medical resource consumption vary across groups. For instance, in the J18.9, D24.X:85.21, and F84.0 groups with higher APV, a notable post-reform increase in case numbers was observed, with total costs and LOS coefficients showing significant upward trends. These results from the DIP groups suggest that higher APV encourage providers to alter PDX for greater reimbursements, indirectly allowing for increases in both total costs and LOS.

Conversely, in groups like O02.1 and Z51.1, where APV are lower, no significant changes were seen in case numbers pre- and post-reform, and both total costs and LOS were effectively managed. The outcomes from these groups demonstrate that when the APV is set at a more reasonable level, total costs and LOS remain controllable.

The analysis results suggest that the DIP-based payment system has instigated behavioral changes which have not significantly controlled healthcare resource utilization [21]. For DIP groups that offer more selectivity, medical institutions may attempt to increase the proportion of health insurance reimbursements through various tactics such as upcoding, confounding diagnoses, and increasing procedures [13]. These findings highlight the critical need for a balanced reimbursement point system to facilitate rational healthcare delivery and efficient cost management.

Recently, several studies have assessed the impact of the DIP system, yet no uniform conclusion on its effectiveness has been reached. Some argue that the DIP is well-suited to China's medical landscape due to its ability to control cost growth and allocate resources rationally [22, 23]. On the other hand, other findings indicate increased total costs for older patients and reduced LOS in younger and young-old groups without deteriorating quality of care [24]. Meanwhile, some studies report a slightly increasing trend in the 30-day readmission rate and LOS post-DIP implementation [25]. There are also concerns about potential cost transfers, with some studies indicating that the DIP system might lead hospitals to shift the cost burden of payment reform onto patients covered by other insurance schemes [26].

Given the DIP is at an early stage of policy implementation, those analyses have been restricted to specific clinical specialties and have not considered the impact of confounding factors. Moreover, research on the effects of the DRG and DIP payment reforms on providers' behavior in China is lacking. Previous DRG studies in China have primarily focused on cost control and changes in diagnostic and treatment outcomes, thereby paying less attention to provider behavior [8].

For instance, in this study, we found that the frequency of diagnoses for pneumonia can vary significantly as such diagnoses may be subjective. Silverman's study [27] also argues that during the implementation of DRGs in the United States, in the case of pneumonia, as it is often difficult to identify Gram-positive or negative bacterial pneumonia through blood or sputum cultures, some consultants suggested hospitals might use other clinical characteristics as proxies. This means hospitals could receive higher reimbursements for a presumptive, not actual diagnosis, so long as the physician went along with it.

The inherent uncertainty in diagnosis provided fertile ground for consultants advising hospitals on how to maximize health insurance reimbursement costs. This can lead to "reporting bias", which can have detrimental effects on academic research and scientific investigations. For example, in our study, the subjective nature of diagnoses might lead to a lower level of matching accuracy in disease group populations before and after the reform.

Additionally, it is important to note that the R-squared values of the models are generally not high, but this does not necessarily indicate poor model performance. This is because there are many confounding factors that influence hospitalization costs and LOS, making it difficult to fully explain these outcomes solely based on health insurance policy. For example, the subjectivity in selecting diagnosis codes is a significant confounding factor.

This study presents distinct conclusions about the effects of the DIP payment reform compared to previous research. Our evaluation of the DIP reform has produced significantly different findings. While previous studies have often reported general trends, our research highlights specific variations in total costs and length of stay (LOS) across different clinical subgroups. This divergence in findings underscores the importance of considering the unique context and specific characteristics of each subgroup when assessing the impact of DIP reforms.

The study's approach of using heterogeneity analysis based on primary diagnoses (PDX) within specialties allowed us to identify and analyze the deeper causes of changes in costs and LOS. By examining these variations, we were able to associate them with the Adjusted Payment Value (APV) allocated to different DIP groups. This method provided a more detailed and accurate picture of how the DIP reform affects different segments of the patient population.

Therefore, at this stage, the DIP payment system has resulted in some negative effects, such as increased explanations to the patients, hindering new technologies, case splitting, and cherry picking [28]. The DIP is sometimes misinterpreted by service providers as merely a cost control policy, leading to strategies to increase the proportion of health insurance reimbursements. This misunderstanding can hinder effective management and exacerbate the issues the DIP was designed to resolve.

Given the current situation, it is necessary to conduct in-depth analysis of the policy effects of different DIP groups to provide valuable information for medical service providers and policy makers, to regulate medical behavior, improve the quality of medical care. This would be a healthy cycle and an important measure to deepen the understanding and improvement of the DIP reform.

Furthermore, medical institutions should unify the principles of disease coding, rather than referring to the DIP rules to modify diagnoses and ICD codes. This can ensure the reasonable management of medical information, the accuracy of statistical analysis of diseases, and the effectiveness of clinical research. It also indicates the necessity of regular checking and auditing of the coding of medical records, where rewards or penalties may be considered and applied to promote the integrity of healthcare providers [27].

Limitation

This study has several limitations that need to be considered. Firstly, the research was concentrated on specific medical specialties, which may limit the broader applicability of our findings to other health conditions. Consequently, there is a pressing need for a systematic and comprehensive investigation to further understand the potential impact of DIP payment.

Secondly, during the analysis of DIP groups, it was impossible to ensure an exact match in the number of cases between the control and treatment groups. For example, in the D25.9:68.29/67.37 and D24.X:85.21 groups, there were comparatively fewer self-pay cases post-reform. This could be attributed to the fact that individuals with health insurance are more likely to opt for hospital treatment for benign tumors. However, despite this discrepancy, the study attempted to maintain consistency in key characteristics, such as age and gender.

Lastly, the treatment group includes insured patients while the control group includes self-paying patients. This discrepancy implies that the DID results may not solely reflect the effects of DIP implementation, making it challenging to ensure that the treatment and control groups are entirely comparable. Additionally, the high level of subjectivity in the diagnosis of certain diseases means that counterfactual populations before and after the implementation of the DIP policy, even with matching PDX and procedures, may represent different disease characteristics. This variation complicates the isolation of the DIP policy's impact. Therefore, finding a sufficient number of unaffected inpatient cases in a comparable environment posed a significant challenge, potentially introducing biases into our findings.

Therefore, these limitations may have introduced biases into our findings, warranting careful interpretation. Future research should employ larger sample sizes and long-term follow-ups for validation.

Conclusion

Our study reveals that the implementation effects of the DIP are still unstable, and it has led to issues such as upcoding and diagnostic ambiguity. As a result, we underscore the need for a multifaceted approach to improve the healthcare payment system, which should consider the actual impacts of policy implementation.

Such measures should include pragmatic DIP piloting design, and revisions to subgroups and rules to optimize the payment system [29]. In addition, it is crucial to improve the clarity and consistency of diagnose and ICD coding, enhance the rigor of regulatory mechanisms, and bolster the technical capacity for health information management.

In the future, the application of intelligent information technologies should be directed towards improving the monitoring of diagnostic and treatment processes, and enhancing the reporting of health insurance information.
