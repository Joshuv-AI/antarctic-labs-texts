High-density lipoprotein: a biomarker and therapeutic target in sepsis
Authors: Mohan Li, Marina Barros-Pinkelnig, Sesmu M. Arbous, Christina Christoffersen, Patrick C. N. Rensen, Sander Kooijman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sepsis is a life-threatening condition that stems from a dysregulated host response to an infection, leading to multi-organ dysfunction and death. Sepsis has a remarkably high global burden and accounts for 20% of all deaths worldwide. Nonetheless, possibilities for treatment are limited mainly to early administration of broad-spectrum antibiotics and providing fluid resuscitation. Innovative strategies that target the excessive inflammatory response while supporting the immune system to clear the infection are highly warranted. It is well-established that sepsis significantly impacts lipoprotein metabolism, leading to a substantial decrease in high-density lipoprotein (HDL) as observed in both experimental and clinical studies. Meanwhile, a high HDL level is associated with better sepsis-related prognosis, indicating that strategies aimed at raising HDL could be beneficial in combating sepsis. In this review, we describe changes in lipoprotein metabolism that occur during sepsis, address the various protective functions of HDL based on its endotoxin-neutralizing, anti-bacterial, anti-inflammatory, and anti-oxidative properties, as well as demonstrate modulation of HDL as a potential therapeutic strategy in sepsis.Graphical abstract

Sepsis and septic shock

Sepsis is a complicated, life-threatening disorder that arises from a dysregulated host response to an infection. Sepsis can progress into septic shock, with a critical reduction in organ perfusion, leading to acute multi-organ failure and a substantial risk of death [1]. There are an estimated 50 million sepsis cases worldwide annually, resulting in over 10 million sepsis-related deaths, making it responsible for as much as 20% of all fatalities [2].

Sepsis can be caused by various types of infections, with bacterial infections being the most predominant cause. Gram-positive bacteria account for more than 50% of the sepsis cases, while Gram-negative bacteria and fungi are responsible for 40% and 5% of cases, respectively [3]. The remaining minority of cases is explained by viral (e.g., COVID-19) and parasitic infections [3, 4]. Infections resulting in sepsis frequently originate in the lung, kidney, or enter the body via wounds and burns [5, 6].

Challenges in the treatment of sepsis and septic shock

Clinical strategies of sepsis are well-defined and outlined in the guideline regarding the management of sepsis and septic shock [7]. Currently, implementation of a ‘1-hour bundle’ is highly recommended, which encompasses key interventions within the first hour of recognizing potential sepsis in a patient with infection [8]. This includes measuring serum lactate levels, obtaining blood cultures, and initiating broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy. Additionally, early hemodynamic stabilization is prioritized through fluid resuscitation, guided by serum lactate levels. In clinics, the priority besides diagnosis of sepsis is to achieve a mean arterial pressure of at least 65 mm Hg with intravenous fluid resuscitation and vasopressors (e.g., epinephrine, norepinephrine) [7].

As the approaches mentioned above do not address the dysregulated host response in patients with sepsis, additional immune- and anticoagulation-based therapeutic strategies have been proposed. For example, corticosteroids are widely applied in patients with septic shock as they potently suppress immune responses [9, 10]. Corticosteroids increase blood pressure and improve glomerular filtration rate; as such, the guideline of the Surviving Sepsis Campaign suggests intravenous administration of hydrocortisone at a dose of 200 mg/day in adults with septic shock when adequate fluid resuscitation and vasopressor therapy fail in restoring hemodynamic stability [7]. However, corticosteroids should be considered with caution. While suppression of inflammation by hydrocortisone may be effective in controlling excessive inflammation, it will also dampen the immune response towards the underlying infection, allowing further spreading of pathogens [11]. Probably for that reason, in a recent retrospective analysis of 31,749 patients with sepsis, we were unable to identify sub-population(s) ultimately benefiting from hydrocortisone use, and even reported associations between use of hydrocortisone and increased risk of death [12].

The dysregulated host response in sepsis is mainly induced by excessive endotoxemia (e.g., caused by lipopolysaccharide (LPS) from Gram-negative bacteria or lipoteichoic acid (LTA) from Gram-positive bacteria), which results in excessive production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as tumor necrosis factor alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-1-beta (IL-1β) and interleukin-6 (IL-6), also known as the ‘cytokine storm’. Notably, severe systemic inflammation resembling sepsis can also arise from non-infectious insults. For instance, cardiopulmonary bypass can induce gut mucosal ischemia, leading to barrier dysfunction and the translocation of microbes or endotoxins, thereby triggering a postperfusion systemic inflammatory response and multiorgan dysfunction [13]. Other non-infectious triggers include major trauma [14], and cytokine release syndrome associated with monoclonal antibody therapies [15]. Interestingly, even in the earliest hours of sepsis, compensatory anti-inflammatory cytokine mechanisms have already been activated. Therefore, several specific immune-suppressing strategies have been tested in the context of sepsis during the past decade, including recombinant IL-1 receptor antagonist [16] and anti-TNF-α monoclonal antibody [17]. Although both strategies were shown to reduce all-cause mortality among patients with sepsis [17], recent studies argued that these treatments are not superior to placebo in improving the survival rate, raising doubts about the safety and veracity of therapies targeting at immune system in sepsis [18, 19]. Additionally, IL-1 receptor antagonism has even been shown to aggravate Staphylococcus aureus-induced septic arthritis and to increase mortality in mice [18].

The difficulty in the treatment of sepsis seems to lie in the fact that the body is trying to protect itself from an active infection. In other words, suppression of the immune response may allow the infection to spread or induce secondary infection [20], while only dealing with the infection may not address the abnormal host response. Anticoagulation medications such as recombinant thrombomodulin, recombinant activated protein C, antithrombin, and heparin have also been evaluated in sepsis with the hypothesis that they may prevent organ failure and death caused by sepsis-associated disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC). However, the usefulness of these strategies is still under debate, mainly because they increase the risk of bleeding, and they do not provide profound protection from mortality [21–23].

In conclusion, the complex pathophysiology of sepsis has thus far hampered the development of adequate treatments aimed at regulating the dysregulated inflammatory response and thus preventing mortality from sepsis. Therapeutics supporting rather than suppressing the host response to the infection may therefore form a better strategy. Additionally, given the similarity shared between sepsis and other systemic inflammatory conditions, the relevance of those therapeutic strategies may extend beyond infectious sepsis.

Changes in lipid metabolism during sepsis

Apart from the inflammatory responses, marked changes in lipid metabolism occur during sepsis, which include a typical rise in free fatty acid (FFA) and triglyceride (TG) levels, and a drastic decrease in low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-cholesterol (LDL-C) and high-density lipoprotein (HDL)-cholesterol (HDL-C) levels [24, 25].

Triglycerides

The rise in FFA and TG levels is likely driven by a combination of factors, including systemic inflammation that promotes insulin resistance of adipose tissue. This results in enhanced intracellular lipolysis and release of FFAs, which bind to albumin and are taken up by the liver to stimulate production of very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL)-TG [26] and VLDL-apolipoprotein B (ApoB) [27]. The increase in triglycerides observed in sepsis may also be a consequence of cytokine-mediated inhibition of lipoprotein lipase (LPL). This mechanism is well-established in other conditions; for instance, decreased adipose tissue LPL activity contributes to the hypertriglyceridemia associated with insulin resistance and type II diabetes, a process mediated by pro-inflammatory cytokines like IL-6 and TNF-α [28, 29]. Consistent with this idea, in vitro experiments have shown that cytokines enhance lipolysis and decrease LPL activity in adipocytes [30], and an in vivo study demonstrated that administration of LPS or cytokines—including TNF, IL-1 and interferons—decreases LPL activity in both adipose tissue and muscle in mice [31]. In addition to sepsis itself, medication used in sepsis often interferes with lipid metabolism; for example, norepinephrine stimulates lipolysis in adipose tissue while corticosteroids cause an overall decrease in metabolic rate [32]. Whether increases in FFA and TG levels contribute to sepsis progression is not clear so far, but fatty acids have been reported to affect the differentiation of immune cells, particularly T cells, as well as their activation and function, with consequences for the balance between anti- and pro-inflammatory signals [33, 34].

Low-density lipoprotein

Most remarkable is the steep drop in total cholesterol (TC), LDL-C, and HDL-C levels during sepsis. These levels are often already low at the time of diagnosis and tend to diminish even further as sepsis progresses [24]. In addition to reduced food intake and poor intestinal absorption of lipids and cholesterol, decreased de novo cholesterol synthesis, impaired cholesterol transport in lipoproteins, and accelerated lipoprotein clearance upon lipoprotein-mediated endotoxin scavenging might be involved in the underlying mechanism [35]. Nevertheless, the precise mechanisms driving sepsis-related hypocholesterolemia remain poorly understood.

LDL carries the majority of cholesterol in the circulation in humans for distribution between tissues, and its levels are strongly associated with the risk of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD). During the progression of sepsis, LDL undergoes alterations in both its structure and overall quantity, with the concentration of LDL-C decreasing by approximately 30% [36]. The reduction in LDL-C levels at least partly results from cholesterol being used for the proliferation and function of immune cells, exemplified by studies showing that cholesterol is needed for monocyte priming, neutrophil activation, hematopoietic stem cell mobilization, and T cell production [37]. In addition, plasma cortisol levels are typically increased in septic patients to counteract inflammation [38], and the majority of the cholesterol needed to produce cortisol in the adrenals is derived from LDL [39]. Furthermore, impaired hepatic function can compromise lipoprotein synthesis and lead to reduced VLDL production [40]. Conversely, accelerated clearance is another potential pathway, facilitated by the formation of LDL-LPS complexes that are rapidly internalized by hepatocytes [41]. Taken together, in sepsis, there seems to be a shift in uptake of LDL from the liver towards the adrenals and immune cells. In line with this notion, administration of LPS decreases hepatic LDL receptor protein levels [42–44] triggered by upregulated hepatic expression of proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) [44], while LPS increases LDL receptor expression in macrophages. LDL can bind and neutralize pathogenic toxins, though less potently than HDL. Therefore, it has been speculated that decreased LDL levels, by reducing the overall endotoxin-neutralizing capacity in the circulation, are associated with higher incidence and long-term mortality rates of sepsis [45, 46].

High-density lipoprotein

HDL plays an essential role in maintaining cholesterol homeostasis, and HDL metabolism is described in Fig. 1. HDL was long considered to have a primary role in counteracting atherosclerosis, partly through its anti-inflammatory and anti-oxidative properties, and mainly by mediating reverse cholesterol transport, i.e., the transport of cholesterol from peripheral cells, including macrophages within atherosclerotic plaques, back to the liver for excretion as bile acids via feces. Therefore, in past decades, many pharmaceutical strategies have been developed to raise HDL as therapy for ASCVD. However, clinical trials generally failed to show protective effects of raising HDL-C on major adverse cardiovascular events, and Mendelian randomization studies did not provide evidence for a causal relationship between HDL-C levels and cardiovascular disease [47, 48]. Yet, since strong reductions in HDL-C levels are observed in patients with sepsis, and normalization of HDL levels after the acute phase response has been reported as a predictor of survival [49, 50], HDL may well play a significant role in controlling inflammatory responses in the context of infections.

In contrast to LDL, which is a relatively homogeneous population of particles with similar size, carrying a single copy of ApoB100 as the main apolipoprotein per particle, HDL comprises a heterogeneous population of particles with a wide range of sizes and (apolipo-) proteins [51]. Based on these characteristics, HDL was suspected to contribute to innate immunity, and it has indeed been shown to bind and modulate endotoxins and other bacterial antigens [52]. Among the various apolipoproteins present in HDL, sepsis mainly decreases the amount of the major structural protein ApoA1. Sepsis thus affects the quantity as well as the quality of HDL, and given the anti-inflammatory and antioxidative properties of ApoA1, the decrease in ApoA1 itself can potentiate the inflammatory response in sepsis [53, 54]. The mechanisms underlying lowered ApoA1 seem to primarily involve displacement of ApoA1 by acute phase proteins, including serum amyloid A (SAA), which may replace up to 80% of the proteins of HDL [55], and as a consequence accelerates the clearance of HDL from blood [56]. Increased expression of secretory phospholipase A2 (PLA2), which promotes inflammation by catalyzing the first step of the arachidonic acid pathway by breaking down phospholipids, has also been shown to trigger the degradation of both HDL and ApoA1 in mice [57]. In addition to the displacement of ApoA1 and accelerated degradation of HDL, TNFα and IL-6 have been shown to dose-dependently inhibit ApoA1 synthesis in hepatic cell lines [40]. The relevance of this observation is not clear but may represent a general inhibition of anabolic processes in the liver during infection.

Sepsis also affects enzymes that are involved in physiological HDL remodeling including lecithin-cholesterol acyltransferase (LCAT), crucial for esterifying cholesterol acquired by nascent HDL and thereby involved in HDL maturation, and hepatic lipase (HL) and endothelial lipase (EL), both of which facilitate lipolysis of HDL particles to accelerate its clearance from circulation by the kidneys. Beyond its central role in cholesterol esterification, LCAT also exhibits protective antioxidative properties by hydrolyzing oxidized phospholipids [58, 59]. In sepsis, this key enzyme is profoundly dysregulated. For example, administration of LPS and TNFα to hamsters has been shown to reduce the hepatic expression and activity of LCAT [40, 60]. Both LCAT concentration and activity are also significantly lower in septic patients compared to non-septic controls, with activity decreasing by more than 50% [61]. Similar observations were made in other studies, where LCAT activities were reduced during infection [62, 63], together underscoring the critical role of functional LCAT in sepsis. Likewise, administration of LPS to Syrian hamsters lowers HL expression in the liver by 75% [64], and inflammatory conditions and infections also decrease the activity of HL [65, 66]. On the other hand, LPS and the immunostimulant poly I: C upregulate expression of EL in macrophages in vitro and in vivo through the activation of Toll-like receptor (TLR) 4 and TLR3, respectively [67]. The collective alterations in the expression and activity of these enzymes involved in HDL remodeling likely account for the increased HDL particle size observed in patients with sepsis [68, 69]. The reductions in HDL-catabolizing enzymes HL and EL may again reflect a general inhibition of anabolic pathways to facilitate the inflammatory response as mentioned above, or may alternatively be an attempt of the body to restore HDL levels after the acute phase. Consistent with the hypothesis that cholesterol is made available for immune cell function during sepsis, LPS, TNFα, and IL-1 were all found to decrease the hepatic uptake of cholesteryl esters (CEs) from HDL through downregulation of scavenger receptor class B type I (SR-B1) mRNA and protein, as shown in Syrian hamsters [70].

Yet another protein at the intersection of HDL and VLDL metabolism, which seems to play a major role during sepsis, is cholesteryl ester transfer protein (CETP). This protein is merely expressed in Kupffer cells within the liver [71] and facilitates the exchange of cholesteryl esters (CEs) and TGs between HDL and TG-rich lipoproteins [72]. We have previously demonstrated that administration of LPS to human CETP-expressing mice induces an acute drop in hepatic CETP expression, related to Kupffer cell activation and/or depletion, accompanied by an increase in HDL [73]. Interestingly, CETP gene expression in the blood of patients with sepsis is higher compared to patients with systemic inflammatory response syndrome (SIRS) by other causes, and CETP may therefore also serve as a biomarker to distinguish between the two syndromes [74]. Trinder et al. performed targeted resequencing of candidate genes involved in HDL metabolism in septic patients with HDL-C levels lower or higher than 0.65 mmol/L [75]. A gain-of-function CETP variant (rs1800777) was found to be associated with reduced levels of HDL-C during sepsis and increased risk of sepsis-related mortality [75]. A meta-analysis aiming at examining the effect of rs1800777 on sepsis-related mortality across seven cohorts demonstrated a notable correlation between this CETP variant and sepsis-related 28-day mortality [76]. In addition, rs1800777 was also found to be associated with an increased risk of sepsis-related acute kidney injury, as indicated by both an observational study and a Mendelian randomization study [77].

Pathophysiological roles of HDL in sepsis

As discussed above, HDL levels drop and HDL functionality is largely impaired during sepsis. Accumulating evidence suggests that this is not merely a consequence of sepsis but also facilitates the inflammatory response. The rate at which HDL levels drop predicts sepsis severity and mortality, and is even superior to levels of lactate, hemoglobin, creatinine, platelets, and leukocytes [78]. Vice versa, higher HDL levels are strongly associated with lowered risk and better sepsis-related outcomes [76, 79]. Mendelian randomization analysis further revealed a potential causal association between lower HDL-C levels and reduced survival rates from sepsis in both the VASST and SPHICU2 cohorts [75].

There are at least three ways in which HDL-raising strategies can aid in protecting against sepsis and septic shock. First, HDL can bind and eliminate circulating bacterial toxins, such as LPS and LTA [80, 81]. Over 90% of circulating LPS in human plasma is bound to lipoproteins, with the highest affinity to HDL compared to LDL (moderate affinity) and VLDL (low affinity) [82]. This binding affinity is primarily determined by the phospholipid content, which is highest for HDL, and is affected during sepsis as discussed before. Therefore, HDL can be considered as the driving force in ‘reverse LPS transport’ [83]. This pathway involves the disaggregation of LPS micelles, the binding of LPS to HDL facilitated by phospholipid-transfer protein (PLTP) and the acute-phase protein LPS-binding protein (LBP), its transport to the liver, uptake of the LBP-LPS complex by hepatocytes, and ultimately, the excretion of LPS via the bile into the feces.

Second, binding of LPS to HDL attenuates TLR4 inflammatory signaling in macrophages [84], which promotes polarization from classically activated, pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages to alternatively activated M2 macrophages that actively fight bacterial infections. Research using human monocyte-derived macrophages has demonstrated that the engagement of LPS with ApoA1 is instrumental in driving the transition towards M2 macrophage polarization [85]. In line with this, HDL was also found to enhance the expression of M2 macrophage markers in atherosclerotic mice [86]. Specifically, the HDL-associated metabolite sphingosine 1-phosphate (S1P) has been shown to promote M2 polarization via enhancing interleukin-4 (IL-4) signaling [87]. Nonetheless, the effect of HDL on skewing of primary human macrophages towards an M2 phenotype is still under debate [88, 89].

Third, by inducing efflux of cholesterol from macrophages, HDL promotes the expression of inflammatory cytokines that facilitate bacterial killing and clearance. As such, HDL has been demonstrated to help macrophages maintain an efficient innate immune response to Pseudomonas aeruginosa infection [90]. This ‘pro-inflammatory’ effect of HDL is specific for macrophages, since HDL exerts anti-inflammatory effects in other vascular cell types, including endothelial cells [91] and smooth muscle cells [92]. In addition, by inducing cholesterol efflux from platelets, HDL prevents platelet hyperreactivity, platelet aggregation, and thrombosis [93]. These collective actions of HDL are probably also efficacious in sepsis, by maintaining the integrity of the vasculature and protecting against organ failure.

In conclusion, HDL plays a beneficial role in sepsis and septic shock by (1) sequestering endotoxins to protect against excessive endotoxemia, (2) skewing macrophages to an alternatively activated phenotype, (3) activating macrophages to promote killing of microbes, and (4) protecting against thrombosis and vascular dysfunction, thereby preventing against organ failure.

Role of HDL-associated apolipoproteins in sepsis

An early review from our lab described the modulating effects of various apolipoproteins on the inflammatory response to LPS [94]. In the sections below, we delve deeper into specific HDL-associated apolipoproteins and their immunomodulatory properties that are relevant for sepsis (Fig. 2).

ApoA1

Approximately 75% of HDL’s protein content, by mass, is comprised of ApoA1 [95]. In a previous section, we already discussed that ApoA1 levels are largely decreased during sepsis, especially in those patients who do not survive [50]. This relationship is likely causal as ApoA1-knockout mice are more susceptible to sepsis-related mortality as induced by cecal ligation and puncture (CLP) than their wildtype littermates. In line with the scavenging function of HDL, LPS neutralization and clearance were also found to be impaired, with amplified TLR4/NF-κB activation and elevated serum IL-6 and TNFα levels as a result [96]. In favorable contrast, overexpression or pharmacological therapy with ApoA1 in mice protects against endotoxemia [97, 98], and both ApoA1 and HDL can prevent LPS-induced cell death in vitro [80]. The ApoA1 mimetic 4 F has been demonstrated to diminish oxidative stress and promote endothelial cell migration [99], indicating its potential in endothelial protection in sepsis. Moreover, ApoA1 mimetic L-4 F as well as ApoA1-Milano have been shown to reduce platelet aggregation, thus decreasing thrombosis formation [100, 101], suggesting that increasing ApoA1 levels could be a potential therapeutic strategy for sepsis-related DIC.

ApoA2

Apolipoprotein A2 (ApoA2) is, with 20% of HDL-s protein content, the second most abundant protein of HDL and is like ApoA1, contributes to the structural integrity, although its impact on HDL functionality is less clear. Previous studies have suggested that lower plasma levels of ApoA2 may contribute to compromised immunity following severe trauma, and ApoA2 has been proposed as a potential target for identifying patients at risk of infection after trauma [102]. ApoA2 was shown to augment monocyte response to LPS by suppressing the LPS-inhibitory effect of LBP, thereby promoting LPS-TLR4 signaling [103]. In addition, overexpression of ApoA2 in mice resulted in HDL with pro-inflammatory rather than anti-inflammatory properties [104]. Collectively, these data may help explain the anti-microbial effects of HDL [90]. Nevertheless, it has to be noted that overexpressing ApoA2 might promote postprandial hypertriglyceridemia [105–107].

ApoC1

In post-surgical patients with severe sepsis, besides the usual decrease in HDL-C and ApoA1, HDL appears to become virtually devoid of apolipoprotein C1 (ApoC1) [62]. Plasma ApoC1 levels are associated with the prognosis of sepsis, as evidenced by a marked decrease in ApoC1 levels among septic non-survivors, while levels gradually returned to normal in survivors [108, 109]. Like ApoA1 and ApoA2, ApoC1 is mainly synthesized by hepatocytes in the liver, and primarily binds to HDL, while it is also present in chylomicrons and VLDL. Previous studies suggested that ApoC1 modulates HDL levels through the inhibition of CETP and SR-BI [110, 111] as well as activation of LCAT [112, 113], and from that perspective, increasing ApoC-1 levels might be protective in sepsis. Homologous to other proteins with antibacterial capabilities, ApoC1 also contains the LPS-binding motif KVKEKLK. ApoC1 binds to LPS to enhance the presentation of LPS to macrophages, thereby stimulating cytokines and the anti-bacterial response in a Klebsiella-induced pneumoniae sepsis mouse model, and gene-dose-dependently protects against sepsis-related mortality [114]. In line with the findings from murine studies, high plasma ApoC1 levels have been shown to reduce the risk of mortality from infection in aged humans, regardless of HDL-C levels [109]. Additionally, ApoC1 overexpression significantly improved insulin sensitivity [115], suggesting its beneficial role in treating sepsis-related comorbidity. Conclusively, increasing ApoC1 may thus be useful in preventing mortality from sepsis, although with caution to avoid an excessive endotoxic response.

ApoD

Apolipoprotein D (ApoD) is one of the lesser-studied HDL-associated proteins, which is found to be associated with neuroprotection and anti-oxidation recently [116]. Unlike other apolipoproteins, ApoD is mainly produced by the brain and testes, and its expression is regulated by many factors, including various acute-phase proteins and NF-κB. ApoD binds to several ligands, with the highest affinity for arachidonic acid, which is the precursor of prostaglandins. These are bioactive chemicals that play a role in various biological processes, including inflammation, platelet aggregation, and cellular regulation [117, 118]. The ability of ApoD to sequester and stabilize arachidonic acid in the cellular membrane, preventing its conversion to pro-inflammatory molecules, might be the mechanism of its described anti-inflammatory effects [119, 120]. Accordingly, ApoD-overexpressing mice are protected against LPS-induced upregulation of TNF-a and IL-6 levels [121]. Furthermore, elevated ApoD protein levels in adipose tissue depots of obese humans are linked to an anti-inflammatory profile [122]. Therefore, raising ApoD levels can be an interesting therapeutic strategy in a variety of inflammation-related disorders that may also include sepsis.

ApoE

ApoE is a critical component of the dysregulated lipoprotein landscape in sepsis, characterized by its increased presence across circulating lipoproteins. Beyond its fundamental role in lipid transport, ApoE is a potent anti-inflammatory agent with a demonstrated capacity to bind and neutralize bacterial endotoxins (e.g., LPS) [123, 124]. This was highlighted by previous work from our group, showing that ApoE targets bound LPS to liver parenchymal cells for degradation and biliary excretion, thereby preventing its accumulation in inflammatory Kupffer cells and the spleen [125]. Intriguingly, the anti-inflammatory potency of ApoE is significantly influenced by its isoform. In humans, the APOE4 allele is associated with a reduced ability to suppress inflammation compared to the APOE3 and APOE2 variants [126]. This is evidenced by a graded inflammatory response to LPS, both in the brain and periphery, where the magnitude of cytokine release (e.g., TNFα, IL-1β, IL-6) follows the order: APOE-KO >APOE4 >APOE3 >APOE2 [127–131]. Consequently, both humans and rodents with the E4 genotype exhibit a more robust pro-inflammatory response to a LPS challenge [132]. The foundational role of ApoE is further confirmed by a study showing that ApoE-deficient mice have exaggerated inflammatory responses to LPS, which can be normalized by reconstituting plasma ApoE levels [133]. Given that impaired ApoE function is linked to increased mortality, these findings establish ApoE as a key modulator of sepsis pathophysiology, a promising therapeutic target, and a potential biomarker for identifying high-risk patients.

ApoM

Apolipoprotein M (ApoM) preferentially binds to HDL, and its levels are also lowered in patients with sepsis [134, 135]. ApoM serves as a carrier for S1P and as such controls circulating S1P levels [136]. In addition to stimulating M2 macrophage polarization, S1P serves to preserve endothelial function, promotes the development of tight junctions, and prevents vascular leakage [137, 138]. These data suggest that ApoM-S1P complex may be responsible for the protective effects of HDL on the vasculature, and thus increasing the levels of ApoM might be advantageous in preventing complications of sepsis. This is particularly relevant given that vessel permeability is a symptom of acute sepsis and SIRS, conditions that escalate the likelihood of circulatory shock. Indeed, overexpression of ApoM was shown to reduce organ damage and attenuate mortality rates in LPS-treated mice [139].

Dapagliflozin, a selective sodium-glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitor (SGLT2i), has been shown to lower the risk of infection-related mortality in patients with chronic kidney disease [140]. In an LPS-induced sepsis mouse model, an SGLT2i was demonstrated to suppress systemic inflammation, attenuate acute kidney injury, as well as improve survival rate [141]. Accordingly, it is not surprising that patients who were on SGLT2i prior to hospitalization and were admitted to the ICU with septic shock showed lower in-hospital mortality compared to those not receiving this therapy before [142]. Given that ApoM and SGLT2i share similar benefits in inflammation and sepsis, a recent study found that dapagliflozin could preserve circulating ApoM levels in an LPS-treated sepsis murine model and in patients diagnosed with COVID-19 [143]. In line with other studies, they also demonstrated that dapagliflozin could reduce serum creatinine levels in the LPS sepsis mouse model, an indicator of less renal injury. Interestingly, dapagliflozin could protect mice from sepsis-induced endothelial leak via ApoM-S1P signalling, although the neutrophil recruitment effect of dapagliflozin is not entirely dependent on ApoM/S1P.

Tools to Raise HDL levels in sepsis

Many pharmaceutical strategies have been developed to raise HDL as therapy for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease. In the following section, we further discuss some of those pharmacological tools that may hold promise in the management of sepsis (Fig. 3).

ApoA1 and Recombinant HDL

In line with the ability of ApoA1 to bind and neutralize LPS [98], administration of human ApoA1 improves short-term survival to LPS-induced endotoxemia in both mouse and rat models [144]. Likewise, the ApoA1 mimetic peptide L-4 F was shown to attenuate LPS-induced acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [145] and mortality [146] in rats. L-4 F administration also enhances the contractile responses to phenylephrine and prevents nitric oxide synthase 2 (NOS2) induction and nitric oxide formation, thus contributing to the preservation of the vascular function in endotoxic rats [147]. Moreover, the ApoA1 mimetic L-4 F improves kidney function and mitigates kidney damage in advanced sepsis in a rat model of CLP [148]. Notably, a synthetic heparan sulphate octadecasaccharide has been reported to expedite the formation of ApoA1/LPS complexes, to protect against sepsis-related injury, and to improve survival in CLP-induced septic mice, which may therefore hold potential to even enhance the anti-sepsis effects of ApoA1 [149].

Recombinant, reconstituted or synthetic HDL, hereafter referred to as rHDL, represents another promising alternative for the treatment of sepsis, since administration of rHDL rapidly and directly increases total HDL levels. Various formulations of rHDL were shown to accelerate LPS neutralization and clearance, to attenuate the inflammatory response, to protect the vasculature, and to improve survival rate in various mouse models of sepsis [150–152]. Infusion of rHDL was also shown to potently dampen the LPS-induced release of cytokines in humans [153]. Interestingly, rHDL (i.e., CER-001) was recently used to treat four COVID-19 patients with severe cytokine storm. Although it is difficult to draw any firm conclusions from such a small study, the three patients who received rHDL and were not on mechanical ventilation showed early recovery [154]. Excitingly, early results of a Phase 2a clinical trial with CER-001 in the treatment of septic patients at high risk of developing acute kidney injury suggest fewer ICU days for patients treated, lower requirement for organ support, and improved 30-day survival [155].

A potentially even more potent rHDL treatment is a formulation based on the ApoA1-Milano variant. In a prophylactic setting, treatment of rats with ApoA1-Milano resulted in marked attenuation of LPS-induced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines, adhesion molecules, and lipid peroxidation, which was significantly more effective as compared with wildtype ApoA1 [156]. However, as far as we know, all efforts to develop rHDL based on ApoA1-Milano have been halted, in large part because it appeared to be very difficult to generate sufficient amounts of the protein for clinical trials.

LCAT activators and Recombinant LCAT

Like many other HDL-raising strategies, small molecular activators of LCAT and recombinant human LCAT (rhLCAT) have been developed for the treatment of dyslipidemia and atherosclerosis [157]. Treatment with these compounds has been shown to increase HDL-C, CE, and ApoA1 levels, and resulted in larger, more functional HDL [158]. The first in-human trial with rhLCAT showed that a single 1-hour infusion can increase HDL levels by up to 42% and maintain levels above baseline for up to 4 days [159]. Interestingly, LCAT-deficient mice have been reported to have higher LPS-induced inflammation, which was counteracted by adenovirus-mediated LCAT expression [160]. Interestingly, LCAT activity shows a significant inverse correlation with markers like C-reactive protein, procalcitonin, and IL-6. Most importantly, higher LCAT activity is associated with a lower risk of mortality [61]. Nevertheless, the question remains to what extent raising LCAT levels or activity might aid in the context of sepsis when ApoA1 and HDL levels are low.

HL and EL inhibitors

The lack of HL or EL generates large phospholipid- and TG-rich HDL particles, while overexpression of either protein leads to decreased levels of HDL-C and increased catabolism of ApoA1 and ApoA2. Blocking EL with the monoclonal antibody MEDI5884 compound was shown to increase HDL-C levels in non-human primates and healthy human volunteers [161]. Whether inhibition of HL or EL in the context of endotoxemia or sepsis, when their levels are already downregulated [64], can still increase HDL levels to a relevant extent is unknown.

SR-B1 Inhibition

Whilst whole-body genetic or pharmacological SR-B1 inhibition strongly increases HDL-C levels, and hepatic SR-B1 knockdown has been shown to reduce atherosclerosis in a mouse model [162], it remains largely unknown whether inhibiting SR-B1 is beneficial for ASCVD, as SR-B1 is critical for reverse cholesterol transport and excretion of cholesterol via the feces. A small number of (pre-clinical) studies suggested some potential for SR-B1 inhibition as anti-cancer treatment, but very interestingly, SR-B1 is also known to be involved in various types of infection and facilitates the uptake of mycobacteria and hepatitis C virus. More recently, SR-B1 was identified as the main receptor for entry of SARS-CoV-2 [163]. Therefore, one might reason that SR-B1 inhibition with, for example, BLT-1 [164], can be used in the treatment of sepsis. However, the anti-inflammatory effects of HDL and ApoA1 are largely dependent on their capability to bind and clear endotoxins from the circulation, for which SR-BI is also crucial [165, 166]. Indeed, SR-BI-deficient mice, despite their elevated HDL levels, display significantly higher levels of plasma TNFα when challenged with LPS than their wild-type counterparts. SR-B1-deficient mice are also more sensitive to LPS-induced lethality [167]. Thus, SR-B1 inhibition to increase HDL-C levels is most likely not the way forward for the treatment of sepsis.

Statins

Beyond its role in atherosclerosis, cholesterol is a key structural component of lipid rafts and caveolae—membrane microdomains essential for organizing toll-like receptor (TLR) signaling [168]. Elevated cellular cholesterol can therefore amplify inflammatory signaling, leading to excessive cytokine production. This provides a strong rationale for cholesterol-lowering strategies, such as statins, to modulate this pathological inflammation. Statins are primarily prescribed to lower LDL-C, however, they also exert pleiotropic anti-inflammatory effects and induce a modest but consistent increase in HDL-C, an effect potentially mediated by reduced CETP activity [169–172]. This dual action—lowering pro-inflammatory LDL and raising anti-inflammatory HDL—may enhance the capacity to neutralize bacterial components during sepsis. Consistent with this, experimental models demonstrate that statin treatment preserves cardiac function and improves survival [173]. In humans, observational studies suggest prior statin use is associated with reduced sepsis-related mortality [174]. However, this protective effect has not been consistently confirmed in randomized controlled trials or meta-analyses, likely due to heterogeneity in patient populations and intervention timing [175–177]. Despite these inconsistent clinical outcomes, the compelling biological rationale underpinning statins’ immunomodulatory and lipid-modulating effects secures their importance in the broader discussion of therapeutic strategies for sepsis.

CETP inhibitors

Downregulation of CETP levels, as observed during severe sepsis, as induced by endotoxins, may be a physiological reaction to raise and restore HDL levels [178]. This raises the question whether pharmacological CETP inhibition can aid in sepsis. Very interestingly, genetically influenced lower CETP activity is associated with increased survival rate among patients with sepsis [76]. A recent Mendelian randomization study also suggests that genetically-influenced higher HDL-C levels via targeting at CETP reduces the risk of developing sepsis [179]. Multiple pharmacological CETP inhibitors have been developed and assessed in clinical trials in the context of ASCVD, and are in theory thus readily available in the context of sepsis.

Strikingly, the CETP inhibitor anacetrapib has recently been shown to improve survival from CLP-induced sepsis in mice, even when administered after induction of sepsis [76]. Subsequently, it was shown that anacetrapib improves survival in an even more clinically relevant Streptococcus pneumoniae-induced pneumonia [180]. In that study, anacetrapib was shown to attenuate host inflammatory responses and enhance bacterial clearance, thereby mitigating organ dysfunction and reducing mortality. Furthermore, in vitro studies revealed that anacetrapib can directly enhance activation of proinflammatory signaling in peripheral blood mononuclear cells in the absence of HDL [180]. The development of obicetrapib, which is the most potent CETP inhibitor thus far increasing HDL-C by as much as 165% [181], and which is currently being evaluated in patients with ASCVD within the phase 3 BROADWAY clinical trial, provides the exciting opportunity to test whether CETP inhibition may also be effective in raising HDL and thereby preventing mortality in human sepsis. Interestingly, CETP inhibitors are intended for patient populations with high risk of ASCVD who also face a significant risk of sepsis. In addition, clinical trials with CETP inhibitors have shown a consistent reduction in the risk of new-onset diabetes, while patients with diabetes account for more than 20% of sepsis cases and have been shown to be more susceptible to infections [182–185]. In these groups, CETP inhibition could offer a ‘two-birds-one-stone’ benefit by simultaneously mitigating cardiovascular and diabetes risk and modulating innate immunity through higher HDL and anti-inflammatory effects to prevent risk of infectious diseases. Although this concept remains speculative, it is readily testable. Secondary analyses of large cardiovascular outcome trials for CETP inhibitors could determine if treated participants exhibited lower rates of serious infection or sepsis. Furthermore, dedicated studies in septic populations are warranted to evaluate the potential of repurposing these agents.

Concluding remarks

Sepsis causes notable changes in lipoprotein metabolism that do not appear to be just the consequence of the disease. Instead, lipoproteins and in particular HDL, play an active role in lowering inflammation as well as resolving the underlying infection. HDL can protect against sepsis and septic shock by (1) sequestering endotoxins to protect against excessive endotoxemia, (2) skewing macrophages to an alternatively activated phenotype, (3) activating macrophages to promote killing of microbes, and (4) protecting against thrombosis and vascular dysfunction, thereby preventing organ failure. These protective effects of HDL seem to be partly dependent on phospholipids, in addition to specific apolipoproteins (e.g., ApoA1, ApoA2, ApoC1, ApoM, ApoD, and ApoE) and signaling molecules (e.g., S1P).

The fact that the capability of the body to restore HDL levels after the acute phase is predictive of survival, highlighting the potential for HDL-raising strategies in sepsis. Indeed, preclinical studies showed that raising HDL levels by administration of rHDL or ApoA1 mimetics attenuates inflammatory responses and improves survival rates in animal models of sepsis. Likewise, CETP inhibition by anacetrapib prevented mortality from sepsis in various murine models [76, 180]. These protective effects of raising HDL are likely caused by mechanisms in addition to the reduction of inflammation and endotoxin scavenging, as many anti-inflammatory and anti-LPS antibodies have thus far failed in clinical trials [186].

Although clinical studies focused on the efficiency of HDL-raising strategies on sepsis are still limited, critically ill patients treated with rHDL (i.e., CER-001) have reduced risk of severe AKI and decreased need for further organ support [155]. It should be noted that rHDL is a non-conventional drug formulation and should be administered intravenously. Given that genetically influenced lower CETP activity is associated with increased survival rate among septic patients, and recent preclinical studies showed that CETP inhibitors have been highly effective in preventing experimental sepsis-related mortality, treating patients with sepsis with recently developed potent oral CETP inhibitors could be a safe and promising strategy that is worthwhile to explore further.
