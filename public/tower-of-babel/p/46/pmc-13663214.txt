Surgical complications, functional outcomes and associated factors among men underwent transurethral resection of the prostate at Benjamin Mkapa Hospital, Tanzania
Authors: Gerald P. Mwing’a, Emmanuel Tairo, Hindu Ibrahim Hussein, Edward Msokwa, Shemsa Said Khatib, Sanuni Ally Kessy, Said Mawji, Okoa Sukunala, Vitus Kajerero, Remigius Rugakingira, Joseph Elisha, Angelina M. Lutambi, Basiliana Emidi, Monica Kessy, Alphonce Chandika, Abel Makubi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionTransurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) is the gold standard surgical treatment for moderate to severe lower urinary tract symptoms due to benign prostatic hyperplasia. However, data on surgical complications and functional adverse outcomes in Tanzania remain limited. This study assessed the incidence and factors associated with surgical complications and described functional adverse outcomes following TURP at Benjamin Mkapa Hospital (BMH).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using routinely collected data from men aged ≥ 40 years who underwent TURP at BMH between 2021 and 2023 with at least one year of follow-up. Patients with prior prostate or urethral surgery, neurogenic bladder, or incidental prostate cancer were excluded. Logistic regression was used to estimate crude and adjusted odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsAmong 705 men (mean age 69 ± 8.9 years), 67 (9.50%) developed surgical complications, and 76 (10.78%) had functional adverse outcomes. Overall, 132 (18.72%) had at least one recorded outcome, noting that the two categories were not mutually exclusive and functional adverse outcomes may include pre-existing conditions due to a lack of baseline assessment. Ejaculatory dysfunction (10.35%) was the most common functional adverse outcome, while urethral stricture (4.54%) was the most frequent surgical complication. Late complications (6.80%) were more common than early complications (2.98%). Hypertension (aOR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.20–3.53) and diabetes (aOR = 4.81, 95% CI: 2.36–9.80) were independently associated with higher odds of surgical complications, while prostate size < 80 cm³ was protective (aOR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.33–0.93).ConclusionSurgical complication rates following TURP at BMH were comparable to international reports. Hypertension, diabetes, and larger prostate size were associated with higher odds of surgical complications. Functional adverse outcomes were common but should be interpreted cautiously because baseline functional status was not systematically assessed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12894-026-02214-1.

Background

Transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) remains one of the widely used endoscopic surgical procedures for the management of moderate to severe lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) secondary to benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), and has been considered a gold standard treatment for this condition for several decades [1, 2]. The procedure involves resection of prostatic tissues from the transition zone using an electrified wire loop, thereby relieving bladder outlet obstruction and improving urinary symptoms. TURP can be performed using either monopolar (M-TURP) or bipolar (B-TURP) energy systems. In monopolar TURP, electrical current passes through the patient’s body to a return electrode, requiring non-ionic irrigation fluids such as glycine or mannitol. In contrast, bipolar TURP confines the current within the resectoscope and allows the use of isotonic saline, reducing the risk of electrolyte disturbances such as TUR syndrome [3]. Current guidelines from both the European Association of Urology (EAU) and the American Urological Association (AUA) recommend TURP for men with prostate volumes between 30 and 80 mL, particularly in those who fail medical therapy or present with complications such as recurrent urinary retention, bladder stones, recurrent urinary tract infections, or renal impairment [4, 5]. Although the global use of TURP has declined with the increasing adoption of minimally invasive techniques such as holmium laser enucleation of the prostate (HoLEP) and photo-selective vaporisation of the prostate (PVP), it remains the most accessible and commonly performed procedure in many low- and middle-income countries [6–8]. In sub-Saharan Africa, including Tanzania, TURP continues to be the predominant surgical option due to limited availability of advanced technologies, financial constraints, and restricted training opportunities [9, 10]. Despite its effectiveness, TURP is associated with a range of surgical complications. Large multicenter studies report overall complication rates of approximately 10–20%, with intraoperative complications around 3% and mortality rates below 0.5% [11].

Common complications include haemorrhage requiring transfusion, clot retention, urinary tract infection, TUR syndrome, urethral stricture, bladder neck contracture, and need for reoperation. In addition, functional outcomes such as urinary incontinence and ejaculatory dysfunction may occur following the procedure and can significantly affect quality of life [12, 13]. Several factors have been reported to influence the risk of complications following TURP. Operative factors such as prolonged surgical duration are associated with increased risks of bleeding, infection, and reoperation [14]. Larger prostate size has also been linked to increased operative complexity, greater blood loss, and higher perioperative risk [15]. Preoperative use of 5α-reductase inhibitors, such as finasteride, has been shown to reduce perioperative bleeding by decreasing prostatic vascularity [16]. Additionally, patient-related factors, including comorbidities (e.g., hypertension and diabetes), pre-existing urinary tract infections, and inadequate preoperative optimisation, may significantly influence postoperative outcomes. In Tanzania, efforts to improve TURP outcomes have focused on increased adoption of bipolar technology, strengthening perioperative monitoring, and enhancing surgical training [2, 10, 17]. However, context-specific evidence on the incidence of surgical complications, functional outcomes, and associated risk factors remains limited, particularly in central Tanzania. Understanding these outcomes is essential for improving perioperative care and guiding clinical decision-making in resource-limited settings. Therefore, this study aimed to assess the incidence of surgical complications, describe functional outcomes, and identify factors associated with surgical complications among men undergoing TURP at Benjamin Mkapa Hospital (BMH) between 2021 and 2023.

Methods

Study design and setting

This was a retrospective cohort study using routinely collected hospital data conducted at Benjamin Mkapa Hospital (BMH) in Dodoma, Tanzania. The hospital is located within the University of Dodoma compounds, about 16 km from the city centre. It serves as a zonal hospital in the central zone of Tanzania. The BMH receives the majority of patients from Dodoma, Singida, Tabora, Manyara, Iringa and Morogoro regions. The hospital has a bed capacity of 400 beds with bed occupancy of approximately 200 to 250 patients. Additionally, it is used as a teaching hospital for the University of Dodoma (UDOM). The BMH was established in 2015 to offer specialised and super-specialised services, including urologic surgery. The urology department has a bed capacity of 25 beds, 7 urologists and 15 nurses. Approximately 30 patients are admitted each month for TURP. The department currently uses a single operating theatre room with a single operating tower.

Study population and eligibility

All adult males aged 40 years and above who underwent TURP at BMH after being diagnosed with Benign prostate hyperplasia (BPH) between January 2021 and December 2023. The study included patients who had attended the clinic for at least one-year post TURP. Participants were followed from the time of TURP to the occurrence of complications or up to 12 months postoperatively, based on available clinical records. However, those who had a prior history of TURP or any prostate or urethral procedure such as open prostatectomy, Transurethral incision of the prostate (TUIP), Direct visual urethrotomy (DVU), or urethroplasty at other facilities were excluded from the analysis. Likewise, those who had a concurrent condition causing LUTS, such as neurogenic bladder, and participants who had incidental prostate cancer were also excluded.

Sample size and sampling procedure

A total of 1,029 men aged ≥ 40 years underwent transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) at Benjamin Mkapa Hospital (BMH) between January 2021 and December 2023. Of these, 324 patients were excluded (124 with incidental prostate cancer, 75 with a history of prior endoscopic surgery, and 97 who were lost to follow-up). Therefore, a total of 705 patients met the eligibility criteria and were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1).

Data extraction and collection procedure

All patients who were planned for TURP were evaluated by urologists and anesthesiologists at an outpatient clinic where baseline investigations were ordered. Urodynamic assessment was not performed due to the unavailability of equipment. TURP was performed using bipolar or monopolar systems based on the availability of equipment. Normal saline and 5% dextrose solution were used for irrigation for bipolar and monopolar TURP, respectively. All procedures were performed by experienced surgeons with a minimum of 3 years of experience. Bladder irrigation was kept until the effluent was clear, and the catheter was removed after 48 h. Upon discharge, the patients were instructed to attend follow-up clinic at 1 month, 3 months, 6 months and 1 year. The dataset for this study was obtained from the hospital’s electronic medical records. All the required information was collected in the system by the trained research assistants who used a list of constructed structured questionnaires. A structured questionnaire was developed specifically for this study for data collection. An English-language version of the questionnaire has been provided as Supplementary File 1. After a thorough review of the dataset structure, variable definitions, and coding framework, additional recoding and data cleaning were performed to refine variables and ensure they aligned with the study objectives. These steps were taken to improve the dataset’s suitability for statistical analysis. The research team evaluated the collected information by observing the ward rounds notes, outpatient clinic notes during follow-up visits, laboratory results, imaging results, cystoscopy findings, and urine culture reports. Collected data included the mode of TURP performed, duration of hospital stays, duration of catheterisation, duration of medical treatment before TURP and preoperative prostate volume. Furthermore, data concerning the occurrence of TUR syndrome, excessive bleeding, UTI, Urethral stricture, Bladder neck contracture, urinary incontinence, and retrograde ejaculation after TURP were collected. Information on the weight of prostatic tissues resected and resection time was not available in the medical records.

Variable measurements

Dependent variable

The primary outcome of this study was the occurrence of postoperative complications following transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP). Postoperative complications were defined as any adverse event related to TURP occurring during the index hospitalisation or within one year after surgery [18], as documented in the hospital electronic medical records and outpatient follow-up notes. Patients were routinely followed at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months. Only patients with at least one year of follow-up were included in the analysis. Complications were categorised into early complications (≤ 30 days), late complications (> 30 days to 1 year), and functional adverse outcomes [19]. Early complications included TUR syndrome (defined as serum sodium < 125 mEq/L accompanied by clinical features of dilutional hyponatremia), urinary tract infection (defined as a positive midstream urine culture within two weeks of TURP with at least one uropathies isolated), and excessive haemorrhage (defined as haemoglobin < 7 g/dL and/or haematocrit < 21% within 7 days postoperatively or the need for blood transfusion during the perioperative period) [20].

Late complications included urethral stricture and bladder neck contracture, which were assessed among patients presenting with obstructive symptoms during follow-up [19]. Urethral stricture was defined based on the presence of obstructive symptoms with confirmation by retrograde urethrography (RUG) and/or cystoscopy, as documented in the medical records. Initial diagnosis was made using RUG interpreted by a radiologist, while cystoscopy was performed by a urologist when RUG findings were inconclusive or normal. Both urethral stricture and bladder neck contracture were assessed only in symptomatic patients during follow-up visits and were not systematically screened in all participants [21].

Functional adverse outcomes included urinary incontinence and ejaculatory dysfunction, as reported during outpatient follow-up visits. Urinary incontinence was defined as involuntary leakage of urine occurring at least six months after TURP. Ejaculatory dysfunction was defined as the absence or abnormal ejaculation, assessed after the recommended six-week postoperative abstinence period. Ejaculatory dysfunction was assessed during routine outpatient follow-up visits based on patient self-report after the recommended six-week postoperative abstinence period [22]. Assessment was not performed using standardised instruments and was not systematically documented in all patients.

The primary outcome variable was constructed as a binary composite variable indicating the occurrence of at least one complication (“1” = yes, “0” = no). In addition, complications were categorised and analysed by timing and type (early, late, and functional adverse outcomes) to enhance clinical interpretability. The severity of surgical complications (early and late) was classified using the Clavien–Dindo system, while functional adverse outcomes were reported separately without grading.

Independent variables

The independent variables included participants’ age (years), occupation, and place of residence, as well as clinical and perioperative characteristics such as TURP modality (bipolar/monopolar), duration of hospital stays (days), and duration of catheterisation (days). Preoperative prostate volume was measured using transabdominal ultrasonography (TAUS) performed by trained radiographers and recorded in cubic centimetres (cm³). It was categorised as < 80 cm³ vs. ≥ 80 cm³ based on prior literature [23, 24]. Comorbid conditions, including hypertension and diabetes mellitus, were defined based on documented medical history or current use of antihypertensive or antidiabetic medications.

Data management and analysis

Relevant variables related to TURP surgery performed between 2021 and 2023 were anonymously extracted from the hospital electronic medical records. Data cleaning, including recoding, variable generation, labelling, and renaming, was conducted before the analysis. All the analyses were performed using Stata version 17 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX) and missing data were handled using complete-case analysis. Continuous variables were summarised as mean ± standard deviation if approximately normally distributed, and as median (interquartile range, IQR) if skewed. Categorical variables were summarised using frequencies and percentages. The distribution of surgical complications across participant characteristics was described using proportions.

Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression models were fitted to identify factors associated with surgical complications. Crude and adjusted odds ratios (cOR and aOR) with 95% confidence intervals were reported. Variables with p-values < 0.20 in bivariate analysis were included in the multivariable model, and statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF), with values < 5 indicating no significant collinearity [25]. Variables assessed included age, hypertension, and diabetes, and all VIF values were close to 1, indicating no evidence of multicollinearity.

Ethical consideration

The anonymised data used in this study were obtained from the Medipro-5 and Mapping tag ERX hospital system with proper authorisation. The extraction process was designed to safeguard privacy and confidentiality to avoid access to personally identifiable information, such as file numbers, in accordance with ethical standards. The ethical approval of using the data was secured from the Benjamin Mkapa Hospital (BMH).

Results

Characteristics of study participants

A total of 705 men were included, with a mean age of 69 ± 8.86 years, and the majority were aged ≥ 60 years 602 (85.39%). Most participants were peasants or engaged in petty business 532 (75.46%) and resided in Dodoma 456 (64.68%). The majority underwent bipolar TURP 461 (65.39%). Most patients had a short postoperative hospital stay (≤ 5 days), 632 (89.65%) and catheterisation < 3 days, 686 (97.30%). Approximately one-quarter had prostate size ≥ 80 cm³ 173 (24.54%). Hypertension was present in 261 (37.13%) and diabetes in 50 (7.10%). The cumulative incidence of surgical complications was 67 (9.50%), while functional complications occurred in 76 (10.78%) patients. The mean preoperative IPSS was 27.24 ± 3.28, indicating predominantly severe symptoms. Median prostate volume was 64 cm³ (IQR: 53–80), with median hospitalisation of 5 days and catheterisation duration of 2 days (Table 1).

Distribution of surgical complications and functional adverse outcomes following TURP

Among 705 men who underwent TURP at BMH, 132 (18.72%) had at least one recorded outcome during follow-up, including 67 (9.50%) with surgical complications and 76 (10.78%) with functional adverse outcomes. Functional adverse outcomes were assessed during follow-up and may include pre-existing conditions, as baseline status was not documented. Functional adverse outcomes were the most frequent, mainly ejaculatory dysfunction (10.35%), while urinary incontinence was uncommon (0.43%); however, 28% of participants were not sexually active, which may influence this estimate. Early surgical complications (≤ 30 days) occurred in 21 patients (2.98%), primarily UTI (1.28%), clot retention (0.99%), TUR syndrome (0.57%), and blood transfusion (0.57%). Late complications (> 30 days to 1 year) were observed in 48 patients (6.80%), including urethral stricture (4.54%), repeat TURP (2.13%), and bladder neck contracture (0.28%). Surgical complications were graded using the Clavien–Dindo system, with grade II in 13 (1.84%), grade III in 54 (7.66%), and grade IV in 4 (0.57%) patients. Functional outcomes were reported separately and not graded (Fig. 2).

Incidence of surgical complications by participant characteristics

A total of 705 men underwent TURP and were followed for up to 12 months. The mean age was 69.0 ± 8.9 years, with most participants aged ≥ 60 years 602 (85.40%). The majority were engaged in peasant or petty business activities 532 (75.46%) and resided in Dodoma 456 (64.68%).

The cumulative incidence of surgical complications was 67 (9.5%) during follow-up. The occurrence of surgical complications was similar across age groups (p = 0.660) and TURP modes (p = 0.389). Variation was observed across occupational categories (p = 0.001), while differences by place of residence were less pronounced (p = 0.075). Participants with prolonged hospitalisation (> 5 days) contributed 22 (32.84%) of complications compared to 45 (67.16%) among those hospitalised for ≤ 5 days (p < 0.001). Similarly, 12 (17.91%) of complications occurred among those with catheterisation > 3 days, compared to 55 (82.09%) among those with shorter duration (p < 0.001). The distribution of complications also showed minimal variation by prostate size (p = 0.174). A larger share of complications was observed among participants with hypertension, 39 (58.21%) and diabetes, 16 (23.88%), compared to those without these conditions (both p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Factors associated with surgical complications following TURP

In bivariate analysis, hypertension (cOR = 2.60, 95% CI: 1.56–4.33, p < 0.001) and diabetes (cOR = 5.56, 95% CI: 2.88–10.76, p < 0.001) were associated with higher odds of surgical complications. Other variables, including age, TURP mode, finasteride use, and prostate size, did not show statistically significant differences.

In the multivariable logistic regression model, hypertension and diabetes remained independently associated with surgical complications. Participants with hypertension had approximately twofold higher odds of complications (aOR = 2.06, 95% CI: 1.20–3.53, p = 0.009), while those with diabetes had nearly fivefold higher odds (aOR = 4.81, 95% CI: 2.36–9.80, p < 0.001). Similarly, prostate size < 80 cm³ was associated with lower odds of complications compared to those with ≥ 80 cm³ prostate size (aOR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.33–0.93), indicating a statistically significant protective effect. Age, TURP mode, and finasteride use were not significantly associated with surgical complications after adjustment (Table 3).

Discussion

This study evaluated surgical complications and functional adverse outcomes among 705 men who underwent transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) at Benjamin Mkapa Hospital (BMH). The cumulative incidence of surgical complications was 9.5%, which falls within the range reported in large international series (approximately 5–15%) and reflects the continued safety of TURP as a standard surgical treatment for benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) [11, 21].

Functional adverse outcomes were observed in 10.8% of participants, predominantly ejaculatory dysfunction (10.35%). However, these estimates should be interpreted with caution, as baseline sexual function was not systematically assessed, and 28% of participants were not sexually active. Reported rates of ejaculatory dysfunction in the literature are substantially higher (30–70%), particularly in Western populations [22], suggesting that underreporting, sociocultural factors, and lack of standardised assessment tools may have contributed to the lower observed incidence in this study. Similar underestimation has been reported in sub-Saharan African settings, where sexual dysfunction is both highly prevalent and frequently underdiagnosed due to cultural stigma and limited routine assessment. For example, erectile dysfunction prevalence in sub-Saharan Africa has been reported to range widely from 17% to over 60% across different populations, including Tanzania, Nigeria, and Uganda [26]. Other is a systematic review of TURP trials reported retrograde ejaculation in approximately 46% of patients [27] while other reviews and cohort studies have documented rates of 47–65% after TURP [28, 29]. Despite this high burden, studies highlight that sexual health conditions are often underreported and under-recognised, largely due to sociocultural barriers and lack of proactive screening in clinical practice [26]. Additionally, population-based data from Uganda indicate that a large proportion of men with lower urinary tract symptoms also experience compromised sexual function, yet these outcomes are rarely systematically documented in routine care [30]. Together, these findings support the likelihood that the relatively low rate of ejaculatory dysfunction observed in this study reflects measurement limitations and contextual underreporting rather than the true absence of the condition.

Regarding the pattern of complications, late complications (6.8%) were more frequent than early complications (3.0%), with urethral stricture (4.54%) being the most common. This finding is consistent with global reports indicating post-TURP stricture rates of approximately 2–10% [19, 27]. This burden of late complications in this setting may reflect delayed presentation and advanced disease at the time of surgery, which have been widely reported in sub-Saharan Africa, where patients often present with long-standing lower urinary tract symptoms and complications of BPH [31]. In addition, pre-existing urinary tract pathology, including recurrent urinary tract infections and chronic bladder outlet obstruction, has been identified as a contributor to postoperative urethral stricture formation in urological studies [32]. Furthermore, limited postoperative follow-up, delayed health-seeking behaviour, and limited access to specialised care in many African settings, including Tanzania, may delay detection and management of late complications, contributing to their apparent burden [10, 33, 34].

In this study, hypertension and diabetes were independently associated with higher odds of surgical complications, even after adjustment for other factors. These findings are consistent with broader surgical literature demonstrating that metabolic and vascular comorbidities impair tissue healing, increase susceptibility to infection, and contribute to postoperative morbidity. For example, a recent meta-analysis found that diabetes mellitus raised the risk of any postoperative complication, including infections, reoperations, and wound healing disorders [35, 36]. Similarly, evidence shows that poor glycemic control has been strongly associated with delayed wound healing, surgical site infections, dehiscence, and extended hospital stays in multiple surgical cohorts [37].

Hypertension, in particular, is highly prevalent among surgical patients in sub-Saharan Africa and is often poorly controlled in perioperative settings, which may adversely affect surgical outcomes [38]. Studies have shown that patients with hypertension are at significantly higher risk of postoperative complications such as bleeding and adverse cardiovascular events compared to normotensive patients [39]. In Tanzania, hospital-based studies have also reported a substantial burden of hypertension among surgical patients, highlighting its role as a key contributor to postoperative morbidity [40]. These findings suggest that hypertension and diabetes play a critical role in determining postoperative outcomes and underscore the importance of preoperative optimisation and perioperative blood pressure control, particularly in low-resource settings such as Tanzania.

We also observed that a smaller prostate size (< 80 cm³) was associated with lower odds of surgical complications, suggesting that larger prostates may confer increased operative risk. This is consistent with existing evidence indicating that larger prostate size is associated with longer operative time, increased bleeding, and higher perioperative risk [41, 42]. However, the effect of prostate size on postoperative complications is not uniform across studies, and its influence on late complications and functional outcomes remains inconsistent.

No significant association was observed between TURP modality (monopolar vs. bipolar) and surgical complications. This finding aligns with randomised trials and meta-analyses demonstrating comparable safety profiles between the two techniques, although bipolar TURP may reduce specific risks such as TUR syndrome and bleeding [21, 43–45]. In our setting, differences in outcomes may be influenced by surgeon experience, case selection, and resource availability, which can reduce the expected advantages of specific technologies.

Importantly, variables such as prolonged catheterisation and length of hospital stay, although associated with complications in descriptive analysis, were not included as independent predictors in multivariable modelling due to the potential for reverse causation, as they may reflect consequences of complicated postoperative courses rather than true risk factors. This highlights the need for cautious interpretation of perioperative care variables in observational studies.

Strengths and limitations

This study provides valuable insights from a tertiary hospital setting in Tanzania, contributing to the limited literature on TURP outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa. However, several limitations should be considered. First, the retrospective design relied on routine clinical records, and the timing of complication detection may have varied across patients, which may have introduced some misclassification in the timing of complications. Second, functional outcomes were based on self-report and lacked standardised measurement tools, which may have led to underestimation. Third, the absence of baseline functional status precluded differentiation between pre-existing and postoperative conditions. Finally, inclusion of only patients with available follow-up may have introduced selection bias, as patients lost to follow-up or excluded may differ from those included, potentially affecting the generalizability of the finding.

Conclusion

The incidence of surgical complications following TURP at BMH was comparable to international reports. Hypertension, diabetes, and larger prostate size were associated with higher odds of complications, highlighting the importance of preoperative optimisation and risk stratification. Functional adverse outcomes were common but should be interpreted cautiously due to the lack of baseline assessment and possible under-reporting. Future prospective studies incorporating standardised outcome measures and baseline assessments are needed to better characterise TURP outcomes in this setting.
