Enhancing the Understanding of Anti-calcitonin Gene-related Peptide Monoclonal Antibodies in Chronic Migraine Prevention
Authors: Prashant Soni
Published: 2024

TO THE EDITOR

I read with great interest the study by Haridas et al. [1] titled “Efficacy and Safety of Anti-CGRP Monoclonal Antibodies in Prevention of Chronic Migraine: A Bayesian Network Meta-analysis”. The authors have provided a thorough analysis of the relative efficacy and safety of various anti-calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP) monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) for the prevention of chronic migraine. However, I would like to address some concerns regarding the methodology and interpretation of the findings.

The study primarily focused on only mean monthly migraine days, responder rates, and adverse events. To provide a more comprehensive assessment, it would be beneficial to include additional efficacy outcomes such as monthly headache days, monthly migraine hours, and other responder rates (e.g., 75% and 100% reduction in migraine days). Similarly, expanding the safety outcomes to include general adverse events and serious adverse events would offer a more detailed safety profile of the interventions.

The authors chose a random-effects model for their analysis but did not explain their preference over a fixed-effects model. Clarifying this choice and discussing the potential implications of model selection would enhance the transparency of the study. Additionally, the study did not report on heterogeneity measures or conduct sensitivity or subgroup analyses to address potential sources of heterogeneity. Incorporating these elements would help validate the robustness of the results.

The analysis did not adhere to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Network Meta-Analyses (PRISMA-NMA) guidelines [2], as no PRISMA-NMA checklist was reported. Following these guidelines would improve the methodological rigor of the study. Furthermore, the Grades of Recommendation, Assessment, Development and Eva-luation (GRADE) framework [3] was not mentioned or used to assess the certainty of evidence. Implementing the GRADE approach would significantly strengthen the confidence in the findings presented.

Based on a recent NMA with searches extending to 2023 [4], it appears that some relevant trials were omitted from the final inclusion [5-7]. Ensuring a comprehensive inclusion of all relevant trials would provide a more accurate and reliable assessment. Moreover, the study did not discuss its limitations. Recognizing potential limitations, such as the inability to assess the adjunctive potential of anti-CGRP mAbs with other preventive treatments, the predominance of female patients, and the reliance on placebo-controlled trials, is crucial for a balanced interpretation of the findings. The inclusion/exclusion criteria of the RCTs could also bias the results. For instance, the FOCUS trial [8] involved patients with a documented inadequate response to 2 to 4 migraine preventive medication classes, which may affect the generalizability of the results.

The authors claim that this is the first NMA using a Bayesian approach for this patient group, yet there are previously published NMAs employing similar methodologies [9,10]. Correctly identifying and differentiating these studies is important for contextual accuracy. Additionally, these previous NMAs were mistakenly referred to as meta-analyses in the discussion, which could lead to confusion among readers [9,10].

In conclusion, while the study by Haridas et al. [1] contributes valuable insights into the prevention of chronic migraine using anti-CGRP mAbs, addressing these caveats and expanding the scope of outcomes, methodology, and discussion would enhance the overall robustness and applicability of the findings.

Thank you for considering these points. I believe that addressing these issues will significantly contribute to the field of migraine research and improve clinical decision-making.
