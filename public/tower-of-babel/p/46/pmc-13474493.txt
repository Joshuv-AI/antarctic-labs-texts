Effect of prior TB preventive therapy on all-cause mortality during TB treatment among people with HIV/TB in rural eastern Uganda: an observational causal analysis
Authors: Jonathan Izudi, Francis Bajunirwe, Saadick Mugerwa Ssentongo, Saidi Appeli, Adithya Cattamanchi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTuberculosis preventive therapy (TPT) is the cornerstone for preventing TB disease. However, it is uncertain whether prior TPT completion improves survival once TB disease develops in people with human immunodeficiency virus (PWH) while on anti-retroviral therapy (ART). We evaluated the effect of prior TPT completion on all-cause mortality during TB treatment among PWH who developed TB disease while on ART in rural eastern Uganda. Prior TPT completion served as a marker of sustained engagement in HIV care.MethodsWe applied inverse probability of treatment weighting using propensity scores, a causal inference analysis approach, to mimic a randomized controlled trial using real-world retrospective cohort data. Inverse probability of treatment weighting addresses design flaws in observational studies, such as selection and confounding biases, providing less biased causal effect estimates. Individuals who had completed a full course of TPT before the start of the index TB treatment episode formed the intervention group, while the control group comprised those with no history of TPT before the index TB treatment initiation. Inverse probability of treatment weighting ensured balanced baseline covariates between the exposure groups. We estimated all-cause mortality rates using person-time methods and Kaplan-Meier curves and performed propensity-score weighted Cox proportional hazards analysis for cause-effect estimation. We reported adjusted hazard ratios (aHR) with 95% confidence intervals (CI).ResultsOf 719 participants, 296 (41.2%) had completed a full course of TPT before the start of the index TB treatment episode, and 83 (11.5%) had died. The mortality rate was 7.13 per 10,000 person-days, higher among participants without prior TPT than those with prior TPT completion (18.0% vs. 2.4%, log-rank χ²(1) = 35.3, p < 0.001). Median survival was 277 days (95% CI: 213–689). Prior TPT completion was associated with an 87% lower hazard of all-cause mortality (aHR 0.13, 95% CI: 0.06–0.29).ConclusionPrior TPT completion substantially reduced all-cause mortality among PWH who developed drug-susceptible TB disease while on ART. Therefore, there is a need to strengthen TPT completion and engagement in HIV care across HIV programs to lower all-cause mortality among PWH.Keywords: HIV, Mortality, Tuberculosis preventive therapy, Uganda

Background

Approximately one-quarter of the global population is infected with Mycobacterium tuberculosis (latent TB infection, LTBI), with about 10% progressing to TB disease (TB hereafter) [1]. Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) is the major risk factor for TB, and people with HIV (PWH) have a 20-37-fold higher risk of developing TB compared to those without HIV [2–4]. Conversely, TB is the leading cause of hospitalization, mortality, and poor treatment outcomes among PWH [3]. Achieving the global target of ending the TB epidemic by 2030 requires effective treatment of TB and expanded identification and treatment of LTBI [5]. TB preventive treatment (TPT) is the cornerstone for preventing progression from LTBI to TB disease [6] and is recommended for PWH, household contacts of bacteriologically confirmed TB, and other clinical risk groups [7].

Evidence shows that TPT substantially reduces TB incidence and all-cause mortality. By 2023, 4.7 million people had received TPT globally, with studies demonstrating more than a 60% reduction in the risk of TB, depending on regimen and duration [7]. High-burden countries, including Brazil, China, and Ethiopia, report significant reductions in TB incidence and all-cause mortality among PWH receiving TPT [8, 9]. Cohort studies further show that TPT combined with ART can reduce all-cause mortality by 94% and TB incidence by 87% in India [10], with similar benefits observed in children in Guinea-Bissau [11], ART-naïve children with HIV [12], and adults on ART in Côte d’Ivoire, Malawi, and South Africa [13]. A network meta-analysis confirms the strong protective effect of TPT on TB incidence [14]. In Uganda, a high TB/HIV burden country where about 40% of people with TB have HIV [15], national guidelines recommend rifamycin-based 3-month once-weekly rifapentine plus isoniazid (3HP) or 4-month daily rifampicin (4R), and isoniazid-based 6-9-month regimens when rifamycin is not feasible.

Despite existing evidence that TPT prevents TB and reduces mortality, limited evidence exists on whether prior TPT completion provides additional survival benefits once TB develops, particularly among PWH on ART. Most studies have evaluated TPT effectiveness before the onset of TB, leaving uncertainty about outcomes for PWH who develop TB after completing TPT. Therefore, we evaluated the effect of prior TPT completion on all-cause mortality during TB treatment among PWH on ART in rural eastern Uganda. We hypothesized that among PWH who develop TB while on ART, prior completion of TPT is associated with a lower risk of all-cause mortality during TB treatment compared with never having received TPT.

This hypothesis is informed by our theory of change, which posits that prior TPT completion may both reduce the TB disease severity at presentation and be a marker of stronger engagement in HIV care. Greater engagement in HIV care may facilitate earlier TB diagnosis, timely initiation of TB treatment, improved TB treatment adherence, and better clinical recovery, thereby reducing the risk of all-cause mortality during TB treatment (Fig. 1).

Methods

Data source and ethical statement

We retrieved retrospective program data from PWH who developed drug-susceptible TB across 22 TB clinics in 11 districts in the Teso Sub-Region of rural eastern Uganda during 2020 and 2021. In our setting, all persons presumed to have TB undergo GeneXpert testing for rapid detection of TB and rifampicin resistance, with additional drug susceptibility testing performed when indicated to assess isoniazid and other resistance patterns. Individuals with drug resistance are referred for specialized management at a multidrug-resistant TB treatment site. Therefore, the data analyzed pertain to people with drug-susceptible TB. The Uganda National TB and Leprosy Control Program (NTLP) guidelines recommend using any of the following regimens for TPT: [1] daily isoniazid for 6 months (6 H) [2], weekly isoniazid rifapentine for 3 months (3HP) for individuals aged > 2 years; [3] daily HP for 1 month (1HP), or [4] daily isoniazid and rifampicin (RH) for 3 months for individuals aged < 15 years [16]. TPT uptake is variable, ranging from 68% to 94%, with a national average completion rate of 94%. The Eastern region (Teso sub-region) has 89% TPT uptake.

The dataset analyzed included variables on demographics, TB diagnosis, HIV status, ART use, TPT history, and TB treatment outcomes, providing a comprehensive source to emulate a randomized trial for assessing the effect of prior TPT completion on all-cause mortality during TB treatment. The dataset has been described previously [17]. Ethical approval and a waiver of informed consent were obtained from the Mbale Hospital Research and Ethics Committee (approval number: MRRH-2025-558). Confidentiality was maintained by using de-identified, encrypted, and password-protected patient-level data, accessible only to the authorized study team. Administrative clearance to access and retrieve medical records was obtained from the heads of each participating health facility. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study design

Our retrospective cohort study applied causal analysis to mimic a randomized controlled trial using real-world TB program data. Observational data inherently limit the ability to generate unbiased estimates of intervention effectiveness because the absence of randomization introduces systematic bias and confounding. Also, randomization is not always feasible or ethical, particularly when the benefits of an intervention are already well established [18]. The application of advanced causal inference methods, such as inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW), addresses these constraints by weighting the exposure groups based on propensity scores. The approach reduces systematic differences and confounding, thereby creating a strong counterfactual (control group) for cause-and-effect estimation. Eligibility criteria

Eligible participants were PWH on ART who initiated treatment for an incident, drug-susceptible TB episode during the study period (January 2020 to December 2021), had a documented history of TPT use before TB treatment initiation—either completed TPT or never received TPT. We excluded individuals with missing key dates necessary to determine TPT timing or follow-up, and those with missing outcome data. Furthermore, we excluded people with retreatment TB to prevent prevalent user bias; transferred-out individuals, as their mortality status could not be ascertained; and people who were on TPT at the time of the study, including those who had been stopped from using TPT due to adverse effects. Therefore, the cohort reflects PWH who develop TB for the first time while on ART and initiate TB treatment.

Exposure/ intervention categorization

The intervention was prior TPT completion, as a marker of HIV care engagement, before the index TB disease treatment. PWH who developed TB while on ART and who had completed a full course of TPT before the start of the index TB treatment episode formed the intervention group, while the control group comprised those with no history of TPT before TB treatment initiation. Exposure classification was based on documented TPT records from HIV/ART clinic files, TB registers, and electronic medical records. Individuals whose TPT completion date occurred after TB treatment initiation were excluded because this represented exposure misclassification; including them would have introduced immortal-time bias by attributing protected time to the TPT-completed group before they actually completed TPT.

Study outcome

The primary outcome was all-cause mortality during TB treatment, as documented in TB and HIV clinic registers. For time-to-event analyses, mortality was assessed from TB treatment initiation until death or censoring. Secondary outcomes included the standard TB treatment outcomes: cure, treatment completion, treatment failure, loss to follow-up, and death.

Baseline covariates

Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and their potential to confound the relationship between prior TPT completion and all-cause mortality.

Variables measured at or before time zero included: (1) sociodemographic: age, sex, and district of residence; (2) HIV-related: ART status and duration, HIV viral load testing, and the World Health Organization (WHO) clinical stage. Among individuals with viral load data, those with a viral load < 1000 copies/mL were considered suppressed, while those with ≥ 1000 copies/mL were considered non-suppressed; (3) TB-related: laboratory tests done (Xpert, microscopy, urine lipoarabinomannan [LAM], chest X-ray, and others), TB disease category, TB treatment year, and TB treatment cohort; (4) health facility–related: facility level and location. No post-baseline variables were included in the causal analysis to preserve the temporal sequence between the treatment strategies and study outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as frequencies and percentages, and numerical variables as means with standard deviations. Comparisons across TPT status (prior TPT completion vs. no prior TPT) were performed using Chi-square tests when all expected cell counts for categorical variables were ≥ 5; otherwise, Fisher’s exact test was applied. Differences in numerical variables across TPT status were evaluated using two-sided t-tests. We set the statistical significance level at p < 0.05. We calculated the incidence rate of all-cause mortality per 10,000 person-days. Time to all-cause mortality was compared using Kaplan-Meier curves, and differences between groups were assessed using the log-rank test.

To emulate randomization, we used IPTW based on the propensity score for prior TPT completion. Here, a logistic regression model including all baseline covariates estimated the propensity scores. Propensity-score weights were calculated as 1/propensity score for the intervention group and 1/(1–propensity score) for the control group, creating a pseudo-population where the covariates are anticipated to achieve balance. We then assessed covariate balance using absolute standardized mean differences (SMD), with SMD < 0.1 indicating adequate balance, and visualized using a propensity score mirror histogram. A weighted Cox proportional hazards (CPH) model, incorporating propensity score weights, robust variance estimators, and adjusting for clustering at the facility level, was used to estimate the effect of prior TPT completion on all-cause mortality during TB treatment. Weighted adjusted hazard ratios (aHRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were reported. Proportional hazards assumptions were evaluated using Schoenfeld residuals, with a global test p-value > 0.05 indicating no violation.

We performed generalized estimating equations (GEE) with an identity link and exchangeable correlation structure, accounting for clustering at the health-facility level, to estimate the weighted risk difference for all-cause mortality. Propensity score weights were applied, and the adjusted risk difference with corresponding 95% CIs was reported.

Sub-group analysis

We evaluated whether the effect of prior TPT completion on all-cause mortality differed by sex through a subgroup, sex-stratified Mantel-Haenszel analysis. Here, we reported the stratum-specific and pooled odds ratios and assessed cause-effect consistency using a homogeneity test. No imputation was done. All analyses were performed in R version 4.2.1 (2022-06-23 ucrt).

Incidence of mortality and TB treatment outcomes

Table 4 shows that 83 of 719 participants died (11.5%), yielding a mortality rate of 7.13 deaths per 10,000 person-days over 116,485 person-days of follow-up, with a median survival time of 277 days (95% CI: 213–689). Survival differed significantly by prior TPT status, with 76 (18.0%) deaths among 423 participants without prior TPT versus 7 (2.4%) deaths among 296 participants with prior TPT completion.

TB treatment outcomes based on prior TPT status

The log-rank test confirmed a significant difference in survival between the groups (χ²(1) = 35.3, p < 0.001). TB treatment outcomes differed by prior TPT status (Table 4). Participants with prior TPT completion were more likely to be cured (56.4% vs. 41.6%; p < 0.001) and had lower mortality (2.4% vs. 18.0%) and loss to follow-up (2.0% vs. 5.9%) compared to those without prior TPT. TB treatment completion was slightly higher (39.2% vs. 34.0%), and no treatment failures occurred in the prior TPT completion group.

Effect of prior TPT completion on all-cause mortality among PWH who developed TB while on ART in rural eastern Uganda

In the adjusted weighted CPH analysis (Table 5), prior TPT completion was associated with an 87% reduction in the hazard of death (aHR 0.13, 95% CI 0.06–0.29). Schoenfeld residual yielded a global test p-value of 0.152, suggesting no violation. In the weighted GEE model, the estimated risk of all-cause mortality among individuals without prior TPT was 17% (95% CI: 12%–23%). Prior TPT completion was associated with a substantially lower risk of all-cause mortality, corresponding to an absolute risk reduction of 15% points (adjusted risk difference = -0.15; 95% CI: -0.22 to -0.09; p < 0.001). This estimate indicates that individuals with prior TPT completion had an approximate mortality risk of 2%, highlighting a strong protective association between TPT completion and survival.

Sex-stratified effect of prior TPT completion on all-cause mortality among PWH who developed TB while on ART in rural eastern Uganda

In sub-group analysis, sex-stratified odds ratios indicated strong protective effects among females (OR 0.09, 95% CI 0.02–0.31) and males (0.14, 95% CI 0.04–0.41), and the pooled Mantel–Haenszel estimate (OR = 0.12, 95% CI 0.05–0.27) confirmed an overall significant reduction in all-cause mortality. Homogeneity test showed no evidence of effect modification by sex (p = 0.592). Therefore, the mortality-reducing effect of TPT completion was consistent across sex strata.

Discussion

This study showed that individuals with prior TPT completion had a lower risk of all-cause mortality during TB treatment compared with those who never received TPT, underscoring the substantial protective effect of TPT in this high-risk population. This finding is consistent with our hypothesis and theory of change. Accordingly, the observed reduction in all-cause mortality among individuals with prior TPT completion is more plausibly explained by greater engagement in HIV care and earlier access to TB services, which might have facilitated earlier diagnosis, timely treatment initiation, and better adherence during TB treatment. Although our study was not designed to evaluate biological mechanisms, prior TPT completion may plausibly reduce mycobacterial burden, attenuate TB severity at onset, preserve immune function, or lower the risk of advanced or disseminated disease, potentially contributing to improved survival. In the absence of direct measures of TB severity, these mechanisms remain speculative, and the association is appropriately explained by prior TPT completion serving as a marker of sustained engagement in HIV care.

Secondary analyses of TB treatment outcomes further support these findings. Among participants with prior TPT completion, a significantly higher proportion achieved cure or treatment completion, and markedly fewer died. Rates of loss to follow-up were also lower in the prior TPT completion group, and treatment completion was higher. Treatment failure was rare in both groups, with no failures observed among those with prior TPT completion. Overall, these results indicate that prior TPT completion as a marker of care engagement is associated with lower all-cause mortality and better treatment success (cure or treatment completion) among PWH who develop TB while on ART. Similar benefits have been noted in multi-center cohort data from Uganda. For instance, Kazibwe and colleagues reported low TB disease incidence and high TPT completion among PWH on ART [20]. Moreover, in Ethiopia, a study found that TPT completion was associated with over 90% reduction in TB disease incidence compared with those who had never used TPT [21]. These findings are consistent with the rationale that TPT reduces the risk of LTBI progression to TB disease and diminishes the severity of subsequent TB episodes. Indeed, a previous study documented that TPT was associated with lower early mortality among individuals initiating ART [22].

Implications of findings

Our findings underscore the importance of TPT completion and the strengthening of engagement in HIV care in high TB-burden, resource-limited settings. Improved care engagement is associated with better survival and TB treatment outcomes among PWH. Our findings are consistent with Uganda’s national guidelines recommending TPT for all PWH without active TB as part of comprehensive HIV care [15]. Future studies should explore optimal strategies for TPT delivery and adherence support to maximize population-level benefits, particularly given the high uptake and completion rates reported in recent Ugandan programmatic data. Studies on the biologic mechanisms through which prior TPT completion reduces all-cause mortality among PWH/TB, including effects on TB disease severity, immune function, and care engagement, are also needed. Additionally, studies examining whether different TPT regimens or delivery models confer varying levels of protection and evaluating long-term population-level impacts of TPT scale-up on TB disease and TB-related mortality are needed.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has strengths and limitations. To the best of our knowledge, our study is among the first to evaluate the effect of prior TPT completion on all-cause mortality during TB treatment among PWH who have developed TB disease while on ART. The use of IPTW strengthened the causal interpretation of the findings by ensuring that observed covariates were balanced across prior TPT completion status, hence approximating a randomized controlled trial. Limitations include the study’s inability to directly assess differences in TB disease severity at presentation, as data on smear status or Xpert grade were limited. Loss to follow-up was considered a non-informative censoring, and although this is acceptable in TB/HIV cohorts, it might have potentially biased the observed association between prior TPT completion and all-cause mortality. Also, prior TPT completion is not the ideal proxy for engagement in care compared to more direct measures of HIV disease and care engagement, such as viral load suppression, clinic attendance, and CD4 count. However, these variables were not consistently available in the available routine programmatic data. Therefore, our findings should not be interpreted as evidence of a direct biological effect of TPT independent of the stated proxies of care engagement. There is potential residual confounding due to unmeasured confounders, including disclosure of HIV serostatus, CD4 counts, the COVID-19 infection or indirect pandemic effects, comorbidities such as diabetes mellitus, and opportunistic infections, which may influence either TPT completion, mortality, or both. Viral load testing and suppression data were only available for individuals who were eligible for viral load testing, defined as ≥ 6 months after ART initiation and annually thereafter. Although imputation could be useful in some circumstances, we considered it inappropriate for participants who were not yet eligible for viral load testing. As a result, virologic control could not be assessed for the entire cohort, and the subset with available viral load results may not fully represent all study participants. Additionally, we analyzed secondary programmatic data collected for routine clinical care, which did not capture detailed information on prior TPT exposure history. The study could, therefore, not distinguish between individuals who were not offered TPT and those who were offered but declined. Nonetheless, such individuals were classified as having no prior TPT (“not exposed”) in our analysis. Furthermore, our findings are limited by the lack of data on the duration between TPT completion and TB disease onset, a factor that likely influences both biological protection and the observed outcomes. Future studies should incorporate information on time since TPT completion to better define the exposure and strengthen causal interpretation. Lastly, the generalizability of our findings may be limited to rural eastern Uganda or similar programmatic settings and to people with drug-susceptible TB.

Conclusion and recommendation

This study showed that prior TPT completion substantially reduced all-cause mortality among PWH who developed drug-susceptible TB disease while on ART in rural eastern Uganda. Therefore, there is a need to strengthen TPT completion and engagement in HIV care to lower all-cause mortality among PWH.
