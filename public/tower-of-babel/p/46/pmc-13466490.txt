Navigating a fragmented lifeworld: a phenomenological study of schizophrenia among China’s Z Generation
Authors: Ya-Jing Tang, Lei Yang, Wen-Hao Wang, Chen Zhu, Chuan Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe subjective lived experience of schizophrenia among China’s Z Generation (born 1995–2009) remains understudied. This population navigates a unique socio-cultural context, making their personal narratives crucial for developing effective, person-centered mental health interventions.MethodsWe employed a descriptive phenomenological approach, conducting in-depth interviews with four Chinese Z-generation females diagnosed with schizophrenia. A multidisciplinary team analyzed the data to elucidate the essential structure of their lived experience.ResultsThe essential structure of the lived experience of schizophrenia among Chinese Z-generation individuals is “navigating a fragmented lifeworld through dialectical negotiation to reconstruct coherence and agency.” This structure manifested through five interrelated themes: (1) rupture of taken-for-granted reality involving perceptual alterations and loss of control; (2) dialogical encounters with the medical gaze balancing validation and alienation; (3) embodied negotiations with pharmacological treatment mediating bodily alienation and functionality; (4) intersubjective navigation of stigma through strategic disclosure and concealment; and (5) temporal projection through future-oriented life reconstruction. Participants demonstrated remarkable agency as meaning-makers, actively constructing “digital enclaves” for support, employing “cultural polyphony” to integrate diverse explanatory frameworks, and drawing upon Chinese cultural scripts for self-reconstruction.ConclusionsRecovery in schizophrenia should be reconceptualized as actively rebuilding a lifeworld through dialectical negotiation. Clinical practice must evolve from symptom management toward supporting patients’ narrative coherence, while valuing their digital practices and cultural resources. This underscores the need for culturally sensitive, responsive, and collaborative mental health support systems.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12888-026-08223-6.

Introduction

Schizophrenia research has traditionally emphasized medical, neurobiological, and cognitive perspectives, focusing on observable symptoms and deficits. While these approaches have advanced explanation of the mechanism of the disorder, schizophrenia to the person who experience it is first and foremost a lived experience [1]. The subjective dimension, how individuals make sense of their changing realities and integrate diagnostic labels into their identities, is essential for developing person-centered care [2]. A resurgence of humanistic inquiry, alongside global destigmatization movements, has renewed focus on first-person perspectives in psychosis [3, 4].

Phenomenology provides a powerful framework for this work. Its commitment to “returning to the things themselves” prioritizes phenomena as they are lived, prior to theoretical interpretation [5]. In psychopathology, this approach, pioneered by Jaspers, emphasizes empathic understanding (Verstehen) over purely causal explanation (Erklären) [6]. Contemporary theorists like Stanghellini advance “second-order empathy,” which involves suspending the natural attitude to reconstruct the other’s world [7]. Empirically, researchers such as Sass and Parnas identify self-disorder as a core gestalt of schizophrenia, manifesting as a weakened first-person perspective, hyper-reflexivity, and diminished presence [8, 9]. Importantly, individuals are not passive recipients of these disturbances. They actively negotiate the meaning of their symptoms through position-taking [2, 10].

This agency is evident in phenomena like delusions. Beyond fixed false beliefs [11], delusions reflect attempts at world-making, often arising amid profound alterations in reality, such as feelings of hypo-reality or hyper-reality [12]. Individuals frequently navigate these through double bookkeeping, maintaining awareness of both delusional and everyday realities [12]. First-person accounts further reveal delusions as involving a radical reorganization of the lifeworld and a drive for meaning, not mere dysfunction [13].

However, this understanding remains culturally limited. Most phenomenological understanding derive from Western, individualistic conceptualization of self. The experiences of individuals from cultures with a constitutive emphasis on relational ontologies, such as China’s family-based ethos, remain underexplored. Existing cross-cultural work, though preliminary, suggests that both the applicability of phenomenology and the significant influence of culture should be considered in shaping how mental illness is understood and addressed [14, 15].

China’s Z Generation (born 1995–2009) inhabits a distinctive socio-cultural space. As digital natives maturing during rapid economic growth and global integration, they negotiate tensions between collectivist traditions and individualistic aspirations [16, 17]. Their identities form under significant academic and social pressures, within evolving family dynamics and a complex digital environment. Although mental health awareness is rising, cultural beliefs linking psychological suffering to personal failure or familial shame persist, influencing help-seeking amid filial obligations and a still-stigmatized mental health system [18].

Despite these salient factors, the lived experience of schizophrenia among China’s Z Generation is virtually absent from the literature. Prevailing studies in China favor quantitative and clinical approaches, which cannot capture how young people interpret and find meaning within their specific cultural and developmental contexts [19].

This study addresses this critical gap through a descriptive phenomenological investigation, asking: What is the essential structure of the lived experience of schizophrenia for Z Generation individuals in China?

Methods

Research design and philosophy

This study employs a descriptive phenomenological approach based on Amedeo Giorgi’s framework [20]. The researchers adopted an attitude of epoché, consciously bracketing preconceived theories, clinical assumptions, and normative judgments regarding schizophrenia [21].

Participant selection and recruitment

Participants were recruited via purposive homogenous sampling according to the following criteria: (a) aged 18–30 (Z Generation); (b) DSM-5 diagnosis of schizophrenia; (c) capacity to provide informed consent; (d) sufficient cognitive and verbal ability to describe subjective experiences. Participants will be excluded if they have: (a) significant comorbid physical illness; (b) organic brain disorders or intellectual disability; (c) inability to consent; (d) acute psychotic state posing risk to self or others.

Participants had no prior relationship with the interviewer. Recruitment was conducted through two channels: referrals from psychiatrists, and an advertisement on a public WeChat platform dedicated to mental health awareness. All recruitment materials directed interested individuals to contact the research team directly, thereby ensuring voluntary participation.

In keeping with the descriptive phenomenological tradition, sampling prioritized depth over breadth. Accordingly, we recruited a small number of participants able to provide detailed experiential accounts. Recruitment ceased once the research team determined that the information richness was sufficient to illuminate the essential structure of the phenomenon under investigation [22].

Data collection

All participants completed a detailed demographic questionnaire to capture key sociodemographic and clinical characteristics.

In-depth, semi-structured interviews were conducted using a narrative approach. Each began with the open-ended prompt: “Today, I’d like to get to know you as a person. Could you tell me your life story? You may start wherever you wish—childhood or any period—and guide me through significant life events up to the present.” A comprehensive description of the interview methodology, encompassing the rationale for the narrative format and the interview prompts, is available in the supplementary materials.

To provide clinical context for our findings, the Positive and Negative Syndrome Scale (PANSS) [23] was administered to participants who consented.

The primary researcher, a former psychiatrist now practicing phenomenological-oriented therapy, conducted all interviews in Mandarin (60–180 min over 1–3 sessions). Interviews were audio-recorded with participant consent and transcribed verbatim for analysis.

Ethical considerations

The study was approved by the ethics committee of Peking University Sixth Hospital [2025-62]. All participants provided written informed consent.

Data analysis

Data analysis followed Amedeo Giorgi’s descriptive phenomenological psychological method [20]. A detailed description of our analysis procedures can be seen in the supplementary materials.

Description of the lived experience

The essential structure of participants’ experiences was formulated as “Navigating a Fragmented Lifeworld: The Dialectical Struggle for Coherence and Agency.” This captures the ongoing effort to integrate the disruptions of psychosis and medicalized identity into a viable life narrative, marked by tensions between rupture and reconciliation, alienation and agency. Five constituents comprise this structure, as shown in Fig. 1.

Rupture of the taken-for-granted reality

This constituent describes the profound disruption of the natural attitude and the initial emotional and strategic responses to a fractured reality.

Disintegration of reality and perceptual alterations

Participants reported a fundamental loss of reality, where the world became alien or hyper-real.

The Dialogical encounter with the medical gaze: between alienation and validation

The diagnostic process was a profound encounter, characterized by tension between validation of suffering and alienation through simplification.

The Violence of Simplification

The medical gaze often reduced complex lived experiences to standardized symptoms, creating moments of profound alienation.When I reported… ‘I don’t hear voices anymore,’ the doctor… looked like he didn’t believe me. (P1)That doctor was quite rushed, it seemed like less than five minutes… He said, ‘Look, she has very, very many delusions’. (P2)

The embodied negotiation of treatment: reconstructing the self amidst the corporeal fissure

Pharmacological treatment involved negotiating the fissure between the objectified body (Körper) and the lived body (Leib).

Experiential alienation: the pharmakon as both remedy and poison

Medication created a split, with therapeutic effects feeling external and undermining agency.It feels like those [mental states] aren’t produced by myself—it’s like an alien self, an alienated self. The effect comes from outside… it’s imposed from the outside, and you lose that sense of agency and empowerment. (P1)

It feels like those [mental states] aren’t produced by myself—it’s like an alien self, an alienated self. The effect comes from outside… it’s imposed from the outside, and you lose that sense of agency and empowerment. (P1)

P3: Described how medication made her creative drive diminish, noting “the ‘writing inspiration’ is mostly gone, feeling there’s ‘nothing much to write’.” She experienced this as a significant “sense of loss,” where a world once felt as “very interesting” and “full of challenge” became “somewhat impatient” and “perfectly normal.”

Pragmatic compromise: surrendering the lived body for functionality

Despite profound alienation, participants recognized the biological necessity of medication, leading to a conscious decision to subordinate the lived body’s discomfort to the managed body’s requirement for stability.Rehabilitation should be drug-based. Taking the smallest maintenance dose for life is the best option… If the side effects are too severe, you discuss them with your doctor to adjust or reduce them, but medication is the foundation. (P1)

Rehabilitation should be drug-based. Taking the smallest maintenance dose for life is the best option… If the side effects are too severe, you discuss them with your doctor to adjust or reduce them, but medication is the foundation. (P1)

P3 acknowledged the paradoxical need for chemical intervention despite its costs, depicting her relationship with medication through a vivid metaphor:

I’m like a rabbit hopping back and forth—on one side refusing medication, on the other chasing after the doctor pleading for pills. (P3)

Micro-agency in lived experience: recalibrating the chemical self

Within the constraints of their treatment regimens, participants exercised subtle, embodied agency by adjusting dosage based on direct feedback from their lived body, striving to preserve a satisfactory quality of life.

P3 engaged in micro-adjustments to balance efficacy against preserving vitality: she “surreptitiously” reduced her dose based on her subjective sense of appetite, energy, and the unwanted sedation that made sleep feel like being “knocked out” and left her “drowsy.”

P2 demonstrated agency in interpreting ambiguous bodily signs. Faced with “tongue-tying” that was diagnostically uncertain (tardive dyskinesia vs. anxiety), she actively chose the explanation that was less threatening to her self-concept.

Participants maintained a clear distinction between pharmacological management of their Körper and psychological work required of their Leib, viewing medication as a tool that enables, rather than replaces, their own agential struggle.

I realize that medication is just an aid; ultimately, I still need to face the delusions and problems myself. If I keep avoiding the fantasies and don’t confront them, they will chase after me relentlessly. (P2)

I realize that medication is just an aid; ultimately, I still need to face the delusions and problems myself. If I keep avoiding the fantasies and don’t confront them, they will chase after me relentlessly. (P2)

P4 exemplified strategic alignment, where strict adherence was a conscious choice to enable broader life goals, viewing medication as the foundation for achieving “cognitive clarity” and improved “social cognition.”

Intersubjective navigation of stigma and authenticity

Stigma manifested as both external exclusion and internalized shame, while authenticity represented the struggle to live in accordance with one’s inner experience. This fundamental tension defined participants’ social worlds, compelling continuous intersubjective negotiation.

The dual structure of stigma

Stigma operated through both social structures and intimate relationships.I think it’s just human nature—where there are differences, there will be discrimination and prejudice. After all, people with psychiatric disorders are a minority. If the majority were ‘mentally ill,’ then it might be the so-called normal people who are discriminated against. There’s also an instinctual element: psychiatric disorders do carry certain risks, and avoiding harm is a basic human instinct. And then there’s culture and history—it’s become a social convention. For example, the term ‘psycho’ has turned into an insult. (P1)

There’s also an instinctual element: psychiatric disorders do carry certain risks, and avoiding harm is a basic human instinct. And then there’s culture and history—it’s become a social convention. P2 experienced familial stigma, where her parents forbade external disclosure while privately suggesting she was “faking her illness,” leading to self-harm behaviors (such as deliberately staying up late to induce fever, or hitting her head against the wall) as physical proof of suffering that could not be ignored.

P3 expressed deep anxiety about the diagnostic label itself, while also viewing her experience through a gendered lens:Fortunately, though this might sound discriminatory, I feel somewhat lucky to be a woman with schizophrenia, especially in intimate relationships… As a woman, if I were to date a man with schizophrenia, knowing his condition, I would be absolutely too afraid. Furthermore, in the common relationship model where men are expected to be the primary supporters—a model I personally prefer—how could one rely on a man with schizophrenia? This doubt extends to myself: I don’t even feel entirely reliable, so how could I perceive them as reliable? The illness brings significant challenges to one’s economic and emotional capacity to fulfill these roles. (P3)

P3’s narrative revealed not a singular or stable attitude toward gender, but rather a field of internal tensions—where empathy for male counterparts coexisted with fear, traditional expectations intertwined with self-awareness, and the stigma of illness became entangled with culturally prescribed notions of masculinity and femininity. She prefaced her statements with reflexive awareness (“though this might sound discriminatory”), recognizing that her own attitudes might participate in the very stigmatization she experiences as a patient. Her doubts about male patients—framed in terms of physical safety and economic reliability—were immediately turned inward: “I don’t even feel entirely reliable.” In a subsequent interview, P3 deepened this self-examination, tracing her attitudes to cultural sources: a society that denies men space for vulnerability, and her own “cunning” deployment of both feminist and traditional discourses.

The desire for genuine self-expression existed in constant tension with the fear of rejection.

Strategies for safe expression

Participants developed sophisticated approaches to self-disclosure, balancing authenticity with protection.

P1 practiced pre-commitment “informed consent” in potential relationships: she deliberately informed partners about the condition, its symptoms, treatment, and legal implications before considering marriage.

P2 found expression through her participation in writing therapy groups and online communities where she used a pseudonym, creating what she called “a place to belong” without fear of misunderstanding.

P3 employed cautious, strategic disclosure in her relationship, sharing only the less-stigmatized term “thought-perception disorder” and carefully selecting “a few relatively safe ideas,” fearing that full disclosure might cause her partner to leave.

The Performance of Normalcy

Some participants adopted strategic assimilation, performing socially valued identities while managing authentic selves.After careful consideration, if a low dose of medication can help me return to a stable state—even if it means losing some of that magical peak experience—I believe this small loss is necessary, a choice I must make in order to immerse myself in the real life I so deeply love. (P3)

P4 employed academic achievement and strict treatment adherence as protective shields, stating proudly: “others can barely tell I have a problem.”

Temporal projection: rebuilding a future from a fractured past

Participants engaged in sustained, future-oriented projects to rebuild meaning and identity following psychotic rupture.

Seeking refuge through creative expression

Creative practice provided an essential space for authentic self-expression.

P2 found that writing provided her “a place to belong” where she “didn’t have to worry about being misunderstood.”

P3 immersed herself in phenomenological texts and kept recording, viewing them as self-help strategies.

Transformation through professional identity

Channeling personal struggle into professional aspiration converted stigmatized experience into expertise.

P4 was “actively preparing for postgraduate entrance exams” and aimed to “research schizophrenia in the future to bring well-being to other patients.”

P1 transformed personal experience into communal support through digital leadership.

Construction of personally meaningful frameworks

Participants employed intellectual and creative strategies to construct manageable frameworks.

P3 constructed the “panda-person” metaphor to carve a place for her uniqueness. This metaphor originated from a diary entry documenting her visit to a wildlife zoo, in which she deliberately adopted the panda’s perspective to enhance the literary quality of the narrative. It was on the day she “could not help but recognize the world anew” that this metaphor struck her as a moment of revelation, offering insight into her own identity and capturing the uniqueness she felt.I began convincing myself—in a somewhat tender, desperate way—that it’s okay to be different. To hell with solipsism—everyone has something to teach me. (P3)

Discussion

This study reveals that the lived experience of schizophrenia among Chinese Gen Z constitutes a fundamental ontological transformation. Its core structure involves reconstructing agency and meaning through dialectical negotiation within a lifeworld fragmented by the rupture of the taken-for-granted reality. These findings enrich phenomenological understanding while highlighting culturally and generationally specific nuances.

The essential structure of schizophrenic experience: dialectical reconstruction of the lifeworld

This study resonates with classical phenomenological psychopathology, particularly the self-disorders model, while substantially extending its theoretical scope. Participants’ narratives provide vivid empirical corroboration: P1’s loss of thought ownership, temporal disorientation, and permeable self-boundaries align with core Examination of Anomalous Self-Experience (EASE) items [24], while P3’s incessant self-world cognizing exemplifies hyper-reflexivity [9].

Beyond validation, our findings reveal patients as active meaning-makers engaged in position-taking, a dialectical process of negotiating contradictory experiences across medical, pharmacological, and social domains [10]. For instance, participants demonstrated both reliance on and alienation from medical authority, both desiring the stabilizing effects of medication and resisting the alienation it induces. This supports adopting a person-centered dialectical perspective, viewing patients as autonomous subjects shaping symptom meanings [2].

At the ontological level, we posit that the onset of the illness, as a compelling, passive affliction, first and foremost brings about a rupture of the intentional arc, which echoes the view of Fuchs [25]. This rupture disrupts the natural flow of an individual’s motivation, distorts their agency, and ultimately leads to a distortion in their fundamental mode of interacting with the world. However, following the breakdown of the self-world unity, patients do not descend into pure passivity or existential nothingness. On the contrary, they tenaciously engage in the dialectical reconstruction of the lifeworld. Herein, temporal projection—the rebuilding of future-directed intentional capacity—emerges as the fundamental force underpinning all dialectical negotiations.

Theoretical contribution lies in shifting from static self-disorder toward a dynamic dialectical reconstruction of the lifeworld model. Where intentionality arc rupture reveals the core genetic mechanism, position-taking and temporal projection illuminate agential recovery processes. Accordingly, we reconceptualize recovery not as symptom removal, but as future-oriented meaning and agency reconstruction through continuous dialectical negotiation.

The pluricultural ecology of schizophrenia among China’s Z generation

China’s Z Generation manifests schizophrenia through distinctive cultural, institutional, and generational imprints. As true digital natives, they demonstrate cultural polyphony—actively weaving biomedicine, phenomenological philosophy, and personal metaphors into coherent explanatory systems [26]. This represents agentic curation of interpretive communities, exemplified by P3 integrating diagnosis, self-disorder theory, and her “panda-person” narrative while maintaining interpretive sovereignty.

The family constitutes primordial aidagara [14]—a relational foundation exhibiting dual nature: as protective aidagara (P1’s parental support; P3’s maternal trust) and oppressive aidagara (P2’s stigmatization). Patients navigate this dialectical field by forging “in-between” spaces that preserve connection while permitting authentic expression.

Gender constitutes one vital dimension within the pluricultural ecology, shaping how patients are perceived and how they perceive themselves and potential partners. P3’s narrative epitomizes the complexity of contemporary Chinese gender attitudes, which scholars characterize as a “coexistence of public modernity and private traditionality” [27]—where egalitarian ideals (gender equality) coexist with persistent traditional expectations (“men should be the breadwinners while women should manage the household”) in intimate relationships. Her stance reflects a broader Z Generation dynamic: gender, like other dimensions of experience, is continuously negotiated where tradition and modernity collide, stigma and empathy entangle, and inherited scripts are transformed without being fully escaped.

Digital platforms function as enclaves for identity work beyond stigma: P1’s patient group enables “project-based companionship,” while P2’s anonymous writing facilitates narrative reconstruction. These Z Generation patients emerge as meaning-weavers across pluralistic ecologies and connection-builders in digital spaces.

Their experiences compel rethinking madness and recovery paradigms. Building culturally responsive mental health systems requires engaging reflexive individuals navigating complexly across lived experience, digital worlds, and personal meaning.

Rethinking clinical insight: from compliance to meaning-making integrity

Our findings challenge the conventional, static conceptualization of clinical insight—often narrowly defined within a biomedical framework as the “awareness of having a mental illness” [28]. Participants demonstrated explanatory flexibility, weaving together biomedical explanations, psychological frameworks, and personal philosophies. This aligns with the phenomenological concept of double bookkeeping, where a person may rationally acknowledge a symptom as a hallucination while simultaneously living within its reality [29]. Such phenomena suggest insight is not a simple binary but a dynamic negotiation between different modes of knowing.

We propose reconceptualizing insight not as a fixed ability, but as the dynamic process of achieving coherent personal understanding. The clinical question shifts from “Does the patient accept the diagnosis?” to “How is the patient making sense of their situation?” P4’s case exemplifies that pragmatic treatment acceptance can coexist with divergent personal understanding—so-called “poor insight” may represent rational disagreement with reductionist models or a protective stance against stigmatizing identity [28].

The clinical imperative is to support patients’ journey toward “narrative integrity”—helping integrate disparate experiences into a life story that feels continuous and meaningful, whether or not it aligns perfectly with disease models. This approach aligns with Davidson’s emphasis on agency and narrative reconstruction in recovery processes [30]. The CHIME framework—identifying Connectedness, Hope, Identity, Meaning, and Empowerment as key recovery processes [31]—provides a useful lens through which to view our participants’ struggles. P3’s ‘panda-person’ metaphor can be understood as an effort to reconstruct a positive identity; P2’s anonymous writing community reflects the pursuit of Connectedness; and all participants’ future-oriented projects (P4’s postgraduate aspirations, P1’s digital leadership) embody Hope and Meaning-making.

We recommend clinicians: (1) recognize multiple forms of understanding; (2) value narrative over checklist assessment; (3) support integrative meaning-making; and (4) adopt supported decision-making to enhance autonomy consistent with patients’ values [32].

Limitations and future directions

Consistent with descriptive phenomenology, this study does not seek statistical generalizability but transferability—the resonance of its findings with others in similar contexts. Nonetheless, the sample’s homogeneity (all female, digitally literate, WeChat-recruited) limits insight into how the essential structure may vary across gender, digital access, or help-seeking pathways. As P3’s narrative underscores—explicitly naming gender differences in stigma and partnership expectations—future research must include male participants to examine how gender, within China’s distinct socio-cultural context, shapes the experience of schizophrenia.

Additionally, this study did not formally involve individuals with lived experience of psychosis in its design or interpretation. While our phenomenological approach centers participants’ voices as the primary source of knowledge, participatory models—engaging those with lived experience as co-researchers—could further enhance the relevance and real-world impact of findings. Future research would benefit from such collaboration.
