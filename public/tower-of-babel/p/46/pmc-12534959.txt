Risk factors of COVID-19 breakthrough infection: a case-control test-negative design study
Authors: Gabriela Tejada-Panduro, Laia Pinós-Tella, Susana Otero-Romero, Blanca Borras-Bermejo, Ingrid Carbones, Andrés Antón, Cristina Andrés, Oleguer Parés-Badell
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe aimed to identify risk factors for breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection after at least one dose of any COVID-19 vaccine.MethodsThis is a case-control test-negative design study in hospitalized patients with a severe acute respiratory infection, at least one dose of any COVID-19 vaccine and a PCR-test for SARS-CoV-2/COVID-19 performed between 1 November 2021 and 28 June 2022. Cases and controls were matched by age and date of test. A logistic regression model with adjusted odds ratios was performed to determine factors related to breakthrough infection.ResultsWe analyzed 1,340 breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infections matched with 4,016 controls. After adjustment, the odds of a breakthrough infection were 64% higher in patients whose last vaccine dose was administered more than 6 months before hospitalization compared to those vaccinated within the previous 6 months (aOR 1.64; CI 95% 1.42–1.88). Moreover, immunosuppression was statistically associated with breakthrough infection (aOR 2.04; CI 95%, 1.58–2.62).DiscussionThe probability of breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection was significantly higher in hospitalized patients who had received their last vaccine dose more than six months before hospitalization; while immunosuppression was the most important risk factor for breakthrough infection.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12879-025-11773-x.Keywords: Risk factors, COVID-19, Breakthrough infection, Vaccines

Background

Coronavirus disease (COVID-19) continues to cause important morbidity and has affected more that 640 million people and resulted in 6.6 million deaths across almost all countries [1]. In order to reduce the impact of COVID-19, vaccines against the severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS-CoV-2) were developed and administered with an unprecedented speed. By October 2022, 63.4% of the world population and 85% of the Spanish population had been fully vaccinated [2, 3].

At the time of this study, three main categories of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines were available in Spain: Protein Subunit (e.g. Novavax), Viral Vector (e.g. AstraZeneca, Janssen) and RNA based vaccines (e.g. Pfizer/BioNTech, Moderna) [4–8]. Randomized trials have demonstrated high vaccine efficacies (> 94%) for the mRNA-based vaccines against COVID-19 [7, 8]. Regarding non-mRNA vaccines, efficacies between 64.3% and 66.1% have been demonstrated [5, 9]. In addition, other studies have demonstrated mRNA-vaccines high effectiveness (around 90%) among different patient populations against severe disease, hospitalization rates and mortality [10–13].

However, the emergence of SARS-CoV-2 variants of concern has resulted in an increased transmissibility, disease severity, and even lower immune response following vaccination against COVID-19 [12]. During a 6-month follow-up of the BNT162b2 mRNA COVID-19 vaccine clinical trial, vaccine efficacy gradually declined from 96.2% 7 days after the second dose, to 83.7% four months later [14]. In addition, a review concluded that 6 months after vaccination there was a drop in efficacy for severe disease of a mean of 9.5–10% [15].

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) defines breakthrough infections as a positive COVID-19 test after 14 days of the recommended vaccination regimen [16]. These infections remain a serious threat to global efforts of easing the pandemic impact, as they may contribute substantially to the onward transmission [17].

A recent systematic review gathered different studies describing characteristics of breakthrough infections and associated risk factors, showed a 0.6% mortality in the 33 papers reviewed. Breakthrough infections were more frequent in healthcare workers and immunodeficient individuals [18]. Nonetheless, they concluded that disease progression in high-risk individuals and the role of combining different types of vaccines should be further explored. Studies related to risk factors of breakthrough infection have reported conflicting findings. Whereas some studies have reported a higher risk of breakthrough infection among individuals with comorbidities [19, 20], other large-scale studies have found no associations with pre-existing conditions [21]. In addition, most of the studies have been limited to specific subgroups [22, 23] or focused on severe outcomes [24–26] and important characteristics such as socioeconomic conditions have been understudied [21]. Hospitalizations due to COVID-19 continue to be a major public health problem, particularly among adults 65 years and older, a group that comprised two‐thirds of hospitalizations among SARS‐CoV‐2–positive persons in a recent study [27].

Therefore, we aimed to describe the risk factors associated with breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infections among our hospitalized population, comprising comorbidities, vaccine-related characteristics and demographic variables including socioeconomic status.

Methods

Study design and participants

This is a case-control test-negative design study, including patients over 18 years-old who were hospitalized at Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron (HUVH), a tertiary public hospital at Barcelona, Spain, and had a SARS-CoV-2 test (RT-PCR) performed from 1 November 2021 to 28 June 2022. We included patients who had received at least one dose of the SARS-CoV-2 vaccine, with a minimum of 14 days since the last administered dose; and who were hospitalized due to an episode of severe acute respiratory infection (SARI). SARI was defined using International Classification of Diseases 10th revision (ICD-10) diagnostic codes, adapting the definition used by the Infection Surveillance Information System of Catalonia. We included patients with a diagnostic code related to respiratory symptoms (R06.0, J80, R06.89, R06.9), acute respiratory infections (J00-J06, J09-J18, J20-J22), bronchitis (J40-J42), respiratory failure (J96) or pneumonia confirmed by SARS-CoV-2 (J12.82, U07.1). The only exclusion criteria was a prior hospitalization episode with a positive SARS-CoV-2 PCR-test during the study period. We defined a breakthrough infection as a positive SARS-CoV-2 test in a vaccinated patient with at least one vaccine dose. During the study period, SARS-CoV-2 RT-PCR testing was systematically performed in all patients admitted with SARI symptoms.

Cases were all hospitalized patients with a positive SARS-CoV-2 test. We used exact matching to select controls, defined as a hospitalized patient with a negative RT-PCR. We used the nearest neighbor method to select controls. The cases and controls were matched by age (+/- 3 years) and date of test (+/- 7 days) with a ratio of 1:3 (3 controls for each case), to achieve a proper balance of the final matching specification. In patients who had more than one positive test in the period described, we only included the first episode of COVID-19 infection (the first positive SARS-CoV-2 test for each individual).

Variables

The primary outcome was having a hospitalization for a breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection. We aimed to determine the association with different exposures that were separated in three categories: sociodemographic characteristics, vaccine-related variables and comorbidities. In the first group we included age, sex, and the socioeconomic status. To obtain the socioeconomic status we used the territorial socioeconomic index (IST, for the initials in Catalan) which is designed by the Catalonian Statistics Institute (Idescat) taking into account four indicators: employment status, education, income and immigration. The index is calculated ecologically using the data for all the residents in Catalonia. It is a relative index without units where 100 is the reference value for Catalonia and a relative value is obtained for every basic health area (the territorial framework for the primary health care). We assigned an IST to each patient assigning the value corresponding to the basic health area where they reside. We present the socioeconomic status in five categories, corresponding to the quintile distribution of the cases and controls, where the first quintile includes IST from 58 to 89 (most deprived), second quintile (90–95), third quintile (96–102), fourth quintile (103–110) and fifth quintile includes IST values over 111 (least deprived).

Regarding vaccination data, we only considered vaccine doses administered at least 14 days prior to the date of the RT-PCR test to ensure adequate time for immune response. We categorized the number of vaccine doses and the time since the last vaccine dose (more or less than 6 months) in one variable. We also included the brand of each dose received. Comorbidities used for the analysis were lung disease, asthma, heart disease, hypertension, renal disease, liver disease, immunosuppression and cancer; all presented as dichotomic variables. Definition and ICD-10 codes for each comorbidity are available on Appendix 1. The variable chronic disease takes into account only the listed comorbidities. We included data regarding deaths and admission to an intensive care unit (ICU) and we also calculated length of stay, using admission date and discharge date. For cases, viral lineage was obtained from the microbiology database when available. It was distributed in categories according to lineage description and the equivalent variant of concern.

Data sources/measurements

We used Electronic Health Records from the Catalonian Health Department to obtain standardized data. Demographic data, chronic comorbidities and PCR-tests data were obtained automatically from the Minimum Basic Data Set (MBDS) of the HUVH Information System Department. Comorbidities were identified using ICD-10 codes. For the SARS-CoV-2 vaccination status we used the Electronic Health Records, in which we included date of administration and brand of each dose. The Catalonian Statistics Institute (Idescat) provided the IST corresponding to each basic health area.

For the data regarding SARS-CoV-2 lineage, we requested HUVH Microbiology Department for the SARS-CoV-2 variants database, which included the Pangolin lineage detected in each case.

Study size

When calculating the minimum sample size for an alpha risk of 0.05 and a beta risk lower than 0.2, we needed 594 cases and 1782 controls to detect at least an odds ratio of 1.5. We included all patients admitted at the HUVH from 1 November 2021 to 28 June 2022 with a hospitalization episode of acute respiratory infection, a PCR-test performed and at least one dose of any SARS-CoV-2 vaccine. During the study period, there were 22,903 hospitalization episodes with SARI criteria, of which 1,340 were test-positive cases. 4,016 controls were selected by matching.

Statistical methods

Firstly, we conducted a descriptive analysis including demographic information, vaccine-related variables, clinical characteristics and comorbidities, which were reported for cases and controls. We also evaluated the differences in each variable between these groups by using the chi-squared, Fisher’s exact test, Mann–Whitney U test or Wilcoxon test as appropriate for each variable.

We performed a univariate analysis to establish the correlation between the exposure variables and the outcome, obtaining the crude odds ratio (OR), confidence interval at the 95% significance level and p-value. To build the final model, we took into account the available literature, the results of the univariate analysis and we used diagram acyclic graphics (DAGs) built on “Daggity” webpage (Appendix 2). We performed multivariable logistic regression to obtain adjusted odds ratio (aOR) modelling the relationship between COVID-19 breakthrough infection and the following variables as exposures: sex, age, socioeconomic status, time since last dose, lung disease, hypertension, renal disease and immunosuppression. All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio software.

Ethical Statement

Databases were developed according to national and international standards of ethical practices (Declaration of Helsinki and Tokyo), and data were used in accordance with the regulations in force regarding the protection of personal data (EU) 2016/679; 27 April of 2016 (GDPR). The need of consent to participate was waived by the Human Research Ethics Committee of the Hospital Universitari Vall d’Hebron, stated in the approval document with code: EOM(AG)041/2022(6030).

Results

Among hospitalized patients that had received at least one dose of any SARS-CoV-2 vaccine and had been PCR-tested between November 1, 2021 and June 28, 2022 there were 1,340 breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infections and we identified 4,016 controls by matching (Fig. 1).

Demographic and clinical characteristics of the cases and controls are summarized in Table 1. Cases and controls had a similar distribution across socioeconomic status quintiles, with a higher proportion of cases in the most deprived quintile (Q1: 19.7% versus 18.5%) and a lower proportion in the least deprived quintile (Q5: 18.2% versus 20.6%). Cases had a longer length of stay in days than controls (median 7 with an IQR 3 – 13 vs median 4 with an IQR 2 – 9). 71% of the cases had a background of at least one chronic disease compared to 69% of the controls [p = 0.08]. We found a significantly higher proportion of cases with lung disease (24% vs 20%), renal disease (26% vs 21%) and immunosuppression (9.8% vs 5.2%). 10% of the cases died during the hospitalization and 11% were admitted to ICU.

Table 3 shows the association between breakthrough infection and different risk factors using crude and adjusted OR estimates. Based on crude OR estimates, the odds of a breakthrough infection in men were 1.15 times higher than in women (CI 95%, 1.02-1.31 [p = 0.02]). We found a tendency showing that low socioeconomic status may relate to breakthrough infections, however, it was not statistically significant (CI 95%, 0.98-1.47 [p= 0.06]). In the multivariate analysis, we found a non-statistically significant association between the most deprived IST category and breakthrough infection (aOR 1.19; CI 95% 0.97-1.46, [p = 0.08]). The association between time since last vaccine dose and breakthrough infection remained consistent with the univariate results (aOR 1.64; CI 95% 1.42-1.88 [p <0.01]). Regarding comorbidities, only immunosuppression remained statistically associated with breakthrough infection (aOR 2.04; CI 95%, 1.58-2.62 [p <0.01]).

Discussion

In this large, case-control test-negative study, we found a significantly higher probability of breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection when the last vaccine dose had been received more than 6 months before hospitalization; while patients with immunosuppression had almost two-times higher odds of breakthrough infection. These results reinforce the need for timely booster vaccinations to effectively protect immunosuppressed individuals against severe manifestations of COVID-19.

This study has several strengths. On the one hand, we used a test-negative design, which ensures that cases and controls are selected from the same theoretical population. During the study period, all hospitalized patients had a PCR-test performed, unless they proceeded from another institution. All the clinical variables included in our analysis were extracted from automatic sources which means we had no missing information. However, this method of data extraction limited our ability to clearly determine whether outcomes such as death and ICU admission were exclusively attributable to COVID-19 or to other underlying conditions. We used well-established and reliable data sources such as MBDS. To minimize the confounding bias, we matched the cases and controls by characteristics that affect the exposure and the outcome (age and date of test). We matched by age because we expected a strong cohort effect since the Spanish population received SARS-CoV-2 vaccines according to age brackets and high-risk conditions prioritized by the national vaccine strategy [29]. We decided to match by date of test to avoid confounding due to circulating SARS-CoV-2 variant (with variable immune scape) or changes in COVID-19 incidence. As our timeline includes the last months of 2021 and first semester of 2022, predominant variants of SARS-CoV-2 were changing, and each one had its own particular characteristics in terms of infectiousness and transmissibility. After January 2022, the Omicron variant represented over 80% of all the COVID-19 infections in Europe, and from March it represented 99% of the variants detected [30]. This correlates with our results where 92% of the genomic variants detected belonged to Omicron. We used an ecological variable (IST) to describe socioeconomic characteristics, therefore, we may have lost individual variability in the socioeconomic status. However, we still consider this as a strength because of the insufficient incorporation of health determinants into clinical research. Moreover, this variable was built from three different data sources, making it more complex and reliable.

Our study also has some limitations. First, we have been able to describe risk factors for breakthrough infections that end up with hospitalization, therefore our results may not be representative of milder infections. Second, even if we excluded all patients with a previous hospitalization with positive PCR-test during the study period, we were not able to take into account previous SARS-CoV-2 infections. Some studies have reported that a prior infection may have a stronger role on the vulnerability of having a breakthrough infection than vaccination [31–33]. Third, even though we included death and admission to ICU as proxy variables for severity of illness in the descriptive analysis, we were not able to estimate the severity of SARS-COV-2 infection as the patients might also be affected by the severity of other disorders. In addition, we were not able to collect data on all the diagnostic codes at admission; therefore, we were unable to determine whether the hospitalization may be attributed to the SARI or another concomitant cause. Fourth, there are multiple causal pathways that can influence the outcome of a SARS-CoV-2 breakthrough infection, including vaccine failure, progression to severe disease, and factors related to increased susceptibility and exposure to the virus. This is particularly relevant in immunosuppressed patients, who exhibit a waning immune response to the COVID-19 vaccine which appears to occur at a more rapid rate [34]. Finally, even though we matched our cases and controls to avoid confounding, we still may have some confusion, for example, due to the design of the COVID-19 vaccination campaign were higher-risk patients were prioritized, not only due to age but by immunosuppressive conditions.

That is why, to our understanding, our results regarding vaccine dose and time contrast with the evidence on the effectiveness of booster dose [13, 35–38]. During the study period, the 4th dose of vaccine was approved in February 2022, and up to June 2022 it was only indicated for healthcare workers, high-risk patients and people over 60 years old [39]. The time from last vaccination is a well-known risk factor of infection described even by the first clinical trials were effectiveness of SARS-CoV-2 vaccines against moderate and severe COVID-19 waned with time after vaccination [40–43]. Another cohort study showed the cumulative hazard ratio of breakthrough infection according to the different risk factors [22]. Consistently in our study, we quantify time since last vaccine dose in a real-world setting and a hospitalized based population. Since we considered both the number of vaccine doses and the time elapsed since the last dose as crucial variables influencing the outcome of breakthrough infection, we decided to create a single variable incorporating both factors. However, we did not include the number of vaccine doses in the multivariate model because dose count was strongly associated with patient risk profile, as individuals with comorbidities and those at higher risk of severe COVID-19 were prioritized for early and additional doses during the vaccination campaign. Including dose count as a covariate could therefore introduce bias due to confounding by indication. Instead, we included time since the last vaccine dose, which we considered a more reliable proxy for waning vaccine-induced immunity, and less influenced by prioritization criteria.

Regarding socioeconomic status, we used an ecological index built as a composite of four indicators: employment status, education, income and immigration. Other studies use similar indexes, but others use individual estimates of socioeconomic status or education level. A study that used a similar ecological index, found that users living in the areas with highest deprivation had an increased odds of SARS-CoV-2 infection following their first vaccine dose (OR 1.11; CI 95%, 1.01-1.23), even after adjusting by age, BMI and sex [21]. However, we did not find a statistically significant association in our model. When analyzing our results, we might not have found a significant association due to the variable values distribution, as most of the counts were proximate to the Catalonian median.

In regard to immunosuppression, multiple studies report that patients with immune dysfunction have a higher risk of breakthrough infection [19, 44, 45] and hospitalization for breakthrough infection [26, 46]. Despite the definitions of immunosuppression may vary, most studies conclude that there is an association between immunosuppression and a higher risk of severe infection and hospitalization. In this study, we define immunosuppression as having a diagnosis of any primary immune deficiency, organ transplant-related immunosuppression or HIV and HIV-related diseases. Therefore, our definition represents a broad category of disorders with varying degrees of immunosuppression which may correspond to different risks of breakthrough infections.

With reference to other comorbidities, we have found a high variability in the literature in terms of the comorbidities that are taken into account and also in terms of their results [20]. We did not find an association between reinfection and other comorbidities after the adjustment, however, one study found that kidney disease (OR 1.95; CI 95%, 1.14-3.31), heart disease (OR 1.30; CI 95%, 1.03-1.65) and lung disease (OR 1.27; CI 95%, 1.02-1.59) were associated with breakthrough infection in adults who had received their first vaccine dose but not the second dose [21]. In contrast, other findings described a two- and three-times higher risk with a cardiovascular disease [26, 27], or no association with hypertension [21, 22].

Our findings strengthen the recommendations of continuing to administer booster vaccine doses on high-risk population, specifically those with immunosuppression; and remarks the importance on the timing for this booster doses, irrespective of the number of previous vaccine doses. This might have implications on prioritizing the comorbidities for vaccination campaigns, but also to emphasize on the importance of vaccination on this population. It is also important to keep vaccines updated to the circulating emerging SARS-CoV-2 strains and optimize vaccination timing and modalities. Even though the association between socioeconomic status and breakthrough infection is not statistically significant in our study, it is still important to consider vulnerability of people with a lower socioeconomic status when public health policies are designed. Future studies should aim to describe the effectiveness of bivalent COVID-19 vaccine on breakthrough infections and the association of different risk factors after boosters with these types of vaccines. In addition, different outcomes such as severity and admission to ICU could be further studied in cost-effectiveness studies in our territory to determine periodicity and target population for vaccination campaigns.

Conclusions

This study found that the probability of breakthrough SARS-CoV-2 infection was significantly higher in hospitalized patients who had received their last vaccine dose more than six months before hospitalization. Immunosuppression was the strongest independent risk factor for breakthrough infection, while other comorbidities lost significance in the adjusted analysis. These findings highlight the importance of timely booster vaccination, particularly for immunosuppressed individuals, to reduce the risk of severe SARS-CoV-2 infection.
