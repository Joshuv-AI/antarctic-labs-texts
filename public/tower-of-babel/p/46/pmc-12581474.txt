Telemedicine in the preoperative setting: turning potential into practice
Authors: Elena Giovanna Bignami, Matteo Guarnieri, Gabriele Baldini, Chiara Barneschi, Giuseppe Coletta, Edoardo De Robertis, Massimilano Greco, Beatrice Loriga, Franco Marinangeli, Vittorio Pavoni, Alba Piroli, Luigi Vetrugno, Valentina Bellini
Published: 2025

Background

The integration of telemedicine (TM) into the preoperative evaluation pathway is an idea whose time has come. Its potential to improve accessibility, efficiency, and clinical outcomes is supported by a growing body of evidence [1–3]. However, in many healthcare systems, implementation remains inconsistent or absent. The slow and fragmented adoption of telemedicine in the perioperative period is not a result of limited technology but rather of complex barriers—organizational, clinical, regulatory, and infrastructural—that have yet to be adequately addressed.

Fragmentation and variability across systems

One of the foremost obstacles is the heterogeneity of healthcare systems. Organizational models differ across regions, institutions, and surgical specialties, impeding the scalability of telemedicine, duplicating effort, and propagating inequities in access. The absence of centralized coordination precludes shared protocols and benchmarking, which are essential for consistent quality assurance [4, 5].

Uncertainty in patient selection and clinical appropriateness

Not all patients are suitable for remote preoperative evaluation. There are no widely accepted guidelines for identifying which surgical patients—based on American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, comorbidities, or procedure complexity—are acceptable for telemedicine. This uncertainty often drives clinicians to default to in-person evaluations, especially in cases involving airway risks or unclear functional status [6, 7]. Recent randomized controlled data demonstrated that pre-anesthesia telemedicine evaluation yielded comparable clinical outcomes to in-person visits, supporting its role in carefully selected surgical populations [8].

Legal, ethical, and professional ambiguity

The medico-legal framework governing telemedicine remains incomplete. Liability rests with the provider, even in remote consults [9]. Selecting telemedicine over in-person consultation—against better judgment—can itself trigger litigation risk [5]. Additional concerns include patient identity verification, informed consent, and compliance with the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) or equivalent legislation—areas where many platforms fall short [5, 10].

Infrastructure gaps and interoperability issues

Many institutions still lack the digital platforms and Electronic Health Record (EHR) integration necessary for effective telemedicine implementation. Platforms often operate in silos without seamless integration into regional health systems, leading to redundant documentation and discontinuous care [7]. Additionally, audiovisual quality and digital device access vary widely, disproportionately affecting vulnerable groups [11].

Training and professional readiness

Telemedicine demands not only new technology but also a shift in clinical practice. Most institutions lack structured training programs for anesthesiologists and perioperative staff in virtual consultation and risk assessment. As reported in a survey of > 2200 healthcare workers, awareness levels are moderate except where targeted training is delivered; willingness to adopt telemedicine is high, but practical readiness remains low [2].

Economical aspects and evidence about cost-effectiveness

Emerging evidence robustly supports the cost-effectiveness of telemedicine (TM) in preoperative pathways, while confirming that its clinical outcomes are non-inferior to those of traditional in-person care.

A recent multicenter randomized controlled trial demonstrated that a structured telehealth platform for elective outpatient surgeries significantly reduced unplanned healthcare utilization costs (mean CAD $323 vs. $370 per patient) while yielding equivalent clinical outcomes in terms of readmissions and complications [12]. This provides direct evidence that virtual preoperative management can maintain clinical standards without increasing financial burden.

Beyond direct cost savings, TM generates substantial indirect economic and environmental benefits. An analysis from a tertiary referral network revealed that teleconsultations saved approximately $120 in travel costs per visit and avoided over 64,000 miles of patient travel, corresponding to a 26,700 kg reduction in CO2 emissions [13]. This underscores TM’s role in promoting sustainable and equitable healthcare.

From a system efficiency perspective, TM optimizes resource allocation by reducing redundant appointments, streamlining interdisciplinary communication, and minimizing last-minute surgery cancellations. Institutions implementing tele-preoperative workflows report more balanced clinic utilization, improved scheduling efficiency, and earlier identification of patients requiring medical optimization [12, 13]. This reallocation of clinical resources translates into shorter waiting times, decreased operational delays, and more sustainable use of healthcare infrastructure.

Collectively, these findings affirm that telemedicine, when integrated into structured preoperative pathways, represents a cost-effective, resource-conscious, and patient-centered model that does not compromise clinical safety or efficacy. The convergence of economic advantages and clinical non-inferiority makes a compelling case for broader adoption.

Challenges in remote prehabilitation and postoperative follow-up

While multimodal prehabilitation and tele-follow-up programs show promise, implementation is limited by patient selection ambiguity and a lack of standardized compliance metrics. Key Predictor Indexes (KPIs) such as early complication detection or unplanned readmissions remain poorly defined and are rarely tracked [11, 14].

Absence of regulatory guidance and national monitoring

Despite some national telemedicine initiatives, few countries maintain real-time registries to monitor telemedicine adoption in preoperative care. Without data collection and feedback mechanisms, identifying best practices or areas for improvement is challenging [7, 15]. The working group has proposed a sample model outlining a potential pathway guiding patients from preoperative assessment to surgery. This model takes into account the type of surgical procedure, the specific challenges of each operative setting, and the patient’s demographic and clinical characteristics. Anesthesiologists must lead the evolution of telemedicine into standardized, ethically informed, and patient-centered preoperative care. Only then can telemedicine realize its full potential—enhancing safety, access, and efficiency without compromising clinical standards.

While these challenges are significant, several promising solutions have already emerged from pilot initiatives and institutional best practices. These include structured patient eligibility criteria, validated risk stratification tools, interoperable digital infrastructures, and targeted professional training. Rather than waiting for comprehensive system reform, stakeholders should act on what is implementable now.

Starting with well-defined, low-complexity use cases—such as low-risk surgical patients or telematic postoperative follow-ups—can offer a pragmatic and scalable entry point. This stepwise approach will help build clinical confidence, institutional alignment, and public trust, forming the bedrock of a broader transformation.
