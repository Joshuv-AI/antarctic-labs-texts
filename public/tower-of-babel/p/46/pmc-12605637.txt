Right sided testicular pain as the sole presenting symptom of an abdominal aortic aneurysm: A case report
Authors: Fred Kenny, Morgan McLoughlin, Simona Bobic, Fergal Moran, Galina Ponomareva, Nawar Masarani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction and importanceThis case highlights the rare presentation of testicular pain in a patient with a symptomatic abdominal aortic aneurysm (sAAA). Atypical presentations provide diagnostic challenges for timely diagnosis. This case report aims to show the importance of considering sAAA in the differential diagnosis of testicular pain, especially in older patients with known cardiovascular risk factors. Prompt diagnosis and early intervention are critical to improving outcomes in cases of ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysms (rAAA).Presentation of caseA 62-year-old male with a medical history of hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, and prostate cancer presented to the emergency department with sudden-onset right-sided testicular pain. Introduction

Background

A ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (rAAA) characteristically presents as abdominal pain radiating to the back, with associated haemodynamic instability. However, the classical triad of flank/back pain, hypotension, and a pulsatile mass is only quoted in approximately 25–50 % of patients [3]. Abdominal aortic aneurysms (AAA) may not be considered in the top differential diagnosis of a presentation of isolated testicular pain.

Rationale

This case report demonstrates an unusual presentation of an sAAA and the importance of thorough examination and good clinical acumen to identify this life-threatening condition. An AAA is defined as a pathologic dilatation of the abdominal aorta to a diameter greater than 3.0 cm or 50 % larger than its expected anteroposterior diameter [4]. Large sAAAs are at an increased risk of rupture and rAAAs are associated with an overall 80 % mortality [5], with a greater than 44 % prehospital mortality. Of those patients that make it to the hospital, a recent multicentre study showed that patient who underwent an emergency open AAA repair, the overall 30-day mortality rate was 32 %. [6]

Guidelines and literature

An AAA is not an uncommon condition, having a global prevalence among people aged 30 to 79 years of 0.92 % [7], increasing to 6 % of men and 1.7 % of women over the age of 65 years [5]. AAAs are often an asymptomatic pathology and may go unnoticed until symptomatic rupture. Smoking, hypertension, advanced age, a family history of AAA, male sex and genetics are clinical risk factors for the development of an AAA [8]. In our case, the patient was not known to have an AAA, and this was his first presentation to the emergency department (ED). A large meta-analysis showed a 42 % misdiagnosis rate of rAAA, with the most common incorrect diagnoses being ureteric colic and myocardial infarction [1]. This is a concerning finding given the prevalence of this condition and the associated deleterious consequences of misdiagnosis. Greater clinical acumen and symptom recognition may improve diagnostic accuracy and improve patient outcomes. The consideration of a broad range of differentials is important in the diagnosis of such a time sensitive, life-threatening condition.

Due to the poor outcomes of rAAAs, many countries have developed screening programmes for at risk individuals [9] which have been shown to reduce aneurysm related deaths [10].

Patient information

We discuss the case of a 62-year-old man who was brought in by ambulance to the emergency department (ED) with right sided testicular pain which started that evening. The pain was sudden in onset and began gradually approximately three hours prior to presentation to the ED. The pain was described as severe and dull in nature and localised to the testicle without radiating. No additional symptoms were reported. The patient has a past medical history of hypertension, hyperlipidaemia, prostate cancer with a prostatectomy, and was recovering from a recent lower respiratory tract infection. He was not on any anticoagulation medication. He is an ex-smoker of 30 pack- years and is independently mobile. There was no family history of aortic aneurysms. He has no allergies.

Clinical findings

On examination, the patient had a soft abdomen with mild tenderness across the lower abdomen. External genital exam was normal, with an intact cremasteric reflex and a negative Phren's sign. The right testicle was identified to be the focal area of pain. The patient had an increased body mass index. Vital signs were within normal parameters and the patient was haemodynamically stable throughout review.

Diagnostic assessment

During the work up in the emergency department, a bedside ultrasound testes and POCUS abdomen were performed. The testicular ultrasound showed normal findings with preserved vascular flow and no evidence of torsion or epididymo-orchitis. The POCUS was performed given the patient's age, vascular risk factors, and undifferentiated testicular pain. It was not routine, but a clinically reasoned decision prompted by risk factors and an atypical presentation. The bedside POCUS identified a 9.1 cm sAAA. This was not previously known. As a result, a CT Aorta (CTA) was arranged in an expedited fashion to assess this finding further, which showed a large aneurysm of the infrarenal aorta with a maximal AP diameter of 9.4 cm over a length of 12.2 cm. No aneurysmal leak or concerning radiographical features for rupture were evident (Fig. 1 A, Fig. 2 A). These findings were discussed with and confirmed with the radiology consultant on call. On return to ED from the CT department, the patient reported an acute worsening in testicular pain. He became haemodynamically unstable with blood pressure dropping to 60/30 mmHg. Bilateral femoral pulses remained palpable. Other vitals consisted of a heart rate of 55 bpm and an oxygen saturation of 95 % on room air. Concerningly, the patient became drowsy and confused. Resuscitation was immediately instigated, and the patient was returned to the CT department for immediate reimaging. Repeat imaging revealed a retroperitoneal haemorrhage on the right lateral side of the aneurysmal sac, new in comparison to the previous scan (Fig. 1 A and 2 A, and Fig. 1B and 2B, respectively). The patient was transferred directly to the emergency operating theatre. The massive transfusion protocol was activated, and theatre staff, anaesthetic teams and senior surgical staff were contacted. The main diagnostic challenge was recognising the sAAA from isolated testicular pain.

Intervention

In the operating theatre of a district general hospital, an open repair of the rAAA was performed. Considering the desperate situation of a rAAA, an individualised approach is advised in choosing the surgical treatment modality, taking the patient's fitness, anatomy, surgeon and patient preferences into account [12]. RCTs comparing OSR with EVAR for rAAA have shown no statistical difference in mortality between the two therapeutic options [[13], [14], [15]]. The procedure was led by a senior vascular consultant with significant experience in open rAAA repair. The patient was placed under general anaesthetic in supine and a longitudinal laparotomy from xiphoid process to pubic symphysis was carried out. A large aneurysm was identified with extensive surrounding haematoma. An aortic clamp was applied infrarenally and the common iliac arteries were controlled with Debakey Iliac clamps. The aortic aneurysmal sac was opened longitudinally, and clot and debris were evacuated. A 20 mm Dacron silver tube graft was used as an end-to-end anastomosis from the infrarenal aorta proximally, to above the bifurcation of the common iliac arteries distally. The blood flow was restored to each lower limb in a sequential fashion to prevent significant hypotension. Intraoperative doppler examination distal to the repair in the iliac and femoral arteries identified good triphasic flow.

Follow-up and outcomes

Postoperatively, the patient was managed in the intensive care unit (ICU) for recovery and close monitoring. Inotropic and blood pressure support were ceased on postoperative day 1. The patient spent two days in the ICU and two days in the High Dependency Unit before being transferred to ward level care. Shortly after the surgery, the patient was able to pass urine independently and mobilise with physiotherapy. The patient's testicular pain resolved following the aneurysm repair. The patient suffered from postoperative ileus which slowly resolved with conservative measures. A normal bowel motion was passed on postoperative day six. With the ileus resolved, the patient was discharged two weeks after surgery. The patient was seen in the outpatient department three weeks later, with no active issue and an overall uneventful recovery. His surgical clips were removed. Subsequent popliteal duplex scans were performed to investigate for concurrent popliteal aneurysms [16]. Advice was provided to recommend AAA screening for first degree relatives at the age of 50. The ESVS 2024 guideline recommends AAA screening in all men and women aged 50 years and older with a first degree relative with an AAA [12]. There were no complications or adverse events. The patient adhered to advice given for post operative recovery including no heavy lifting and lifestyle modification. No recurrence of testicular pain was reported at early follow-up which further supports the proposed hypothetical mechanism of the testicular pain.

Discussion

Abdominal aortic aneurysms presenting with testicular pain are rare with only a few documented cases in the literature. Most individuals with AAA are asymptomatic with aneurysm detection incidentally or through a screening programme [17]. No formal screening programme is currently in place in Ireland. This case highlights that the characteristic rAAA triad is not always present and the importance of considering a broad clinical differential to capture atypical symptomatology.

Testicular pain is a characteristic feature of urological conditions that may present to the ED, such as testicular torsion, epididymitis, hydrocele or urinary calculi [18]. A proposed theory to the aetiology of the symptom of isolated testicular pain in sAAA pathology pertains to local irritation of the genitofemoral nerve by the aneurysmal sac [19]. Further nerve compression may be caused by haematoma or local haemorrhage [20]. The genitofemoral nerve arises from the lumbar plexus at L1/L2. It travels inferiorly along the posterior abdominal wall, over the anterior surface of the psoas major muscle before dividing into the genital and femoral branches. The genital branch goes on to supply sensation to the scrotum as well as innervating the cremaster muscle. The right psoas muscle, in this case study, is infiltrated by the haematoma, as shown on Cross sectional CT imaging (Fig. 1B and 2B). The authors hypothesise that local irritation from the aneurysm sac or haematoma could produce a referred pain phenomenon to the testes [21] .The aorta typically lies to the left of the midline in the abdomen making the atypical presentation of testicular pain in sAAA more common on the left [22], additionally compounding the atypical nature of this case report. Although this theory remains hypothetical, the involvement of the psoas region in the imaging and its proximity to the genitofemoral nerve supports this explanation. The anatomical basis for this pain is supported by reviews of genitofemoral neuralgia, which describes neuropathic pain arising from compression or injury to the genitofemoral nerve, with symptoms including scrotal pain [23]. However, most literature on genitofemoral pain focuses on iatrogenic injury rather than vascular compression.

The key determining factor of survival in a rAAA is time from rupture to surgical treatment [24]. The testicular pain commenced hours prior to aneurysmal sac rupture. A period of testicular pain followed by acute worsening and then subsequent haemodynamic instability was a common theme in the few previously reported cases in the literature [20,25,26]. The onset of symptoms occurring before rupture may provide an opportunity for expedited repair while the patient remains haemodynamically stable, highlighting the importance of prompt diagnosis. However, ESVS (European Society of Vascular Surgery) guidelines recommend that symptomatic aneurysm should be operated on the next available elective operating list (class IIb Evidence) [12].

Given the relatively high incidence of AAAs and the high mortality rate of rAAAs, clinicians should keep a high level of suspicion for this diagnosis when examining patients with testicular pain. Time is crucial in a ruptured aneurysm and prompt diagnosis may have a significant effect on surgical outcomes.

Take away lesson

Clinicians should maintain a high level of suspicion for vascular causes in older males with testicular pain.

Strengths and limitations

The case has great cross-speciality relevance, involving emergency medicine, radiology, vascular surgery, and anaesthesia. Limitations include the single-patient design, an absence of long-term follow-up, and the speculative nature of the proposed anatomical mechanism of pain.
