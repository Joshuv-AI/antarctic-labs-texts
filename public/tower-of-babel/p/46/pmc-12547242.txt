Prospective Multicenter Longitudinal Measurement of Liver Stiffness in School-Age Children With Cholestatic Liver Disease

Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background and AimsA multicenter prospective longitudinal study of vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) in school-age children with biliary atresia (BA), alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (a1-AT) and Alagille syndrome (ALGS) was undertaken to test the hypothesis that there would be measurable disease progression over 2 years.MethodsVibration-controlled transient elastography was performed annually for 2 years in children with BA, a1-AT and ALGS.ResultsValid liver stiffness measurement (LSM) was determined at baseline/second follow-up in 254/180 (71%), 104/58 (56%) and 100/61 (61%) participants (mean elapsed time 2.27 years) with BA, a1-AT and ALGS, respectively. Modeling did not reveal a relationship between LSM and time since baseline: BA 1.2% (-1.6, 4.2%), a1-AT 0.1% (-3.8, 4.2%), and ALGS 3.6% (-2.9, 10.5%) LSM (% change/year; mean [95% confidence interval]). Similarly, mean LSM did not change significantly from baseline to visit 2 (BA 13.6 + 11.0 vs 15.1 + 12.8; a1-AT 7.8 + 5.1 vs 8.5 + 7.6; ALGS 10.6 + 9.4 vs 12.2 + 12.1 kPa, mean + standard deviation). Albumin and total bilirubin levels did not change in these participants. Platelet counts dropped at rates that were similar to a national representative sample, the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (ie, 5000 to 7000/μL per year).ConclusionSurprisingly, longitudinal measurement of LSM and laboratory parameters of liver disease severity over 2 years in school-age children with compensated BA, a1-AT, and ALGS did not reveal significant change, consistent with slow progression of cholestatic liver disease in this age group. These findings have implications for both clinical care and interventional trials in this patient population.

Introduction

Elastographic assessment of liver stiffness is a promising methodology for noninvasive measurement of hepatic fibrosis and by corollary liver disease severity. Numerous studies have demonstrated the feasibility of the technique and pediatric studies have shown a correlation with invasive liver biopsy-based histologic assessment of liver fibrosis.1, 2, 3 It has been assumed that this noninvasive technique would lend itself to longitudinal assessment of progression of liver disease severity. However, limited data examining its longitudinal use exist, especially as it pertains to liver diseases in children.4, 5, 6 Correlation of longitudinal measurements of liver stiffness with biopsy-based measures of hepatic fibrosis would be ideal, but given the invasive nature of liver biopsy and lack of sequential biopsies in children this type of direct correlation with advancing hepatic fibrosis is rarely available in pediatrics. An underlying assumption for the use of liver stiffness measurement (LSM) as a longitudinal measure of pediatric liver disease severity is that it is precise and reproducible, which has been demonstrated in few studies under controlled circumstances, but not in routine clinical practice.7

In the FibroScan in Pediatric Cholestatic Liver Disease (FORCE; NCT02922751) study, LSM using vibration-controlled transient elastography (VCTE) in over 400 children with biliary atresia (BA), Alagille syndrome (ALGS) or alpha-1 antitrypsin deficiency (a1-AT) was shown to strongly correlate with various markers of liver disease severity and the presence of portal hypertension.8 Participants in this prospective study were primarily children who had survived with their native liver past the first 2 years of life. In the present study, prospective follow-up VCTE assessment of LSM over 2+ years was undertaken in this cohort testing the hypothesis that there would be progressive fibrosis which could be documented using LSM. Ultimately, demonstration that longitudinal VCTE can track fibrosis would allow LSM to be used as a noninvasive marker monitoring therapeutic effects in clinical trials of novel interventions including antifibrotics.

Methods

Study Population

The FORCE study (NCT02922751) collected longitudinal LSM on participants enrolled in three distinct prospective longitudinal studies conducted in the Childhood Liver Disease Research Network: Prospective Database of Infants with Cholestasis [PROBE; NCT00061828], Biliary Atresia Study in Infants and Children [BASIC; NCT00345553], and Longitudinal Observational Study of Genetic Causes of Intrahepatic Cholestasis [LOGIC; NCT00571272]. Participants in one of the studies with BA, a1-AT, or ALGS younger than 22 years of age were approached for FORCE enrollment. Detailed descriptions of FORCE inclusion/exclusion criteria, LSM requirements and baseline analysis have been reported.8 Longitudinal clinical and laboratory data were collected in the parent longitudinal observational study (PROBE, BASIC, or LOGIC), while LSM was measured in FORCE. LSMs were performed at baseline and two follow-up visits originally scheduled at 1 year and 2 years after baseline but extended for up to 1 year in response to adjustments made during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Liver stiffness (reported in kilopascals, kPa) was measured by VCTE in nonfasted and nonsedated participants, using FibroScan (Echosens, Waltham, Massachusetts). Probe selection and techniques were according to the manufacturer's instructions. All FibroScan operators were trained and certified by a designated trainer from Echosens. A valid LSM required at least 10 valid measurements using the appropriate probe size (S or M) and exam type (S1 or S2) based on thoracic perimeter and skin to liver capsule distance with interquartile kPa range/median kPa value of < 30%.

Liver disease severity in this study was assessed on the basis of LSM and standardized laboratory studies (total bilirubin [TB], albumin and platelet count) and aspartate aminotransferase to platelet ratio index (APRI) collected as part of clinical care during prospective follow-up in PROBE, BASIC, or LOGIC. For longitudinal assessment of laboratory studies up to 5 years of data surrounding the FORCE study was used.

We analyzed National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) wave 2015-16 and 2017-18 to provide benchmark data for platelet count in the normal population (https://www.cdc.gov/nchs/nhanes/index.htm [last accessed on July 23, 2024]). A total of 5690 NHANES participants younger than 22 years had platelet count data. We calculated age stratified mean and standard deviation of platelet count, and fitted penalized splines to visualize how population mean platelet count changes by age. Using these measurements, platelet count Z-scores were calculated for FORCE participants.

All research was conducted in accordance with both the Declarations of Helsinki and Istanbul. All research was approved by the appropriate ethics and/or institutional review committee(s) and written consent was given in writing by all subjects or their legal guardian.

Statistical Methods

Analyses were stratified by disease diagnosis. Descriptive statistics for continuous variables include mean, standard deviation, median, 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and interquartile range. For categorical variables, frequencies and percentages are given. Boxplots were used to display several continuous variables. Operator experience was measured as the number of FibroScans performed as part of this study, but did not include examinations performed outside of FORCE.

Differences in LSM among groups were tested with two sample t-tests. LSM was log-transformed before statistical comparisons and modeling to account for the skewed distribution. A linear mixed effects model was used to assess whether mean LSM changes significantly over time and assess between and within-individual variation of LSM. The model uses LSM as the dependent variable, time measured in years since baseline as a continuous fixed effect, and subject-specific random effects (random intercept and random slope) to account for correlation among measurements taken on the same subject. The time coefficient was used to test whether there is a significant change in LSM over time. The potential effect of fasting time was examined. The distribution of LSM was compared for participants with fasting time of at least 3 hours to those with less than 3 hours. We also tested the effect of fasting time in the above linear mixed effects model.

We examined longitudinal models for albumin, platelet count (and its z-score), TB (log transformed), and APRI from 1 year before and 4 years after the FORCE baseline. Similar to the LSM models, the lab measurements were the dependent variables with time from baseline as a fixed effect and subject-specific random effects (random intercept and random slope).

The reliable change index (RCI) was used to determine changes from baseline LSM that would represent a significant change.9 Based on the linear mixed effect models, we estimated the standard error of the measurement. The RCI for log (LSM) = (1.96)x 2 ×standard error of the measurement. Upper and lower limits of changes from baseline that were not significant were baseline LSM x exp(RCI) and baseline LSM/exp(RCI). Values outside the range would represent significant changes from baseline. Statistical analyses were performed using SAS (version 9.4; SAS Institute Inc, Cary, North Carolina).

Results

Characteristics of the Cohort

Five hundred fifty participants were enrolled in FORCE, of whom, 455 (83%), 346 (63%), and 299 (54%) participants had valid LSM at baseline, visit 1 (at ∼ 1 year) and visit 2 (at ∼ 2 years) (Figure A1). As previously reported, at baseline participants had a mean age of approximately 9 years, generally had normal serum TB and albumin levels with varying degrees of thrombocytopenia (Table A1).8 On average, the time between the baseline visit and visit 2 was 829 days (Figure A2). Valid LSM was obtained at baseline in FORCE in 254, 104, and 100 participants with BA, a1-AT, and ALGS, respectively. Of these, 180 (71%), 58 (56%), and 61 (61%) participants had completed the study with valid LSM determined at visit 2; 160 (63%), 49 (47%), and 51 (51%) had valid LSM at all three time points. The baseline characteristics of the participants who dropped out and did not have a visit 2 LSM assessment were similar or insignificantly worse compared to those with a visit 2 measurement (Table A2). Mean LSM was higher in participants with BA who did not have a visit 2 measurement compared to those who did (20.0 vs 13.6, P = .002) (Table A3); differences in participants with a1-AT and ALGS were not statistically significant (a1-AT: 11.6 vs 7.8, P = .13; ALGS: 12.8 vs 10.6, P = .26). During the FORCE study, 19, 1, and 5 participants with BA, a1-AT, and ALGS, respectively, underwent liver transplantation (Figure A1). Eighty different operators performed LSM at 13 sites. Five of these operators performed at least 50 study exams over the course of the study (Figure A3).

Change in LSM over time

The mean trajectory of LSM did not change significantly over time during the course of this study among any of the three disease cohorts (Figure 1). The rate of change in mean LSM was not significant (BA 1.2% per year [−1.6, 4.2%], a1-AT 0.1% per year [−3.8, 4.2%], and ALGS 3.6% per year [−2.9, 10.5%] [95% CI]). The trajectories of the participants who subsequently died (n = 2) or underwent liver transplantation (n = 19) almost all originated with an LSM > 10 kPa (Figure A4). Conclusion

This is the largest prospective longitudinal study of VCTE assessment of LSM in children with cholestatic liver disease. The underlying hypothesis for the study was there would be progressive liver disease in BA, a1-AT, and ALGS over the planned 2 year time period of the study and that VCTE assessment of LSM would detect and quantify that disease progression. Surprisingly, there was no significant change in LSM in the BA, a1-AT, and ALGS cohorts over the course of the study. Analysis of disease cohorts by study visit and by modeling individual trajectories relative to time from baseline LSM failed to show a significant change over time. The slopes derived from modeling the trajectories of those children with BA and ALGS who did not survive with their native liver was numerically higher although there was not a statistically significant change with time from baseline measurement. However, due to limited numbers of events and the variability of the measures, this analysis may not have been adequately powered to identify a distinct trajectory for transplant or death. The lack of change was unexpected and is counter to the general understanding of the natural history of BA. Additional laboratory parameters that are felt to reflect liver disease severity were assessed longitudinally in the same participants. Albumin and TB similarly did not change over time during the study period in all three disease cohorts.

Notably, there was a significant reduction in platelet count over time in BA, a1-AT, and ALGS, which could be interpreted as a manifestation of progressive portal hypertension. It has been previously shown that platelet counts drop during childhood, although granular quantitative details of that change were not available previously for comparison to this study’s findings.10,11 The NHANES database was leveraged to fill this void and revealed changes with age (decrease of ∼ 5000–7000/μL per year) that were similar to the changes seen in the participants in this study. Overall, the elastographic and laboratory data do not support the supposition that there is relevant disease progression over 3–5 years in these cholestatic liver diseases.

The participants in this cohort represent a specific subset of children with these diseases and as such care should be taken in generalizing the findings from this study. The population studied in FORCE reflects survivors beyond 3–4 years of age with native liver who might be routinely seen in the outpatient setting. In these patients, our data suggests that disease progression (estimated by LSM, albumin, TB, and platelet count) may be less than expected. This is an important finding for counseling families, who will be reassured by these findings. The findings are also relevant for the design of clinical trials. For instance, it would not be feasible to show that an antifibrotic or other therapeutic agent slows fibrosis or disease progression over a two to 3 year time period in this specific patient population.

This study was not designed to investigate the precision and/or reproducibility of the technique used for elastographic assessment of LSM. Rigor in these parameters is critical for the use of a technique in longitudinal follow-up of a measurement. The higher the precision/reproducibility, the greater the power to detect a change over time. Our analysis suggests there is considerable variability in LSM and the variability is proportional to the magnitude of the measurement. Previous studies in healthy children revealed good inter and intraobserver variability in LSM.7 Similar studies have not been performed in children with significant liver disease. In two adult studies, one in individuals with chronic liver diseases and the other with type 2 diabetes, significant variability in LSM was observed.6,12 The precision/reproducibility of LSM is a critical issue in its use both for routine clinical care and in clinical trials.

There are a number of important limitations in this study. As with any technology, operator expertise is an important parameter in its use. All operators in this study were certified by Echosens trainers. However, the vast majority of operators performed fewer than 50 measurements as part of this study. At the time of initiation of the study, this technique was not in wide use in pediatrics and as such additional expertise in clinical practice was likely limited. For measurement in adults with liver disease, an experienced operator is felt to be one who has performed > 500 valid measurements.12,13 This standard is less likely to be met in routine pediatric clinical practice. The very sickest patients may not have been enrolled in this study, although they were not specifically excluded with the exception of uncontrolled ascites which precludes valid VCTE. Enrollment of infants and toddlers in this study was lower, presumably due to concerns about feasibility of or technical difficulties with the measurement.8 As with our previous study, children in this study were not routinely fasted for the performance of Fibroscan. This approach was utilized to maximize study enrollment and follow-up. It also may reflect the real world. The impact of fasting on LSM is real but small in healthy children.7 Its impact in adults with liver disease is also real but does not approach the magnitude of the variability of LSM seen in this study.14 Furthermore, multiple analyses of the data from this study did not reveal an impact of fasting on the findings. One final limitation is that the interval between scans in this study was limited to approximately 2–3 years. It is likely that longer time periods of follow-up are needed to identify a change in LSM over time in survivors of cholestatic liver diseases through school-age.

In summary, this study did not reveal a significant change in VCTE assessment of liver stiffness over two to 3 years in children of school age with BA, ALGS, or a1-AT. In keeping with these findings, longitudinal measurement of routine laboratory parameters of liver disease showed no changes typically associated with progression in the same participants. These findings are critically important in our understanding of measuring the natural history of these diseases and in the potential design of clinical trials. Care should be taken in the application of these findings as the study primarily included children of school age with relatively well-compensated cholestatic liver disease and not infants who may have more rapidly progressive disease. These investigations also suggest, although they do not prove, that there may be significant variability in annual LSM in children with cholestatic liver disease which may have implications for its use clinically and as an outcome measure for clinical trials.
