Assessment of real-life visual outcomes and treatment efficacy of diabetic macular edema in patients with type 2 diabetes
Authors: Annika Konttinen, Joonas Wirkkala, Anna-Maria Kubin, Pasi Ohtonen, Nina Hautala
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDiabetic macular edema (DME) is a major cause of vision loss in individuals with diabetes. This study evaluated the long-term visual prognosis of patients with type 2 diabetes (T2D) by assessing real-world DME treatment outcomes.MethodsPatients with T2D and DME at Oulu University Hospital during 2010–2023 were included. The effect of DME intervention (anti-VEGF-agents, macular laser or both, intravitreal corticosteroids, observation) was evaluated by changes in visual acuity (VA) and residual edema. The dataset comprised additional variables, including age, sex, age at T2D diagnosis, timing of onset for diabetic retinopathy (DR) and DME, DR severity, glucose levels, other comorbidities, occurrences of treatment interruption, and adverse effects related to intravitreal therapy.ResultsOf 549 screened patients with T2D, 1145 DME episodes in 355 patients (560 eyes) were included. Mean ages at T2D, DR, and DME diagnosis were 51.0, 61.7, and 66.4 years, respectively. Mean HbA1c at DME treatment initiation was 64.3 mmol/mol; 93.0% had hypertension medication and 47.6% had diabetic nephropathy. DME was most treated with anti-VEGF injections alone (55.3%) or combined with laser (20.4%), yielding mean VA improvements of 3.8 [3.0–4.6] and 3.6 [2.5–4.6] ETDRS letters (both p < 0.001), respectively. Intravitreal corticosteroids (3.1%) resulted in a gain of 4.3 [1.2–7.4] letters (p = 0.014), whereas macular laser alone (12.8%) and observation (8.3%) showed no significant effect. Residual edema occurred in 38.1% (anti-VEGF), 43.2% (combination), 54.7% (observation), and 80.6% (corticosteroid) of cases. Adverse events related to any DME treatment were rare.ConclusionsDespite recurrent episodes of DME, most patients with T2D experienced improvement in visual acuity. Anti-VEGF agents alone or combined with macular laser appeared to be beneficial in terms of both visual gain and reduction of edema.Keywords: Diabetic macular edema, Type 2 diabetes, Diabetic retinopathy, Anti-VEGF, Macular laser, Intravitreal corticosteroid, Residual edema

Background

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) and its vision-threatening complication, diabetic macular edema (DME), affect an increasing number of individuals worldwide [1, 2]. The International Diabetes Federation estimates that the global population with diabetes will rise to 783 million by 2045 [1, 3]. In parallel with the growing prevalence of diabetes, the global burden of diabetic retinopathy (DR) is projected to reach 160.5 million cases, with DME accounting for 28.6 million cases [1, 3]. Among individuals with T2D, approximately 35% develop some form of DR, a substantial proportion of whom may progress to DME. The prevalence of DME increases from approximately 3% after 5 years of T2D to 28% after 20 years [2, 4]. Duration of diabetes is a key risk factor for DME, particularly in combination with systemic conditions such as hyperglycemia and hypertension [4]. DME is the leading cause of visual impairment in patients with T2D and imposes a significant global economic burden [1]. Recent advances in DME treatment, particularly the introduction of intravitreal anti-vascular endothelial growth factor (anti-VEGF) agents, have contributed to a reduction in vision loss associated with DME [1, 5, 6].

The pathophysiology of DME involves a complex interplay of vascular, inflammatory, and neurodegenerative processes that lead to the breakdown of the blood–retinal barrier and subsequent retinal swelling [7, 8]. Vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) plays a central role in the development of DME by increasing vascular permeability, allowing proteins, lipids, and other plasma constituents to accumulate in retinal tissue and contribute to macular edema [9]. VEGF expression is upregulated by hyperglycemia-induced oxidative stress and inflammation, resulting in vascular dysfunction and increased macular permeability, ultimately leading to vision loss [9, 10]. Anti-VEGF agents, including ranibizumab, bevacizumab, aflibercept, and faricimab, target this key mediator in DME pathogenesis [11]. These therapies have revolutionized the management of DME and have demonstrated the ability not only to stabilize vision but also to improve visual outcomes in affected patients [12]. The visual prognosis of patients with T2D varies depending on the overall management of the disease and the presence of sight-threatening complications of DR, such as DME or advanced proliferative diabetic retinopathy (PDR). It is crucial for individuals with diabetes to maintain good glycemic control and adhere to healthcare recommendations to effectively prevent or manage diabetic complications. Regular screening, along with timely intervention using intravitreal anti-VEGF or corticosteroid therapy, or retinal laser treatment, are key factors in optimizing treatment outcomes and preserving visual function in patients with T2D [5].

Currently, there is limited long-term data on the efficacy and safety of continuous or frequent anti-VEGF therapy over several years in patients with T2D and DME. Moreover, the implications of prolonged treatment and the sustainability of visual improvements remain incompletely understood. In this real-world study, we evaluated the effectiveness and safety of different treatment modalities for DME in patients with T2D and their impact on visual outcomes. In addition, we assessed the occurrence of residual edema and long-term glycemic control in this population.

Methods

This population-based, real-life cohort study was conducted at Oulu University Hospital, Finland. The study followed the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and was conducted with the approval of the Oulu University Hospital Ethical Research Committee (175/2016). All patients were informed of the study protocol, and a written consent of participation was obtained from each participant. Complete anonymity was ensured, and all study information was anonymized. The article does not include any data that could identify the person.

This cohort study included patients that were diagnosed with T2D and diabetic maculopathy during 2006–2016, and developed DME between December 1, 2010, and December 31, 2022. The patients were followed up until June 30th, 2023. A retrospective search from the hospital’s electronic patient database was made to identify patients using the ICD-10 (International Classification of Diseases) diagnosis codes for diabetic maculopathy (H36.1) and T2D (E11.3).

Patient records were reviewed for dates of active treatment during DME episodes (intravitreal anti-VEGF or corticosteroid, macular laser photocoagulation, combination of anti-VEGF and laser) or observation (without treatment) during the study period. Minimum follow-up time was set at six months. A single DME episode was defined as the period from the diagnosis of DME to the resolution of the edema, indicated by a central retinal thickness (CRT) of less than 300 μm, although some residual fluid might be present. After achieving the resolution of DME or CRT < 300 μm following anti-VEGF-treatment, subsequent episodes of DME were considered as part of the initial episode if recurrence occurred within 4 months. If recurrence occurred after 4 months, it was considered a new episode of DME. A 16-week cutoff was selected in accordance with a recent study by Choi et al., which classified patients into a “no-recurrence” group if the resolution of DME was maintained at a follow-up visit 16 weeks after the last injection. In contrast, patients were classified into a “recurrence” group if the resolution was not sustained at that time point [13]. The best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) was measured as ETDRS-letters at the initiation of DME, after each treatment period, and at the end of the follow-up. Number of intravitreal injections per DME treatment period were gathered and the anti-VEGF-agent (bevacizumab, aflibercept) used was reported. The severity of DR was assessed at DME onset based on the 5-scale classification system (no DR, mild, moderate or severe non-proliferative diabetic retinopathy (NPDR) or PDR) according to the Finnish Current Care Guidelines for Diabetic Retinopathy [14]. Additional baseline characteristics included parameters for age, sex, age at the time of diabetes onset, duration of diabetes and age at DR and DME onset, laterality of DME, and prior cataract surgery. Data from complications of intravitreal treatment (endophthalmitis, uveitis, increase in intraocular pressure), rate for visual impairment at the end of follow-up, rate for residual edema, patient-related reasons for interruptions of treatment, HbA1c level (mmol/mol) at the beginning of treatment and after each DME episode, arterial hypertension and use of anti-hypertensive medication were gathered. The development of chronic kidney disease was examined by decreased GFR (< 60 ml/min/1,73m2) at least during the three months period or albuminuria (> 3 mg/mmol) measured twice during the six months period.

The current protocols for DME treatment were adhered to [14, 15]. DME was considered clinically relevant if optical coherence tomography (OCT) showed intraretinal or subretinal fluid in the macula with a central retinal thickness of ≥ 300 μm. In Finland, bevacizumab is commonly used as the first-line intravitreal drug for DME. Anti-VEGF treatment is usually initiated with 3 monthly injections and then, if needed, continued as treat-and-extend- or pro re nata (PRN) regimens. Treatment with another anti-VEGF agent or intravitreal corticosteroid is initiated in cases of non-sufficient treatment response from the first-line drug. Patients with extrafoveal macular edema located ≥ 500 μm from the central fovea were either observed or subjected to macular laser therapy depending on the location and amount of intraretinal or subretinal fluid. Patients with central macular edema within 500 μm of the fovea were primarily subjected to intravitreal anti-VEGF treatment. Patients were subjected to combination treatment with anti-VEGF and macular laser in cases of subsequent central and extrafoveal edema.

Exclusion criteria included co-existing active PDR, other ocular disorders affecting visual acuity, prior chronic and treatment resistant DME, short follow-up time, or lacking clinical data concerning outcomes of DME. Statistical analyses were performed using the SPSS for Windows (IBM Corp. IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 28.0. Armonk, NY: IBM Corp), SAS (version 9.4 SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA) and R (version 4.5.2). Summary data is presented as a mean with standard deviation (SD) for continuous data and number of cases with proportion (in %) for categorical data, unless other stated. The data of both eyes (for bilateral cases) and all DME episodes during the data collection time were used for the analysis of the visual gain. The dependency within a patient and eyes were considered by using a linear mixed model (LMM) approach where the patient and eye within the patient were set as a random effect. The LMM was adjusted with BCVA and age at DME onset, and sex. Mean visual gain with 95% confidence intervals are presented as the result of LMM. Two-tailed p-values below 0.05 are regarded as statistically significant.

Results

After excluding the confounders and other excluding factors in a population-based cohort of 549 patients with T2D screened for DME, 355 patients (560 eyes) were included in the study. A total of 211 (59.4%) of the patients were males. During the follow-up period from 2010 to 2023, a total of 1145 separate episodes of DME were documented. Over this period, 206 patients (58.0%) developed DME in both eyes. At the onset of DME, most patients (n = 208, 58.6%) had moderate NPDR, whereas only 48 patients (13.5%) had PDR. By the end of the follow-up, 85 patients (23.9%) had progressed to PDR.

The mean age at diagnosis of T2D was 51.0 ± 11.1 years. At the onset of DME, the mean age was 66.4 ± 9.3 years. The average time from T2D diagnosis to the development of DR was 9.8 ± 7.7 years, from T2D to DME 15.2 ± 9.5 years, and from DR to DME 5.2 ± 4.1 years. The mean long-term blood glucose level (HbA1c) was 64.3 ± 16.4 mmol/mol during the first DME episode and 61.4 ± 15.5 mmol/mol during the last DME treatment episode of the follow-up period. Furthermore, 330 patients (93.0%) were receiving medication for arterial hypertension, and nearly half (n = 169, 47.6%) had chronic kidney disease associated with diabetic nephropathy during the follow-up.

At the beginning of the follow-up, the mean BCVA was 72.9 ± 12.1 ETDRS letters (range 20–95). At the end of the last DME episode, the mean BCVA was 73.8 ± 12.5 ETDRS letters (range 4.9–92). Eleven patients (3.1%) were visually impaired at the onset of DME (defined as BCVA < 0.32 on the Snellen chart or < 60 ETDRS letters in the better eye, according to WHO criteria), and a total of 24 patients (6.8%) developed visual impairment by the end of the follow-up period. The mean duration of DME episodes requiring treatment was 9.3 ± 7.3 months in patients with T2D. In most cases, DME was treated with intravitreal anti-VEGF injections (n = 633, 55.3%), resulting in a mean BCVA improvement of 3.8 [3.0 to 4.6] ETDRS letters (p < 0.001) by the end of each DME episode. Combination therapy with anti-VEGF and macular laser (n = 234, 20.4%) led to a gain of 3.6 [2.5 to 4.6] ETDRS letters (p < 0.001). Intravitreal corticosteroids were used less frequently (n = 36, 3.1%) and were associated with a BCVA gain of 4.3 [1.2 to 7.4] ETDRS letters (p = 0.014). No significant visual improvement was observed with macular laser alone (n = 147, 12.8%; change 0.7 [− 0.4 to 1.8] ETDRS letters, p = 0.21) or with observation (n = 95, 8.3%; change − 1.5 [− 3.8 to 0.8] ETDRS letters, p = 0.22). After DME intervention, no intraretinal or subretinal fluid was observed in 60.3% of cases. Residual edema following completed treatment was present in 454 episodes (39.7%). Residual edema occurred most frequently after intravitreal corticosteroid treatment (80.6%) and in cases managed with observation alone (54.7%). Among patients treated with intravitreal anti-VEGF injections, residual edema was observed in 38.1% of cases, and in 43.2% of cases treated with a combination of anti-VEGF and focal macular laser. In contrast, residual edema was least common in the macular laser group, where it was noted in 21.1% of cases.

In the majority of DME episodes treated with anti-VEGF agents (90.1%), therapy was initiated with bevacizumab. A switch from bevacizumab to aflibercept during the follow-up period was documented in 9.6% of these cases. Aflibercept was used as first-line treatment in 9.9% of cases.

The mean follow-up duration was 5.9 ± 2.2 years. By the end of the follow-up, 124 participants (34.8%) had died. During this period, the prevalence of visual impairment (BCVA < 0.32) increased from 3.1% to 6.8% in the study population. Adverse events related to DME treatment were rare. Bacterial endophthalmitis associated with intravitreal anti-VEGF injections occurred in four cases out of 5274 injections (0.08%). In addition, anterior uveitis was observed in three patients (0.06%), bilateral intermediate uveitis in one patient (0.04%), and one patient (0.02%) developed angle-closure glaucoma after intravitreal treatment.

As is typical in real-world settings, interruptions in DME treatment occurred in 11.8% of cases during the follow-up period. The reasons for these interruptions included the patient’s decision to discontinue treatment (5.0%), severe illness or death (5.3%), treatment-related complications (0.4%), and unknown reasons (1.1%). Severe illness or hospitalization were typical reasons for patient-related interruptions of treatment of DME. Continuous follow-up was conducted for all patients except those who died, and re-treatment was administered as indicated.

Discussion

Our real-world results from a population-based cohort of patients with T2D demonstrate that, despite suboptimal glycemic control, multiple comorbidities, and recurrent DME over a long follow-up period, most patients experienced a gain in vision. In line with current clinical guidelines for DME, treatment in this real-life setting was predominantly based on intravitreal anti-VEGF therapy, either alone or in combination with focal macular laser, accounting for 75.7% of treatment approaches [15, 16]. Anti-VEGF-drugs have revolutionized the treatment and visual prognosis of DME over the past two decades [6, 8, 10, 12]. However, the rapidly increasing number of individuals with diabetes, along with those affected by DR, places a substantial burden on healthcare systems. Therefore, treatment decisions for sight-threatening forms of DR, such as DME and PDR, should be guided by robust evidence [1, 3].

The primary goal of DME treatment is to stabilize or improve visual acuity in patients with diabetes. In the present study population, the greatest improvement in BCVA was observed following treatment with intravitreal anti-VEGF agents, either alone or in combination with focal macular laser, as well as with intravitreal corticosteroids. Focal macular laser photocoagulation has been used since the 1990s as a treatment for DME, primarily to stabilize vision. However, anti-VEGF agents have demonstrated superior efficacy compared with laser monotherapy in improving BCVA and stabilizing macular anatomy, and they have become the current gold-standard treatment for DME [17–20]. Low-cost bevacizumab is widely used as a first-line therapy for DME in many countries, including Finland; if an adequate visual or anatomical response is not achieved, a switch to another anti-VEGF agent is typically considered. Accordingly, in 90% of DME episodes, participants receiving anti-VEGF therapy were initially treated with bevacizumab at DME onset, and 10% of these cases were later switched to aflibercept during the follow-up period. Initiating DME treatment with bevacizumab has been shown to yield visual acuity outcomes comparable to aflibercept monotherapy over a 2-year period [21], while offering the advantage of greater cost-effectiveness given the economic burden associated with anti-VEGF therapy [22]. In a previous study, older age, higher CRT, male sex, and worse baseline visual acuity were associated with an increased likelihood of switching from bevacizumab to aflibercept [23]. Furthermore, combining anti-VEGF therapy with focal macular laser - particularly with less destructive techniques such as subthreshold or micropulse laser - has been shown to be beneficial, providing rapid edema reduction with anti-VEGF agents and longer-term stabilization through laser treatment [20]. A statistically significant gain of 3.6 ETDRS letters was also observed following combination treatment with anti-VEGF injections and focal macular laser in the current study cohort. Consistent with this finding, previous studies have shown that subthreshold micropulse laser therapy combined with anti-VEGF injections can reduce the total number of injections required, improve visual outcomes, and decrease CRT [24]. In the present study, a minority of cases (3.1%) were treated with intravitreal corticosteroids, although targeting inflammatory pathways with these agents has been shown to be effective in DME [25]. In line with this, a significant gain of 4.3 ETDRS letters was achieved in patients receiving intravitreal corticosteroids. However, it should be noted that the lower baseline BCVA in this group may have allowed for greater visual improvement compared with other treatment groups with higher baseline visual acuity. Only one third of the study participants were pseudophakic prior to the onset of DME. Corticosteroid-induced cataract formation, along with the almost invariably effective initiation of treatment with anti-VEGF agents, may explain the limited use of corticosteroids in the current cohort.

Patients with DME and relatively good baseline visual acuity may initially be managed with observation, with treatment initiated if visual symptoms such as metamorphopsia or decreased visual acuity develop. Previous studies have shown that favorable visual outcomes can be achieved with initial observation; however, greater baseline CRT, more severe DR, and bilateral DME have been associated with an increased risk of BCVA decline and the need for intravitreal treatment [26]. Nevertheless, delayed treatment may lead to fluctuations in CRT and poorer long-term visual outcomes [27, 28]. In line with these findings, the poorest visual outcomes in the present study were observed among patients managed without treatment. Factors associated with increased CRT fluctuations include worse baseline vision, baseline renal disease, hypertension, and deferred anti-VEGF therapy. In contrast, prompt anti-VEGF treatment has been shown to reduce CRT fluctuations, underscoring the importance of timely intervention in DME [27, 28].

Approximately 40% of the study participants with DME had residual edema after the treatment or observation period, despite generally favorable visual outcomes. Treatment with anti-VEGF agents, either alone or in combination with macular laser, appeared to be effective in reducing intraretinal fluid and CRT, whereas residual edema was observed in 80.6% of patients treated with intravitreal corticosteroids, and in 54.7% of those managed with observation alone. Although evidence regarding the impact of residual fluid on visual acuity in DME remains limited, a recent study suggests that more aggressive fluid resolution may be beneficial [29]. In this context, the frequent use of anti-VEGF therapy, either alone or in combination with macular laser, in the treatment of DME appears to be justified.

It is well established that poor glycemic control, hypertension, and diabetic kidney disease are significant predictors and risk factors for DME in patients with T2D [30, 31]. A recent study, however, reported that systemic markers of dysmetabolism, such as obesity and dyslipidemia, were not associated with an increased risk of DME development in patients with diabetes [32]. Consistent with previous findings, 93.0% of participants in the current study were receiving medication for arterial hypertension, the mean HbA1c level at DME onset was elevated to 64.3 mmol/mol, and 47.6% had diabetic nephropathy. Despite advances in ocular treatment, it remains essential that patients with T2D and DME - who often have multiple comorbidities - maintain adequate glycemic control, lipid levels, and blood pressure [27].

The retrospective nature of the present study represents a limitation, although it also enables the evaluation of treatment outcomes in a real-world setting. Participants were identified from a heterogeneous patient population, and several cases were excluded due to potential confounding factors that could have influenced visual acuity, such as other significant ocular diseases or concomitant anti-VEGF treatment for active PDR. In addition, treatment decisions for DME were made by the treating physicians based on clinical assessment of visual acuity, severity of DR, CRT, and other OCT parameters; thus, some variability in management may have occurred among retina specialists despite established clinical guidelines. Baseline confounders were adjusted for age, sex, and BCVA; however, minor differences between participants who continued treatment and those who discontinued may have introduced bias into the results. Nevertheless, the population-based design and the long duration of real-world follow-up can be considered major strengths of this study. Furthermore, the inclusion of only patients with T2D and DME represents an additional strength, as many previous studies have included both T1D and T2D populations. Given that demographic characteristics and risk factors for DR and DME differ between these groups, combining them may introduce bias.

The goal of optimal DME management is to maximize therapeutic benefits while minimizing risks and treatment burden. Although anti-VEGF therapies have markedly advanced the treatment of DME, the challenge of incomplete responders and the impact of residual edema on long-term visual outcomes remain critical areas of concern. Further research, along with individualized treatment strategies, is essential to improve outcomes in this subgroup of patients.

Conclusions

Our results indicate that anti-VEGF agents, either alone or combined with macular laser, appear to be effective in both visual improvement and edema reduction. Further studies are needed to establish whether combination therapy can reduce the frequency of anti-VEGF injections and thus lower treatment burden and costs in the growing population of patients with T2D.
