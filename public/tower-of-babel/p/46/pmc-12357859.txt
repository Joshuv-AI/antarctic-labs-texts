Neural transmission in the wired brain, new insights into an encoding-decoding-based neuronal communication model
Authors: Sivan Kinreich
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBrain activity is known to be rife with oscillatory activity in different frequencies, which are suggested to be associated with intra-brain communication. However, the specific role of frequencies in neuronal information transfer is still an open question. To this end, we utilized EEG resting state recordings from 5 public datasets. Overall, data from 1668 participants, including people with MDD, ADHD, OCD, Parkinson’s, Schizophrenia, and healthy controls aged 5–89, were part of the study. We conducted a running window of Spearman correlation between the two frontal hemispheres’ Alpha envelopes. The results of this analysis revealed a unique pattern of correlation states alternating between fully synchronized and desynchronized several times per second, likely due to the interference pattern between two signals of slightly different frequencies, also named “Beating”. Subsequent analysis showed this unique pattern in every pair of ipsilateral/contralateral, across frequencies, either in eyes closed or open, and across all ages, underscoring its inherent significance. Biomarker analysis revealed significantly lower synchronization and higher desynchronization for people older than 50 compared to younger ones and lower ADHD desynchronization compared to age-matched controls. Importantly, we propose a new brain communication model in which frequency modulation creates a binary message encoded and decoded by brain regions for information transfer. We suggest that the binary-like pattern allows the neural information to be coded according to certain physiological and biological rules known to both the sender and recipient. This digital-like scheme has the potential to be exploited in brain-computer interaction and applied technologies such as robotics.Subject terms: Diagnostic markers, Physiology, Neuroscience

Introduction

The research on the relationship between recorded brain oscillations, brain functional connectivity, and mental activity is a cornerstone of neuroscience, providing insights into the mechanisms underlying cognition, behavior, and mental health [1–3]. In essence, the knowledge from oscillations studies is not just about understanding the brain, it’s about harnessing this knowledge to improve human health, learning, and quality of life [4]. Yet, the role of brain oscillations in intra-brain communication (IBC) remains a profound enigma within neuroscience, serving as a pivotal yet unresolved facet in comprehending brain function.

The research over the years of neural interaction, which is based on scalp signal recording and processing, used various mathematical methods [5]. Some of these methods involve traditional approaches such as correlation or coherence [6, 7], while others use more complex methods, such as Granger causality [8], transfer entropy [9], and graph theory [10]. These studies incorporated either data- or task-driven approaches to reveal brain topography connectivity of the neural networks and the associated signal measurements. Studies delving into the details of the neural information being communicated focused on micro and macro levels. While the mechanisms underlying signal transmission between neurons through action potentials and synapses are extensively documented at the cell level [11], the intricacies of the processes governing both short and long-distance communication of multiple neurons persist as elusive frontiers.

Previous studies and review publications presenting a theoretical framework of information transfer between brain regions were primarily based on neuronal oscillations and spike (action potential) frequencies recorded from an intracerebral microelectrode or the scalp [12–14]. When recorded from the intracerebral microelectrode, the two main theoretical models of neuronal information transfer based on neuronal spikes are “rate coding” and “temporal coding“ [15]. While the former proposes that information is encoded in neuronal firing rates, the latter suggests that information is encoded in the timing of neuronal activity rather than solely in the rate of spiking [16]. The temporal method leverages the precise timing of action potentials, patterns, or inter-spike intervals to represent sensory [17], motor [18], or cognitive information [19]. For example, a neuron may respond to a sensory stimulus with spikes occurring at specific delays that encode the stimulus’ properties, or spikes occur at specific phases of an ongoing oscillation [20]. Other spike properties associated with mental activity include specific temporal sequences in populations of neurons [21] or the time intervals between consecutive spikes [22].

When recorded from the scalp, brain electrical activity reflects the activity of many neurons in the depth of the cortex [23]. This collective neuronal behavior was proposed as a potential explanation for neural communication [24]. Collective activity is based on the concept that one neuron in the oscillating network cannot be singled out as the pace leader. Instead, a large number of principal cells and interneurons contribute to the formation of any single oscillator [24] and that small and large networks produce the same type of rhythm [24]. It implies that the macroscopic brain recording, such as Electroencephalogram (EEG) patterns, describes the large-scale function of neuronal networks as a unified whole, independent of the details of the dynamic processes occurring at the subunits that comprise the whole [25, 26]. Synchrony (higher degree of correlation) between neuronal activity or brain areas recorded by neuroimaging methods such as Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging (fMRI), EEG, or Magnetoencephalography (MEG) occurs when some triggers bring the oscillations together in a given temporal window [25, 27].

During the synchronization process, it is assumed that the brain’s signal flow is regulated by specific neuronal inhibitory-excitatory interacting circuits, and the temporary silencing of excitatory neurons to create shorter message frames [12, 28]. Building on the former assumption, it was proposed that information from one brain area to another occurs in small temporal packages at a certain phase of the oscillatory cycle [29]. Specifically, multiple key properties of oscillations, such as phase-phase, phase-amplitude, and amplitude-amplitude couplings, were found to be associated with neuronal functional connectivity during different cognitive and mental tasks [30]. While these features have been proposed as a communication channel between distant brain regions [28, 31], concrete evidence and the exact neuronal mechanisms by which brain networks transmit information are still unclear.

The aim of the present work was to investigate novel neuronal communication patterns in healthy people and people suffering from brain disorders based on EEG signals. Specifically, we hypothesized that the amplitude of certain frequencies may serve as channels of communication across long-distance brain regions, mainly by studying inter-hemispheric brain communication. While not part of the current study, other neural signal properties, such as phase, can also be used to transmit data and are important future targets in research on neural communication in the brain. Communication between the hemispheres, in which processed information is shared and integrated, is done through several commissural routes, including the corpus callosum, the anterior and posterior commissures, the hippocampal commissure, or, indirectly, via subcortical connections [32–34]. Studies found that sharing information between the two hemispheres is highly important for maintaining cognitive and mental performance [35].

To investigate the communication between the hemispheres, we utilized EEG recording. The scalp EEG recording lacks the spatial precision of MRI scanning but has the advantage of capturing the immediate changes associated with neuronal activity at a resolution of several milliseconds [36]. The captured instantaneous neuronal signal makes it possible to study the connection and correlation between the activity of brain regions [36]. Our study focused on the resting state paradigm as the basis for establishing a ‘baseline’ neuronal communication, which can subsequently be compared with activity during tasks [37]. The Resting-state neuronal activity is commonly used to understand baseline brain function and intrinsic connectivity networks [38], and has been linked to a range of physiological and cognitive processes, as demonstrated by age-related, developmental, and clinical studies [14, 39, 40], revealing primary networks such as the visual network (VN) and the default mode network (DMN) [41]. Five public datasets of resting-state EEG recordings were part of this study. The overall sample comprises participants with major depressive disorder (MDD), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), Schizophrenia, and Parkinson’s disease, as well as healthy individuals, to evaluate the newly revealed communication patterns as a biomarker for brain function and brain disorders.

Materials and methods

Datasets

This study included five public datasets of eyes-open and closed resting-state EEG recordings.

All studies were approved by an ethical committee, and informed consent was obtained from all subjects. Datasets included “The two decades brainclinics research archive for insights in neurophysiology (TDBRAIN)“ [42], “Stimulus-Selective Response Modulation (SRM)“ [43], “Multi-modal Open Dataset for Mental-disorder Analysis (MODMA)” [44], ‘Rest eyes open’ (REO) [45], and “First Episode Schizophrenia Psychosis (FEP)” [46] (Table S1). The initial cohort consisted of 1,668 participants aged 5–89 years. The cohort included healthy controls and people suffering from MDD, OCD, ADHD, Schizophrenia, and Parkinson’s (Table S2). Sex was not part of the analysis due to the small sample. More research is needed to confirm the results stratified by sex, specifically regarding the association between the synchronization variable and the psychiatric diagnosis. The demographic information of the study participants can be found in the supplementary file, including age and psychiatric diagnosis distribution.

EEG preprocessing and signal harmonization procedure

Matlab (Mathworks Inc, Natick, MA), Python, including packages from MNE [47], EEGLAB [48], and Fieldtrip toolbox for MATLAB [49], were used for all calculations.

The same preprocessing procedure was applied to all EEG resting state datasets: First, two minutes of signal were extracted (Two minutes was the longest period that could be extracted from all the public databases that participated in the current study). We excluded the first 5 s in the analysis to avoid the initial effect of closing the eyes [50]. Next, each electrode was re-referencing according to the average to ensure similarity in referencing. Denoising analysis for muscle and electrical effects included removal of low pass 60 Hz, removal of notch 60 Hz, removal of notch 50 Hz (to ensure similarity for datasets from Europe), and high pass 0.1 Hz to remove slow drift in the recording [51]. The time-frequency representation of each of the electrodes’ continuous EEG recordings was calculated using the Stockwell transform [52] with a time resolution of 0.002 s and a frequency resolution of 0.3 Hz., to create the common frequencies power modulation over time (Delta, Theta, Alpha, Beta, Gamma). Next, the frequencies were averaged over electrodes for each lobe (Frontal, Temporal, Parietal, and Occipital, see Fig. S1 for electrodes and lobes) to create one time-frequency power modulation over time for each of them (for example, the right frontal includes the average of the frequency power modulation of F4 and F8). In the last step, the upper envelope of the spectral signal (separately for each frequency time course) was calculated using the Hilbert function and downsampled to 100 per second (Fig. 1B,C) to ensure a similar frequency across all datasets.

Synchronization and desynchronization

We aimed to investigate the neuronal synchronization between the hemispheres, focusing on the frontal lobe, which has been found to be associated with resting state activity [37, 53] (i.e., contralateral, frontal-frontal) and between lobes of the same hemisphere (ipsilateral, left frontal-left parietal, left frontal-left temporal, left frontal-left occipital). Spearman correlation [54] was used for the calculation of synchronization between the lobes (the frequency envelopes of each lobe). We employed a sliding window technique for each frequency envelope to compute correlations, tracking the functional connectivity dynamics between contralateral/ipsilateral lobes over time.

Using a window size of 10-time points and a step of 1, we recorded the Spearman correlation [54] values, yielding 100 correlation values per second. To further investigate the robustness of the correlation pattern, we executed the same correlation analysis with different parameter choices. The sliding window correlation was additionally done with one TDbrain participant using the original 500 Hz sampling rate (not downsampled) and with two window sizes of 10 and 30 sampling points. Our primary focus was on the Alpha frequency, given its demonstrated prominence as the primary frequency engaged during rest period (Fig. 1B) [55, 56].

Peak analysis of synchronization and desynchronization

To quantify the overall synchronization and desynchronization time (per second), the percentage time of synchronization and desynchronization was computed for every second for each participant and then averaged across seconds and participants. The calculation was conducted for any correlation value above 0 f(x) >0 (synchronization) or any correlation value below zero f(x)<0 (desynchronization) (Fig. S2) separately.

Peak analysis

A second calculation for a deeper understanding of the synchronization state was done by conducting peak analysis. Amplitude peak detection analyses are commonly used as part of the EEG feature extraction process [57] and are applied as part of brain disorder analysis [58]. We quantified the correlation peak values by setting the thresholds of ‘full synchronization’ as f(x) >0.98 and ‘full desynchronization’ f(x)<−0.98 (Fig. S2) to identify the most highly correlated times. These two values (times of full synchronization and times of full desynchronization) were part of the following analyses quantifying synchronization time:

Peak analysis (frequencies)

In the first peak analysis, we investigated the sync performance of Alpha versus all other frequencies in healthy participants. This analysis aimed to establish a full profile of the neuronal synchronization phenomenon across frequencies during the resting state paradigm. Frontal Alpha’s synchronization/desynchronization times were compared with all the other Frontal frequencies (Delta, Theta, Beta, Gamma). Two-tailed t-tests were applied to assess the significance of the differences between the frontal contralateral Alpha’s full synchronization and desynchronization times (the averaged time per second, per participant) and the synchronization time of the other frequencies (Delta, Theta, Beta, and Gamma). Data included: SRM dataset (healthy participants), N = 109, average age = 37 ± 13.9. (Tables S2/S3).

Peak analysis (contralateral and ipsilateral)

In a second peak analysis with healthy participants, Alpha’s synchronization/desynchronization time difference between the frontal contralateral and the ipsilateral lobes (frontal-temporal, frontal-parietal, and frontal-occipital) was assessed. The purpose of the analysis was to investigate frontal synchronization performance during rest compared with the other brain lobes. Two-tailed t-tests were applied to assess the significance of the differences between the frontal contralateral Alpha’s full synchronization and desynchronization times (the average time per second per participant) and the ipsilateral lobes.

Data included: TDbrain, SRM, and REO datasets, N = 174, average age = 31.4 ± 13.5 (Tables S2/S3).

Peak analysis (contralateral occipital lobes)

Additional peak analysis repeated the above steps to investigate the synchronization between the two hemispheric occipital lobes, focusing on their activity during rest, providing deeper insights into overall brain synchronization during rest. Data included: TDbrain, SRM, and REO datasets, N = 174, average age = 31.4 ± 13.5 (Tables S2/S3).

Peak analysis (age)

In the next peak analysis, we investigated the frontal Alpha synchronization/desynchronization time across the lifespan to understand the effect of age on hemispheric neuronal communication during eyes closed and eyes open. The additional task of open eyes can provide a more comprehensive understanding of neural communication. A t-test was calculated to compare the two groups (eyes open, eyes closed) across the life span (the groups were age-matched). A follow-up analysis of age differences stratified the eyes-closed group by age (<20, 20–50, >50). The age range groups were based on previous studies distinguishing between the teen brain, the adult brain, and the aging brain [59, 60]. (The age analysis was conducted only with the eyes-closed group because there were insufficient participants in the eyes-open group for age stratification). One-way ANOVA was applied, followed by post-hoc T-tests to study the differences between the groups.

Data included: TDbrain, SRM, MODMA, FEP, and REO datasets, N = 291 (Tables S2/S3).

Peak analysis (brain disorders)

In the next analysis, we aimed to assess synchronization time as a biomarker for brain disorder classification. We compared Alpha synchronization when the eyes were closed in people with MDD, ADHD, and OCD to healthy controls and Schizophrenia and Parkinson’s while the eyes were opened. For this comparison, we used logistic regression, adjusting for collection site (for analyses where there was more than one site) and sex. Additionally, the similarity within groups for analyses that include more than one dataset was confirmed by comparing the datasets’ means (using a t-test for two groups and a one-way ANOVA for three groups, as shown in Table S4). Details on the number of participants and their ages according to analysis are in Table S3. The Beating phenomenon

To understand the interpolation between the envelope correlation in-depth, we delved deeper into the Beating phenomenon [61]. In the physical realm, a Beating is an interference pattern between two signals of slightly different frequencies, perceived as a periodic variation in volume or synchronization of the envelope of the signals (Fig. 2A) [61]. The exceptional Beating patterns are expressed in alternating states of 0 and 1 that can be perceived as binary code. The unique correlation pattern found during the calculations between distant brain locations, such as the two frontal hemispheres, resembles the results of the Beating phenomenon (Fig. 2).

Results

Communication (synchronization and desynchronization) between the contralateral lobes across hemispheres was studied by calculating the correlation between the Alpha-frequency envelopes. Fig. 3 shows the unique patterns of the Alpha correlation between the frontal lobes in three participants, each from a different dataset. The individual Alpha signals are presented in Fig. 3A, where clear synchronization and desynchronization times are colored blue and red. The revealed neuronal communication exhibits continuous oscillations between full synchronization and full desynchronization, with approximately 2–5 transitions every second (Fig. 3B). The same pattern can be seen in one participant in ten continuous seconds (Fig. 3C). Alternate synchronization and desynchronization also appeared when the original signal was used (not downsampled), and different window sizes were assigned in the sliding window analysis (Fig. S3), confirming the robustness of this pattern, which resembles the Beating phenomenon. The Beating phenomenon of superposition of two sinusoidal waves with very similar frequencies creates the alternating dichotomous states of synchronization and desynchronization (Fig. 2). In our analysis, the Beating phenomenon may stem from the dynamic interplay of distinct frequencies contributing to the Alpha band (such as 8–10 Hz). Intriguingly, the pattern resembles a sequence of 0 and 1 s reminiscent of a binary code (Fig. 2C).

Next, we set out to understand whether this unique pattern of neuronal synchronization occurs in other frequencies as well. For that, we calculated the average time per second spent during synchronization and desynchronization of Spearman correlation between the frontal lobes for each of the other frequencies (Delta, Theta, Beta, and Gamma)(Fig. 4). The results of the cumulative synchronization time (Fig. 4 upper panel) indicated that during rest, communication between the frontal lobes at all frequencies was about 50% of the time in synchronization and 50% of the time in desynchronization, except for Alpha (Fig. 4 middle panel). T-tests were applied to compare Alpha synchronization and desynchronization time (per second) with each of the other frequencies, showing significantly higher Alpha levels of synchronization (p < 0.001 for Theta, Beta, and Gamma when compared to Alpha) and significantly lower levels of desynchronization (p < 0.001, for Theta, Beta, Gamma when compared to Alpha) (Fig. 4 middle panel).

The results of the peak analysis (Fig. 4 upper panel) of full synchronization/desynchronization were more distinctive. During rest, frontal Alpha showed significantly higher full synchronization (30% of the time) than the other frequencies (p < 0.001 for Theta, Beta, and Gamma when compared to Alpha) (Fig. 4, lower panel). Significantly lower full desynchronization Alpha was also detected (p < 0.001 for Theta, Beta, and Gamma when compared to Alpha) (Fig. 4). As expected, the higher frequencies (Beta and Gamma) showed lower values of full synchronization or desynchronization time than the lower frequencies (Delta, Theta, and Alpha, Fig. 4 lower panel).

Next, we investigated whether this unique pattern of neuronal synchronization (alternating between full synchronization and synchronization) also occurs between the frontal lobe and the ipsilateral lobes (Temporal, Parietal, and Occipital) (Fig. S4). Results showed that these brain areas were fully synchronized on average ~30% of the time, and on average ~18% were fully desynchronized (Fig. S4). T-tests revealed that synchronization was significantly higher in frontal-frontal than frontal-temporal (p < 0.001) and frontal-parietal (p < 0.01) communication for full synchronization (Fig. S4). Desynchronization was significantly lower between frontal-frontal, frontal-temporal and frontal-parietal (p < 0.001). Occipital-frontal synchronization/desynchronization showed similar results to frontal-frontal. In a follow-up analysis focusing on the occipital lobe, it was found that the synchronization between the contralateral occipital lobes is significantly higher than between the frontal lobes, and the contralateral desynchronization of the occipital lobes is found to be significantly lower than between the frontal lobes. (Fig. S5).

We compared frontal Alpha synchronization in people with MDD, ADHD, OCD, Parkinson’s, and Schizophrenia to healthy controls using logistic regression. Discussion

Communication within the brain is incredibly complex and occurs at multiple levels, from individual neurons to large-scale neuronal networks. Understanding how the brain communicates is essential for unraveling its mysteries and developing treatments for neurological disorders. Here, we present new insights into brain communication based on scalp EEG recording. We found that the amplitude of the Alpha rhythm alternates between full synchronization and full desynchronization amongst brain areas, with eyes closed or open, a phenomenon evident throughout the participants’ lifespan, across frequencies. In contralateral frontal lobes communication (closed-eyes Alpha), the transitions between complete synchronization and desynchronization happen 2–5 times a second. Furthermore, ~30% of the time, the amplitude of the Alpha rhythm across the contralateral frontals and the ipsilateral fronto-occipital lobes was fully synchronized. This unique pattern may be related to the visual process of the thalamocortical pathways. Of particular interest, Alpha frontal interhemispheric synchronization and desynchronization were found to be significantly lower for people older than 50 years old compared to younger ones and desynchronization was significantly lower in ADHD compared to matched-age healthy control. Finally, we present a theoretical model where neuronal communication is expressed by utilizing the “Beating” phenomenon to modulate frequencies and create signal patterns resembling a binary code of 0 and 1 s to encode and decode information between brain source and destination. This model is based on wired transmission principles that involve the modification of frequencies that can potentially be employed in a brain-computer interface (BCI).

Neuronal synchronization between brain regions is generally considered necessary for functional connectivity and the transfer of neuronal information. The unique pattern of alternating states between full synchronization and full desynchronization might play a fundamental role in data transfer.

New insights into the wired brain communication theoretical model of encoding and decoding information

Our results showed that the correlation between the Alpha envelope of two distant brain locations (recorded signals from two distant EEG electrodes) depicts alternating states of full synchronization and desynchronization at every second. An intriguing idea would be to suggest that frequency modulations might be used as a communication channel between brain regions. Here, we propose a theoretical model in which a neuronal network may utilize the “Beating” phenomenon within the Alpha frequency band to transmit information between long and short-distance partners by manipulating almost similar frequencies to create a digital message of 0 and 1 s. Most data transfer in today’s electronic networks (radio, internet, media) works under the same principle [62]. At one end, a transmitter “encodes” or modulates messages by varying the amplitude or frequency of the wave [62]. On the other end, a receiver tuned to the same wavelength picks up the signal and ‘decodes’ it back to the desired form: sounds, images, data, etc. In telecommunication, the frequencies are used as information carriers in various applications and enable communication, sensing, visualization, and navigation between different domains [62]. Our results, which describe correlations between brain regions, demonstrate continuous alternating states of 0 and 1 s (synchronization and desynchronization), suggesting that brain communication may use some of the embedded rules of wired electrical transmission to transfer information between brain areas. We assert that our neuronal transfer model employs the same embedded rules as the wired data, where data is typically transmitted in packets according to an embedded clock (Fig. 6A). Each packet contains description bytes such as packet length/checksum/status/etc [62]. Specifically, the model suggests that two brain regions utilize their neural activity to transmit and receive information by modulating the neural frequency that is translated into binary code. The binary code contains data and metadata and is transmitted according to biological and logical rules shared and recognized by both the sending and receiving brain regions. (Fig. 6A, B). The encoding and decoding process (frequency modulation) is perceived as an alternating state of synchronization and desynchronization between the two communicators. In this manuscript, we address the terms encoding and decoding in the context of embedding information in binary code. Multiple neurons control the encoded binary code by generating and modulating frequencies. Similar to wired electronic transmission, the binary code, generated during the encoding process, is used as a means of transmitting (sending and receiving) information across brain areas. This conceptual framework supports the previous neuronal transmission theoretical models [12, 24, 30, 63], where neuronal information is packed into small frames and transmitted under rules known by both parties [28]. This digital-like scheme has the potential to be exploited in brain-computer interaction (BCI) and applied technologies such as robotics and neuroprosthetics, which work under the same principles.

Synchronization and desynchronization patterns as a biomarker for aging and brain disorders

The quantification of the unique phenomenon of alternating states of synchronization and desynchronization was found to distinguish people over 50 years old from younger ones, showing lower Alpha synchronization and higher desynchronization time between the frontal hemispheres. These results are in line with previous EEG studies showing age-related reduced functional connectivity in adults during a resting state paradigm [64]. The study of brain signals as a biomarker or index of age-related brain changes includes study fields such as cognitive decline and sleep quality [65]. Findings from the age-related assessment of signal fluctuations, connectivity, and ERP components were found to be related to mental abilities, mild cognitive impairment, and dementia [66, 67]. The results suggest slower oscillations with age, manifested in the fact that Alpha and Beta wave activity tends to decrease with age, while Theta and Delta activity may increase [68]. In a recent study with healthy individuals, the authors applied graph theory algorithms to EEG resting state data to investigate the effect of aging on network topology [66]. The results showed significantly reduced network topology features in the elderly group, including local efficiency, global efficiency, and clustering coefficient with eyes-open, eyes-closed, and visual working memory task states [66]. Specifically, older adults show a decline in global functional connectivity across multiple frequency bands, particularly in the Alpha and Beta bands [69, 70], diminished resting-state slow-wave phase synchronization [71], and reduction in interhemispheric coherence connectivity, particularly in frontal and temporal regions [72]. In general, these findings indicate that over the years and into old age, there are morphological changes in the brain that are captured by the externally recorded neuronal activity [73]. The reduced synchronization and increased desynchronization findings in older age presented in this manuscript can add further insight into understanding brain age-related transformation. Similarly, abnormal EEG functional connectivity was found in previous studies for various brain disorders (i.e., stroke, depression, emotional disorders, epilepsy, ADHD, autism, and Alzheimer’s disease) [74]. Our findings indicate a significant desynchronization difference in the biomarker comparison between individuals with ADHD and healthy age-matched controls. Previous studies of ADHD functional connectivity have found reduced long-range connectivity, particularly involving the prefrontal cortex and posterior parietal regions [75] and EEG graph-theoretical analyses showed disrupted small-world properties, particularly in frontal-parietal networks [76]. Altered brain functional connectivity based on EEG has been shown in individuals with ADHD both at a young age and in adults [77]. Our results of ADHD lower frontal Alpha inter-hemispheric desynchronization add to these findings, underscoring the need for further validation given the small sample size.

Alpha synchronization and the visual system

Our analysis of the EEG recordings during closed eyes showed frontal and occipital Alpha rhythm amplitude synchronization (both ipsilateral and contralateral). Previous evidence suggests that frontal and occipital neural synchronization is associated with visual activity during resting state, which involves eye movement and imaging processing [78]; an activity that is predominantly generated in the thalamocortical pathways [78–80]. These pathways are considered the main route of communication between the eye and the cerebral cortex, including the occipital area (visual cortex) and the pre-frontal area [80, 81]. Some in-vitro studies of thalamic nuclei in cats have found that a subset of the relay cells in the lateral geniculate nucleus (LGN) have the ability to oscillate at Alpha frequencies through high-threshold bursting and suggested that these cells are a source of Alpha activity in V1 [81–83]. Another more recent study used micro- and macro-electrodes in epilepsy patients to measure the intracortical and thalamic generators of the Alpha rhythm during quiet wakefulness [55]. They found that Alpha in both visual and somatosensory cortex propagates from higher-order to lower-order areas [55]. In the posterior cortex, Alpha propagates from higher-order anterosuperior areas toward the occipital pole, whereas Alpha in the somatosensory cortex propagates from associative regions toward the primary cortex [55]. Overall, our data add to this accumulating evidence pointing to the involvement of the visual network during resting state brain activity and specifically to the direct communication between the frontal and the occipital ipsilateral and contralateral hemispheres.

Understanding how the brain encodes and decodes new data to transmit information across regions is critical for advancements in basic neuroscience research, medicine, and applied technologies such as BCIs, robotics, and neuroprosthetics. Here, we present a new neuronal coding method based on the Beating phenomenon and frequency modulation to create a digital binary transmission of 0 and 1 s. Furthermore, the results of the Beating phenomenon create a unique synchronization and desynchronization pattern between brain areas that can be used as a biomarker in age and brain disorder studies. Further research is needed to understand the depth and complexity of the binary code principles of neuronal transmission.

Study limitations and future directions

The model we present is limited by the extent of brain activity (resting state) and the EEG frequencies (Alpha) involved in this human function. Another limitation is the small size of the dataset. Future work by us and others needs to investigate this phenomenon further in various cognitive and emotional tasks reflecting a wide range of frequencies, other signal properties, and brain regions, while considering sex differences. Furthermore, due to EEG’s spatial limitation in identifying specific brain regions involved in neuronal communication, the model is limited to a theoretical description that still needs confirmation. It is important to emphasize that the neuronal transmission model proposed in this manuscript is purely a theoretical interpretation of the Beating phenomenon and lacks more local neuronal empirical evidence.
