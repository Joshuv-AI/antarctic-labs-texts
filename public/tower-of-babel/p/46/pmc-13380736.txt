Effects of ultrasound-assisted fermentation at different power levels on fermentation quality and nutritional value of a corn protein powder-wheat bran mixture
Authors: Shun Cui, Shuai Zhang, Yu Ding, Qiyue Wu, Xueqi Yu, Qining Li, Xin Zheng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Corn gluten meal (CGM) and wheat bran are widely recognized as two major by-products generated by the food-processing industry. The corn gluten meal–wheat bran mixture (CWM) was fermented using Aspergillus niger and Lactobacillus fermentum, and the potential of ultrasound-assisted fermentation to enhance its value was evaluated. Five fermented corn gluten meal–wheat bran mixtures (FCWMs) were prepared by applying ultrasound at 0, 130, 195, 260, and 320 W for 5 min. The results indicated that ultrasound-assisted fermentation at 195 W yielded the most favorable effects. At this power level, microbial biomass was increased, while high-molecular-weight proteins were effectively degraded, resulting in substantial increases in trichloroacetic acid-soluble protein (TCA-SP) and total free amino acid (TFAA) contents. Furthermore, the levels of beneficial metabolites, including total phenolics, flavonoids, and carotenoids, were elevated. Concurrently, antinutritional factor levels were reduced, and antioxidant capacity was enhanced.

Introduction

The rapid expansion of agricultural production and food processing is driven by continued growth in global food demand and the world population. Consequently, large quantities of agricultural by-products and food-processing residues, including wheat bran and corn gluten meal (CGM), are generated [1], [2], [3]. CGM was produced as a major by-product of industrial corn starch processing. Owing to its protein content of up to 60 %, CGM was considered to have substantial nutritional potential [4]. However, its limited water solubility and unbalanced amino acid profile, particularly its deficiencies in lysine and tryptophan, restrict its direct use in food and feed applications [4]. Wheat bran is a fibrous by-product obtained from the outer tissues of wheat kernels during milling. It is rich in dietary fiber, B vitamins, and minerals; however, its high insoluble fiber content and compact cell wall structure limit the release and bioavailability of these nutrients [5]. In this study, wheat bran was used as the primary source of fermentable fiber and structural carbohydrates, whereas corn gluten meal provided nitrogenous compounds and proteins. Their combination provided a nutritionally balanced substrate that supported microbial growth and metabolic activity [9].

Microbial fermentation was extensively investigated as an effective strategy for improving the nutritional quality and digestibility of agro-industrial by-products [10]. Various microorganisms, including Aspergillus, Bacillus, and lactic acid bacteria, were employed to hydrolyze proteins and polysaccharides in CGM and wheat bran [4]. Previous studies demonstrated that the levels of soluble protein, free amino acids, and bioactive compounds were increased by fermentation, whereas antinutritional factor levels were reduced [5], [6]. Enzymatic hydrolysis was also employed to improve protein digestibility and release bioactive peptides. However, the effectiveness of these approaches was often constrained by the compact structure of plant cell walls and limited substrate accessibility [5], [6].

Ultrasound has emerged as a promising green processing technology for enhancing fermentation performance [7]. Acoustic cavitation induced by ultrasound generates mechanical, thermal, and sonochemical effects that can modify substrate structure, mass transfer, enzyme activity, and microbial physiology [8]. Depending on the ultrasound intensity and treatment duration, the resulting biological effects range from mild cellular stimulation to cell damage [9]. Low-intensity ultrasound can increase cell membrane permeability, stimulate microbial metabolic activity, enhance mass transfer, and promote enzyme secretion. Previous studies demonstrated that protein hydrolysis, phenolic release, antioxidant activity, and substrate degradation were enhanced by ultrasound-assisted fermentation in various agricultural substrates [10]. Nevertheless, excessive ultrasound intensity may compromise microbial viability and fermentation stability. Therefore, optimization of the ultrasound treatment conditions remains essential.

Our previous studies demonstrated that the nutritional quality of CWM was enhanced by the complementary enzymatic activities of Aspergillus niger and Lactobacillus fermentum [4]. Proteins and fibers were degraded by extracellular enzymes secreted by Aspergillus niger, resulting in increased protein solubility, reduced levels of antinutritional factors, and improved nutrient bioavailability [11]. Meanwhile, proteins were further hydrolyzed by extracellular enzymes secreted by Lactobacillus fermentum, resulting in increased TFAA and peptide contents [12]. The pH of the fermentation system was lowered by the lactic acid produced, thereby creating an antimicrobial environment that helped maintain the stability of the co-culture [13]. The inherent limitations of CWM were partially mitigated by these synergistic effects, thereby enhancing its nutritional value, functional properties, and potential for use as a feed ingredient. Therefore, the ultrasound-assisted co-fermentation of CGM and wheat bran with Aspergillus niger and Lactobacillus fermentum was investigated. Different ultrasound power levels were applied, and their effects on protein-related properties, total free amino acid content, bioactive compound levels, antioxidant capacity, and antinutritional factor levels were evaluated. This study was designed to provide a scientific and technical basis for improving CWM utilization and developing value-added feed ingredients from agro-industrial by-products.

Materials and methods

Microbial strains

A strain of Lactobacillus fermentum isolated from bovine rumen and a strain of Aspergillus niger isolated from fermented distillers' grains were selected for this study. The microorganisms used in this study were confirmed to be nonpathogenic by the China Center of Industrial Culture Collection. Potato dextrose agar (PDA; Solarbio) plates were inoculated with Aspergillus niger and incubated at 30 °C for 7 days. Spores were dislodged from the plate surfaces with saline and collected in centrifuge tubes. After vortexing, the spore concentration was determined using a Neubauer hemocytometer under an Olympus BX63 microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and adjusted to 1 × 10⁶ spores/mL to prepare the Aspergillus niger inoculum. The Lactobacillus fermentum inoculum was prepared by cultivation in de Man–Rogosa–Sharpe (MRS) broth (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA) at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 48 h. The cell concentration was then adjusted to 1 × 10⁶ CFU/mL, and the resulting inoculum was stored at 4 °C until use.

Ultrasound-assisted fermentation procedure

CGM and wheat bran were ground, passed through a 40-mesh sieve, and dried at 50 °C to a constant weight. Subsequently, 60 g of CGM and 40 g of wheat bran were combined at a mass ratio of 3:2 and transferred to a 500-mL Erlenmeyer flask. Distilled water was then added at a material-to-water ratio of 1:2 (w/w), followed by the addition of the prepared Aspergillus niger and Lactobacillus fermentum inocula. The mixture was fermented aerobically at 30 °C for 6 days in a flask covered with a sterile breathable membrane. On the third day of fermentation, the mixture was ultrasonically treated for 5 min using a probe-type ultrasonic processor (20 kHz, Shanghai Huxi Analytical Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) equipped with a 10-mm-diameter probe. Before each treatment, the probe was disinfected with 75 % ethanol. The probe was positioned 2 cm below the sample surface without contacting the wall or bottom of the flask. Ultrasound was applied at power levels of 0 W, 130 W, 195 W, 260 W, and 320 W in pulse mode (1 s on, 1 s off). Pulsed ultrasound treatment was chosen to maintain the ultrasound-induced cavitation effect while mitigating heat buildup and reducing the risk of ultrasound-induced damage to microbial cells [19]. At the end of fermentation, a portion of each wet sample was collected, whereas the remaining sample was dried at 55 °C for 24 h, cooled, and ground. The processed samples were subsequently stored in an ultra-low-temperature freezer until further analysis.

Microorganisms and chemical analysis

Wet samples were mixed with sterile saline at a sample-to-saline ratio of 1:9 (w/v) and shaken at 100 rpm and 37 °C for 15 min using an orbital shaker (RADOBIO Stab M1, Shanghai, China). The pH of the resulting supernatant was measured using a pH meter (LSTAR1118, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Tenfold serial dilutions were prepared in sterile saline, and viable Lactobacillus fermentum counts were determined after incubation on MRS agar at 37 °C for 48 h [4]. Ergosterol content was determined according to a previously reported method [14]. TFAA were quantified using a commercial assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Crude fiber (CF), acid detergent fiber (ADF), neutral detergent fiber (NDF), and trichloroacetic acid-soluble protein (TCA-SP) contents were determined according to the corresponding AOAC Official Methods (AOAC, 2025). Lactic acid levels were assessed with a commercially available colorimetric kit supplied by Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). The amount of reducing sugars was estimated colorimetrically with DNS reagent according to a previously published procedure [11].

Electrophoresis and microstructure analysis

Protein profiles were analyzed by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS–PAGE) according to a previously established method [15]. The samples were mounted directly on conductive adhesive tape and sputter-coated with gold for 5 min to ensure complete surface coverage. The surface morphology and elemental composition of the samples were characterized using a scanning electron microscope (SEM; SU8600, Hitachi, Japan) equipped with an energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy detector (EDS; Octane Plus, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). SEM imaging and EDS analysis were performed at an accelerating voltage of 20 kV. SEM images were acquired at a working distance of 27 mm and a magnification of 50×, whereas EDS spectra were collected for 100 s. All measurements were conducted at 25 °C. The samples were stained with fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC) according to a previously reported method [5]. Protein distribution was subsequently visualized using a fluorescence microscope (IX71, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan).

Digestive enzyme activity

Crude enzyme extracts were prepared by mixing the samples with sterile phosphate-buffered saline at a sample-to-buffer ratio of 1:9 (w/v). Acid protease activity was measured using a commercial assay kit (Boxbio, Shanghai, China). Protease activity was expressed in units per gram (U/g), where one unit was defined as the amount of enzyme required to release 1 μmol of tyrosine from the substrate per minute under the specified assay conditions. Cellulase activity was determined according to a previously published method [16]. Lipase activity was measured using a commercial assay kit (Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China). Lipase activity was expressed in units per gram of sample (U/g), where one unit was defined as the amount of enzyme required to release 1 μmol of fatty acids from olive oil per minute under the specified assay conditions.

Anti-nutritional factors

Phytic acid levels were measured with the BC5840 kit supplied by Solarbio (Beijing, China). Trypsin inhibitor (TI) content was determined according to a previously reported method [17].

Carotenoid content

Carotenoids were extracted according to a previously reported method [18]. The samples were mixed with n-hexane at a sample-to-solvent ratio of 1:5 (w/v) and shaken at 100 rpm for 10 min using a temperature-controlled shaker (Jinghong, Shanghai, China). The mixtures were then centrifuged at 800 rpm and 4 °C for 15 min. The upper n-hexane phase was collected and brought to a final volume of 50 mL, after which the absorbance at 450 nm was measured using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Shanghai Precision & Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., China). Changes in carotene color among the treatment groups were evaluated according to a previously published method [4].

Determination of antioxidant activity

Antioxidant capacity was evaluated by measuring DPPH and ABTS radical-scavenging activities, hydroxyl radical-scavenging activity, and ferric-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) using commercial assay kits (Beyotime Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). Total phenolic and flavonoid contents were determined using commercial assay kits (Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Malondialdehyde (MDA) content was determined using a commercial assay kit (Solarbio, Beijing, China).

Statistical analysis

Results were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 26.0. Differences among treatment groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test for post hoc comparisons. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Figures were prepared using GraphPad Prism 10.6 (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results and discussion

Microbial and physicochemical properties

Ergosterol is the predominant sterol in fungal cell membranes and is widely used as a biomarker of fungal biomass [14]. Because Aspergillus niger was the only fungal species inoculated into the fermentation system, ergosterol content was used as an indirect measure of its biomass. As shown in Fig. 1B and C, Aspergillus niger biomass and viable Lactobacillus fermentum counts were significantly higher in all fermented groups than in the unfermented control (CON), indicating successful growth of both microorganisms during fermentation. Furthermore, compared with the nonsonicated fermentation group, the growth of both microorganisms was enhanced by ultrasound-assisted fermentation, with the greatest enhancement observed at 195 W. The stimulatory effect of ultrasound was likely attributable to the moderate cavitation and acoustic microstreaming generated during sonication. Under appropriate sonication conditions, transient increases in cell membrane permeability were likely induced, thereby facilitating nutrient uptake and metabolite exchange, while enhanced mass transfer increased substrate accessibility and microbial metabolic activity [6], [19], [20]. These effects likely enhanced microbial growth and substrate utilization following sonication. Accordingly, among the ultrasound power levels tested, the 195 W treatment resulted in the highest Aspergillus niger biomass and viable Lactobacillus fermentum count. However, both microbial growth indicators and overall fermentation performance decreased when the ultrasound power was increased to 260 and 320 W. This finding suggested that excessive acoustic cavitation generated stronger shear forces and localized pressure fluctuations and induced oxidative stress, which may have partially damaged microbial cells or impaired their physiological activity. Thus, under the conditions tested, 195 W appeared to provide an optimal balance between microbial stimulation and ultrasound-induced stress. Consistent with the microbial growth results, the highest lactic acid concentration and lowest pH were observed in the 195 W treatment group (Fig. 2A and B). Appropriate ultrasound pretreatment was reported to promote the rapid growth of Lactobacillus fermentum during fermentation, thereby increasing lactic acid production [21]. In addition, the reduction in antinutritional factor levels during fermentation may have been partly attributable to lactic acid production, thereby improving the nutritional value of FCWM [22]. Compared with the unfermented control, crude fiber (CF), acid detergent fiber (ADF), and neutral detergent fiber (NDF) contents were significantly reduced in all fermented groups, whereas reducing sugar content was significantly increased. Relative to the nonsonicated fermentation group, further reductions in fiber contents and an increase in reducing sugar content were observed following ultrasound-assisted fermentation. Among the ultrasound-treated groups, the greatest reductions in CF, ADF, and NDF contents and the highest reducing sugar content were observed at 195 W (Fig. 2C–F). Protein molecular weight profiles in FCWM following ultrasound-assisted fermentation were characterized by SDS–PAGE. The predominant proteins in CGM are alcohol-soluble zeins, mainly α-zein (approximately 19–24 kDa), β-zein (approximately 15 kDa), and γ-zein (approximately 27 kDa) [4], [23]. α-Zein accounts for approximately 90 % of the total zein fraction. Because of their limited water solubility, zein proteins have restricted potential for food and feed applications [24]. The protein profile of the unfermented control contained several bands spanning approximately 10–55 kDa. In contrast, the intensities of protein bands within this range were markedly reduced after fermentation and were further diminished by ultrasound-assisted fermentation. The greatest reduction in band intensity was observed in the 195 W treatment group. Our previous study demonstrated that TCA-SP and TFAA contents were increased by ultrasound-assisted fermentation, likely because of the hydrolysis of high-molecular-weight proteins, particularly corn zeins [4]. In a preliminary experiment, no marked protein degradation was observed after ultrasound treatment alone, without microbial inoculation or fermentation, compared with the untreated control. This result suggested that acoustic cavitation was not the primary driver of protein hydrolysis under the selected sonication conditions. Nevertheless, protein degradation may have been indirectly facilitated by ultrasound through disruption of the substrate structure, increased protein accessibility, and enhanced enzyme–substrate mass transfer. Therefore, the enhanced protein degradation observed in the ultrasound-assisted fermentation groups was likely attributable to microbial enzymatic hydrolysis facilitated by acoustic cavitation. In addition, complex macromolecules in agro-industrial by-products, including proteins and carbohydrates, were effectively degraded through the proteolytic and hydrolytic activities of Aspergillus niger and Lactobacillus fermentum [25], [26]. The enhanced microbial growth observed at moderate ultrasound power, particularly at 195 W, may have further promoted enzyme production and substrate utilization, thereby accelerating protein degradation and increasing TCA-SP and TFAA formation. However, the extent of protein degradation was reduced at the higher ultrasound power levels of 260 and 320 W (Fig. 2G). Compared with the CON, TCA-SP and TFAA contents in the nonsonicated fermentation group were 4.72- and 12.78-fold higher, respectively. Both TCA-SP and TFAA contents were further increased by ultrasound-assisted fermentation. The highest values were observed in the 195 W treatment group, in which TCA-SP and TFAA contents were 49 % and 9 % higher, respectively, than those in the nonsonicated fermentation group. However, slight decreases in both parameters were observed at 260 and 320 W (Fig. 2H and I). These observations were further supported by the SDS–PAGE profiles. Improved FCWM solubility was reported to be associated with increased TCA-SP and TFAA contents [16]. In summary, corn zeins were effectively degraded by ultrasound-assisted fermentation, resulting in increased TCA-SP and TFAA contents and improved nutritional quality of FCWM.

Ultrasound power is recognized as a key operational parameter governing energy input and cavitation behavior in sonochemical processes [7]. Accordingly, ultrasound power was selected as the primary experimental variable, whereas sonication time, probe immersion depth, pulse mode, and substrate loading were maintained constant to minimize potential confounding effects. Nevertheless, ultrasound power was recognized as only one of several parameters influencing ultrasound-assisted fermentation. Previous studies demonstrated that cavitation behavior, mass transfer, microbial activity, and enzymatic reactions were significantly influenced by treatment duration, pulse duty cycle, probe position, and substrate concentration [7]. Therefore, although 195 W was identified as the most favorable nominal ultrasound power under the conditions tested, the optimal combination of sonication parameters had yet to be established.

Morphological structure

The microstructure of the samples was examined by scanning electron microscopy (SEM). At 50 × magnification, the apparent particle size was markedly reduced in the nonsonicated fermentation group compared with the CON, and a further reduction was observed following ultrasound-assisted fermentation. Among the treatment groups, the smallest apparent particle size was observed at 195 W (Fig. 3A). The reduction in particle size was reported to enhance the solubility of FCWM [28]. These structural changes were consistent with the reductions in CF, NDF, and ADF contents and the increases in TCA-SP and TFAA contents observed following ultrasound-assisted fermentation. Furthermore, changes in the apparent size of protein aggregates in the samples were visualized using fluorescence microscopy. The apparent size of protein-rich structures was markedly reduced in the nonsonicated fermentation group compared with the CON, and a further reduction was observed following ultrasound-assisted fermentation. Among the treatment groups, the smallest protein-rich structures were observed at 195 W (Fig. 3B). Protein solubility and emulsifying activity were reported to be enhanced by a reduction in protein aggregate size [28]. These findings were consistent with the increased TCA-SP and TFAA contents and the degradation of corn zeins and high-molecular-weight proteins. Microbial growth, reproduction, and metabolism are known to depend on carbon and nitrogen [29]. EDS was used for analyzing the samples' carbon and nitrogen distribution. Compared with the CON, the normalized carbon proportion in the nonsonicated fermentation group decreased from 75.30 to 67.70 at.% and from 70.02 to 61.77 wt.%, whereas the normalized nitrogen proportion increased from 24.70 to 32.30 at.% and from 29.98 to 38.23 wt.%. Following ultrasound-assisted fermentation, further decreases in the carbon proportion and corresponding increases in the nitrogen proportion were observed. Among the ultrasound-treated groups, the lowest carbon proportions (65.00 at.% and 59.27 wt.%) and the highest nitrogen proportions (35.00 at.% and 40.73 wt.%) were observed at 195 W (Fig. 4A). Microbial bioconversion of nitrogen-containing compounds was reported to improve the nutritional value of agricultural by-products [30]. During wheat bran fermentation, total nitrogen content was reported to increase progressively, while the concentration of free amino acids was significantly elevated [30]. Enzyme activity

Enzymatic activity was considered essential for microbial growth and metabolism, as well as for substrate degradation during ultrasound-assisted fermentation of FCWM [16]. Accordingly, enzyme activities were commonly used as indicators of fermentation performance and final product quality. Compared with the CON, acid protease activity was significantly increased in the nonsonicated fermentation group and was further enhanced by ultrasound-assisted fermentation. Among all treatment groups, the highest acid protease activity was observed at 195 W (Fig. 5A). This observation was consistent with previous reports that enzyme release was promoted by ultrasound-induced thermal effects [6]. Macromolecular proteins and corn zeins in FCWM were hydrolyzed by acid proteases through the cleavage of peptide bonds, resulting in the release of amino acids and short peptides [5], [16]. This trend was consistent with the shift toward lower-molecular-weight protein fractions observed in the ultrasound-assisted fermentation groups by SDS–PAGE. Fibrous components are known to reduce the digestibility and nutrient bioavailability of FCWM [5], [30], whereas cellulose can be enzymatically hydrolyzed by cellulases to release soluble sugars [16]. Compared with the CON, cellulase activity was significantly increased in the nonsonicated fermentation group and was further enhanced by ultrasound-assisted fermentation. Among all treatment groups, the highest cellulase activity was observed at 195 W (Fig. 5B). Cellulase-mediated hydrolysis significantly reduced the CF, NDF, and ADF contents of FCWM, while markedly increasing its reducing sugar content, indicating enhanced fiber degradation.

Elevated lipase activity is known to accelerate lipid hydrolysis in CWM, resulting in the release of free fatty acids that are readily oxidized to form compounds with potentially adverse biological and ecological effects [32]. Compared with the CON, lipase activity was markedly reduced in the nonsonicated fermentation group and was further decreased by ultrasound-assisted fermentation. Among all treatment groups, the lowest lipase activity was observed at 195 W (Fig. 5C). The present findings were consistent with previous reports that lipase activity was inhibited by the acidic conditions established during fermentation by Aspergillus niger and Lactobacillus fermentum [16], [24]. During storage, lipids in CWM are susceptible to oxidation, resulting in the formation of secondary lipid oxidation products such as malondialdehyde (MDA) [16]. MDA content in FCWM was significantly reduced by ultrasound-assisted fermentation, with the lowest level observed at 195 W (Fig. 5D). This pattern was consistent with the changes in lipase activity. MDA formation is closely associated with lipoxygenase activity, which is strongly affected by acidic conditions [32]. Therefore, the reduction in malondialdehyde content was likely attributable to the inhibition of lipoxygenase under the acidic conditions established during fermentation, which may have slowed lipid oxidation. Collectively, these changes may have improved the oxidative stability and storage quality of FCWM.

Anti-nutritional factors

Antinutritional factors in CWM are known to impair nutrient digestion, absorption, and utilization [24]. Among these, trypsin inhibitor (TI) and phytic acid are two of the most prevalent antinutritional factors in plant-derived materials [4], [5]. Phytic acid limits the utilization of magnesium, iron, zinc, and calcium by chelating these minerals into poorly soluble forms. Digestion is further impaired by its binding to proteins and digestive enzymes [33]. Compared with the CON, phytic acid content was significantly reduced in the nonsonicated fermentation group and was further decreased by ultrasound-assisted fermentation. Among all treatment groups, the lowest phytic acid content was observed at 195 W (Fig. 5E). Phytase capable of degrading phytic acid was reported to be secreted by Aspergillus niger [34]. In the present study, the highest Aspergillus niger biomass was observed at 195 W, which may have favored greater phytase production and, consequently, more extensive phytic acid degradation. Trypsin inhibitors are known to impair protein digestion and nutrient utilization by inhibiting trypsin activity [35]. TI content was reduced by ultrasound-assisted fermentation and reached its minimum in the 195 W treatment group (Fig. 5F). Previous studies reported that TI was effectively degraded by cellulase-producing microorganisms [16]. In the present study, the highest cellulase activity was observed in the 195 W treatment group, and the further reduction in TI content may have been associated with the enhanced metabolic activity of these microorganisms.

Determination of carotenoids

The carotenoids lutein and zeaxanthin are abundant in FCWM [36]. A pronounced color change was observed following carotenoid extraction from the samples (Fig. 6A, B). Colorimetric analysis showed that, compared with the CON, redness was reduced in the nonsonicated fermentation group, whereas yellowness and chroma were increased. A further reduction in redness and additional increases in yellowness and chroma were observed following ultrasound-assisted fermentation. Among all treatment groups, the lowest redness and the highest yellowness and chroma were observed at 195 W, whereas slight increases in redness and modest decreases in yellowness and chroma were observed at 260 and 320 W. These changes accounted for the observed color differences among the treatment groups (Fig. 6C–F). Compared with the CON, carotenoid content in the nonsonicated fermentation group was 2.44-fold higher. Flavonoids and total phenols

Phenolic compounds and other bioactive constituents are abundant in agro-industrial by-products [37]. However, a large proportion of these phenolics are covalently bound to insoluble lignocellulosic cell-wall components, thereby limiting their extractability and bioavailability [38], [39]. Previous studies reported that increases in phenolic and flavonoid contents during ultrasound-assisted fermentation were associated with enhanced microbial enzymatic activity [5], [16], [38]. Compared with the CON, phenolic and flavonoid contents in the nonsonicated fermentation group were 2.74- and 1.48-fold higher, respectively. Further increases were induced by ultrasound-assisted fermentation, with the maximum values recorded at 195 W. At this power level, phenolic and flavonoid contents were 3.00- and 1.90-fold higher than the corresponding CON values, respectively. However, slight decreases in phenolic and flavonoid contents were observed at 260 and 320 W (Fig. 7A and B). This decrease may have been attributable to partial microbial inactivation induced by excessive ultrasound power [27], which may have suppressed enzyme secretion by Aspergillus niger and Lactobacillus fermentum and consequently reduced overall enzymatic activity. Overall, these findings were consistent with the observed changes in enzyme activities and suggested that ultrasound power influenced enzymatic processes and the accumulation of bioactive compounds during fermentation. The higher phenolic and flavonoid contents observed at 195 W may have been attributable to the combined effects of enhanced microbial growth, increased enzymatic hydrolysis, and ultrasound-induced disruption of lignocellulosic structures, which collectively facilitated the release of bound phenolic compounds. Localized temperature increases generated during probe sonication may have affected the stability and extractability of phenolic compounds. To minimize heat accumulation, pulsed sonication (1 s on/1 s off) and a relatively short treatment duration (5 min) were employed. Antioxidant capacity

The antioxidant capacity of CWM is partly conferred by its phenolic and flavonoid constituents [40], while additional antioxidant activity is provided by peptides generated during fermentation [16]. The antioxidant capacity of FCWM, as indicated by ABTS and DPPH radical-scavenging activities, hydroxyl radical-scavenging activity, and FRAP, is markedly enhanced by increased levels of these antioxidant compounds [9]. Compared with the CON, DPPH and ABTS radical-scavenging activities, FRAP, and hydroxyl radical-scavenging activity in the nonsonicated fermentation group were 3.82-, 3.30-, 0.50-, and 4.30-fold higher, respectively. These antioxidant indices were further enhanced by ultrasound-assisted fermentation, with the greatest increases observed at 195 W. At this power level, DPPH and ABTS radical-scavenging activities, ferric-reducing antioxidant power (FRAP), and hydroxyl radical-scavenging activity were 4.32-, 4.25-, 0.94-, and 7.88-fold higher than the corresponding CON values, respectively. Slight decreases in these indices were observed at 260 and 320 W (Fig. 7C–F). This trend was consistent with the previously observed changes in carotenoid, phenolic, and flavonoid contents. Furthermore, improved storage stability in fermented products was reported to be associated with enhanced antioxidant capacity [16], [24]. Overall, the nutritional value, antioxidant capacity, and potential storage stability of CWM were further improved by ultrasound-assisted fermentation.

Because this study was conducted using a laboratory-scale probe-type ultrasound system, the direct extrapolation of the findings to industrial-scale production may have been limited. For industrial-scale applications, ultrasound-assisted fermentation can be implemented using continuous-flow reactors, multi-probe configurations, or high-power ultrasound systems to achieve more uniform acoustic energy distribution and greater process efficiency. Nevertheless, further studies are required to assess pilot-scale performance, energy consumption, process stability, and economic feasibility, and thereby facilitate the industrial-scale production of fermented corn gluten meal–wheat bran feed products.

Conclusions

In this study, CWM was fermented with Aspergillus niger and Lactobacillus fermentum under different ultrasound power levels to identify the most favorable sonication condition. At an ultrasound power of 195 W, the greatest reductions in fiber and antinutritional factor contents and the most extensive degradation of corn zeins and high-molecular-weight proteins were observed, which may have improved digestibility and nutrient availability. The contents of TFAA and reducing sugars were also significantly increased. In addition, the highest phenolic, flavonoid, and carotenoid contents were observed in the 195 W treatment group, accompanied by a marked enhancement in antioxidant capacity. In summary, the physicochemical properties, nutritional quality, and antioxidant capacity of FCWM were enhanced by ultrasound-assisted fermentation, indicating the potential of this treatment as an effective processing strategy.
