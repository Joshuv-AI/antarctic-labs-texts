Thiamine deficiency in cancer patients at initial admission to a palliative care unit: a single-centre cross-sectional study
Authors: Ryota Sato, Mayumi Ishida, Nozomu Uchida, Yoshitaka Ooya, Izumi Sato, Hiroshi Ito, Akira Yoshioka, Nobuyuki Onizawa, Takao Takahashi, Hideki Onishi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesThiamine deficiency (TD) has been increasingly reported among patients undergoing active cancer treatment. However, its status in end-of-life cancer patients admitted to palliative care units remains unclear. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of TD and associated factors in end-of-life cancer patients on initial admission to a palliative care unit.MethodsThis single-centre descriptive cross-sectional study included end-of-life cancer patients admitted for the first time to a palliative care unit between 1 January 2020 and 31 December 2023. Whole blood thiamine concentrations measured on admission were analysed using high-performance liquid chromatography. The TD group was defined as <24.0 ng/mL and the thiamine normal group as 24.0–66.0 ng/mL. Patient characteristics were summarised using descriptive statistics, and factors associated with TD were examined using multivariable logistic regression, adjusting for age and clinical background variables.ResultsTD was identified in 85 (36%) of 234 patients. Multivariable logistic regression showed that only a serum albumin level <3.0 g/dL was significantly associated with TD (adjusted OR 2.05, 95% CI 1.10 to 3.90; p=0.023). Age, body mass index, Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status, a history of chemotherapy within 1 month prior to admission and gastrointestinal malignancy were not significantly associated with TD.ConclusionTD was identified in 36% of end-of-life cancer patients and found to be associated with serum albumin level. In end-of-life cancer patients, particularly those with low serum albumin levels, clinicians should be mindful of the possibility of TD.

Introduction

Thiamine is converted in the body to thiamine pyrophosphate and serves as an essential coenzyme in glucose metabolism.1 As it cannot be synthesised endogenously, it must be obtained from external sources. However, physiological stores are depleted in approximately 18 days, and deficiency may develop after 2–3 weeks of insufficient intake.1 A representative neuropsychiatric disorder caused by thiamine deficiency (TD) is Wernicke encephalopathy (WE), which may progress to Korsakoff syndrome, an irreversible brain disorder, if diagnosis is delayed.2 WE often presents with nonspecific symptoms, and measurement of whole blood thiamine concentration is considered important for diagnosis.1 Intravenous thiamine administration is an effective treatment, and early detection and intervention can lead to recovery without sequelae.2 3

In recent years, TD has been reported in cancer patients undergoing treatment, with a prevalence of 33% in elderly patients with gastrointestinal cancer receiving chemotherapy,4 55% in cancer patients also undergoing psychiatric consultation5 and 45% in cancer patients with delirium evaluation during psychiatric consultation.6

Multiple pathophysiologic mechanisms have been shown to contribute to TD, including decreased dietary intake, impaired absorption, reduced physiological stores and excessive thiamine consumption due to inflammation or tumor-induced metabolic activation.1 7 These findings suggest that various cancer-related pathophysiologic mechanisms may increase the risk of TD.5

Many cancer patients experience decreased appetite,8 and TD resulting from reduced dietary intake has been reported.9 10 In chronic diseases such as cancer, inflammation can lead to decreased serum albumin levels,11 which are considered indicators of worsening clinical status.12 Furthermore, in end-of-life cancer patients, anorexia, weight loss and declines in Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG) performance status are common, making changes in indicators reflecting nutritional status, such as body mass index (BMI), clinically relevant.8 However, reports on the prevalence of TD and its associated factors in end-of-life cancer patients admitted to palliative care units are limited,13 suggesting that TD may be overlooked.

In this study, we hypothesised that TD is present at a certain prevalence even in end-of-life cancer patients at first admission to a palliative care unit and that clinical indicators reflecting cancer-related chronic inflammation and nutritional status may be associated with TD. We conducted an epidemiologic study to determine the prevalence of TD and its associated factors in this patient population.

Methods

Study design and setting

This single-centre descriptive cross-sectional study included end-of-life cancer patients admitted for the first time to the palliative care unit at Maruki Kinen Fukushi Medical Center (hereafter, MKFMC).

Participants

The study period was from 1 January 2020 to 31 December 2023. Eligible patients were end-of-life cancer patients at first admission to the palliative care unit, whose physicians estimated a life expectancy of ≤3 months at the time of admission.

Eligibility criteria

All patients meeting the inclusion criteria but not any exclusion criteria were included (figure 1).

Flowchart of patient selection. BMI, body mass index.

Inclusion criteria: end-of-life cancer patients aged ≥18 years at first admission to the MKFMC palliative care unit, who had whole blood thiamine concentration measured on the day of admission.

Exclusion criteria: patients who were already receiving thiamine-related treatment (oral, intravenous or enteral nutrition) at admission, or whom the investigators judged inappropriate for inclusion. In addition, patients whose whole blood thiamine concentrations exceeded the upper limit of the reference range were excluded from analysis, as they were considered likely to be taking thiamine-containing supplements.

Data collection

Patient background information and clinical data collected from electronic medical records included age, sex, whole blood thiamine concentration, serum albumin level, BMI, cancer type (classified according to the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, 10th Revision: C00–C96 and D37–D48), cancer treatment history within 1 month prior to admission (chemotherapy, surgery, hormonal therapy, radiotherapy) and ECOG performance status.14

Clinical laboratory values included serum albumin level (reference range: 4.1–5.1 g/dL) and whole blood thiamine concentration (reference range: 24.0–66.0 ng/mL). Whole blood thiamine concentrations were measured in blood samples collected on the day of admission using high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) at an external laboratory (SRL, Inc., Tokyo).

Categorisation of variables

For the assessment of factors associated with TD, each variable was categorised according to the following criteria. Whole blood thiamine concentration was classified based on HPLC reference values: <24.0 ng/mL as the thiamine deficiency group (TDG) and 24.0–66.0 ng/mL as the thiamine normal group (TNG). Elderly patients were defined as those aged ≥75 years, based on the joint statement of the Japan Federation of Gerontological Societies and the Japan Geriatrics Society.15 Serum albumin level was categorised using a cut-off of 3.0 g/dL based on the boundary values of the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events V.5.0 Grade classification,16 and ECOG performance status and BMI were dichotomised at the median values within the study population. Primary tumours were classified as gastrointestinal malignancies (C15–C26) or other types, given their potential impact on thiamine intake and absorption. Additionally, the presence or absence of cancer treatment within 1 month prior to admission was dichotomised as a potential factor influencing TD.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics were summarised using descriptive statistics, stratified by the TDG and TNG. Factors potentially associated with TD were examined using univariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses, with calculation of crude and adjusted ORs, 95% CIs and p values.

The covariates included in the primary multivariable logistic regression model were as follows: age (<75 years/≥75 years), serum albumin level (<3.0 g/dL/≥3.0 g/dL), ECOG performance status (<3/≥3), BMI (<19.6 kg/m2/≥19.6 kg/m2), cancer treatment within 1 month prior to admission and gastrointestinal malignancy (C15–C26). Covariates were selected to ensure ≥10 events per variable (EPV).17 Cases with missing data were excluded from the analyses. This was an exploratory study, and the sample size was determined by the number of eligible patients during the study period.

For sensitivity analyses, the cut-off values of covariates in the primary analysis were varied to evaluate their impact on results. Additional analyses treated age, serum albumin level, BMI and ECOG performance status as continuous variables, while cancer treatment within 1 month prior to admission and gastrointestinal malignancy (C15–C26) remained binary.

All statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro V.17.0.0 (SAS Institute, Cary, North Carolina, USA), with two-sided p values <0.05 considered statistically significant.

Percentages were rounded to the nearest whole number.

Results

During the study period, 695 end-of-life cancer patients were admitted to the palliative care unit, of whom 234 met the eligibility criteria (figure 1). Among the 461 excluded patients, 40 were readmissions, 79 did not undergo measurement of whole blood thiamine concentration on the day of admission, 333 were already receiving thiamine-related treatment at admission and 9 were judged inappropriate for inclusion by the investigators.

Among the 234 included patients, 47% (n=109) were female, and the median age was 77.0 years (IQR: 71.8–82.0). Based on the predefined criteria for whole blood thiamine concentration, 85 patients (36%) were classified into the TDG (table 1).

In the sensitivity analyses, models using alternative cut-off values for the covariates and models treating covariates as continuous variables were examined. In the models with modified cut-off values, the association between serum albumin level and TD remained consistent with the primary analysis. In the model using continuous variables, statistical significance was not observed for serum albumin level; however, the adjusted odds ratio for serum albumin level (per 1 g/dL increase) was less than 1 (online supplemental table 1).

Discussion

In this study, we investigated the prevalence of TD and its associated factors among end-of-life cancer patients who were admitted for the first time to a palliative care unit. As a result, TD was identified in 36% of the patients, and an association between TD and serum albumin level was suggested. However, given the cross-sectional study design, the temporal relationship between TD and serum albumin level cannot be determined and the findings should be interpreted with caution. In contrast, age, BMI, gastrointestinal malignancy (C15–C26), chemotherapy within 1 month prior to admission and ECOG performance status were not associated with TD in the multivariable logistic regression analysis.

A previous study reported that TD was identified in 28% of end-of-life cancer patients admitted to a palliative care unit in Australia.13 In the present study, an even higher prevalence was observed, suggesting that TD may be present in a substantial proportion of end-of-life cancer patients across different regions and healthcare systems. Whole blood thiamine concentration is not routinely assessed in daily clinical practice; therefore, TD may not be readily recognised in clinical settings and may be overlooked among patients with end-stage cancer. Potential mechanisms of TD include (ⅰ) decreased thiamine intake, (ⅱ) impaired thiamine absorption and (ⅲ) increased thiamine consumption due to tumour-related hypermetabolism, as suggested by previous studies.2 8 18 19 All of these mechanisms could plausibly occur in patients with end-of-life cancer.

In the primary analysis in this study, serum albumin level was suggested to be associated with TD. The sensitivity analyses demonstrated consistent results across models using alternative cut-off values for the covariates, supporting the association between serum albumin level and TD. However, when serum albumin level was treated as a continuous variable, the association did not reach statistical significance. Therefore, the strength and robustness of the association between serum albumin level and TD should be interpreted with caution.

A decreased serum albumin level has been considered an indicator reflecting cancer-related inflammation and deterioration of clinical condition.12 20 In the end-of-life cancer patients included in this study, the observed association between serum albumin level and TD may have been influenced by these underlying pathological conditions.

Diagnosis of TD requires measurement of whole blood thiamine concentration through blood sampling. However, it takes several days to obtain the results, and the test is not performed unless healthcare providers suspect TD. Therefore, the presence of TD may not be readily recognised, and it may have been overlooked in end-of-life cancer patients to date. For example, cases have been reported in which anorexia and fatigue in patients with cancer improved following thiamine supplementation and were subsequently diagnosed as TD.21 22 Furthermore, delirium is common among end-of-life cancer patients.23 Although various factors have been identified as causes of delirium in previous studies, TD has not been included as a potential cause in those reports.24 25 As mentioned above, one study reported that approximately half of cancer patients diagnosed with delirium during psychiatric consultation had TD.6 Therefore, among patients considered to have terminal delirium, there may be cases of reversible delirium caused by treatable TD. Delirium in the terminal stage is distressing not only for patients but also for their families and healthcare professionals. If TD is one of the underlying causes, thiamine administration may contribute to alleviating distress associated with end-of-life delirium.

Based on the above findings, it may be clinically important to consider TD as one of the differential diagnoses for physical and neuropsychiatric symptoms occurring in end-of-life cancer patients. Although measurement of whole blood thiamine concentration requires several days and lacks immediacy, serum albumin level is a laboratory parameter that can be readily and rapidly assessed in routine clinical practice. Therefore, in end-of-life cancer patients, particularly when serum albumin levels are low, clinicians should consider not only inflammation or general clinical deterioration but also the possibility of TD. Maintaining proper vigilance for TD may provide an opportunity for earlier diagnosis and timely therapeutic intervention.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-centre, descriptive cross-sectional study and therefore cannot establish causal or temporal relationships. Second, the sample size was limited, and the statistical power may have been insufficient. Third, the influence of confounding factors could not be completely eliminated, and unmeasured variables may have influenced the results. Fourth, because only patients whose whole blood thiamine concentration was measured on the day of admission were included, selection bias based on judgement or patients’ clinical condition may have occurred, potentially influencing the estimated prevalence of TD and its associated factors. Fifth, we were unable to assess the presence of symptoms attributable to TD or the clinical effects of thiamine supplementation. Sixth, dichotomisation of continuous variables may have reduced statistical power and resulted in residual confounding. Finally, because this study included all end-of-life cancer patients admitted to a palliative care unit, we did not examine the prevalence of TD in specific cancer types. Moreover, as this was a single-centre study conducted in Japan, caution is warranted when generalising these findings to countries or regions with different healthcare systems and patient populations.

Conclusion

This study demonstrated that TD was present in 36% of end-of-life cancer patients who were admitted for the first time to a palliative care unit, and suggested an association between serum albumin level and TD. In patients with end-stage cancer, particularly those with low serum albumin levels, clinicians should remain aware of the possibility of TD to avoid overlooking this potentially reversible and treatable condition.
