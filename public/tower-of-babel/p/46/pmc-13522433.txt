A dataset of geographic entities and relationships from Song Dynasty texts on Lin’an
Authors: Chi Zhang, Fengchen Zhong, Zuohui Chen, Wei Wu, Debao Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe automatic extraction of geographical entities and spatial relationships from historical texts is a fundamental task for Named Entity Recognition (NER) and relation extraction (RE), with important implications for historical geography and digital humanities. Classical Chinese documents describing ancient cities pose particular challenges due to archaic language, implicit spatial expressions, and complex entity hierarchies. In this study, we present a manually annotated dataset designed for joint geographical entity and spatial relationship extraction from texts related to Lin’an, the capital of the Southern Song Dynasty. The dataset consists of 18 in-domain and 1 out-of-distribution historical documents comprising approximately one million Chinese characters, annotated with 24 categories of geographical entities and 34 types of spatial relationships. This dataset provides a valuable resource for advancing NER and spatial relation extraction in historical texts and supports future research in historical Geographic Information Systems (GIS), cultural geography, and digital heritage reconstruction.

Background & Summary

The Southern Song Dynasty (1127-1279 AD) established its capital at Lin’an, present-day Hangzhou, where it remained from 1138 until the dynasty’s fall1. At its height, Lin’an was among the most populous cities in the medieval world, with a population exceeding one million2. Its spatial organization combined imperial ceremonial axes with dense commercial districts, religious compounds, waterway networks, and residential quarters, producing an urban fabric of exceptional complexity3,4. The richness of surviving textual documentation makes Lin’an an especially well-documented case for the study of historical urban geography.

Extracting geographic information from ancient literature plays a crucial role in contemporary digital humanities. Classical texts not only preserve historical narratives but also encode extensive spatial knowledge, including place names, mountain and river systems, transportation routes, and urban layouts. Systematically organizing and modeling this information can significantly advance historical geography while creating new opportunities for cultural heritage protection and virtual reconstruction5.

Despite its importance, research on geographical entities and spatial relationships in ancient texts has traditionally relied on manual interpretation and expert knowledge. This process is time-consuming, labor-intensive, and prone to subjectivity, making it difficult to support large-scale, reproducible studies. The emergence of information extraction techniques, such as Scientific Information Extraction (SciIE), offers promising solutions to these limitations6,7. SciIE converts unstructured text into structured knowledge through Named Entity Recognition (NER) and Relation Extraction (RE), and it has demonstrated strong performance in modern applications such as knowledge graph construction, intelligent question answering, and academic recommendation systems8–10.

However, directly applying these methods to ancient literature remains challenging. Ancient Chinese differs from modern Chinese in its syntactic structures, semantic ambiguity, and referential expressions. Historical documents also contain vague descriptions and culturally specific geographic features that can easily lead to misidentification, such as confusing personal names with place names or overlooking unique architectural structures. Moreover, historical texts vary considerably in genre such as spanning official histories, local gazetteers, and travel miscellany, and are temporally stratified. A single document may juxtapose place names and descriptions from multiple historical periods11. They are also noisy in the computational sense, as orthographic variants and heterographic representations of the same place name are pervasive12. The challenges are further amplified by the scarcity of annotated training data, which limits the generalization capability of existing models13.

To address these challenges, we construct a new dataset titled Geographical Entities and Positional Relations of Lin’an City in the Southern Song Dynasty (GEPR-LINAN). Lin’an, the capital of the Southern Song Dynasty, was one of the most prosperous urban centers of its time. Its spatial organization embodied the administrative logic and ritual order of the dynasty, while simultaneously accommodating diverse architectural forms and complex urban patterns. Palaces, government offices, markets, temples, residential quarters, alleys, and other structures were intricately interwoven, producing a spatial landscape that balanced hierarchical organization with urban openness14,15. These characteristics make Lin’an an exemplary case for studying medieval urban history4. In addition, Lin’an benefits from exceptionally rich historical documentation, including local chronicles such as Fangyu Shenglan and Wulin Jiushi, as well as various notes, gazetteers, and travel accounts. These texts provide a solid foundation for geographic information extraction and urban reconstruction. Compared with archaeological remains or surviving historical maps, textual records offer richer insights into everyday urban life, spatial usage, and patterns of social interaction16.

To our knowledge, we present a manually annotated dataset capturing geographical entities and spatial relationships related to Lin’an City during the Southern Song Dynasty. The dataset includes a comprehensive taxonomy of entity types, with categories that support structured representation of urban spatial organization in historical texts.

To address the complexity of spatial expressions in Classical Chinese, we design a relational schema covering orientation, topological relations, aliases, and historical evolution. The schema accommodates both physical spatial structures and historically embedded semantic links between places.

The dataset is produced through a multi-stage annotation workflow incorporating expert review, consistency checking, and iterative refinement to ensure high annotation quality. All entity and relation instances are linked to their original Classical Chinese passages, enabling transparent verification and reinterpretation.

This dataset provides a reusable resource for research in digital humanities, historical geography, knowledge graph construction, and Classical Chinese Natural Language Processing (NLP). It supports systematic computational analysis of ancient urban spatial layouts, investigations of historical urban evolution, and the development and evaluation of models for entity recognition and relation extraction.

Methods

Data Collection and Preprocessing

Lin’an, the capital of the Southern Song Dynasty, serves as the geographic focus of our corpus. The dataset is derived from official histories, local chronicles, scholarly works, notes, and travelogues, comprising 19 classical Chinese texts (18 in-domain text units drawn from 15 source works, plus 1 out-of-distribution text) with a total length of approximately one million characters. Texts were selected based on their direct descriptions or indirect references to the spatial layout of Lin’an, encompassing core elements such as palace complexes, streets and alleys, river systems, hydraulic facilities, and religious buildings. The materials range from officially compiled chronicles such as Fangyu Shenglan to privately authored travel accounts and notes like Wulin Jiushi, collectively capturing both macro-level institutional narratives and depictions of daily urban life17. This diversity of genres and sources ensures comprehensive coverage of urban spatial information, providing a robust foundation for downstream geographic information extraction and reconstruction.

The corpus is derived from 18 text units drawn from 15 classical Chinese source works (see Table 1), supplemented by one out-of-distribution (OOD) text (Yudi Jisheng, vols. 1–2). All source texts were consulted via Shidian Guji (https://www.shidianguji.com), a publicly accessible digital repository for classical Chinese texts that provides open, free access to page images of historical editions. We produced machine-readable transcriptions by applying optical character recognition (OCR) to the historical page images available on the platform, followed by systematic manual proofreading and correction of recognition errors. The resulting transcriptions are entirely our own work product and do not incorporate any pre-existing digitised text from the platform or other external sources. Prior to annotation, we conducted extensive text cleaning and preprocessing. We standardised the script by unifying traditional, simplified, and variant characters into consistent normalised forms, a common practice in digital humanities research18. We addressed OCR-induced errors—including incorrect sentence segmentation and misspellings—using contextual cues and model-assisted correction19. Finally, for documents originally formatted in vertical layouts and lacking clear structural boundaries, we applied paragraph segmentation to ensure that each unit contains a clear core referent, facilitating coreference resolution and contextual understanding20.

Entity and Relation Definition

Entity Definition

Compared with the relatively single feature of entity type classification in datasets such as CHisIEC16, this study combines the characteristics of geographical entities in ancient books of the Southern Song Dynasty and designs and proposes an entity classification system. Entity type classification has been proven to be of great significance in geographic information extraction and cultural heritage research21. This system not only makes up for the deficiency of existing Chinese ancient book datasets in the geographical dimension, but also provides a solid foundation for subsequent knowledge modeling and spatio-temporal evolution analysis.

Relation Definition

To more accurately reflect the multi-level correlations among geographical entities and provide references for the subsequent construction of relationship maps and the restoration of the locations of historical sites, this study proposes 34 types of spatial and semantic relationships. These relationships can meticulously depict the spatial topology and temporal evolution characteristics among entities, thereby providing semantic support for urban space modeling and historical scene reconstruction. This system is in line with the geographical pattern characteristics of ancient cities and can support the dynamic reconstruction of the complex spatial structure of Lin’an City and the construction of a knowledge graph. Existing studies have attempted to hierarchically divide relationships such as “orientation/proximity/distance” and map them to real coordinates for epidemic monitoring and geographic visualization22, indicating that this kind of relationship modeling is of practical significance for spatio-temporal restoration. A total of 34 types of relationships were defined in this study, further classified into four major categories. Annotation Process

This study adopts a semi-automatic annotation scheme of “automatic candidate generation using large language models and manual verification” to ensure efficiency while taking into account consistency and accuracy. This human-in-the-loop approach has been widely adopted recently23,24. The overall process is divided into four stages:

Automatic Candidate Generation

After data preprocessing, initial entity and relationship annotations are generated using GPT-5 (OpenAI, accessed November 2025; no version identifier was available via the API) with a sampling temperature of 0.3. The prompt words include a task description, enumerated entity and relation categories with definitions, and few-shot examples drawn from the corpus. Model output is constrained to JSON format to ensure that the structure can be parsed. The results obtained at this stage are regarded as “candidate annotation” and serve as the starting point for human review.

Manual Verification and Revision

Candidate annotations generated by GPT-5 were delivered to annotators in structured JSON format for sentence-by-sentence review. Modern large language models are capable of producing well-formed JSON output directly, obviating the need for third-party annotation tools. Annotators were scholars of Classical Chinese linguistics who received systematic training in the annotation schema prior to the production phase; this training included instruction on interpreting and editing the JSON structure, entity boundary conventions, relation directionality rules, and procedures for handling edge cases. For the majority of sentences, annotators applied the pre-established guidelines independently to correct entity spans, resolve name variants, and confirm relation types. Sentences that remained genuinely ambiguous after individual review were escalated to a senior annotator for adjudication. Domain expertise in classical Chinese philology was the primary qualification required for this task, as the principal challenge lies in interpreting archaic linguistic expressions and resolving referential ambiguity in historical texts rather than in software operation.

Iteration and Quality Control

During the verification process, common errors were collected and used to update the prompt template and alias dictionary, thereby improving the quality of subsequent automatic annotation. This iterative process of continuously optimizing the model’s annotation performance through manual feedback has been proven effective in relevant studies23,24.

Consistency Measurement

To evaluate the reliability of the annotations, we conducted a double-annotation experiment on a randomly sampled subset of the corpus. Two annotators with backgrounds in Classical Chinese independently annotated the same sentences following identical guidelines. Agreement was measured using precision, recall, and F1 score under strict matching criteria25.

Through this process, we significantly reduced the manual burden while ensuring the scale of the corpus, and maintained the consistency and high quality of the annotations.

The annotation pipeline comprised four iterative rounds. In Round 1, GPT-5-generated candidates delivered as JSON files were reviewed and corrected by Annotator A, who has expertise in both computational linguistics and classical Chinese. In Rounds 2 and 3, two additional scholars of classical Chinese independently verified the annotations in the same JSON format; systematic errors identified in each round were collected and used to update the annotation guidelines and prompt templates, and cases where the two verifiers disagreed were escalated to a senior expert in classical Chinese philology for adjudication. In Round 4, the same two verifiers independently annotated approximately 600 sentences (approximately 12% of the full corpus) from scratch in JSON format without access to any pre-generated labels, to compute Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA). A final post-processing pass applied systematic corrections uniformly across the full dataset. In total, four scholars with expertise in classical Chinese linguistics and literature participated in the annotation process.

The full prompt template used for candidate generation, including entity and relation type definitions and few-shot examples, is provided in the annotation guidelines document available in the dataset repository (see Data availability).

Data Records

The GEPR-LINAN dataset is available at https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.1897447026 and mirrored at https://github.com/tornadoboys/GEPR-LINAN. All files are encoded in UTF-8 and released under the Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International licence (CC BY 4.0). The repository is organised as follows:

The IND portion is divided into training (train), development (dev), and test (test) subsets at an 8:1:1 ratio at the document (volume) level. The ood split contains the out-of-distribution evaluation set drawn from Yudi Jisheng (vols. 1–2), a source work independent of all IND texts. All four splits are provided in each of the three formats.

LLM/*.json is a JSON array in which each element represents one sentence. Each element contains five fields: doc_id (document identifier, e.g. DOC_0001), sent_id (sentence identifier, e.g. SENT_000001), sentence (the raw Classical Chinese text), ner (a list of entity spans, each recorded as a two-element array [entity_text, entity_type]), and relations (a list of relation instances, each recorded as a five-element array [head_text, head_type, tail_text, tail_type, relation_type]). Entities and relations are represented as verbatim text spans without positional indices, making this format suitable for prompt-based and in-context learning experiments with large language models.

PLM/json-format/*.json is a JSON array at the sentence level using character-level token indices. Each element contains five fields: doc_id (document identifier), sent_id (sentence identifier), tokens (a list of individual characters),entities (each entity recorded as an object with start index (inclusive), end index (exclusive), and type), and relations (each relation recorded as an object with head and tail as zero-based indices into the entities array of the same sentence, and type). The directory also contains types.json, a companion schema file that declares the full type vocabulary shared across all four splits. It is a JSON object with two top-level keys: entities, which maps each of the 24 geographical entity category names to an object with short and verbose name fields, and relations, which maps each of the 34 spatial relation types to an analogous object additionally containing two Boolean metadata fields—symmetric (set to true for relations that hold equally in both directions, such as “adjacent to", “borders", and “confluent with") and transitive (set to false for all relation types in this dataset). This file contains no annotated text and serves solely as the type-vocabulary declaration loaded at model initialisation. Together, the data splits and types.json are directly compatible with span-based supervised models such as SPERT27 and ITER28.

PLM/jsonl-format/*.jsonl is a line-delimited JSON file at the paragraph level, where each line represents one paragraph comprising up to five consecutive sentences from the same document volume. Each line contains four fields: doc_id (document identifier), para_id (paragraph identifier, e.g. PARA_000001), sentences (a list of character lists, one per sentence), ner (entity spans parallel to sentences, each span recorded as [start, end_incl, type] with cumulative inclusive token indices across the paragraph), and relations (relation tuples parallel to sentences, each recorded as [head_start, head_end_incl, tail_start, tail_end_incl, type] using the same cumulative index convention). This format follows the PURE29 schema and is compatible with document-level joint extraction models.

Across all three formats, entity and relation types follow the 24-category and 34-category schema defined in the Methods section. English definitions and directionality conventions for all types are provided in supplementary/codebook_en.json. The mappings/ directory provides eight bidirectional JSON files linking doc_id, para_id, and sent_id identifiers, enabling cross-format navigation and provenance tracing for any sentence or paragraph. Complete field-level documentation, indexing conventions, and annotated examples are provided in supplementary/DATA_RECORD.md.

Data Overview

The GEPR-LINAN dataset comprises 4,920 sentences drawn from 18 in-domain (IND) classical Chinese source texts and one out-of-distribution (OOD) source, with a total corpus size of approximately one million characters. The dataset contains 25,397 annotated geographical entity instances across 24 categories and 17,881 spatial relation instances across 34 categories.

A preliminary analysis of corpus characteristics shows that the mean sentence length is approximately 49 characters (median: 25 characters), reflecting the mixed style of Classical Chinese prose ranging from terse single-clause statements to extended descriptive passages; one to two geographical entities appear per approximately 30 characters, indicating a high density of proper nouns; and a wide variety of directional terms appear throughout, reflecting the richness of spatial description in these texts.

Figure 1 shows the statistical distribution of entity and relation types as Pareto charts. The 24 entity categories are grouped into six functional classes: Political & Administrative, Religious & Cultural, Living & Urban, Natural Environmental, Transport & Military, and Economic & Social. The top five entity categories account for approximately 50% of all instances. The 34 relation categories are organised into four classes: Topological, Orientation, Naming Evolution, and Dynamic Change. Topological relations dominate, collectively comprising nearly 45% of all relation instances.

The dataset is split at the volume level, treating each volume as an independent document unit. This reflects the organizational structure of classical Chinese gazetteers, in which individual volumes typically address distinct geographic or thematic domains and were often compiled independently. Several source works, most notably Xianchun Lin’an Zhi, Meng Liang Lu, and Chunyou Lin’an Zhi, contribute volumes to both the development and test sets in addition to the training set. This is a deliberate consequence of stratified sampling: these three works collectively account for 75.7% of the IND corpus, and assigning their volumes exclusively to a single evaluation split would leave the remaining splits without representative coverage of the dominant textual tradition. Each individual volume appears in exactly one split; no sentence appears in more than one split. The out-of-distribution evaluation set (Yudi Jisheng, DOC_0089–DOC_0090) is drawn entirely from a source work with no volumes in the IND splits, providing a fully held-out cross-work evaluation condition7,16.

Technical Validation

Annotation Consistency

To assess the reliability of the annotations for geographical entities and spatial relations in Southern Song classical texts, we conducted a double-annotation experiment on a subset of the GEPR-LINAN corpus. Following previous work on domain-specific NER corpus construction25, we measure agreement using precision, recall, and F1 score at the span and relation levels. We adopt span-level F1 rather than Cohen’s Kappa, as F1 directly measures entity boundary and type matching accuracy without being inflated by the predominant non-entity tokens30.

The quality of the GEPR-LINAN annotations depends primarily on how clearly and consistently the annotation guidelines were understood and applied. In the production phase, annotators corrected GPT-5-generated candidate annotations in accordance with the pre-established guidelines; annotation reliability in this workflow is therefore governed by whether the guidelines are sufficiently unambiguous for trained annotators to interpret them consistently. To test this directly, we conducted a separate inter-annotator agreement (IAA) experiment using a different procedure: two annotators worked independently from raw Classical Chinese text, without access to any LLM-generated candidates or each other’s labels, and applied the same final version of the annotation guidelines from scratch. The degree of overlap between their independently produced annotations measures guideline clarity and inter-annotator consistency under conditions unaffected by LLM influence. Because the IAA annotators are the same individuals who performed the full production annotation and followed the identical guidelines, the resulting agreement scores provide evidence that the guidelines were clear and consistently applicable, which in turn supports the reliability of the production annotations.

The IAA experiment was designed to be representative of the full annotation effort. The 600 sentences were selected by stratified sampling across all 19 source texts and all six functional categories of entity types, ensuring that the resulting agreement scores reflect annotation behaviour across the full range of text genres, spatial description styles, and entity distributions present in the corpus. Both annotators were the same individuals who produced the full dataset annotations and followed the identical final version of the annotation guidelines, annotating from scratch in JSON format without access to any LLM-generated candidates (in contrast to the production rounds, where annotations were corrected from GPT-5 JSON output). We therefore consider the reported F1 scores to be a valid indicator of the consistency with which the guidelines were applied throughout the production annotation, acknowledging the caveat regarding anchoring bias described below.

It should be noted that the inter-annotator agreement experiment was conducted on raw text without pre-generated labels, and therefore does not fully capture the risk of anchoring bias present in the production annotation phase, where annotators corrected LLM-generated candidates rather than annotating from scratch. Correcting a proposed annotation is a cognitively different task from generating one, and annotators may have been systematically influenced by the LLM’s proposals even when those proposals were incorrect. This is a limitation of the current annotation methodology.

Usage Notes

The dataset is primarily designed for geographical entity recognition (NER) and relationship extraction (RE) tasks in ancient texts. It is not only applicable to named geographical entity recognition, entity relationship extraction, and joint entity relationship extraction, but also serves as a benchmark dataset for extracting information from ancient texts, providing a valuable benchmark for classical Chinese NLP12. At the application level, the dataset can be used beyond text information extraction and knowledge structuring. For example, the annotations can support distinct downstream uses. The entity and relation triples can be used to populate a geospatial knowledge graph, enabling structured queries over historical spatial relationships. Geocoded entities can be integrated into Geographic Information System (GIS) as vector layers, supporting spatial visualisation and cartographic reconstruction of historical Lin’an. These applications are complementary but technically distinct. Knowledge graph construction operates on the symbolic triple structure of the annotations, while GIS integration requires additional geocoding steps not provided in this dataset.

The data is organized in the format of SCIERC’s labeled datasets, including text, entity boundaries, entity types, and relationship triples. It’s divided into standard training set, validation set, and test set to ensure the reproducibility and fair comparison of research results. Additionally, we also provide an OOD dataset to facilitate further evaluation on more diverse and challenging data. It should be noted that there is often a high degree of ambiguity in the context of ancient Chinese, such as the confusion of personal names and place names or the coexistence of alternative names and old names. Therefore, it is recommended to use it in combination with annotation guidelines and pre-trained ancient Chinese models31. Meanwhile, the sample size of some relationship categories, such as “relocated to” (0.5% of instances) and “rebuilt from” (1.0%) is relatively sparse, and the problem of category imbalance needs to be paid attention to when using. Furthermore, this dataset does not contain annotations related to people and events. If the research objective is to model broader historical knowledge, other resources need to be combined32.

Limitations

This paper presents GEPR-LINAN, a manually annotated dataset for geographical entity and spatial relation extraction from Classical Chinese historical texts describing Lin’an, the capital of the Southern Song Dynasty (1127-1279 AD). The dataset comprises 18 text units drawn from 15 classical Chinese source works and 1 out-of-distribution text, totalling approximately one million characters, annotated with 25,397 geographical entities across 24 categories and 17,881 spatial relation instances spanning 34 relation types. The annotation was produced through a multi-stage pipeline combining LLM-assisted candidate generation with expert human verification, and its reliability is supported by inter-annotator agreement scores of F1 = 86.38% for entity annotation and F1 = 77.82% for relation annotation.

The dataset makes several contributions to historical Chinese NLP and digital humanities. The entity taxonomy, which distinguishes 24 spatially grounded categories ranging from palace complexes and religious sites to waterways and military facilities, offers substantially finer granularity than existing Classical Chinese corpora. The relational schema, which covers orientation, topological, naming evolution, and dynamic change relations, is specifically designed to capture the spatial expression conventions of Classical Chinese narrative prose, including relative directional expressions that are pervasive in historical gazetteers and travelogues. The inclusion of both an in-domain evaluation split and an out-of-distribution held-out set derived from stylistically distinct source texts provides a rigorous basis for evaluating model generalization across genres.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the corpus is geographically and temporally restricted to Lin’an during the Southern Song period; the extent to which the annotation schema and trained models generalize to other cities or dynasties remains an open question. Second, the production annotation was performed by correcting LLM-generated candidate labels rather than from scratch, which introduces the risk of anchoring bias: annotators may have been systematically influenced by the LLM’s proposals even when those proposals were incorrect. The inter-annotator agreement experiment, in which annotators worked from raw text without seeing any pre-generated labels, does not fully capture this risk. Third, certain relation types such as “relocated to” (0.5% of instances) and “rebuilt from” (1.0%) are sparsely represented, which may limit model performance on these categories.

Future work may address these limitations by extending the corpus to texts describing other Song Dynasty cities or capitals of other dynasties, enabling cross-domain evaluation of both the annotation schema and computational models. Linking annotated entities to georeferenced coordinates and historical gazetteers would further support integration with GIS platforms and spatial reasoning systems. The dataset is intended as a reusable foundation for research in historical geography, knowledge graph construction, and Classical Chinese NLP, and we hope it will facilitate systematic computational investigation of urban spatial organization in pre-modern China.
