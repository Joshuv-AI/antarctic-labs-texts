Individual and community-level determinants of women’s healthcare decision-making autonomy in extremely high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries: a multilevel mixed effect analysis
Authors: Tesfahun Zemene Tafere, Andualem Yalew Aschalew, Getachew Teshale Kefale, Endalkachew Dellie, Nigusu Worku, Chalie Tadie Tsehay, Asebe Hagos, Nebebe Demis Baykemagn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectiveThis study was conducted to identify the individual and community level determinants of women’s autonomy of healthcare decision-making in extremely high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries.DesignThe study utilised secondary data from the recent Demographic and Health Surveys conducted between 2014 and 2024. A multilevel mixed-effect logistic regression model was fitted. Adjusted OR (AOR) with a 95% CI was used to assess the strength and direction of association and statistical significance was considered at p value <0.05.SettingThe study focused on sub-Saharan African countries identified as having extremely high or very high maternal mortality ratios.ParticipantsA weighted sample of 98 910 women who were married or living with their partners were included in this study.Outcome measureWomen’s autonomy in decision-making for their own healthcare was the outcome variable in this study.ResultsThe pooled prevalence of women’s healthcare decision-making autonomy in extremely high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries was 50.40% (95% CI 50.09 to 50.71). Age 15–24 years (AOR=1.43, 95% CI 1.36 to 1.50) and 35–49 years (AOR=2.11, 95% CI: 2.02 to 2.22), rich wealth quantile (AOR=1.10, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.15), women’s primary education status (AOR=1.56, 95% CI 1.49 to 1.64), secondary (AOR=1.88, 95% CI 1.78 to 1.98) and higher (AOR=2.58, 95% CI 2.33 to 2.87), partner’s primary education status (AOR=1.51, 95% CI 1.43 to 1.59), secondary (AOR=1.16, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.22) and higher (AOR=1.13, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.21), having media exposure (AOR=1.11, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.15), rural residence (AOR=0.92, 95% CI 0.87 to 0.96), high community literacy (AOR=2.15, 95% CI 1.86 to 2.50), high community wealth status (AOR=1.25, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.44), not big problem of distance (AOR=1.08, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.13) were significant determinants of women’s autonomy of healthcare decision-making.ConclusionsThis study concluded that half of the women in extremely high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries were autonomous in making decisions about their own healthcare. The study recommends policies that prioritise improving women’s education, economic empowerment and employment opportunities to enhance women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making. Additionally, policymakers should enact laws and policies that protect women’s healthcare rights. Moreover, efforts should leverage media to raise awareness of women’s healthcare autonomy, address gender inequalities through partner education, and reduce healthcare access barriers, especially in rural areas.Keywords: PUBLIC HEALTH, Maternal medicine, Health policy, Decision Making, Health Equity

Background

Autonomy refers to the ability to access information and make decisions regarding one’s personal matters.1 Accordingly, women’s autonomy can be defined as their ability and freedom to make independent decisions about various aspects of their lives, including matters related to their own healthcare.2 The autonomy of women in decision-making is essential for the social, economic and sustainable development of any nation.3 Women’s access to maternal healthcare services, including family planning, antenatal care, health facility delivery, postnatal care, child immunisation and treatment for their illnesses relies on their autonomy in making healthcare decisions.4 Acknowledging the essential role of women’s involvement in decision-making for promoting comprehensive development in communities and nations, women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making and rights gained worldwide recognition after the 1994 International Conference on Population and Development in Cairo.5 In this context, tackling critical issues such as poverty, inequality and violence against women and girls is a key focus of the 17 ‘Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs)’ aimed at transforming and enhancing the quality of life worldwide.6 SDG 5 focuses on ensuring universal access to sexual and reproductive healthcare and reproductive rights,7 which is measured by ‘the proportion of women aged 15–49 years who make their own informed decisions regarding sexual relations, contraceptive use and healthcare (SDG 5.6.1)’.8

Women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making was included in the national questionnaires of the Demographic and Health Surveys (DHS) conducted in the 2000s in low and middle-income countries (LMIC).9 Research on women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making is gaining attention because of its significance for both human rights and healthcare outcomes.10 Empowering women in healthcare decisions can lead to better access to healthcare services, timely interventions and adherence to medical advice, contributing to the reduction of maternal mortality.5

Despite significant reductions, maternal mortality remains alarmingly high in LMIC.11 The rates of maternal death show stark disparities between high-income and low-income countries, surpassing those of most other health indicators.12 According to estimates from the WHO, United Nations International Children’s Emergency Fund (UNICEF), United Nations Population Fund (UNFPA), the World Bank Group and United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs (UNDESA), sub-Saharan Africa had the highest maternal mortality rate (MMR) in 2020, at 545 deaths per 100 000 live births. In comparison, the overall MMR for the African continent was 531, while the global average was 223.13 14 This is a significant concern for sub-Saharan Africa’s efforts to achieve the SDG 3 target of reducing maternal mortality to fewer than 70 deaths per 100 000 live births by 2030.15

The utilisation of maternal healthcare is shaped by women’s autonomy in decision-making within the household.16 17 In developed countries, women’s autonomy in decision-making largely drives the use of maternal healthcare services.18 However, in many cultures worldwide, women are often deprived of the opportunity to engage in decision-making and shape their own destinies.19 This is especially evident in sub-Saharan Africa, where women often have limited control over household assets, minimal involvement in healthcare decision-making, and face restrictions on leaving their homes to seek healthcare.20 21

Studies exploring the link between women’s autonomy and their health have shown that women’s autonomy is vital for accessing maternal healthcare services and enhancing their overall well-being.22 23 Women’s ability to make independent healthcare decisions is a precondition for accessing maternal healthcare services.24 25 However, women are frequently unable to exercise their autonomy on their health due to harmful and discriminatory social norms, limited agency and insufficient financial resources.26 27 This, in turn, can lead to poor health outcomes for the woman, her child, her family and ultimately, the community and nation as a whole.28

Evidence shows that in low-income African countries, a significant proportion of maternal and neonatal deaths is often linked to limited women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making.23 Poor autonomy of women’s healthcare decision-making can reduce the utilisation of maternal healthcare services, which potentially raises the risk of unintended maternal and reproductive health outcomes.29 It is crucial to address the root causes that influence women’s autonomy of healthcare decision-making to effectively reduce maternal mortality and morbidity in resource-limited settings. However, there is a paucity of research on women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in countries with a significant burden of maternal mortality, using nationally representative DHS data. Moreover, previous studies were limited to specific countries, regions, facility-based settings and specific healthcare services, which frequently failed to provide a broader perspective.4 30–32 There is also a need for high-quality, large-scale population-based research in sub-Saharan African countries, where a significant proportion of maternal mortality occurs. This study was, therefore, conducted to determine the individual and community-level determinants of women’s healthcare decision-making autonomy in extremely high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries using nationally representative DHS data. The study will provide valuable insights for policymakers and other stakeholders, urging them to take immediate action to empower women and improve their autonomy in healthcare decision-making in sub-Saharan Africa, ultimately contributing to a reduction in maternal mortality in the region.

Methods

Study settings and data sources

The study used the latest DHS from eight sub-Saharan African countries with extremely high or very high MMRs.33 DHSs are standardised, publicly available cross-sectional datasets designed to provide nationally representative data on key health indicators, including morbidity, mortality and the use of maternal and child health services. The DHS is a nationwide survey conducted every 5 years in LMICs. Data are collected by national statistics agencies in partnership with the United States Agency for International Development (USAID). DHS adhere to standardised procedures across all countries, including sampling, questionnaire design, data collection, cleaning, coding and analysis, enabling cross-country comparisons.34 The primary aim of DHS is to offer high-quality data for monitoring and evaluating population health programmes and to support evidence-based health policy development.35

The survey employed a multistage (two-stage) stratified sampling approach to select participants. Initially, enumeration areas (EAs) were randomly selected using probability proportional to size after stratification by region and urban–rural residence. In the second stage, households within each selected EA were chosen using systematic random sampling. All eligible women aged 15–49 years in the selected households were interviewed. The data used in this analysis were weighted to adjust for non-response and variations in the probability of selection.

In this study, missing data were assessed for all study variables prior to analysis. Most variables had complete data, while some variables (occupation, mass media exposure, distance from the health facility and health insurance) had missing values ranging from minimal to relatively high proportions. A complete case analysis was conducted for the main analysis, and observations with incomplete data were excluded. To assess the potential impact of missing data, a sensitivity analysis was performed by comparing model estimates before and after excluding observations with missing values. The results were consistent, with only minimal changes in the effect estimates, suggesting that the missing data did not materially affect the study findings.

The countries identified as having extremely high and very high MMRs were selected from the 2023 maternal mortality estimation report jointly published by WHO, UNICEF, UNFPA, the World Bank Group and UNDESA.14 According to the report, South Sudan, Chad and Nigeria were identified as having extremely high MMRs (over 1000 maternal deaths per 100 000 live births). Meanwhile, Central Africa, Guinea-Bissau, Liberia, Lesotho, Somalia, Guinea, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Kenya and Benin were classified as having very high MMRs (500 to 1000 maternal deaths per 100 000 live births) in sub-Saharan Africa. South Sudan, Somalia and Guinea-Bissau were not included in the study due to the lack of a DHS dataset. One country, Central Africa, was also excluded due to having an outdated data set. Despite these exclusions, the included countries represent a substantial proportion of the highest maternal mortality settings in sub-Saharan Africa. Finally, we included Chad, Nigeria, Liberia, Lesotho, Guinea, Democratic Republic of the Congo, Kenya and Benin in our study. This study has included a total weighted sample of 98 910. While the survey collected data from unmarried, married, living with a partner, divorced and widowed women, this study focused on women of reproductive age (15–49 years) who were married or living with their partners. This focus is due to power imbalances within households between women and their husbands or partners, which may more significantly limit women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making compared with other women. Variables and measurements

Dependent variable

The outcome variable in this study was women’s autonomy in decision-making for their own healthcare. This was assessed through the question: ‘Who usually makes decisions about healthcare for yourself?’ This question had five response options: (a) respondent alone, (b) respondent and husband/partner, (c) husband/partner alone, (d) someone else and (e) other. We dichotomised the women’s autonomy of healthcare decision-making into two categories: not autonomous=0 and autonomous=1. Women who reported that the decision regarding their healthcare was made primarily by their partner alone, someone else or other were categorised as not autonomous, while women who reported that the decision regarding their healthcare was primarily made by the respondent alone and/or jointly with their partners were classified as autonomous in healthcare decision-making. This was done based on the existing literature.36 37

Independent variables

The covariates included in the analysis were at both the individual and community levels. These variables were chosen based on their theoretical relevance and practical importance to the outcome variable and a review of similar published articles.17 37 38 The individual-level variables examined in this study included age, educational status, occupation, partner’s education, family size, wealth index, mass media exposure and sex of the household head. The community-level variables considered include residence, community-level women’s literacy, community-level media exposure, community-level poverty and distance from the health facility. Community-level variables like residence and distance to health facilities were sourced directly from the DHS data, retaining their original categorisations. Meanwhile, community media exposure, community literacy rate and community-level poverty were derived by aggregating individual-level data within the study clusters. These community-level variables were then categorised as high or low based on the distribution of the proportional values computed for each variable. Accordingly, community-level media exposure, education and poverty were categorised as high if the proportion exceeded 50%, and low if the proportion was 50% or less. This cut-off point for categorisation was determined based on the national median value, as these variables were not normally distributed.39 Accordingly, the community level of women’s education was classified as high if the proportion of women with at least primary level education was higher than the median cut-off value (50%) and low if the proportion was 50% or less. The variable wealth index was constructed using data on various household assets to evaluate overall household wealth status. In the dataset, the wealth index was presented with five groups: poorest, poorer, middle, richer and richest. A new variable was created by merging poorest with poorer and richest with richer, resulting in three categories: poor, middle and rich categories to simplify our analysis and interpretation of the findings. Community poverty level was categorised as high if the proportion of women in the lowest wealth quintiles is above the median value, and as low if it was below the median.40 In this study, media exposure was determined by aggregating responses related to radio listening, TV watching and newspaper reading. Women who watched TV or listened to radio or read a newspaper less than once a week and at least once a week were considered as having media exposure, while those who did not watch TV or listen to the radio or read a newspaper at all were categorised as not having media exposure. Accordingly, the variable was categorised as high if the proportion of women exposed to at least one form of media less than once a week and at least once a week was greater than 50%, and low otherwise.41

Data analysis and model building

The data were cleaned, coded and analysed using Stata V.17 software. Prior to analysis, the data were weighted to account for unequal probabilities of selection and non-response. The individual women’s sampling weight (v005) was divided by 1 000 000, as recommended by DHS. After appending the datasets, the weights were applied to ensure representativeness and to obtain valid population-level estimates and standard errors.

A multilevel mixed-effect binary logistic regression analysis, incorporating both fixed and random effect, was conducted to identify factors at the individual and community levels. Four models were applied in this study: the null model (without independent variables), model I (including individual-level factors), model II (including community-level factors) and model III (combining both individual-level and community-level factors).

We evaluated and compared the fitness of the nested models by examining the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC), median OR (MOR), proportional change in variance (PCV) and deviance (−2LLR). ICC was calculated for each model as ICC=VAVA+3.29×100%.39 42 Moreover, MOR was calculated for each model, which is MOR=exp (0.95√VA).43 PCV was also computed for each model, PCV = ((Vnull−Vm1,m2,m3Vnull)×100%,44 45 where Vnull=the variance in the null model, Vm1=variance in model 1, Vm2=variance in model 2, Vm3=variance in model 3. A model with the lowest deviance was selected as the best-fitting model for predicting women’s healthcare decision-making autonomy. Finally, in the multivariable analysis, adjusted OR (AOR) with a 95% CI and a p value of less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Before fitting the final regression model, multicollinearity among the independent variables was assessed using the variance inflation factor (VIF). The analysis revealed no significant collinearity among the explanatory variables, with a mean VIF of 1.29. Earlier studies have suggested that VIF values below 10 are deemed acceptable.46 47 Descriptive statistics, including frequencies and percentages, were utilised to summarise the data. These findings were presented through narratives, tables and figures.

Results

Individual-level factors

This study analysed data from 98 910 women of reproductive age from eight sub-Saharan African countries. The majority of women (40.34%) were aged between 25 and 34 years. Moreover, 41.51% of the respondents were uneducated. Approximately two-thirds (66.04%) were employed, and 51.92% had a family size of 5 or more. Additionally, 35.13% of respondents’ partners were uneducated, and 40.39% were from the rich wealth quintiles. Furthermore, 66.45% of the respondents had no media exposure. The results further showed that 97.58% of the respondents had no health insurance, and a little over four-fifths (86.18%) of the respondents were with the household head of male sex (table 2).

Random effect and model fitness

The fit of the nested models was evaluated and compared using the ICC, MOR, PCV and deviance (−2LLR). The ICC in the null model was 32.72%, suggesting that 32.72% of the variation of women’s healthcare decision-making autonomy among women was due to differences between clusters, while the remaining unexplained 67.28% was attributable to individual differences. Moreover, the MOR for women’s healthcare decision-making autonomy among women in the null model was 8.88, indicating variation in women’s healthcare decision-making autonomy across clusters. This indicates that if women were randomly selected from different clusters, those in clusters with higher women’s healthcare decision-making autonomy would have 8.88 times higher odds of healthcare decision-making autonomy compared with those in the lower clusters. Model III, which includes both individual-level and community-level variables, showed a higher PCV (36.25%) compared with model I (23.16%), indicating that it explained a greater proportion of the variability in women’s healthcare decision-making autonomy. Furthermore, model III was identified as the best-fitting model for predicting women’s healthcare decision-making autonomy as it had the lowest deviance (−2LLR=85 2170.08) (table 4).

Prevalence of women’s autonomy of healthcare decision-making in extremely high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries

The pooled prevalence of women’s healthcare decision-making autonomy in extremely high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries was 50.40% (95% CI 50.09 to 50.71). There were notable variations in the prevalence of women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making across countries, with the highest, at 89.32% in Lesotho and the lowest at 25.02% in Chad (figure 1).

Determinants of women’s autonomy of healthcare decision-making in extremely high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries

In the final model, individual-level factors such as age, educational status of women, wealth index, occupation, partners’ education status, family size, sex of the household head and media exposure were found to be associated with women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making. Similarly, community-level characteristics such as residency, community literacy level, community wealth status and distance from the health facility appeared to be significantly associated with women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making in extremely high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries.

In this study, the odds of women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making among women aged 25–34 and 35–49 years were 1.43 (AOR=1.43, 95% CI 1.36 to 1.50) and 2.11 (AOR=2.11, 95% CI 2.02 to 2.22) times higher as compared with those women aged 15–24 years, respectively. We further observed that women in the rich wealth quantile were 1.10 (AOR=1.10, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.15) times more likely to have autonomy in healthcare decision-making than those women in the poor wealth quantile. Moreover, women who have jobs were 1.82 (AOR=1.82, 95% CI 1.75 to 1.89) times more likely to have autonomy in healthcare decision-making as compared with their counterparts. Our study has revealed that the autonomy of healthcare decision-making was 1.56 (AOR=1.56, 95% CI 1.49 to 1.64), 1.88 (AOR=1.88, 95% CI 1.78 to 1.98) and 2.58 (AOR=2.58, 95% CI 2.33 to 2.87) times higher for women who had primary, secondary and higher education status, respectively, than for women who had no education. In this study, the odds of having autonomy in healthcare decision-making was 1.51 (AOR=1.51, 95% CI 1.43 to 1.59), 1.16 (AOR=1.16, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.22) and 1.13 (AOR=1.03, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.21) times higher for women whose partner had primary, secondary and higher education status, respectively than for women whose partner had no education. Women with a family size of more than 5 members were 24% less likely to have autonomy in healthcare decision-making compared with those with fewer than three family members, 0.76 (AOR=0.76, 95% CI 0.72 to 0.80). Compared with women from male household heads, the autonomy of healthcare decision-making was higher by 44%, 1.44 (AOR=1.44, 95% CI 1.37 to 1.52) among women in female household heads. Furthermore, the likelihood of autonomy of healthcare decision-making among women who had media exposure was 1.11 (AOR=1.11, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.15) times higher than their counterparts. Women in rural areas were 8% less likely to have autonomy in making healthcare decisions compared with those residing in urban areas, highlighting the influence of community-level factors 0.92 (AOR=0.92, 95% CI 1.86 to 2.50). Moreover, the odds of autonomy of healthcare decision-making among women from high community literacy level was 2.15 (AOR=2.15, 95% CI 1.86 to 2.50) times higher compared with their low community literacy level counterparts. The odds of autonomy of healthcare decision-making among women from high community wealth status was 1.25 (AOR=1.25, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.44) times higher compared with their low community wealth status counterparts. Those women with no big problem of distance from the health facility had 1.08 (AOR=1.08, 95% CI 1.05, 1.13) higher odds of having autonomy in healthcare decision-making as compared with women with a big problem of distance from the health facility (online supplemental table 1).

Discussion

This study aimed to examine the pooled prevalence and determinants of women’s autonomy of healthcare decision-making in extremely high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries. Our findings revealed a low level of autonomy in healthcare decision-making among women. The pooled prevalence of women’s healthcare decision-making autonomy was 50.40. This finding was higher compared with those studies conducted in Bangladesh (48%),48 Malawi (18%)49 and Tanzania (19%),50 however was lower than the studies conducted in Ethiopia (81.6%),36 East Africa (68.37%),51 Bale zone (41.4%),52 Ghana (75%)53 and India (64.55%).54 The discrepancy might be due to differences in sociocultural norms, women’s empowerment initiatives and policy interventions that vary across these nations. This finding was also comparable with the study conducted in Zambia (50.31%).55 The similarity in prevalence may be attributed to comparable sociocultural contexts, gender norms and levels of women’s empowerment across sub-Saharan African countries. Additionally, both studies utilised DHS data, which employ standardised measurement approaches, further supporting the consistency of the findings.

The study has identified multiple individual and community-level factors that have a significant influence on women’s autonomy of healthcare decision-making in extremely high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries. In this study, older women were observed to have greater autonomy in healthcare decision-making compared with younger women. The odds of being autonomous in healthcare decision-making among women aged 25–34 and 35–49 years was 1.43 and 2.11 times higher, respectively, than those of women aged 15–24 years. Similar associations were observed in previous studies.29 56 57 This may be because older women tend to have more life experience with health-related situations, a higher likelihood of economic independence and greater influence within household dynamics compared with younger women. However, our finding differs from the study carried out in Rwanda,58 which found that older women were less likely to make autonomous decisions regarding their own healthcare compared with younger women. This discrepancy may be explained by contextual differences between the study settings. Differences in the measurement of autonomy, population characteristics and sociocultural factors may also account for the contrasting results.

Women’s healthcare decision-making autonomy was found to increase with higher educational status. Women with primary, secondary and higher education were more likely to have autonomy in healthcare decision-making compared with those with no education. This finding is supported by other studies; Nepal,59 Nigeria60 and Chad.61 A possible explanation is that education enhances women’s understanding of healthcare matters and strengthens their ability to make autonomous healthcare decisions. Additionally, a higher level of education is associated with increased employment opportunities, which enhances women’s economic independence and, in turn, their autonomy in healthcare decision-making.

Moreover, we found that there was a direct significant association between household income and women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making. In this study, women from high-income households were more likely to be autonomous in healthcare decision-making compared with those from low-income households. This observation aligns with studies conducted in Ghana,62 Bangladesh63 and Pakistan,64 in which wealthier women were more likely to have greater autonomy in healthcare decision-making compared with poorer women. This might be because women from high-income households have better access to education, healthcare and resources, which empower them to make autonomous healthcare decisions. This implies that economic empowerment is crucial for enhancing women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making. But it was inconsistent in studies done in Southern Ethiopia4 and Nepal.10 The discrepancy may be due to differences in sociocultural contexts and variations in the measurement of women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making across settings.

The job status of the women was another significant determinant factor. The likelihood of having autonomy in healthcare decision-making was higher by 82% for those women who have jobs in this study. This finding is consistent with studies conducted in Nigeria,65 Ethiopia66 and Bangladesh.48 This increased autonomy in healthcare decision-making may be due to greater financial independence. This implies that policies promoting women’s employment could enhance their autonomy in healthcare decision-making, potentially leading to improved family health and greater gender equality in decision-making. In contrast, studies done in Ethiopia67 and South Africa68 found that women who were not employed demonstrated greater autonomy in making healthcare decisions compared with those who were employed. The observed difference may be attributed to variations in gender roles and economic dynamics across countries. Additionally, methodological differences, such as study design and analytical approaches, may have contributed to the inconsistent findings. Therefore, further qualitative assessments would be appropriate to explore these seemingly unusual findings.

This study has revealed that women whose partners had primary, secondary and higher education status were found to have a higher likelihood of autonomy in healthcare decision-making. Women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making was 1.56, 1.88 and 1.24 times higher for women whose partner had primary, secondary and higher education status, respectively, than for women whose partner had no education. This finding is congruent with other studies conducted in East Africa,51 Senegal69 and Indonesia.70 A plausible explanation is that educated partners may value and support women’s independence, promote shared decision-making and encourage gender equality. Therefore, interventions aimed at improving women’s autonomy should consider engaging both women and their partners.

We further found that women with a family size of 5+ were 24% more likely to be autonomous in healthcare decision-making compared with those women with fewer than three family members. This finding was supported by the study conducted in Nigeria,71 but contrasts the previous studies conducted in southern Ethiopia4 and Addis Ababa,72 where the probability of the women’s healthcare decision-making was reported to be decreased as the family size increased. The discrepancy could be due to differences in sociocultural norms and household structures across nations. Moreover, methodological differences in sample composition, measurement of autonomy and data analysis methods may have contributed to the observed inconsistency.

Another predictor associated with women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making was the sex of the household head. We observed that women from female household heads were 44% more likely to have autonomy in healthcare decision-making compared with those from male household heads. This finding was consistent with studies conducted in Ethiopia4 73 and Indonesia.74 This may be because women in female-headed households experience greater empowerment and support for making independent healthcare decisions.

Consistent with previous studies in Ethiopia,75 Nigeria65 and Pakistan,32 our study has further revealed that the odds of women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making was higher among women who had media exposure compared with their counterparts. This finding highlights that media exposure enhances women’s knowledge and awareness, thereby leading to greater autonomy in healthcare decision-making.

Women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making was also found to be affected by community-level factors. Those women in rural areas were 8% less likely to have autonomy in healthcare decision-making compared with women living in urban areas. This finding is in agreement with results from previous studies conducted in Nigeria,65 sub-Saharan Africa76 and Nepal.10 The consistency may be due to similar sociocultural and economic patterns existing across the countries. This implies that interventions targeting rural areas are needed to improve women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making.

Community literacy level was another important predicting factor found in our study, where a higher community literacy level was associated with a higher probability of women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making, similar to other studies in Afghanistan77 and Uganda.78 This suggests that improving community literacy may contribute to enhancing women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making.

In this study, it was observed that there was a significant association between community wealth status and women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making in extremely high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries. Women from high community wealth status had higher odds of autonomy in healthcare decision-making compared with those from low community wealth status. This may be due to better access to information, education and healthcare resources in wealthier communities as well as more progressive gender norms that can enhance women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making.

This suggests that policies should focus on strengthening community socioeconomic conditions to improve women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making, particularly in low-wealth settings.

Distance from the health facility was another factor identified as influencing women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making in this study. Women with no big problem of distance from the health facility had 1.08 times higher odds of autonomy in healthcare decision-making as compared with women with a big problem of distance from the health facility. A possible explanation is that easier physical access to health facilities increases women’s exposure to health information and services, thereby enhancing their knowledge and empowering them to make autonomous healthcare decisions.

The key strength of this study lies in its utilisation of nationally representative survey data and a large sample size. Moreover, it applied a multilevel mixed-effect model to find a more valid result and to account for the hierarchical nature of the survey data. Despite its notable strengths, this study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional nature of the DHS data limited the ability to establish causal relationships between the dependent and independent variables. Second, the study relied on secondary data, which restricted the analysis to variables available in the DHS dataset; therefore, some important factors could not be included. Third, the outcome variable was derived from self-reported responses and dichotomised, which may introduce social desirability bias and lead to a loss of detailed information. Fourth, differences in survey years (2014–2024) and the exclusion of some countries may slightly limit generalisability. Moreover, some study variables contained missing data. Although a complete case analysis was performed and sensitivity analyses indicated that the findings were generally robust, the potential for bias arising from missing information cannot be entirely ruled out. Consequently, the missing data may have introduced some uncertainty into the study findings. Finally, despite adjusting for a range of individual and community-level factors, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out.

Conclusion

Our study concluded that women have limited autonomy in healthcare decision-making in sub-Saharan African countries with extremely high and very high maternal mortality. Overall, half of the women had autonomy in making decisions about their own healthcare. Autonomy in healthcare decision-making was found to be strongly correlated with factors at both the individual and community levels. The study identified women aged 25–34 and 35–49 years, educational status (primary, secondary and higher education), rich wealth quantiles, having jobs, partner’s educational status (primary, secondary and higher education), family size of 5+, female household head, ever had media exposure, as individual-level determinants of women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making. Moreover, rural residency, high community literacy level, high community wealth status and no big problem of distance from the health facility were identified as community-level determinants of women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making in extremely high and very high maternal mortality sub-Saharan African countries.

Thus, to enhance women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making in sub-Saharan African countries with extremely high and very high maternal mortality, interventions should address both individual-level and community-level factors. The study recommends policies that prioritise improving women’s education, economic empowerment and employment opportunities. Additionally, efforts should leverage media to raise awareness about the benefits of women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making and address gender inequalities through partner education. Community-level initiatives should focus on enhancing literacy rates and reducing barriers to healthcare access, particularly in rural areas, to foster an environment that supports women’s decision-making autonomy. Furthermore, policymakers should actively contribute to the autonomy of women’s healthcare decision-making by enacting laws and policies that safeguard women’s rights, promote gender-responsive healthcare services and ensure access to comprehensive information. These measures can collectively enhance women’s autonomy in healthcare decision-making, thereby contributing to a reduction in maternal morbidity and mortality in sub-Saharan Africa.
