Credible is in
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractSelf-promotional language may attract readers but is discouraged in Communications Psychology. Because style matters in fostering credibility.Subject terms: Human behaviour, Psychology, Peer review, Publication characteristics, Publishing

Malte Mueller

Psychological research can make a difference. Individual studies in basic research can shift the attention of a field and influence research for decades1,2. Research can translate into new interventions, policy recommendations, or law3. Who gets to decide whether something constitutes a “paradigm shift”, a “fundamental” contribution, or an “incredible” finding? At Communications Psychology, we maintain that these judgments should not be pronounced by the authors.

A recent Article4 in our pages showed that promotional language in Abstracts increases online attention and citations. This suggests readers are attracted by, and potentially susceptible to, such language. Therefore, it may be surprising that editors at the journal work with authors to ensure they refrain from any self-promotional language in their work. Similarly, we do not allow explicit or implicit novelty claims in our research papers, editing out “unprecedented”, “novel”, “first”, as well as “no research has shown”. Why so mean?

The case against novelty claims – shared across many portfolio journals - is easily explained. Novelty claims lead to unproductive disagreement, not least because they are not provable. With a count of 96,348 publications in psychology journals indexed in Web of Science in 2024 alone, statements about what hasn’t been shown or doesn’t exist are (informed) guesswork. Moreover, it’s not clear when an effect is “first” truly established – the first time the effect is shown? The first time that boundary conditions that make the effect appear or vanish are identified? The first independent replication? Removing novelty claims is a simple solution to acknowledge that science is a collective enterprise in which independent, high-quality publications matter regardless of order.

The case against self-promotional language is more subtle; one that weighs immediate gains in reach against midterm consequences of buzz. Science does not exist in a vacuum. A significant proportion of research is funded by the public through taxes. Open-access online publishing makes science accessible to a broad audience, increasing the awareness of the insights derived from tax spending on R&D. The public is not only involved in funding science, it is also meant to benefit from its findings. Research informs policy and intervention. The public’s response to climate change, sustainability measures, and public health recommendations offer stark reminders of how much trust in science matters for approval of scientific research and adoption of what we learn from it. The long-term credibility of research—and thus people’s trust and preparedness to follow where the science leads—hinges upon how it is communicated to the public, a point made in a Comment5 in the journal. Premature hype around preliminary findings may erode public trust in science in the long run. It is imperative for science communication to ensure its credibility by managing expectations. This means describing research matter of fact and avoiding hyperbole, starting from the Title and Abstract. All research has limitations and caveats, and communicating these matters for long-term credibility.

Meeting authors who are enthusiastic about their work and excited about their findings is inspiring for editors. But while infectious enthusiasm enlivens a conversation, overselling of the research in writing is not advised. It is not the authors but the expert readers who are best placed to judge the work, who may feel “amazed” and who can most credibly assign the label of “incredibly valuable contribution”.

Is this a call to develop a Hemingwayan distrust of adjectives? By no means. Research that was preregistered should carry this label. Studies powered at 90% to detect the smallest effect size of interest deserve to be described as “well-powered” in the Abstract. The inclusion of a replication may qualify work as “robust”. Demonstrable attributes should, of course, be listed—while overly subjective, flowery proclamations of the work’s virtues and import are best taken out by sober editing.
