Plant-part specific in vitro antibacterial activity of crude ethanolic extracts of Mangifera indica against Salmonella typhi: an exploratory study
Authors: Ivan Ronald Omara, Juliet Kyayesimira, Stephen Charles Olet
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMedicinal plants remain widely used in the management of infectious diseases, but evidence on the antibacterial activity of specific plant parts is often inconsistent. This study evaluated the plant-part-specific in vitro antibacterial activity of crude ethanolic extracts of Mangifera indica against Salmonella typhi.MethodsAn exploratory in vitro study was conducted using crude ethanolic extracts prepared from the seed kernel, stem bark, root, and a combined extract (1:1:1 mixture) of Mangifera indica (M. indica). Antibacterial activity was assessed using the agar well diffusion method. Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC) and Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC) were determined using broth microdilution and sub-culture methods. Assays were performed in triplicate. Data were analysed using the Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Holm adjustment.ResultsThe seed kernel extract showed the largest mean zone of inhibition (18.0 ± 0.5 mm, strong activity), followed by tetracycline (14.0 ± 0.5 mm, moderate activity) and the combined extract (11.5 ± 0.5 mm, moderate activity). Stem bark, root, and Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) showed no detectable inhibition. Overall differences among treatments were statistically significant (Kruskal–Wallis test: H = 16.76, df = 5, p = 0.005). Dunn’s post-hoc test showed that the seed kernel extract produced significantly larger inhibition zones than the combined extract (adjusted p = 0.0219). The seed kernel and combined extracts had MIC values of 16.67 mg/mL and MBC values of 33.34 mg/mL, with an MBC/MIC ratio of 2, indicating bactericidal activity.ConclusionsCrude ethanolic extracts of M. indica showed plant-part-specific antibacterial activity against S. typhi in vitro. The seed kernel was the most active plant part and warrants further phytochemical, toxicity, and in vivo evaluation before any therapeutic application can be inferred.Keywords: Mangifera indica, Salmonella typhi, Antimicrobial activity, Medicinal plants, Minimum inhibitory concentration, Minimum bactericidal concentration

Introduction

Infectious diseases remain a major cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with a disproportionate burden in low- and middle-income countries where access to diagnostic services and effective antimicrobial therapy is often limited. In such contexts, plant-based remedies continue to play an important role in primary health care because of their accessibility, affordability, and cultural acceptance [1, 2].

The global increase in antimicrobial resistance among bacterial pathogens has renewed interest in plant-derived substances as potential sources of antimicrobial agents [3]. Preliminary in vitro studies remain useful at the early screening stage because they help identify plant parts and extracts with measurable antibacterial activity before more advanced phytochemical, toxicity, and in vivo investigations are undertaken. However, reported antibacterial activity within the same plant species can vary considerably across studies, partly because of differences in plant parts tested, extraction methods, bacterial strains, and assay conditions [4–6]. Systematic comparisons under standardized conditions are therefore needed to inform evidence-based use.

Mangifera indica L. (M. indica), family Anacardiaceae, commonly known as mango, is widely cultivated in tropical and subtropical regions and has a long history of ethnomedicinal use [7, 8]. Different parts of the plant, including the seed kernel, stem bark, leaves, and roots, have been used traditionally in the management of gastrointestinal disorders and febrile illnesses, including conditions locally associated with typhoid fever [9, 10]. Despite this broad ethnomedicinal application, experimental evaluations of M. indica have produced variable findings, particularly with respect to antibacterial activity across different plant parts [11, 12].

Typhoid fever, caused by Salmonella enterica serovar Typhi (S. typhi), remains a public health concern in many developing regions, including sub-Saharan Africa. In Uganda, recurrent outbreaks have been associated with inadequate water supply, poor sanitation, and high population density [13]. Although antimicrobial therapy remains central to disease management, challenges related to antimicrobial resistance and access to treatment continue to motivate exploratory investigations into locally available plant-based alternatives [14, 15].

Within this context, this study evaluated the in vitro antibacterial activity of crude ethanolic extracts of selected parts of Mangifera indica against Salmonella typhi, with emphasis on plant-part specificity. The study was intended to generate preliminary laboratory evidence on which selected plant parts demonstrate measurable antibacterial activity against S. typhi under controlled in vitro conditions, while recognizing that such findings require further phytochemical, toxicity, and in vivo validation before any therapeutic application can be inferred.

Materials and methods

Study design

This study was designed as an exploratory in vitro investigation aimed at evaluating the antibacterial activity of crude ethanolic extracts prepared from selected parts of Mangifera indica against Salmonella typhi.

Study area and laboratory setting

All laboratory procedures were conducted at the Biosciences Laboratory, Department of Biological Sciences, Kyambogo University, Kampala, Uganda.

Bacterial strain and culture conditions

Salmonella typhi was selected as the sole test organism because the study aimed to evaluate the antibacterial activity of Mangifera indica against the causative agent of typhoid fever. The investigation was therefore designed as a targeted exploratory assay rather than a broad-spectrum antibacterial screening.

A laboratory strain of Salmonella typhi previously characterized by polymerase chain reaction (PCR) was obtained from the Biosciences Laboratory, Kyambogo University [16]. The strain was maintained in glycerol stocks at − 80 °C, revived on tryptone soya agar, and working cultures maintained at 4 °C.

Collection and identification of plant materials

Fresh, disease-free seed kernels, stem bark, and roots of Mangifera indica L. (family Anacardiaceae) were collected from a single mature cultivated mango tree located in Omaa Village, Lira City East Division, Lira City, Uganda (2°10’26.6"N, 32°54’49.1"E; 1101 m above sea level). Leaves were not included because the study was designed to compare the antibacterial activity of the selected non-leaf plant parts that are also commonly used in traditional medicine. The investigation focused on these plant parts to address the study objectives and maintain consistency in sample collection and laboratory analysis.

To minimize damage to the mother plant, only small quantities of stem bark and root material were collected. Stem bark was removed superficially from a limited area using a clean, sharp panga, avoiding ring-barking. Root material was collected from a small exposed lateral root using a hoe and panga, without uprooting or destroying the tree. The sampled areas were limited to what was required for laboratory extraction. Permission to collect the plant materials was obtained from the landowner.

The plant material was identified by the first author, Ivan Ronald Omara, based on standard morphological characteristics of Mangifera indica L. A voucher specimen was subsequently deposited at the Makerere University Herbarium, Makerere University, Kampala, Uganda, under voucher number MUH51645.

Samples were washed thoroughly with sterile distilled water, shade-dried at room temperature for 14 days, and ground into fine powder using a sterilized electric grinder. The powdered samples were stored in sealed containers until extraction.

Preparation of crude ethanolic extracts

Crude extracts were prepared using ethanolic maceration [10]. Five grams (5 g) of each powdered plant material were soaked in 50 mL of ethanol (99.9%) for 72 h at room temperature with intermittent agitation. The mixtures were filtered using sterile filter paper (Whatman No. 1) and concentrated at 40 °C using a rotary evaporator to obtain dried crude extracts. Each extract was reconstituted in Dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to obtain a stock concentration of 66.67 mg/mL. A combined extract was prepared by mixing equal volumes (1:1:1) of the seed kernel, stem bark, and root extracts.

Preparation of bacterial inoculum

Bacterial inoculum was prepared by suspending fresh colonies of Salmonella typhi in Mueller–Hinton broth. The turbidity of the suspension was adjusted to 0.5 McFarland standard (approximately 1.5 × 10⁸ CFU/mL), verified spectrophotometrically at 600 nm to ensure consistency across assays [17]. For each independent replicate, a fresh inoculum was prepared.

Agar well diffusion assay

Antibacterial activity was assessed using the agar well diffusion method [18]. Mueller–Hinton agar plates were uniformly inoculated with the standardized bacterial suspension using sterile cotton swabs. Wells of 6 mm diameter were aseptically punched into the agar and filled with 100 µL of each plant extract at a concentration of 66.67 mg/mL. Tetracycline (6.667 µg/mL) served as the positive control. The tetracycline concentration was selected based on laboratory standard operating procedures for comparative susceptibility screening. DMSO was used as the negative control.

Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. Zones of inhibition (including the well diameter) were measured in millimeters using a digital caliper. For descriptive interpretation in this study, inhibition zones were categorized as follows: no activity, 0 mm; weak activity, < 10 mm; moderate activity, 10–15 mm; and strong activity, > 15 mm. These categories were used only to aid descriptive presentation and were not used as a substitute for statistical analysis. All assays were performed in triplicate using three independent bacterial inoculum preparations on three separate days.

Determination of Minimum Inhibitory Concentration (MIC)

Minimum inhibitory concentration was determined using the broth microdilution method [19]. Two-fold serial dilutions of each extract were prepared in Mueller–Hinton broth to obtain final concentrations ranging from 33.34 mg/mL to 2.08 mg/mL in 96-well microtiter plates. Each well was inoculated with 100 µL of standardized bacterial suspension (approximately 5 × 10⁵ CFU/mL final concentration). Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. MIC was defined as the lowest concentration of extract that showed no visible turbidity. Each MIC determination was performed in triplicate with independent inoculum preparations.

Determination of Minimum Bactericidal Concentration (MBC)

Minimum bactericidal concentration was determined by sub-culturing 10 µL aliquots from wells that showed no visible growth during MIC determination onto fresh Mueller–Hinton agar plates [20]. Plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The lowest concentration of extract that showed no bacterial growth was recorded as the MBC. Each MBC determination was performed in triplicate.

Statistical analysis

The analysis used three replicate laboratory sets for zones of inhibition, MIC and MBC. Zones of inhibition were summarised using mean, standard deviation, median, minimum, maximum and activity classification. MIC and MBC were summarised as determined or not determined. The MBC/MIC ratio was used to classify the antimicrobial effect, with a ratio of 4 or less interpreted as indicating bactericidal activity. Because each treatment had three replicate sets and some groups had identical values, non-parametric tests were prioritised. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used as the global test, and Dunn’s post-hoc pairwise comparisons with Holm adjustment were used to identify pairwise differences. A p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were performed using RStudio version 2023.12.0 Build 369 (Posit Software, PBC).

Biosafety and ethical considerations

All procedures involving bacterial handling were conducted in accordance with standard biosafety practices for non-clinical laboratory strains. No human participants or animal subjects were involved in this study; therefore, formal ethical approval was not required. Plant material collection complied with applicable local regulations and institutional guidelines for cultivated plant species. No protected, endangered, or regulated plant species were involved in the study.

Results

Antibacterial activity of Mangifera indica extracts against Salmonella typhi

The agar well diffusion assay demonstrated significant differences in antibacterial activity among the tested treatments (Kruskal–Wallis test statistic: H = 16.76, df = 5, p = 0.005). The seed kernel extract produced the largest mean zone of inhibition (18.0 ± 0.5 mm), followed by tetracycline (14.0 ± 0.5 mm) and the combined extract (11.5 ± 0.5 mm). In contrast, the stem bark extract, root extract and DMSO produced no measurable zones of inhibition (0.0 mm). Based on the descriptive activity classification adopted in this study, the seed kernel extract demonstrated strong antibacterial activity, whereas the combined extract and tetracycline demonstrated moderate antibacterial activity. The stem bark extract, root extract and DMSO exhibited no detectable antibacterial activity (Table 1).

Minimum inhibitory concentration and minimum bactericidal concentration

The seed kernel extract and combined extract demonstrated MIC of 16.67 mg/mL and MBC of 33.34 mg/mL (Table 2). MIC and MBC values were not determined for the stem bark and root extracts because these extracts did not show detectable antibacterial activity. Tetracycline was included as a positive control in the agar well diffusion assay and was not evaluated for MIC or MBC.

Discussion

This study evaluated the antibacterial activity of crude ethanolic extracts prepared from different parts of Mangifera indica against Salmonella typhi. Among the plant parts evaluated, the seed kernel exhibited the greatest antibacterial activity, producing significantly larger inhibition zones than the combined extract, while no significant difference was observed between the seed kernel extract and tetracycline. The combined extract demonstrated moderate antibacterial activity, whereas the stem bark and root extracts showed no detectable antibacterial activity. Furthermore, the seed kernel and combined extracts demonstrated identical MIC (16.67 mg/mL) and MBC (33.34 mg/mL) values, with an MBC/MIC ratio of 2, indicating bactericidal activity under the experimental conditions of this study.

The strong activity observed for the seed kernel extract is consistent with previous studies reporting antibacterial activity of M. indica seed kernel extracts against foodborne and enteric bacteria [10, 21, 22]. Mango seed kernels have previously been reported to contain antimicrobial constituents, supporting the finding that the seed kernel was the most active plant part in the present study [22]. Unlike many previous studies that evaluated only a single plant part, the present study compared selected plant parts collected from the same tree, thereby minimizing genetic variation and allowing direct comparison of antibacterial activity under the same experimental conditions. The statistically greater activity of the seed kernel extract compared with the combined extract further supports the conclusion that antibacterial activity was plant-part specific. Differences in inhibition-zone size among studies may reflect variation in mango variety, extraction solvent, extract concentration, bacterial strain, assay method, and environmental conditions influencing phytochemical composition [10, 21].

The greater activity of the seed kernel extract may be related to the concentration of bioactive secondary metabolites in mango seeds. Mango seed kernels have been reported to contain antimicrobial constituents, including phenolic compounds, tannins and flavonoids, which may contribute to inhibition of bacterial growth [22, 23]. The absence of detectable activity in the stem bark extract contrasts with previous reports of antimicrobial activity from mango stem bark under different experimental conditions [6]. For the root extract, the lack of inhibition should be interpreted cautiously because antibacterial activity may vary with plant part, extraction solvent, extract concentration, bacterial strain and assay conditions. The lower activity of the combined extract compared with the seed kernel extract may reflect dilution of active seed-kernel constituents after mixing with less active extracts. Antagonistic interactions among extract components also cannot be excluded, although no formal synergy or antagonism testing was conducted [24, 25].

The MIC and MBC findings further support the antibacterial activity observed in the agar well diffusion assay. Both the seed kernel and combined extracts inhibited visible growth of S. typhi at an MIC of 16.67 mg/mL and exhibited an MBC of 33.34 mg/mL. Although these concentrations are relatively high compared with purified antimicrobial agents, this is expected for crude plant extracts because they contain complex mixtures of active and inactive constituents rather than isolated compounds [26]. The MBC/MIC ratio of 2 for both active extracts indicates bactericidal activity, based on the commonly used interpretation that antibacterial agents are considered bactericidal when the MBC is no more than four times the MIC [27]. This suggests that the active extracts could kill S. typhi at the tested bactericidal concentration under the tested conditions.

Strengths and limitations

The present study incorporated several methodological features that strengthen confidence in the findings. Plant materials were collected from a single mature tree, thereby minimizing genetic variation among samples and allowing direct comparison of antibacterial activity among the selected plant parts. In addition, antibacterial assays were performed in triplicate using independent bacterial inoculum preparations, and non-parametric statistical analyses were applied to account for the small sample size and distribution of the data.

Nevertheless, the study had the following limitations: Only one laboratory strain of S. typhi was evaluated, limiting generalizability to other strains or clinically resistant isolates. In addition, only crude ethanolic extracts were investigated, and the specific bioactive compounds responsible for the observed antibacterial activity were not identified. The study was also limited to in vitro experiments and did not evaluate toxicity, pharmacokinetic properties, or in vivo efficacy. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings.

Conclusion

Crude ethanolic extracts of M. indica exhibited plant-part-specific antibacterial activity against S. typhi in vitro. Among the selected plant parts, the seed kernel demonstrated the strongest antibacterial activity and was significantly more active than the combined extract. The seed kernel and combined extracts also exhibited bactericidal activity, as indicated by an MBC/MIC ratio of 2. These findings identify the seed kernel as the most promising plant part for further investigation as a potential source of antibacterial compounds against S. typhi. Future studies should isolate and characterize the active phytochemical constituents, evaluate activity against a broader range of clinical Salmonella isolates, assess safety, and determine in vivo efficacy before any therapeutic application is considered.
