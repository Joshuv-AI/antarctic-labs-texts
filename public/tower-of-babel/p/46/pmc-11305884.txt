Saving lives from sudden cardiac arrest: every second and action counts
Authors: The Lancet Regional Health – Western Pacific
Published: 2024

On June 30, 2024, a 17-year-old Chinese badminton player, Zhijie Zhang, passed away after experiencing a sudden cardiac arrest (SCA) on the court of the Asia Junior Championships in Indonesia. This unexpected event sparked condolences but also a great deal of public concern and outrage, because this young life could have been saved if prompt and adequate medical responses were provided. This tragedy underscores the importance of recognising and responding to SCA in a timely and appropriate manner.

SCA is defined as the sudden cessation of cardiac activity with haemodynamic collapse, often occurring out of hospital, and has a survival rate lower than 10% in most parts of the world. Sudden cardiac death, an unexpected death caused by SCA, contributes to more than half of all cardiac deaths and up to 20% of overall mortality globally. The 2023 report by the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation revealed a rising trend in the estimated annual incidence of emergency medical services-treated out-of-hospital cardiac arrest (OHCA), ranging from 40.8 to 100.2 individuals per 100 000 population globally. However, current data primarily originate from high-income settings such as Australia, the USA, the UK, and various European nations. The burden of SCA could be considerably underestimated in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) due to insufficient surveillance and reporting systems. Furthermore, the surge in the prevalence of coronary artery disease in LMICs, which is responsible for most sudden cardiac deaths, will likely worsen the SCA burden in these regions.

Factors that may increase the risk of SCA include a personal or family history of cardiovascular diseases, comorbidities such as diabetes and obesity, and lifestyle factors such as smoking. Furthermore, additional risk factors have emerged to put extra pressure on the global burden of SCA, such as population ageing, climate change, and pandemics. Typically, the age for sudden cardiac death occurrence is around 65–70 years. Accordingly, sudden cardiac death cases are anticipated to increase with the ageing population. Nevertheless, it is also important to consider the younger population as, while less common, instances of SCA in younger adults are increasingly being reported. This trend may be attributed to factors such as long working hours, unhealthy lifestyles, or intensive physical activities (sports-related SCA). It is possible that the age of onset for SCA might decrease in the future. The increased prevalence of SCA is also influenced by environmental factors such as changing climate and air pollution. Research conducted in the Western Pacific region has projected that the attributable fraction of OHCA due to high temperatures and heatwaves would increase by 4.94% and 6.99% from the 2010s–2090s if gas emissions remain uncontrolled. Elevated PM2.5 concentration have been found to be associated with an initial increased risk of OHCA. Overwhelmed health-care systems and limited medical resources during pandemics can also hamper the outcomes of SCA. For instance, recent data from Japan have shown that the COVID-19 pandemic was associated with worse neurological outcomes and reduced public access to defibrillators for use in patients with OHCA. Targeted strategies must be developed to prevent SCA by addressing both these traditional and novel risk factors.

Challenges in improving outcomes following OHCA involve inadequate distribution of emergency medical services resources—eg, inequities in ambulance allocation and the standard of emergency medical services in urban and rural areas, extended emergency medical services response times, underutilisation of automated external defibrillators, low rates of bystander cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), insufficient public awareness of SCA and lack of basic life support knowledge, and sometimes cultural factors that hinder individuals from contacting emergency medical services or administering CPR. The latest data from the national population-based OCHA registry in China has shown figures that are below the standards typically seen in high-income countries (HICs): in China, only 20.3% of patients with OHCA received bystander CPR (19.1–79% in HICs), fewer than 0.1% were assessed with a defibrillator (2.0–37.4% in HICs), and the median emergency medical services response time was 12 min (5–11 min in HICs). High-income settings within the Western Pacific region also demonstrate superior performance, with the bystander CPR rates exceeding 40% in South Korea and Singapore, and reaching as high as 63.3% in Australia.

Successful outcomes in cases of SCA require not only effective coordination between bystanders and health-care providers, but also community engagement in extensive public education and training. It is necessary to strengthen public knowledge to recognise an SCA and their ability to start immediate actions, including calling the emergency services, performing CPR, and using defibrillators. Also required is government investment in medical training, allocation of health-care resources, and implementation of legislation and policies. This is to ensure public access to defibrillators and high-quality emergency medical services in various locations, establish proper medical protocols under different scenarios, and protect and encourage bystander interventions. The most recent study indicates that the implementation of CPR legislation in China is positively associated with an increase in bystander CPR, public defibrillation, and improved survival among patients with OHCA. By focusing on the key components of the chain of survival—early recognition of cardiac arrest, early CPR, early defibrillation, and early advanced care—outcomes for SCA can be significantly enhanced. Meanwhile, strategies beyond this chain are being developed to maximise the management of SCA. The International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation recently introduced the notable concept of a chainmail of survival, a framework with multiple joint links strengthening the whole mission that can be tailored to low-resource settings. Global initiatives, such as World Restart a Heart Day have been launched since 2018 to raise awareness about the importance of bystander CPR and improve bystander CPR rates worldwide. Furthermore, novel methodologies such as genetic testing, big data and artificial intelligence, and wearable devices are being developed for risk prediction of SCA in the general population, and to enable automated cardiac arrest detection.

As a time-critical medical emergency, every second and every action from bystanders, first responders, and emergency care professionals counts to save lives from SCA. If bystander CPR and prompt emergency medical services were administered, tragedies like that of Zhijie Zhang and many others could have been avoided. We should raise public awareness of SCA and equip ourselves with the knowledge and skills to effectively respond to such situations. The time to act is now.
