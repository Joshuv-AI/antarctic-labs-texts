Digital mirror therapy combined with repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation for upper limb motor function in patients who had a stroke: protocol for a single-centre randomised controlled trial in China
Authors: ZeXiang He, Lili Wang, Qiuxiang Lin, Jiawei Qin, Wanting Wang, Jiali Lin, Jingfeng Wang, Keh-Chung Lin, Jie Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionUpper limb motor dysfunction after stroke is a leading cause of disability worldwide, resulting in a significant economic burden on society. Rehabilitation is a key strategy for improving this condition. Although studies have shown that digital mirror therapy and repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation individually provide benefits, the effectiveness of their combination remains unclear. We are conducting a randomised controlled trial to evaluate the efficacy of combining digital mirror therapy with repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation in treating upper limb motor dysfunction post-stroke, in order to meet the urgent clinical need for more effective treatment options.Methods and analysisThis is a single-blind randomised controlled trial. A total of 46 participants will be randomly assigned in a 1:1 ratio to either the digital mirror therapy combined with repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation group or the repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation group. Both groups will undergo the intervention protocol spanning four consecutive weeks. Assessments will be conducted at baseline and after 4 weeks of treatment. The primary outcome will be evaluated using the Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Upper Extremity score. Secondary outcomes include the Action Research Arm Test, Box and Block Test, Modified Barthel Index, Fugl-Meyer Assessment - Upper Extremity Sensory subscale, Modified Ashworth Scale, as well as pinch strength, grip strength and adverse events. Intention-to-treat analysis will be applied to all data, including missing or dropout data.Ethics and disseminationThe study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Quanzhou First Hospital on 23 September 2024 (2024K193). The final results of this study will be published in a peer-reviewed journal.Trial registration numberChiCTR2400092692.

Introduction

Stroke is a major neurological disorder and a leading cause of death and long-term disability worldwide, imposing a substantial burden on individual health and socioeconomic systems.1 The 2024 Lancet Neurology Global Burden of Disease analysis reported 93.8 million stroke cases in 2021, ranking stroke as the fourth leading cause of disability-adjusted life years (160.5 million) lost and the third leading cause of death globally.2 Notably, approximately 75% of stroke survivors experience upper limb motor impairment.3 Even after a period of routine rehabilitation, upper limb dysfunction remains a major cause of limitations in patients’ daily activities and participation,4 5 negatively affecting their quality of life.6 Given the high disability associated with post-stroke upper limb dysfunction, restoring upper limb function has been identified as a top research priority from both the perspective of patients and healthcare professionals.7 Upper limb motor dysfunction is a critical challenge in stroke rehabilitation. Therefore, we are seeking an effective treatment with better patient compliance to address upper limb motor dysfunction in patients who had a stroke.

Repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation (rPMS) is a novel non-invasive neuromodulation technique.8 By delivering strong pulsed magnetic fields that penetrate deep tissues to stimulate peripheral nerves (eg, the brachial plexus at Erb’s point), rPMS simultaneously induces contractions of target muscles and generates high-intensity proprioceptive afferent input.9 10 Compared with transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS), rPMS has greater peripheral penetration depth and is not affected by cranial attenuation,11 allowing for efficient stimulation of deep peripheral nerves and providing stronger proprioceptive drive for central reorganisation; whereas TMS focuses more on directly modulating cortical excitability.12 Current studies have shown that rPMS improves upper limb function in patients who had a stroke.13 Clinical evidence indicates that rPMS can enhance proximal muscle strength and distal motor function,1416 as well as reduce flexor spasticity.10 17 18 However, its ability to drive central nervous system reorganisation remains limited, as patient involvement is relatively passive, lacking active motor intention and learning processes, which restricts its broader and more enduring therapeutic effects. Recently, clinical studies have confirmed that rPMS, when used as an adjunct therapy, can significantly enhance treatment outcomes and provide a novel intervention approach for upper limb functional rehabilitation after stroke.19 Nevertheless, rPMS alone has limitations in promoting active patient engagement and effectively facilitating central reorganisation, highlighting the need for combination with other therapies to achieve complementary effects and optimise rehabilitation outcomes.

Mirror therapy (MT), first introduced by Ramachandran et al,20 is a powerful tool for central nervous system stimulation and motor learning. Meta-analyses have demonstrated that MT can significantly improve upper limb function.21 Digital mirror therapy (DMT)22 uses high-definition cameras to capture movements of the unaffected limb and generate real-time mirrored visual feedback. Its core mechanism involves activating bilateral primary motor cortices (M1) and the mirror neuron system, thereby promoting callosal reorganisation to optimise impaired interhemispheric information transfer.23 Compared with conventional MT, DMT overcomes spatial limitations, is more easily integrated into training and substantially enhances patients’ active motor intention and engagement.24 25 Our rationale for combining DMT with rPMS is based on the hypothesis that the two modalities may produce a ‘central–peripheral synergistic effect’. Specifically, rPMS provides supplementary sensorimotor input at the peripheral level, while DMT modulates cortical excitability and plasticity at the central level; together, they act on the peripheral–central motor control pathway to promote functional recovery. The strong sensory input generated by rPMS may enhance the cortical connectivity reorganisation induced by DMT, while the network-stabilising effect of DMT26 may improve the cortical activation efficiency of rPMS. Furthermore, rPMS-mediated modulation of corticospinal excitability,15 in combination with the interhemispheric regulation induced by DMT, may compensate for the limitations of rPMS monotherapy.

Therefore, we designed this study to compare the clinical effects of DMT combined with rPMS (DMT+rPMS) versus rPMS alone on upper limb motor function in patients who had a stroke. We hypothesise that DMT+rPMS will be more effective than rPMS alone, as the synergistic combination may more comprehensively promote neuroplasticity and enhance interhemispheric communication, thereby producing superior improvements in upper limb motor recovery after stroke.

Material and methods

Study design

This study is a single-centre, prospective, single-blind randomised controlled trial aimed at evaluating upper limb motor function in patients who had a stroke (see figure 1: study flowchart). Eligible participants will be randomly assigned to either the DMT+rPMS group or the rPMS group. Participants in both groups will receive a total of 20 intervention sessions, conducted five times per week over a period of 4 weeks. The primary outcome after the intervention will be assessed using the FMA-UE (Fugl-Meyer Assessment for Upper Extremity) score. Secondary outcomes will include the Action Research Arm Test (ARAT), Box and Block Test (BBT), Modified Barthel Index (MBI), Fugl-Meyer Assessment-Upper Extremity Sensory subscale (FMA-UE-S), Modified Ashworth Scale (MAS), as well as pinch strength, grip strength and adverse events, in order to assess whether the DMT+rPMS group provides more benefits than the rPMS group. Participants

Randomisation and blinding

To ensure blinding, an independent researcher will generate the allocation sequence using a randomisation website (http://www.randomization.com/). When participants meet the eligibility criteria, sealed envelopes will be drawn to randomly assign 46 participants, in a 1:1 ratio, to either the rPMS control group or the rPMS combined with DMT group (rPMS+DMT group). The randomisation sequence will be generated by personnel independent of the study, and they will also be responsible for the group assignment. The results of the group allocation will be placed in sealed, opaque envelopes and securely stored by designated personnel. The randomisation plan will only be revealed after the intervention.

Throughout the study, the evaluation process will be blinded. The same evaluator will be responsible for assessing all participants, and this evaluator will remain unaware of the participants’ group assignments throughout the study. All participants will have signed informed consent forms and are aware of their group allocation. During the study, if a participant experiences a serious adverse event or a change in their medical condition, unblinding may be required to ensure that the participant receives appropriate and timely treatment. In such cases, approval from the principal investigator must be obtained prior to unblinding, and detailed records of the relevant circumstances should be maintained to minimise potential bias in the study as much as possible.

The study is being conducted at Quanzhou First Hospital Affiliated to Fujian Medical University, with an anticipated enrolment of 46 patients who had a stroke meeting the inclusion criteria outlined in table 1.

Sample size

Since there is currently no research on the combination of DMT and repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation, we referred to previous studies on DMT for upper limb motor function treatment.22 The FMA-UE was used as the primary outcome measure. Based on the post-treatment mean and SD of FMA-UE scores (experimental group: 45.70±14.81; control group: 30.40±16.17), sample size estimation was performed using G*Power (V.3.1.9.7), yielding an effect size of 0.986. With the statistical power (1−β) set at 0.8 and α at 0.05, and allowing for a 20% dropout rate, the final sample size was determined to be 23 participants per group (46 in total).

Intervention

During the intervention period, in addition to undergoing routine rehabilitation training, all patients will receive, in addition to conventional rehabilitation, 15 min of rPMS daily, 1 hour of standardised upper limb functional training with fixed intensity and duration and 30 min of either mirror or non-mirror mode upper limb functional training. Specifically: rPMS group: 15 min of rPMS+1 hour of upper limb functional training of the same intensity and duration+30 min of non-mirror mode upper limb functional training; DMT+rPMS group: 15 min of rPMS+1 hour of upper limb functional training of the same intensity and duration+30 min of mirror mode upper limb functional training.

Repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation group

First, patients undergo peripheral magnetic stimulation, followed by 30 min of non-mirror mode upper limb function training. The specific procedure is as follows:

The device used is the Nanjing Weisi MAgNeuro T200 for peripheral magnetic stimulation. An ‘8’ shaped coil is applied to the affected upper limb at the Erb’s point. The stimulation intensity is set to the minimum level that causes a visible, slight contraction of the affected thumb flexor muscles. The stimulation frequency is 20 Hz, with each sequence consisting of 40 pulses, repeated for 75 sequences. The interval between sequences is 10 s, with a total of 3000 pulses delivered over 15 min.

During the treatment, the patient lies in a supine position, remains awake and places both palms facing upward. When stimulating the Erb’s point, the patient is instructed to tilt their head 30° toward the unaffected side. After the magnetic stimulation, non-mirror task-oriented upper limb function training is performed for 30 min.

Digital mirror therapy combined with repetitive peripheral magnetic stimulation intervention group

In this study, we used the DMT device (Smart Multimodal Mirror Assessment and Training System). This system is equipped with a dual-channel high-resolution USB camera, symmetrically mounted on the top of a 23.8-inch display screen for the patient. It accurately captures the real-time movements of the hand, wrist and forearm, allowing for detailed recording of the patient’s hand, wrist and forearm actions. During the training, patients are instructed to sit comfortably in front of the screen with both hands placed on the tabletop. The design of the patient-end screen blocks the real visual feedback of both hands (see figures35). During the training process, the screen simultaneously displays real-time images of the patient’s unaffected hand and its mirror image. The DMT system includes basic motion training, functional movement training, sensorimotor observation training and more. It is also equipped with a voice-guided system with standard motion demonstration videos, providing comprehensive assistance for the patient’s rehabilitation training.

First, the patients will receive peripheral magnetic stimulation treatment (the method is the same as that of the rPMS group), followed by 30 min of DMT-based upper limb function training. During the MT intervention, the patient will engage in 30 min of MT training. The patient is first guided to focus on observing the image of their affected hand on the screen, with the suggestion that the hand moving on the screen is their own. Simultaneously, the patient is instructed to actively move the affected hand and arm in synchrony with the unaffected hand and arm. If necessary, an experienced therapist will assist the patient in moving the affected hand to ensure it moves in sync with the unaffected hand. The training programme refers to previous studies,22 each 30 min MT training session for the patient will include 4–5 tasks (3–4 basic tasks and 1–2 functional tasks), with each task repeated 60 times. Each treatment session will include 1–2 functional tasks, which will vary across sessions and be designed with progressive difficulty. Within the same group, the task framework will remain consistent: 4–5 tasks will be selected from the task pool (including 3–4 basic tasks and 1–2 functional tasks) to ensure standardisation of the intervention within the group. There will be no difference in movement types between the two groups (eg, grasping, forearm pronation/supination); the key distinction will be that the DMT+rPMS group will receive digital mirror visual feedback, whereas the rPMS group will not. According to the severity of upper limb motor impairment27 (FMA-UE score: severe: 0–28, moderate: 29–42, mild: 43–66), therapists prioritised basic training tasks from the task pool for patients with severe impairment (≤28), while functional training tasks were selected for those with moderate (29–42) and mild (43–66) impairment. Individual adjustments were made based on each patient’s condition to ensure both safety and feasibility. Repetitive task practice was further employed to enhance neuroplasticity and promote motor function recovery.

Study outcomes

The clinical outcome assessments will primarily be conducted before treatment (ie, baseline assessment) and after 4 weeks of treatment, carried out by an independent blinded assessor (an experienced physical therapist). Prior to the study, the assessor underwent relevant training to ensure high inter-rater reliability for the assessments. The baseline assessment conducted before the intervention includes a demographic questionnaire, which gathers information on age, gender, stroke duration (classified as acute/chronic), lesion location, stroke type and other related details.

Primary outcomes

The FMA-UE28 will be used to evaluate motor impairment as the primary outcome. The improvement of upper limb motor function in patients who had a stroke will be assessed using the Fugl-Meyer Upper Extremity Motor Function Assessment Scale. This scale is a performance-based tool suitable for patients who had a stroke and is highly reliable. It consists of 33 items, focusing on three aspects: motor function, reflexes and coordination of the affected upper limb. Each item is scored on a scale of 0, 1 or 2, with higher scores indicating better motor function in the patient.

Adverse events

We will record all adverse events that occur during the treatment process, such as local discomfort, falls, persistent pain and dizziness. The recorded details will include the time, location, event description and the patient’s symptoms.

Data management and analysis

Data management

To ensure comprehensive protection of participant privacy and data security, researchers will use digital codes to replace participants’ personal information after the recruitment process is completed. Data will be collected at two time points: during the baseline phase and after the 4-week intervention period. Two professional data staff members will be responsible for entering the participants’ paper-based data into the same database. Afterwards, a third researcher will review the entered data and cross-check it with the original documents to ensure accuracy. All research data will be stored both in paper form and as electronic files and will be retained for 3 years from the completion of the study.

Data analysis

This study will use the intention-to-treat principle for data analysis. Baseline data include age, sex, stroke duration (acute/chronic), lesion location, stroke type and pretreatment clinical scores. Continuous variables with normal distribution, such as age and FMA-UE, will be presented as mean±SD (x-±s) and analysed using independent t-tests. For non-normally distributed continuous variables, the median (IQR) will be used and analysed with the Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Categorical variables with non-normal distribution, such as MAS and sex, will be compared between groups using χ2 tests or Fisher’s exact test.

For the primary outcome measure, FMA-UE, an analysis of covariance will be conducted. The 4-week post-treatment value will be used as the dependent variable, with group as the independent variable and pretreatment clinical assessments as covariates. This analysis will compare the performance difference between the rPMS+MT group and the rPMS group. We will adjust for baseline FMA-UE scores and potential confounding factors (such as stroke duration) to estimate the adjusted inter-group differences and their 95% CIs. Missing data will be handled using multiple imputation to maintain data completeness. A p value of <0.05 will be considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Discussion

Upper limb motor dysfunction is a common sequela after stroke. Given the high incidence of upper limb impairment and its significant impact on patients’ daily functioning and social participation,35 finding effective rehabilitation methods to improve upper limb function is crucial. Although both DMT and rPMS have been individually proven to improve upper limb motor dysfunction in patients who had a stroke, there is limited evidence regarding the combined treatment of these two methods for upper limb motor function. Therefore, this study will adopt a randomised controlled trial design to compare the effectiveness of DMT+rPMS and rPMS in treating upper limb motor function in patients who had a stroke.

This study has several advantages: first, it innovatively combines DMT and rPMS to treat upper limb motor dysfunction in patients who had a stroke, exploring their synergistic effects. Second, DMT is highly interactive, overcoming the limitations of traditional MT. At the same time, rPMS has high safety and patient acceptance, which helps increase patient participation. Third, this study measures a variety of outcome indicators, including FMA-UE, ARAT, BBT, MBI, MAS, FMA-UE-S, grasp and pinch strength and adverse events. However, there are also several potential limitations. First, this study will be designed as a single-blind controlled trial because blinding participants and therapists will not be feasible. MT will depend on the therapist’s operation of the device and the participant’s visual feedback, making it difficult to blind therapists and only partially effective to blind participants, who will often be able to perceive differences between interventions. Although DMT will be performed in separate rooms, inherent limitations will remain and bias will be mitigated solely through blinded outcome assessment. Second, there is no long-term follow-up, which makes it difficult to observe the long-term effects. Additionally, objective brain function indicators (such as MRI, electroencephalogram analysis) were not measured, and the sample size was relatively small.

In conclusion, this study aims to evaluate the effectiveness of DMT combined with rPMS in treating upper limb motor dysfunction in patients who had a stroke. The results of this study will provide strong evidence for the use of DMT combined with rPMS as an effective treatment method for upper limb function in patients who had a stroke.

Trial status

Currently, this study is in the participant recruitment phase. The first participant was enrolled in December 2024, and according to the research plan, the project is expected to conclude in April 2026.
