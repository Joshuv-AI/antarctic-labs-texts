Effect of SSRIs on clinical progression in amnestic mild cognitive impairment stratified by Alzheimer's disease pathology
Authors: Yan Li, Wenying Liu, Xuechu Wang, Wei Qin, Zhaojun Liu, Diyang lyu, Ying Li, Bingqiu Li, Lingzhi Xu, Shuman Cao, for the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative, Joyce R. F. Chong, Mitchell K. P. Lai, Christopher L. H. Chen, Jianping Jia
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThis study examined whether selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) treatment influenced cognitive trajectory and progression to Alzheimer's disease (AD) dementia in amnestic mild cognitive impairment (MCI) patients, stratified by AD pathology.METHODSFour hundred fifty‐seven amnestic MCI participants in the ADNI database were analyzed. AD pathology was determined by baseline amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau positron emission tomography. Kaplan‐Meier survival analysis and Cox proportional hazards models evaluated MCI‐to‐AD progression. Linear mixed models analyzed longitudinal cognitive trajectories, amyloid accumulation, and cortical thickness.RESULTSSSRI treatment showed no significant effect on AD dementia progression (hazard ratio = 1.64, 95% confidence interval: 0.61 to 4.38) or cognitive trajectories, regardless of AD pathology. No significant differences in Aβ accumulation or cortical thickness were observed between SSRI users and non‐users. External validation confirmed no significant SSRI effect on AD progression or cognitive decline.DISCUSSIONSSRI treatment was not associated with long‐term cognitive effects in amnestic MCI, irrespective of underlying AD pathology.Highlights SSRI treatment was not associated with long‐term AD dementia risk in MCI.SSRI treatment had no impact on long‐term cognitive performance changes in MCI.SSRI treatment did not affect Aβ accumulation or cortical thickness in MCI.SSRIs had no effect on MCI progression, regardless of underlying AD pathology.

INTRODUCTION

Depressive symptoms frequently accompany amnestic mild cognitive impairment (MCI), affecting approximately 50% of patients. 1 , 2 Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) represent one of the most commonly prescribed antidepressants in older adults 3 , 4 and theoretically could slow cognitive decline by addressing this modifiable risk factor. 5 , 6 , 7 However, despite their widespread clinical use, evidence remains inconclusive on whether SSRI treatment actually affects long‐term cognitive outcomes or alters Alzheimer's disease (AD) progression in individuals with MCI. 8 , 9 , 10 This conflicting evidence highlights the need for more nuanced investigation into the complex relationship between SSRI treatment and cognitive outcomes in this vulnerable population.

Existing research on SSRI effects in MCI is limited by insufficient consideration of underlying pathology, as amnestic MCI represents a heterogeneous condition where only some cases reflect prodromal AD – substantially complicating interpretation of treatment outcomes across studies. 11 , 12 , 13 , 14 Amyloid beta (Aβ) and tau pathology are fundamental biomarkers in the A/T/(N) framework for the biological definition of AD. Amnestic MCI patients who are positive for AD pathology demonstrate significantly higher rates of progression to AD dementia. 15 , 16 Molecular imaging examining the relationship between serotonin degeneration and Aβ deposition identified a spatial covariance pattern of decreased serotonin transporters availability alongside increased Aβ accumulation, which was significantly associated with cognitive deficits (memory and executive function) in the MCI group. 17 , 18 Thes findings suggest that SSRI treatment may benefit Aβ‐positive individuals with MCI. In addition, SSRIs may interact with amyloid processing pathways by shifting the balance toward non‐amyloidogenic processing of amyloid precursor protein, potentially influencing Aβ production and clearance in the brain. 19 , 20 , 21 However, clinical evidence of SSRI effects on progression from MCI to AD dementia remains scarce, particularly in studies considering AD pathology – a critical gap given the implications for clinical treatment decisions. Moreover, structural magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) biomarkers reveal neurodegeneration patterns that precede cognitive decline, with AD signature cortical thickness measures in temporal, parietal, and frontal regions offering superior sensitivity for predicting MCI progression compared to whole‐brain volumetric analyses. 22 , 23 Therefore, examining both AD pathology and structural changes in SSRI‐treated MCI patients provides complementary information crucial for understanding potential disease‐modifying effects of SSRI.

This study investigated the influence of SSRI treatment on clinical progression from amnestic MCI to AD dementia and its potential modification stratified by baseline AD pathology. We hypothesized that the effects of SSRIs treatment on dementia risk, cognitive decline, and neuroimaging biomarkers may vary based on underlying AD pathology. A better understanding of this association between SSRI treatment and AD pathology will guide amnestic MCI therapeutic strategies in clinical practice.

METHODS

Study design and inclusion criteria

Primary cohort

Data used in the preparation of this article were obtained from the Alzheimer's Disease Neuroimaging Initiative (ADNI) database (adni.loni.usc.edu) (ADNI 1, 2, 3, 4, GO). The ADNI was launched in 2003 as a public‐private partnership, led by Principal Investigator Michael W. Weiner, MD. The primary goal of ADNI has been to test whether serial MRI, positron emission tomography (PET), other biological markers, and clinical and neuropsychological assessment can be combined to measure the progression of MCI and early AD. After obtaining Institutional Review Board approval from each participating site and informed consent from all participants, individuals with amnestic MCI were enrolled. Study participants aged 55 to 90 years were screened to exclude those with Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fourth Edition (DSM‐IV) major depressive disorder. 24 Data were extracted from the ADNI database as of August 8, 2025, where diagnostic classifications followed standard ADNI criteria. Normal cognition (NC) individuals met the following inclusion criteria: Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) performance between 24 and 30 points, Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) of 0, preserved memory performance on Wechsler Memory Scale Logical Memory II (WMS‐II) assessment, and no notable cognitive or functional decline. Amnestic MCI classification was based on MMSE range of 24 and 30, presence of memory concerns validated through education‐corrected WMS‐II performance, CDR rating of 0.5, and preservation of activities of daily living. 25 AD dementia identification throughout the study period followed National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke‐Alzheimer's Disease and Related Disorders Association (NINCDS‐ADRDA) guidelines for probable AD, characterized by CDR ratings ≥ 1. 26

A total of 3773 participants from the ADNI database were initially screened. Of these, 3105 were excluded due to insufficient medication documentation, incomplete neuropsychiatric assessment, or baseline diagnosis of NC or dementia. Among the remaining 668 participants with amnestic MCI, 42 were excluded for non‐SSRI antidepressant use. A further 139 were excluded for either having insufficient medication follow‐up (fewer than two visits) or only a single recorded instance of any antidepressant use. This resulted in 487 eligible participants with amnestic MCI, comprising 332 non‐users and 155 SSRI users. From this cohort, 30 participants with baseline plasma tau phosphorylated at threonine 181 (p‐tau181) were selected for matching with an external validation cohort from Singapore (detailed below), leaving 457 participants (140 SSRI users and 317 non‐users) in the primary analysis group (Figure 1).

RESEARCH IN CONTEXT

Systematic review: A comprehensive literature search of PubMed and Google Scholar databases through 2025 revealed limited evidence on SSRI treatment outcomes in amnestic MCI patients, particularly when stratified by AD pathology. Existing studies yielded inconsistent results and suffered from inadequate AD biomarker characterization, precluding clear understanding of SSRI effects on MCI‐to‐AD progression. Interpretation: Using longitudinal ADNI data, SSRI treatment was not associated with long‐term changes in AD dementia risk, cognitive performance, Aβ accumulation, or cortical thickness in individuals with amnestic MCI. This effect was independent of baseline AD pathology and duration of use. Future directions: Large‐scale, long‐term randomized controlled trials incorporating comprehensive AD biomarker panels for biological staging are needed to clarify the clinical effects of SSRIs in this at‐risk population.

Validation cohort

For validation purposes, we utilized data from an ongoing, prospective memory clinic cohort study conducted by National University Hospital (NUH) in Singapore. This study enrolled participants aged 50 and older who were followed for up to 5 years with annual clinical and neuropsychological assessments. This study was conducted in full accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and received ethical approval from the National Healthcare Group Domain Specific Review Board. Prior to enrollment, all participants gave written informed consent in their preferred language. Following clinical assessment, participants were categorized into three distinct clinical groups. Participants with NC were recruited from the community and the NUH memory clinic, showing no signs of cognitive deficits on objective neuropsychological tests. The MCI category included individuals with impairment in at least one cognitive domain on a comprehensive, locally validated test battery who did not meet DSM‐IV criteria for dementia. 24 Within this category, those with objective memory impairment received a diagnosis of amnestic MCI. 25 Finally, the clinical dementia group consisted of individuals diagnosed according to the DSM‐IV, 24 with etiologies determined by NINCDS‐ADRDA criteria for probable AD and NINDS‐AIREN criteria for vascular dementia. 26 , 27 Individuals were excluded if they had a history of major psychiatric conditions, substance abuse, tumors, multiple sclerosis, epilepsy, or cognitive impairment from a traumatic brain injury. In accordance with the 2024 revised criteria for the diagnosis and staging of AD from the Alzheimer's Association workgroup, we incorporated amyloid PET or plasma p‐tau181 to stratify participants into amyloid‐positive and amyloid‐negative groups. 28

The Singapore memory cohort included 700 subjects. Of these, 541 were excluded due to insufficient medication records and neuropsychiatric assessments or having a baseline diagnosis of NC or non‐amnestic MCI or dementia; and 7 were excluded for having ever used non‐SSRI antidepressants or having SSRI Unidentifieduse fewer than 2 medication records. This resulted in an eligible population of 152 individuals with amnestic MCI, comprising 15 SSRI users and 137 non‐users. To construct the validation cohort, 15 SSRI users were matched with 15 non‐users from the Singapore memory cohort using propensity score matching (PSM) based on baseline characteristics to create a balanced comparison. To enhance statistical power, this initial group of 30 participants from the Singapore memory cohort was supplemented with 30 PSM‐matched amnestic MCI participants from the ADNI cohort. These ADNI participants, who were not included in the primary analysis, had baseline plasma p‐tau181 measurements and complete medication records, resulting in a total validation cohort of 60 amnestic MCI participants. The analysis adhered to the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines for observational cohort studies.

SSRI exposure

SSRI users took citalopram, escitalopram, paroxetine, fluoxetine, fluvoxamine, or sertraline at baseline and at least two follow‐ups. Non‐users never received antidepressants and completed at least two follow‐ups. Detailed information regarding baseline drug categories and treatment duration of SSRI users can be found in Table S1 and Table S2 in the Supporting Information. Exposure was assumed constant, limiting confounding from disease progression on treatment decisions, mimicking an intention‐to‐treat design.

Neuropsychological assessment

Cognitive function was evaluated at baseline and annually using standardized assessments. Participants in the ADNI cohort were assessed for global cognition via the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE), disease severity via the Clinical Dementia Rating Sum of Boxes (CDR‐SB), and specific domains through ADNI composite scores (ADNI‐MEM for memory, ADNI‐EF for executive function). Participants in the Singapore memory cohort completed the same assessments, with the exception of the ADNI composite scores. Cognitive decline manifested as increased CDR‐SB scores and reduced MMSE, ADNI‐MEM, and ADNI‐EF scores. For all participants, depressive symptoms were assessed using the 15‐item Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS), with a score of ≥6 indicating depression. 8 , 29

Aβ and tau PET analysis and structural MRI imaging in ADNI cohort

Aβ status was determined using [18F] florbetapir (FBP) or [18F] Forbetaben (FBB) PET imaging according to standardized ADNI protocols. PET images underwent spatial smoothing with a 6‐mm Gaussian kernel and were co‐registered to structural MRI scans using Statistical Parametric Mapping software (https://www.fil.ion.ucl.ac.uk/spm/). For both FBP‐PET and FBB‐PET scans, region of interest (ROI)‐based standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) were calculated by dividing the tracer uptake in each cortical region by the mean uptake in the whole cerebellum, which was used as the reference region. Participants were classified as amyloid‐positive if their global SUVR exceeded the established threshold of 1.11(FBP PET) and 1.08 (FBB PET) as recommended by ADNI. Detailed methodological procedures are available in ADNI's published analysis protocols “Florbetapir (AV45) processing methods” (https://adni.bitbucket.io/reference/docs/UCBERKELEYAV45/UCBERKELEY_AV45_Methods_11.15.2021.pdf) and “Florbetaben (FBB) processing methods” (https://adni.bitbucket.io/reference/docs/UCBERKELEYFBB/UCBerkeley_FBB_Methods_11.15.2021.pdf). To standardize the Aβ PET measurements, SUVRs from both FBP and FBB PET scans were converted to the Centiloid scale. This conversion was performed according to the established ADNI pipeline “Centiloid Level‐2 Analysis of [18F]Florbetaben (FBB) and [18F]Florbetapir (FBP) PET Image Data using the ADNI Pipeline” (https://adni.loni.usc.edu/wp‐content/themes/freshnews‐dev‐v2/documents/pet/ADNI%20Centiloids%20Final.pdf) using the following formulas: for FBP PET: Centiloid = (196.9×SUVRFBP​) − 196.03; for FBB PET: Centiloid = (159.08×SUVRFBB​) − 151.65.

For tau PET, the uptake of [18F] flortaucipir (AV1451) reliably indicates the presence of advanced tau pathology (Braak stages V‐VI). 30 To summarize the tracer uptake, we used a standard composite Temporal‐metaROI (including entorhinal, parahippocampal, fusiform, amygdala, inferior temporal, and middle temporal) using inferior cerebellar cortex intensity normalization. 31 A previously established threshold of 1.37 for this Temporal‐metaROI was applied to determine tau PET positivity (T+). 32

Three‐dimensional T1‐weighted MRI scans were obtained using 3T scanners across ADNI phases (ADNI2, ADNI3, ADNI‐GO), employing IR‐SPGR or MPRAGE protocols (sagittal plane, TR/TE/TI = 2300/2.95/900 ms, flip angle 9°, 26 cm FOV, 256 × 256 matrix, 1.2 mm thickness). The FreeSurfer (version 7.2, Laboratory for Computational Neuroimaging, Athinoula A. Martinos Center for Biomedical Imaging, Massachusetts General Hospital, MA, USA) was used to analyze cortical thickness, measured as the pial to white‐matter surface distance. 33 The analysis focused on two distinct regional patterns: (1) AD‐signature regions, comprising nine ROIs associated with AD‐specific neurodegeneration, including the medial temporal, inferior temporal, temporal pole, angular, supramarginal, superior parietal, precuneus, superior frontal, and middle frontal cortices; and (2) aging‐signature regions, consisting of eight ROIs characterized by atrophy in normal aging, including the calcarine, cuneus, lateral occipital, caudal fusiform, caudal insula, dorsomedial frontal, precentral, and inferior frontal cortices. 22 , 34 Each signature's thickness was calculated as the mean value across its constituent ROIs.

Plasma biomarkers

In the Singapore memory cohort, blood samples were collected from non‐fasting participants into ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid tubes. These samples were subjected to centrifugation at 2000 × g for 10 min at a temperature of 4°C. Following this, the resulting plasma was carefully extracted, thoroughly mixed, and dispensed in 0.2‐mL aliquots into polypropylene tubes for storage at −80°C until analysis. The concentration of plasma p‐tau181 was quantified using a commercially available Single Molecule Array (Simoa) ptau‐181 Advantage V2 kit on a Simoa HD‐1 Analyzer, following the manufacturer's protocol (Quanterix), as previously described. 35 Using a three‐range reference for amyloid PET positivity, the cutoff for plasma p‐tau181 was found to be 2.35 pg/mL in the Singapore memory cohort (Figure S1 in the Supporting Information).

For the ADNI database samples, the analysis of plasma p‐tau181 was conducted at the Clinical Neurochemistry Laboratory at the University of Gothenburg, Sweden. This analysis utilized an in‐house Simoa assay, which employs a pair of monoclonal antibodies (Tau12 and AT270) to detect N‐terminal to mid‐domain p‐tau181 fragments. 36 The established cutoff value for plasma p‐tau181 positivity in the ADNI cohort was 18.85 pg/mL. 37

Statistical analysis

To minimize potential selection bias, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was employed to balance baseline characteristics between SSRI users and non‐users in unstratified population. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression with treatment status as the dependent variable and potential confounders as predictors, including age, gender, education, apolipoprotein E (APOE) ɛ4 status, baseline Aβ status, presence of depression, baseline cognitive assessment, and concurrent medication use (antidementia, benzodiazepines, and antipsychotics). Weights were calculated as the inverse of propensity score (for treated, SSRI users) or inverse of 1‐propensity score (for untreated, non‐users), then normalized after trimming extreme values. For subgroup analyses, PSM was used because IPTW could not achieve adequate covariate balance in smaller sample sizes. Specifically, a 1:1 PSM was implemented using a genetic matching algorithm, which was selected to optimize between‐group comparability and ensure robust covariate equilibrium for analyses of AD pathology and neuroimaging. Group comparisons employed chi‐squared tests for categorical variables, with Fisher exact test applied when any expected cell frequency was less than 5 and Mann‐Whitney U tests for continuous variables. Variables with less than 5% missing data were imputed using multivariate imputation by chained equations. 38 Five imputed datasets were generated and pooled for statistical analyses.

Kaplan‐Meier survival analysis was used to estimate time to progression to AD dementia in SSRI users versus non‐users, with differences between survival curves assessed using the log‐rank test. Cox proportional hazards models quantified the association between SSRI use and progression to AD in both unadjusted (Model 1) and adjusted models (Model 2) controlling for age, gender, education, APOE ɛ4 status, baseline cognition, depression, and concurrent medication use (anti‐dementia drugs, benzodiazepines, and antipsychotic drugs). Interaction terms examined whether Aβ status modified treatment effects. Linear mixed‐effects (LME) models were used to assess differences in cognitive function between SSRI users and non‐users. The models were adjusted for age, gender, education, APOE ε4 status, baseline cognition, depression, and concurrent medication use (antidementia drugs, benzodiazepines, and antipsychotics). Similarly, LME models were used to examine longitudinal changes in neuroimaging markers (AD signature regions, aging‐only signature regions, and cortical Centiloids). These models were adjusted for the same covariates, with an additional adjustment for the baseline value of the respective imaging outcome.

Sensitivity analyses were conducted, including (1) excluding participants with incident dementia diagnosed within 1 year of the index date to minimize potential reverse causation (N = 443) and (2) incorporating additional potential dementia risk factors (body mass index [BMI], history of smoking, and history of alcohol abuse) in a subset of participants for whom these data were available (N = 318). All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.0.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), with a two‐sided p < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Baseline characteristics

The study comprised 457 participants with amnestic MCI aged 55 to 90 years (196 [42.89%] female; mean age 72.16 ± 7.46 years; education 16.22 ± 2.55 years) with mean follow‐up of 3.95 ± 2.61 years. Before IPTW adjustment, SSRI users (N = 140) compared to non‐users (N = 317) were significantly younger (70.32 vs 72.97 years, p < 0.001), more likely female (51.40% vs 39.10%, p = 0.02). SSRI users also showed higher rates of benzodiazepine use (5.00% vs 1.60%, p = 0.05) (Table 1). IPTW adjustment removed many of the significant imbalances, creating comparable groups for outcome analyses. Additionally, 1:1 PSM was employed for SSRI‐amyloid stratification in the ADNI cohort with detailed characteristics presented in Table S3 in the Supporting Information.

Effects of SSRI treatment on MCI‐to‐AD progression and cognitive trajectories

Kaplan‐Meier analyses showed no difference in progression rates from amnestic MCI to AD between SSRI users and non‐users before (log‐rank χ2 = 1.70, p = 0.20) and after IPTW (log‐rank χ2 < 0.001, p = 0.99) (Figure 2). The IPTW‐adjusted Cox proportional hazards models showed no overall SSRI effect on AD dementia progression between SSRI users and non‐users (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.64, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.61 to 4.38, p = 0.32) and no significant interaction between SSRI use and Aβ status (HR = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.28 to 2.27, p = 0.68) (Table 2). LME models revealed that there was no difference in MMSE (β = 0.11; 95% CI: −0.01 to 0.23; p = 0.06), CDR‐SB (β = −0.001; 95% CI: −0.08 to 0.08; p = 0.92), ADNI‐MEM (β = 0.01; 95% CI: −0.01 to 0.03; p = 0.59), and ADNI‐EF (β = 0.01; 95% CI: −0.01 to 0.03; p = 0.45) between SSRI users and non‐users (Table S4 in the Supporting Information).

Stratification analysis of SSRI effects on MCI‐to‐AD progression among different AD pathology groups

Participants were stratified into four groups based on amyloid status and SSRI use: Aβ+/non‐users, Aβ+/SSRI users, Aβ−/non‐users, and Aβ−/SSRI users. Among Aβ+ individuals, Kaplan‐Meier analysis showed no significant difference in the progression from amnestic MCI to AD between SSRI users and non‐users (log‐rank χ2 = 1.40, p = 0.20; Figure 3). This was corroborated by Cox model analysis, which also found no significant effect of SSRI use on conversion in either unadjusted (HR = 1.32, 95% CI: 0.83 to 2.11; p = 0.24) or adjusted models (HR = 1.55, 95% CI: 0.92 to 2.61; p = 0.10) (Table 3). Similarly, SSRI use showed no significant effect on MCI‐to‐AD progression in Aβ− participants between SSRI users and non‐users (Kaplan‐Meier analysis: log‐rank χ2 = 0.04, p = 0.80, Figure 3; Cox model: unadjusted: HR = 1.13, 95% CI: 0.37 to 3.43, p = 0.57; adjusted: HR = 1.69, 95% CI: 0.45 to 6.36, p = 0.44). Compared to non‐users, SSRI use was also not associated with changes in cognitive and functional scores (MMSE, CDR‐SB, ADNI‐MEM, and ADNI‐EF) in either the Aβ+ or Aβ− subgroups (Table S5 in the Supporting Information).

Effect of SSRI treatment duration on MCI‐to‐AD progression

To examine the potential effect of treatment duration, we stratified participants into short‐term and long‐term SSRI groups based on the median treatment duration of 2.4 years. Kaplan‐Meier analysis revealed no significant difference in the progression from amnestic MCI to AD between the short‐term and long‐term SSRI groups, either in the total population (log‐rank χ2 = 0.11, p = 0.74) or within the Aβ+ (log‐rank χ2 = 0.10, p = 0.70) and Aβ− subgroups (log‐rank χ2 = 0.60, p = 0.40) (Figure S3). Consistent with these findings, Cox proportional‐hazards analysis also showed no significant association between SSRI duration and progression from amnestic MCI to AD in the total population (HR = 0.64, 95% CI: 0.32 to 1.25, p = 0.19), the Aβ+ subgroup (HR = 0.62, 95% CI: 0.27 to 1.41, p = 0.25), or the Aβ− subgroup (HR = 0.51, 95% CI: 0.06 to 4.17, p = 0.53) (Table S7).

Effects of SSRI treatment on longitudinal changes in amyloid accumulation and cortical thickness

Analysis revealed no significant differences in global amyloid burden (Centiloids) between SSRI users and non‐users at baseline or throughout the 12‐year follow‐up period. Similarly, when stratified by baseline amyloid status, amyloid burden in Aβ+ individuals showed no significant differences between groups follow‐up assessments (Figure 4 and Table S8 in the Supporting Information). Examination of region‐specific cortical thickness showed no detectable differences between groups in either AD‐signature regions or aging‐signature regions. The absence of significant differences remained consistent between SSRI users and non‐users in the Aβ+ subgroup (Figure 4, and Table S9 in the Supporting Information).

Validation cohort

The validation cohort included 60 subjects (50.00% female; mean age: 72.25 ± 7.08 years; mean education: 10.48 ± 4.98 years) who were followed for a mean of 2.61 ± 1.70 years. Baseline characteristics for the cohort and the amyloid‐stratified population are detailed in Table S10 and Table S11, respectively, in the Supporting Information. The validation cohort replicated the primary results, showing no significant effect of SSRIs on MCI‐to‐AD progression or cognitive decline.

In the analysis of the entire validation cohort, Kaplan‐Meier curves showed no significant difference in the rate of progression from amnestic MCI to AD between SSRI users and non‐users (log‐rank χ2 = 0.50, p = 0.50) (Figure S4). Consistent with this, the Cox proportional hazards model revealed no overall effect of SSRI use on MCI‐to‐AD progression (HR = 0.82, 95% CI: 0.20 to 3.40; p = 0.79), nor a significant interaction between SSRI use and Aβ status (HR = 1.98, 95% CI: 0.34 to 11.61; p = 0.45) (Table S12). Furthermore, LME models showed no significant differences between SSRI users and non‐users in the rates of change for the MMSE (β = −0.07; 95% CI: −0.50 to 0.36; p = 0.74) or the CDR‐SB (β = −0.08; 95% CI: −0.41 to 0.25; p = 0.64) (Table S13).

In a secondary analysis stratified by amyloid status, these findings remained consistent. Within the Aβ+ subgroup, there was no significant difference in MCI‐to‐AD progression between SSRI users and non‐users, as shown by Kaplan‐Meier analysis (log‐rank χ2 = 0.90, p = 0.30; Figure S5) and both unadjusted (HR = 1.65, 95% CI: 0.59 to 4.62; p = 0.34) and adjusted Cox models (HR = 1.41, 95% CI: 0.35 to 5.77; p = 0.63) (Table S14). Similarly, in the Aβ− subgroup, SSRI use was not associated with MCI‐to‐AD progression in either unadjusted (HR = 1.27, 95% CI: 0.34 to 4.72; p = 0.72) or adjusted models (HR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.23 to 4.25; p = 0.99) (Table S14). Finally, no association was found between SSRI use and changes in cognitive scores (MMSE and CDR‐SB) within either the Aβ+ or Aβ− subgroups (Table S15 in the Supporting Information). ,

Sensitivity analyses

To assess the robustness of the findings and address potential reverse causality, sensitivity analyses were conducted excluding early AD converters (subjects who progressed to AD within 1 year of the index date). These analyses replicated both the IPTW approach for the overall population and the 1:1 PSM for Aβ status stratification (Table S16 and Table S17). Kaplan‐Meier curves showed no significant difference in the progression rate from amnestic MCI to AD between SSRI users and non‐users. This finding was consistent across the total population (log‐rank χ2 = 0.10, p = 0.75), the Aβ+ subgroup (log‐rank χ2 = 0.00, p = 0.90), and the Aβ− subgroup (log‐rank χ2 = 0.30, p = 0.60) (Figure S6 and S7 in the Supporting Information). The Cox proportional hazards model revealed no significant association between SSRI use and AD progression in either the unstratified population (HR = 1.49, 95% CI: 0.54 to 4.12, p = 0.45, Table S18 in the Supporting Information) or the stratified subgroups (Aβ+ group: HR = 1.22, 95% CI: 0.70 to 2.13, p = 0.47; Aβ− group: HR = 2.35, 95% CI: 0.49 to 11.34, p = 0.29, Table S19 in the Supporting Information). Additional adjustment for potential dementia risk covariates (BMI, history of smoking, and history of alcohol abuse) similarly showed no significant effect of SSRI use on AD progression in the total population (HR = 1.35, 95% CI: 0.86 to 2.12, p = 0.19), the Aβ+ subgroup (HR = 1.46, 95% CI: 0.81 to 2.65, p = 0.21), or the Aβ− subgroup (HR = 1.85, 95% CI: 0.40 to 8.56, p = 0.43) (Table S20).

DISCUSSION

In this study, SSRI treatment was not associated with the rate of clinical progression to AD dementia, cognitive decline, or changes in amyloid accumulation and cortical thickness among older adults with amnestic MCI, regardless of their baseline AD pathology or duration of SSRI exposure.

Various research efforts have previously investigated whether antidepressants affect cognitive outcomes in MCI populations, with mixed results. 8 , 39 , 40 , 41 , 42 The current investigation found no significant effect of SSRI treatment on MCI‐to‐AD dementia progression, aligning with several prior reports. 8 , 39 , 40 , 41 Two retrospective studies, one examining MCI patients and another investigating both cognitively impaired and cognitively normal participants, demonstrated that SSRI treatment showed no significant beneficial effects on cognitive outcomes. 39 , 41 An 8‐week clinical trial showed that fluoxetine enhanced cognition in patients with MCI. 42 However, the follow‐up time was relatively short and unlikely to conclusively demonstrate the long‐term effects of cognitive benefit. One study of MCI patients revealed nuanced temporal effects – while SSRI treatment reduced progression risk during the first 3 years of follow‐up, this protective effect diminished after extended observation periods beyond 3 years. 8

Interpreting the observed association between antidepressant use and dementia has been persistently challenged by confounding by indication and the potential for reverse causation. Because depression is an established risk factor for dementia, 43 an apparent link between antidepressant treatment and increased dementia risk may emerge even without a causal relationship. Furthermore, depressive symptoms frequently manifest during the prodromal phase of dementia, either as a psychological reaction to cognitive decline or due to shared neurobiological pathways. 44 This can lead to antidepressant prescription as a consequence of the early neurodegenerative process itself. To address potential reverse causation, analyses for this study were stratified by baseline AD pathology. Initial results showed that SSRI use had no discernible effect on progression from amnestic MCI to dementia or on cognitive decline, regardless of baseline Aβ status. To refine this analysis, participants were further stratified by incorporating baseline tau PET, which allowed for a more precise AD biological staging. This refined analysis confirmed the primary findings, demonstrating that SSRIs did not modify disease progression in amnestic MCI, irrespective of the underlying amyloid and tau pathological stage.

The potential association between the duration of SSRI treatment and the rate of conversion from amnestic MCI to AD dementia was subsequently investigated. No significant association was found, a result that contrasts with previous research indicating that long‐term SSRI treatment delays AD conversion compared to short‐term use. 8 A potential explanation for this discrepancy is that different treatment durations may reflect distinct underlying pathologies. Longer antidepressant exposure could correspond to early‐onset depression, whereas shorter treatment durations might signify late‐onset depression, a condition potentially associated with prodromal AD. Therefore, the previously observed delay in AD progression may not be attributable to the duration of SSRI exposure itself but rather to the distinct underlying pathologies. To mitigate this potential confound, the present analysis stratified participants by baseline Aβ status to control for underlying AD pathology. After stratification, the duration of SSRI treatment remained unassociated with dementia conversion, regardless of baseline Aβ status. Because this was the first study, to our knowledge, to investigate the role of SSRIs in the progression of amnestic MCI based on AD pathology, these findings await confirmation from future prospective clinical trials.

Current longitudinal analysis over a 12‐year follow‐up period revealed no significant differences in global amyloid burden between SSRI users and non‐users. These findings align with previous investigations examining SSRI effects on amyloid pathology, which have consistently failed to demonstrate direct effects on amyloid deposition. Previous studies employing different biomarker modalities – CSF Aβ1‐42 or amyloid PET – found no significant SSRI effects on amyloid burden at baseline or during longitudinal follow‐up. 8 , 41 , 45 Neuroanatomical analyses revealed no differences in cortical thickness or volumetric measurements between treatment groups over the 12‐year observation period. Both groups exhibited comparable rates of atrophy in AD signature and aging signature regions, with no significant treatment‐by‐time interactions in any brain structures examined. AD‐signature and aging‐signature measures demonstrate superior sensitivity compared to global gray matter volume assessments, as regional cortical thickness provides a biologically meaningful and highly reliable biomarker that can detect subtle atrophy patterns characteristic of neurodegenerative diseases. 22 , 46 While a previous 2‐year study reported that SSRIs reduced frontotemporal gray matter atrophy in a mixed MCI/AD population, discrepancies with findings in amnestic MCI cohorts were likely attributable to population heterogeneity and the prior study's relatively short follow‐up period. 45 Consistent with the current results, a prospective study found that SSRI treatment was not associated with brain atrophy in dementia‐free participants. 10

The current study has several limitations. First, the observational nature of the study precludes definitive causal inference; unmeasured factors might contribute to both SSRI prescription and differential progression rates. Second, lacking detailed SSRI information (dosage, duration, adherence) and excluding other antidepressant classes may exert different effects on MCI progression. Third, the sample was relatively small and primarily composed of White, educated, non‐Hispanic individuals, potentially limiting generalizability to more diverse populations. Fourth, incomplete and inconsistent SSRI dosage data precluded dose‐group stratification due to low statistical power and the calculation of a cumulative defined daily dose.

In conclusion, this study showed that SSRI treatment was not associated with long‐term changes in AD dementia risk, cognitive performance, Aβ accumulation, or cortical thickness in individuals with amnestic MCI, irrespective of their baseline AD pathology. These observational findings warranted confirmation through long‐term, randomized controlled trials that incorporated a comprehensive panel of AD biomarkers for biological staging to clarify the clinical effect of SSRIs in this at‐risk population.
