AES-SDM meta-analysis of spontaneous brain activity in cerebral small vessel disease
Authors: Yue Li, Zhenlu Yang, Rongpin Wang, Zi Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) is a major cause of vascular cognitive impairment and dementia, characterized by microvascular dysfunction and disruption of cortico-subcortical circuits. Although resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) studies have reported altered spontaneous brain activity in CSVD, their findings remain inconsistent across studies.MethodsA coordinate-based meta-analysis was performed using Anisotropic Effect-Size Signed Differential Mapping (AES-SDM) to quantitatively synthesize rs-fMRI studies investigating spontaneous neural activity in patients with CSVD. Relevant literature published up to October 19, 2025 was systematically retrieved from PubMed, Embase, Web of Science, China National Knowledge Infrastructure, and Wanfang. Studies reporting between-group differences in local spontaneous brain activity were included. A random-effects model was applied with thresholds of uncorrected p < 0.005, peak height Z > 1, and cluster extent ≥ 5 voxels. Heterogeneity, sensitivity, and publication bias were assessed using Q/I² tests, Jackknife analysis, and Egger’s test, respectively.ResultsNine studies comprising 277 CSVD patients and 287 healthy controls were included. Compared with controls, CSVD patients exhibited increased spontaneous brain activity in the bilateral postcentral gyri, bilateral precuneus, and left inferior frontal gyrus. No brain regions showed decreased activity. Egger’s test indicated no significant publication bias (all p > 0.05), and Jackknife analysis confirmed the stability of results across all iterations.ConclusionThis AES-SDM meta-analysis identified convergent increases in spontaneous brain activity in the somatosensory cortex, precuneus, and inferior frontal gyrus in CSVD. These results provide neuroimaging evidence of functional disruption in CSVD and help clarify the neural substrates potentially associated with its clinical manifestations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-026-02327-x.

Introduction

Cerebral small vessel disease (CSVD) comprises a group of pathological processes affecting the small arteries, arterioles, venules, and capillaries of the brain. It is one of the major causes of vascular cognitive impairment and dementia in older adults [1]. The characteristic neuroimaging features of CSVD include white matter hyperintensities, lacunes, cerebral microbleeds, and enlarged perivascular spaces, reflecting widespread microvascular dysfunction and subsequent disruption of cortico-subcortical circuits [2]. However, the mechanisms by which these structural alterations influence spontaneous neural activity within the brain remain insufficiently understood.

Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) provides a noninvasive and reproducible technique for investigating intrinsic brain activity by capturing low-frequency fluctuations in the blood oxygen level–dependent (BOLD) signal [3]. Commonly used indices include the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) and its derivatives—fractional ALFF (fALFF) and dynamic ALFF (dALFF)—which represent the intensity and temporal variability of spontaneous neural activity [4, 5]. Another important measure, regional homogeneity (ReHo), quantifies the synchronization of BOLD signals among neighboring voxels [6]. Conceptually, ALFF/fALFF primarily quantify the amplitude of local low-frequency oscillations, ReHo reflects the local temporal coherence of spontaneous activity, and dALFF captures time-varying fluctuations in ALFF (typically estimated using a sliding-window approach), thereby providing complementary characterizations of intrinsic brain dynamics. Accordingly, integrating these metrics can improve the sensitivity for identifying convergent alterations in spontaneous activity across studies that use different, yet related, local rs-fMRI measures [7]. Collectively, these indices index intrinsic spontaneous neural activity from complementary perspectives (i.e., amplitude, local synchrony, and temporal variability) and have been widely used in neurological and psychiatric neuroimaging research [8].

In recent years, multiple rs-fMRI studies have reported altered spontaneous brain activity in patients with CSVD; however, their findings have been inconsistent [9–17]. Some studies observed decreased ALFF or ReHo in frontal and parietal regions [10, 13], suggesting reduced neuronal efficiency or metabolic decline, whereas others reported increased activity in the sensorimotor, parietal, or default mode–related areas, possibly reflecting functional modulation or adaptive adjustments to microvascular injury [12]. These discrepancies may stem from variations in study populations, disease burden, MRI acquisition parameters, preprocessing pipelines, and statistical thresholds [18]. Therefore, a quantitative meta-analytic approach is required to synthesize existing evidence and to identify convergent spatial patterns of spontaneous brain activity changes in CSVD.

Anisotropic Effect-Size Signed Differential Mapping (AES-SDM) is a coordinate-based meta-analytic technique that integrates peak coordinates and corresponding effect-size information from individual studies to reconstruct whole-brain statistical maps and quantify the consistency of functional alterations [19]. By accounting for sample size, study heterogeneity, and potential publication bias, AES-SDM has been successfully applied to functional neuroimaging research in various neurological conditions such as stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, and Parkinson’s disease [20–22].

Given the inconsistency observed across previous rs-fMRI studies, a systematic and quantitative synthesis is needed to identify brain regions showing convergent alterations in spontaneous brain activity in cerebral small vessel disease. Coordinate-based meta-analysis using AES-SDM provides a robust framework for assessing spatial convergence across neuroimaging studies and allows integration of findings derived from different rs-fMRI metrics [23]. Therefore, the present study aimed to apply AES-SDM to integrate rs-fMRI studies of CSVD and to identify the convergent patterns of altered spontaneous brain activity. This work is expected to enhance understanding of the potential neurobiological mechanisms underlying CSVD and to provide imaging-based evidence for early detection and monitoring of disease progression.

Methods

Literature search and selection

This meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) guidelines [24]. The study protocol was registered in PROSPERO (registration number: CRD420251207059). A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Embase, Web of Science (WOS), China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), and Wanfang Data for all relevant studies published up to October 19, 2025, without restrictions on publication year. The search strategy included the following keywords: (“cerebral small vessel disease” OR CSVD OR “small vessel disease”) AND (resting OR “resting-state” OR rsfMRI OR “resting state”) AND (ReHo OR “regional homogeneity” OR dReHo OR ALFF OR fALFF OR dALFF OR mALFF OR “amplitude of low-frequency fluctuation” OR “fractional ALFF” OR “dynamic ALFF” OR “mean amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations”). In addition, manual screening of potentially relevant studies was performed to ensure completeness of inclusion.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria: (1) Participants were diagnosed with CSVD based on recognized clinical or imaging criteria and included a healthy control group; (2) The study employed whole-brain voxel-wise rs-fMRI to compare CSVD patients with healthy controls (HCs); (3) Results reported between-group differences in ReHo, ALFF, fALFF, or dALFF, providing peak coordinates (in the Montreal Neurological Institute [MNI] or Talairach space) and corresponding statistical values (t or Z); and (4) Peer-reviewed original articles published in English or Chinese.

Exclusion criteria: (1) Studies limited to region-of-interest (ROI) analyses or those that did not report whole-brain results; (2) Studies lacking sufficient coordinate or statistical information for AES-SDM reconstruction; (3) Duplicate or overlapping datasets (only the study with the largest or most complete dataset was retained); or (4) Studies involving participants with other major neurological or psychiatric disorders (e.g., stroke, Alzheimer’s disease, or depression).

Although dReHo was included in our search terms, no eligible whole-brain voxel-wise CSVD studies reporting dReHo were identified; therefore, dReHo was not included in the present meta-analysis.

Data extraction and quality assessment

Two independent reviewers (Li Yue & Wang Rongpin) screened and extracted data from eligible studies, with discrepancies resolved through discussion or consultation. For each included study, the following information was extracted: first author, publication year, sample size (CSVD and HCs), mean age, sex ratio, MRI scanner field strength (1.5T or 3T), statistical threshold and correction method, as well as brain regions showing significant between-group differences, peak coordinates (x, y, z), statistical values (t/Z), cluster size, and direction of change (CSVD > HCs or CSVD < HCs).

The methodological quality of each study was assessed using a modified Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) [25], combined with a checklist for rs-fMRI methodological reporting (covering study design, data preprocessing, and multiple-comparison correction). Only studies rated as moderate or high quality (NOS score ≥ 6) were included in the meta-analysis.

AES-SDM meta-analysis

The meta-analysis was conducted using AES-SDM implemented in the SDM-PSI software package (version 6.21, www.sdmproject.com). All reported peak coordinates were converted into MNI space (Talairach coordinates were converted using the built-in transform function). Effect-size maps were reconstructed with an anisotropic Gaussian kernel (full width at half maximum, FWHM = 20 mm), following standard AES-SDM methodological recommendations [26]. The anisotropic kernel models the spatial uncertainty surrounding reported peak coordinates and improves the reconstruction of effect-size maps, weighted by sample size and variance. A random-effects model was applied to estimate the mean difference in spontaneous brain activity across the whole brain. The statistical thresholds (uncorrected p < 0.005, peak height Z > 1, and cluster extent ≥ 5 voxels) were selected according to validated AES-SDM guidelines, which provide an optimal balance between sensitivity and specificity in coordinate-based neuroimaging meta-analyses [26]. Sensitivity analysis was performed using the leave-one-out (Jackknife) method to evaluate result stability. Egger’s regression test was used to detect potential publication bias (p < 0.05 considered significant). Given the limited number of included studies and reduced statistical power, subgroup and meta-regression analyses were not performed to avoid unstable or spurious findings. Future studies with larger sample sizes may further explore the effects of imaging parameters, clinical characteristics, and cognitive status on the observed results.

Visualization and reproducibility

Significant clusters were anatomically labeled using the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas, and the results were visualized using Mango software. All coordinates are reported in MNI space.

Results

Literature selection and study characteristics

A total of 1,596 articles were retrieved from the database search, and 9 studies met the predefined inclusion criteria for this meta-analysis (Fig. 1) [9–17]. No additional relevant studies were identified from the reference lists of these articles. The included studies employed various rs-fMRI analysis methods, including ReHo, ALFF, fALFF, and dALFF. To facilitate accurate comparisons, studies using different analytical methods were treated as independent datasets. For instance, Feng et al. [10] used a combination of ALFF, fALFF, and ReHo, while Song et al. [13] employed ALFF and dALFF. As a result, 9 articles were included in the meta-analysis, comprising 277 CSVD patients and 287 HCs.

Discussion

In this AES-SDM meta-analysis integrating rs-fMRI studies of CSVD, increased spontaneous brain activity was identified in the bilateral postcentral gyri, precuneus, and left inferior frontal gyrus, whereas no regions showed significant decreases. Furthermore, the publication bias test showed no significant bias, and the sensitivity analysis confirmed the robustness of the findings. These results suggest that CSVD may be associated with functional alterations within the sensorimotor and frontoparietal networks, reflecting potential modulation or reorganization of brain activity in response to microvascular pathology.

The postcentral gyrus (Brodmann areas [BAs] 2 and 4) is the primary somatosensory cortex, responsible for sensory integration and afferent processing [27]. The observed increases in activity within the bilateral postcentral gyri may reflect altered sensorimotor processing or functional adjustment due to CSVD-related microvascular damage. This could indicate a compensatory response to disrupted sensory integration, where the brain increases activity to maintain functional efficiency despite white matter lesions or reduced connectivity in the sensorimotor network. Proprioceptive loss and gait disorders, commonly observed in CSVD patients, may further underscore the relevance of altered sensorimotor processing in the postcentral gyrus [28]. Previous studies have shown that sensorimotor networks, including the postcentral gyrus, are highly sensitive to microvascular injury, with altered activation patterns observed in CSVD and vascular cognitive impairment [28, 29]. The increased activity might also be related to neurovascular uncoupling, where impaired blood flow to regions like the postcentral gyrus forces the brain to enhance activity in certain areas to compensate for vascular damage, thereby exacerbating these changes in CSVD [30]. Additionally, these neurovascular impairments in the postcentral gyrus may impact both sensory and cognitive functions [31].

The precuneus (BA 5), a core hub of the default mode and frontoparietal control networks, plays a role in self-referential processing and higher-order cognitive functions, such as memory retrieval and spatial navigation [32]. The increased spontaneous activity observed in this region may reflect compensatory neural reorganization in response to microvascular damage. Mechanistically, injury to the neurovascular unit in CSVD may impair neurovascular coupling and thereby perturb default mode network integrity, which may be relevant to altered precuneus activity [33]. This increased activity could be an attempt to preserve cognitive functions, such as memory and executive control, by engaging the precuneus more intensively despite disrupted connectivity in other brain areas [34]. Consistent with this notion, Mo et al. reported that visuospatial dysfunction emerges early in CSVD-related cognitive impairment and is linked to cortical alterations in posterior associative regions [35]. These changes may also represent neuroplasticity, whereby the brain adapts to vascular injury by strengthening or reorganizing existing neural circuits. Further research is needed to elucidate the precise neurobiological significance of these changes, particularly their role in preserving cognitive integrity in CSVD.

The left inferior frontal gyrus (triangular part, BA 45) is a key region involved in executive control and language processing, contributing to working memory, attention, and semantic operations [36]. The increased activity observed here may be related to enhanced engagement of frontal control networks in response to reduced processing efficiency [37]. Structural imaging studies have linked damage in frontal white matter tracts to executive dysfunction and slowed information processing in CSVD, indicating that functional alterations in the inferior frontal gyrus may represent a regulatory response to reduced network efficiency [38]. This finding is consistent with the characteristic executive slowing and reduced verbal fluency observed in CSVD, which critically depend on the integrity of fronto-subcortical and frontoparietal circuits involving the inferior frontal gyrus [39]. Increased spontaneous activity in this region at rest may therefore reflect increased baseline recruitment of fronto-subcortical control circuits to sustain task-related performance.

Taken together, these findings indicate that CSVD is associated with increased spontaneous activity in several cortical regions involved in sensorimotor and cognitive functions, suggesting that functional modulation or network-level adjustments may occur in the presence of microvascular damage [40]. Nevertheless, the nature of these changes remains uncertain. Whether the observed increases represent compensatory activation, altered neurovascular coupling, or other physiological adjustments requires confirmation from future multimodal and longitudinal studies [41]. The lack of significant decreases in brain activity may be due to compensatory mechanisms, where the brain increases activity in response to vascular damage to maintain functional efficiency despite microvascular injury [42]. These findings highlight the potential for altered brain activity to serve as an important biomarker for understanding the functional consequences of CSVD, providing insight into how the brain adapts to and compensates for the pathological changes associated with this condition. However, additional longitudinal studies are needed to explore the dynamics of these changes over time and their implications for disease progression and cognitive decline in CSVD patients.

The results offer preliminary evidence for functional changes associated with CSVD but should be interpreted cautiously. Several limitations warrant consideration. First, the relatively small number of included studies precluded subgroup and meta-regression analyses. Second, despite the AES-SDM method’s ability to synthesize convergent patterns across studies, it ensures result robustness and employed rigorous multiple-comparison correction to reduce statistical differences [43], but the AES-SDM approach relies on reported peak coordinates rather than original statistical maps, potentially limiting spatial precision. Third, all participants in the included studies were from China. Given the possible influence of ethnic, genetic, and socio-cultural differences on both brain function and disease manifestation, the results may not be entirely applicable to CSVD populations outside of these regions [44]. However, the AES-SDM method provides valuable insights into the neural mechanisms of CSVD. Future research with more diverse populations, including subgroup analyses based on neuroimaging metrics (e.g., ALFF, ReHo) and cognitive impairment severity, is needed to improve the generalizability of these findings to broader clinical settings.

Conclusion

This AES-SDM meta-analysis identified convergent increased spontaneous brain activity in the bilateral postcentral gyri, bilateral precuneus, and left inferior frontal gyrus in CSVD. These findings provide neuroimaging evidence of functional disruption in CSVD and may inform future studies on the neurobiological mechanisms underlying the disease.
