Treatment outcomes and safety of concurrent chemoradiation in older adults with unresectable stage III non-small-cell lung cancer
Authors: So Jeong Kim, Na Young Kim, Soo Jie Chung, Changhwan Kim, Jeong-Hee Choi, In Gyu Hyun, Yohwan Yeo, Ji Young Park, Seung Hun Jang, Taehee Kim, Yun Su Sim, Chang Youl Lee, Junghyun Kim
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundConcurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) is the standard treatment for patients with unresectable stage III non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and good performance status. However, there is no consensus regarding the treatment of older patients with stage III NSCLC. Therefore, this study aimed to investigate treatment outcomes and safety of CCRT in older patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC compared with those in younger patients.MethodsWe retrospectively examined all patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC treated with CCRT between 2012 and 2023 at four Korean hospitals. Progression-free survival (PFS), overall survival (OS), and adverse events (AEs) were compared between patients aged ≥70 years (older) and those aged <70 years (younger). We also analyzed data for age cutoffs of 60, 65, and 75 years.ResultsAmong 131 participants, 47 were classified as older patients and 84 as younger patients. Baseline characteristics, including Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance status and comorbidities, were mostly similar. In multivariable-adjusted models, older and younger patients had similar PFS [adjusted hazard ratio (aHR), 1.45; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.85–2.48]; the median PFS in older and younger patients was 9.9 and 12.9 months, respectively (P=0.94). However, in multivariable-adjusted models, older patients had worse OS (aHR, 2.33; 95% CI: 1.27–4.27); the median OS in older and younger patients was 24.1 and 27.2 months, respectively (P=0.36). AEs of any grade and those of grade ≥3 were not significantly different between older and younger groups.ConclusionsAmong patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC undergoing CCRT, older patients aged ≥70 years had similar PFS and AE rates compared with those aged <70 years. However, OS was worse in older patients. Considering age as a crucial factor in CCRT outcomes, efforts should be made to provide a more meticulous and careful approach for older patients.Keywords: Concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), chemoradiotherapy, geriatric assessment, non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC), older patients

Highlight box

Key findings

• Among patients with unresectable stage III non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) undergoing concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT), older patients aged ≥70 years had similar progression-free survival and adverse event rates compared with those aged <70 years. However, overall survival was worse in older patients.

What is known and what is new?

• CCRT is the standard treatment for patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC and good performance status. However, there is no consensus regarding the treatment of older patients with stage III NSCLC.

• This real-world multicenter study demonstrated the treatment outcomes and safety of CCRT according to age in older Asian patients.

What is the implication, and what should change now?

• Considering age as a crucial factor in CCRT outcomes, efforts should be made to provide a more meticulous and careful approach for older patients.

Introduction

The incidence of lung cancer is higher in older patients than in younger patients, with a mean age of 71 years at diagnosis. Lung cancer is most commonly diagnosed in the 65–74 years age group (35.2%), followed by the 75–84 years age group (27.1%) (1). Considering the aging population and the increasing incidence of lung cancer in older adults, old age is an important risk factor in lung cancer treatment. Firat et al. reported age as an independent factor in patient selection for chemoradiation therapy for stage III non-small-cell lung cancer (NSCLC) (2), and increased age is associated with reduced chances of receiving chemotherapy (3).

Concurrent chemoradiotherapy (CCRT) is the standard treatment for patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC and good performance status. However, clinicians often encounter the challenging decision of treating older patients with CCRT, which is known for its considerable toxicity, vs. opting for radiation therapy alone or a sequence of chemotherapy followed by radiation therapy. This is because it remains unclear whether CCRT is suitable for unselected older patients, given limited and inconsistent data from clinical trials. In addition, many studies have demonstrated that as age increases, the number of comorbidities increases and performance status decreases (4-6). Therefore, determining the most suitable treatment for older patients becomes more complicated, potentially leading to their inadequate treatment (7).

Previous studies have observed diverse outcomes regarding CCRT in older patients (8-11). Stinchcombe et al. conducted a pooled analysis using individual patient data of patients aged ≥70 years from 16 prospective cooperative group studies focused on chemoradiotherapy for stage III NSCLC (8). They reported that older patients experienced worse overall survival (OS) and more treatment-related toxicity than younger patients. However, half the included studies were conducted 15 years ago, failing to reflect current advancements in chemotherapy, radiation therapy, and conservative approaches. Recently, Canadian researchers conducted a retrospective, single-center study to analyze the safety and therapeutic effects of CCRT in older patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC (10). They noted that patients aged ≥70 years showed similar safety, progression-free survival (PFS), and OS rates compared to those observed in patients aged <70 years.

Furthermore, in the above studies, White participants accounted for the majority, comprising 736 of 832 (88%) patients aged ≥70 years, with 2,367 out of 2,768 (86%) patients aged <70 years in the Stinchcombe et al. study (8). Additionally, in the Canadian study, White participants accounted for 33 of 44 (75%) patients aged ≥70 years, and 51 out of 71 (72%) patients were aged <70 years (10). One study analyzed treatment outcomes and safety of CCRT in 36 Asian patients aged ≥65 years with unresectable stage III NSCLC (11). However, to date, no study has compared older Asian patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC and younger patients. Therefore, outcome and safety data to guide treatment choices for unresectable stage III NSCLC in older patients are urgently needed.

In this study, we aimed to investigate treatment outcomes and safety of CCRT in older patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC compared with those in younger patients. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://jtd.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/jtd-2025-489/rc).

Methods

Study design and participants

For this multicenter retrospective cohort study, we reviewed data of all patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC who were identified using treatment order codes from electronic medical records as having been treated with CCRT from 2012 to 2023 at four affiliated hospitals: Hallym University Dongtan Sacred Heart Hospital, Hallym University Sacred Heart Hospital, Hallym University Kangnam Sacred Heart Hospital, and Hallym University Chuncheon Sacred Heart Hospital; each representing a different region in Korea. The exclusion criterion was patients under the age of 18 years. We collected data on demographic characteristics, such as age; sex; body mass index; smoking history; performance status; Eastern Corporative Oncology Group (ECOG) score; comorbidities; histopathologic tumor type; clinical stage [according to the 8th edition of the Tumor-Nodes-Metastasis (TNM) International Staging System] (12); results of molecular tests, including epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) mutation, anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) translocation, and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1); treatment modality; survival status; and adverse events (AEs). The treatment modality consisted of platinum-based chemotherapy with concurrent radiotherapy. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Boards of all participating centers: Hallym University Dongtan Sacred Heart Hospital (IRB No. 2023-03-002), Hallym University Sacred Heart Hospital (IRB No. 2023-05-010), Hallym University Kangnam Sacred Heart Hospital (IRB No. 2023-11-015), and Hallym University Chuncheon Sacred Heart Hospital (IRB No. 2023-12-013). The Institutional Review Boards waived the requirement for written informed consent from the participants owing to the retrospective nature of the study.

Outcomes and follow-up

The investigated endpoints (PFS, OS, and AEs) were compared between patients aged ≥70 years (older) and those aged <70 years (younger). We opted for the age cutoff of 70 years as it has been the standard definition for older patients in previous prospective trials and retrospective analyses (13-16), although other age limits (≥65 or ≥75 years) have also been used (17). This choice facilitates the comparison of our analysis with other studies. PFS was defined as the time between initiation of treatment to the occurrence of disease progression or death (whichever occurred first) (18). Computed tomography images were assessed by the study investigators to determine the best response and date of progression according to the Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors version 1.1 (18). OS was defined as the time from the initiation of treatment to the time of death resulting from any cause (18). AEs were identified and classified according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events, version 5.0 (19). The study cohort was followed from the index date, the date of treatment initiation, to the date of death, or the end of the study period (July 15, 2023), whichever came first.

Based on the results of the PACIFIC trial, durvalumab has been reimbursed in Korea since April 1, 2020. Accordingly, patients who underwent CCRT after this date received durvalumab if they met the reimbursement criteria, which include PD-L1 (SP263) positivity (expression level ≥1%), receipt of at least two cycles of platinum-based CCRT, no evidence of disease progression, and administration within 42 days after completion of CCRT in unresectable stage III NSCLC (20). After completing one year of durvalumab therapy, patients were followed with regular chest CT scans for disease surveillance. Prior to the reimbursement approval, patients were managed with close surveillance through regular chest CT scans following CCRT.

Statistical analysis

Patient characteristics, CCRT patterns, and AEs were compared across age groups using Fisher’s exact tests for categorical variables and the Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables. The normality test was performed to justify the use of non-parametric tests. PFS and OS were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier method and tested for differences using the log-rank test. Multivariable Cox regressions were performed to adjust for confounding factors [sex, smoking, ECOG score, comorbidities including diabetes mellitus (DM) and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), and clinical stage]. We further analyzed data for age cutoffs 60, 65, 75 and <60, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, and ≥75 years instead of 70 years. The level of statistical significance was set at 0.05. All statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.3.3; R Foundation, Vienna, Austria).

Results

Patient characteristics

Among 131 patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC treated with CCRT between 2012 and 2023 at four hospitals, 47 were classified as older patients and 84 as younger patients. The median age was 66 years, and most patients were men (96.9%) and had a history of smoking (96.2%). Baseline characteristics such as sex, body mass index, ECOG performance status, tumor histology, presence of EGFR or ALK mutations, level of PD-L1 expression, and best response to CCRT were similar in both groups (Table 1). However, the number of pack-years was higher in the older group than in the younger group {50 [40–60] vs. 35 [29–40] pack-years, P<0.001}. The frequencies of most comorbidities were similar, but DM (42.6% vs. 22.6%, P=0.03) and COPD (40.4% vs. 17.9%, P=0.009) were more common in the older group than in the younger group. The number of patients with stage IIIA was similar in both age groups, but stage IIIB was more common in the older group and stage IIIC was more common in the younger group. In further analyses with different age cut-offs (60, 65, 75, or <60, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, and ≥75 years), baseline characteristics were similar in both groups (Tables S1-S4).

CCRT patterns

Patients received platinum-based chemotherapy, in combination with paclitaxel, pemetrexed, etoposide, vinorelbine, or docetaxel, concurrently with radiotherapy. The platinum types, regimens, number of planned cycles received, and dose intensities of chemotherapy were similar between both groups. Additionally, the total radiation dose was not significantly different between the two groups (60 [60–66] vs. 63 [60–66] Gy, P=0.74; Table 2). Of the 131 patients included in the study, 118 (90.1%) completed the planned CCRT, while 13 (9.9%) did not. Among these 13 patients, six (one in the older group and five in the younger group) discontinued treatment due to CCRT-related AEs, including septic shock (n=1), general deterioration (n=2), and pneumonia (n=3). These patients discontinued after 0.67 cycles (n=3), 1 cycle (n=1), 3 cycles (n=1), and 1.33 cycles (n=1), using the modified chemotherapy cycle definition. Four patients (two in each age group) died from treatment-related complications before completing CCRT. Causes of death included bronchial bleeding (n=1) and respiratory failure secondary to pneumonia (n=3). These patients died after receiving 1 cycle (n=2), 0.33 cycles (n=1), and 3 cycles (n=1). The remaining three patients discontinued CCRT by personal choice—two after 0.33 cycles and one after 1 cycle (Table 2). Older and younger patients completed treatment (89.4% vs. 90.5%, P>0.99), discontinued treatment secondary to an AE (2.1% vs. 6.0%, P=0.42), died during treatment (4.3% vs. 2.4%, P=0.62), and refused treatment (4.3% vs. 1.2%, P=0.29) at similar rates (Table 2). In further analyses with different age cutoffs (60, 65, 75, or <60, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, and ≥75 years), the conclusions remained the same (Tables S5-S8).

AEs

AEs of any grade and grade ≥3 were not significantly different between the older and younger groups (Table 3). The most common AEs in both groups were esophagitis, neutropenia, radiation pneumonitis, and pneumonia. Esophagitis of any grade occurred in 8.5% and 20.2% of patients in the older and younger groups, respectively (P=0.13), whereas grade 3 or higher esophagitis was reported only in the younger group at 4.8%. In the older and younger groups, neutropenia of any grade occurred in 19.1% and 15.5%, respectively (P=0.77), and grade ≥3 neutropenia was observed in 2.1% and 4.8%, respectively (P=0.65). In both older and younger groups, the usage of granulocyte colony-stimulating factor was similarly low, at 6.4% and 6.0%, respectively (P>0.99) (Table 2). Radiation pneumonitis of any grade occurred in 21.3% and 27.4% of the older and younger groups, respectively (P=0.57), whereas radiation pneumonitis of grade ≥3 was observed only in the younger group at 6.0%. In the older and younger groups, pneumonia of any grade occurred in 17.0% and 17.9%, respectively (P>0.99), and grade ≥3 pneumonia was reported in 8.5% and 7.1%, respectively (P=0.75). The hospitalization rates were not significantly different between the two age groups. No significant differences in AEs of any grade or grade ≥3 were observed between the older and younger patients (Tables S9-S12).

Outcomes

In unadjusted models and multivariable models adjusted for sex, smoking, ECOG, comorbidity (DM and COPD), and clinical stage, older and younger patients had similar PFS [hazard ratio (HR), 0.98; 95% confidence interval (CI): 0.64–1.50 and adjusted HR (aHR), 1.45; 95% CI: 0.85–2.48, respectively; Figure 1A]. The median PFS in older and younger patients was 9.9 and 12.9 months, respectively (P=0.94; Table 4). In the unadjusted model, older and younger patients had similar OS (HR, 1.23; 95% CI: 0.78–1.95), but in the multivariable-adjusted model, older patients had worse OS (aHR, 2.33; 95% CI: 1.27–4.27; Figure 1B). The median OS in older and younger patients was 24.1 and 27.2 months, respectively (P=0.36; Table 4).

According to further analyses for an age cutoff at 60 years, PFS and OS did not significantly differ between the two age groups (aHR, 1.58; 95% CI: 0.88–2.82 and aHR, 1.79; 95% CI: 0.97–3.32, respectively; Figure S1). Likewise, no significant difference in PFS between the two groups was found at a cutoff value of 65 years (aHR, 1.10; 95% CI: 0.65–1.88), but OS was worse in the older group (aHR, 1.88; 95% CI: 1.06–3.34; Figure S2). When analyzed using a cutoff age of 75 years, PFS and OS were worse in the older group (aHR, 2.72; 95% CI: 1.47–5.04 and aHR, 3.55; 95% CI: 1.77–7.13, respectively; Figure S3). When analyzed by age group (<60, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, and ≥75 years), patients aged ≥75 years had worse PFS and OS than patients aged <60 years (aHR, 3.74; 95% CI: 1.58–8.83 and aHR, 6.83; 95% CI: 2.61–17.91, respectively; Figure S4). Furthermore, when examined in 1-year increments, the age cutoffs that revealed differences between the older and younger groups were identified as 72 years for PFS and 65 years for OS in both model 1 and model 2 (Tables S13-S15). We also performed sensitivity analysis for durvalumab as a standard treatment after CCRT in stage III NSCLC in previous studies (20,21) by dividing the participants into two groups: those who received durvalumab (22 patients) and those who did not (109 patients) (Figures S5,S6). The results of the sensitivity analysis were consistent regardless of whether durvalumab was used.

Discussion

In the present study, compared with that in younger patients, CCRT in patients aged ≥70 years with unresectable stage III NSCLC resulted in similar PFS and AEs of any grade and grade ≥3. However, older patients had worse OS. According to further analyses with age cutoffs at 75 years, CCRT in older patients resulted in worsening OS and PFS. This real-world multicenter study demonstrated the treatment outcomes and safety of CCRT according to age in older Asian patients.

Additional age-based analyses revealed that PFS showed a difference between age groups from 72 years, whereas OS diverged from the age of 65 years. This might be because additional anticancer treatments are more challenging in older patients than in younger patients, and factors in addition to lung cancer may contribute to mortality. Furthermore, the observed difference in PFS between the older and younger groups, with an age cutoff of 75 years, might be associated with risks of CCRT toxicity. The incidence of toxicities from chemoradiation appeared to increase among older adults. In particular, patients aged >70 years have higher rates of hematological toxicities and renal impairment because of diminished bone marrow and renal reserves (22,23). Moreover, an elevated incidence of pneumonitis was observed in older patients undergoing chemoradiation and receiving platinum etoposide as chemotherapy (22). Age influences drug metabolism, with adipose tissue prolonging the half-life of lipophilic drugs while shortening that of soluble drugs (24,25). In older adults, hepatic and renal drug clearance may markedly decrease owing to inherent organ senescence and comorbidities impacting the circulatory system (24). Additionally, the influence of cognitive disorders on patients increases with age and can notably affect the management and eventual outcome of AEs (26). The effects of senescence on the capacity of older adults to endure stressful conditions, such as cancer treatments, should also be considered (27). Distinguishing between the cause of death from cancer-related factors and age-associated conditions such as increased comorbidities, declining physiological function, and malnutrition is challenging, particularly in individuals aged >75 years; however, this group of very old patients was associated with an unfavorable prognosis. Considering the low life expectancy of this patient group, we recommend considering this factor when selecting treatment strategies.

According to a previous study by Stinchcombe et al., when considering the age criterion of 70 years, PFS appeared similar in the older group compared with that in the younger group (HR, 1.00; 95% CI: 0.91–1.09), but older patients had worse OS (HR, 1.17; 95% CI: 1.07–1.29) (8). These findings are similar to our study results. In the same study by Stinchcombe et al., the incidence of grade ≥3 AEs was higher in the older group (odds ratio, 1.38; 95% CI: 1.10–1.74) (8). However, the occurrence of AEs was in our study not significantly different between both groups. Furthermore, in the study by Stinchcombe et al., the treatment completion rate was lower in older patients than in younger patients (47% vs. 57%, P<0.01), with more treatment discontinuations owing to AEs (20% vs. 13%, P<0.01) and higher mortality during treatment (7.8% vs. 2.9%, P<0.01) in older patients (8). In contrast, our study found no significant difference between the two groups. Therefore, our recent data provide evidence that CCRT can be safely performed without AEs in patients aged ≥70 years. Another study by Canadian researchers found no difference in PFS and OS when comparing older and younger patients with an age cutoff of 70 years, and the occurrence of AE was also similar (10). Moreover, the overall rate of planned chemotherapy cycles received was similar between older and younger patients (90.9% vs. 95.5%, P=0.10) (10), which is equivalent to the findings of our study. Likewise, the rates of older and younger patients receiving all planned cycles with no dose reductions were similar (68% vs. 76%, P=0.39) (10), which was similar to the results of our study.

To date, the results of CCRT studies for older patients are inconsistent, and this differs somewhat from the results of our study. The lack of significant differences in AEs among our older patients in contrast to the findings of Stinchcombe et al. (8) might be attributed to recent advancements in chemotherapy, radiotherapy, and conservative therapy. In the participating institutions of our study, all patients undergoing CCRT were examined in radiotherapy quality assurance rounds. Patients were also evaluated on a weekly basis throughout CCRT, enabling the prompt implementation of supportive measures such as intravenous hydration, pain control, and antiemetic or antibiotic administration. We anticipate the development of more tolerable chemotherapy regimens, enhanced radiation techniques, and improved supportive treatments, including more potent antiemetics, more effective pain control, stronger anti-acid agents, and early hyperalimentation. Over the past 10–15 years, radiation therapy techniques underwent a complete transformation, resulting in notably decreased esophagitis rates solely because of the preservation of healthy tissue (28). In the future, further advancement in these treatments might result in older patients who undergo CCRT showing similar PFS and OS compared with younger patients.

However, even with advancements in treatment methods, it might still be impossible to disregard the effect of age itself. Conversely, numerical age alone should not prevent people from receiving the most appropriate treatment. Therefore, a comprehensive geriatric assessment, including comorbidity, polypharmacy, functional status, geriatric syndromes, mood, and cognition, should also be considered. Comprehensive geriatric assessment has not been universally adopted as a standard of care as it requires significant time and resources, which can be challenging for busy oncological practices (29). The Vulnerable Elders Survey (VES-13) demands less time and professional intervention, demonstrating independent prognostic value and a significant association with a higher risk of toxicity (30). It is anticipated that geriatric assessments can identify patients who can withstand CCRT and benefit from it.

There are limitations to this study. First, it was retrospective and had a small sample size. However, we believe that this study is meaningful as it represents a multicenter real-world investigation reporting on outcomes specifically related to older adults. In particular, the results of additional analyses with different age cutoffs guided establishing criteria for safe and effective CCRT in older patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC. Second, there could be concerns regarding potential confounding impacts of covariates, as well as insufficient detail on patients’ physiological and psychological status, which might influence overall health and their capacity to comprehend and adapt to treatments. We look forward to the development of more sophisticated and comprehensive geriatric assessments in the future.

Conclusions

This study found that among patients with unresectable stage III NSCLC undergoing CCRT, older patients aged ≥70 years had similar PFS and AEs of any grade and grade ≥3 compared with younger patients. However, older patients had poorer OS. Both PFS and OS were worse in patients aged ≥75 years than in younger patients. Therefore, it is recommended to decide on CCRT after a careful comprehensive geriatric assessment. Future prospective research should focus on the appropriate identification and optimization of older patients, ensuring the safe administration of therapies with curative intent.
