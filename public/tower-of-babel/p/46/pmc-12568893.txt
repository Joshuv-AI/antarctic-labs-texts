Association of sarcopenia and pancreatic exocrine insufficiency in older adults type 2 diabetes mellitus patients
Authors: Sevkican Gunes, Mustafa Altinkaynak, Timur Selcuk Akpinar, Sebile Nilgun Erten, Sebnem Garip Ustaoglu, Bulent Saka
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and aimAn association between type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (PEI) has been increasingly recognized. Sarcopenia is also common among older adults with T2DM, contributing to poor functional outcomes and increased frailty. Recent findings indicate a potential link between PEI and sarcopenia. The present study aimed to investigate the relationship between PEI and sarcopenia in older adults patients with T2DM, independent of other contributing factors.MethodsA total of 120 patients aged ≥ 65 years with T2DM were enrolled in the study. Demographic characteristics, diabetes-related clinical data, and malnutrition risk were assessed. Muscle strength, muscle mass, physical performance, and fecal elastase-1 levels were measured. Sarcopenia was diagnosed according to the criteria defined by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People 2 (EWGSOP2).ResultsPEI was present in 22.5% of patients, including 10% with severe and 12.5% with mild-to-moderate insufficiency Low muscle mass was observed in 15% of participants, while confirmed sarcopenia was identified in 10%. Patients with reduced hand grip strength exhibited significantly lower Mini Nutritional Assessment – Short Form (MNA-SF) scores and serum vitamin D levels; however, in male patients, only MNA-SF was significantly lower. The prevalence of PEI was significantly higher in males with probable sarcopenia compared to those without (34.8% vs. 10%, p = 0.05). In multivariate analysis among men, obesity showed a borderline significant association with probable sarcopenia, whereas PEI demonstrated a nonsignificant trend toward association.Discussion and conclusionPEI may contribute to sarcopenia in older adults males with T2DM and should be considered in the clinical evaluation of sarcopenic patients.Keywords: Type 2 diabetes mellitus, Sarcopenia, Pancreatic exocrine insufficiency, Older adults

Introduction

The incidence of type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) increases with age, affecting more than a quarter of people over the age of 65 [1]. The prevalence of sarcopenia and sarcopenic obesity is significantly higher in older people with T2DM [2].

Sarcopenia is a chronic skeletal muscle disorder characterized by reduced muscle strength, low muscle mass and decreased physical performance, leading to an increased risk of falls, fractures, physical disability and mortality [3]. The underlying mechanisms of sarcopenia are multifactorial and involve dysregulation of the cycle of protein synthesis and degradation in skeletal muscle [2]. Insulin plays a critical role in stimulating protein synthesis. However, insulin resistance, which increases with age, has been implicated in the pathogenesis of sarcopenia [4].

In T2DM, progressive pancreatic beta-cell dysfunction leads to impaired endocrine function over time. Studies have also demonstrated a strong association between endocrine dysfunction and pancreatic exocrine insufficiency (PEI) [5–8]. Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain PEI in diabetic patients, including pancreatic atrophy due to vasculopathy, neuropathy-induced impairment of exocrine enzyme secretion, autoimmune responses targeting exocrine cells, and genetic predisposition [9].

Recent studies have highlighted an increased incidence of sarcopenia in patients with chronic pancreatitis and pancreatic cancer [10–12]. These conditions are associated with a severe catabolic state, which further increases the risk of sarcopenia. Furthermore, recent evidence suggests an association between sarcopenia and PEI due to benign disorders of the pancreas [13].

In this study, we aimed to investigate the relationship between PEI and sarcopenia in patients with T2DM without known primary or secondary pancreatic disease. We hypothesized that PEI may be involved in the etiology of sarcopenia in T2DM as a secondary complication.

Materials and methods

Patient population

This study included 120 T2DM patients aged ≥ 65 years who were admitted to the internal medicine outpatient clinic of Istanbul University, Faculty of Medicine, between May and December 2021. An informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. Exclusion criteria were, advanced dementia, immobility, acute inflammatory disease/medical disorder, presence of a pacemaker, active alcohol use or history of heavy alcohol consumption, active malignancy, pancreatic diseases (including chronic pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer, or prior pancreatic surgery), inflammatory bowel disease, and current use of pancreatic enzyme replacement therapy (PERT) for any reason.

Ethical approval

was obtained from the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Istanbul University, Faculty of Medicine (Approval number: 181035, Date: 26.04.2021). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the World Medical Association’s Declaration of Helsinki.

Clinical and laboratory outcomes

Demographic data and the anthropometric measurements of all participants were recorded. T2DM duration, current medications and diabetes-related complications were documented A stool sample was obtained from each participant for the measurement of fecal elastase-1 (FE1). Samples were stored at − 80 °C until analysis. FE1 levels were determined using a sandwich enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Bioserv Diagnostics GmbH), employing two monoclonal antibodies specific to human pancreatic elastase. Based on FE1 levels, participants were categorized as, normal exocrine function (FE1 > 200 µg/g), mild to moderate PEI (FE1: 100–200 µg/g) or severe PEI (FE1 < 100 µg/g) [14].

Assessment of muscle strength, muscle mass and physical performance

Handgrip strength (HGS) was evaluated using a hand-held dynamometer (Jamar, USA). While seated in an upright position with the arms located on both sides and they were flexed through the elbows at 900, the patients were instructed to squeeze the hand dynamometer as hard as possible for 2–3 s. The measurements were repeated three times for both hands, and the highest recorded value was obtained [3]. Muscle mass was measured via bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) (TANITA MC 780 MA, Japan). BIA was performed under standardized conditions as recommended in previous studies [15]. Patients were instructed to fast for at least 4 h, avoid vigorous physical activity for 12 h, and void their bladder prior to the measurement. All assessments were conducted barefoot, without metal accessories, and in a thermoneutral environment (20–25 °C), in accordance with published guidelines for optimal accuracy [15]. The appendicular skeletal muscle mass index (ASMI) was calculated by dividing the appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) measured via BIA by the square of the patient’s height (m²). ASM was estimated using the Sergi predictive equation, defined as follows: ASM (kg) = − 3.964 + (0.227 × resistance index [RI]) + (0.095 × body weight [kg]) + (1.384 × sex) + (0.064 × reactance [Xc]), where sex was coded as 1 for males and 0 for females. This formula demonstrated a high degree of accuracy, with R² = 0.92 and a standard error of estimate (SEE) of 1.14 kg [16].

The diagnostic criteria established by the European Working Group on Sarcopenia in Older People-2 (EWGSOP2) were applied to diagnose sarcopenia [3]. The following thresholds were used: Muscle strength < 16 kg in females and < 27 kg in males were considered low; ASMI: <5.5 kg/m² in females and < 7 kg/m² in males were classified as low [3]. Physical performance was evaluated using the four-meter Usual Gait Speed (UGS) test, which measures walking speed (m/s). Patients with a walking speed ≤ 0.8 m/s in the four-meter walk test were considered to have poor physical performance.

Patients with low muscle strength alone were considered to have probable sarcopenia, whereas patients with both low muscle strength and low muscle mass were considered to have confirmed sarcopenia.

Nutritional status assessment

Nutritional status was assessed using the Mini Nutritional Assessment-Short Form (MNA-SF). A total score of ≤ 11 out of 14 indicated a risk of malnutrition [17].

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were expressed as categorical variables (number, percentage, and 95% confidence interval) and continuous variables (mean, standard deviation, median, interquartile range, minimum, maximum, and percentage). The distribution of variables was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test.

For quantitative independent data, the Independent Sample Student’s t-test and Mann-Whitney U test were used. Categorical independent variables were analyzed using the Chi-square test, while the Fisher’s exact test was applied when the assumptions of the Chi-square test were not met. Logistic regression model was used to evaluate relationships between variables. To account for potential differences, analyses were conducted separately for male and female participants.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

The association between PEI and probable sarcopenia, confirmed sarcopenia, and malnutrition risk was evaluated in the total cohort and stratified by sex. In the overall population, no statistically significant association was observed between PEI and probable sarcopenia (p = 0.81) or confirmed sarcopenia (p = 0.30). Similarly, in female patients, PEI was not significantly associated with probable sarcopenia (p = 0.52) or confirmed sarcopenia (p = 0.90).

However, in male patients, probable sarcopenia was significantly more prevalent among those with PEI compared to those without (80% vs. 45%, p = 0.05), suggesting a potential sex-specific relationship. Although the proportions of confirmed sarcopenia and malnutrition risk were also higher among males with PEI, these differences did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.12 and p-values not shown, respectively) (Table 4).

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the association between PEI and sarcopenia in older adults patients with T2DM. While no significant relationship was observed between PEI and sarcopenia in the total population or in females, a significant association between PEI and probable sarcopenia was identified in male patients. This finding suggests a potential sex-specific interaction, possibly reflecting differences in body composition, hormonal profiles, or the clinical manifestations of PEI between men and women. In the study by Shintakuya et al., which investigated the association between PEI and sarcopenia in patients with chronic pancreatitis, sarcopenia was also found to be more prevalent among male patients [10].

The link between sarcopenia and PEI has been previously hypothesized based on shared mechanisms such as malnutrition, fat-soluble vitamin deficiencies, and systemic inflammation. However, evidence from human studies remains limited. Our findings add to the existing literature by highlighting that even in the absence of overt malnutrition, PEI may contribute to the decline in muscle function in certain subgroups, particularly older adults men with T2DM.

Sarcopenia was defined as primary and secondary, in which the former is related with ageing, and the latter is associated with many clinical and metabolic disorders, such as malnutrition, inflammation, cancer, multimorbidity, immobility, osteomalasia, neurological denervation, drugs, etc [2]. PEI was recently related with sarcopenia. PEI results in malabsorption and subsequent nutritional deficiencies that can accelerate muscle loss [10, 11]. While PEI is classically linked to chronic pancreatitis, pancreatic cancer, or pancreatic surgery, its occurrence in diabetes appears to follow a distinct pathophysiological profile and may develop in the absence of overt pancreatic pathology [12, 13]. Importantly, the Catalan Pancreas Society has recently recognized sarcopenia as a clinical manifestation of PEI in its diagnostic guidelines [18].

Protein malabsorption resulting from PEI is a potential mechanistic link to muscle wasting and sarcopenia. While fat malabsorption is a well-established consequence of PEI, experimental animal studies have also demonstrated significant protein malabsorption [19–21]. In a porcine model of PEI, a marked reduction in the incorporation of labeled nitrogen into plasma proteins was observed, indicating impaired protein metabolism [22]. Despite these findings, direct human studies evaluating the impact of PEI-induced protein malabsorption on muscle mass and function remain scarce. Future research is warranted to explore whether PERT could attenuate muscle loss in patients with coexisting PEI and sarcopenia.

The univariate associations between probable sarcopenia and low vitamin D levels, cerebrovascular disease, sulfonylurea use (in females), and DPP-4 inhibitor use and obesity (in males) are consistent with previous reports. Vitamin D deficiency has been widely recognized as a contributor to muscle weakness and frailty, especially in older adults [23]. Sulfonylureas have also been associated with increased risk of hypoglycemia and subsequent muscle loss, particularly in frail older adults patients [24].

In male patients, the observed associations between probable sarcopenia and both DPP-4 inhibitor use and obesity may be multifactorial. DPP-4 inhibitors have been hypothesized to affect muscle metabolism via incretin-related pathways, though results are conflicting and mechanistic data remain limited [25]. Obesity, particularly in the context of sarcopenic obesity, is increasingly recognized as a clinical phenotype that combines excess adiposity with diminished muscle function, and is associated with increased risk of functional decline and adverse outcomes [26]– [27].

In the multivariate analysis, although not statistically significant, both PEI and DPP-4 inhibitor use showed trends toward association with probable sarcopenia in men. The limited sample size in this subgroup may have reduced the statistical power to detect significant associations. Nevertheless, the observed trends support the hypothesis that PEI may play a contributory role in the development of sarcopenia in older adults male patients with T2DM, potentially through malabsorption-related mechanisms that impair protein and micronutrient balance [5].

Our study has several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, although the total sample size was determined to be adequate based on a priori power analysis, it may have been insufficient for certain subgroup analyses—particularly among male participants, who constituted only 35.8% of the cohort (n = 43). This limited statistical power may explain the lack of significance in the multivariate analysis examining the relationship between PEI and sarcopenia in men. Similarly, the absence of a significant association between malnutrition risk and sarcopenia in both the total cohort and in sex-stratified analyses may reflect the same limitation.

Second, the cross-sectional design of the study precludes the ability to infer causal relationships. Therefore, while PEI and sarcopenia may be associated, it remains unclear whether PEI plays a direct etiological role in the development of sarcopenia.

Third, the diagnosis of PEI was based solely on FE1 levels. Although this is a widely used and non-invasive diagnostic method, it may have limited sensitivity in detecting mild to moderate exocrine insufficiency, and it does not differentiate between primary pancreatic insufficiency and secondary causes. Additionally, advanced imaging or functional diagnostic modalities to rule out underlying pancreatic pathology in patients with low FE1 levels were not employed.

To minimize potential confounding, individuals with known conditions that could affect pancreatic function or muscle mass—such as active alcohol use, history of pancreatitis, pancreatic malignancy, hereditary pancreatic disorders, use of PERT, or new-onset diabetes—were excluded from the study.

Despite these limitations, this is, to our knowledge, the first study to investigate the potential association between PEI and sarcopenia specifically in older adults with T2DM. The study design was methodologically robust, incorporating validated tools to assess both muscle mass and function based on EWGSOP2 criteria. All assessments were performed by a trained and experienced clinical nutrition team, consisting of two nurses and one dietitian, using calibrated equipment under standardized procedures. These strengths enhance the reliability and reproducibility of our findings.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study highlights the high prevalence of both sarcopenia and PEI among older adults with T2DM. Our findings suggest that PEI may be a contributing factor to sarcopenia, particularly in older adults male patients. This observation may add a novel etiological component to the multifactorial pathogenesis of secondary sarcopenia in this population. Future longitudinal and interventional studies are needed to confirm these associations and to evaluate whether targeted strategies—such as nutritional optimization and the management of PEI—can prevent or mitigate sarcopenia in older individuals with T2DM.
