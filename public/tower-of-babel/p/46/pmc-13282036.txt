GADD45B promotes apoptosis in intestinal ischemia/reperfusion through DNA demethylation of MST1/Hippo
Authors: Fengyuan Yang, Xuzi Zhao, Yan Zhao, Shili Ning, Zhao Chen, Guorong Wang, Zhecheng Wang, Jihong Yao, Xiaofeng Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Intestinal ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) injury is a prevalent pathophysiological occurrence that results in significant morbidity and mortality. Growth arrest and DNA damage-inducible β (GADD45B) mediates various cellular responses and is engaged in apoptosis, DNA demethylation and repair, and cell survival. Nonetheless, the role of GADD45B in intestinal I/R injury remains inadequately defined. This study aimed to elucidate whether GADD45B contributes to intestinal I/R injury through the regulation of apoptosis and DNA demethylation.Methods:In this study, we established a mouse intestinal I/R model using mesenteric artery occlusion, and Caco-2 cells were used to create the in vitro hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R) model. Using these models, the function of GADD45B in intestinal I/R injury was investigated by knockdown and overexpression approaches, combined with histological staining, immunohistochemistry, and Western blotting. Transcriptomic analyses were performed to identify downstream signaling pathways and molecular targets of GADD45B. The specific molecular mechanisms by which GADD45B regulates intestinal I/R injury were further elucidated using quantitative real-time reverse transcription-polymerase chain reaction (qRT-PCR), methylation-specific polymerase chain reaction (MSP), co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), and chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP) assays.Results:Our research revealed a notable increase in GADD45B in both H/R-induced Caco-2 cells and mouse models of intestinal I/R. GADD45B knockdown significantly mitigated intestinal barrier dysfunction and apoptosis resulting from H/R, whereas its overexpression had the opposite effect in vitro. GADD45B-knockdown mice were generated via the adeno-associated virus (AAV)-short hairpin (sh)-GADD45B and subjected to I/R. These findings indicated that GADD45B knockdown alleviated intestinal injury and reduced apoptosis triggered by intestinal I/R. Transcriptomic Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) enrichment analysis revealed that GADD45B regulated apoptosis during intestinal I/R by activating the Hippo pathway, with mammalian sterile 20-like kinase 1 (MST1) recognized as a downstream target gene of GADD45B. ChIP confirmed that GADD45B bound to the MST1 promoter and that ten-eleven-translocation 1 (TET1) occupied the MST1 promoter region following GADD45B overexpression. Co-IP confirmed that GADD45B interacts with TET1. MSP confirmed that TET1 knockdown impaired GADD45B-induced MST1 promoter DNA demethylation. Mechanistically, GADD45B interacted with TET1 to enhance MST1 expression through DNA demethylation, which subsequently activated the Hippo pathway and exacerbated apoptosis following intestinal I/R.Conclusion:GADD45B represents a pivotal regulatory factor in intestinal I/R injury, and targeting the GADD45B/TET1/MST1 axis might serve as a feasible treatment strategy.

Introduction

Intestinal ischemia/reperfusion (I/R) is a prevalent pathophysiological phenomenon encountered in diverse scenarios, such as acute and chronic intestinal obstruction, small-bowel transplantation, mesenteric dysfunction, and sepsis.[1] I/R injury compromises the intestinal mucosal barrier, allowing intestinal bacterial endotoxins and locally produced free radicals to enter the bloodstream, potentially leading to multiorgan dysfunction and failure.[2–4] Current treatments for intestinal I/R injury encompass pharmacological and mechanical approaches, including preconditioning, postconditioning, and remote ischemic preconditioning. However, pharmacological interventions have not significantly improved patient outcomes, and mechanical methods are invasive and not universally applicable.[5] Consequently, investigating the underlying mechanisms of intestinal I/R and pinpointing possible therapeutic targets are essential.

Growth arrest and DNA damage-inducible 45 β (GADD45B), along with GADD45A and GADD45G, constitutes the GADD45 gene family, which serves as a critical stress sensor that mediates various cellular responses. GADD45B functions through protein–protein interactions and plays roles in DNA demethylation and repair, apoptosis, cell cycle arrest, genomic stability, immunity, and responses to both carcinogenic stress and physiological conditions.[6–8] In a Dutch study in which whole-genome expression profile analysis was performed on samples collected continuously from 7 patients during human intestinal I/R, GADD45B was identified as one of the top 20 differentially expressed genes (DEGs).[9] Our group’s integrated RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis similarly demonstrated notable upregulation of GADD45B in a mouse model of I/R (GSE232246).[10] Although GADD45B has been studied in other contexts, such as the brain,[11,12] heart,[13] and kidney I/R,[14] there is currently no research regarding its involvement in intestinal I/R. Thus, the role of GADD45B in intestinal I/R warrants further investigation.

Methyl groups are added to DNA nucleotides by DNA methyltransferases (DNMTs), a process referred to as DNA methylation, which causes chromatin condensation and changes in gene expression.[15] The removal of methyl groups from cytosine is known as DNA demethylation.[16] Ten-eleven translocation (TET) methylcytosine dioxygenase catalyzes the demethylation of 5-methylcytosine, promoting active DNA demethylation.[17] Altered DNA methylation profiles have been identified in organ damage associated with I/R injuries.[18–20] Studies have demonstrated that GADD45B facilitates DNA demethylation through nucleotide excision repair mechanisms.[21] Therefore, GADD45B could be pivotal in intestinal I/R injury through DNA demethylation mechanisms.

Mammalian sterile 20-like kinase 1 (MST1), also referred to as kinase responsive to stress 2 (KRS-2) or serine/threonine protein kinase 4 (STK4), belongs to the Ste-20 family.[22] It is a ubiquitously expressed proapoptotic kinase that serves as a crucial link in the apoptosis signaling pathways associated with tissue damage.[23] MST1 is essential in the Hippo pathway and regulates cell apoptosis and proliferation to preserve tissue homeostasis and organ size.[24] Recent investigations have demonstrated that MST1 is critical for mediating I/R injury, including stroke, cerebral infarction, and postreperfusion myocardial infarction.[23] In addition, research has shown that MST1 expression is regulated by promoter methylation.[25] Therefore, we hypothesize that GADD45B may regulate apoptosis during intestinal I/R through promoting the demethylation of MST1.

This study seeks to investigate whether GADD45B modulates apoptosis in intestinal I/R through DNA demethylation mechanisms. In this research, we first demonstrated that GADD45B has a proapoptotic effect on intestinal I/R, explored its downstream pathways and targets, and further confirmed the critical role of epigenetics in intestinal I/R. Mechanistically, GADD45B interacts with ten-eleven translocation 1 (TET1) to facilitate MST1 DNA demethylation, thereby activating the Hippo pathway and exacerbating intestinal I/R. In summary, these results establish the GADD45B/TET1/MST1 axis as a potential treatment target for intestinal I/R.

Methods

Animal experiments

Male C57BL/6J mice (8–10 weeks old, weighing 20 ± 2 g), obtained from the Laboratory Animal Center of Dalian Medical University (Dalian, China), were provided with regular laboratory feed and water. Each group was assigned 6–8 mice at random. The mice in the model group underwent mesenteric artery occlusion for 45 min and subsequent reperfusion for 1, 2, 4, or 8 h.[26] The same treatment was given to the sham mice, but without vascular occlusion. The mice were euthanized following I/R, and samples were collected for analysis. Three weeks before intestinal I/R, C57BL/6J mice were administered either adeno-associated virus (AAV)-short hairpin RNA (shRNA)-GADD45B or the empty AAV vector.[10] Three weeks following injection, the AAVs were expressed steadily (Hanbio Biotechnology, Shanghai, China). This study, which complied with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, was approved by the Dalian Medical University Ethics Committee (No. AEE20004).

Cell culture, hypoxia/reoxygenation (H/R), and treatment

Caco-2 cells, which were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium (MEM) (#11090081, Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 1% glutamine (#35050061, Gibco), 10% fetal bovine serum (#FSD500, ExCell Bio, Shanghai, China), 1% sodium pyruvate (#11360070, Gibco), and 1% nonessential amino acids (#11140050, Gibco), were obtained from the American Type Culture Collection. The cells were maintained at 37°C with 5% CO2 in a humidified atmosphere. The cells were cultivated for 12 h in an environment consisting of 5% CO2, 1% O2, and 94% N2 in a hypoxia chamber (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) to mimic hypoxic conditions. The cells were subsequently restored to normoxic conditions and subjected to reoxygenation for 1, 2, 4, or 8 h.[26]

Transient transfection

Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (#L3000015, Invitrogen, Shanghai, China) was used to transfect Caco-2 cells with either control or specific small interfering RNA (siRNA) (GenePharma, Shanghai, China) for 48 h. Lipofectamine 3000 reagent (#L3000015, Invitrogen) and P3000 (#L3000015, Invitrogen) were used to transfect Caco-2 cells with the plasmids pEX (plasmid containing the giant cell myocarditis virus promoter, multiple cloning site, enhanced green fluorescent protein, and neomycin resistance gene [pGCMV/MCS/EGFP/NEO])-3-GADD45B (GenePharma, Shanghai, China), Flag-GADD45B (MIAOLING BIOLOGY, Wuhan, China), Flag-TET1 (MIAOLING BIOLOGY), and the pEX-3 empty vector for 48 h. The siRNA sequences were as follows: si-GADD45B: 5′-GAU GAC AUC GCC CUG CAA ATT-3′ and 5′-UUU GCA GGG CGA UGU CAU CTT-3′; si-TET1: 5′-CCC ACC UCC AGU CUU AAU ATT-3′ and 5′-UAU UAA GAC UGG AGG UGG GTT-3′; si-MST1: 5′-GAG CUA UGG UCA GAU AAC UTT-3′ and 5′-AGU UAU CUG ACC AUA GCU CTT-3′.[27]

RNA-seq

After Caco-2 cells were transfected with si-NC or si-GADD45B and exposed to H/R, RNA was collected from the cells. Bosite Biotech (Shenyang, China) measured the RNA concentration and purity as well as the library quality. Sequencing was performed via the PE150 strategy on the Illumina platform (San Diego, California, USA), followed by data analysis with the R package (1.20.0, https://www.r-project.org/).

Western blotting

Lysis buffer was used to lyse intestinal tissue or cellular samples, and the protein concentration was ascertained with a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay (#PC0020, Solarbio, Beijing, China). Proteins were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and incubated with the appropriate primary antibody. Densitometric analysis was performed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health, Bethesda, Maryland, USA).[28] The antibodies used were as follows: GADD45B (#AB230646, Abcam, Cambridge, UK), GADD45B (#sc-377311, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Shanghai, China), zonula occludens-1 (ZO-1) (#21773-1-AP, Proteintech, Wuhan, China), Occludin (#27260-1-AP, Proteintech), Bax (#A0207, ABclonal, Wuhan, China), B-cell lymphoma-2 (Bcl-2) (#A0208, ABclonal), cleaved caspase3 (#A11021, ABclonal), MST1 (#22245-1-AP, Proteintech), p-MST1 (#28953-1-AP, Proteintech), large tumor suppressor 1 (LATS1) (#17049-1-AP, Proteintech), phospho-Yes-associated protein (p-YAP) (#T55743F, Abmart, Shanghai, China), YAP (#13584-1-AP, Proteintech), phospho-transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (p-TAZ) (#59971S, Cell Signaling Technology, Shanghai, China), TET1 (#CQA2733, Cohesion Biosciences, Suzhou, China), p-p65 (#YP-mAb-17805, UpingBio, Hangzhou, China), p65 (#10745-1-AP, Proteintech), p38 (#YP-mAb-14885, UpingBio), p-p38 (#28796-1-AP, Proteintech), and β-actin (#A5092, Selleck, Shanghai, China).

RNA extraction, genomic DNA (gDNA) extraction, and quantitative real-time reverse transcription-PCR (qRT-PCR)

TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) was used to separate total RNA from Caco-2 cells. PrimeScript RT Master Mix (TaKaRa, Kusatsu, Japan) was used for the synthesis of complementary DNA (cDNA). qRT-PCR was subsequently conducted with a SYBR Premix Ex TaqTM II kit (TaKaRa). Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C829 shows the sequences of the primers used.[29]

Methylation-specific PCR (MSP)

MethPrimer software was used to analyze CpG islands within the MST1 promoter region. DNA from Caco-2 cells was extracted with the Easy Pure Genomic DNA Kit (#EE101-11, TransGen Biotech, Beijing, China) and subsequently converted to bisulfite with the EZ DNA Methylation-GoldTM Kit (#D5005, Zymo Research, Orange County, California, USA). The transformed DNA was subjected to MSP with ZymoTaqTM PreMix (#E2003, Zymo Research) and both methylated and unmethylated primers, following the manufacturer’s guidelines. With the use of agarose gel electrophoresis, the PCR findings were displayed. The primer sequences are presented in Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C829.[25]

Chromatin immunoprecipitation (ChIP)

The Flag-GADD45B and Flag-TET1 overexpression plasmids (MIAOLING BIOLOGY) were transfected into Caco-2 cells for 48 h in preparation for ChIP analysis. ChIP was then performed using a DYKDDDDK antibody (#20543-1-AP, Proteintech) and a CST kit (#9003, Cell Signaling Technology), as directed by the manufacturer. To detect DNA sequences bound by Flag-GADD45B and Flag-TET1 in the chromatin fraction, qRT-PCR was performed with primers that targeted the MST1 regulatory region. The primer sequences used are listed in Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C829.[30]

Coimmunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Protein A/G magnetic beads (#B23202, Selleck) were used to incubate the appropriate antibodies for 4 h. The antibody–bead complex was then mixed with the supernatant and incubated for an additional hour. Following resuspension in 1× loading buffer, the samples were brought to a boil for ten minutes. Following magnetic separation to collect the supernatant, anti-GADD45B (#sc-377311, Santa Cruz Biotechnology) and anti-TET1 (#CQA2733, Cohesion Biosciences) antibodies were used for Western blotting.[26]

TdT-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) assay

A one-step TdT-mediated dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) in situ apoptosis assay kit (#E-CK-A320, Elabscience, Wuhan, China) was utilized to evaluate apoptosis. Following intestinal I/R, intestinal sections that were embedded in paraffin were deparaffinized with xylene, subjected to a series of alcohol washes, rinsed with PBS (#G4202, Servicebio), and permeabilized with proteinase K. After H/R, paraformaldehyde (#BL539A, Biosharp) was used to fix the Caco-2 cells, which were then permeabilized with Triton X-100 (#T8200, Solarbio). Both the paraffin sections and adherent cells were subsequently stained with TUNEL and 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI).[31]

Histology and immunofluorescence staining

The paraformaldehyde-fixed intestine, lung, and liver tissues of C57BL/6J mice were embedded in paraffin and serially divided into 4 µm thick sections. Following dewaxing and rehydration, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was conducted, and the pathological grades of the intestine, liver, and lungs were assessed using the Chiu, Eckhoff, and Mikawa grading methods, respectively. The GADD45B antibody (#AB230646, Abcam) was used to treat paraffin slices of the intestine before they were stained for immunohistochemistry (IHC). Briefly, after dewaxing and permeabilization of the intestinal tissue sections, bovine serum albumin (BSA) blocking was performed, followed by incubation with primary antibodies, secondary antibodies, and horseradish peroxidase-conjugated streptavidin dilutions. Subsequently, a color development reaction was performed, followed by hematoxylin staining, and the sections were visualized using an optical microscope.[32]

Lung myeloperoxidase (MPO) activity and the serum aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) levels

Serum AST, ALT, and lung MPO activities were measured with specific assay kits (#C010-2-1, #C009-2-1, #A044-1-1, Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, China) as directed by the manufacturer.

Serum levels of TNF-α (#F11630, Westang Bio-tech, Shanghai, China) and IL-6 (#RXW203049M, Ruixin Biotech, Shanghai, China) were assessed using mouse-specific enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits under the guidelines provided by the manufacturer.[26]

Further information about the materials and methods is provided in the Supplementary Material, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C829.

Statistical analysis

The data from all of the experiments were analyzed using GraphPad Prism software (version 9.0, GraphPad Prism Software, La Jolla, CA, USA) and are presented as means ± standard deviations (SDs). Two-group comparisons were analyzed using an unpaired Student’s t test, whereas multigroup comparisons were assessed with one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) post hoc test. P <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

GADD45B expression is increased after intestinal I/R

Previous research has indicated that GADD45B is among the top 20 DEGs after human intestinal I/R injury and that its expression increases following reperfusion.[9] In our previous study, RNA-seq (GSE232246) results indicated that GADD45B levels were substantially increased in intestinal I/R model mice [Figure 1A]. To further validate the alterations in GADD45B levels, GADD45B expression was assessed in vivo and in vitro following intestinal I/R. GADD45B expression was markedly elevated in the intestinal I/R model mice, especially at 4 h postreperfusion [Figure 1B]. Comparable outcomes were identified in the H/R model [Figure 1C]. The immunohistochemical findings were consistent with these observations [Figure 1D]. These findings suggest that GADD45B is relevant to intestinal I/R injury. In addition, we found that the intestinal I/R induced upregulation of GADD45B expression may be associated with the activation of stress-activated signaling pathways and DNA damage [Supplementary Figure 1A, B, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C829].

GADD45B knockdown alleviates intestinal injury and reduces apoptosis in intestinal I/R

The critical role of GADD45B in regulating apoptosis has been reported.[12,33] Nevertheless, studies have indicated that a particular cell type may influence the role of GADD45B in apoptosis.[11] To confirm that GADD45B regulates apoptosis during intestinal I/R, GADD45B-knockdown mice were generated via AAV-shRNA-GADD45B for functional assessment [Figure 2A]. The results revealed that GADD45B knockdown alleviated intestinal barrier dysfunction and inflammation, suppressed nuclear factor kappa-B (NF-κB) activation, and decreased the degree of apoptosis caused by intestinal I/R [Figure 2B–E]. GADD45B-knockdown mice presented a noticeable decline in TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells in intestinal tissue, as demonstrated by TUNEL staining [Figure 2F]. Similarly, H&E staining revealed that GADD45B-knockdown mice exhibited reduced histological damage to intestinal tissue after I/R [Figure 2G]. However, GADD45B knockdown did not significantly alter the mRNA expression levels of Bax or Bcl-2 [Supplementary Figure 2, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C829].

The in vitro GADD45B knockdown model was generated by transfecting Caco-2 cells with the si-GADD45B plasmid, and the knockdown efficiency was subsequently confirmed [Figure 2H]. Consistent with the in vivo results, GADD45B knockdown improved intestinal barrier damage and reduced H/R-induced apoptosis [Figure 2I–K]. These results indicate that GADD45B knockdown reduces apoptosis, improves barrier function, and subsequently alleviates intestinal I/R injury.

GADD45B overexpression exacerbates intestinal injury and aggravates apoptosis in intestinal H/R

To further investigate the involvement of GADD45B in apoptosis during intestinal I/R, GADD45B was overexpressed by transfecting the pEX-3-GADD45B plasmid into Caco-2 cells, and the overexpression efficiency was verified [Figure 3A]. The results indicated that GADD45B overexpression exacerbated barrier dysfunction and increased apoptosis [Figure 3B, C]. A significant increase in TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells with GADD45B overexpression after H/R was detected by TUNEL staining [Figure 3D]. These findings reveal that the overexpression of GADD45B exacerbates intestinal damage and aggravates apoptosis during intestinal H/R injury.

GADD45B activates the Hippo pathway to promote intestinal I/R injury

GADD45B is engaged in several signaling pathways, including the mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPK),[34] NF-κB,[35] and p53 pathways.[36] During intestinal I/R injury, we further observed that GADD45B exhibits functional crosstalk with the NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways [Supplementary Figure 3A–C, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C829]. RNA-seq analysis was conducted on the H/R and si-GADD45B+H/R groups to elucidate how GADD45B modulates intestinal I/R injury. The genes with differential expression are presented in Figure 4A. Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) analysis revealed notable enrichment of the Hippo pathway [Figure 4B], which is intimately linked to apoptosis and proliferation.[37] The Hippo pathway is critical in I/R injury across various organs.[38] Therefore, we hypothesized that GADD45B may be engaged in the Hippo pathway. To investigate the association between the Hippo pathway and GADD45B, the levels of major proteins in the Hippo pathway were evaluated via GADD45B knockdown or overexpression. The findings suggested that GADD45B knockdown reduced the levels of MST1, LATS1, and p-YAP/YAP both in vivo and in vitro, whereas GADD45B overexpression increased these levels [Figure 4C–E]. In addition, GADD45B knockdown reduced the phosphorylation levels of MST1 and transcriptional coactivator with PDZ-binding motif (TAZ) [Supplementary Figure 4, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C829]. Notably, variations in MST1 mRNA levels were consistent with the knockdown or overexpression of GADD45B [Figure 4F, G]. These findings indicate that GADD45B exacerbates intestinal I/R injury via stimulating the Hippo pathway. MST1, a proapoptotic kinase that is extensively expressed and integral to the Hippo pathway, is critical for mediating cell death induced by I/R injury.[23] Typically, DNA methylation of gene promoters suppresses gene expression, whereas DNA demethylation activates transcription.[39] In addition, studies have demonstrated that the MST1 promoter contains CpG sites and that its expression is correlated with DNA methylation levels.[25] Given the above findings, MST1 could be a potential target for GADD45B with a high probability.

GADD45B induces the expression of MST1 through DNA demethylation

MethPrimer Promoter was used to predict the CpG islands for MST1. The results indicated that the MST1 promoter region contained CpG islands [Figure 5A]. It has been demonstrated that GADD45B enhances gene expression by decreasing DNA methylation at specific sites.[21,40,41] To verify the potential mechanism of GADD45B involvement in the Hippo pathway, the methylation levels of the MST1 promoter were evaluated using MSP. MST1 promoter methylation was lower in the H/R group than in the control group, indicating that the increased MST1 levels after H/R may be associated with DNA demethylation. Furthermore, GADD45B knockdown increased the methylation level of the MST1 promoter in contrast to that in the H/R group, whereas GADD45B overexpression decreased the methylation level [Figure 5B]. In addition, the MST1 promoter region was analyzed using National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) data, and five pairs of primers were designed accordingly [Supplementary Table 1, http://links.lww.com/CM9/C829]. ChIP demonstrated that GADD45B binds to the MST1 promoter region [Figure 5C]. These results reveal that GADD45B promotes MST1 expression through DNA demethylation.

GADD45B cooperates with TET1 to promote the DNA demethylation of MST1

TETs are a common class of demethylases that reduce DNA methylation levels at gene promoters, thereby stimulating the expression of specific genes.[17] Prior research has shown that GADD45B can bind to TET1 and directly perform region-specific demethylation.[40] To further verify whether the molecular basis of GADD45B regulation of MST1 DNA demethylation in intestinal I/R depends on TET1, we first investigated whether intestinal I/R affects TET1 expression in Caco-2 cells. The findings indicated a remarkable rise in TET1 mRNA expression following intestinal I/R [Figure 6A]. Furthermore, via ChIP analysis, we observed that in Caco-2 cells overexpressing GADD45B, TET1 occupied a substantial proportion of the MST1 promoter [Figure 6B]. Co-IP experiments further demonstrated a direct interaction between TET1 and GADD45B [Figure 6C]. In addition, TET1 was knocked down in Caco-2 cells, and the knockdown efficacy was subsequently verified [Figure 6D]. MSP analysis of the MST1 promoter region methylation revealed that, in contrast to the pEX-3-GADD45B+H/R group, TET1 knockdown inhibited the DNA demethylation induced by GADD45B overexpression [Figure 6E]. These results indicate that GADD45B cooperates with TET1 to promote MST1 DNA demethylation. The proapoptotic impact of GADD45B in intestinal I/R injury partially depends on MST1/Hippo

To verify that MST1 may be necessary for the proapoptotic impact of GADD45B on intestinal I/R injury, we transfected plasmids for GADD45B overexpression and MST1 knockdown into Caco-2 cells. The efficiency of MST1 knockdown was then validated [Figure 7A]. The results indicated that the impairment of intestinal barrier function, increased apoptosis, and elevated expression of Hippo pathway markers caused by GADD45B overexpression in intestinal I/R were reversed by MST1 knockdown [Figure 7B–D]. MST1 knockdown also reduced the increase in TUNEL-positive apoptotic cells caused by GADD45B overexpression, as observed by the TUNEL assay [Figure 7E]. In a word, these findings illustrate that the proapoptotic impact of GADD45B on intestinal I/R injury at least partially relies on MST1/Hippo.

Discussion

Intestinal I/R is a common and severe condition[42] that can ultimately result in multiorgan dysfunction or failure.[43] In this study, we report the following findings: (1) Knockdown of GADD45B protects against apoptosis during intestinal I/R injury; (2) GADD45B activates the Hippo pathway, thereby exacerbating intestinal I/R; and (3) GADD45B interacts with TET1 to promote DNA demethylation of the MST1 promoter, thereby stimulating MST1 expression to promote apoptosis during intestinal I/R [Figure 9].

MST1 further activates the Hippo pathway, thereby exacerbating apoptosis induced by intestinal I/R. Apoptosis is a key cause of reperfusion injury. During ischemia, shortages of oxygen and energy, along with ATP depletion, alter the intracellular environment, thereby triggering apoptosis.[29] Dysregulation of apoptosis is a critical mechanism responsible for intestinal injury caused by I/R.[44] The involvement of GADD45B in apoptosis is widely recognized. Kim et al[13] demonstrated that GADD45B mediates ischemia/hypoxia-induced cardiomyocyte apoptosis and that GADD45B knockdown protects against ischemic heart injury in rats. Other studies have indicated that GADD45B overexpression promotes apoptosis in gastric cancer cells.[36] In addition, GADD45B can induce apoptosis in hepatocytes.[33] Conversely, Liu et al[11] demonstrated that GADD45B mitigates neuronal apoptosis following cerebral ischemia. Therefore, the impact of GADD45B on apoptosis varies according to both the type of cell and the specific stimuli involved.[7] Our study results are consistent with previous findings, showing significant apoptosis in intestinal tissues following I/R and in Caco-2 cells following H/R. The knockdown of GADD45B inhibited the activation of Bax and cleaved caspase-3, induced Bcl-2 expression, and reduced the TUNEL-positive rate, thereby alleviating intestinal I/R-induced apoptosis. However, we found that GADD45B knockdown did not directly alter the transcription levels of Bax and Bcl-2, suggesting that GADD45B may regulate Bax and Bcl-2 expression indirectly to induce apoptosis. On the contrary, GADD45B overexpression had the opposite effect. Therefore, we propose that GADD45B serves as an effective proapoptotic factor in intestinal I/R, and its knockdown can mitigate intestinal I/R injury. In addition, recent studies have indicated that GADD45B also plays a critical regulatory function in the pathogenesis of multiple inflammatory disorders. Our findings are in line with earlier research,[35,45] which revealed significant activation of NF-κB and elevated inflammatory cytokines following intestinal I/R. GADD45B knockdown in mice inhibited NF-κB activation and reduced related inflammatory cytokines. These findings imply that GADD45B may also regulate the inflammatory response during intestinal I/R through the NF-κB pathway, highlighting GADD45B as a critical target for intestinal I/R injury. Intestinal barrier dysfunction is a pivotal factor in exacerbating the harmful complications of intestinal I/R, as it can lead to intestinal bacterial translocation, secondary organ injury, multiple organ dysfunction syndrome (MODS), and even death.[46] The outcomes of this study align with prior findings,[47] showing exacerbated intestinal barrier damage after intestinal I/R, which was significantly improved upon GADD45B knockdown. These findings imply that GADD45B could serve as a vital target for intestinal I/R-induced MODS treatment.

MAPKs serve as critical regulators of cellular survival, proliferation, and differentiation. It has been shown that GADD45B is essential for sustaining the p38 MAPK pathway activation.[7] In this investigation, following H/R, the NF-κB inhibitor pyrrolidinedithiocarbamate ammonium and the p38 inhibitor SB203580 significantly reduced the expression of GADD45B, whereas GADD45B knockdown markedly diminished the activation of p38 and NF-κB. These findings suggest that GADD45B crosstalks with NF-κB and p38 during intestinal I/R, indicating that GADD45B is crucial to the pathogenesis of intestinal I/R.

GADD45 proteins participate in a number of cellular processes, typically correlated to cellular damage responses and stress signaling. Research has revealed that GADD45B is triggered by DNA damage and other stress-activated pathways, such as p38 and Jun N-terminal kinase (JNK).[6] In this study, we found that the p38 inhibitor SB203580, the JNK inhibitor SP600125, and GADD45B knockdown notably suppressed the expression of GADD45B following intestinal I/R, alleviating I/R-induced cell apoptosis and DNA damage. These results indicate that intestinal I/R upregulates GADD45B through activating the p38 and JNK pathways and that inhibition of GADD45B significantly reduces the DNA damage caused by intestinal I/R. Targeting GADD45B with stress signaling pathway inhibitors or DNA damage inhibitors may hold promise for treating intestinal I/R injury.

The Hippo pathway serves as an extremely conserved kinase cascade, regulating a variety of biological processes.[48] After the Hippo pathway is triggered, large tumor suppressor 1/2 (LATS1/2) are phosphorylated and activated by MST1/2, subsequently inhibiting YAP and TAZ.[49] The Hippo/YAP pathway is essential for mediating I/R injury across various organs.[38] Nogo-B stimulates the MST1-mediated Hippo/YAP pathway, which in turn enhances I/R-induced liver damage.[50] Neurofibromin 2 (NF2) exacerbates I/R injury by stimulating MST1 and suppressing YAP, thereby modulating Hippo signaling within the heart.[51] Hippo/MST1 signaling is implicated in acute cerebral I/R injury.[52] In this investigation, to elucidate the mechanisms of GADD45B during intestinal I/R, RNA-seq was conducted on H/R and si-GADD45B H/R samples, and KEGG analysis revealed significant enrichment of the Hippo pathway. GADD45B knockdown significantly reduced MST1 expression and phosphorylation levels, decreased LATS1 expression, and lowered the phosphorylation levels of YAP and TAZ, whereas GADD45B overexpression produced the opposite effect. These findings reveal that GADD45B promotes intestinal I/R by stimulating the Hippo pathway.

The GADD45 proteins may serve as a critical link between pathological or physiological stimuli and both epigenetic DNA modifications and gene expression.[40] Epigenetic modifications include noncoding RNAs, chromatin remodeling, DNA methylation, and histone modifications.[53] In eukaryotic genomes, DNA methylation predominantly occurs at CpG islands. DNMTs inhibit gene expression by converting cytosine (C) to 5-methylcytosine (5mC). In contrast, DNA demethylases (TETs) increase gene expression by converting 5mC to 5-hydroxymethylcytosine (5hmC).[54] The findings of previous research have suggested that the pathophysiology of I/R injury involves DNA methylation.[15] The multifunctional protein GADD45B is responsible for coordinating the active DNA demethylation processes in the nucleus.[55] Ma et al[21] demonstrated that the activity of GADD45B induces specific promoter DNA demethylation and corresponding gene expression. The downregulation of GADD45B modifies DNA methylation levels in a manner that is specific to phenotype, gene, and site.[56] GADD45B, in conjunction with TET1, facilitates DNA demethylation, thereby increasing gene expression.[40] In this study, to investigate how GADD45B regulates the Hippo pathway, we knocked down and overexpressed GADD45B to assess the consequent changes in MST1 mRNA and protein levels. Our findings demonstrated that the alterations in MST1 mRNA and protein levels mirrored those in GADD45B, suggesting that MST1 is a downstream target of GADD45B. GADD45B likely regulates MST1 expression through DNA demethylation of the MST1 promoter.

The activation of MST1/2 proteins acts as the primary signal initiating the Hippo pathway.[50] Increasing evidence reveals that MST1 is essential for the cell death associated with I/R injury.[23] MST1 increases cardiomyocyte apoptosis and exacerbates myocardial I/R injury.[57] MST1 deficiency reduces neuronal cell death, thereby preventing ischemic stroke.[52] Li et al[58] demonstrated that MST1 is implicated in renal I/R injury. Research has demonstrated that DNA methylation leads to reduced MST1 expression.[59,60] In this study, we investigated whether GADD45B-induced MST1 expression is linked to changes in DNA methylation by assessing MST1 promoter methylation levels via MSP. The results revealed that, compared with H/R, knockdown of GADD45B increased MST1 promoter methylation levels, whereas overexpression of GADD45B decreased MST1 promoter methylation levels. Moreover, ChIP assays revealed that GADD45B binds to the MST1 promoter site. Therefore, these findings indicate that GADD45B promotes MST1 expression through DNA demethylation, thereby activating the Hippo pathway.

According to recent studies, the GADD45 family exhibits a variety of activities in different biological systems by promoting active DNA demethylation at specific genomic loci. Although GADD45 proteins do not possess intrinsic enzymatic activity, growing evidence indicates that GADD45A and GADD45B participate in DNA demethylation by collaborating with other modifiers, such as thymine DNA glycosylase (TDG) and TET, which enhances the activity of TDG or TET, possibly by targeting them to specific loci undergoing DNA demethylation.[41,61,62] To further investigate the molecular basis of GADD45B-mediated MST1 DNA demethylation in intestinal I/R, we first determined that TET1 level was significantly upregulated following intestinal I/R, aligned with the trend observed for GADD45B. Through ChIP, Co-IP, and MSP, we subsequently confirmed that GADD45B can promote MST1 DNA demethylation by physically interacting with TET1. In a word, our findings indicate that GADD45B may function as a bridging protein that enhances DNA methylation turnover.

In summary, our research suggests that GADD45B promotes intestinal I/R injury by mediating apoptosis. Furthermore, GADD45B interacts with TET1 to promote DNA demethylation, which enhances MST1 expression and subsequently activates the Hippo pathway. The findings above imply that the GADD45B/TET1/MST1 axis is critical in intestinal I/R injury, providing useful insights into possible treatment targets.
