Measurement of Gas Diffusion Coefficients in Cores of Fine-Grained Lithologies Considering Stress and Adsorption Effects
Authors: Zexiang Sun, Shixin Zhou, Pengpeng Li
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractMatrix diffusivity is vital for developing tight sandstone and shale gas reservoirs. This study proposes a method to test the diffusivities of a core under confining pressure conditions using the gas diffusion technique. The diffusivities of methane and helium were examined in fine-grained rocks (sandy shale, silty sandstone, tight sandstone, and shale) under specific stress conditions. The results revealed that the gas diffusivities varied among the samples. The tight sandstones exhibited diffusivity higher than that of silty sandstone, sandy mudstone, and shale. Helium diffusivities in shales and sandy mudstones were 1 order of magnitude smaller, while methane diffusivities were 2 orders of magnitude smaller than those in tight sandstones. A positive correlation was observed between the stress sensitivity factor and the clay mineral content, indicating the influence of the clay minerals’ mechanical properties. Additionally, the shale gas diffusivities exhibited significant anisotropy due to slit-like gas channels parallel to laminae in shale. It was found that the impact of adsorption on diffusivity was positively correlated to the amount of adsorption. While the adsorption effect was negligible in tight sandstones, organic-rich shales and sandy mudstones experienced an order-of-magnitude reduction in methane diffusivities compared to helium. This study presents a method to evaluate the diffusion coefficient of a core matrix under a confining pressure. It provides insights into gas diffusion behavior in different fine-grained rocks, considering the effects of stress, clay mineral content, and adsorption. These findings contribute to understanding tight sandstone gas and shale gas reservoirs, aiding in the optimization of gas production strategies.

Introduction

The gas-in-place content and migration ability are critical for developing and evaluating shale gas.1−4 The pores in shale can be classified into two categories: microfractures and matrix pores, and gas follows different migration mechanisms in these two types of pore structures.5 In microfractures, laminar flow is the primary mechanism, driven by the fluid pressure gradient, and can be described by Darcy’s law and the Hagen–Poiseuille equation or by first-order or second-order slip equations considering the slip effect.6−9 In matrix pores, diffusion mainly dominates fluid transport, as described by Fick’s law.10,11 The effective diffusion coefficient in the matrix is influenced by factors such as matrix pore size, oil–water saturation, and methane adsorption effects.11,12 Among them, the adsorption expansion effect is the focus of research; methane adsorption is generally believed to reduce the pore size, thereby reducing the effective diffusion coefficient.13−15

The evaluation of the methane diffusion capacity under in situ conditions is a crucial research topic. The matrix diffusion coefficient/matrix permeability is currently tested in fixed particle size samples under laboratory conditions.10,16 Still, the test results are questioned because there is a significant difference between the particle samples and the actual conditions in geological formations. For instance, the stress conditions in geological formations can change the size of fluid migration channels, especially in clay-rich shale, coal seams, and tight sandstones.17−20 However, there are few reports on the method for measuring gas diffusion coefficients under stress conditions and there is a lack of valuable experimental data.

This study proposes a new method for testing the gas diffusion coefficients of core-scale rocks under stress conditions. The study tested methane and helium diffusion coefficients in fine-grained rocks under different stress conditions. And we analyzed the effects of stress and gas adsorption on the matrix diffusion coefficient. The method proposed in this study further improves the quantitative evaluation of the shale gas drainage capacity under in situ stress conditions.

Samples and Methods

Samples

The fine-grained core samples for this study include marine shale of the Longmaxi Formation in the Sichuan Basin (parallel bedding direction L1P and perpendicular bedding direction L1V), sandy shale (YKCO1, YKCO2), silty sandstone (YKCO3), and tight sandstone (YKCO4) of Yanchang Formation in the Ordos Basin. The experimental samples were processed into rock core plugs with a diameter of 2.5 cm and a length of 2.5–3.5 cm. Methods

Measurement of Methane Adsorption Capacity

Methane adsorption experiments of powdered samples (60–80 mesh) were conducted using a gravimetric sorption analyzer with a magnetic suspension balance (Rubotherm, ISOSORP-HP III Static), according to Chinese Standard GB/T 19560-2008. After sample pretreatment and buoyancy measurements, methane adsorption was tested at 10 pressure test points over a pressure range of 0–12 MPa, with an equilibration time of 2 h at each test point. All measurements were conducted at 30 °C with a relative error of ±0.02 °C. Detailed test procedures are reported in some pieces of literature.21−24

Measurements of Stress-Loaded Gas Diffusivity in the Core Scale

In the study by Cui et al.,10 the matrix permeability/diffusivity of crushed samples with a certain mesh count was determined using late-time data. This method was widely used for the characterization of matrix permeability and/or diffusivity of mudstones, especially for fine-grained rocks.11,12,26 In this study, we extend their method to the core scale to characterize the gas diffusivity of stress-loaded cores. In this study, helium and methane are used to measure diffusivity.

The experimental apparatus for measuring the gas diffusivity is shown in Figure 3. The test steps are as follows: (1) The tested sample is placed in the core holder, and the Cp is loaded through a booster pump. (2) Valve 1 is closed, and valves 2 and 3 are turned on, and then the reference cell and sample are vacuumed to remove air. (3) Valve 2 and valve 3 are closed, and valve 1 is opened, Making the gas pressure in the reference cell reach approximately 200 psi (Pr0), and then valve 1 is closed. (4) High-pressure gas in the reference cell is filled into the pressure-loaded core by turning valve 2 on until equilibrium is reached with a final equilibrium pressure Pe, during which a high-precision pressure sensor records the pressure decay with time. (5) Finally, valve 3 is opened and the gas in the core is released through a vacuum pump. As shown in Figure 3, the entire gas device is placed in a thermostat to avoid pressure fluctuations due to temperature changes during the gas dynamic filling.

The diffusion coefficient of helium varies significantly among the different samples. As shown in Figure 6, the diffusivity of helium is greater than that of methane. The range of the helium diffusion coefficient in the tight sandstone (YKCO4) of the Yanchang Formation in the Ordos Basin is 2.33–3.96 (×10–7 m2/s), which is greater than the helium diffusivity of the mudstone and sandy mudstone and 2 orders of magnitude larger than that of the Longmaxi Formation shale in the Sichuan Basin. The helium diffusion coefficient of shale exhibits significant anisotropy, with the diffusion coefficient of the horizontal core being approximately an order of magnitude larger than that of the vertical core. Some studies suggest this is related to the anisotropy of gas channel tortuosity in the core.28,7

The variation range of methane diffusivity is much more extensive, reaching 3 orders of magnitude. The methane diffusivities of samples YKCO1 and YKCO2 are the smallest, with a range of 4.87–9.17 (×10–9, m2/s), followed by the methane diffusivity of sample YKCO3, and the methane diffusion coefficient of YKCO4 is the largest, with a range of 2.37–3.97 (×10–7, m2/s). The methane diffusivities of the Longmaxi Formation shale are also anisotropic, with sample L1V (perpendicular to the bedding) having methane diffusivities ranging from 1.95 to 3.21 (×10–9 m2/s), which is an order of magnitude smaller than that of sample L1P (parallel to the bedding).

In this study, the methane and helium diffusivities show a decreasing trend with an increase of the stress state. Discussion

The matrix diffusion coefficient of fine-grained rocks is an essential parameter in describing gas migration behavior and is vital to evaluating gas exploration and development. The gas expansion approach is a classical laboratory measurement method that is founded on a fixed particle size sample but does not evaluate the gas diffusivities under in situ stress conditions. This study extends this method to the core scale and enables measurement of gas diffusion coefficients under radial stress conditions; the gas diffusivities of different fine-grained rocks were compared under various stress conditions, and it was found that the gas diffusivities are stress-sensitive, the methane diffusivities are related to the amount of methane adsorbed, and the stress sensitivity and its influencing factors and the effect of adsorption on the methane diffusion coefficients deserve further discussion.

Stress Sensitivity of Diffusion Coefficients and the Influencing Factors

There are few studies on the stress sensitivity of diffusion coefficients, and the stress sensitivity of permeability has been more thoroughly studied.34−39 It is generally accepted that stress sensitivity is related to clay mineral content.40 It is suggested that clay minerals are more susceptible to deformation under confining pressures than rigid minerals such as feldspar and quartz, making the gas channels narrower and thus showing greater stress sensitivity.7,29 As shown in Figure 7, the stress sensitivity of the diffusion coefficients of helium and methane for the samples in this study showed a significant positive correlation with clay mineral content, with correlation coefficients R of 0.95 and 0.9, respectively, and P-values less than 0.01.

Pearson correlation coefficient (R) matrix (A) and P-value matrix (B) compare the characteristic parameters of various samples. Furthermore, the stress sensitivity of the shale gas diffusion coefficients is significantly anisotropic, with higher stress sensitivity in the horizontal lamina direction than in the vertical laminae direction. This study suggests that this may be related to the gas channel morphology, with slit-like channels in the shale parallel to the laminae and stresses perpendicular to the laminae more likely to compress and narrow the slits than stresses parallel to the laminae.

Effect of Adsorption on the Diffusion of Methane

To investigate the effect of adsorption on methane diffusivities, the ratio of the helium diffusion coefficient to the methane diffusion coefficient, H, is defined in this study. A larger value of H indicates a more significant effect of adsorption on methane diffusion and vice versa.

Figure 8 shows the H-values for the studied samples at various confining pressure conditions; YKCO4 has the smallest H-value of the analyzed samples at around 1, indicating that methane and helium have the same matrix diffusion capacity for this sample. The sample with the largest H-value was L1V, with an H-value ranging from 5.16 to 6.05, indicating that the diffusion capacity of helium was much greater than that of methane in sample L1V.

H-values are for samples at different overlay pressures. The adsorption of the sample at 0.5 MPa is defined by a color bar, and the colors of the markers indicate the amount of adsorption.

It is found that there is no significant correlation between the H-value and confining pressure and clay mineral content, with P-values of 0.59 and 0.25, respectively, in Figure 9B. However, H-values were positively correlated with the total organic carbon (TOC) content and the equilibrium methane adsorption capacity (SP0.5 MPa) of the samples in a statistically significant manner with correlation coefficients of 0.75 and 0.78, respectively, and P-values less than 0.01. It shows that the adsorption effect decreases the diffusivities of methane, and the greater the adsorption amount, the more noticeable the adsorption effect’s suppression effect on methane diffusion, which is consistent with the study by Zhao et al.15

Stress–Adsorption Coupling Effects on Methane Diffusivities

The confining pressures cause the core matrix to contract, leading to a reduction in the diffusivities, known as the stress effect, while the adsorption effect causes the core matrix to expand, leading to a decrease in gas channels and a reduction in the diffusivities, known as the adsorption effect.

The methane matrix diffusion coefficient under stress conditions is controlled by the stress effects and sorption effects. To quantify the contribution of these two effects to the loss of methane diffusivities, this study defines a stress effect factor (HCp) and an adsorption effect factor (HAd).

HCp and HAd vary from 0 to 1, with larger values indicating more significant stress or adsorption effects and HCp plus HAd equal to 1.

The variation of sample HCp and HAd values with stress is shown in Figure 9. The stress effect and adsorption effect of the methane diffusivities are very different among the samples; the stress effect gradually increases and the adsorption effect gradually decreases with increasing overburden pressure.

In the experimental confining pressure range, the methane diffusion coefficient of sample YKCO4 was mainly controlled by stress effects, with HCp values ranging from 0.96 to 0.99. In contrast, the methane diffusion coefficient of sample L1V was mainly controlled by adsorption effects with HAd values ranging from 0.97 to 0.91, and the HAd values for samples L1P, YKCO1, and YKCO2 were mainly influenced by sorption effects and ranged from 0.71 to 0.88, 0.56 to 0.78, and 0.64 to 0.84, respectively.

In the case of sample YKCO3, the methane diffusivities are affected by the adsorption effect when the overburden pressure is less than 15 MPa, while the stress effect of the methane diffusivities is greater than the adsorption effect when the confining pressure is greater than 15 MPa.

Conclusions

The matrix diffusion coefficient is a characterization of the fluid activity of the pore matrix and is essential for tight sandstone gas and shale gas development. This study proposes a method for testing the diffusion coefficient of a core matrix with confining pressure conditions based on the gas diffusion method. Using the new approach, we tested the matrix diffusivities of methane and helium under certain stress conditions in typical fine-grained rocks (sandy shale, silty sandstone, tight sandstone, and shale). The effects of stress and gas adsorption on the gas diffusion coefficients were analyzed. The following insights were obtained.

Among the samples studied, the gas diffusion coefficients of the tight sandstones are greater than those of the silty sandstone, greater than those of the sandy mudstones, and greater than those of the shales. The helium diffusion coefficients of the shales and sandy mudstones are 1 order of magnitude smaller than those of the tight sandstones, while the methane diffusivities are 2 orders of magnitude smaller than those of the tight sandstones.

The stress sensitivity of the gas diffusion coefficient varies considerably among samples, with a positive correlation between the stress sensitivity index and the clay mineral content, which is related to the smaller mechanical properties of the clay minerals, and a significant directional anisotropy in the stress sensitivity of the shale gas diffusion coefficient, which is determined by the morphology of the slit-like gas channels parallel to the laminae in the shale.

The adsorption effect on the methane diffusivities is positively correlated with the amount of adsorption; for tight sandstones, the adsorption effect hardly affects the methane diffusivities, but for organic-rich shales and sandy mudstones, the adsorption effect can lead to an order of magnitude reduction in the methane diffusivities compared with that of helium.
