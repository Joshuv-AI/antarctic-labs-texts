Association of periodontal disease with risk atrial fibrillation: systematic review
Authors: Luo Xin-nian, Luo Xin-yi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeriodontal disease (PD), a chronic inflammatory condition affecting nearly 50% of adults globally, drives systemic inflammation and cardiovascular diseases. Atrial fibrillation (AF), the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia, shares pathophysiological links with inflammation. This systematic review evaluates the association between PD and AF risk, addressing gaps in existing evidence.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted in MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library up to September 2025. Observational studies that met PICOS criteria (adults with/without PD, PD exposure, AF outcome) were included. Study quality was assessed using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS). Data were synthesized narratively due to heterogeneity in PD/AF definitions.ResultsFour high-quality studies (NOS ≥ 6) were analyzed. All studies demonstrated a consistent association between PD and increased AF risk, with adjusted hazard ratios (HRs) ranging from 1.03 to 1.31. Severe PD showed stronger associations (HR: 1.31, 95% CI: 1.06–1.62), while PD recovery attenuated risk (HR: 1.00, 95% CI: 0.97–1.04). Mechanistically, PD-related systemic inflammation (e.g., elevated C-reactive protein) and pathogen translocation (e.g., Porphyromonas gingivalis) was implicated in atrial remodeling. Regular dental care was associated with reduced AF incidence.ConclusionPD is significantly associated with an elevated risk of AF, particularly in severe or persistent cases. The findings underscore PD as a modifiable risk factor, highlighting the potential of periodontal interventions in AF prevention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-025-07135-3.Keywords: Periodontal disease, Atrial fibrillation, Systemic inflammation, Risk factor, Oral health

Introduction

Periodontal disease (PD), encompassing chronic inflammatory conditions such as gingivitis and periodontitis, represents a substantial global public health burden, affecting nearly 50% of adults worldwide and ranking as the sixth most prevalent human disease [1]. In 2021, PD afflicted approximately 951 million individuals—corresponding to an age-standardized prevalence of 17,000 per 100,000—and accounted for 6.2 million disability-adjusted life years [2]. Characterized by the progressive destruction of the tooth-supporting apparatus through dysbiotic microbial communities and aberrant host immune responses, PD not only precipitates local periodontal breakdown but also elevates systemic inflammatory markers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) [3]. This persistent, low-grade inflammatory state contributes to both local periodontal breakdown and systemic pathophysiological processes, exerting detrimental effects on distal organ systems. Among these, cardiovascular diseases have received particular attention, positioning PD as a potentially modifiable risk factor with implications beyond oral health [4].

Atrial fibrillation (AF) is the most common sustained cardiac arrhythmia globally and is associated with significantly increased risks of ischemic stroke, heart failure, myocardial infarction, and all-cause mortality [5]. It also imposes a substantial burden on healthcare systems and impairs patients’ quality of life [5–7]. The global burden of AF is increasing, driven by population aging, rising prevalences of hypertension, obesity, and diabetes, and improved detection of asymptomatic or subclinical cases [8–11]. Despite advancements in pharmacological and interventional therapies, AF remains clinically challenging due to its variable presentation, limited success in maintaining sinus rhythm, and complex thromboembolic risk stratification.

Accumulating evidence underscores the role of systemic inflammation in the initiation and progression of AF. This association is primarily mediated through atrial structural and electrical remodeling, driven by oxidative stress, myofibroblast proliferation, and extracellular matrix deposition [12–14]. As a chronic source of systemic inflammation and bacteremia, PD represents a plausible contributor to AF pathogenesis. Periodontal pathogens and inflammatory mediators may translocate into the systemic circulation, eliciting endothelial dysfunction, autonomic dysregulation, and atrial remodeling—mechanisms implicated in AF development. Thus, a biologically and pathophysiologically plausible link exists between PD and AF.

Several epidemiological studies have investigated the association between PD and AF risk [15–21]. However, their findings remain heterogeneous due to variations in diagnostic criteria, control of confounders, and population characteristics. Given the substantial public health implications of both PD and AF, and the potential for periodontal interventions to reduce cardiovascular risk, a systematic synthesis of the available evidence is warranted. Therefore, this systematic review aims to critically evaluate and synthesize data from epidemiological studies assessing the association between PD and AF incidence.

Methods

Literature search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was performed in MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library from database inception through September 2025. The MEDLINE (PubMed) strategy combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH) and free-text terms to capture periodontal disease and atrial arrhythmias. Equivalent search strategies were constructed for EMBASE (EMTREE terms plus free text) and the Cochrane Library; searches were limited to human subjects and were carried out without date restriction up to September 2025. The complete electronic search strings for MEDLINE, EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library are provided in Supplementary Tables 1 to allow reproducibility. In addition to the primary database search, we employed several secondary approaches: we manually screened the reference lists of all eligible studies and of relevant review articles; we performed forward citation chasing using Google Scholar and Web of Science to identify subsequent reports citing included studies; we searched grey literature sources, including OpenGrey and the proceedings of major cardiology conferences (European Society of Cardiology and American Heart Association); and we consulted two domain experts (one in periodontology, one in cardiovascular epidemiology) to minimize the risk of missing eligible studies.

Study selection criteria

Studies were included if they met the following PICOS criteria:

Population: Adults (≥ 18 years) with or without diagnosed PD.

Population: Adults (≥ 18 years) with or without diagnosed PD.

Exposure: PD defined by clinical or radiographic criteria, International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes, or validated indices such as the Periodontal Profile Class (PPC) or Community Periodontal Index (CPI).

Exposure: PD defined by clinical or radiographic criteria, International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes, or validated indices such as the Periodontal Profile Class (PPC) or Community Periodontal Index (CPI).

Comparator: Individuals without PD.

Comparator: Individuals without PD.

Outcome: Incident AF confirmed by electrocardiogram (ECG), administrative codes (e.g., ICD-9-CM 427.31/32), or medical records.

Outcome: Incident AF confirmed by electrocardiogram (ECG), administrative codes (e.g., ICD-9-CM 427.31/32), or medical records.

Study Design: Observational studies including cohort or case-control designs.

Study Design: Observational studies including cohort or case-control designs.

Exclusion criteria encompassed animal studies, reviews, editorials, conference abstracts, cross-sectional analyses, mendelian randomisation studies, investigations of post-ablation AF recurrence, studies lacking disaggregated PD and AF data, and those with overlapping datasets.

Study screening and data extraction

Two independent reviewers (XN-L and XY-L) screened titles and abstracts, followed by full-text evaluations. Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Data were extracted using a standardized form, including study author, publication year, country, design, sample size, follow-up duration, PD and AF definitions, and effect estimates (odds ratios [ORs] or hazard ratios [HRs] with 95% confidence intervals [CIs]). Both unadjusted and adjusted estimates were recorded, along with the covariates used for adjustment.

Quality assessment

The quality of the studies was evaluated using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS) for cohort and case-control studies. The NOS assesses three domains: (1) Selection (e.g., representativeness of the cohort, ascertainment of exposure); (2) Comparability (e.g., adjustment for confounding variables such as age, sex, smoking, diabetes, and hypertension); and (3) Outcome (e.g., method of AF ascertainment, adequacy of follow-up). Studies with a total score ≥ 6 out of 9 were classified as high quality [22–25]. Two reviewers (XN-L and XY-L) independently performed the quality assessment, and disagreements were resolved by consensus.

Data synthesis

Due to anticipated heterogeneity in definitions of PD (e.g., ICD codes vs. clinical indices), AF ascertainment (e.g., ECG vs. administrative data), and the extent of covariate adjustment across studies, a quantitative meta-analysis was not conducted. Instead, we performed a narrative synthesis. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 guidelines for systematic reviews [26].

Results

Study selection

A total of 214 records were retrieved from MEDLINE (via PubMed), EMBASE, and the Cochrane Library. After removing duplicates and screening titles and abstracts, 206 records were excluded, leaving 8 articles for full-text evaluation. Following full-text review, 4 studies were excluded for the following reasons: one Mendelian randomization study [27], one cross-sectional analysis [21], and two studies with overlapping datasets [19, 20]. Ultimately, 4 observational studies [15–18] met al.l eligibility criteria and were included in this systematic review (Fig. 1).

PD assessment varied and included clinical periodontal indices (e.g., CPI), longitudinal dental examinations, and administrative coding. AF ascertainment methods also differed, encompassing ICD codes, electrocardiographic confirmation, and adjudicated outcomes. Despite these methodological differences, all studies consistently identified that PD was associated with unfavorable cardiometabolic profiles, supporting the plausibility of its link to AF.

Quality assessment

All four studies were rated as high quality based on NOS criteria, with total scores ≥ 6 out of 9 (Supplementary Table 2). Selection domains were well-addressed, particularly in the nationwide cohort studies by Chen et al. [15] and Park et al. [18], which provided strong population representativeness. Confounding was appropriately addressed in most studies through adjustment for key variables such as age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, and smoking. However, Chen et al. [15] lacked adjustment for obesity and alcohol consumption, which may introduce residual confounding. AF outcomes were reliably ascertained in all studies through ECG findings, hospital records, or validated administrative codes. Im et al. [16], although well-executed, had a small sample size and was limited to a single-center, affecting external validity. Overall, the included studies demonstrated methodological rigor, strengthening confidence in their findings.

Association between PD and AF risk

Four cohort studies were included in this systematic review, encompassing diverse populations and methodologies. Despite heterogeneity in exposure definitions, outcome ascertainment, and covariate adjustment, all studies consistently reported a significant association between PD and an increased risk of AF.

Chen et al. (2016), utilizing a nationwide Taiwanese claims database, found that patients with PD had a 31% higher risk of developing AF or atrial flutter (adjusted HR: 1.31, 95% CI: 1.25–1.36) compared to non-PD controls after adjusting for comorbidities and healthcare utilization. Im et al. (2018), in a hospital-based cohort, demonstrated that AF patients with PD had significantly higher risks of major adverse cardiac events (MACE) and arrhythmic events (including AF), along with elevated inflammatory markers, suggesting a role for chronic inflammation in AF progression. Sen et al. (2021), using data from the ARIC study, showed that severe PD was associated with a 31% increased risk of AF (adjusted HR: 1.31, 95% CI: 1.06–1.62). Regular dental care was protective, reducing AF risk by 12% (adjusted HR: 0.88, 95% CI: 0.78–0.99). Mediation analysis indicated that AF partially explains the link between PD and cardioembolic stroke. Park et al. (2023), in a Korean nationwide cohort, observed that chronic PD was associated with the highest AF risk (adjusted HR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.07), while recovery from PD reduced AF risk compared to persistent PD (adjusted HR: 0.97, 95% CI: 0.94–0.99). Development of PD increased AF risk relative to those remaining PD-free (adjusted HR: 1.04, 95% CI: 1.01–1.08).

Discussion

The consistent findings across the included studies indicate that periodontal disease is associated with an increased risk of AF, likely mediated through systemic inflammation and bacteremia. Recovery from PD or regular dental care may attenuate this risk, suggesting that PD is a modifiable risk factor for AF. These results underscore the importance of oral health management in cardiovascular risk stratification and support the integration of periodontal care into preventive strategies for AF. Further prospective studies are warranted to establish causality and evaluate the impact of periodontal interventions on AF incidence and outcomes.

PD is mechanistically linked to AF through systemic inflammation and direct pathogen-mediated effects. PD fuels systemic inflammation via elevated CRP, IL-6, and TNF-α [28, 29], promoting atrial electrical and structural remodeling—key drivers of AF [12–14, 29, 30]. Histological evidence confirms that PD correlates with atrial fibrosis, mediated by galectin-3 and TGF-β1 upregulation [31, 32]. Additionally, Porphyromonas gingivalis, a keystone periodontal pathogen, translocates hematogenously to atrial tissue [33]. This translocation exacerbates fibrosis via TLR-2/4 activation and promotes thrombogenesis through endothelial dysfunction and platelet aggregation, linking PD to AF-related complications like stroke [16, 33].

For clinicians, these findings highlight PD as a potentially modifiable cardiovascular risk factor. Routine periodontal screening and treatment could be integrated into cardiovascular care to reduce systemic inflammation. Supporting this, Sen et al. [17] found that regular dental care (versus episodic visits) was associated with a lower AF risk, and Park et al. [18] showed that PD recovery reduced AF incidence compared to chronic PD. Conversely, untreated PD in AF patients predicted worse outcomes: Im et al. [16] reported that AF patients with PD had higher rates of recurrent arrhythmias and adverse cardiac events. Together, these data suggest that periodontal interventions and good oral hygiene may not only improve dental health but also contribute to AF prevention and better arrhythmia management [34].

On a population level, the PD–AF link has broad public health implications. Periodontal disease is extremely common (affecting roughly half of adults in many populations [29], so even a small relative increase in AF risk translates into a large absolute number of cases. Importantly, PD is largely preventable and treatable with relatively low-cost measures. Public health strategies that promote oral hygiene and access to dental care could therefore yield cardiovascular benefits, potentially lowering AF incidence. These findings reinforce the view that integrated health policies targeting chronic inflammation (including oral infections) may contribute to reducing the rising global burden of AF and stroke.

A recent meta-analysis by Leelaviwat and colleagues [35] reported a pooled association between PD and AF/atrial flutter (pooled OR ≈ 1.33), which suggests a consistent positive relationship across prior cohort studies. However, that meta-analysis included multiple reports from Taiwan’s NHIRD [15, 20] and did not include Park et al. [18], the large Korean National Health Insurance cohort that we included in the present review. Because inclusion of overlapping administrative cohorts may influence pooled estimates and because omission of large, independent cohorts can bias inference about effect magnitude and generalizability, we elected to avoid pooling and instead to perform a structured narrative synthesis after excluding overlapping reports where necessary; this approach preserves the independent information contributed by large, high-quality cohorts (for example Park et al. [18]) while transparently highlighting areas of clinical heterogeneity and residual confounding. Our review therefore complements and refines the conclusions of Leelaviwat et al. [35] by (i) explicitly documenting and removing potential duplication of source populations, (ii) incorporating the most recent large cohort evidence, and (iii) presenting a synthesis that foregrounds differences in exposure/outcome ascertainment that limit straightforward pooling.

Beyond methodological heterogeneity, the included studies differ in clinically meaningful ways that affect interpretation. Administrative cohorts frequently rely on diagnostic and procedure codes to define periodontal disease and may lack reliable data on key confounders such as tobacco use, body mass index and alcohol consumption; clinical cohorts (for example the ARIC dental subcohort) offer detailed periodontal phenotyping but are smaller and may be enriched for particular demographic groups. These differences are important because periodontal disease is associated with cardiometabolic comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidemia) that are themselves causal risk factors for AF; thus, incomplete adjustment for such confounders can inflate apparent associations. Mechanistically, however, there is a biologically plausible pathway linking periodontitis to atrial remodeling via systemic inflammation and microbial translocation, as discussed in prior reviews [36, 37] and supported by recent epidemiologic and mechanistic studies that connect oral inflammation to systemic inflammatory burden and to adverse cardiovascular phenotypes [38–40]. These sources strengthen the plausibility of a causal contribution of oral inflammation to AF risk but do not eliminate the possibility of residual confounding in observational studies; randomized intervention trials and mechanistic human studies are therefore needed to clarify causality and quantify the preventive potential of periodontal therapy.

Limitations

This systematic review has several limitations that warrant consideration. First, all included studies were observational in nature, precluding definitive causal inferences between PD and AF. Residual confounding remains possible, particularly regarding variables such as smoking, alcohol consumption, obesity, and genetic susceptibility, which were not uniformly adjusted for. Second, heterogeneity in PD assessment—ranging from clinical indices to diagnostic codes—may have led to misclassification and limited cross-study comparability. Third, differences in AF ascertainment methods, including reliance on administrative data in some studies, may have introduced variability in outcome accuracy. Fourth, generalizability may be limited due to geographic variation, as studies were conducted in Taiwan, South Korea, and the United States, each with distinct population demographics and healthcare infrastructures. Finally, we acknowledge that the review was not prospectively registered in PROSPERO. Although the absence of a PROSPERO registration does not change the study data or the reported effect estimates, prospective registration is recommended to increase transparency and reduce the risk of selective reporting; this omission is therefore a limitation of the present report and should be considered when interpreting our findings.

Conclusions

This systematic review provides compelling evidence for an association between periodontal disease and an increased risk of atrial fibrillation. All four high-quality observational studies consistently demonstrated positive associations, with the strongest risks observed in individuals with severe or persistent PD. Future research should focus on prospective longitudinal studies with standardized PD and AF definitions, robust adjustment for lifestyle and clinical confounders, and exploration of mechanistic pathways.
