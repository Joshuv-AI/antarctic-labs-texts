Temperature matters: the potential impact of thermoregulatory mechanisms in brain–body physiology
Authors: Elizabeth A. Repasky, Bonnie L. Hylander, Hemn Mohammadpour
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
In this Outlook, Repasky et al. discuss the importance of thermoregulation in both preclinical and clinic research, delineating the energetic trade-offs underlying thermal and immune metabolism.

The impact of thermoregulatory physiology is not fully appreciated in preclinical research

An understanding of the basic elements of the “brain–body” reflexes that maintain a stable core body temperature emerged as one of the earliest accomplishments in physiology, becoming “textbook” in the 1950s, when generations of physiologists worked on revealing the role of the hypothalamus and the large number of autonomic and behavioral mechanisms involved with warming or cooling the body. The Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 2021 was awarded jointly to David Julius and Ardem Patapoutian, both participants at the 88th Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory Symposium on Brain Body Physiology for their discoveries of the receptors for temperature and touch, fundamental research that revealed missing links in the sensory arm of thermal perception. The surprising fact that all mammalian and avian species maintain a core temperature within a narrow range (generally within 2°C–3°C of 37°C) and that other “exothermic” species strive for their own narrow internal temperature preference suggests a remarkable evolutionary dependence on a stable temperature range and an association with the development of the central nervous system. Over two decades ago, the late Christopher Gordon noted a decline in the number of physiologists who were working in the area of thermoregulation and suggested that it was because “thermoregulation works so well” (Gordon 1993), with no known inherent diseases associated with its function, in comparison with other homeostatic processes. We may add to his insight that the specific centers of the brain known to regulate core temperature (i.e., the hypothalamus) are associated with few known intrinsic pathologies compared with the frequency with which other regions of the brain are susceptible to diseases such as epilepsy or Alzheimer's or to psychological disorders such as depression.

The relatively limited attention given to thermoregulation in today's biomedical research environment is reflected by the observation that essentially all preclinical rodent research has taken place using mice housed at Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC)-mandated ambient temperatures below their thermoneutral zone; this results in a mild yet chronic cold stress that activates the sympathetic nervous system to drive an increase in heat-generating metabolism and heat-conserving behavioral changes. Indeed, most researchers are not aware that in the background of experimental neural measurements obtained in laboratory mice, there is a substantial activation of multiple thermoregulatory nerves and brain loci required for warming metabolism and behavior and that these activities have the potential to significantly skew data obtained in experimental protocols for a variety of mouse models of human diseases (for reviews, see Hylander et al. 2019; James et al. 2023). In our opinion, it is especially critical for studies focused on brain–body physiology that we conduct preclinical research with more understanding of how outcomes are influenced by thermoregulatory processes generated by housing conditions in preclinical rodent models. One suggestion is to conduct “control” experiments at thermoneutral temperatures.

“Hot” new research is emerging on thermoregulation

At the same time, presentations at the 88th CSHL Symposium reflect a growing recognition of how much we can still improve our understanding of thermoregulation itself. For example, there are new loci in the brain being discovered that play critical roles in the survival of endotherms in the cold. As discussed in podium and poster presentations from the laboratory of Dr. Li Ye (Scripps Research), maintaining the proper body temperature is energetically costly for endothermic animals, prompting food-seeking behavior in the cold, yet neural mechanisms involved in this fundamentally important behavior are not well understood. This team found that the xiphoid (Xi) nucleus, a small, understudied nucleus in the thalamus, was selectively activated by chronic but not acute cold exposures and established Xi as a key region in the control of cold-induced feeding critical for the maintenance of energy homeostasis (L. Ye, pers. commun.).

A poster presentation by Sahoo and Carcea (Rutgers Health) addressed the still poorly understood neural basis for “huddling,” which helps maintain a constant core body temperature, by examining the role of the TRPM3 sensor for “warmth” and the TRPM8 “cold” sensor (B. Sahoo and I. Carcea, pers. commun.). This research is important because in many diseases modeled in mice (e.g., tumor growth), animals are often sequentially removed from the cage for time-point analysis, leaving the remaining mice unable to optimally huddle for sufficient warmth. Thus, experimental designs using singly housed mice, longitudinal studies where mice are sequentially removed from group housing, or experiments comparing singly housed mice with group-housed mice can all be introducing variables that researchers need to take into consideration.

Using a novel open-source thermal camera for recording temperature changes, Vinograd and colleagues (California Institute of Technology) showed that emotional states manifest not only through behavioral responses but also via alterations in body temperature (https://www.github.com/Davimeleon/Hot-Button-Camera). The specific neuronal mechanisms coordinating these thermal responses, particularly in relation to emotions such as aggression, fear, and mating, remain largely unknown. Most intriguingly, they observed that activation of Sf1-expressing neurons in the ventromedial hypothalamus, known to modulate fear responses, elicited significant increases in core temperature. Conversely, the introduction of a known fear-inducing stimulus (a rat) had no effect on the temperature of the mice, suggesting that there is a heterogeneous subpopulation of Sf1 neurons that is specific for regulating different physiological and emotional stimuli (A. Vinograd, D.B. Peral, J. Kim, and D.J. Anderson, pers. commun.).

Our own work, presented at the meeting, emphasizes that the energy needed for thermoregulation in laboratory mice is balanced by adrenergic receptor-driven suppression of immunity when tumors arise. For this work, we are exploiting the mildly cool and IACUC-required housing temperature for mice (22°C–23°C) and comparing this with data obtained in mice housed at a more thermoneutral temperature (29°C–30°C), which reduces the production of norepinephrine needed for adaptive thermogenesis. Our findings show that signaling through β2-adrenergic receptors (β2-AR) in intratumor CD8+ T cells during chronic cold stress at 22°C–23°C drives an “exhausted” phenotype (Qiao et al. 2021). On the other hand, the suppressive function of myeloid cells, especially myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs), which rely on oxidative phosphorylation for their immunosuppressive function, improves in mice housed at 22°C–23°C (Mohammadpour et al. 2021). The immunosuppression that is caused by thermoregulatory demands also results in reduced responses to immunotherapy, radiation, and chemotherapy (Eng et al. 2015; Daneshmandi et al. 2024), and we are using these data in the rationale for combining propranolol with other cancer therapy protocols in clinical trials (Gandhi et al. 2021). In contrast to what we have reported (that cold ambient temperature suppresses antitumor immunity in favor of maintaining a normal core temperature), Ganeshan et al. (2019) reported a different physiological trade-off. When comparing mice injected with LPS to simulate an infection, mice at 30°C were able to divert energy to the innate immune response by reducing physical activity alone, whereas mice at 22°C coped with this demand by lowering basal metabolic rate and decreasing adaptive thermogenesis (i.e., entering a hypometabolic state). Additionally, they found that the mice at 22°C tolerated a higher Escherichia coli burden and survived better than those at 30°C. These responses suggest that a tumor may elicit different energetic trade-offs than pathogens; however, this also indicates how variable these trade-off decisions can be and hints at a dynamic regulation by the brain–nerve–immunity axis.

Summary and personal insight gained at the 88th CSHL Symposium

There are still large gaps in what we know about thermoregulation, including how background (and unmonitored) activation of thermoregulatory pathways under standard housing temperature could be skewing data obtained while studying other brain–body physiologies. Indeed, we believe that thermoregulatory pathways are influencing many neural interactions that, on the surface, seem to have little to do with temperature. These examples of how energy usage results in physiological trade-offs in the cold and how emotional stress can impact body temperature clearly indicate the pervasive nature of thermoregulatory pathways. Defining trade-off mechanisms of immune suppression under conditions of thermal stress may clarify how other forms of adrenergic stress that use the sympathetic nervous system affect disease outcome.

On a personal note, upon arriving at CSHL for the 88th Symposium, I (E.A.R.) went to the bookstore, intent on finding something related to CSHL to read while attending my first Symposium. There were several choices, and I selected a biography of Barbara McClintock by Evelyn Fox Keller, “A Feeling for the Organism.” This selection proved to be quite fortuitous, as during the meeting I was given a tour of the Demerec Laboratory by Dr. Jeremy Borniger, who mentioned that he now works in the laboratory originally occupied by Dr. McClintock. I was also shown her home on campus. As the meeting ended, I reached the last chapter of the book and was astonished to learn that in her later years, Dr. McClintock had developed an intense scientific interest in the ability of Tibetan monks to use a meditation technique to significantly increase their own body temperature even in very cold environments. This remarkable phenomenon has since been measured in a quantitative fashion (Kozhevnikov et al. 2013; see also https://www.newyorker.com/science/elements/can-you-warm-yourself-with-your-mind). I was surprised and grateful to learn about this through Dr. McClintock, and this insight, plus the entirety of new information presented at CSHL, convinced me even more firmly that temperature matters (a lot!) and that there is much more that thermoregulation can teach us about brain–body interactions.
