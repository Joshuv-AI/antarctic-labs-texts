The impact of school empowerment on the mental health of left-behind children in china: exploring the mediating role of psychological empowerment
Authors: Jun Li, Lingjie Wang, Junyan Liu, Xiaoshuang Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe mental health of left-behind children in China has become a core societal concern. This study examines the impact of school empowerment on the mental health of left-behind children through the internal pathway of psychological empowerment, aiming to address the gap in existing research on the “environment-psychology” interaction mechanism.MethodsA questionnaire survey was conducted among 317 left-behind children in Yunnan Province, China. The School Empowerment Scale, Psychological Empowerment Scale, and Mental Health Scale were used for measurement, and the mediator model was tested using Model 4 of SPSS PROCESS.ResultsAmong the left-behind children sample in this study, school empowerment exerted a direct positive impact on mental health; psychological empowerment demonstrated a substantial partial mediating effect (49.015%) between school empowerment and mental health, indicating that nearly half of the total effect of school empowerment on mental health was transmitted via psychological empowerment. Additionally, both the direct path of school empowerment to mental health and the indirect path through psychological empowerment exhibited stronger explanatory power in left-behind children compared to non-left-behind children (direct path R²: 0.206>0.180; mediation model R²: 0.374>0.340). Psychological empowerment also showed a more pronounced explanatory role in left-behind children (R²: 0.226>0.214).ConclusionThis study validates the pathway of “school empowerment influencing psychological empowerment, which in turn affects mental health” among left-behind children in China. The findings not only expand the application of empowerment theory in non-Western contexts but also provide an actionable practical framework for mental health education for left-behind children in China.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40359-025-03382-9.Keywords: Empowerment theory, Left-behind children, School empowerment, Psychological empowerment, Mental health

Introduction

Since China’s reform and opening up, the large-scale migration of rural populations to urban areas has resulted in the emergence of a significant left-behind children (LBC) population in rural regions. LBC refer to minors under the age of 18 whose parents have been working away from home for more than 6 months [1]. According to the 2020 Facts and Data on the Status of Children in China, the number of LBC in China has reached 41.77 million [2]. Compared with non-LBC, LBC encounter a series of challenges in their healthy development. Besides external issues such as poor behavioral adaptation, physical health impairments, and unsatisfactory academic performance, mental health problems are particularly pronounced. Data released by the Institute of Psychology, Chinese Academy of Sciences in 2021 revealed that the detection rate of depression detection rate among LBC was 28.5%, and the excessive anxiety detection rate was 27.7%, reflecting a higher mental health risk compared to non-LBC [3]. Recent studies have shown that the overall prevalence of mental health problems among LBC in China has reached 37.4%, which is significantly higher than that of non-LBC [4]. In recent years, a series of adverse behavioral incidents involving LBC have further highlighted their mental health issues as a societal concern [5].

However, it is worth noting that there exists another subgroup of LBC who exhibit optimism and resilience and have even achieved more positive development compared to non-LBC [6]. In-depth research has found that these children often share a common characteristic: they receive more care and support in school life and possess stronger autonomy and self-efficacy [7–9]. In other words, the healthy psychological traits and behaviors of LBC are malleable. If schools provide them with a supportive environment and greater autonomy, this combined support may positively influence their mental health. Empowerment theory provides a theoretical foundation for explaining this phenomenon, emphasizing the process by which individuals acquire power and capabilities in social environments and the positive implications of this process for individual development. For example, Rogers [10] divided the empowerment process into structural empowerment and psychological empowerment, arguing that individuals facing social adversity were not merely passive recipients but could enhance their resilience through these two dimensions of empowerment to achieve positive development. Årdal et al. [11] further applied empowerment theory to the field of education, demonstrating the positive effects of school empowerment on students’ competencies, self-confidence, and other aspects, while also refining the measurement approaches for school empowerment.

Previous studies have primarily relied on ecological systems theory to explore the impact of family, school, and other environmental factors on the mental health of LBC [12, 13]. Some studies have also found that LBC lacking psychological resilience, self-esteem, and other psychological traits are more prone to mental health issues [14, 15]. While these studies provide valuable insights into environmental and psychological factors influencing the psychological development of LBC, they possess several limitations. First, they often overlook the integrated mechanisms between environmental and individual factors, failing to fully reveal how external environments influence internal psychological processes (e.g., psychological empowerment) and subsequently affect the mental health of LBC. Second, most applications of empowerment theory have been rooted in Western cultural contexts, with insufficient consideration for the different sociocultural characteristics of China [16]. In traditional Chinese culture, where family values are deeply embedded, the prolonged absence of parental relationships has a more profound impact on LBC [17]. Therefore, investigating whether empowerment measures can compensate for the lack of parental relationships holds significant implications for the psychological development of LBC in China. Additionally, previous research has primarily focused on empowering organizational employees and adult vulnerable groups, such as improving employee performance [18], enhancing patients’ life beliefs [19], and protecting the rights of vulnerable populations [20]. Few studies have applied empowerment theory to school settings, particularly in the context of China’s large population of LBC, leaving a gap in research on how empowerment can promote their positive development.

In summary, this study constructs a mediator model based on empowerment theory to explore the impact of empowerment on the mental health of LBC in China, with school empowerment as the independent variable, psychological empowerment as the mediator, and mental health as the dependent variable. This study aims to reveal how school empowerment (external factor) influences the mental health of LBC through psychological empowerment (internal factor). The findings not only extend the application of empowerment theory to the context of LBC in Chinese culture but also provide practical implications for educators to promote their mental health development.

Literature review

Empowerment theory

The concept of “empowerment” originated in the field of sociology, aiming to enhance the status and influence of individuals or groups in social, organizational, or personal contexts by increasing their control, participation, and access to resources [21, 22]. Building on prior research, Rogers [10] categorized the empowerment process into two dimensions: organizational empowerment and psychological empowerment, proposing a dual-path mechanism of empowerment theory from “structural” to “psychological”. This mechanism has been widely validated across diverse research domains. For example, in healthcare, structural empowerment (such as information-sharing mechanisms) improves patients’ health management capabilities by enhancing their sense of psychological empowerment [23]. In tourism research, local residents’ knowledge perception promotes decision-making participation through the mediating role of social empowerment [24]. Organizational management studies have confirmed that structural empowerment (resource support) enhances employee engagement via psychological empowerment (self-efficacy) (Monje-Amor et al., 2021) [25]. These findings provide a multidimensional theoretical basis from “structural” to “psychological” for constructing an empowerment research framework for left-behind children.

In the field of education, researchers have explored how empowerment enhances autonomy and decision-making power for school participants (both teachers and students), termed “school empowerment” [26, 27]. Simultaneously, empowerment theory was widely applied in psychology to explain how individuals improve their mental health by enhancing self-efficacy and a sense of control, termed psychological empowerment [27–29]. School empowerment (structural) reflects institutional practices that grant students participatory rights (e.g., decision-making roles in classroom policies), while psychological empowerment (individual) embodies internalized capabilities and autonomy [23, 27]. The former fosters the latter by creating environments where students can exercise autonomy, thereby promoting mental health [28].

It is worth noting that the concept of psychological empowerment is distinct from self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation. According to Spreitzer’s [30] research, psychological empowerment comprises four core dimensions: meaning, competence, self-determination, and impact. Meaning refers to the degree to which work goals align with an individual’s values (e.g., LBC perceive that participating in class affairs aligns with their sense of responsibility). Competence emphasizes an individual’s belief in their work-related capabilities. Self-determination refers to the autonomous control over the work process. Impact refers to an individual’s perceived ability to influence organizational or team outcomes (e.g., LBC feel their contribution to the collective through participating in campus activities). While psychological empowerment encompasses the connotations of self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation, it places greater emphasis on an individual’s multidimensional dynamic cognitive system and perception of environmental control, representing a cognitive aggregate formed through the interaction between the individual and the environment [31].

The impact of school empowerment on mental health

Previous research has not provided sufficient evidence regarding the direct impact of school empowerment on children’s mental health. However, many studies have confirmed the positive effects of supportive school environments (a core component of school empowerment) on students’ mental health. For example, Farella et al. [32] found that allowing students to engage in expressive writing, where they articulated their thoughts, could alleviate psychological stress, enhance self-awareness, and positively influence mental health. Similarly, studies focusing on the psychological pressure induced by competitive school cultures suggest that school empowerment can mitigate stress and improve students’ psychological well-being by fostering a positive atmosphere [33]. Wiedermann et al. [34] emphasized the importance of psychological education support systems, demonstrating that integrating mental health education into curricula and teacher training could enhance students’ psychological resilience.

Additionally, previous research has confirmed the positive impact of structural empowerment on individual mental health in community and corporate settings [35, 36]. Although these studies do not directly confirm the impact of school empowerment on children’s mental health, they collectively provide theoretical underpinnings for the relationship between the two.

In a similar study, Kirk et al. [37] studied a multicultural urban high school in the U.S. Midwest and found that shared decision-making and equal teacher-student relationships significantly enhanced students’ sense of empowerment. For instance, students participated in curriculum adjustments (e.g., incorporating Mexican traditional art into art classes), co-created classroom rules, and interacted with teachers informally in corridors, making them feel “treated as equals.” In their study, this empowering environment directly fostered positive self-perceptions, as evidenced by students stating, “I can freely express opinions and am no longer afraid to ask questions.” Their study further highlighted that structural interventions (e.g., student advisory committees, and cultural diversity activities) reduced depression and anxiety while enhancing psychological safety, thereby confirming that school empowerment improves mental health through environmental transformation. Therefore, this study builds on these findings to explore the impact of school empowerment on the mental health of LBC in China, addressing the gap in empirical research. This study proposes H1: School empowerment has a positive impact on the mental health of LBC.

The mediating role of psychological empowerment

Recent research has demonstrated that school empowerment, by granting students participatory decision-making rights and autonomous learning opportunities, enables them to experience respect and trust, thereby enhancing their self-efficacy and ultimately fostering their psychological empowerment [38]. This relationship has been empirically validated in organizational settings such as corporations and healthcare institutions [25, 39]. In the educational domain, while direct empirical evidence linking school empowerment to psychological empowerment among school-aged children remains scarce, several studies have confirmed the positive impact of supportive school environments on students’ intrinsic motivation. For instance, Tannert & Gröschner [40] identified a significant positive correlation between supportive school environments and students’ self-efficacy. Likewise, Liu et al. [41] found that teacher support fulfilled students’ psychological needs, activated autonomous motivation, and consequently influenced their achievement motivation. These findings are particularly pertinent, as self-efficacy and intrinsic motivation are widely recognized in the literature as core components of psychological empowerment [30, 31]. Thus, this study posits that school empowerment may similarly exert a positive influence on the psychological empowerment of LBC.

Furthermore, psychological empowerment has been shown to positively affect mental health outcomes. In workplace settings, a meta-analysis by Llorente-Alonso et al. [42] revealed significant negative correlations between psychological empowerment and adverse psychological states, such as job stress and emotional exhaustion. Parallel findings have also emerged in student mental health research. For example, Bessa et al. [43] demonstrated through experimental studies that physical education could effectively enhance students’ psychological empowerment, bolster self-confidence, and thereby improve mental health. In a study involving 580 adolescents, Poteat et al. [44] found that psychological empowerment fostered self-efficacy and hope while reducing symptoms of depression and anxiety, further corroborating its beneficial effects on student mental health.

This body of evidence suggests that school empowerment fosters a supportive environment in which students feel valued and trusted, thereby enhancing psychological empowerment. The resulting sense of autonomy and control strengthens students’ self-confidence and psychological adaptability, ultimately promoting mental well-being. For LBC specifically, school empowerment may serve a dual role: it not only activates intrinsic motivation but also provides emotional fulfillment that compensates for the absence of parental care, thereby mitigating negative emotions [45].

Consequently, this study proposes H2: Psychological empowerment mediates the relationship between school empowerment and the mental health of LBC.

By integrating these theoretical perspectives and empirical findings, this study constructs a mediation model to investigate both the direct effect of school empowerment on mental health and the intermediary role of psychological empowerment. This approach aims to elucidate the underlying mechanisms through which empowerment influences the mental health of LBC, providing both theoretical insights and empirical evidence for addressing their psychological challenges. Method

Participants and procedure

This study employed a convenience sampling method to conduct a questionnaire survey among junior high school students in Yunnan Province, China. Yunnan, located in the southwestern frontier of China, has a per capita GDP and disposable income below the national average, resulting in a high number of LBC as many young adults migrate to more economically developed provinces for employment [46]. Additionally, junior high school students undergo rapid physical and psychological development during adolescence, making them more vulnerable to mental health challenges and external environmental influences [47]. Thus, selecting junior high school students in Yunnan as the sample was highly representative of this study.

Given that the research participants were minors, written consent from the children’s guardians was obtained before administering the questionnaires. Before completing the questionnaires, the researchers read out the content of the informed consent form to the participants, which included key information such as the voluntary nature of participation, the anonymity and confidentiality of data, and the right to withdraw their participation at any time. The participants were informed that completing and submitting the questionnaires signified their consent to participate in this study To ensure the reliability and validity of the survey data, the questionnaires were administered offline. During the completion process, detailed explanations of each item to the children were provided as needed. Data collection was finalized in February 2025.

According to the official definition of LBC in China, they are minors whose parents have been working away from home for more than 6 months [1]. Therefore, in this study, the identification of LBC was based on the work status of their parents as reported in the questionnaires. A total of 1,500 questionnaires were distributed in this study. After excluding invalid responses, 1,258 valid questionnaires were ultimately obtained, yielding an effective response rate of 83.867%, including 317 questionnaires from LBC and 941 from non-LBC. The proportion of LBC (317/1258, 25.20%) was close to the overall proportion of LBC in China (22.49%) [2], which validated the rationality of the sample proportion. According to the sample size calculation criterion proposed by Ghiselli et al. [48], the number of valid samples should be more than 10 times the total number of questionnaire items. In this study, the total number of questionnaire items was 28, meaning that the required number of valid samples should exceed 280. Therefore, the sample size obtained in this study satisfied the minimum sample size requirement. Among the 317 LBC sample, there were 172 boys (54.3%) and 145 girls (45.7%); 92 students in the 7th grade (29.0%), 107 students in the 8th grade (33.8%), and 118 students in the 9th grade (37.2%).

Research instruments

School empowerment scale

This study employed the school empowerment scale revised by Årdal et al. [11], which is an adaptation of the adolescent empowerment scale originally developed by Ozer & Schotland [49]. The scale consisted of four dimensions: sociopolitical skills, motivation to influence, participatory behavior, and perceived control, with a total of 11 items (e.g., “Students have a say in what happens at school”). Responses were measured on a 5-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating a higher perceived level of school empowerment among LBC. To ensure the scale’s applicability in the Chinese context, this study rigorously adhered to the translation-back-translation procedure proposed by Brislin [50] for translating and revising the original scale, followed by a pilot test conducted on a small sample of 150 participants. The item analysis results showed that the critical ratio (C.R.) values for all items were greater than 3, the correlation coefficient of each item with the total score was greater than 0.4, and removing any item did not improve Cronbach’s α, thus satisfying the criteria for item retention in the item analysis [51] and all items of the scale were retained for the formal test. In the formal test, Cronbach’s α value of the scale was 0.895, which surpassed 0.700, indicating excellent reliability of the scale [51]. The results of the confirmatory factor analysis (CFA) revealed that RMR = 0.004, lower than the critical threshold of 0.080 [52]; GFI = 0.999, AGFI = 0.995, NFI = 0.999, and RFI = 0.996, all exceeding the recommended standard of 0.900 or above, thus indicating a good model fit [53]. Additionally, composite reliability (CR) was 0.825 (> 0.60), and the average variance extracted (AVE) was 0.544 (> 0.40), confirming the convergent validity [54].

Psychological empowerment scale

The psychological empowerment scale developed by Spreitzer [23] was employed, comprising four dimensions: meaning, competence, self-determination, and impact, with 12 items in total. The scale demonstrated good applicability for measuring psychological empowerment among Chinese adolescents [55]. The items were adapted to the school context based on the work of Han et al. [55] (e.g., “I am confident in my ability to complete my work” was revised to “I am confident in my ability to complete my studies”). Responses were measured using a 7-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 7 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating a higher level of psychological empowerment among LBC. The pilot test results showed that all items had C.R. values greater than 3, the correlation coefficient between each item and the total score was greater than 0.4, and removing any item did not enhance Cronbach’s α, thus satisfying the criteria for item retention in the item analysis [51]. Therefore, all items of the scale were retained for the formal test. In the formal test, reliability analysis revealed that the scale achieved a Cronbach’s α of 0.900, greater than 0.700, indicating good reliability [51]. The CFA results indicated that the RMR value was 0.062, below the critical threshold of 0.080 [52]. The values of GFI, NFI, IFI, and CFI were 0.871, 0.808, 0.812, and 0.810, respectively, all meeting the acceptable standard of 0.800 or above, suggesting that the model fit was acceptable [53]. Additionally, CR was 0.788 (> 0.60), and AVE was 0.492 (> 0.40), confirming convergent validity [54].

Mental health scale

This study employed a 5-item mental health scale adapted by Li et al. [56], which was modified from the psychological subscale of the WHOQOL-BREF Taiwan version developed by Lin [57]. The original psychological subscale consisted of 6 items, including a reverse-scored item. After further testing, Li et al. [56] removed the reverse-scored item (due to potential comprehension bias) and validated the applicability of the 5-item version for assessing mental health among mainland Chinese adolescents. Considering that reverse-scored items might cause comprehension difficulties among child populations, this study ultimately adopted the 5-item mental health scale adapted by Li et al. [56] to evaluate the mental health status of left-behind children (e.g., “I enjoy my life”). Given that this study explored the comprehensive impact of school and psychological empowerment on mental health (rather than specific issues like depression or anxiety), a unidimensional scale was chosen to reflect overall mental health. Responses were measured on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = strongly disagree, 5 = strongly agree), with higher scores indicating better mental health among LBC. During the pilot test, item analysis revealed that all items had C.R. values exceeding 3, with the correlation coefficient between each item and the total score greater than 0.4. Deleting any item did not improve Cronbach’s α, thus satisfying the criteria for item retention in the item analysis [51]. Consequently, all items were retained for the formal testing phase. In the formal test within this study, reliability analysis showed the scale achieved a Cronbach’s α of 0.879, exceeding the threshold of 0.700, indicating good reliability [51]. The CFA results showed that RMR = 0.027, lower than the critical threshold of 0.080 [52]; GFI = 0.922, NFI = 0.921, RFI = 0.842, and IFI = 0.927, all meeting the acceptable standard of 0.800 or above, thus indicating that the model fit was acceptable [53]. Moreover, CR = 0.880 (> 0.60), and AVE = 0.594 (> 0.40), confirming convergent validity [54].

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was conducted using SPSS 21.0 and AMOS 25.0. First, item analysis, reliability analysis, and CFA were performed to evaluate the discriminability, reliability, and validity of each scale item. Second, since the data were self-reported by LBC, Harman’s one-factor test was employed to examine common method bias. Third, t-tests and analysis of variance (ANOVA) were carried out to examine the mean differences in the three variables (school empowerment, psychological empowerment, and mental health) between LBC and non-LBC, as well as among LBC of different genders and grades. Fourth, descriptive statistics and Pearson correlation analysis were performed to evaluate the overall performance of participants on the variables and the correlations among them. Fifth, the SPSS PROCESS program was utilized to test the mediator model, specifically analyzing the direct effect of school empowerment on mental health and the mediating role of psychological empowerment. Sixth, the bias-corrected nonparametric bootstrap method (with 5,000 resamples) was applied to test the significance of the mediating effect. If the 95% confidence interval (CI) of the mediating effect path did not include 0, the mediating effect was deemed significant. Finally, two mediation models were constructed using the SPSS PROCESS program to compare the explanatory power of school empowerment and psychological empowerment on the mental health of LBC and non-LBC.

Result

Common method bias test

Harman’s one-factor test was conducted to assess common method bias. The Kaiser-Meyer-Olkin (KMO) measure was 0.886 (exceeding the threshold of 0.800), and Bartlett’s test of sphericity was significant (p < 0.001), indicating that the data were suitable for factor analysis [58]. An unrotated principal component factor analysis of all variables extracted five factors with eigenvalues greater than 1, with the first factor explaining 36.323% of the variance (below the critical threshold of 50%), suggesting the absence of severe common method bias [59].

A t-test was conducted to analyze differences in school empowerment, psychological empowerment, and mental health between LBC and non-LBC. As shown in Table 1, no significant differences were found between the two groups in school empowerment (t = -0.799, p = 0.425) or psychological empowerment (t = -0.965, p = 0.335). However, significant differences were observed in mental health (t = -2.484, p < 0.05), indicating that LBC face more severe psychological challenges compared to non-LBC.

Discussion

First, this study revealed that school empowerment has a significant positive impact on the mental health of LBC. Previous research has confirmed the role of structural empowerment in promoting mental health among community residents and corporate employees in Western cultural contexts [35, 36]. Building on these findings, this study extends the application of empowerment theory to LBC in China, highlighting the critical value of school empowerment in promoting their mental health. This result not only aligns with previous research but also broadens the scope of empowerment theory’s applicability. According to social support theory, school empowerment creates a supportive system for LBC by granting them participation and autonomy [61, 62]. Within such an environment, LBC can achieve emotional fulfillment, alleviating loneliness caused by parental absence, thereby positively influencing their mental health. Given the strong emphasis on family values in Chinese culture, parental relationships play a uniquely significance role in child development [17]. School empowerment serves as a compensatory mechanism for the lack of familial emotional support, thereby promoting the mental health of LBC. Additionally, school empowerment provides LBC with a platform to showcase their abilities and achieve a sense of accomplishment, which is essential for enhancing their mental health.

Second, this study confirmed the partial mediating role of psychological empowerment in the relationship between school empowerment and the mental health of LBC, elucidating the underlying psychological impact of this relationship. This finding aligns with previous research, such as Terkamo-Moisio et al. [38], who demonstrated that supportive school environments enhanced psychological empowerment and improved psychological well-being among Finnish students. Similarly, Bessa et al. [43] found that psychological empowerment boosted confidence and fostered positive mental health through physical education. The mediating mechanism of psychological empowerment can be explained by cognitive development theory. School empowerment grants LBC more opportunities for autonomous decision-making and learning, enabling them to form positive self-perceptions and reinforcing these perceptions through practice [63, 64]. When LBC successfully complete academic tasks or participate in school activities, they develop a more positive evaluation of their abilities, significantly enhancing their psychological empowerment. This positive self-perception encourages them to adopt proactive coping strategies when facing challenges, ultimately promoting mental health.

Notably, this study found that school empowerment and psychological empowerment exhibited stronger explanatory power for the mental health of LBC compared to non-LBC, which might be closely related to the unique growth environment of LBC. From the perspective of social support theory, LBC experience weaker family emotional support systems due to prolonged parental absence, rendering them more reliant on support from non-family members such as peers and school teachers [65, 66]. This may stem from the long-term separation from parents, which induces a sense of “emotional deprivation,” and drives LBC to seek substitute emotional support from external environments (e.g., schools). As a result, LBC are more sensitive to the care and autonomy brought by school empowerment [67]. In contrast, non-LBC have relatively more complete family support systems, which may cause the effect of school empowerment to be partially overshadowed by the family environment, resulting in weaker explanatory power.

Theoretical significance

First, this study applied empowerment theory to LBC within the Chinese cultural context, expanding the theory’s application boundaries and addressing its limitations in non-Western vulnerable populations. It provided a novel model for empowerment practices in cross-cultural settings. Second, this study enriched the theoretical literature on mental health research by discovering the dual pathways of empowerment theory on mental health—direct effects through school empowerment’s emotional fulfillment and indirect effects via psychological empowerment’s cognitive transformation. These findings deepen our understanding of how institutional support systems mitigate psychological risks in vulnerable groups and provide a robust theoretical foundation for education departments to design targeted interventions.

Practical implications

This study has two important practical implications based on its findings:

Firstly, given the positive impact of school empowerment on the mental health of LBC, schools should endeavor to optimize their management strategies. Specifically, schools can establish an effective student participation mechanism. For example, by regularly organizing class affairs discussion activities, LBC can be encouraged to fully express their ideas and participate in the formulation of class rules, thereby enhancing their sense of agency in school life. Besides, in terms of curriculum design, schools can introduce more practical and interest-based courses to provide a platform for LBC to showcase their abilities and enhance their sense of self-worth. In addition, schools should strengthen teacher training, equipping teachers with a deep understanding of the significance of school empowerment. In daily teaching, teachers should respect the individual differences of LBC, encourage independent thinking and autonomous exploration, and provide timely affirmation and support. By fostering a campus atmosphere filled with trust and care, the promoting effect of school empowerment on the mental health of LBC can be fully realized.

Secondly, considering the partial mediating role of psychological empowerment in the impact of school empowerment on the mental health among LBC, schools, and families should prioritize the psychological empowerment level of LBC. Teachers can guide students to engage in positive self-reflection and evaluation, assisting them in recognizing their strengths and potential. By setting challenging yet moderately difficult tasks in classroom interactions, teachers can enable LBC to enhance their self-efficacy as completing these tasks. Additionally, although parents working long-term away from home may not be able to interact face-to-face with their children, they should maintain regular communication through phone calls, video calls, etc. Parents should show concern for their children’s studies and lives, encourage them to solve problems independently, and help their children feel a sense of control over their own lives, thereby strengthening their psychological empowerment.

Conclusion

This study constructed a mediator model based on empowerment theory to explore the impact of school empowerment and psychological empowerment on the mental health of LBC in China. The findings revealed that school empowerment had a significant positive impact on their mental health, with psychological empowerment partially mediating this relationship. Additionally, school empowerment and psychological empowerment explained variance in mental health more strongly among LBC than among non-LBC, suggesting that LBC, due to their unique family environment, might derive greater benefits from school-based empowerment strategies. These results not only extend the application of empowerment theory to the context of LBC within Chinese culture but also offer practical implications for schools aiming to promote their mental health development.

Limitations and future research directions

This study has several limitations. First, the data were self-reported by LBC. Although Harman’s one-factor test indicated no severe common method bias, future research should incorporate multiple methods, such as interviews and observations, to reduce biases inherent in single-method studies. Second, this study only sampled 317 LBC in Yunnan Province, China. Future research could expand to multiple provinces with a larger sample size to validate the generalizability of the results. Third, although the unidimensional mental health scale effectively captured overall well-being, it lacked the granularity needed to distinguish specific mental health domains (e.g., depression, anxiety, social adaptation). Future research could employ multidimensional scales such as the strengths and difficulties questionnaire (SDQ) or general health questionnaire (GHQ-12) to explore how school and psychological empowerment affect different mental health outcomes. Finally, although the study highlighted the psychological mechanism by which school empowerment influenced mental health, this effect might vary across cultural contexts. Research suggests that individuals with collectivist values exhibit more positive development under empowerment compared to those with individualist tendencies [68, 69]. Future studies should incorporate potential moderating variables to refine school empowerment strategies and maximize their benefits for the mental health of LBC.
