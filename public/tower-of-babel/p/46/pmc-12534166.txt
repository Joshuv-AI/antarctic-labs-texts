Cardiac Arrest After Inhalation of Hazardous Gas Suspected Hydrogen Sulfide at Cucumber Pickle Factory: Two Case Reports
Authors: Thi Bich Van Ha, Duc Lich Nguyen, Tat Luat Bui, Dinh Thanh Son Le, Na Le, Ngoc Anh Tran, Hong Thai Phan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTWe report two cases of acute occupational exposure to hazardous gas, suspected to be hydrogen sulfide. The 34‐year‐old male and 48‐year‐old female patients worked as cleaners at a pickled cucumber factory. The two victims cleaned a waste tank (containing organic waste that can produce hydrogen sulfide) that had been abandoned for 2 years, with a volume of about 8 cubic meters. After 1 min of going down the tank, the female victim smelled a strong, foul odor and then became dizzy and fainted. After hearing the sound of the patient fainting, the second victim who went down to save her also fainted shortly after. The two victims were brought up; they both suffered a cardiac arrest. Co‐workers performed cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR), and return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC) was achieved 5–7 min after the collapse. They were sent to the local hospital and immediately intubated. They experienced hypotension and were treated with epinephrine and were transferred to our department. The female patient was still comatose (Glasgow Coma Scale 6 [E 1, V 1, M 4]) upon arrival at our department 3.5 h after ROSC. She was administered extravascular temperature control with a target temperature of 33°C within 24 h. We rewarmed her at a rate of 0.25°C/h to 37°C within 16 h. The two patients were then extubated after 3 days of treatment and discharged after 14 days of treatment without neurological complications. The patients were scheduled for a follow‐up examination in 3 months. The two victims described what they smelled as rotten eggs. The available clinical evidence suggests that they were exposed to hydrogen sulfide.

Introduction

Hydrogen sulfide is a highly toxic, flammable, colorless gas that is heavier than air and tends to accumulate and concentrate near the floors of poorly ventilated rooms rather than dispersing into the air. Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) possesses the characteristic smell of rotten eggs [1, 2].

It is produced naturally by decaying organic matter and is also a by‐product of many industrial processes. Hazardous levels may be found in petroleum refineries, tanneries, mines, pulp‐making factories, sulfur hot springs, carbon disulfide production, commercial fishing holds, hot asphalt fumes, and pools of sewage sludge or liquid manure. It sometimes is referred to as “pit gas” or “sewer gas” [1, 3].

Hydrogen sulfide toxicity was first described in 1700 by an Italian physician who described eye damage in sewer workers. He described the painful irritation and inflammation of “sewer gas” on the eye in sewer workers [4]. The brain is the primary target organ, with the respiratory and cardiovascular systems also affected [5, 6, 7]. Hydrogen sulfide is the second most common cause of fatal gas inhalation in the workforce; carbon monoxide exposure is the most common [8, 9].

This case report aims to raise awareness of the serious risk of hydrogen sulfide exposure in occupational settings, particularly in confined and poorly ventilated environments such as waste tanks. It highlights the critical importance of early cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) and advanced post‐resuscitation care, including targeted temperature management (TTM), in improving neurological outcomes after toxic gas‐induced cardiac arrest. The report also underscores the diagnostic challenges in cases where gas concentration measurements are unavailable, emphasizing the value of clinical clues—such as the characteristic rotten egg odor—and a thorough occupational history. By documenting two rare and successfully managed cases, this report contributes to the limited literature on hydrogen sulfide poisoning and provides valuable insights for both clinical practice and occupational safety.

Case History

Case 1

A 48‐year‐old healthy woman working as a sanitation worker was exposed to a hazardous chemical while cleaning an anaerobic waste tank in a pickled cucumber production factory. The tank had a volume of about 8 cubic meters (Figure 1). About 1 min after entering the tank, she became unconscious and comatose. She was brought up in a state of circulatory arrest. Upon removal from the tank, the patient received immediate resuscitation. Coworkers witnessed and immediately started cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ROSC achieved in 5–7 min). After the return of spontaneous circulation (ROSC), decontamination was performed prior to hospital transfer. Coworkers witnessed and immediately started cardiopulmonary resuscitation (ROSC achieved in 5–7 min). The patient was admitted to the nearest emergency department in a district medical center, intubated, treated with vasopressor (epinephrine), and transferred to our Intensive Care and Poisoning Department. In our department, she was still comatose (Glasgow Coma Scale 6 [E 1, V 1, M 4]), and mechanical ventilation was continued, high doses of epinephrine (0.3) mcg/kg/min. Both pupils were 3 mm in diameter, with good light reflex bilaterally. There were no signs of injuries, including her thorax, peritoneal cavity, retroperitoneal cavity, pelvic, spine or long bone fractures, and externally. The focused assessment using sonography in trauma (FAST) exam was done to rule out of intra‐abdominal, pleural, and pericardial hemorrhage. On admission, the patient had leukocytosis, increased liver enzymes, and increased lactate (Table 1). Chest CT scan showed a small pleural effusion and ground‐glass opacity. Brain CT scan showed normal result.

Case 2

A 34‐year‐old healthy man working as a sanitation worker. On July 16, 2024, he went down to the mentioned anaerobic tank to save the female patient above. About 1 min after entering the tank, he became unconscious and comatose. He was admitted to the nearest emergency department in a district medical center, intubated, treated with vasopressor (epinephrine), and transferred to our department. In our department, he was still comatose (Glasgow Coma Scale 8 [E 2, V 1, M 5]), and mechanical ventilation was continued, vasopressor epinephrine at (0.1) mcg/kg/min. Both pupils were 3 mm in diameter, with good light reflex bilaterally. There were no signs of injuries, including his thorax, peritoneal cavity, retroperitoneal cavity, pelvic, spine, or long bone fractures, and externally. The focused assessment using sonography in trauma (FAST) exam was done to rule out intra‐abdominal, pleural, and pericardial hemorrhage. On admission, the patient had leukocytosis, increased liver enzymes, and increased lactate (Table 1). Chest CT scan showed a small pleural effusion and ground‐glass opacity. Brain CT scan showed normal results.

Method

Case 1

After 4 h from ROSC, the patient's consciousness did not improve, and she did not follow commands. Targeted temperature management (TTM) was initiated with a target temperature of 36°C, maintained using an extravascular temperature control device (Arctic Sun Temperature Management System, Medivance, Louisville, CO, USA) in accordance with standard post‐cardiac arrest care protocols [10]. Broad‐spectrum antibiotics including coverage for aspiration pneumonia (ceftriaxone and levofloxacin) were initiated. Supportive care was provided with maintenance intravenous fluid infusions, nutritional support, electrolyte correction, and close monitoring. After 2 days of treatment on July 18, 2024, the patient regained consciousness and followed verbal orders. Therefore, we decided to end the TTM and obtained a brain MRI which showed lesions on the white matter of the frontal lobe (Figure 2). On July 19, the patient was extubated. After 2 weeks of treatment, the patient had another brain MRI which still showed damage on the white matter of the frontal lobe while a repeat chest CT demonstrated the resolution of the previously seen effusion and ground‐glass opacities. The blood tests returned to normal too. The patient was then discharged from the hospital, scheduled for a follow‐up visit after 3 months.

Case 2

This patient was also treated with antibiotics (ceftriaxone and levofloxacin) coverage for aspiration pneumonia. Supportive care was provided with maintenance intravenous fluid infusions, nutritional support, electrolyte correction, and close monitoring. After 2 h from ROSC, the patient opened his eyes naturally and responded to verbal commands. On July 18, a brain MRI showed damage on the white matter of the frontal lobe (Figure 2). On July 19, the patient was extubated. After 2 weeks of treatment, the patient had another brain MRI and still had damage on the white matter of the frontal lobe. While there was still some exudation in the lower lobe of the lung on chest CT images, the lesion was smaller than it was upon admission. Other blood tests returned to normal. The patient was then discharged from the hospital and scheduled for a follow‐up visit in 3 months.

Conclusion

We report two cases of suspected hydrogen sulfide (H2S) poisoning, diagnosed based on clinical manifestations and confirmed exposure. Both patients presented with respiratory failure, cardiovascular collapse, and imaging findings showing lower lung lobe injury and right frontal lobe abnormalities on brain MRI. Targeted temperature management was applied following cardiac arrest, and both patients were discharged without neurological sequelae. This incident highlights the importance of improving awareness and prevention of occupational H2S exposure. Governments and employers should implement exposure evaluations, eliminate or control H2S sources, apply engineering and administrative controls, and ensure proper use of personal protective equipment [11, 12].

Discussion

Hydrogen sulfide (H2S) exerts toxicity by inhibiting mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase, arresting aerobic metabolism and ATP synthesis—similar to cyanide—leading to lactic acidosis, oxidative stress, and cell death. It also disrupts calcium homeostasis, depletes glutathione, increases reactive oxygen species (ROS), and induces DNA damage, persulfidation, and ion channel dysregulation. Inhalation causes rapid absorption with acute symptoms such as respiratory failure, coma, or death [3, 13]. H2S is commonly produced by anaerobic decomposition of organic matter [14, 15]. Diagnosis relies on exposure history, characteristic odor, and clinical signs, as no specific test is widely available. Whole blood sulfide and thiosulfate levels may indicate exposure but require prompt sampling and are often inaccessible in routine clinical settings [3, 8, 13, 16].

Two workers experienced suspected hydrogen sulfide (H2S) poisoning with rapid “knockdown” symptoms after cleaning a decomposing cucumber waste tank at a pickle factory, where organic decay likely generated toxic H2S gas, confirmed by clinical manifestations and the characteristic rotten egg odor. The diagnosis was further supported by the exclusion of other common toxic agents such as carbon monoxide, cyanide, or organophosphates, which were not consistent with the clinical picture.

Inhalation damage from H2S mainly causes damage to the central nervous system and lungs, as shown in some reports [17, 18, 19], this is consistent with the clinical situation in our two patients. Brain damage in H2S poisoning can be found in the basal ganglia, thalamus, cortex, frontal lobe, and brain stem [17, 20]. In our report, brain lesions in both patients were in the frontal lobe on brain MRI.

The presence of persistent right frontal lobe hyperintensities on MRI in both patients with known acute H2S exposure and cardiac arrest supports the diagnosis of toxic/metabolic encephalopathy secondary to hydrogen sulfide poisoning. While the findings are nonspecific, their consistency across both patients, location, and timing strongly suggests a common toxic mechanism rather than incidental or degenerative changes. These findings provide radiological support for our clinical diagnosis.

Targeted temperature management (TTM) has been demonstrated in several trials and case reports to enhance neurological function and survival [21, 22, 23]. Surface cooling systems, intravascular cooling systems, and conventional cooling techniques are the three primary types of cooling procedures [24]. One of our two patients remained comatose and unresponsive to verbal commands 4 h after ROSC. TTM was initiated with a target core temperature of 36°C, which was maintained for 24 h before gradual rewarming [10].

In our study, although two patients were in a coma, one patient had circulatory arrest immediately after poisoning. Afterward, both patients were conscious; one patient had mild demented when discharged from the hospital but was still able to perform daily activities with mRS (modified Rankin scale): 1 point, CPC [25] (cerebral performance category): 1 point. We will continue to monitor the two patients in the coming time (scores on the modified Rankin scale range from 0 to 6, with 0 representing no symptoms, 1 no clinically significant disability, 2 slight disability, 3 moderate disability, 4 moderately severe disability, 5 severe disability, and 6 death) [26].

Several reports support the clinical presentation of hydrogen sulfide (H2S) poisoning. Genjiafu et al. described a similar case in which a worker became dizzy and lost consciousness within 1 min of entering a factory's waste treatment tank [17]. While Munasinghe reported two fatal cases of H2S poisoning confirmed by postmortem detection of thiosulfate—a known biomarker of exposure—our diagnosis relied solely on the exposure context and clinical manifestations due to the lack of toxicological testing [27]. Additionally, Tvedt et al. documented persistent neurological and neuropsychological deficits, including memory loss, motor impairment, and severe dementia, in five of six patients who had been unconscious for 5–20 min following H2S exposure [28].

Although the two victims were discharged without any clinical sequelae, we will continue to schedule follow‐up visits. However, we did not collect data on H2S measurements in blood and in the environment. We will try to improve these issues in the future.
