(In) visibility of health and illness: Instagram as an unregulated public health platform
Authors: Rachael Kent
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper examines the changing terrain of accessing health and illness guidance and information through the lens of social media, specifically It argues how Instagram, has increasingly become an unregulated ­public health platform in today’s digital society. Drawing on extensive empirical interview data from two research projects before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, which explored how users showcased and performed health and illness on Instagram, this paper demonstrates how Instagram has became an important forum from which to perform proactive health practices, as well as to legitimate ill health through making healthy behaviours visible, as well as invisible illnesses like disease, COVID-19, and mental health conditions. Over time and through continuous sharing of this content may contribute to increased understanding or even a de-stigmatisation of such illnesses or chronic conditions. With the social media market currently valued at 49.9 billion, and influencer spending around 4.9 billion, the value, reach and direct impact of this ‘attention economy’ on public health should not be underestimated. Instagram serves as a forum for these practices and helps legitimize ill health by making both healthy behaviours and ­invisible illnesses visible. This visibility can contribute to increased understanding and de-stigmatization of chronic conditions and mental health issues. The paper argues that Instagram has evolved into an informal, unregulated public health platform.Keywords: Digital health, visibility, Instagram, public health, influencers, invisible illness

Introduction

The digital society of today presents a cornucopia of ways for individuals to monitor and track, as well as perform and make visible health-related behaviours, in attempt of improving health or for communicating about ill health or invisible illnesses like mental health issues or COVID-19. Social media platforms have become important sites for individuals to share experiences, seek information, and make health decisions in conjunction with traditional healthcare guidance and support. 4.9 billion people currently use social media (Wong and Bottorff 2023) with some of the most popular social media platforms being Instagram and TikTok with over 2 billion monthly users and 1.67 billion monthly users respectively (Statistica 2023). The focus of this paper is Instagram, which serves as a forum for these practices and which this paper argues helps legitimize ill health by making both healthy behaviours and invisible illnesses visible online. This visibility can contribute to increased understanding and de-stigmatization of invisible illness like mental health issues. However, the pressure to perform healthy behaviours on and offline can also lead to stress and guilt.

Through these practices this paper argues that Instagram has evolved into an informal, unregulated public health platform.

Background literature

Research from the medical humanities has long identified the challenges of the subjective experience of illness, chronic pain, and mental health issues like depression or anxiety (Hurwitz 2017; Roach 2017). This is often at odds with western medicines attempts to often universalise the experiences of ill health or disease (Lupton 2012; Brandt and Rozin 1997). Even when the feeling or impact of pain or invisible symptoms is uncapturable, even though words, medicine attempts to capture and quantify it. For example, language used to define invisible illnesses like depression often sits at the threshold of description and subjectivity, making further importance to the sharing of patient’s experiential knowledge (Caron-Flinterman, Broerse, and Bunders 2004), on social media in particular. Through influencers and everyday users of Instagram, sharing personal experiences of how to achieve good health, or how to manage invisible illness, knowledge production takes place at the level of emotionality (Boler and Davis, 2018) rather than scientific rationality or clinical credentials. Under platform capitalism, Instagram has now algorithmically weaponized the wellness industry, as a key site for circulating and making ‘viral’ health or dietary misinformation, myths and conspiracy theories (Walsh and Baker 2020: 577). Often achieved through high-profiled ‘influencers’ (Freberg et al. 2010: 1), whose authenticity is achieved largely through follower count and performed relatability (Baker and Walsh 2018), who ‘patent new lifestyles’ (Rose 2007) under the attentive gaze of millions of followers (Abidin 2018; Lawrence 2022). This has contribed to a value shift away from clinically legitimate guidance towards lay, ‘influencer’ and ‘guru’ health knowledge conceived as a key source of the ‘informationalisation’ of everyday life (Baker and Rojek 2020; Ajana 2023). With the social media market currently valued at 49.9 billion, and influencer spending around 4.9 billion (Wong and Bottorff, 2023), the value, reach and direct impact of this ‘attention economy’ (Simon 1971; Abidin 2018) on public health should not be underestimated. This paper argues how social media such as Instagram, can now be identified as form of unregulated public health platform for the everyday user to follow health guidance or share their own health or illness experiences.

Health influencers conform to the etiquettes and aesthetic trends which work most effectively to capture attention on different social media platforms (Abidin 2018; Baker and Walsh 2018). This highly adaptive, flexible and curated influencer ‘attention economy’ (Simon, 1971), with an increasing wealth of ‘click-bait’ style content (Abidin 2018), means that capturing and sustaining attention becomes an increasingly valuable commodity. In 2023, this contributed to half of millennials now trusting product recommendations from social media influencers (Wong and Bottorff 2023). Interestingly, and most alarmingly in relation to the focus of this paper, research has now identified how 90% of weight management advice shared by ‘influencers’ is inaccurate or misleading (Sabbagh et al. 2020) and 67–92% of teens report using social media for health information (fitness, sexual health and nutrition), many of which are not able to identify reliable health information (Plaisime et al. 2020). From my own research, and it is arguable from these statistics, how much the public now rely on social media for health guidance and it is very likely this is not isolated to adolescents, and is increasing and widespread across age groups. Social media users, turn to sites like Instagram over accessing health information through clinically reputable ­services health services like the WHO (World Health Organisation) or government services in the UK such as National Health Service (NHS, NHS Digital, 111,), Public Health England (PHE) and Department of Health (DOH) (Plaisime et al. 2020). With the increasing value of social media content, both in terms of ideological and commercial assets, and for shaping public health, influencers engage the attention of their audiences within the economic framework of platform capitalism (Srnicek 2016). The focus of this paper is Instagram content, which from the empirical data collected included gym selfies, ‘food porn’, and broader content which demonstrates a life-stylisation of health (Kent 2018) signalling self-optimisation, productivity and moral worth (Kent 2020b; Cederstrom and Spicer 2015).

COVID-19 was first identified in December 2019, and quickly grew into a global pandemic affecting at least 223 countries, with 770,563,467 confirmed cases, sadly including 6,957,216 deaths (World Health Organisation, 13/09/23). Governments around the world attempted to manage the spread through a variety of digital health and surveillance tools (for example, China’s ‘Health Code’). In the UK, the government attempted to manage the spread of COVID-19 through NHS public health initiatives such as ‘Test and Trace’ (testing for COVID-19 through home testing or walk-in sites), and the NHS COVID-19 App (NHS, 2021) which provided users with contact tracing services, local area alerts, and geo-tagging venue check-ins. The COVID-19 pandemic not only placed pressures on the general public to gain a level of expertise of medical health management and knowledge, but also to responsibilise their citizenship in ways we have never yet seen in global public health (Kent 2023). This paper examines the changing terrain of accessing health and illness guidance and information, with the increasingly important role of the visibility of these practices on social media. The medical legitimacy and accuracy of what is shared is not always a priority or concern for both ‘health influencers’ and everyday users, leading to the potential for harms at a large scale to mental or physical public health. On the other hand, this may contribute to a wider public discourse of increased understanding or de-stigmatisation of invisible illnesses.

Under COVID-19, being able to self-diagnose and become lay experts of viral illness and self-manage symptoms for personal safety became increasingly important for citizens across the world (Kent 2023). COVID-19 then, on a global scale intensified the importance of not only becoming a lay expert of viral illness (Kent 2023), but of also being able to self-monitor and self-diagnose one’s own symptoms, which were often invisible or unseen, which could save yours or your loved one’s lives. Other widely available digital tools which similarly contributed to these practices, as well as to the commercialisation of public health, incorporate interactive chatbots and symptom checkers, enabling users to navigate health-related queries quickly to determine risk (Parviainen and Rantala 2022). This everyday traversing of risk management, health self-awareness and self-diagnosis of sometimes invisible symptoms is a key contributing factor to the global mental health crisis we see today, exacerbated by COVID-19 (WHO, 2023). This increasingly shifted the public’s perception and acceptance of social media (in particular, Instagram) as a key and valuable source of health-related guidance and information. Advocated by governments internationally, COVID-19 placed pressure on individuals to protect themselves (and those with whom they came in close contact with), which, particularly in the early phases of the pandemic when little was known about COVID-19 and how it was transmitted (Ajana et al. 2023b), and with the additional rise of conspiracy theories circulating both on and offline, (Harambam, Grusauskaite, and de Wildt 2022), encouraged the general public increasingly to turn to social media for health guidance and information (Kent 2023; Chaudhary et al. 2021). This Further shifted the public’s perception and acceptance of social media (in particular, Instagram) as a key source of health-related guidance and information, regardless of the clinical legitimacy of the information available (Baker and Rojek 2020; Ajana 2023). Through these practices, this paper argues how social media, and in particular Instagram has evolved into an informal and unregulated public health platform. This paper will now discuss the methodology in more detail before moving on to analysis and discussion of the key research findings related to how health and illness is made visible on Instagram.

Methodology

This paper draws on extensive empirical interview data from two research projects (A: LRS-15/16-2156 and B: MRA-19/20-18193) before and during the COVID-19 pandemic, which explored users’ own experiences of sharing health related content on Instagram. Project A (LRS-15/16-2156) undertook empirical ethnographic research over a nine-month period with 14 participants (7 m/7w, between 26-49 years) who self-selected through a call for participants on Facebook and Instagram, 26 and 49 years of age who regularly (daily/weekly) share health and fitness-related content on Facebook and/or Instagram. These participants ranged from the everyday layperson, those who were dieting or training for marathons, to those dealing with illness or disease. The content shared came in the form of self-tracking data from applications (for example Nike + or Strava) and devices (for example, Fitbit or Garmin Watch), gym or fitness selfies, or more general ‘healthy’ self-representations such as food photography. The anonymised data collected included semi-structured interviews, reflexive diary entries, screenshots of shared content, and textual and thematic analysis. For the purpose and focus of this paper only transcripts of verbal (interviews) and written (reflexive diaries) data reflections are included.

Project B (MRA-19/20-18193) undertook one-off interviews with 14 participants (9 m/5 w, aged 21-50 years), who self-selected through a call for participants on Facebook and Instagram. No specification of selection criteria was given to enable a broad spectrum of respondents, as interview questions were focused broadly on how any digital technology influenced their mental and physical health, and how their everyday digital habits had changed since COVID-19. All data presented in this paper is anonymised.

Results

This paper presents the empirical data of two research projects (LRS-15/16-2156 and MRA19/20-18193) transcripts of verbal (interviews) and written (reflexive diaries) data drawing on reflections of everyday users of Instagram who follow health influencers, and who conform to their similar aesthetic logics of self-representation. In turn, this analysis identified how these practices ensure Instagram can now be seen as a new unregulated public health platform, and in turn may contribute to the increasing de-stigmatisation of invisible illness as well as, encouraging engagement with unqualified influencers following health-related misinformation or inaccurate guidance. This is achieved through three key technological practices and socio-political shifts within the dynamic relationship between health influencers and everyday users of Instagram; ‘Visibility and Legitimisation’, the ‘Influence of COVID-19′, and broader ‘Neoliberal Pressures’. Firstly, the role of ‘Visibility and Legitimisation’ is key as Instagram serves as a forum for making health behaviours visible and legitimizing invisible illnesses or injury. Continuous sharing can contribute to increased understanding and de-stigmatization of conditions such as mental health issues and chronic illnesses Secondly, the ‘Influence of COVID-19′ meant the pandemic intensified the use of social media for health-related information, with users sharing health management practices and experiencing increased intimacy and connection through visible health behaviours. Lastly, the ‘Neoliberal Pressures’ participants felt meant they prioritised management of their own health autonomously through viewing and sharing content on Instagram, often conforming to the ideals of ‘healthism’ (Crawford 1980) and ‘toxic productivity’ (Kent 2020a), which were exacerbated during the pandemic. The following sections will now illustrate and analyse these findings in greater detail.

Visibility and legitimisation

Firstly, the concepts of ‘visibility and legitimization’ are crucial as Instagram acts as a platform to make health behaviours visible and to legitimize invisible illnesses. Regular sharing can enhance understanding and reduce the stigma associated with conditions like mental health issues and chronic conditions. Participants perceived health often in terms of ­polarising frames, such as ‘success’ (being ‘healthier’) or failure (being ‘unhealthy’ or ill), which were attributed to a personal belief in their own success or failure to optimise, follow, and share their ‘health’ behaviours online. Inability to perform or reach specific self-proclaimed optimal health goals may reverse health improvement and cause health deterioration.

“I think there’s a different way of conceptualising illness when it’s physical, like a hernia rather than viral stuff. Legitimating illness and injury which is unseen. Yeah, you haven’t got a bandage, you haven’t got stitches. It’s all internal, isn’t it” (Tim, 34, M).

In this regard, many of the participants when unwell pushed themselves to undertake what they considered ‘healthy’ physically tiresome activities, which frequently made them feel more ill, but which mentally appeased them once shared on Instagram, in turn, it made them feel ‘productive’ and able to maintain visibility of their ‘healthy’ lifestyle. Ironically, the contradiction was that the attempt to ‘push’ oneself before physically capable, frequently delayed recovery and was at times to the detriment of their physical health. As Sontag (1978: 3) argues “Illness is the night-side of life, a more onerous citizenship. Everyone who is born holds dual citizenship, in the kingdom of the well and in the kingdom of the sick. Although we all prefer to use only the good passport, sooner or later each of us is obliged, at least for a spell, to identify ourselves as citizens of that other place”. In this regard, doing exercise mitigates mental anxiety, even if it is bad for their body and perhaps means that anxiety related to being a citizen of the ill (Sontag 1978) is reduced or alleviated through sharing their visible bodily activity online. A tension, therefore, lay between being ‘legitimately’ inactive due to rest and recovery, and an inherent and embodied ambition to fulfill the objectives of ‘health optimisation’, whereby further self-improvement becomes the driving goal for these participants. In this way, individuals strive to be ‘healthy’ regardless of any negative impacts or damage upon their physical bodies. Interestingly, for Lara, listening to her body in pain was not an excuse not to race and such a decision had to come from a medic with an official authority to ‘sign off’ on her injury, which legitimated not competing in a race.

“I think because I signed up for this race, I think there was a pressure to maintain, because I was just about up to 10km when the doctor said I shouldn’t run. I was like ‘I need to maintain this level because I’ve got this race’ but then it was also like ‘now I’ve got an excuse not to do it’” (Lara, Final Interview, 28, F).

“When I did that yoga session when I was really ill it made me feel really awful afterwards. I nearly threw up, I nearly passed out, but mentally I also felt good for it and I shared it” (Tim, Final Interview, 34, M).

Tim expanded on this perspective in his final interview, when he asserted that even though he was extremely unwell he had to do his yoga practice, quite simply to maintain continual improvement. Once support is received, it contributes to him feeling mentally and physically ‘well’ or ‘better’. He explains that the support and feedback from others online, contributed to the participants’ perceptions of their experiences, especially when they were unwell. Fascinatingly, perceived attempts towards healthy behaviours added to feelings of happiness and in turn offline experience of personal enjoyment and health satisfaction. If perceptions of the self are interpreted through the body (Merleau-Ponty 1965) then social media feedback and visibility could be argued as contributing to our mode of existence. If, sense of self and identity is experienced through the body’s mode of existence (Merleau-Ponty 1965), it can be argued that the visibility of our body through the lens of a viewing community on social media contributes to the visibility of our existence and bodily sense. Explaining why this online support received through messages from friends on social media genuinely makes Tim feel happier and (mentally) healthier despite his illness. Perhaps this also reduces or removes the potential for shame associated with a lack of proactivity or being seen as not productive.

For these participants, unseen illness being publicly shared makes them feel emotionally and mentally healthier, with a stronger sense of identify and self. There is, however, a dangerous tipping point. For Tim, the pressure to push yourself is important to maintain progression, even if this is detrimental towards physical ‘health’, because mentally it made him feel well and ‘healthy, and regardless of the fact that it physically made him feel worse over time. In the quote above, Tim highlights frustrations related to being unable to exercise or eat ‘healthily’. When it came to invisible illness or internal injuries, the participants struggled to rest and let their bodies recover as they felt they would be ‘losing’ the physical fitness improvements they had perceivably ‘gained’, as well as admiration of productivity from their Instagram community. Perhaps also the visibility of their Instagram communities seeing their bodies contributed to their sense of self and without this they felt increasingly isolated and even to some degree as Jacob articulates, become “an invisible statistic” (32, M).

“The people live blogging their coronavirus journeys as well, I struggle with it because they all sound horrible, but I know about the attention economy and algorithms, and these are only a very small minority of people who are having a very bad time with it and I do not disbelieve them at all. And like, this is really important and horrible, but like I'm trying to apply my criticalities of research and obviously everyone’s engaged with that because it sounds horrific. And everyone’s looking at it, which is why you’re seeing it more, not because it’s the common experience that most people are having. But I struggle with that a bit, because it’s just horror stories, like never ending” (Martha, F, 29).

“Yeah, definitely. I feel guilty. Like I've been smoking. Hahaha. I smoke more than I've ever smoked before, haha. Like I know it’s stupid, so there’s a strong moral element to it, especially influencers and celebrities and all of the people that I follow on social media are kind of jumping in the bandwagon and being like “stay at home!”. So that’s fine, but I feel like the moralistic is coming from everywhere, and I think like people who are not qualified” (Martha, F, 29).

The visibility of health and illness on social media during the pandemic also led to peer policing and stigma. Participants spoke of their networks calling out their behaviours if their social media content signaled their visible frequency outside of their home. Similarly, content showcasing ‘laziness’ or poor health and lifestyle management received hyper-survillant social backlash. Stigma and judgment became associated with not taking preventative health measures to manage risk. At one stage, some participants concealed diagnoses or symptoms for fear of judgment of personal irresponsibility for contracting the disease. In these instances, judgments which surround illness are even more challenging to overcome through only the visual representations of social media, as the metaphor of illnesses and disease stands tall to silence and shame patients. However, this visibility can also foster understanding and de-stigmatization of invisible illnesses, and in time this visibility helps legitimate ill health and make invisible illnesses like mental health and COVID-19 visible.

Influence of COVID-19

The ubiquitous use of social media for acquiring health-related information has ­demonstrated a marked increase in patient involvement in their health decisions (Zhao and Zhang 2017). The daily use of social media for sociality and communication changed significantly during the COVID-19 pandemic, particularly during the early phases of lockdowns and restrictions. Interestingly, sharing etiquettes on social media, in particular Instagram reflected a discourse of increased intimacy and bringing one’s network into the home and daily isolation practices (Kent 2020a) as well as creating connection through sharing health-related behaviours (for example, frequent handwashing, exercising, or eating nutritious meals). ‘Fear of missing out’ (FOMO) became replaced by a ‘fear of not being [or perceived as] productive’, with a related desire to share ‘personal growth’ and health management content during these periods of isolation on social media. Elsewhere I have referred to this as a shift towards ‘toxic productivity’ (Kent 2020a, 2023), doing as much as possible during lockdown (baking, house renovating, exercising, learning new hobbies/skills, etc), and sharing these behaviours to signal productivity in one’s own life, to perform being as healthy as possible during the pandemic for others online. Here Mia and Alice discuss the powerful influence of seeing others doing ‘healthy’ behaviours or heightened productivity during the early phases of lockdown which made them consider they should be more active from inside their homes.

“I suppose the intimacy in that is that it allows, there’s vulnerabilities for everyone right now, so it allows for… I suppose, yeah, a deeper connection in being able to talk more openly with people. And how it’s impacting people’s lives in terms of their relationships, their families, their work, their own emotional response, so you can kind of… unlike usually where you maybe would check in with someone about how works going, how their family is, how they are, but this is with the additional layer of the pandemic and how those topics are relating to this situation specifically” (Mia, 26, F).

“People have been like, learn a new language. I saw someone the other post the other day saying, “if you don’t come out of this with a new skill you didn’t lack time, you lacked discipline”. And it’s some kind of yoga teacher saying that. People are in crisis and trauma. So, if you get out of bed each day, that’s enough” (Alice, 41, F).

Viewing other users and influencers activities on social media during lockdown, was simultaneously motivating for others to follow suit in their behaviours and created an intimacy of connection through sharing health and lifestyle online. These intimacies were enabled not only because content was often situated inside one’s home (exercise routines, for example), but also there was a shift towards sharing more mental health-related and what participants perceived as ‘vulnerable content’. The visibility of bodies, of self-care, of new routines of health or illness management both physically and mentally enabled the participants to feel comfortable checking in with their wider network, people they rarely spoke to in daily life (old school friends, or colleagues, for example) on a closer more ­emotionally intimate level. New connections were created due to the universalisation of the traumatic experience of responding to living through COVID-19.

“You know, if you’re not sharing stuff on social media, you can start to feel a bit isolated because you’re not so connected, especially when you can’t connect” (Jacob, 26, M).

Over time, however, stigma became associated with inabilities to perform or reach ­specific health management or productivity goals during lockdowns, considered to reverse health improvement and even cause health deterioration. This caused a lot of stress for these individuals who were committed to the ‘toxic productivity’ of their health or illness management. Here we started to see the conflation of health moralisation and stigma with the contraction of COVID-19 as some kind of moral failing or irresponsibility which ­interestingly became a form of content and performative judgement through Instagram posting. This discourse became rife during the early stages of the pandemic and reflected through social media sharing of individual values of what users thought their fellow community or citizens ‘should’ be doing or not be doing. In particular, the circulation of the “stay at home” stickers and hashtags circulating on Instagram was a key way to promote this discourse;

“I haven’t ever posted anything about how everyone else should stay home. Whereas that’s a really prolific thing that people do on social media at the moment. So, I haven’t really been an advocate, but I’ve been a participant. (…) I think it’s one of those things where power is kind of, comes through subordination, so I think that in that way, yes, even as an individual, telling everyone else that I think everyone else should stay at home, my actions do directly contribute to this. Umm… but as a person, I’m not really someone that shouts about things. So, I do a very quiet subordination” (Eloise, 31, F).

“Yeah, definitely. I feel guilty. Like I've been smoking. Hahaha. I smoke more than I've ever smoked before. Like I know it’s stupid, so there’s a strong moral element to it, especially influencers and celebrities and all of the people that I follow on social media are kind of jumping in the bandwagon and being like “stay at home!”. So that’s fine, but I feel like the moralistic is coming from everywhere, and I think like people who are not qualified” (Martha, F, 29).

During the pandemic this evolved into peer policing in many circles; participants spoke of their networks calling out their behaviours online in the form of comments, individual posts, or direct messages if their social media content signaled their visible frequency outside of their homes. Similarly, too much content deemed to showcase ‘laziness’ or poor health and lifestyle management (drinking alcohol or eating unhealthy foods regularly) received similar hyper-survillant social backlash on Instagram. In time, and like many pandemics before, stigma and judgement became associated with not taking preventative health measures to manage risk (healthy foods, exercise and isolated living). At one stage, some participants concealed diagnoses or symptoms for fear of judgement of personal irresponsibility for contracting the disease. Like many epidemics before it, disease has long been viewed as “a great mystery, a visitation, a curse, a judgement” (Meyers 1985: 2). As Sontag (1989: 113) has argued “both cancer and HIV/Aids are represented as punishments for living unhealthy lives, for taking health risks, for excess of diet and lifestyle, weaknesses of will, self-indulgence and addiction”. Therefore, the judgements which surround illness are even more challenging to overcome through only the visual representations of social media.

“She’s been hospitalised with it [COVID-19], and not telling people that she had it. She’s quite a guilt-based person, so it’s in her nature to not, especially if it’s stigmatised. I know other people who just wanted to keep it to themselves, or tell a few people, or not tell everyone. That’s wrong, that’s fundamentally incorrect to have that attitude, because if we’re talking about collective responsibility, there’s people here with compromised immune systems and it’s like, I see it as very important and responsible to inform the people you cohabit with” (Mia, 26, F)

“I would say a slight annoyance of people not taking it seriously and not making the effort to protect themselves and therefore others in this” (Jacob, 26, M).

“Yeah, and the fact some of them aren’t fitted. Someone did a funny post about taking off their mask to sneeze. People aren’t using them properly, reusing them. And actually, if you’re going to take your mask off you should dispose of it, double bag it. No ones doing that” (Martha, 29, F)

It is arguable how during any pandemic health and illness become mutually reinforcing, where wellness or health becomes the opposite of illness or chronic incapacity, and how those dichotomies become normalised, and lived through for many years to come, and as we still see today in post-peak COVID times. Participants identified that the more they researched and shared healthy practices on social media, the more they can improve ­themselves and ‘become healthier’ to avoid contracting COVID-19. There was widespread commitment to demonstrating what you were doing to prevent ill health and concealing ‘unhealthy behaviours’ during lockdowns (eating junk food, drinking alcohol, smoking, inconsistent hand washing to name just a few examples), whilst also navigating potential judgement and backlash from users own communities online. A key complaint from my participants was that people sharing content deemed to be out of the house too much or for too long or were seen to be socialising as not according to the rules and restrictions, in turn this attached judgement to their behaviours through this visibility on Instagram. They were aware of the silent voyeurism, which extended in some cases to verbal feedback publicly on posts or through private direct messages. As Cederstrom and Spicer (2015) argue, guilt plays an important role in commanding the self to be healthy. Individuals who do not adopt these technologies, therefore, may be ‘constructed as failing to achieve this ideal and as consequently at fault for becoming ill or contracting a disease’ (Lupton 2012: 240), promoting continual self-improvement of health as the core ambition of the human being and human body when continuously seeing this content on Instagram. In turn, this can lead to an increase in pervasive feelings of guilt and judgement during the lockdown phase.

“So, if I read a really alarmist article, well not alarmist because it’s obviously real, (…) the stuff that’s really intense reports of young people having coronavirus. And another thing that’s happened is a lot of people are blogging their coronavirus journey. Which is really horrible. So sometimes if I've just read that I'll just be like, ok I'm not going to go to the shop, or maybe there was something I was thinking about doing and then I wouldn’t do that (…) Something I'm trying to get information on is masks because there’s so much conflicting information about masks” (Martha, 29, F).

The visibility of health and illness showcased on social media speaks to what is prescribed as desirable and undesirable of the materiality of the body, and what counts as a ‘good healthy lifestyle’ today. The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated our reliance on social media for health guidance and information, which become increasingly entwined with making visible aesthetically ‘Insta-Worthy’ lifestyles, with COVID-19 restrictions moving citizens of the pandemic from ‘FOMO’ (fear of missing out) to ‘toxic productivity’ (the fear of not being perceived as productive). Not only inside the home with the increasing digital ­mediatisation and blurring of labour and leisure in the pandemic, but also productive health management and risk reduction individually, as well as collectively for public health safety. ‘Toxic productivity’ was promoted and truly embodied, and today we still see these ideologies of hyper productive and proactive lifestyles being associated with good health and a moral life online today in 2024. New intimacies and new connections were also enabled during the pandemic through the visibility of health and illness, and through the overt or covert, explicit or implied risk or precaution of personal behaviours or of judgements towards others. The influence of COVID-19′ meant the pandemic intensified the use of social media for accessing health-related information, with users sharing health management practices and experiencing increased intimacy and connection through visible health behaviours.

Neoliberal pressure

‘Neoliberal pressure’ plays a key role in how participants prioritised management of their own health autonomously through viewing and sharing content on Instagram, often ­conforming to the ideals of ‘healthism’ (Crawford 1980) and ‘toxic productivity’ (Kent 2020a). Participants often reflected how COVID-19 had intensified the neoliberal pressure to become increasing autonomous managers of their own healthcare. This focus on ‘healthism’ (Crawford, 1980) the commitment to prioritise health to achieve lifestyle and health transformation existed before COVID-19 but, influencer culture and social media depictions have accelerated the relationship between good health being representative of a full, productive and rich life (Abidin 2018; Ajana 2023; Baker and Walsh 2018), and ill health as some kind of personal, unproductive or immoral failing. The role of risk in managing health post-COVID-19 has become exacerbated, and through social media sharing, influencers, whether qualified or not, increasingly prescribe to users what they should or should not be doing with regard to health or exercise in their everyday lives, especially when it comes to diet, fitness, invisible illnesses, inactivity or injury. For example, the dictatory ‘go for a walk’ trend we see advocated across Instagram is rife amongst all types of influencers, not just health or fitness focused ones. These influencers are conforming to the neoliberal, self-responsibalising, and Thatcherite ideologies of self-management, toxic wellness and ­productivity, and ultimately regardless of individual capacities, if you’re not proactive you are ‘lazy’. A stigma widely associated with and seen in the ‘fat shaming’ epidemic, if you are ‘fat’, it’s because you are ‘lazy’ rather than taking into consideration socio-economic or personal health or capacity factors (Lupton 2012). Rigakos and Law (2009: 80) argue that risk is a key ideology under neoliberalism and may be ever coming, if it does not already exist it may happen sometime in the future an ‘unrealized potentiality’. Therefore, risk is always moral as it incorporates the notion of prevention and harm (Beck 1992; Rigakos and Law 2009). Risk and uncertainty are often used interchangeably (Lupton 2012), especially in relation to health and illness. This paper argues, due to the current moralisation of health that existed pre-covid and accelerated during and post Covid, for those who regularly share health-related behaviours, when you have an illness or injury, communicating that effectively on social media becomes increasingly important to legitimate your rest or inactivity to avoid stigma or feelings of personal shame or perceptions of heightened risk to one’s health.

“I posted a vid of my latest mountain adventures! (,…) I'd been super rough for a few days and was feeling a little low and sorry for myself: (. I was a bit surprised at how good the messages and calls because of the post made me feel. Really lifted me mentally which in turn helped me to start feeling better physically too:)… You would probably say this was typical of me…. Practically dying with flu and a chest infection but still hiking off through snow covered woods, to a lake, to do yoga” (Tim, Diary Entry, 34, M).

As a concept discussed earlier, legitimating inactivity demonstrates intensified neoliberal and self-regulatory practices in the management of the self and conformance to ‘toxic productivity’. This was prevalent even in the cases when the participants’ poor physical health prevented them from prioritising exercise. During the research period, all the ­participants acknowledged that being perceived as ‘healthy’ and ‘active’ by their social media communities was incredibly important to their own sense of self and health identity, as a vehicle of expression, and an instrument of impression formation and management (Featherstone, 2010). Being accountable for one’s condition or illness ‘against better ­knowledge or actions’ under neoliberal advocation of personal healthcare management (Lorig and Holman 2003), means that one should be responsible for their condition (Prainsack and Buyx 2012). Therefore, if morality is a process of ethical self-stylisation (Foucault 1986), and social media have become the forum for demonstrating health self-stylisation through the moralisation of health (Kent 2023), whereby good health becomes linked with being a good person, the challenge of articulating ill health becomes ever more prioritised to effectively communicate online. With that, comes pressures of the challenges of making visible the invisible physical or mental illnesses and conditions. These aesthetic logics (Abidin 2018) are also widely adopted by everyday users who do not necessarily commercially profit from content sharing on Instagram but subscribe and conform to the visual register (Elias, Gill, and Scharff 2017), of influencer culture of what is and what is not ‘insta-worthy’ (Kent 2018, 2023) or shareable content to legitimate and justify good or poor personal health. In turn, Instagram with billions of users, have become key sites for sharing health experiences, seeking information, and used to make health decisions from.

“It’s just when I see what other people are doing and it makes me question if I should do more” (Penny, 37, F).

“What this seemed to provide, whether they’re true or not, was quite a lot of fact. There’s so much unknown in this situation that to read this persons take on it, it just felt sort of quite solid, and it felt like it made sense because there were facts you could build on. But reading the comments underneath there were loads of comments saying, “this is b******, this is just fake information”. But despite that, I still found it interesting” (Martha, F, 29).

While this lay knowledge is developed via people’s lived experience, their increasingly collective nature has been recognised (Barker and Galardi 2011). The concept of experiential authority distinguishes between traditional medical authority and knowledge developed by patient communities through self-help and peer support, marking a shift towards more collective and experienced-based health knowledge (Noorani 2013; Castro et al. 2019). For example, Pitts (2020) refers to ‘the patient voice’ as the most powerful weapon to fight Covid-19. Instagram users feeding back positively to the participants’ posts in the form of ‘likes’ and written affirmations made them feel positive about their own practices and sharing, and also made them want to ‘inspire’ others by making their health and illness experiences visible. The participants felt a sense of pride in documenting their practices, to motivate the community. For example, Annie was diagnosed with a brain tumour in her mid-twenties and went through life-threatening surgery to have it removed. She now suffers regular periods of what she describes as ‘health anxiety’ and feels she wants to inspire others who struggle with any mental or physical health difficulties:

“The feedback I received was overwhelming and all positive. I was very touched and felt extremely loved and supported by all the kind comments. People had said how much I had inspired them and how impressed they were with the way I was handling my situation. It made me feel reassured in everything I had written about and like I was on the right path” (Amy, Diary Entry, 27, F).

For Sophie, her injury from the marathon ‘legitimated’ her decision to stop training and she extensively discussed how she wanted to post about her injury, which in turn authorised her rest, and helped to mitigate questions or judgement from other users online.

“I was gutted [I couldn’t do more running] but then the only comfort I had from it was that I had just done the marathon so I felt like I had a good excuse, because everyone would be like ‘oh how’s your running going?’, so I was like ‘I’ve run the London marathon and my knee’s bad” (Sophie, Final Interview, 31, F).

Gallop (1990) posits that the body can be reconceptualized as a locus of knowledge, rather than a mere pre-given medium that the mind transcends, and thus through the increasing externalisation of the interior body enabled by the infrastructure of datafication of the self through social media sharing, means the body can perform in all its glory, its wealth and breadth of personal insights through data, self-realisation and achieve its end goal – a status of morally driven productivity. The stigma attached to invisible ill health and injury, meant participants felt that they had to ‘prove’ such ailments when they were unseen, as a form of diagnostic pantomime (Roach, 2017), or representation in which the community’s vision is transported and pulled into the participants’ clinical space, as illustrated in many of these participants experiences. Participants had to legitimate inactive behaviours to avoid feelings of guilt, by most importantly sharing their inactivity, illness or injury on Instagram to authorise it. As Josie Gorge articulates it in her memoir of living with a ‘mystery invisible illness’ her internal gaze increasingly intensifies as her body slows (2021). Understanding my participants’ sharing through Gorge’s (2021) lens, if social media enable a space for the showcasing of proactive self-surveillance, of visible self-analysis through sharing the internal gaze, this might be part of their attempt to understand their illness, injury, and most importantly communicate that effectively for their online community leading to the potential for broader shifts around increasing understanding and ­destigmatising unseen illness.

Conclusion

Under the platform economics of the attention economy (Srnicek 2016) Instagram today, tells an audience of billions (Statistica 2023) what is and what is not healthy. Instagram influencers and users adopt many visual and representative tools to perform health and/or illness for others online, gain influence, followers, and in turn commercial revenue (Abidin 2018). Additionally, they legitimate and make ‘visible’ invisible illnesses such as mental health issues or viral illnesses like COVID-19. These practices to some extent fosters ­community and motivates improvements in well-being and attempts to de-stigmatize ­invisible illnesses. However, the medical legitimacy of the content shared is often questionable, posing potential harms to public health. We seem to pendulum between these two spectrums, at one end Instagram is being used as an unregulated public health platform for experiential connection, information, and health guidance, and at the other, using it as a method of self or peer surveillance tying together poor health with sinful or ‘lazy’ behaviour or embodying that judgement over ourselves for lack of productivity to manage and sustain good health, or worse contributing to physical health harms. Our personal health and illness biographies are made visible to our social media communities through datafication and commodification of our bodies on Instagram.

The findings in this paper illustrate how there was a perceived commonality of public understanding of the pandemic and simultaneous suspicion and blame towards those who were not following governmental guidance. With that, comes pressures of the challenges of making visible invisible physical or mental illness or conditions. In this regard, this paper illustrated how making health or illness visible on social media can at times mitigate anxiety and some mental health concerns, even if it is bad for these individuals’ body, and perhaps means that anxiety related to being a citizen of the ill (Sontag 1978) is reduced or alleviated through sharing their visible bodily activity online, which in turn might harm the physical body. The emotional state has of mind has a powerful condition over its bodily matter (Moore and Robinson 2016). Perhaps the visibility of the body on Instagram is a passport of the citizen of the well (Sontag 1978), thus contributing to a greater sense of identity and self. With social media drawing attention and spatial visibility to the invisible, either good or poor health speaks to the qualitative texture of the human body. Of what we use to speak and make visible of ourselves into existence, to universalise ‘health’ or pain, viruses, or mental health suffering. When abstract and pungent definitions (Sontag 1978) carry a heavy stigma participants acknowledged that motivation to share was to provide a positive representation of dealing with disease and mental health issues. These promissory economies of hope (Rose 2007; Novas 2006) situates viewing health guidance or content on social media as an important component of one’s health management to aid personal self-care, analysis, and attention through visibility. These preventative measures and visibility of bodies on social media, not only normalises physical symptoms we associate with illness sharing online, through ­externalising the interior of the body, either by making ourselves visible or watching others, additionally, it is often in the attempt of a reduction of health risks.

During the pandemic, the visibility of one’s health and illness became a normalised insight into the everyday lived existence of our bodies and health on Instagram. The role of experiential and lay knowledge both helped and hindered users, navigating tsunamis of health information and misinformation online, with Instagram becoming a key source of public health knowledge. There is an increased awareness and potential de-stigmatisation of invisible illnesses like mental health conditions, chronic conditions, or COVID-19 through the intimacy enabled by sharing on Instagram. Different visual and textual strategies are employed to communicate trauma, injury and surgery, invisible symptoms, and legitimate inactivity to authorise the existence of these states of suffering or pain in the human body. Perhaps most fascinatingly, what we see now with the use of Instagram as a form of public health platform, is the increasing visibility of our body through the lens and attention of a community on social media contributing to personal motivations to perform health and fitness, legitimate and de-stigmatise illness or inactivity. The data representation of health and self authorises our body into existence online. At such times of great global instability and uncertainty, being visible on social media means not being isolated, being proactively healthy means a sense of community and unification, and sharing and viewing your health online ensures you do not become forgotten and means with this data trail there is no risk of becoming “an invisible statistic” (32, M). Instagram both provides a space for health guidance and community support, but also a breeding ground for misinformation and unqualified health advice. In addition, social media ­amplifies the moral discourse surrounding health, where good health is associated with being a morally good person, and poor health is seen as a personal failing. Instagram’s role as an unregulated public health platform has significant implications. While it fosters community and de-stigmatization of invisible illnesses, it also propagates misinformation and imposes pressure to conform to unrealistic health standards. The visibility of health and illness on Instagram highlights what is considered desirable or undesirable in terms of body materiality and lifestyle, shaping public perceptions and behaviours of health, illness and personal productivity under the dual regulation of neoliberalism and digital capitalism. Further research is needed to understand the full impact of this ­commodification of health on social media, and how that shapes public health, in turn developing strategies to mitigate the potential harms while leveraging its benefits for health communication and support.
