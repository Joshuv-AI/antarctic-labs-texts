A two-stage volunteer assignment model for post-disaster search and rescue operations
Authors: Umit Ozdemir, Suleyman Mete, Muhammet Gul
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Effective volunteer management requires ensuring operational continuity and maximizing benefits from all team members. Prioritizing volunteers with adequate knowledge and experience in task assignments is crucial for maintaining workflow continuity. This study employs a two-stage model for allocating areas and tasks to volunteers in search and rescue operations. The first stage identifies the optimal number of volunteer response centers using the P-median problem. Considering population density as demand, 7 out of 14 accessible and protected disaster collection areas meeting basic needs are selected as optimal response centers based on distance. In the second stage, a mixed-integer programming model is proposed for volunteer assignment, which prioritizes assigning the most knowledgeable and experienced volunteers to critical positions. The model’s flexibility and applicability are demonstrated by incorporating voluntary rest periods, which are allocated based on the urgent needs of response centers in various post-disaster scenarios. Solved using GAMS/CPLEX, the model’s results show that expert volunteers in low-demand scenarios are prioritized, working 5 out of 9 shifts, while other volunteers receive insufficient assignments. In high-demand scenarios, some response centers face volunteer shortages, with a deficit of 62 volunteers across 9 shifts in non-emergency areas. The study evaluates the benefits and drawbacks of the volunteer assignment model and offers recommendations for future research.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-41627-8.

Introduction

Natural disasters such as earthquakes, floods, and landslides, along with human-made disasters like war, nuclear, and chemical accidents, cause substantial damage to nations, affecting both their physical and mental well-being. An example of this is the earthquake that occurred in Turkey on February 6, 2023, which resulted in a death toll of over 48,000, destroyed more than 500,000 buildings, and caused many people to lose their jobs and relatives1. Disaster management minimizes the impact of such large-scale earthquake-like disasters through four stages: preparedness (risk identification and planning), mitigation (loss prevention), response (emergency response during the disaster), and recovery (restoration of infrastructure and living conditions). Effective personnel management is also crucial for reducing or mitigating the impact of such disasters and minimizing the resulting significant losses. By focusing on evacuation points and personnel deployment during the disaster preparation phase, effective disaster management can be ensured during the disaster response phase, thereby reducing the disaster’s impact2. In case of a disaster, immediate search in the response phase, and rescue efforts along with initial medical assistance, should be prioritized, followed by addressing shelter and nutritional requirements. After any disaster, local and international volunteer organizations and professionals mobilize swiftly to address urgent needs. Following the earthquake in Turkey, 36,527 volunteers, along with numerous expert personnel, were involved in the relief operations3. However, managing a volunteer group is different from managing professional staff. Since volunteers work without financial incentives, assigning them tasks according to their appropriate periods and abilities is important.

When assigning tasks to volunteers based on their abilities, it is crucial to maintain consistency within the organisation by prioritising their satisfaction and skills, in contrast to the approach taken with professionals. Disregarding the individual characteristics of volunteers and assigning tasks without considering their skills and expertise can lead to significant disruptions in workflow, lower morale, and satisfaction levels among volunteers, and potentially result in them discontinuing their duties4. Hence, when assigning volunteers to roles within an organization, their knowledge and skills should be assessed and matched accordingly. Priority in task allocation should be given to the volunteer group with the highest level of expertise. Providing the personnel needs primarily from the expert team will ensure that the work in the organisation is conducted in the best possible way without any disruption. Amini et al.5 study of disaster response in Iran found that training volunteers to improve their knowledge, skills, and attitudes increased their expertise, leading to more effective and efficient disaster response operations compared to a group that received no training.

Identifying appropriate service locations is just as critical as assigning suitable tasks to volunteers. It is of vital importance that personnel who will take part in disasters reach their place of duty promptly. When disasters occur, safe areas are needed to ensure the immediate safety of the people affected. To meet this need, disaster collection areas have been determined before any type of disaster in Turkey. The purpose of these areas has been determined as the closest accessible area where the victim can meet their basic needs and be away from danger after any disaster6. Identifying volunteer response centers through these designated gathering areas will help volunteers reach disaster victims faster and convey requests from victims to the center promptly.

This study aims to optimize disaster response by strategically positioning volunteers before disasters and allocating tasks effectively during the response. It addresses two fundamental questions: “How can the number and location of volunteer response centers be optimized?” and “How can volunteers with varying expertise be assigned to centers with different urgency levels, considering constraints?” To address these research questions, the study proposes a two-stage volunteer assignment model. In the first stage, the volunteer location problem is addressed using the P-median problem for disaster preparedness management. In the second stage, an assignment model based on experience-urgency matching is used to optimize volunteer task assignments tailored to real-world scenarios (high demand, variable demand, and low demand) by considering the urgency status at these designated locations, thereby aiming to improve disaster response. The rest of this paper is structured as follows: the literature review is analysed in the next section. In section three, volunteer response centers are designated based on disaster collection areas. The centers are selected from reliable, accessible locations that meet basic needs, guided by the P-median problem. In addition, a task assignment model is developed to efficiently allocate responsibilities among volunteer groups in gathering areas. The model prioritizes tasks for the most experienced volunteers based on the demands and urgency of the response centers, while considering each volunteer’s experience level and work-rest cycles, using mixed-integer programming. To demonstrate the model’s applicability, volunteer personnel from search and rescue teams are assigned based on various post-disaster scenarios in section four. The final section evaluates the volunteer assignment model used in these scenarios and suggests improvements for future research.

Literature review

P median problem

The P-median problem, a facility location selection method introduced by Revelle and Swain7, has been applied across diverse fields. These include emergency room placement in healthcare, facility location optimization based on customer demand in retail and logistics, and multi-objective optimization for coverage and service levels in healthcare and emergency services. The improved p-median problem also incorporates advanced metaheuristics, matrix decompositions, and heuristic/hybrid methods8.

The p-median problem helps determine optimal locations for disaster collection areas, particularly when demand is high during disaster management. Several studies have employed the P-median model for locating disaster relief facilities. For example, Pan9 used it to optimize typhoon emergency shelter placement in coastal rural China, while Xi et al.10 modified the model to account for rescue time constraints in a Western China neighbourhood. Zhu et al.11 presented a robust P-median model incorporating scenario analysis for uncertain disaster area prediction. More recently, Hwang and Geo12 used the algorithm to locate shelters, supply depots, and control centers around the Kori nuclear site, and Ayudhya13,14 employed it to identify flood shelter locations in southeastern Thailand and relief facility locations on Thailand’s east coast, respectively. Similarly, Eliguzel15 optimized emergency operation center allocation in the Philippines using a weighted P-median approach with Pictorial Fuzzy.

Volunteer assignment model

The volunteer assignment model first introduced by Falasca and Zobel16 is a bi-criteria integer programming model designed to minimize planning costs while adhering to volunteers’ preferences to avoid undesirable assignments. Table S.1 (in the Supplementary file) provides a chronological summary of volunteer assignment models across four key aspects17. The columns detail: (1) objective functions (maximizing or minimizing utility based on shortage, undesired assignment, and time); (2) constraints considered (task, preference, shifts, skills, and travel); (3) solution methods employed (integer, goal, heuristic, and artificial intelligence); and (4) shortcomings identified in the studies (daily wage, demand, application area, or travel time).

When voluntary assignment models are examined in terms of their solution methods, some studies employ integer programming models; however, existing approaches have limitations. García et al.18 maximized volunteer benefits and minimized costs, yet their model struggles with complex scenarios and lacks clear optimization strategies. Kaur et al.19 balanced several factors to improve retention but recognized limitations in volunteer participation and diversity. Chen et al.20 optimized volunteer dispatch in emergencies but highlighted the need for better task coordination. Atik et al.21 maximized demand served and minimized travel costs, while neglecting key factors such as transportation costs and stochasticity.

Mathematical modelling of volunteer assignment problems has been approached in several ways. Degel et al.22 minimized the number of fire stations based on volunteer availability and travel times, but did not consider volunteer schedules, per diem, or uncertain rescue demand. Lassiter et al.23 dynamically allocated volunteers to minimize unmet task demands, assignment costs, and skill mismatches, achieving a Pareto optimum through a constrained allocation model. Urrea et al.24 optimized pairings of experienced and inexperienced volunteers but neglected organizational financial or time contributions. Wang et al.25 categorized and ranked risk areas for emergency supply distribution and modelled volunteer deployment but did not consider multi-hour cost functions or volunteer substitution.

Heuristic methodology has also been used in some studies for the volunteer assignment model. Abualkhair et al.26 used a simulation to minimize arrival and service times in a two-queue system for donors and donations but ignored heterogeneity and uncertainty. Paret et al.27 developed a multi-server queuing model to manage the spontaneous volunteers who emerged after the disaster, but not training/skills. Fei and Wang28 applied the Dempster-Shafer theory to optimize rescuer dispatch, which needs broader analysis. Xue et al.29 applied the same theory to consider the volunteers’ abilities, task demands in disaster areas, and waiting time, while development is needed for estimating the time in both the disaster and dispatched areas. Matinrad and Granberg30 minimized start times, but their model assumed uniform skills and did not account for travel, task transfer, or equipment installation times. Ata et al.31 investigated the dynamic control of the system by formulating a multi-class queuing network model to maximize volunteer efficiency. Kapukaya and Satoglu32 developed a two-stage stochastic programming model for optimising resource allocation in search and rescue efforts, but it should also apply to other disasters and missions and consider longer time horizons to address resource allocation during recovery. Karahalios et al.33 used a novel two-level nested heuristic called the Graver Augmented Multiple Seed Algorithm (GAMA) to solve the problem by formulating the first response network design problem (FRNDP) as a mixed integer nonlinear program.

In addition to these solution methods, Aman et al.34 developed a goal-programming volunteer assignment model that minimizes penalty costs associated with volunteer numbers, shift changes, and task deviations; however, this model neglects volunteer preferences and unused workforce. Rabiei et al.35 used NSGA-II and NRGA, neural networks in artificial intelligence, to assign volunteers considering their abilities, preferences, and task demands, but the application of their model is limited to nursing, and its performance has not been compared against other algorithms.

According to the data obtained from the literature analysis, there is one research on a two-stage volunteer assignment model32. To address the gap, this study introduces a two-stage disaster management model, optimizing response center locations and volunteer assignment across preparation and response phases. While skills-based volunteer deployment has been explored in some studies19,23,25,32, this model incorporates expert-emergency area matching based on regional urgency. Some models address disaster implementation20,21,30, but they lack studies on diverse disaster scenarios. This study analyses model applicability through realistic disaster scenarios and designs volunteer emergency response plans based on expert personnel needs. Furthermore, it prioritizes volunteer satisfaction by including rest period restrictions, an aspect overlooked in previous research. Methodology

Immediate intervention in disaster areas is crucial to alleviate chaos, minimize risks, and optimize the use of personnel. Establishing operational centers and pre-determining intervention areas facilitates access, prevents disorder through effective volunteer distribution, and ensures optimal working conditions. Deploying volunteer to these designated areas enhances intervention capability and ensures their safety. When distributing tasks to volunteers, their knowledge and experience levels are taken into consideration. Assigning volunteers with the highest level of knowledge to risky tasks ensures that task distribution is planned effectively and that volunteers remain within the organization. This research is designed with this aim in mind, and the implementation stages are summarized in Fig. 1.

Fig. 1Developed two stages of the volunteer assignment model.

Developed two stages of the volunteer assignment model.

In the first stage of the proposed approach, an optimum number of volunteer response centers are selected from among the pre-disaster collection areas (candidate locations) determined by AFAD, taking into account the centers’ needs and distances from other centers, using the P-median problem. These centers should be easily accessible, away from risks and structures, and equipped to meet basic needs36. The aim is to guarantee a rapid response to every disaster area by ensuring the shortest possible access time to all centers.

In the second stage of the proposed approach, a volunteer assignment model is developed to meet the demands and urgency of the designated volunteer response centers. The main goal of the model is to ensure that volunteers with the highest level of knowledge are assigned to the most critical regions. When distributing tasks, the principle of not assigning consecutive shifts is applied, taking work and rest periods into account. Different disaster scenarios are used during task distribution, aiming to meet the demands and urgency levels of each volunteer center. The AFAD Volunteers Club (AFC) selected for the proposed assignment model was included in this study after being informed and providing consent in accordance with guidelines and regulations and was informed about potential disaster response task assignments. The study concludes with a detailed analysis of the results from these scenarios, discussing the advantages and limitations of the model. The methods and formulas used in the study are summarized below.

P-median problem

P-median, proposed by Revelle and Swain7, is a location selection problem aiming to find the location of p facilities using n nodes in a network to minimise the total average weighted distance between the demand and the facility. Unlike p-center problems that minimize maximum distance and capacity-oriented problems that balance capacity and costs, the p-median problem focuses on minimizing total demand distance or cost. This is particularly useful in disaster management, where maximizing the response area takes precedence over minimizing individual distances or coverage. By establishing response centers in high-demand areas and deploying resources, the p-median approach creates a service network with minimal distance, optimizing equipment operations with limited resources. It can also be used for logistical services like food aid and evacuation37. Despite its advantages, the p-median problem is NP-hard, requiring heuristic and meta-heuristic algorithms for all but very small-scale problems. This study will not be subject to the limitations of the method because it will prefer the P-median problem to determine the optimum number of facilities p (relative to demand and distance) among 14 small-scale regions, taking into account population densities, demand, and distances to these points.

The proposed mathematical model for volunteer assignment

After analysing volunteer assignment models in the literature, this study aims to develop a mixed integer programming model. This model is an improved volunteer assignment model that is based on the principle of maximum benefit, aims to assign the most experienced volunteers to the most critical region, and allows volunteers to rest after their working hours. The objective function (6) aims to fulfil volunteer needs primarily by using highly skilled volunteers and allocating those with the highest expertise to the most critical regions based on urgency. Thus, it maximizes the weighted sum of assignments, and the weights combine volunteer expertise and central urgency. The proposed model includes 4 constraints. The first constraint (7) ensures that response centers are not assigned more volunteers than the number of volunteers needed in each period. Constraint (8) ensures that volunteers are assigned to only one response center per period. Constraint (9) ensures that there must be at least one rest period between working periods. Constraints (10) ensure that the assignment model is integer, non-negative, and binary.

Application

Distributing a classified volunteer group to specific areas helps prevent post-disaster chaos and enables faster response. However, the selected areas must be easily accessible, safe, and capable of meeting basic human needs38. Therefore, when selecting volunteer response centers, an analysis is required beforehand to ensure these conditions are met. In the implementation phase of this study, volunteer response centers are chosen from disaster collection areas with the specific features outlined by Tunceli Afad39 in each neighbourhood within the main area of Tunceli province. Task distribution is then conducted using an assignment model that incorporates the rest periods of the Munzur Emergency Disaster Club, whose members voluntarily participated in emergency disaster response activities and served in various roles at these selected centers.

Assigning volunteers to response centers

Volunteer selection and classification

The AFAD Volunteers Club (AFC), established by the Munzur University Department of Emergency Aid and Disaster Management, is a volunteer-oriented community dedicated to providing pre- and post-disaster assistance. In this paper, the volunteer assignment model is employed to determine the duty locations and working periods of volunteers in the club under various scenarios, both before and after the disaster in Tunceli province. The classification levels of volunteers according to their knowledge and experience in disasters were taken from the papers conducted. Their by Ozdemir et al.42,43. Their studies categorized disaster management volunteers into four classes: expert (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{C}}_{1})$$\end{document}, experienced\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{(\mathrm{C}}_{2})$$\end{document}, sufficient\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{(\mathrm{C}}_{3})$$\end{document}, and inadequate (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{\mathrm{C}}_{4}$$\end{document})} based on their competencies in search and rescue. This classification, determined using the TOPSIS-Sort method and weighted criteria from expert surveys, primarily aims to identify the most prepared volunteers for specific needs and planning. Nine criteria were considered, including first aid, teamwork, and search and rescue equipment skills. To optimize volunteer utilization and minimize risks, the expert group receives priority in task assignments. Volunteers in the inadequate category will be assigned basic tasks like food distribution or communication with victims or can be assigned more specialized tasks after sufficient training. This will allow them to specialize in professional operations such as first aid or search and rescue through pre-disaster training and drills. Scenario 1: Assign an equal number of volunteers for very urgent situations

This scenario aims to equally distribute volunteers across all regions for 9 shifts, prioritizing all volunteer response centers as if they are in the most urgent situation. This approach is designed for the immediate aftermath of a disaster, ensuring maximum utilization of available personnel45, particularly crucial following large-scale events. For instance, the February 6, 2023, Kahramanmaraş earthquake in Turkey revealed that organizational issues hindered the deployment of search and rescue teams to some districts within the critical first 72 h, and insufficient personnel caused serious disruptions in certain regions46.

The proposed model is analysed in 1.073 s using the GAMS/CPLEX solver on an Intel i5 11th Gen (2.40 GHz) computer with 8 GB of RAM, based on data in Table 4. Discussion

This study assigned experienced volunteers to critical areas, matching them to specific response centers based on needs and urgency across shifts. Volunteers were allowed rest periods, working a maximum of one out of every two consecutive shifts. The model’s effectiveness was evaluated under varying scenarios, considering diverse needs and urgencies. The data indicates that the volunteer assignment model prioritises experts before selecting from other groups, assigning lower-class volunteers as needed after the top-tier volunteers are allocated. Volunteers in class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{1}$$\end{document} are assigned more tasks in all scenarios, while those in class \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{4}$$\end{document}. took on fewer or no tasks compared to the other class. In scenarios with low volunteer needs (Scenario 3), no volunteers are selected from the less experienced groups (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{3}$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{4}$$\end{document}). When assessing the need for volunteers in various situations, it has been found that if the requirement fits the size of the group \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:{C}_{1}$$\end{document},all volunteers are provided by this group, and the volunteer class order is applied in the assignment.

In Scenario 1, volunteers are assigned tasks to maximize their working time and address urgent personnel needs. The goal is to deliver immediate assistance to all disaster victims across a broad area by evenly distributing the most suitable volunteers to each response center. In Scenario 2, it is believed that a disaster adversely impacts a specific region, leading to secondary disasters later. For instance, an earthquake first affects a particular area, and subsequently, other initially unaffected areas require assistance due to aftershocks. This scenario aims to prioritize emergency areas when personnel shortages arise during disasters. Data shows that assigning the most experienced individuals to the most urgent needs maximizes benefits; however, due to a limited number of volunteers, less urgent regions cannot be adequately staffed and experience volunteer shortages. Scenario 3 is inspired by a regional disaster, like a flood, affecting the riverbed area. The timeframe is short, and the demand for volunteers is minimal. Results show that inexperienced volunteers are not assigned tasks, while all experts participated in the operations.

This model considers relevant disaster scenarios, including earthquakes and floods, along with varying volunteer needs and urgency levels. The criteria for classifying volunteers by disaster type can be adjusted, allowing for varied task assignments within the model. For secondary disasters following the primary event, such as fires or landslides, volunteers can be allocated additional tasks in categories like search and rescue, first aid, or food distribution. The model adjusts working time based on the critical first 72 h (8-hour shifts) following the most destructive earthquake. For shorter disasters like storms, the study period can be reduced accordingly. The model, while primarily aimed at post-disaster volunteer assignments, can also be applied to volunteer training and drills before a disaster. For instance, considering regional characteristics, less experienced and expert volunteers can be mixed and organized into groups for drills related to previous avalanche disasters, allowing them to gain valuable experience.

Managerial insights

This study contributes to emergency volunteer management by presenting a two-stage assignment model. This model prioritizes volunteer satisfaction by matching expert volunteers to critical areas, considering the urgency of needs. The first stage strategically identifies response centers based on population density for effective and timely intervention. The second stage uses a skill-based allocation model, matching volunteer expertise to regional criticality, ensuring optimal task allocation across diverse disaster scenarios. The study offers significant academic and practical implications.

This model adopts a two-stage approach to disaster preparedness and volunteer management: first, contributing to the disaster preparedness phase by assessing location suitability and identifying an optimal number of volunteer response centers, and providing drill and training opportunities in these areas; second, maximizing the effectiveness of personnel by assigning tasks to volunteers during disaster response. Conclusion

In the paper’s initial phase, suitable areas for volunteer work are identified. These areas are selected based on safety and accessibility criteria to ensure an optimal working environment. Then develop a volunteer assignment model that pairs the most knowledgeable and experienced volunteers with the highest-priority needs. This model is designed to be flexible, maximizing the effectiveness of volunteer contributions while adapting to various scenarios. It also ensures that volunteers have adequate rest periods outside of their working hours.

The initial model stage assessed population densities as demand across disaster collection areas. Considering distances, it selected 7 optimal volunteer response centers from 14 candidate collection areas by P Median. This aims to balance volunteer placement, avoiding excessive centers that increase travel times and too few, which would cause overcrowding given capacity limits.

The second stage of the proposed volunteer assignment model results indicate that during low demand, volunteers from other groups are only assigned after all experienced volunteers have been allocated. For instance, in the low-demand Scenario 3, only expert and experienced volunteers were assigned tasks. This strategy focuses on assigning less experienced volunteers to lower-risk tasks rather than to critical ones. In the more-demand scenario, expert volunteers worked 5 out of 9 shifts, while less experienced volunteers worked 4 shifts to meet staffing needs. In contrast, when demand surpasses the available volunteer numbers, some centers are inadequately staffed since urgent needs are prioritized during emergencies. In Scenario 2, high-priority centers (e.g., 10 and 13) were fully staffed during peak demand (e.g.,2–3 shifts), while lower-priority areas (e.g., level 1, center 11) experienced a shortage of 63 volunteers over 9 shifts. The model distributes volunteers fairly, even if it results in shortages, and accounts for rest periods when immediate post-disaster support is required. The number of volunteers here can be adjusted based on specific center requests and desired periods.

To evaluate the model’s real-world applicability, a disaster and emergency relief volunteer group was selected. This group, which aimed to raise disaster awareness and expand the volunteer base through community training, consisted of 124 members throughout the study, providing a suitable small-scale test case. Furthermore, because the volunteer group was in areas accessible to disaster response centers, the scenarios were designed as real-world disaster simulations. The existence of a practical application example allows the model to demonstrate its potential for adaptation to various volunteer organizations operating in different fields.

The study has several limitations. The small size of the research area, low population density, and limited volunteer pool prevent the model from demonstrating its application and ability to handle the complex structures typical of large volunteer activities. It is recommended that the model be applied to organizations operating in diverse fields with more volunteers covering broader regions. Although the model is designed around a two-stage volunteer placement task distribution, it uses a single objective function, ignoring some constraints found in volunteer assignment problems. Including the following constraints would allow for a more comprehensive study, enhancing the model’s applicability and effectiveness:

A new two-stage assignment model could be developed, using artificial neural networks instead of TOPSIS for classifying volunteers by knowledge and skill level in the first stage.
