Safe and successful fecal microbiota transplantation for recurrent Clostridioides difficile infection in a child with an intestinal transplant
Authors: Juliana Kennedy, Kishore Iyer, Ari Grinspan, Joanne Lai
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) treats patients with recurrent Clostridioides difficile infections (CDI) by restoring the colonic flora with a balanced microbiome. There are currently no reports of safe and successful FMT in pediatric patients with history of intestinal transplant on immunosuppression. Our case report describes a 6-year-old boy with a multi-visceral transplant (including: stomach, small bowel, colon, liver, pancreas, and en-bloc bilateral kidneys) who had recurrent CDI episodes treated with antibiotics (including prolonged tapers). After multiple recurrences, he had a FMT via colonoscopy. The fecal preparation was inserted in the transplanted cecum without complication. The patient did well without any adverse events or need for hospitalization and the FMT was a success, as he had no recurrent symptoms in the 3 months following FMT.Keywords: C. difficile, Transplant, Pediatric, Infection, Fecal, Intestine

Introduction

Patients with organ transplants are at an increased risk for Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) due to their chronic immunosuppression, frequent need for antibiotics, and frequent hospital admissions [1]. These exposures contribute to a decreased microbial diversity that is also associated with higher risk of recurrent CDI, defined as a repeat infection within 8 weeks of prior episode [2]. Fecal microbiota transplantation (FMT) is used to treat recurrent CDI by restoring colonic flora to a balanced microbiome [3], [4]. However, FMT is designated as an investigational procedure by the Food and Drug Administration. There are no randomized controlled trials for FMT in pediatric patients and the adult trials exclude immunocompromised patients due to the potential risk of transmitting a new infectious organism from the donor stool to the recipient [5], [6].

Despite these limitations, FMT has been shown to be a safe and effective treatment for recurrent CDI in both pediatric and adult immunocompromised patients and patients with solid organ transplants [5], [7], [8]. There is a paucity of data on FMT in patients with intestinal transplant, likely due to concerns for safety. One multicenter study reported 1 successful FMT in an adult patient with intestinal transplant, but did not include details of the case [9]. In pediatrics specifically, a recent multicenter study by Conover et al. [10] included a patient with intestinal transplant, but the FMT was not successful in this individual (Nicholson, personal correspondence). Success of FMT is typically defined as no recurrence of CDI within 3 months of FMT.

We present the case of a child with history of a multi-visceral transplant who was safely and successfully treated with FMT for recurrent CDI.

Case report

Our patient is a 6-year-old male with autosomal recessive polycystic kidney disease who underwent bilateral nephrectomies at 6 months of life and was dialysis dependent. His medical history is also notable for pulmonary hypoplasia, liver disease, short bowel syndrome, and chronic inferior vena cava (IVC) thrombosis.

Due to progressing chronic IVC thrombosis, at age 2 he underwent an en bloc multi-visceral transplant that included the donor IVC, aorta, stomach, small intestine, colon, liver, kidneys, and pancreas. Post-operatively the patient developed severe bilateral vesicoureteral reflux with frequent urinary tract infections (UTIs) and growth of drug-resistant organisms (vancomycin-resistant Enterococcus and extended-spectrum beta-lactamase E. coli). He receives daily prophylactic antibiotics and underwent two deflux procedures to treat grade 5 vesicoureteral reflux.

In recent years, the patient’s immunosuppression regimen has been tacrolimus (steady level of 8–10 ng/mL) and prednisolone (dose of 1.5 mg daily). He is also maintained on prophylactic penicillin V for asplenia and omeprazole for gastroesophageal reflux disease. He no longer requires parenteral nutrition and does not have a central line.

Despite prophylactic measures, he was admitted to the hospital multiple times in 2023 for febrile illnesses (UTIs and respiratory infections) and for CDI episodes with significant diarrheal output and electrolyte abnormalities requiring intravenous fluids (IVF) (Fig. 1). The patient received multiple treatment courses for CDIs including standard vancomycin treatment, vancomycin tapers, and fidaxomicin. CDI testing details are included in Fig. 1 where available. Given his history of intestinal transplant, when presenting with significant diarrhea, the patient often required endoscopic evaluation to rule out transplant rejection as the cause of his diarrhea.

In October, while being treated with a prolonged vancomycin taper, the team began discussion of FMT to treat his recurrent CDI. The patient had numerous risk factors for recurrent CDI including frequent hospitalizations, broad spectrum antibiotics for complicated UTIs, acid suppression therapy, and immunosuppression. Given his history of more than 3 episodes of recurrent CDI and failure of vancomycin taper, the decision was made to proceed with FMT.

The patient continued on vancomycin 125 mg twice a day until 2 days prior to the FMT. The night before the procedure, he completed a bowel preparation with polyethylene glycol 3350. Two hours prior to the FMT, he had a dose of loperamide.

The FMT was performed via colonoscopy with 40 mL of OpenBiome fecal microbiota preparation (standard dose of ≥ 5 ×1011 bacteria suspended in normal saline with 10 % glycerol) inserted in the transplanted cecum. OpenBiome is a distributor of investigational FMT preparations manufactured by the University of Minnesota under an FDA-reviewed investigational new drug application. The patient tolerated the procedure well and did not exhibit signs of adverse events such as post-procedure infection, bacterial translocation, diarrhea, allergic reaction, or symptoms of irritable bowel syndrome. The FMT was declared successful as the patient did not experience any CDI symptoms in the 3 months following the FMT.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first case demonstrating a safe and successful FMT to treat recurrent CDI in a child with an intestinal transplant. In medically complex and immunocompromised patients, like our patient, it can be difficult to weigh the risks and benefits for FMT when there is limited data available. The rate of serious adverse events (SAE) from FMT in the general pediatric population has been reported as low as 5 % [11]. However, in pediatric immunosuppressed patients, the rate of SAE (hospitalizations or life threatening event) is reported as high as 31 % [10]. Though in these studies, the authors comment that only a fraction of the SAE are thought to be related to the FMT. Majority of the patients who experienced an adverse event required hospitalization (29 %). Serious adverse events related to the FMT included cecal perforation after FMT via colonoscopy, aspiration pneumonitis following FMT via upper endoscopy, fever requiring hospitalization and antibiotics given immunosuppression, and intractable diarrhea. Other more common and less serious adverse events included diarrhea, emesis, abdominal pain, and fever without hospitalization.

The potential adverse events of FMT in an immunocompromised child must be weighed against the sequela of recurrent CDI. CDI in our patient caused multiple prolonged hospitalizations and the need for procedures with anesthesia (endoscopy to evaluate for transplant rejection). In the literature, recurrent CDI in transplant patients has been associated with inpatient mortality, longer lengths of hospital stay, and higher total hospital charges [12]. Additionally, a Swiss study found that CDI was an independent risk factor transplant graft loss [13]. Balancing the risks of repeated hospitalizations, intermittent concern for intestinal transplant rejection, and potential graft loss, against the risk of adverse events from FMT in immunocompromised patients, our team opted to treat this patient with FMT.

While FMT remains an investigational procedure, there are recommendations for how to optimize the treatment and minimize FMT failure, so that the risks associated with FMT are not taken more than once. Fortunately for patients like the one described in our case, the number of CDI recurrences and immunosuppressed status are not independent risk factors for FMT failure [14]. FMT procedure recommendations include stopping CDI antibiotics (typically vancomycin) 12–48 hours prior to the procedure and providing a bowel clean out with laxatives [3]. Beran et al. [14] found that poor quality bowel prep was a significant predictor of FMT failure. There is no specific recommendation regarding acid suppression therapy, but ‘caution is advised’ [3]. Our patient continued his proton pump inhibitor throughout all illnesses and following FMT. FMT via colonoscopy has been shown to be more successful compared to other modes of delivery [7].

Our patient had a successful FMT based on the frequently used definition of success as no diarrheal or CDI related symptoms for 3 months following the FMT. He was not tested for C. difficile during this time as it is not recommended to test for cure in asymptomatic patients and our patient remained asymptomatic [3]. More importantly, he did not require hospitalization following the procedure and did not experience any adverse events. More and longer-term studies are needed to further evaluate the safety of FMT in the intestinal transplant population.
