Outcomes of Surgical Versus Conservative Management in Severe Traumatic Brain Injury (GCS 3–4) With Evacuable Mass Lesions: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Hanuman Prasad Prajapati, B. K. Ojha, Jason Golmei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveManagement of patients with severe traumatic brain injury (TBI) presenting with Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores of 3–4 and surgically evacuable intracranial hematomas remains controversial because of perceived futility and poor outcomes. This study compared outcomes of surgical versus conservative management in this patient population.MethodsThis retrospective observational study included patients with TBI (GCS 3–4) and surgically evacuable intracranial mass lesions admitted to a tertiary care centre between December 2015 and December 2023. Among 3,657 TBI admissions, 752 patients met inclusion criteria: 568 underwent surgical management and 184 were treated conservatively. Clinical and radiological variables were analysed. Outcomes were assessed at 6 months using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS). Favorable outcome was defined as GOS 3–5, given the high mortality in this cohort. Subgroup analysis and multivariable logistic regression analysis were performed.ResultsSix-month mortality was significantly lower in the surgical group (79.2% vs. 94.5%). Surgery was associated with shorter intensive care unit stay (p=0.001) and fewer systemic complications (p=0.003). Favourable outcomes were significantly more frequent following surgery in patients aged <40 years, with extradural hematoma, unilateral or bilateral pupillary dilation, and intervention within 6 hours of injury (all p<0.05). On adjusted analysis, surgical management was significantly associated with favorable outcome (odds ratio, 3.7; 95% confidence interval, 2.0–6.8; p<0.001).ConclusionDespite an overall poor prognosis, surgical evacuation of mass lesions in TBI patients with GCS 3–4 was associated with improved survival and functional outcomes in selected patients, although causality cannot be inferred.

INTRODUCTION

Traumatic brain injury (TBI) affects approximately 50 million people globally.4) It is a leading cause of mortality and morbidity worldwide.22) The primary brain injury which occurs at the time of impact may be followed by secondary brain injury caused by a series of events, including elevated intracranial pressure (ICP), reduced cerebral blood flow, cerebral hypoperfusion, and further brain swelling.3) The goal of management of these injuries is to prevent secondary brain insults. Although the management for brain oedema and increased ICP is primarily medical, in cases refractory to medical treatment, the alternative is to perform surgery.24)

Decompressive craniectomy (DC), with or without the removal of the intracranial hematoma, plays a crucial role in the management of patients with TBI with substantial mass lesions or uncontrolled elevation of the ICP.9) Although surgery has been routinely utilized for the treatment of extradural hematoma (EDH), subdural hematoma (SDH) and intracerebral hematoma (ICH) as indicated, the application of such strategy for TBI patients presenting with GCS 3–4 is limited.316)

Although DC has saved multiple lives in the past decades, the high post operative mortality and morbidity rates in these ultra-severe TBI patients are not only devastating to patients and their family members but also challenging for neurosurgeons.78) Nevertheless, it is unclear whether DC should be performed in all severe TBI (GCS 3–4) with acute intracranial hematoma on admission.1012) Therefore, this study aimed to compare outcomes between surgical and conservative management in patients with severe TBI (GCS 3–4) and surgically evacuable mass lesions.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

This retrospective observational study was carried out at Department of Neurosurgery in a Tertiary Trauma center. A total of 3,657 patients with TBI admitted to the Neurosurgery Trauma Centre between December 2015 and December 2023 were evaluated retrospectively. Out of 3,657 patients treated at our centre, 752 patients followed our inclusion criteria and were included in this study.

On arrival to the emergency department primary resuscitation was done. A complete neurological examination including Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), motor response, pupillary size and reactivity, blood pressure, and pulse rate was performed. Plain CT brain was done in all cases. Patient’s radiological findings like type of brain injury, location, size of hematoma, mass effect and midline shift were recorded. All patients were offered DC and evacuation of hematoma as per institutional protocol after informed consent. Treatment allocation was not randomized; the final decision was influenced by clinical judgment and family preference after counseling regarding prognosis, surgical risks, and expected outcomes. Patients selected for surgical intervention may have had more favorable baseline prognostic characteristics, introducing potential selection bias.

All the patients managed either surgically or conservatively were monitored in intensive care unit (ICU) as per institute protocol. Conservative management included standard intensive care measures such as airway protection, mechanical ventilation when indicated, sedation, osmotherapy (mannitol or hypertonic saline), hemodynamic stabilization, and supportive care as per institutional TBI protocols. Tracheostomy was performed early after surgical intervention and also in selected patients managed conservatively as clinically indicated. Systemic complications were defined as documented pulmonary infection, systemic infection/sepsis, deep vein thrombosis, or other organ dysfunction occurring during hospitalization.

Outcome analysis

Outcomes were assessed at 6 months using the Glasgow Outcome Scale (GOS). When assessment occurred beyond 6 months, the earliest follow-up beyond 6 months was recorded as the 6-month outcome. The GOS is a 5-point scale (1=death; 2=persistent vegetative state; 3=severe disability; 4=moderate disability; 5=good recovery). Favorable outcome was defined as GOS 3–5, and unfavorable outcome as GOS 1–2.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with the SPSS, trial version 25 for windows statistical software package (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). The categorical data were presented as numbers (percentage) and were compared among group using χ2 test and Fisher’s exact test. The value of p<0.05 was considered statistically significant. Sub-group analysis was done to show the different favourable and unfavourable outcomes based on the variables. Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between surgical management and favorable outcome after adjusting for clinically relevant covariates.

Ethical statement

This retrospective study was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee. The requirement for informed consent was waived because the study involved retrospective analysis of anonymized clinical data.

DISCUSSION

The management of TBI in patients presenting with GCS 3–4 remains challenging due to high mortality and uncertain prognosis, and evidence regarding aggressive surgical management in this subgroup remains limited.316) As the Glasgow Coma Scale is widely used for initial assessment of TBI severity, outcomes in patients presenting with very low scores such as GCS 3–4 should be interpreted within this clinical context.25)

Only a few studies have reported favorable results with surgery. Scotter et al.18) in their meta-analysis reported that a favourable prognosis is possible in patients with bilateral fixed dilated pupils if surgery is performed in selected patients. Our study also had favorable results with surgery. There was a significant difference (p<0.001) in mortality rate between the surgical group (450/568; 79.2%) and the conservative group (172/184; 94.5%). Most of the other previous studies had reported unfavorable results with surgery in patients with GCS 3–4. Park et al.14) performed DC in 127 severe TBI patients, and they found that the mortality rate was 82.2% in patients with GCS scores of 4 and 5. Jamous et al.10) performed DC in 21 patients with bilateral pupil enlargement and a GCS score of 3. The authors pointed out that the mortality rate of these patients was 100%. Studies by Jamous et al.10) and Gouello et al.6) found that surgery in these patients with GCS <5 was associated with significantly higher mortality. They consequently encouraged clinicians to limit the performance of DC in patients with very severe TBI (initial GCS score <5 and bilateral fixed pupil dilation).

The present study demonstrated a statistically significant difference in favourable outcome with surgery (n=21, 26.92%) compared to conservative treatment (n=2, 5.88%) in patients <40 years age group (p=0.02). In patients >60 years of age, there was no significant difference in favourable outcome in both modality of management (p=1.00). Age may also influence GCS assessment and prognostication in patients with traumatic brain injury.17) A large prospective study of 244 TBI patients found that risk of unfavourable outcome at 6 months increased with age in a stepwise manner even after adjusting for confounders. In the same study: 1 month mortality was 15% for age <18 years; 44% for age 18–59; and 52% for age >59.5) In very elderly (≥75 years) patients with severe TBI, studies report very high rates of poor outcome or death, especially when combined with severe CT findings (e.g., SDH, midline shift and cisternal obliteration).15)

In this study, most surgically evacuable intracranial pathologies including acute SDH, ICH and contusion had unfavourable outcome (p>0.05). Only patients with evacuable EDH had statistically significant favorable outcomes with surgery compared to conservative treatment. A retrospective series of ultra-severe closed TBI (GCS 3–5) with mass lesion (mostly SDH) reported that surgical management may be associated with survival benefit in selected cases.13) In this subgroup, overall prognosis remains poor regardless of treatment modality. However, surgery may still produce occasional survivors with acceptable functional outcomes especially if some favourable factors are present (e.g., surgically treatable mass lesion, reactive pupils, younger age and timely intervention). Importantly, the presence of a surgical lesion on imaging does not necessarily guarantee improved outcome when baseline GCS is very low. The benefit of surgical intervention appeared most pronounced in patients with EDH, while other lesion types demonstrated limited or non-significant differences, highlighting the heterogeneity within this cohort.

In the present study, compared with conservative treatment (n=2, 1.85%), surgery (n=30, 10.38%) still had significant (p=0.0101) favorable outcomes in TBI patients presenting with GCS 3–4 with surgically evacuable mass lesion with bilateral dilated pupils. In TBI patients with GCS 3–4, dilated pupils, and an evacuable mass lesion on CT, urgent surgical evacuation may be associated with an increased likelihood of immediate survival and may provide a potential opportunity for meaningful recovery. Conservative management in the presence of an evacuable mass lesion and herniation signs was associated with extremely poor outcomes in this cohort.21) Kaushik et al.11) had reported no favorable outcomes with conservative treatment while surgery yielded limited survival. Interestingly, the “non-reactive normal size pupil” subgroup demonstrated less favorable outcomes compared to patients with dilated pupils. This finding may reflect underlying diffuse brain injury or brainstem dysfunction in this subgroup, rather than focal mass effect. In contrast, pupillary dilation in the presence of evacuable mass lesions may represent reversible transtentorial herniation, which is potentially amenable to surgical decompression. Additionally, the relatively small sample size in this subgroup may have contributed to this observation.

In this study, compared to conservative (n=7, 7.45%) treatment, surgical (n=84, 23.68%) treatment had significant (p=0.0008) favorable outcome when performed early (<6 hours). In severe TBI patients (GCS 3–4) with surgically evacuable mass lesion, early presentation and prompt surgical evacuation were associated with better survival and functional outcomes compared to late presentation. While surgery was associated with lower mortality even in late presenters, meaningful neurological recovery is largely confined to patients undergoing early surgical intervention, whereas conservative treatment alone was associated with very high mortality irrespective of timing.1219) However, early intervention did not retain independent significance in multivariable analysis, suggesting that it may be a surrogate marker for other favorable clinical factors such as patient selection and injury characteristics. Other prognostic markers, including biochemical parameters, have also been evaluated in moderate-to-severe traumatic brain injury.20)

Previous study done by Van Essen et al.23) stated that systemic complications like pneumonia, sepsis, deep vein thrombosis, bed sore and multi organ issues are common in both surgical and conservative groups due to prolonged ICU stay, mechanical ventilation and immobility. In our study, although complications occurred in both groups, surgically treated patients had significantly shorter ICU stay and fewer systemic complications compared with conservatively managed patients. This may be because surgical evacuation leads to early reduction of raised ICP, which may reduce ICU stay and systemic complications. These findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of a treatment effect. Furthermore, the conservative management group may have included a higher proportion of patients with treatment limitations or withdrawal of life-sustaining therapy, which could have contributed to higher mortality independent of treatment modality.

This study has several important limitations. First, the retrospective single-center design limits generalizability. Second, treatment allocation was non-randomized and influenced by clinical judgment and family preference, introducing significant selection bias, as patients selected for surgery may have had more favorable baseline prognostic characteristics. Third, important prognostic variables such as midline shift, basal cistern status, hematoma volume, and secondary insults (e.g., hypotension and hypoxia) were not uniformly available, which may result in residual confounding. Fourth, conservative management protocols were not standardized across all patients, and some patients in the conservative group may have had treatment limitations or withdrawal of care, which could independently influence outcomes. Fifth, heterogeneity of lesion types may affect interpretability of pooled analyses. Finally, the absence of propensity score matching or advanced statistical adjustment methods further limits causal inference.

Based on the findings of this study, surgical evacuation may be considered in selected patients with severe TBI (GCS 3–4) and surgically evacuable mass lesions, particularly in younger individuals and those presenting early, irrespective of pupillary status. Time to presentation and intervention appears to play a critical role in outcome, and efforts should be made to minimize delays through rapid referral and standardized trauma protocols. Conservative management may be appropriate for patients deemed unsalvageable after thorough clinical and radiological assessment, while acknowledging the overall poor prognosis and inherent limitations of the available evidence.

Severe TBI patients presenting with GCS 3–4 and surgically evacuable mass lesions have extremely high mortality. In this cohort, surgical management was associated with improved survival and functional outcomes; however, given the retrospective design, non-randomized treatment allocation, and risk of selection bias and residual confounding, these findings should be interpreted with caution and do not establish a causal relationship. Careful patient selection remains essential, and further prospective studies are required to validate these observations.
