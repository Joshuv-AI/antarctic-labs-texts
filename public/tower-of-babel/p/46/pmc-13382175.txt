Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia secondary to diabetic ketoacidosis in a patient receiving olanzapine: A case report
Authors: Taiki Horie, Akitoshi Inoue, Yugo Imai, Fuyuki Nagano, Yoshiyuki Watanabe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-occlusive mesenteric ischemia is a life-threatening condition caused by mesenteric vasoconstriction without arterial occlusion. Early diagnosis remains challenging because of nonspecific clinical symptoms.Here, we report a case of non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia associated with a hyperglycemic crisis in a patient treated with olanzapine. A 29-year-old man presented with altered mental status, abdominal pain, and diarrhea. Laboratory assessment revealed severe hyperglycemia, ketosis, dehydration, and acute kidney injury. Non-contrast abdominal computed tomography (CT) demonstrated pneumatosis intestinalis. After four days, follow-up contrast-enhanced CT revealed diffuse lack of bowel wall enhancement consistent with severe bowel ischemia. No occlusive lesion of the superior mesenteric artery was identified.The patient was diagnosed with non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia based on imaging findings and systemic hypoperfusion due to diabetic ketoacidosis. This case highlights the limitations of non-contrast CT in suspected bowel ischemia and the importance of early contrast-enhanced CT, even in patients with renal dysfunction, because delayed diagnosis may lead to irreversible bowel necrosis.Keywords: Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia, Diabetic ketoacidosis, Pneumatosis intestinalis, Computed tomography, Olanzapine

Introduction

Non-occlusive mesenteric ischemia (NOMI) is an acute mesenteric ischemia subtype characterized by intestinal hypoperfusion without an organic occlusion of the main mesenteric arteries. It typically develops in the setting of systemic circulatory failure and mesenteric vasoconstriction and is associated with bowel necrosis, high mortality, and devastating sequelae, including short bowel syndrome (SBS) with profound quality of life impairment when diagnosis and definitive management are delayed [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Clinical symptoms and laboratory findings are frequently nonspecific; however, contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) plays an important role as a first-line modality for suspected acute mesenteric ischemia, with high diagnostic performance reported in systematic reviews and meta-analyses [5,6].

Historically, angiography has been considered the reference standard for evaluating mesenteric arterial vasoconstriction in NOMI. Narrowing at the origins of major superior mesenteric artery (SMA) branches, irregularity of branch caliber, spasm of mesenteric arcades, and impaired visualization of intramural vessels are catheter angiographic features [7]. However, with modern CT techniques, similar vascular findings can be noninvasively assessed [8]. Further, quantitative assessment of mesenteric vessel caliber has been proposed as a supportive diagnostic marker [[8], [9], [10]]. Recent reviews for interventional radiologists further highlight the importance of prompt CT-based triage to guide resuscitation, endovascular therapy consideration, and timely surgical consultation when irreversible ischemia is suspected [11,12].

Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA) may precipitate systemic hypoperfusion through severe osmotic diuresis and dehydration, thereby predisposing susceptible patients to NOMI. Case reports have described NOMI with intestinal necrosis occurring in the setting of DKA after antipsychotic treatment, including quetiapine [13]. Moreover, antipsychotic-associated DKA has been reported and evaluated in pharmacovigilance and clinical case literature, including cases involving olanzapine [14,15].

Contrast-enhanced CT is well established for the evaluation of mesenteric and bowel ischemia, whereas non-contrast CT is limited in identifying them. Non-contrast CT may underestimate the severity and extent of bowel ischemia because bowel wall enhancement and mesenteric perfusion cannot be adequately assessed. In this case, due to renal dysfunction and missed intestinal pneumatosis, only a non-contrast CT was initially performed. Hence, bowel wall enhancement and the extent of ischemia could not be directly assessed on contrast-enhanced CT. This case highlights an important diagnostic pitfall in the imaging workup of suspected NOMI and emphasizes the need for careful consideration of contrast-enhanced CT even in high-risk patients and a relatively rare cause, dehydration caused by DKA. Therefore, this case emphasizes the diagnostic challenges and the potential influence of early imaging decisions on patient outcomes.

Case presentation

A 29-year-old man with a history of depression visited the emergency department with altered mental status, abdominal pain, and diarrhea. He had been largely homebound and had experienced malaise, abdominal pain, and diarrhea for approximately 1 week. Three days before the presentation, he developed a fever and poor oral intake. His outpatient medications included escitalopram (Lexapro; Mochida Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan), olanzapine (Zyprexa; Cheplapharm Arzneimittel GmbH, Tokyo, Japan), estazolam (Eurodin; T’s Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan), and doxazosin (Cardenalin; Viatris Pharmaceuticals Japan G.K., Tokyo, Japan).

On arrival, vital signs were notable for hypotension (99/43 mmHg), tachypnea (35 breaths/min), and hypothermia (35.0°C). His consciousness level was E3V1M1 on the Glasgow Coma Scale. Further history revealed marked polydipsia for approximately 1 year (6 L/day) and polyuria for 3 months.

Initial laboratory tests revealed severe hyperglycemia and ketosis. Specifically, plasma glucose was 1,207 mg/dL with urine glucose (4+) and urine ketones (4+). Electrolytes indicated hyponatremia (Na 114 mmol/L), hyperkalemia (K 6.6 mmol/L), and hypochloremia (Cl 82 mmol/L). Renal function was impaired (blood urea nitrogen 80.6 mg/dL, creatinine 3.12 mg/dL; estimated glomerular filtration rate 21.3 mL/min/1.73 m2). Inflammatory markers were increased (C-reactive protein 6.56 mg/dL, procalcitonin 100 ng/mL). Creatine phosphokinase was markedly increased (creatine phosphokinase 4,170 U/L), and coagulation testing revealed increased fibrinogen (589 mg/dL) and D-dimer (2.8 ng/mL). These findings were consistent with a hyperglycemic crisis with dehydration and systemic hypoperfusion.

A 64-slice scanner (Aquilion ONE, Canon Medical Systems, Tochigi, Japan) was used for non-contrast CT on the day of admission and demonstrated slightly small caliber of the SMA and gas along the small-bowel wall, consistent with pneumatosis intestinalis (Fig. 1). The initial interpretation did not identify the intramural pneumatosis; thus, intravenous administration of iodine contrast media was not performed due to acute renal dysfunction. Adjusted window setting helps identify intramural pneumatosis. Abdominal pain and increased CK level were caused by DKA and neuroleptic malignant syndrome related to antipsychotic agents. The patient underwent intensive care management for a hyperglycemic crisis with dehydration and electrolyte abnormalities. Initial treatment included aggressive fluid resuscitation, insulin therapy, and electrolyte disturbance correction according to established treatment protocols for DKA [16]. Empirical antimicrobial therapy was initiated because infection could not be excluded at presentation.

Non-contrast computed tomography obtained on the day of admission. The superior mesenteric artery appears small in caliber, with a 4.3 mm diameter, although the finding is limited (A, arrowhead). Intramural pneumatosis is present in an approximately 15 cm segment of the small bowel (B, C arrows). Intramural pneumatosis is more obvious on the adjusted window setting (WL: −40, WW: 400) than the default one (WL: 40, WW: 350). Bowel wall thickening and ascites are not seen. During scanning, the patient kept his hands on his abdomen, possibly because of severe abdominal pain, despite instructions to raise them.

A follow-up contrast-enhanced CT was obtained 4 days after admission to investigate the cause of suspected adynamic ileus, as indicated on abdominal radiograph, and to rule out mechanical bowel obstruction. The contrast-enhanced CT scan demonstrated massive pneumatosis intestinalis, segmental high-attenuating bowel wall indicating hemorrhagic necrosis, and diffuse lack of enhancement in the bowel wall and massive ascites. Maximum intensity projection and volume rendering images illustrated narrowing of the main trunk of the SMA (Fig. 2). The patient was diagnosed with massive bowel necrosis caused by NOMI based on the clinical setting of hypovolemia due to hyperglycemic crisis and the CT pattern of segmental bowel ischemia without an identifiable arterial occlusion.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) obtained our days after admission. High attenuation of the bowel wall with 6 mm bowel thickness is seen on non-contrast-enhanced CT (A, asterisk). On contrast-enhanced CT, the bowel wall appears hyperattenuating, similar to non-contrast-enhanced CT, which is not contrast enhancement, but suggestive of hemorrhagic bowel necrosis (B, C, asterisks). Further, extensive pneumatosis is noted in the thin-walled small bowel, accompanied by diminished mural enhancement of the involved small bowel and right-sided colon (A–C, arrows). Maximum intensity projection and volume rendering images illustrate a narrowed distal main trunk of the superior mesenteric artery with a 2.6 mm diameter (D, E; arrows).

An emergency laparotomy was performed, considering the CT findings suggestive of massive intestinal ischemic necrosis. An exploration revealed approximately 3 L of turbid, bloody ascites accompanied by a large amount of contaminated fibrin clots. The small bowel appeared viable up to 1 m distal to the ligament of Treitz, whereas more distal segments demonstrated a blackish discoloration suggestive of necrosis. The large intestine was not suspected of necrosis. Indocyanine green fluorescence imaging revealed impaired blood flow extending from 90 cm distal to the ligament of Treitz to the ascending colon just beyond the cecum, prompting resection of the affected segment. The resected small bowel measured 240 cm, except for 90 cm of viable residual jejunum. A double-barrel stoma involving the residual jejunum and colon was created in the right lower quadrant, and the abdomen was closed.

Postoperatively, the patient was managed in the intensive care unit under a ventilator and received total parenteral nutrition for SBS. He was extubated on postoperative day (POD) 6. However, the patient’s course was complicated by suspected catheter-related bloodstream infection and aspiration pneumonia, which required antibiotic therapy. He had irreversible hypoxic encephalopathy. He was transferred to a long-term care hospital approximately 3 months postoperatively and died 5 months later due to severe sepsis secondary to disseminated intravascular coagulation and acute respiratory distress syndrome.

Discussion

NOMI is a subtype of acute mesenteric ischemia characterized by intestinal hypoperfusion without organic obstruction of the main mesenteric arteries. Mesenteric vasoconstriction typically occurs in the setting of systemic circulatory failure, especially in hospitalized patients with risk factors, including hemodialysis and post-cardiovascular surgery. NOMI is associated with high mortality when diagnosis is delayed [1,3]. Persistent vasoconstriction and impaired microcirculation may cause progressive intestinal ischemia and bowel necrosis [2]. Extensive bowel necrosis requiring large-volume resection may lead to SBS. SBS is characterized by a marked reduction in intestinal absorptive capacity, causing chronic malnutrition and dehydration, and frequently requires long-term parenteral nutrition. This condition is associated with significant complications, including catheter-related infections and liver dysfunction, and has markedly impaired the quality of life. Furthermore, previous studies have demonstrated that SBS adversely influences long-term survival and functional outcomes [17,18].

Clinical symptoms are often nonspecific; thus, imaging plays a crucial role in diagnosing NOMI. Angiography has historically been considered the reference standard for detecting mesenteric vasoconstriction [7]; however, multidetector CT now enables noninvasive evaluation of both mesenteric vascular changes and bowel ischemia [6,8]. Characteristic CT findings include both vascular and bowel abnormalities. Vessel abnormality is the pathophysiology of NOMI, characterized as mesenteric vessel narrowing, but no vascular abnormality is observed after remission vasoconstriction [12]. Previous studies have demonstrated that the diameter of the SMA and vein may be smaller in patients with NOMI compared with normal controls, although individual variation limits the use of absolute cutoff values [[8], [9], [10]]. Bowel abnormalities include pneumatosis intestinalis, which is inconclusive of bowel necrosis because various benign conditions are associated with intestinal pneumatosis, and the patient’s symptoms and other imaging findings indicating mesenteric or bowel ischemia are clues to differentiate benign and life-threatening pneumatosis. A hyperattenuating bowel wall, a paper-thin bowel wall, and diminished bowel wall hyperenhancement are highly suggestive of transmural bowel necrosis (Fig. 3). A hyperattenuating bowel wall is observed on non-contrast CT. Side-by-side comparison of non-contrast CT and contrast-enhanced CT is essential to distinguish hyperattenuating bowel wall and enhanced bowel wall (Fig. 2) [19]. Therefore, early CT recognition is essential for prompt resuscitation and appropriate surgical or endovascular management [11,12]. Pneumatosis on initial non-contrast-enhanced CT in this case was a clue to suspect NOMI, although pneumatosis may occur in various causes ranging from benign to life-threatening conditions [20].

Schematic illustration of bowel wall findings in reperfusion and delayed reperfusion. Decreased or absent bowel wall enhancement reflects impaired arterial inflow in the early stage of arterial bowel ischemia. A target sign is typically observed when reperfusion occurs before developing bowel necrosis. In cases of bowel necrosis after arterial ischemia, imaging findings may include hemorrhagic necrosis, pneumatosis, and a paper-thin bowel wall, indicating advanced ischemic injury and transmural necrosis.

In this case, intestinal pneumatosis was not identified, and increased CK levels due to bowel necrosis were misinterpreted as being related to DKA and neuroleptic malignant syndrome associated with antipsychotic agents. Hence, follow-up contrast-enhanced CT and laparotomy were delayed. Intestinal pneumatosis in this case is less obvious on the default window setting for abdominal CT. Adjusting the window setting helps identify intramural gas (Fig. 1C). In retrospect, despite the presence of acute kidney injury, earlier contrast-enhanced CT should have been considered in patients with symptomatic intestinal pneumatosis because current evidence supports the use of contrast-enhanced CT in life-threatening conditions, including suspected mesenteric ischemia, where delayed diagnosis may cause irreversible bowel necrosis [21,22].

In the present case, severe hyperglycemia with ketosis and dehydration indicated a hyperglycemic crisis consistent with DKA. Hyperglycemic crises cause profound osmotic diuresis and intravascular volume depletion, leading to systemic hypoperfusion and compensatory mesenteric vasoconstriction that may precipitate NOMI [1,3]. The patient was also receiving antipsychotic medication, including olanzapine, which has been associated with hyperglycemia and DKA [[13], [14], [15]], and an increased CK level was wrongly considered as a result of DKA and neuroleptic malignant syndrome. Previous reports have described NOMI as an antipsychotic-associated DKA, including cases related to quetiapine therapy [13].

Conclusion

We report a case of delayed diagnosis of NOMI resulting in massive bowel necrosis and SBS, presenting in the setting of severe hyperglycemic crisis with ketosis, dehydration, and systemic hypoperfusion. Adjusting window settings helps identify intestinal pneumatosis, and proceeding with subsequent contrast-enhanced CT is critical in pneumatosis with patient symptoms and imaging findings associated with mesenteric and bowel ischemia, even in renal dysfunction (Table 1).
