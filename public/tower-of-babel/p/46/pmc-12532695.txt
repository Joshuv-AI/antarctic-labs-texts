Plasma proteomic associations with Alzheimer’s disease endophenotypes
Authors: Shiva Afshar, Eric B. Dammer, Shijia Bian, David A. Bennett, Richard Mohs, Doug Beauregard, John Dwyer, Chadwick M. Hales, Felicia C. Goldstein, Monica W. Parker, Antoine R. Trammell, Caroline M. Watson, Todd E. Golde, Nicholas T. Seyfried, Blaine R. Roberts, Cecelia M. Manzanares, James J. Lah, Allan I. Levey, Erik C. B. Johnson
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Clinical Alzheimer’s disease is currently characterized by cerebral β-amyloidosis associated with cognitive impairment. However, most cases of Alzheimer’s disease are associated with multiple neuropathologies at autopsy. The peripheral protein changes associated with these disease endophenotypes are poorly understood. In this study, we analyzed the plasma proteomes of individuals from four cohorts (n = 2,139 participants) to identify proteins and pathways associated with cerebral β-amyloidosis and other neuropathologies, including tau, Lewy bodies, TDP43, cerebral amyloid angiopathy, atherosclerosis, arteriolosclerosis and infarcts as well as cognitive function. Analyses in a cohort with paired brain data showed that known neuropathologies could account for only half of proteins associated with cognitive function and that many plasma proteins associated with these neuropathologies are not strongly correlated to levels in brain, suggesting a potential contribution of peripheral factors to the development of Alzheimer’s disease endophenotypes. Targeting pathways represented by these peripheral proteins may modify Alzheimer’s disease risk or disease progression.

Main

Current clinical practice operationalizes Alzheimer’s disease (AD) as the presence of cerebral amyloid-β (Aβ) plaques (β-amyloidosis) and cognitive impairment in the proper clinical context, whereas asymptomatic (or preclinical) AD is defined as cerebral β-amyloidosis in the absence of cognitive impairment according to the 2024 Alzheimer’s Association Workgroup revised diagnostic criteria1. Patients who have β-amyloidosis as assessed through imaging, cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) or plasma biomarkers and cognitive impairment in the proper clinical context are candidates to receive anti-amyloid immunotherapy to slow AD progression2,3. Clinical trials are underway to assess the efficacy of anti-amyloid immunotherapy in asymptomatic AD4. However, neuropathologies in addition to β-amyloidosis are commonly observed at autopsy in AD brain tissue and are the rule rather than the exception5–7. Assessment for the presence of other neuropathologies is not required in the current AD clinical diagnostic framework, although both the 2018 National Institute on Aging and 2024 Alzheimer’s Association diagnostic criteria recommend the inclusion of other co-pathologies in the amyloid, tau and neurodegeneration diagnostic framework as appropriate1,8. Neuropathologies other than amyloid and tau are associated with cognitive impairment and contribute to variability in disease presentation. Such neuropathologies include those that can be observed by immunohistochemical (IHC) techniques such as aggregates of α-synuclein (Lewy bodies), aggregates of TAR DNA-binding protein 43 (TDP43) and microvascular disease, and those that have been identified through unbiased omics approaches such as proteomics that are less easily captured by IHC, such as extracellular matrix (ECM) changes, RNA splicing defects, alterations in proteostasis, synaptic changes and changes in sugar metabolism and mitochondria9. Advancement of more effective therapeutics and personalized treatment approaches for AD will likely require treatment and prevention of these non-Aβ and tau co-pathologies. A key challenge for the development of non-Aβ-targeted AD therapeutic approaches is the development of non-invasive biomarkers for their presence and evolution over the course of the disease.

Recent studies using CSF and plasma proteomics have suggested that alterations in multiple proteins and their associated biological pathways are associated with AD pathology10–15 and can occur decades before the onset of cognitive symptoms in AD16–19. Some of these changes are associated with the strongest population-level genetic risk factor for AD, APOE ε4. These proteomic observations, supported by brain imaging changes many years prior to symptoms20–22, suggest that AD is a chronic disease that evolves over decades. However, the extent to which plasma proteomic changes are associated with the multiple endophenotypes that characterize and are associated with AD is not clear. It is also unclear whether changes in plasma proteins associated with AD and AD risk are directly observable in brain and CSF compartments or whether such changes are restricted to plasma and influence AD through peripheral mechanisms. The possibility that peripheral factors can influence AD pathophysiology is suggested from the observed beneficial effects of lifestyle modification on AD symptoms23 as well as from animal modeling studies where introduction of blood from young mice into AD model mice can modify AD brain pathology24. Such peripheral systemic influences on central nervous system (CNS) AD pathology suggest the possibility that disease modification for multiple AD-associated endophenotypes could occur without direct targeting of the brain and the difficulties associated with developing therapeutics that cross the blood–brain barrier.

To better understand the role of biological pathways in the periphery and how they influence AD and its heterogeneous pathophysiology, here we assess associations of approximately 7,000 plasma proteins with multiple AD neuropathologies and endophenotypes, including cognitive function. We also assess how pathways in the periphery are related to those in brain and CSF compartments.

Results

Plasma protein associations with cerebral β-amyloidosis

We analyzed 2,645 plasma proteomes from 2,139 individuals across four separate cohorts using the SomaLogic SomaScan 7K aptamer-based platform, which allowed us to quantify the levels of 6,345 proteins using 7,287 aptamer assays without missing values across these samples. Plasma samples were obtained from the Bio-Hermes study25, the Emory Goizueta Alzheimer’s Disease Research Center (GADRC), the Religious Orders Study and Rush Memory and Aging Project (ROSMAP)26 as well as a collection of clinical research studies at Emory University (Emory Other) (Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 1). All cohorts contained participants with and without cognitive impairment and biomarkers of cerebral Aβ. Bio-Hermes had amyloid positron emission tomography (PET) and plasma pTau217 measurements available for nearly all participants to assess the presence and levels of cerebral Aβ. Emory GADRC participants had plasma pTau217 measurements, and Emory Other participants had CSF Aβ and tau measurements to assess levels of cerebral Aβ deposition. All ROSMAP participants underwent autopsy and had direct measurement of Aβ plaque burden. In ROSMAP, we restricted neuropathology correlations with plasma protein measurements to within 3 years of autopsy, which comprised about half of the ROSMAP participants available in our dataset (n = 221 out of 436). We tested whether the associations in the ROSMAP cohort with cerebral Aβ were significantly affected by time to autopsy within 3 years and found that only 2% of proteins were significantly affected, with none of the most strongly associated proteins affected by time between blood draw and autopsy. This finding is consistent with the slow evolution of amyloid pathology that occurs over decades27. We first assessed the association of plasma proteins to cerebral Aβ separately in each cohort. Using linear models, we associated each plasma protein measurement with the Aβ PET standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) in Bio-Hermes, plasma pTau217 in Emory GADRC, CSF total Tau (tTau)/Aβ42 in Emory Other and a composite measure of neuritic and diffuse plaque load in ROSMAP, adjusting for age, sex and race as needed in each cohort based on exploratory analyses (Supplementary Table 2). To leverage information about associations with β-amyloidosis across cohorts and assessment methods, we performed a meta-analysis of results across all four cohorts. After adjustment for false discovery, 214 proteins were observed to be significantly associated with β-amyloidosis in the meta-analysis, with 131 proteins positively associated and 83 proteins negatively associated (Fig. 2a and Supplementary Table 2). Most of the proteins identified as significantly associated with Aβ plaque using a quantitative linear modeling approach were also observed as significant in a logistic meta-analysis model in which β-amyloidosis was defined using a binary cutoff in each cohort (Extended Data Fig. 1b and Supplementary Table 3). SPC25 was the plasma protein most strongly associated with β-amyloidosis. Gene Ontology analysis showed that plasma proteins positively associated with cerebral β-amyloidosis were enriched in synapse and ECM pathways, whereas proteins negatively associated with β-amyloidosis were enriched in metabolism and proteostasis pathways (Fig. 2d). Because APOE ε4 genotype is strongly positively associated with β-amyloidosis, we assessed which amyloid-associated proteins were dependent upon APOE ε4 (Supplementary Tables 2 and 3). Fifty-six proteins remained significantly associated with β-amyloidosis in the linear model after adjustment for APOE ε4, with complexin 2 (CPLX2) remaining the most strongly associated protein (Fig. 2a,b). One protein—matrilysin (MMP7)—gained significance in association after adjustment. Therefore, approximately 75% of proteins were no longer significantly associated with amyloid after APOE ε4 adjustment, including SPC25 (Fig. 2c and Supplementary Table 2). We assessed overlap of the Aβ-associated APOE ε4-dependent proteins in plasma with proteins previously identified as associated with AD and ε4 dependent in serum in the AGES-Reykjavik cohort16 (Fig. 2e). Approximately half of the ε4-dependent proteins in serum associated with AD overlapped with plasma amyloid-associated ε4-dependent proteins, suggesting that these proteins are robustly specific for the β-amyloidosis AD endophenotype regardless of blood matrix sampling. Aβ plaque-associated proteins after ε4 adjustment reflected similar biological pathways to those without adjustment (Extended Data Fig. 1c). This was also observed for ε4-dependent proteins, although ε4-dependent proteins negatively associated with amyloid were more enriched in neuronal, apoptosis and endosome pathways (Extended Data Fig. 1d). Adjusting for cognitive function in addition to APOE ε4 further reduced the number of proteins associated with cerebral Aβ to 13, highlighting proteins associated with cerebral Aβ regardless of level of cognitive impairment (Extended Data Fig. 1e,f). In summary, we identified plasma proteins robustly associated with cerebral β-amyloidosis across different cohorts and biomarker outcome measurements. Overlap with brain Aβ plaque-associated proteins

To determine the relationship between plasma proteins associated with Aβ plaque and brain proteins associated with Aβ plaque, we assessed overlap of plasma proteins associated with Aβ plaque with those directly measured in brain in prior mass spectrometry-based studies9,28. Seventeen proteins out of 425 plaque-enriched or plaque-depleted proteins in brain identified by laser capture microdissection mass spectrometry-based proteomics of Aβ plaques were found in common with plasma proteins associated with Aβ plaque (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 4). Proteins such as SPARC-related modular calcium-binding protein 1 (SMOC1), spondin 1 (SPON1) and alpha-1-antichymotrypsin (SERPINA3) demonstrated concordance between brain and plasma, with enrichment in cerebral plaque and plasma levels positively associated with Aβ plaque burden. Others such as APOE, serine protease HTRA1 (HTRA1) and serum amyloid P-component (APCS) were discordant in brain and plasma, with enrichment in brain plaques but plasma levels negatively associated with plaque. CPLX1 and zinc-alpha-2-glycoprotein (AZGP1) were also discordant, with depletion in cerebral plaque but plasma levels positively associated with plaques. We also assessed overlap with plaque-associated brain proteins based on a prior consensus co-expression mass spectrometry-based analysis9 (Extended Data Fig. 1g). SMOC1, SPON1 and pleiotrophin (PTN) were concordant in association, whereas APOE and HTRA1 were discordant in association, consistent with the laser capture microdissection data. Using previously generated mass spectrometry proteomic data from ROSMAP dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (DLPFC)9, we also tested the association between plasma protein levels as measured by SomaScan and brain protein levels as measured by mass spectrometry for all overlapping proteins and not those just associated with plaques, restricting the analysis to within 3 years of autopsy and adjusting for time between blood draw and autopsy. We identified 150 plasma proteins positively associated and 87 plasma proteins negatively associated at a nominal statistical threshold with levels in brain (Extended Data Fig. 1h and Supplementary Table 5). Positively associated proteins were enriched in complement, immunoglobulins, reduction–oxidation and high-density lipoprotein pathways, whereas negatively associated proteins were enriched in innate immune response, protein transport, autophagy, actin and Golgi pathways (Extended Data Fig. 1i). Nine proteins overlapped between Aβ-associated and brain-linked proteins in plasma, including acetylcholinesterase (ACHE) (Extended Data Fig. 1j). In summary, we identified cerebral Aβ-associated plasma proteins that were both concordant and discordant with their association with Aβ plaque in brain. A limited number of Aβ-associated plasma proteins were found to be linked to brain levels within individuals in the ROSMAP cohort, suggesting that the relationship between plasma and brain levels for these nine proteins is highly robust across cohorts, proteomic platforms and time to autopsy.

Diagnostic utility of plasma proteomic measurements for cerebral β-amyloidosis

To determine whether the available plasma proteomic measurements could be used as a diagnostic marker for cerebral Aβ, we tested the ability of a select number of proteins to discriminate Aβ-positive (Aβ+) and Aβ-negative (Aβ−) participants in each cohort. We searched the top 42 most strongly positively and top 42 most strongly negatively Aβ-associated proteins from the meta-analysis to develop combinatorial protein ratios that best predicted Aβ status, as previously described for proteomic prediction of drug response12, with the goal of developing a single value as a predictor of Aβ status that could leverage information from multiple Aβ-associated proteins. The best one-protein, two-protein, three-protein and four-protein ratios (n = 2, 4, 6 and 8 total proteins) are provided in Supplementary Table 6. The discrimination accuracy for the best four-protein ratio (SPC25+CPLX2+SMOC1+ACHE/NEFL+VPS29+PTPRU+NOMO2) reached approximately 80% area under the curve (AUC) in the Bio-Hermes, Emory GADRC and Emory Other cohorts, with lower accuracy in ROSMAP (Extended Data Fig. 2a,b). This was lower than pTau217, which had an AUC of approximately 90% in Bio-Hermes for amyloid positivity based on PET. The accuracy improved slightly if ratios were developed that were specific to each cohort rather than using the meta-analysis results, with the largest increase in accuracy observed in ROSMAP (approximately 64–81%) and the highest accuracy in Emory Other (90%) (Extended Data Fig. 2c). Because many of the top Aβ-associated plasma proteins were dependent upon APOE ε4, and not all Aβ+ participants carry an ε4 allele, we also explored whether the discrimination accuracy would improve if ratios were developed using amyloid-associated proteins less dependent on ε4 and used in only non-ε4 individuals for amyloid prediction. Discrimination accuracy did not improve using proteins less influenced by ε4 in non-ε4 individuals (Extended Data Fig. 2d and Supplementary Table 6). These results suggest that the proteins measured on the SomaScan 7K platform cannot currently outperform plasma pTau217 as a discriminator of cerebral Aβ positivity.

Plasma proteomic associations with cognitive function

We performed a similar analysis across cohorts to investigate the associations of plasma proteins with cognitive function, using the Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE) in Bio-Hermes and Emory GADRC, the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) in Emory Other and a composite measure of cognitive function in ROSMAP. One protein, the neuronal pentraxin receptor (NPTXR), was nominally significantly positively associated with cognitive function across all cohorts (Extended Data Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 7). After meta-analysis, synaptic vesicle membrane protein VAT-1 homolog (VAT1) and amyloid-β precursor-like protein 1 (APLP1) were most strongly positively associated with cognitive function (higher levels associated with better cognitive function), whereas ACHE and SPC25 were most strongly negatively associated with cognitive function (higher levels associated with worse cognitive function) (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 7). Pathways associated with better cognitive function included protein translation, cadherins and exosome, whereas pathways associated with worse cognitive function included synapse, immune response and ECM (Extended Data Fig. 3b). Out of a total of 283 proteins associated with cognitive function, 42 were also significantly associated with cerebral β-amyloidosis (Fig. 3b and Supplementary Table 7). After adjusting for Aβ, VAT1 and APLP1 remained significantly positively associated, and ACHE remained significantly negatively associated with cognitive function (Fig. 3a and Supplementary Table 7). The top proteins whose association with cognitive function was dependent upon Aβ levels are shown in Fig. 3c. Proteins positively associated with cognitive function independent of cerebral β-amyloidosis were enriched in protein translation, vesicular transport, cadherin, exosome and mitochondrial pathways, whereas proteins negatively associated with cognitive function independently of amyloid were enriched in inflammation and chemokines, fatty acid metabolism, ECM and endocytosis pathways (Fig. 3d). When comparing proteins associated with cognitive function from the meta-analysis with brain-linked proteins from ROSMAP, we identified 11 proteins that overlapped, including apolipoprotein A-IV (APOA4) and Ras-related protein Rab-1A (RAB1A) that were positively associated with cognitive function and ACHE that was negatively associated with cognitive function (Fig. 3e and Supplementary Table 7). Plasma proteomic associations with other neuropathologies

We used the rich neuropathological data from ROSMAP to identify plasma protein associations with multiple different neuropathologies commonly observed in AD cases at autopsy, including Lewy bodies, TDP43, cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) and different types of vascular disease (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Table 8), adjusting for age, sex and race as needed but without adjustment for other pathologies. Biological processes enriched in the plasma protein associations for each neuropathology are provided in Supplementary Fig. 1. We then identified proteins associated with each neuropathology that were also associated with global cognitive function within the ROSMAP cohort. We found that approximately half of the proteins associated with cognitive function in ROSMAP (255 out of 496) were also associated with at least one neuropathology (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Table 8). The other half not associated with measured neuropathologies were enriched for multiple pathways, including T cell biology, glucose metabolism, Golgi and glycosylation, fibronectin and mitochondria (Extended Data Fig. 3c). A large majority of plasma proteins associated with each neuropathology were not significantly correlated with brain levels (Extended Data Fig. 4 and Supplementary Table 8). In ROSMAP, four proteins associated with cognitive function and significantly brain linked were found to be in common with meta-analysis proteins identified in the same analysis and included tumor protein D54 (TPD52L2), alpha-1,3/1,6-mannosyltransferase (ALG2), kininogen 1 (KNG1) and eukaryotic initiation factor 4A-II (EIF4A2) (Fig. 3g). ALG2, KNG1 and EIF4A2 were also not associated with the neuropathologies assessed in ROSMAP (Fig. 3h), indicating their link to other brain processes associated with cognitive function not assessed by neuropathological examination in ROSMAP.

In a separate analysis, we identified cognitively impaired participants who were amyloid negative in Bio-Hermes (254 participants), Emory GADRC (60 participants) and Emory Other (16 participants) cohorts (CI.other) and the plasma proteins associated with this diagnostic state. A total of 211 proteins were associated with CI.other after meta-analysis (Extended Data Fig. 5a and Supplementary Table 9). Proteins positively associated with CI.other were enriched in leukocyte chemotaxis, ECM, heparin binding, cytokine and proteostasis pathways, with immune and ECM pathways found to be in common with pathways negatively associated with cognitive function after adjustment for cerebral Aβ as noted above. Proteins negatively associated with CI.other were enriched in nucleic acid metabolism and peptidase activity pathways, among others (Extended Data Fig. 5b). We observed minimal overlap between proteins associated with CI.other and proteins associated with different neuropathologies in ROSMAP (Extended Data Fig. 5c and Supplementary Table 9), consistent with the findings observed within the ROSMAP-only analysis.

In summary, we identified plasma proteins associated with multiple different neuropathologic endophenotypes commonly observed in AD as well as with cognitive impairment in the absence of cerebral Aβ. Proteins associated with these neuropathologic endophenotypes constituted approximately half of the plasma proteins associated with cognitive function, indicating that a large fraction of plasma protein associations with cognitive function is not accounted for by existing neuropathologic assessments.

Plasma proteomic associations with risk of cognitive decline

Longitudinal data were available in the Emory GADRC and ROSMAP cohorts. Approximately 30 participants in the Emory GADRC cohort were cognitively normal (control) at the first plasma collection but converted to mild cognitive impairment or AD at a later visit, and, therefore, because of the low conversion numbers and the fact that the Emory GADRC data did not meet statistical assumptions for Cox proportional hazard analysis, we instead focused on the ROSMAP cohort for risk analyses in which there were 172 participants who became cognitively impaired as determined by a change in diagnostic status out of a total of 310 selected for analysis. In the risk analyses, cognitive decline did not have to occur within 3 years of autopsy. Although no proteins survived correction for multiple comparisons, proteins whose levels at baseline were nominally associated with increased risk of all-cause cognitive decline were enriched in ECM, heparin binding, cytokines and immune cells (Fig. 4a,d and Supplementary Table 10), regardless of pathology at autopsy in converters and non-converters. When converters were restricted to those who had a sufficiently high degree of AD pathology confirmed on autopsy (n = 131)9, proteins that nominally increased risk for cognitive decline were associated with heparin binding, ECM, fibronectin and muscle (Fig. 4b,e and Supplementary Table 10). Further restricting the analysis to include only those non-converters with low levels of Aβ and tau pathology on autopsy (total n = 187 with n = 131 converters), we identified Erk signaling, axon regeneration, myelination, T cell, cytokine and SNARE biology nominally associated with increased risk of cognitive decline (Fig. 4c,f and Supplementary Table 10). Proteins in common among the three analytical approaches that either increased or decreased risk of cognitive decline were enriched in cell recognition, T cell, cytokine/immune, glycosaminoglycan binding, ECM and post-synapse pathways (Fig. 4g, Extended Data Fig. 6a and Supplementary Table 10). We assessed for overlap of proteins in ROSMAP associated with risk of cognitive decline at a nominally significant level (all converters) with proteins associated with risk of cognitive decline in the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) cohort18,19, and we identified CPLX1, ephrin type-A receptor 2 (EPHA2) and collagen alpha-1(X) chain (COL10A1) that were commonly associated with increased risk of cognitive decline (Fig. 4h). We performed the same analysis with the AGES-Reykjavik cohort16 in which conversions were defined as AD based on clinical evaluation and observed SMOC1, tissue factor pathway inhibitor (TFPI), myomesin 2 (MYOM2) and cyclic GMP-AMP phosphodiesterase SMPDL3A (SMPDL3A) as associated with increased risk of conversion and linker for activation of T cells family member 2 (LAT2) and anaphase-promoting complex subunit 10 (ANAPC10) as associated with decreased risk of conversion (Fig. 4i). COL10A1, EPHA2, MYOM2 and LAT2 survived five-fold cross-validation (Extended Data Fig. 6b,c). Association of AD endophenotypes with plasma protein co-expression modules

As a separate approach to identify the biological pathways associated with neuropathological and cognitive AD endophenotypes in plasma, we applied a systems biology technique called protein co-expression analysis to reduce the dimensionality of the plasma proteomic data into clusters, or ‘modules’, of proteins defined by their co-abundance across cases9,11,12,29. These clusters represent biological processes identified in a data-dependent fashion based on the underlying plasma proteomic measurements with which we could then assess for enrichment in proteins identified to be associated with each AD endophenotype. A co-expression network built from all individuals across the four cohorts identified 27 modules representing diverse biological pathways and processes (Fig. 5, Extended Data Fig. 8 and Supplementary Table 11), with most modules having clear primary ontologies. Comparison of our network to a previously published serum network from the AGES-Reykjavik cohort16 showed excellent overlap, particularly in lipoprotein, complement and neuronal modules (Extended Data Fig. 7 and Supplementary Table 12). Proteins associated with cerebral β-amyloidosis were most strongly enriched in the M16 cholesterol/lipid/APOE module, as expected, but also showed strong enrichment in M19 insulin growth factor binding, M17 small molecule metabolism, M4 ECM/TNF/neuron development and M5 neuron development modules. Using proteins associated with different neuropathologies as assessed in the ROSMAP-specific analyses to test for module enrichment, M4 ECM/TNF/neuron development also showed strong enrichment for proteins associated with Aβ plaques, whereas M12 CXCR chemokine/neutrophil chemotaxis, M16 cholesterol/lipid/APOE and M3 mitosis regulation/transcription modules showed the strongest enrichment for proteins associated with tau tangles. Proteins associated with TDP43 deposits were enriched in M3 mitosis regulation/transcription and M2 transcription factor/DNA binding/receptor ligand activity modules, whereas proteins associated with cerebral atherosclerosis were associated with M16 cholesterol/lipid/APOE, among other modules. Proteins associated with Lewy bodies did not show significant enrichment in network modules except for M25, which did not have a clear primary ontology. Modules that showed the strongest enrichment for proteins involved in risk for cognitive decline included M18 coagulation/complement/wound healing, M3 mitosis regulation/transcription, M21 skeletal muscle, M4 ECM/TNF/neuron development and M5 neuron development. In summary, we observed more overlap in co-expression between plasma and CSF compartments than plasma and brain compartments. More plasma proteins associated with cerebral β-amyloidosis had overlap with CSF modules compared to brain modules, but plasma proteins associated with cognitive function had similar degree of overlap with both brain and CSF modules. Discussion

In this study, we analyzed the plasma proteomes of more than 2,000 participants from four separate cohorts using a diverse set of outcome measures to better understand the proteins and biological pathways in blood associated with AD and its associated endophenotypes. The results from our analysis highlight the key associations of synaptic, ECM, metabolic and proteostasis proteins with cerebral Aβ deposition and the strong influence of APOE ε4 genotype and lipoprotein biology on this pathology; the importance of protein translation and degradation, mitochondria, inflammation and ECM pathways for cognitive function; and the importance of immune system, ECM and synaptic proteins in blood for prediction of cognitive decline. Many plasma proteins associated with cognitive function are not associated with brain pathology as assessed by neuropathological techniques, suggesting the importance of peripheral mechanisms for cognitive decline and the likely presence of AD-associated neuropathology silent to current assessment techniques. The relationship among blood, CSF and brain compartments for AD endophenotype associations is concordant for some biological pathways, such as ECM, and discordant for others, such as metabolism and synapse. These findings highlight the particular importance of ECM, synaptic, energy metabolism, inflammation and proteostasis pathways in AD and the importance of studying proteins associated with these processes in multiple compartments. We discuss some of the important biological domains and pathways identified in our study below.

ECM

Our previous proteomics studies in AD brain and CSF highlighted the importance of the ECM in disease pathophysiology9,11,12,14. The results from the present study show that the ECM as measured in plasma is also strongly and independently associated with two key AD endophenotypes: cerebral β-amyloidosis and cognitive decline. ECM proteins that are strongly associated with Aβ plaques in brain tissue, such as SMOC1 and SPON1, are also elevated in AD plasma, whereas other Aβ-associated proteins, such as HTRA1, are elevated in brain but decreased in plasma. Many of the same ECM proteins in plasma are also associated with CAA as assessed in the ROSMAP cohort. Mechanistic studies that dissect how these ECM proteins influence Aβ accumulation are currently underway and will be important to further understanding of how they may influence Aβ toxicity30. However, we also observed association of the ECM with cognitive function and risk of incident cognitive decline even after adjusting for Aβ plaque burden in multiple analyses, indicating that changes in the ECM may affect this key AD endophenotype even in the absence of altered Aβ metabolism or potentially precede it. One such mechanism that could mediate the effect of the ECM on cognitive function may be loss of vascular integrity, as we observed an association between the ECM and microinfarcts, in addition to CAA. Increased levels of ECM proteins were previously observed in AD cerebrovasculature31. In autosomal dominantly inherited AD (ADAD), CSF levels of ECM proteins SMOC1 and SPON1 appear to change contemporaneously with changes in CSF Aβ levels, highlighting early changes in these proteins even in young individuals genetically predisposed to develop AD17. Plasma levels of SMOC1 were associated with incident cognitive decline in our study and were also associated with incident dementia in the AGES-Reykjavik cohort. How these proteins influence cognitive function in the presence and absence of cerebral Aβ is a key area for future research.

Proteostasis

Separate from the ECM, proteostasis as assessed in plasma was also found to be a key biological process associated with cerebral β-amyloidosis and cognitive function. There are two aspects to proteostasis: protein synthesis and protein clearance. Protein synthesis (translation) was positively associated with cognitive function. The protein translation module in CSF also highly overlapped with plasma proteins positively associated with cognitive function, suggesting concordance between plasma and CSF compartments. Interestingly, this pathway in brain was not as enriched for plasma proteins positively associated with cognitive function, suggesting that, perhaps, protein translation represents a more systemic indicator of health associated with better cognitive function. Conversely, protein clearance processes in brain were highly enriched in proteins positively associated with cognitive function in plasma. Plasma proteins associated with protein degradation were negatively associated with cognitive function, with a more complex relationship in CSF (M4 autophagy/ubiquitination) depending on adjustment for amyloid. Autophagy was a process that appeared to be discordant between brain and plasma proteins in ROSMAP. The extent to which overall protein synthesis and protein degradation pathways are connected between the brain and periphery requires further study, but interventions that restore or maintain proper proteostasis in the brain or systemically are likely to have strong disease-modifying effects in AD and likely in other neurodegenerative diseases.

Immune

Immune processes in brain have long been recognized as a key contributor to AD pathophysiology32. Our results show that immune processes as assessed in plasma are also strongly associated with AD. Immune processes were strongly enriched in proteins negatively associated with cognitive function, suggesting that activation of such immune processes in the periphery is detrimental to cognitive function. This result was observed with or without accounting for the presence of cerebral β-amyloidosis. Furthermore, activation of the immune system was a risk factor for the development of incident cognitive impairment in cognitively normal individuals. These findings are consistent with studies that identified an association between severe non-CNS infections and increased risk of dementia33,34 as well as with studies that showed an association between surgical procedures and cognitive impairment mediated by increased brain inflammation35. In ADAD, we previously observed a temporal association between immune system activation as measured in the CSF and cognitive decline17. In the current plasma study, we found that proteins in the M12 CXCR chemokine/neutrophil chemotaxis module strongly overlapped with proteins positively associated with tau tangles in ROSMAP. Others have observed expansion of peripheral T cells in AD and T cells associated with tau pathology in human brain and mouse models36–38. Although the M12 module, the M4 ECM/TNF/neuron development module and ontology terms that involve leukocytes such as T cells were observed in our analysis, the exact nature of the immune system response in the periphery that is associated with cognitive decline requires further study.

Synaptic

The only protein commonly associated with cognitive function across all four cohorts was the synaptic protein NPTXR. Increased levels of NPTXR in plasma were associated with better cognitive function. The neuropentraxins and their receptor are well-known AD CSF biomarkers associated with cognitive decline39,40. In ADAD, lower CSF levels of NPTX2 and NPTXR are observed at the onset of symptoms and decrease with worsening cognitive function. Lower levels of NPTX2 have been associated with complement-mediated synaptic loss41. Although higher levels of NPTXR were associated with better cognitive function, we observed a diversity of neuronal protein associations in our study with AD endophenotypes. Synaptic proteins were positively associated with cerebral Aβ levels and were positive risk factors for incident cognitive decline, yet the M5 neuron development module was enriched for proteins positively associated with cognitive function. Similar to our observations with M5, recent studies have observed that a decrease in the neuron projection regeneration pathway in plasma is associated with accelerated brain aging42,43. We previously observed both positive and negative neuronal protein associations in AD CSF11,14, and this phenomenon appears to also occur in plasma. It is possible that levels of some neuronal and synaptic proteins in plasma may be positively associated with synaptic and neuronal levels in brain as a marker of general brain health, whereas increased levels of other synaptic and neuronal proteins in plasma may indicate damage to neurons. Identifying what specific normal biological or pathological process each group of neuronal-derived proteins in plasma reflects in brain will be important for their use as disease biomarkers.

Metabolism

In our previous studies, we identified glycolytic metabolism as strongly associated with cognitive decline in both AD brain and CSF, with increased levels of the brain M7 MAPK/metabolism module and the CSF M20 glycolysis/redox homeostasis modules associated with cognitive impairment9,12. However, similar to the discordant pattern of some synaptic protein associations by compartment, plasma proteins positively associated with cognitive function were enriched in these brain and CSF modules, indicating an inverse relationship between plasma and brain/CSF for these associations. Our prior brain proteomic study suggested that glycolytic proteins are related to astrocyte and microglial activation in AD brain29. We did not have a neuropathologic assessment of glial activation in our analysis to test this potential association, but glucose metabolism was a pathway for proteins related to cognitive function that were not associated with a neuropathologic measure in ROSMAP (along with T cell biology, Golgi and glycosylation, fibronectin and mitochondria proteins). The inverse relationship in plasma may reflect a non-neuronal source of these proteins in the periphery, potentially reflecting lower overall metabolic flux in a diseased state. Numerous observations in our data could support this hypothesis: (1) PHGDH, one of the four proteins commonly associated with cerebral β-amyloidosis across cohorts, is involved in glycolytic flux and was observed to be negatively associated with cerebral Aβ; (2) general small-molecule metabolism was negatively associated with Aβ levels in brain; (3) mitochondrial proteins were positively associated with cognitive function; and (4) skeletal muscle proteins were associated with risk of incident cognitive impairment, perhaps reflecting a general failure to thrive prior to cognitive decline. Alternatively, transport of glycolytic metabolic proteins across the blood–brain barrier could be a regulated process. This may also be the case for some neuronal proteins that show discordant associations between compartments.

Lipoproteins and APOEε4-related proteins

Lipoprotein biology was strongly associated with cerebral β-amyloidosis and cognitive function. The lipoprotein module is a very well-defined module in both plasma and serum and is highly enriched in proteins associated with cerebral Aβ and cerebral atherosclerosis. Cerebral atherosclerosis was shown to independently contribute to cognitive decline in AD44. Proteins in this module that were enriched for cerebral atherosclerosis were positively associated with this trait. Interestingly, proteins in the module associated with cerebral Aβ had an opposite direction of association to those associated with atherosclerosis, with lower plasma levels associated with higher levels of cerebral Aβ. Many of the proteins in this module associated with cerebral Aβ were strongly dependent on APOE ε4 and have been observed to be dependent on ε4 in other studies16,45, with decreased plasma levels associated with increased risk for AD, including decreased levels of APOE itself46,47. However, as also highlighted in our comparison with ε4-dependent proteins described in Frick et al.16, APOE ε4 imparts AD risk outside of its association with cerebral Aβ. This module also overlapped with ε4-related modules that we previously observed in CSF related to oxidant detoxification/MAPK signaling, neddylation and mitochondrion pathways12, highlighting the pleiotropic nature of APOE ε4 (refs. 48,49). Given that we observed in our study that ε4-dependent plasma proteins map to multiple pathways, our findings are consistent with such pleiotropic effects.

One such pleiotropic effect that may be particularly relevant to AD is the potential influence of ε4-related proteins on neurofibrillary tangle (NFT) formation. SPC25 and tubulin-specific chaperone A (TBCA) were the plasma proteins most strongly associated with cerebral Aβ and also entirely dependent upon ε4, and such proteins may provide insight into how APOE ε4 influences tau pathology. A known function of SPC25 is organization of the kinetochore complex with binding to microtubules during mitosis50, whereas TBCA is involved in the proper folding of β-tubulin51. These observations could suggest a potential pathway by which APOE ε4 alters microtubule dynamics leading to NFT pathology. It is notable that mitotic spindle and microtubule binding were pathways identified for proteins positively associated with Aβ plaques in ROSMAP, and the M3 mitosis regulation/transcription module from the consensus plasma co-expression network was significantly enriched for proteins associated with tau in ROSMAP. M3 was also enriched in proteins associated with incident cognitive decline. APOE ε4-related proteins commonly associated with mitosis and tubulin may have a role in connecting Aβ and tau pathology in post-mitotic cells such as neurons in an ε4-dependent manner and deserve further mechanistic interrogation in model systems.

Limitations

Our study has several limitations. Although we adjusted for important covariates in our models, we could not adjust for all covariates such as medication use. This may have led to changes in plasma levels of certain proteins affected by medications commonly used in AD. One such example is ACHE, which was negatively associated with cognitive function possibly due to a compensatory elevation in plasma levels of ACHE with use of ACHE inhibitors in individuals with cognitive impairment. We did not have information on NFT burden in our cohorts except for ROSMAP, and inclusion of tau PET or biofluid markers of NFTs in future studies will be important to assess proteomic associations with this important endophenotype. Our study was also limited in the amount of longitudinal data available to assess how plasma proteomic changes evolve over time and the degree to which plasma proteomic changes can predict rate of progression in individuals affected by AD. Follow-up studies are required to address these points. Additional discussion of complement proteins, amyloid biomarkers, lipoproteins, biomarkers of co-pathologies, cohort differences and limitations is provided in the Supplementary Information.

In summary, we identified plasma protein associations to multiple AD endophenotypes, highlighting the diverse pathophysiology of AD that can be measured in the periphery. Proteins associated with these pathways may serve as useful therapeutic targets and biomarkers for AD.

Methods

Participants and case classification

Participants from four cohorts were used for this study: the Bio-Hermes cohort25, the Emory GADRC cohort, the ROSMAP cohort26 and Emory Other. Cohort summary characteristics are provided in Supplementary Table 1. In total, 2,657 plasma samples (including 503 longitudinal paired samples) from 2,139 individuals were analyzed in this study. All research participants provided written informed consent under protocols approved by the Emory Institutional Review Board for Emory studies, the Rush University Medical Center Institutional Review Board for ROSMAP and Advarra—a central institutional review board—for Bio-Hermes. Plasma samples were collected, processed and stored under multiple different protocols. Cerebral Aβ was assessed in each cohort using different biomarker or direct approaches. In Bio-Hermes, cerebral Aβ was assessed with amyloid PET using the florbetapir F-18 (18F-AV-45) tracer and with plasma pTau217 (Eli Lilly). In Emory GADRC, cerebral Aβ was assessed using plasma pTau217 (AlzPath) for most participants and the CSF tTau/Aβ42 ratio (Roche Elecsys Gen 1) for those participants who had CSF data available within 1 year of blood collection. In ROSMAP, cerebral Aβ was assessed using silver stain microscopic quantification of diffuse and neuritic plaques. In Emory Other, cerebral Aβ was assessed using the CSF tTau/Aβ42 ratio (Roche Elecsys Gen 1). For analyses in which a binary cutoff for amyloid positivity was required, in Bio-Hermes, a positive amyloid PET scan was determined by a central expert reader25; in Emory GADRC, a pTau217 threshold of >0.50 pg ml−1 (refs. 52–54) for participants who did not have CSF data available (n = 540 samples) or a pTau181 (Quanterix Simoa version 2) threshold of >3.5 pg ml−1 (refs. 55,56) when neither pTau217 nor CSF data were available (n = 43 samples); in Emory GADRC and Emory Other, a tTau/Aβ42 cutoff of >0.27 based on previous reports in the Alzheimer’s Disease Neuroimaging Initiative57 and validated using a Gaussian mixture model of all participants at Emory; and in ROSMAP, the presence (>0) or absence of diffuse or neuritic plaque by neuropathologic examination within 3 years of blood collection. APOE genotype was determined by targeted sequencing for most participants and by proteotyping using mass spectrometry for those who did not have available APOE genotyping. Proteins were quantified by SomaScan as previously described58–60. Plasma aliquots from each participant were sent to SomaLogic for analysis using the modified aptamer SomaScan assay (version 4.1). The SomaScan 7K (version 4.1) platform provides 7,596 assays, of which 7,289 target human proteins. These assays cover 6,345 unique gene products. All samples passed quality control measures and were randomized by SomaLogic prior to analysis on single plates. For the Emory Other cohort, the SomaScan version 4.1 assay was performed at Emory using the same protocol used at SomaLogic. Results were reported as relative fluorescence units (RFUs) for relative quantification of protein abundance.

Quantification of proteins by SomaScan with data normalization and quality control

We used SomaScan data normalized using the SomaLogic Adaptive Normalization by Maximum Likelihood (ANML) approach in all our analyses. Custom R scripts using the SomaDataIO R package (https://github.com/SomaLogic) were developed to load and quality control the data using inspection of median RFU, signal-to-noise and multidimensional clustering. Protein abundance data were preprocessed and further normalized as follows:

Discovery and removal of preanalytical technical covariates

Each cohort’s log2-transformed RFU data underwent regression analysis to identify and correct for preanalytical technical covariates. Initial cluster analysis revealed variance differences across samples attributed to collection protocols. Regression on technical covariates was used to mitigate these biases. We used the SomaLogic SomaSignals composite measures of preanalytic variance to identify clusters of samples in hierarchical clustering on a pioneer co-expression network’s modules that had low or high intracluster variance not only in module eigenproteins but also in these surrogate measures of preanalytic variability. Key protein assays were found to faithfully correlate with the SomaSignal TimeToSpin and TimeToDecant measures. Levels of heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoproteins A2/B1 (HNRNPA2B1) highly correlated to TimeToSpin in all four cohorts, whereas hemoglobin subunit zeta (HBZ) correlated to TimeToDecant. Neither protein correlated with any biological covariates of interest. The median coefficients of variance after 1,000 permutations of non-parametric bootstrap regression modeling only HNRNPA2B1 and HBZ were used to subtract variance attributable to these technical covariates from the protein abundance data in each cohort.

Harmonization of cohorts after regression

After the regression of technical covariates, the data were further harmonized across the four cohorts. The goal was to align the median RFU values of ‘clean control’ samples across cohorts, using the Bio-Hermes cohort as the reference. Clean control samples were defined separately in each cohort, restricting to only white participants. Tunable median polish of ratio normalization treating cohorts as batches

To prepare protein abundance data for co-expression network analysis, the tunable median polish of ratio (TAMPOR) normalization method was applied to treat each cohort as a batch62. We used TAMPOR to align the RFU distributions of clean control samples across batches (cohorts). The algorithm underwent 250 iterations to harmonize the data, with minimal technical variance remaining between cohorts after normalization as assessed using the variancePartition framework63.

Alternative sample-centric normalization

An alternative sample-wise normalization and scaling strategy was performed to prepare the complete abundance data for testing multiprotein combinatorial ratios for discrimination of amyloid positivity (described below). Here, individual samples were normalized starting with the harmonized RFU values described above and then subtracting the sample median measure and dividing by the sum of squared intensities. This method was designed to eliminate intersample variance while preserving relative differences in protein abundance and centering the population of measurements near unity.

Identification of proteins associated with traits

Data that had undergone quality control section were used for association analyses in each cohort. Linear, logistic and ordinal regression models were chosen as robust and interpretable approaches to identify protein associations with AD endophenotypes. Linear regression was used to quantify the strength and direction of protein associations with continuous traits; logistic regression was used to model binary traits; and ordinal regression was applied to ordinal traits. The statsmodels (version 0.14.0) library64 in Python was used to fit the regression models and evaluate the associations. To satisfy the independence assumption required for linear, logistic and ordinal regression, only one measurement per participant was included. If a participant had two measurements, the measurement was randomly selected from either the first or second visit. For traits with a substantial number of zero values in ROSMAP (diffuse and neuritic plaques and a composite summary plaque trait to capture both types of plaques), zero values were assumed to represent levels below the detection limit. Given high correlation between the global pathology (gpath) measure in ROSMAP, which is a continuous trait with only two zero values in our dataset, and the three plaque traits mentioned above (Pearson correlations \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ge$$\end{document}≥0.84), linear regression was applied using gpath as the dependent variable to handle these zeros through imputation. This approach enabled estimation of likely values for zero entries based on observed data patterns, preserving associations without arbitrary replacements that could skew results. Specifically, a linear regression was fitted on individuals with non-zero values, and for those with zeros, predicted values were calculated and then shrunk (divided by 10) to remain small and close to zero, maintaining alignment with the underlying data structure. Protein associations with cerebral β-amyloidosis

Linear regression was performed in each cohort to evaluate the association between proteins and measures of cerebral β-amyloidosis. The measures of cerebral β-amyloidosis used were amyloid PET SUVR (Bio-Hermes), plasma pTau217 (AlzPath and Emory GADRC), diffuse and neuritic plaque load by silver stain within 3 years of death (ROSMAP) and CSF tTau/Aβ42 (Emory Other). For the composite plaque trait in ROSMAP, the zero-imputation method described in ‘Identification of proteins associated with traits’ was applied. Box-Cox transformation was applied to PET SUVR and CSF tTau/Aβ42 and inverse normal transformation to AlzPath pTau217 and composite plaque traits. Then, linear regression models were fitted to identify significant confounding variables and protein associations to the traits. We also conducted logistic regression to evaluate the association between proteins and amyloid positivity as a binary outcome. Amyloid positivity was used as the dependent variable in Bio-Hermes, Emory GADRC and Emory Other cohorts; in ROSMAP, the composite plaque trait was binarized (0 if no plaque, 1 otherwise).

Protein associations with cerebral β-amyloidosis after adjustment for APOE ε4 and cognitive function

Adjustment for APOE ε4 status was performed by adding it as a covariate in both the linear and logistic regression models, thereby controlling for its influence to identify protein associations with cerebral β-amyloidosis that are independent of the APOE ε4 gene. In a second model, measures of cognitive function in each cohort were added as a covariate in the linear models to identify protein associations with cerebral β-amyloidosis that are independent of both the APOE ε4 gene and cognitive function.

Protein associations with cognitive function

Linear regression models were used to associate protein levels with MMSE score in Bio-Hermes and Emory GADRC, the global cognitive function measure (cogn_global) in ROSMAP and MoCA score in Emory Other. Inverse normal transformation was applied to each trait to ensure normality and enhance the linear regression fit and residual distribution. Linear regression models were used to identify proteins associated with these traits as shown in equation (2), considering significant confounding variables as formulated in equation (1).

Protein associations with cognitive function after amyloid adjustment

Amyloid-related traits (amyloid PET SUVR in Bio-Hermes, plasma AlzPath pTau217 in Emory GADRC, composite plaque trait within 3 years of death in ROSMAP and CSF tTau/Aβ42 in Emory Other) were added as covariates in the model to control for amyloid burden. This adjustment aimed to isolate the protein association with cognitive function independent of amyloid burden.

Protein associations with cognitive impairment without amyloid (CI.other)

Proteins associated with cognitive impairment in participants without cerebral β-amyloidosis (CI.other) in Bio-Hermes, Emory GADRC and Emory Other cohorts were assessed using a logistic regression model, adjusting for significant confounding variables in the final model.

Meta-analysis

To leverage protein association results across multiple cohorts, meta-analysis was performed using the method proposed by Willer et al.66. z-scores were calculated for each protein association in each cohort. Weights were then assigned to each cohort based on its sample size, and z-scores were multiplied by these weights to give a weighted measure of association. The weighted scores were used to calculate an overall z-score, which was then transformed into a meta P value. Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) correction was applied to the resulting meta P values to correct for multiple testing.

Protein associations with neuropathological traits in ROSMAP

We identified protein associations with neuropathological traits in ROSMAP participants whose plasma samples were collected within 3 years of death. Only white participants were analyzed given the racial distribution in this subset (215 white, six non-white). Neuropathological traits were categorized into continuous, binary and ordinal traits. Continuous traits included diffuse plaque (plaque_d), neuritic plaque (plaque_n) and diffuse and neuritic plaque (plaque_d+plaque_n, or composite plaque trait), neurofibrillary tangles by silver stain (NFT) and neurofibrillary tangles by IHC (tangles). Binary traits consisted of chronic microinfarcts (ci_num2_mct) and chronic gross infarcts (ci_num2_gct). Ordinal traits included Lewy bodies (dlbdx), TDP43 (tdp_st4), cerebral atherosclerosis (cvda_4gp2), arteriolosclerosis (arteriol_scler) and CAA (caa_4gp). Models were fitted as described above. Inverse normal transformation was applied to all continuous traits prior to model fitting. For the traits with a substantial number of zero values (plaque_d, plaque_n and plaque_d+plaque_n), a zero-imputation method was described above.

Overlap was performed at the gene symbol level.

Protein co-expression network analysis

A protein co-expression network was constructed from plasma proteomic data using the WGCNA package in R (version >4.2.0), as previously described9,11,12. Protein abundance data that had undergone quality control and normalization as described above were used as input for network construction. All samples passed a sample connectivity outlier check, with no samples falling below 3 s.d. from mean connectivity. A soft threshold power of β = 9 was used to approximate scale-free topology. All correlations were computed using bicor to enhance robustness against outliers. Protein–protein adjacency was computed as adjacency = ∣bicor∣β. The ‘blockwiseModules’ function was used to calculate initial module assignments with the following key parameters: networkType = ‘signed’, deepSplit = 4, mergeCutHeight = 0.07, minModuleSize = 10 and pamRespectsDendro = TRUE. In an iterative refinement of module assignments, intramodular connectivity was quantified by module membership (kME)—where the ‘signedKME’ function was used to calculate the bicor between each protein’s abundance and the module eigenprotein—and proteins with insufficient intramodular kME (<0.28) were iteratively reassigned to gray (unassigned) or moved to more appropriate modules if the difference between their top kME and current module kME exceeded a threshold of 0.10, as previously described9,12. This process was repeated until module memberships stabilized, within 30 iterations. Final module eigenproteins and memberships were respectively calculated with the ‘moduleEigengenes’ and ‘signedKME’ functions.

Ontology gene set enrichment analysis

Gene set enrichment analysis was performed using the ‘GOparallel’ function using all proteins on the SomaScan version 4.1 7K assay as background. This script calculates one-tailed enrichment P value for input lists into over 13,000 annotated human gene lists updated monthly, as previously described12. The open-source function and its documentation are available at https://www.github.com/edammer/GOparallel. Postprocessing of ‘GOparallel’ output into bubble plot visualizations representing significance of enrichment for top core ontology terms and fraction of a term’s genes hit by an input list (gene ratio) was performed using a custom R script.

Other enrichment and overlap analyses

Network module enrichment with model outputs

The lists of gene products representing protein members of co-expression network modules were checked for enrichment (hypergeometric overlap) using a one-tailed Fisher’s exact test implemented in the open-source function ‘geneListFET’ (https://github.com/edammer/cellTypeFET) with nominally significant protein gene product lists resulting from each analysis.

Plasma and CSF association overlaps

Plasma proteins associated with cerebral Aβ and cognitive function from the respective meta-analyses were compared with CSF protein associations to cerebral Aβ and cognitive function as described in Dammer et al.12. Correlations in Dammer et al.12 were performed using bicor to CSF tTau/Aβ42 for cerebral Aβ and MoCA score for cognitive function, with significance set at Student’s P < 0.05. Overlaps of CSF and plasma associated proteins were performed at the assay level to avoid mixed directionality of association seen for some gene products at the symbol level.

Plasma network module overrepresentation analyses with other networks

Overrepresentation analysis (ORA) heatmaps were generated by a custom script (netORA, available at https://github.com/edammer/netOps), which calculated Fisher’s exact P values for module-level overlap between pairs of co-expression networks at the level of gene symbols. P values were adjusted by the Benjamini–Hochberg method and −log10 transformed before scaling as a heatmap. Module memberships for the networks compared to the plasma 27 modules described in this study were the 44-module brain network described in Johnson et al.9, the 34-module CSF network described in Dammer et al.12 and the 27-module serum network described in Emilsson et al.48.

Statistics and reproducibility

Data acquisition was performed in a blinded fashion. Analysis was necessarily performed unblinded to diagnosis and traits on deidentified data. Given the discovery nature of the study, all available samples were used to increase study power to identify effects, and, therefore, no statistical methods were used to predetermine sample sizes. Statistical analyses were performed in R (version >4.2.0) and Python (version 3.11.5). P values were calculated as either one-sided or two-sided and adjusted for multiple comparisons by FDR correction according to the Benjamini–Hochberg method where indicated. z-scores were calculated as fold of standard deviation from the mean. The circular heatmap view of the plasma network module enrichments was constructed using the R ‘circlize’ package functional framework with a custom R script. Venn diagrams were generated using the ‘venneuler’ function in R. The ‘parANOVA’ open-source (https://www.github.com/edammer/parANOVA) function ‘plotVolc’ was used to generate volcano plots.
