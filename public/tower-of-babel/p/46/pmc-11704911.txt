Recognition and Differentiation of Baker Lesions in Lateral Epicondylitis During Arthroscopic Debridement
Authors: Joshua M Reside, Larry D Field
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractRecognition of lateral capsular disruptions (Baker lesions) that are commonly identified during arthroscopic lateral epicondylitis release is important because understanding that these lesions exist—and the variation of their arthroscopic appearance—is important. In addition, identifying Baker lesions serves as evidence supporting lateral epicondylitis as the cause of symptoms. The purposes of this article are to describe the classification system and to arthroscopically show examples of each lesion type, as well as to describe our technique for arthroscopic lateral epicondylitis release.

Lateral epicondylitis, commonly known as “tennis elbow,” refers to pain on the lateral aspect of the elbow attributed to tendinosis of the common extensor origin and is a frequent cause of discomfort in that region. This condition is believed to stem from repetitive microtrauma that occurs from wrist extensor engagement, common in both sports and work-related activities. Lateral epicondylitis typically presents in the clinical setting either with an acute onset of symptoms or with a more chronic and recalcitrant history. The degenerative process in lateral epicondylitis is marked by vascular and fibroblastic proliferation accompanied by focal hyaline degeneration of the aponeurotic origin of the extensor carpi radialis brevis (ECRB) tendon.1 Conservative measures including medication, injections, and physical therapy are typically attempted as the first-line approach and have nearly a 90% success rate.2 When conservative therapy fails, surgical intervention may be indicated.

Surgical treatment of elbow lateral epicondylitis is typically performed through either an open approach or an arthroscopic approach,3,4 both with similar success rates and complications.5 We advocate the arthroscopic approach for surgeons trained in elbow arthroscopy primarily owing to its benefits, including enhanced visualization of the joint, the ability to assess articular-sided pathologies, and the potential to avoid disturbance of the superficial extensors that are not pathologic,6 Baker and Cummings7 described a classification system to evaluate the damage to the lateral capsule during arthroscopic release. The Baker lesion classification consists of 3 types: type 1, intact lateral capsule; type 2, linear tear of the lateral capsule; and type 3, complete lateral capsule tear.3 The goals of this article are to describe and arthroscopically show these commonly encountered types of Baker lesions during arthroscopic ECRB release, as well as to show our preferred arthroscopic technique for ECRB release.

Video 1 presents a large series of arthroscopic case examples of the 3 types of Baker lesions described in the aforementioned classification system. To display the wide spectrum of lesions encountered, we have included 8 separate arthroscopic surgical cases, all with variable degrees of Baker lesion pathology represented. Within the spectrum of lesion examples provided in Video 1, there exists varying severity within type 2 and type 3 lesions. Surgeons performing arthroscopic lateral epicondylitis release will hopefully benefit from seeing the Baker lesions presented in Video 1 because recognizing the differences between these lesion types is helpful and because it improves our understanding of what these lesions represent.

Surgical Technique

The patient is brought into the operating room, and general anesthesia is induced. The patient is then carefully transferred onto the operating table in the prone position. A U-shaped arm holder is fastened to the operative side so that the arm is parallel with the floor. A nonsterile tourniquet is applied to the proximal arm. A Trimano arm holder (Arthrex, Naples, FL) with the wrist attachment can be used electively to support and position the forearm as well. The operative extremity is prepared and draped in the normal, sterile fashion. The ulnar nerve is carefully palpated and marked with a surgical marker. It is important to recognize that if the patient has undergone a prior ulnar nerve procedure, then altered ulnar nerve anatomy may be present. With any history of transposition of the nerve, an open approach to lateral epicondylitis release is often preferred by the senior author (L.D.F.).

The medial epicondyle and intermuscular septum are palpated, and a proximal anteromedial portal location is marked. The lateral epicondyle is palpated, and a specialized tennis-elbow portal site is marked immediately anterior to this landmark. The posterior-lateral and posterior-central portals are marked. After exsanguination, the tourniquet pressure is raised to 250 mm Hg. The elbow joint is then insufflated with normal saline solution by advancing a needle with a 50-mL syringe into the olecranon fossa until capsular tightening is confirmed in the posterior-lateral gutter. Once the anterior-medial trocar and cannula are inserted, a 30° arthroscope is advanced into the joint and the lateral capsule, capitellum, and radial head are visualized and assessed for pathologic changes. At this point, the lateral capsule is thoroughly evaluated for the presence of capsular disruption characteristic of a Baker lesion. Arthroscopic identification of a type 2 or 3 Baker lesion supports the preoperative diagnosis of lateral epicondylitis.

After diagnostic assessment of the anterior elbow compartment for pathologic changes, including the presence of a Baker lesion, arthroscopic lateral epicondylitis release using our “bayonet” technique is carried out.4 First, an 18-gauge spinal needle is used to localize the location and orientation of the anterolateral tennis-elbow portal site with the goal of percutaneously releasing the ECRB and adjacent anterior-lateral capsule while remaining anterior to the equator of the radial head (Video 1, Fig 1). Once the needle is in optimal position arthroscopically, it is important to visualize the angle and orientation of the spinal needle relative to the external anatomy on the skin to reproduce this angle when the knife is subsequently percutaneously advanced. The needle is then removed (ideally by an operating room assistant), and a scalpel with No. 15 blade is percutaneously advanced along this angle and into the joint. The blade is used to carefully and blindly release the ECRB from its lateral epicondyle attachment (Fig 2). After ECRB release, an arthroscopic shaver is advanced through this defect, created by sharp ECRB and capsular release, into the anterior elbow compartment to debride the diseased ECRB tissue released by the knife. After completion of arthroscopic lateral epicondylitis debridement, diagnostic arthroscopy should be completed by accessing the posterior compartment of the elbow.

Discussion

The arthroscopic identification of the 3 types of Baker lesions helps the surgeon to confirm the presence of pathology commonly seen intra-articularly in patients with lateral epicondylitis. Recognition of Baker lesions also aids the surgeon in understanding what these lesions represent and how they might be differentiated from other intra-articular elbow pathology. To our knowledge, however, no statistical relation between Baker lesion severity and postoperative outcomes or nonoperative management success has been published. This highlights the potential of research related to Baker lesions not only to enable surgeons to better understand the cause of these lesions but also to potentially provide improved methods to manage patients with this common condition.

Some researchers believe that there may be overlap between common lateral epicondylitis symptoms and minor instability patterns. Arrigoni et al.8 described symptomatic minor instability of the lateral elbow that can occur in the setting of a repetitive-use injury. They proposed the following cascade of pathology: (1) elongation of the annular ligament and radial collateral ligament (RCL), resulting in hypermobility of the radial head; (2) minor incongruence of the proximal radioulnar joint, resulting in radial head impingement with the sigmoid notch in pronation causing chondropathy of the radial head; and (3) shear or abrasion of the lateral capsule in elbow varus and pronation, leading to the development of capitellar chondropathy and possible capsular tears. Arrigoni et al. believed that at the end of this pathologic progression, the ECRB is relied on as a dynamic lateral stabilizer, opposing varus forces to assist a compromised or lax RCL. Consequently, ECRB tendinopathy may develop as the ultimate outcome after recurrent stress and overuse on the RCL.2

Intra-articular findings that provide evidence to suggest that minor instability could potentially play a role in lateral elbow symptoms typically associated with lateral epicondylitis include the following: plica in the posterior compartment, synovitis anterior to the radial head or at the insertion of the annular ligament, chondropathy of the lateral aspect of the capitellum, capsule tearing, and chondral damage to the radial head in full pronation8 (Table 1). We recommend a careful preoperative assessment for instability as well as heightened suspicion if these characteristic intra-articular findings are observed during diagnostic arthroscopy.
