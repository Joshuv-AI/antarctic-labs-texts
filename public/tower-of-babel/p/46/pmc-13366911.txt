The relationship between trace element imbalance, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction in preeclampsia diagnosis
Authors: Aycan Baş, Rıza Dur
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPreeclampsia is a complex and heterogeneous complication during pregnancy, with oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction also playing a role in its pathogenesis. This prospective case-control study aimed to investigate the roles of trace and toxic element homeostasis, as well as oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction markers, in the pathophysiology of preeclampsia.MethodsThe study included 100 third-trimester pregnant women (50 with preeclampsia and 50 matched normotensive controls). Trace—Zn, Se, Fe, Cu, V, Mn, Co—and toxic—As, Cd, Pb—elements were measured using inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS). To evaluate oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, malondialdehyde (MDA), superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity, and asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA) levels were measured by ELISA.ResultsFe, Mn, Zn, Co, Cu, Se, As, Cd, and V levels were significantly higher in the preeclampsia group than in controls (all p < 0.001), whereas Pb levels did not differ significantly (p = 0.669). SOD activity decreased (p = 0.027), while MDA (p = 0.005) and ADMA levels (p = 0.039) increased in the preeclampsia group. Compared with healthy controls, the element-correlation network was weaker in preeclampsia, and Cu/Zn, Fe/Zn, and MDA/SOD ratios were significantly altered (all p < 0.05). ROC analyses demonstrated high diagnostic performance for Fe (AUC = 0.891), Mn (AUC = 0.881), Zn (AUC = 0.876), and Se (AUC = 0.856). In binary logistic regression analyses, Zn (OR = 1.11, p < 0.001) and Fe (OR = 1.002, p = 0.004) were positively related to preeclampsia, whereas SOD activity showed an inverse relationship (OR = 0.99, p = 0.025).ConclusionsThese findings indicate that oxidative stress is increased and endothelial function is impaired in preeclampsia in parallel with disruption of elemental balance. Background

Preeclampsia is a severe obstetric syndrome characterized by the involvement of multiple systems; it is clinically defined by the development of hypertension (≥ 140/90 mmHg) and proteinuria (≥ 300 mg/day) after the 20th week of gestation, or by maternal organ dysfunction [1]. Affecting 2%–8% of all pregnancies worldwide, preeclampsia is among the leading causes of maternal and perinatal morbidity and mortality and is responsible for ~ 50,000 maternal deaths annually [1, 2]. Although the pathophysiology of preeclampsia has not yet been fully elucidated, placental insufficiency and endothelial dysfunction constitute a fundamental basis. During placental ischemia resulting from abnormal spiral artery remodeling, the levels of the antiangiogenic factor soluble fms-like tyrosine kinase-1 (sFlt-1) increase, while those of the pro-angiogenic placental growth factor (PlGF) decrease. This imbalance leads to endothelial injury and systemic vascular leakage [3]. Consequently, free radical production, systemic inflammation, and widespread endothelial dysfunction develop. As a result, increased vascular resistance, alterations in capillary permeability, and multiorgan dysfunction occur [4].

Oxidative stress plays a central role in the development and progression of preeclampsia [5]. Oxidative stress arises when reactive oxygen species (ROS) overwhelm antioxidant defense systems, leading to maternal and fetal complications. In this condition, placental ischemia and mitochondrial dysfunction cause a pathological elevation in ROS production [6]. Meta-analysis indicates that the total activity of superoxide dismutase (SOD), one of the principal enzymes of the antioxidant defense system, is significantly suppressed in preeclamptic cases [5], contributing to the inability to control ROS accumulation and exacerbation of oxidative damage. Malondialdehyde (MDA), the end product of lipid peroxidation, frequently increases in the plasma and placental tissues of women with preeclampsia. Meta-analyses have demonstrated markedly higher MDA levels in women with preeclampsia [7]. Increased ROS reduces endothelial nitric oxide (NO) bioavailability and elevates levels of NO inhibitors such as asymmetric dimethylarginine (ADMA). Serum ADMA was significantly enhanced in preeclamptic women compared with healthy women, suggesting it as a potential biomarker for an early diagnosis and monitoring of the disease [8].

Altered trace element levels during pregnancy may reflect maternal nutritional status and underlying conditions. Micronutrient imbalances are linked to established preeclampsia risk factors—including obesity, gestational diabetes, and chronic hypertension—via metabolic and inflammatory pathways that impair nutrient utilization, disrupt redox balance, and promote elemental dysregulation. Environmental and dietary exposures may further influence this balance, as supported by recent evidence [9]. Trace elements are nutrients required at micromolar levels for human physiological function; they include transition and non-metallic elements such as vanadium (V), manganese (Mn), iron (Fe), cobalt (Co), copper (Cu), zinc (Zn), and selenium (Se). Some of these elements participate in redox reactions and thereby determine oxidative balance. In particular, Cu, Fe, and Mn can trigger pro-oxidant processes, whereas Se and Zn play protective roles by maintaining antioxidant defense-related mechanisms [9]. In contrast, toxic metals such as arsenic (As), cadmium (Cd), and lead (Pb) interact with functional groups of proteins, disrupt enzymatic activity, increase ROS production, and cause structural and functional damage in multiple tissues [10]. Exposure to As mainly occurs through contaminated food, water, and industrial activities [11]. Cd exposure is commonly linked to cigarette smoke, contaminated foods, and industrial materials, whereas Pb is a ubiquitous environmental toxicant that may accumulate in bone tissue and enter the human body through contaminated food, water, and environmental exposure [12–14]. Moreover, synergistic and antagonistic interactions among metals can markedly modulate the severity of biological responses. While V and Cd synergistically increase cardiovascular risk, the Zn/Cd ratio has emerged as a crucial determinant of toxicity levels [13]. As a result of this disruption in redox homeostasis, imbalances in trace elements and toxic metals are thought to contribute to the pathogenesis of preeclampsia by simultaneously triggering oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction. Although trace elements, oxidative stress markers, or endothelial dysfunction indicators in the context of preeclampsia have been examined individually, studies co-evaluating a broad panel of trace and toxic elements in a ratio-based manner simultaneously with these biochemical axes are limited. Thus, the present study primarily aimed to ascertain and compare the serum levels of (i) trace elements: V, Mn, Fe, Co, Cu, Zn, and Se; (ii) toxic elements: As, Pb, and Cd; (iii) oxidative stress and antioxidant defense: MDA and SOD; and (iv) endothelial dysfunction—ADMA—in pregnant women with preeclampsia and healthy controls. Secondarily, we aimed to determine the potential roles of classical element ratios and biochemical indices in the etiopathogenesis of preeclampsia and their relationships with clinical severity.

Methods

Study design

Pregnant women in the third trimester with singleton pregnancies who were diagnosed with preeclampsia according to ACOG criteria [1] (n = 50), as well as age-matched normotensive healthy pregnant women (n = 50) who presented to the Obstetrics and Gynecology Outpatient Clinic of Afyonkarahisar Health Sciences University (AFSÜ) Hospital, Afyonkarahisar, Türkiye, were included in the study. Preeclampsia cases encompassed both early (n = 21) and late-onset (n = 29) forms and were analyzed as a mixed cohort without subtype stratification.

Inclusion criteria were pregnant women in the third trimester diagnosed with preeclampsia according to standard clinical criteria and healthy pregnant women without any systemic disease. Exclusion criteria included the use of any medication or dietary supplements during pregnancy (e.g., heparin), a history of alcohol or substance use, multiple pregnancies, conception via assisted reproductive technologies, fetal anomalies, and intrauterine fetal demise.

Demographic and clinical data, including maternal age (years), educational status, employment status, body mass index (BMI), gestational age (weeks), and gravidity, were recorded for all participants at the time of blood sampling. This study was approved by the AFSÜ Clinical Research Ethics Committee (03.09.2021; decision no: 2021/440). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The subsequent experimental stages shown in Fig. 1 were then carried out.

Study design

The sample size was determined a priori based on expected between-group differences reported in previous studies evaluating trace elements and oxidative stress markers in preeclampsia [14–16]. Assuming a moderate-to-large representative effect size (Cohen’s d ≈ 0.6) for key biomarkers (e.g., Zn, Fe, and MDA), consistent with values reported in similar biomarker studies, a two-sided independent-samples comparison with a significance level of α = 0.05 and 80% power indicated that at least 45 participants per group were required. To account for potential biological variability across multiple biochemical parameters, 50 participants were included in each group.

Serum isolation

Blood samples collected from the volunteers into trace element tubes were allowed to clot at room temperature for 15–20 min in accordance with preanalytical standards and were then centrifuged at approximately 1500 × g for 10 min. Following centrifugation, the serum obtained as the upper phase was transferred to sterile Eppendorf tubes and stored at − 80 °C until analysis. All samples were analyzed after a storage period of approximately 18 months.

Element determination by Inductively Coupled Plasma-Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS)

Trace and toxic elements in serum samples were quantified using an iCAP Qnova series ICP-MS instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific, MA, USA) at an authorized, accredited laboratory (Turklab, Ankara, Türkiye). After ionization in an argon plasma, elements were separated according to their mass-to-charge (m/z) ratios and quantitatively determined. Prior to analysis, 1 mL of serum was diluted with 4 mL of ultrapure water. A 2% (v/v) nitric acid solution was used as the reagent; argon with a purity of 99.996% was used as the plasma and carrier gas, and helium with a purity of 99.999% was used in the collision cell to suppress spectral interferences. System stability was ensured by employing a ThermoFlex 2500 cooling unit and an ASX-280 autosampler. For each analytical run, multipoint calibration utilized a Thermo 4AREV, 5 A elemental standard solutions, and Recipe ClinCal serum calibrators. Accuracy and precision were monitored based on two-level quality control materials. Method performance was verified in accordance with the laboratory’s method validation and measurement uncertainty procedures. The obtained results were evaluated based on the reference intervals defined in the Tietz Clinical Guide to Laboratory Tests.

Determination of SOD activity as well as MDA and ADMA levels

Commercially available ready-to-use Human SOD, MDA, and ADMA ELISA kits (Bioassay Technology Laboratory, Shanghai, China) were used to analyze serum samples in accordance with the manufacturers’ protocols. A five-point standard curve was plotted employing a serial dilution of stock standards, and 50 µL of standards and 40 µL of serum samples equilibrated to room temperature were added to 96-well microplates. The sample wells were added with the relevant antibody and streptavidin–HRP, and the plates were incubated at 37 °C. The wells were washed with the diluted washing solution, and color was developed utilizing chromogen A/B substrates provided in the kit. After terminating the reaction, the OD450 was measured with a Chromate 4300 ELISA microplate reader (Awareness Technology, USA). Concentrations were calculated based on the standard curves and analyzed accordingly.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS for Windows version 25.0. Continuous variables with normal distribution were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD), whereas ordinal and non-normally distributed continuous variables were presented as median [IQR]. Data distribution characteristics were evaluated using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and because the assumption of normal distribution was not met, the non-parametric Mann–Whitney U test was used for comparisons between groups. Relationships between variables were assessed using Spearman rank correlation analysis. Correlation matrices were visualized as heat maps in a Python environment (Matplotlib library) using a zero-centered color scale (ranging from − 1 to + 1) to reflect the direction and strength of associations. Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) analysis was applied to evaluate the data, and the area under the curve (AUC) as well as sensitivity and specificity values were calculated. In ROC analyses, optimal cut-off values were determined based on the Youden index, which maximizes the sum of sensitivity and specificity. In all statistical analyses, a p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

In the binary logistic regression analysis, Zn and Fe levels were found to be positively related to the presence of preeclampsia (Zn: OR = 1.11, 95% CI: 1.05–1.17, p < 0.001; Fe: OR = 1.002, 95% CI: 1.001–1.004, p = 0.004), whereas SOD activity was inversely related to the presence of preeclampsia (OR = 0.99, 95% CI: 0.98–1.00, p = 0.025) (Table 7). The significant positive relationship of Zn levels with the presence of preeclampsia (OR > 1, p < 0.001) and the significant inverse relationship of SOD activity (OR < 1, p < 0.05) indicate that the model is statistically significant. BMI was also found to be an independent predictor of preeclampsia (OR = 1.15, 95% CI: 1.01–1.30, p = 0.040), and the inclusion of BMI in the model did not materially alter the relationship of Zn, Fe, and SOD with preeclampsia.

Discussion

Our study demonstrated that the levels of several elements were significantly altered in the preeclampsia group; those of V, Mn, Fe, Co, Cu, Zn, As, Se, and Cd were significantly elevated in the preeclampsia group compared with healthy controls. Most of these elements are known to play roles in the induction of oxidative stress, impairment of endothelial function, or triggering of inflammatory processes.

In our study, the absence of a significant age difference between the preeclampsia and control groups (Table 1) is consistent with previous reports, indicating that maternal age alone is not a decisive risk factor for preeclampsia [14, 17]. Educational status was also comparable between groups (Table 2), in line with earlier findings [18], although population-based studies suggest that lower educational level may be related to greater disease severity, reflecting socioeconomic and regional influences [11]. The higher BMI observed in the preeclampsia group (Table 1) accords with studies linking increased BMI to preeclampsia [18], while discrepancies across cohorts may relate to genetic, metabolic, and sociodemographic heterogeneity [17]. Gestational age was significantly lower in the preeclampsia group (Table 1), consistent with reports of earlier delivery in preeclamptic pregnancies [14, 15, 18]. Gravidity and parity distributions were similar between groups (Table 2), supporting evidence that obstetric history alone has limited explanatory value for preeclampsia risk [5, 16]. The significantly higher systolic and diastolic blood pressure values in the preeclampsia group (Table 1) are consistent with the established diagnostic role of hypertension in preeclampsia [17, 18].

Due to increased metabolic demands during pregnancy, the placenta forms a protective barrier against oxidative damage through antioxidant enzymes such as selenium-dependent glutathione peroxidase (GPx) and Cu/Zn- and Mn-dependent superoxide dismutase (SOD). A reduction in antioxidant capacity may impair placental blood flow, triggering hypoxia and ischemia, and consequently preeclampsia and fetal growth restriction [15]. The Cu, Zn, Mn, Se, V, Fe, Co, As, Cd, and Pb levels in the preeclampsia and control groups ascertained in our study are presented in Table 3. Elevated serum Cu levels observed in the preeclampsia group are consistent with the literature and supported by a meta-analysis reporting increased serum/plasma Cu contents during preeclampsia [19, 20]. Given that Cu functions as a cofactor for antioxidant enzymes, particularly SOD, such an increase may reflect a biological response to enhanced oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction during preeclampsia. In our study, the serum Se levels were markedly higher in the preeclampsia group than in the control group. In contrast, the literature reports significantly lower serum Se levels in preeclampsia compared with normotensive pregnancies and a higher prevalence of Se deficiency in preeclamptic pregnancies [15, 17]. However, no marked relationships between Se levels of hair, plasma, and erythrocytes and preeclampsia have also been reported [15, 21]. Additionally, significantly higher 24-hour urinary Se concentrations have been observed in preeclamptic individuals compared with controls [22]. The elevated Se levels observed in the preeclampsia group in our study may be explained by redistribution of Se from tissues due to its role in antioxidant metabolism, as well as differences in measurement timing and population characteristics. The higher Zn levels observed in the preeclampsia group compared with controls, when evaluated alongside contradictory literature findings reporting both increased [15] and decreased [19] Zn levels, suggest that Zn is sensitive to biomatrix type, gestational age, and environmental and demographic factors. Increased Zn levels have been reported to contribute to hypertensive processes through vascular tone and Ca²⁺ homeostasis [15]. In the context of our study, Zn may represent a compensatory response, in which it is mobilized within tissues—into from circulation—under enhanced oxidative stress to regulate antioxidant defense- and angiogenesis-related molecular pathways. As previously reported [23], serum Mn contents were remarkably higher in the preeclampsia group than in healthy controls. This finding contrasts with data from the Boston Birth Cohort reporting decreased Mn levels in preeclampsia [21] and with serum- and hair-based studies reporting reduced Mn levels [15]. Differences between studies may be explained by biomatrix selection, measurement timing, and environmental and demographic variables. Although Mn serves as a cofactor for antioxidant enzymes, particularly Mn-SOD, elevated serum Mn levels may reflect an early compensatory response not yet consumed by antioxidant systems or mobilized from tissues into circulation.

Elevated serum V levels in the preeclampsia group differ from some previous clinical findings. Ovayolu et al. [18] reported lower plasma V levels in late-onset preeclampsia and related this with hypoalbuminemia and increased renal excretion reducing V transport. However, considering the differences between plasma and serum as biological matrices, protein-binding characteristics, and bioavailable fractions, serum ICP-MS analysis allows an evaluation of the freely circulating V fraction. Moreover, although concentration-based measurements demonstrated increased V levels, no marked inter-group variation was observed when 24-hour urinary excretion was considered, suggesting a concentration effect related to urine volume and renal hemodynamics [22]. Elevated urinary V levels have been related to hypertension prevalence; higher V contents accompany increases in systolic and diastolic blood pressure [24]. Evidence suggests that V may regulate vascular tone and blood pressure via Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase inhibition and oxidative stress modulation [25]. Taken together, these findings suggest that the elevated serum V levels observed in our study may be related to hypertension and endothelial dysfunction, which are common during preeclampsia. Enhanced serum Fe levels detected in the preeclampsia group are consistent with a previous meta-analysis [16] and suggest that Fe content imbalance may exacerbate endothelial damage via oxidative stress. Although increased hair Fe and decreased serum Fe levels have been reported [15], Fe deficiency or excess can promote free radical production and predispose to vascular dysfunction. High Fe contents have been related to preeclampsia via an induction of lipid peroxidation and oxidative damage to endothelial cell membranes, thereby impairing vascular function [26], rendering the enhancement observed in our study biologically meaningful. The remarkably elevated serum Co levels observed in the preeclampsia group can be evaluated in light of limited literature, which reports increasing urinary Co content with advancing gestation, inversely related to Fe loss [27]. Similarly, serum Co levels increased across trimesters and were higher among pregnant Korean women than in the general population [19]. These findings suggest that elevated Co levels in preeclampsia may be related to increased Fe demand and oxidative stress.

Higher As contents detected in the preeclampsia group are consistent with studies reporting a stepwise increase in blood As concentrations from control to mild and severe preeclampsia patients [11]. Conversely, no statistically significant inter-group differences have been reported despite numerically higher As levels [15]. Given that As induces toxicity through oxidative stress and directly damages endothelial cells, triggering vascular dysfunction, the elevated As content observed in our study suggests that As exposure may contribute to the pathophysiological process of preeclampsia. Regarding Cd, the markedly higher serum levels observed in the preeclampsia group are consistent with the literature, which demonstrates increased Cd accumulation in maternal blood and placental tissue; however, fetal transfer remained limited [14]. Cd may contribute to preeclampsia pathophysiology by enhancing ROS-mediated oxidative stress and pro-inflammatory responses, although such inter-group variations were not previously observed [18]. In contrast, similar Pb levels in preeclampsia and control groups detected by us (Table 3) are consistent with a Turkish study [18] and a multiethnic cohort [21] reporting that Pb does not predict preeclampsia risk.

SOD activity, as well as MDA and ADMA levels in the preeclampsia and control groups, ascertained in this study, are presented in Table 3. The SOD activity was reduced in the preeclampsia group, indicating an impaired antioxidant defense capacity. Correspondingly, increased MDA levels suggest pronounced oxidative stress during preeclampsia. Elevated levels of ADMA, an endogenous NO inhibitor, indicate impaired vascular tone and aggravated endothelial dysfunction. These findings are consistent with meta-analysis data showing that oxidative stress arising from increased contents of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species due to defective placentation related to hypoxia suppresses enzyme-based antioxidant defense systems—particularly SOD and GPx—and increases lipid peroxidation (MDA) [28]. Enhanced blood and placental MDA levels support the notion that systemic manifestations accompany placental oxidative damage and may correlate with disease severity [7]. Elevated ADMA contents during preeclampsia have also been confirmed by a meta-analysis involving 16 case-control studies, suggesting that ADMA may be a potential biomarker for early diagnosis and risk stratification [15]. The literature consistently emphasizes increased oxidative stress marker levels in mothers with preeclampsia and their offspring, as well as their central role in disease pathophysiology [29]. Furthermore, enhanced lipid peroxidation, decreased NO and total antioxidant capacities, and marked reductions in GPx and CAT activities in blood and placenta have been reported [7].

The widespread positive correlations among V, Mn, Fe, Cu, Zn, As, and Se observed in the control group (Fig. 2A) indicate coordinated regulation of trace elements under physiological pregnancy conditions. This pattern is consistent with the roles of Mn and Se as cofactors of antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and GPx [7, 28], shared redox pathways of Cu and Zn via Cu/Zn-SOD [15], and the involvement of Fe in lipid peroxidation processes [25], reflecting an intact metallomic network. In contrast, this coordinated pattern was markedly attenuated in the preeclampsia group, with only limited relationships (V–Se, V–Cd, Mn–Zn, Fe–Cd, and Zn–Se) remaining (Fig. 2B). This disruption is consistent with increased oxidative stress, impaired endothelial integrity [15, 29], and the redox-disruptive effects of toxic elements such as As and Cd [11, 14]. Fe- and Cu-mediated lipid peroxidation [26], Zn–Cu antagonism affecting antioxidant responses [15], and altered Se- and Mn-dependent GPx and SOD activities [7, 15, 17] likely contribute to the fragmentation of the metallomic network observed in preeclampsia.

The ratio-based evaluations performed in our study (Table 4) demonstrate that trace element and toxic metal homeostasis in preeclampsia is disrupted not only at absolute concentration but also at the relative inter-element relationship level. Although the relationships between a few ratios, such as Cu/Zn, Fe/Zn, and Cu/Fe, and oxidative stress or metallomic imbalance have been observed, to our knowledge, ratios such as V/Cu, Pb/Se, Mn/Cu, As/Se, and Cd/Pb have not been systematically reported. The introduction of these ratios in the context of preeclampsia provides a novel analytical framework. The decrease in the Cu/Zn ratio is consistent with elevated Cu contents and population-dependent variability in Zn levels, indicating a disruption of the antioxidant–pro-oxidant balance [15, 19, 20]. The increase in the Fe/Zn ratio parallels studies reporting elevated Fe levels in preeclampsia [16], supporting a biomechanism in which Fe-mediated lipid peroxidation enhances oxidative burden [26]. A decline in the Cu/Fe ratio reflects a relative predominance of Fe [15, 16], while an increase in the Cd/Pb ratio is aligned with more pronounced Cd accumulation in preeclampsia [14, 18]. Similarly, Mn/Cd and As/Se ratios may be explained by the sensitivity of these elements to biomatrix and population differences [11, 17]. In contrast, prominent alterations in Mn/Cu, Pb/Se, and V/Cu ratios are consistent with impaired multiple metal–metal interactions seen in preeclampsia [15, 18, 23]. Similar patterns were observed with the ratios involving SOD, MDA, and ADMA (Table 4). The increased MDA/SOD ratio supports reduced SOD activity and elevated lipid peroxidation during preeclampsia [7, 28]. While the ADMA/SOD ratio stabilized, the increased MDA/ADMA ratio is consistent with the findings of a meta-analysis highlighting the endothelial-dysfunction-determining role of ADMA [15]. Such an integrated profile of ratios suggests a mechanism in which trace element–toxic metal imbalance, oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction progress in preeclampsia. However, as these ratios were evaluated for the first time by this study, their diagnostic or prognostic value requires confirmation via future validation studies.

The discriminatory performance of serum element levels in predicting preeclampsia was evaluated employing ROC analysis (Table 5; Fig. 3). The high AUC values identified for Fe, Mn, Zn, and Se (0.85–0.89) are consistent with vital biocomponents of preeclampsia, including placental dysfunction, increased oxidative stress, and endothelial injury [15, 23, 26]. The pro-oxidant influence of Fe, the roles of Mn and Se as cofactors of antioxidant enzyme systems, and the potential compensatory response of Zn explain their high diagnostic performance. The moderate–high AUC values observed for Cu, As, V, Co, and Cd (0.70–0.82) are consistent with the literature describing their toxic and pro-oxidant impacts [11, 18, 19]. In contrast, the low discriminative power of Pb suggests that it may not be a decisive standalone biomarker for preeclampsia and that its effects should be coevaluated with exposure levels and interactions with other elements [18, 21]. The ROC analysis results suggest that the metallomic response related to preeclampsia exhibits a distinct biochemical pattern and that the Fe–Mn–Zn–Se axis potential clinical biomarker value. Complementing single-element assessments, ROC analyses of ratio-based parameters more comprehensively reflect the toxic metal burden–antioxidant capacity imbalance, as well as disruptions in essential microelement homeostasis (Table 6; Fig. 4A-B). In particular, the discriminatory performance of the Cu/Zn, Fe/Zn, and MDA/SOD ratios presents a biochemical profile reflecting the combined effects of enhanced oxidative load and loss of antioxidant defenses, consistent with studies reporting increased Cu–Fe and decreased Zn–SOD levels [7, 19, 26]. Such an integrated approach demonstrates that ratio-based ROC analyses capture metallomic imbalance in preeclampsia more robustly than single biomarkers and offer a framework complementary to classical diagnostic approaches.

In this study, a binary logistic regression revealed that Fe and Zn levels were positively related to the occurrence of preeclampsia, whereas SOD activity was inversely correlated with the occurrence of preeclampsia (Table 7). The physiological increase in oxidative stress during healthy pregnancies is accentuated in preeclampsia; it is related to free radical-induced endothelial damage and maternal vascular dysfunction. Increased ROS production during preeclampsia disrupts uteroplacental perfusion through trophoblast dysfunction and shallow invasion, forming a fundamental pathophysiological component of placental and endothelial dysfunction. The positive relationship between Fe levels and preeclampsia suggests that Fe may exacerbate placental and endothelial damage by enhancing oxidative stress and lipid peroxidation through its pro-oxidant properties. The serum Zn contents were elevated in the preeclampsia group, and logistic regression demonstrated a positive relationship between Zn and preeclampsia (OR > 1). This finding suggests that increased Zn levels may reflect a compensatory response in which this element is mobilized from tissues into circulation under elevated oxidative stress rather than pathogenic accumulation. Indeed, the regulatory effects of Zn on antioxidant enzyme function, cell-level redox balance, and endothelial integrity have been reported [30, 31]. Although BMI emerged as an independent predictor in the multivariable logistic regression model, its lack of significant correlations with all evaluated elements and biomarkers (SOD, MDA, ADMA) suggests that BMI is unlikely to be the primary driver of the observed elemental alterations; rather, these changes more plausibly reflect disease-related pathophysiological processes inherent to preeclampsia. The independent protective relationship of SOD activity observed in the regression model does not contradict the decline in SOD activity observed in preeclampsia; rather, it reflects a heterogeneous and often insufficient compensatory antioxidant-based response to enhanced oxidative burden. This finding is consistent with studies reporting markedly increased oxidative stress in preeclampsia and inadequate antioxidant defense mechanisms to prevent endothelial dysfunction and clinical progression [30, 31]. Trace element imbalance during preeclampsia is not limited to oxidative stress but involves a broader cell dysfunction network encompassing metal metabolism, suppression of the Nrf2/KEAP1-mediated antioxidant response, and ferroptosis. In this context, ferroptosis, characterized by Fe accumulation and increased lipid peroxidation, contributes to placental dysfunction [32]; the Nrf2/KEAP1 pathway is suppressed in preeclamptic placentas [33], and transferrin receptor-mediated Fe uptake has been implicated as a key mechanism increasing cellular susceptibility to ferroptosis [32].

The enhancement in Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, As, Se, Cd, and V levels, concomitant with decreased SOD activity and elevated MDA and ADMA levels during third-trimester preeclamptic cases, indicates an integrated pathophysiological axis in which metallomic imbalance, increased oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction progress together. The high ROC performance of Fe and Zn and the identification of SOD as an independent protective determinant in binary logistic regression support the relationship between redox balance and clinical outcomes. These findings are consistent with studies reporting that placental Fe accumulation increases ROS production, thereby triggering lipid peroxidation and elevating MDA levels, together with reduced GPX4 and GSH levels and ferroptosis-related mitochondrial alterations [34]. In this context, the Fe-related pro-oxidant burden, SOD insufficiency, increased MDA, and, in particular, ADMA-related endothelial dysfunction identified in our study may be considered complementary indicators reflecting a potential biological basis of the severe clinical course observed in early-onset preeclampsia phenotypes. These findings provide a mechanistically supported framework for a more detailed understanding of the biochemical foundations of preeclampsia and for the development of future diagnostic, monitoring, and preventive strategies pending validation in large-scale prospective cohorts across different gestational stages.

Discrepancies between our findings and those in the literature may arise from a combined evaluation of preeclampsia subtypes, differences in measurement time points, heterogeneity in biological matrices (blood, plasma, serum, urine, and hair), and analytical methods. The inclusion of smaller sample sizes in many studies highlights the need for larger, standardized cohorts. Furthermore, limited data on the influence of environmental metal exposure complicate the roles of toxic and essential elements in preeclampsia pathogenesis. In this context, a simultaneous evaluation of oxidative stress and elemental homeostasis conducted in our study allows a more integrated interpretation of the biochemical profile observed during the disease. In particular, the discriminatory findings for Fe, Zn, and SOD suggest that these parameters should be considered as supportive biomarkers in clinical assessment rather than definitive diagnostic markers. Nevertheless, a validation of these results in large-scale prospective studies encompassing the different stages of pregnancy is required before clinical translation.

A key limitation of the present study is its cross-sectional design and the lack of stratification by preeclampsia subtype (early- vs. late-onset), both of which may have influenced the observed relationships. In addition, trace element levels were evaluated only during the third trimester; therefore, temporal changes preceding the onset of preeclampsia could not be assessed. Future longitudinal prospective studies are needed to determine whether alterations in trace element homeostasis occur before clinical disease onset and to clarify their potential predictive and pathophysiological roles.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that trace element homeostasis is disrupted during the pathophysiology of preeclampsia, with increases in pro-oxidant elements and oxidative stress markers and concomitant reductions in antioxidant defense components. These findings suggest that oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction are simultaneous and integrated mechanisms underlying the disease. In addition, trace element imbalance may represent an important biochemical component that interacts with oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, particularly through alterations in Zn and Fe levels and increased lipid peroxidation (MDA), providing further insight into the underlying mechanisms of preeclampsia.
