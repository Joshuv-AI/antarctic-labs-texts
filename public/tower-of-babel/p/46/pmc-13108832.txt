Graft Embolization versus Nephrectomy in Patients with Graft Intolerance and Infection: A Retrospective Cohort Study
Authors: Mattheüs F. Klaassen, Joost Otterspeer, Madelon van Agteren, Sebastiaan Heidt, Diederik Kimenai, Kay J. Pieterman, Robert C. Minnee, Annelies E. de Weerd
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAfter graft failure, kidney transplant recipients may develop immunological intolerance to the allograft left in situ. In such cases, surgical removal of the allograft is usually performed, but is associated with substantial morbidity and mortality. A less invasive option is percutaneous embolization of the renal artery. Here, we describe our clinical experience with embolization and report observed differences with graft nephrectomy.MethodsIn this retrospective, single‐center study, we included kidney transplant recipients who underwent either graft nephrectomy or embolization between 2018 and 2024. Patients with indication “gain of space” were excluded. We reported on major post‐procedural complications within three months (Clavien Dindo score 3–4), post‐embolization syndrome, blood transfusions, length of hospitalization and readmissions, and mortality.ResultsWe included 13 embolizations and 32 graft nephrectomies. Major post‐procedural complications occurred in 23% after embolization versus 41% after nephrectomy (p = 0.32). Length of hospitalization was shorter after embolization compared to nephrectomy (median 1 vs. 5 days, p = 0.001). Post‐embolization syndrome occurred in 9 of 13 patients (69%) and was managed conservatively. One patient with recent graft infections required a subsequent nephrectomy for infected necrosis after embolization. Another patient with residual blood flow after embolization required a nephrectomy for persisting hematuria. Two patients with pre‐existing sepsis and bacteriemia died after embolization.ConclusionsEmbolization results in shorter length of hospitalization and is likely associated with a reduced incidence of procedure‐related major complications as compared to nephrectomy. Pre‐existing sepsis is a risk factor for adverse outcomes after embolization, and recent graft infections should be considered a contra‐indication.

Introduction

In kidney transplantation, roughly half of transplanted allografts fail within ten years posttransplant [1]. After graft failure, immunosuppression is mostly tapered to reduce concomitant infection and malignancy risks [2, 3]. The failed graft is generally left in situ to not expose the recipient to additional surgery [4, 5].

However, after withdrawal of immunosuppression, about 40% of patients develop symptoms indicating immunological intolerance to the failed allograft [6]. This graft intolerance syndrome is characterized by localized pain, hematuria, fever, malaise, and anemia, in the absence of infection [6]. First‐line treatment is high‐dose corticosteroids to reduce symptoms and inflammation. Subsequently, the failed graft is typically removed [5, 7, 8]. The proportion of patients in whom nephrectomy is performed after graft failure differs substantially, between 27% and 74% [3, 5, 9].

Traditionally, the renal allograft was removed surgically, via either an intracapsular or an extracapsular approach [10]. However, a graft nephrectomy has significant risks: complication rate is estimated between 20% and 30% and mortality ranges between 0% and 11% [4, 5, 7, 9, 11, 12]. An alternative treatment has been described by Lorenzo et al., which entails a percutaneous, arterial embolization of the graft [13]. This technique is considered less invasive, and studies have shown less complications and lower mortality rates compared to surgical removal [11]. Nonetheless, about 20% of patients proceed to graft nephrectomy after unsuccessful embolization. Additionally, patients often experience post‐embolization syndrome, characterized by fever, malaise, or hematuria [11].

The embolization procedure was recently introduced in our center. Here, we describe our clinical experience with percutaneous graft embolization for rejection and infection, and we report observed differences with graft nephrectomy.

Materials and Methods

The EMBOTRANSPLANT study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethical Review Committee of the Erasmus Medical Center Rotterdam (MEC‐2025‐0200). The need for informed consent was waived.

Inclusion

Kidney transplant patients that initially underwent percutaneous embolization or graft nephrectomy between January 2018 and December 2024 at our center were included. We excluded patients younger than 16 years of age, and patients for whom the indication was malignancy or ‘gaining space’ for re‐transplantation, as these are generally not considered indications for embolization.

Embolization Procedure

The decision to proceed with either nephrectomy or embolization was left to the treating physician's discretion. Via an endovascular catheter (4‐7 French), embolization was performed using histoacryl glue or polyvinyl alcohol particles (75–700 micron) with subsequent coil embolization (2–4 mm).

Data Collection

Baseline characteristics were collected from the electronical patient records. Comorbidity of the patients was scored with the age‐adjusted Charlson Comorbidity Index [14]. For patients that underwent nephrectomy, we collected American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) score, surgery technique and duration, and blood loss.

Outcomes

The primary outcome was the occurrence of major complications, defined as a Clavien‐Dindo score 3‐4 within three months post‐procedure [15]. Secondary outcomes included post‐embolization syndrome, which was retrospectively defined as fever within five days post‐embolization. Other outcomes were blood transfusion within one month, length of post‐procedural hospitalization, readmission within three months, and all‐cause mortality up to six months post‐procedure.

Statistical Analysis

Univariate analysis was performed. Normally distributed data was reported with mean and standard deviation, and non‐normally distributed data with median and interquartile range (IQR). Normality was assessed by using the Shapiro–Wilk test.

Numerical outcomes were compared between the groups with the t‐test for parametric and the Mann‐Whitney U test for non‐parametric data. Categorical variables were compared using the Pearson Chi‐square test. In case the minimum expected cell count was below five, Fisher's exact test and Fisher‐Freeman‐Halton test for two or more categories, respectively, were used instead of Chi‐square test. Statistical analysis was performed with SPSS IBM software version 28.0.1.0. Statical tests were two‐sided, and p‐values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Results

During the study period, 13 patients were initially treated with percutaneous embolization while 51 patients underwent (initial) graft nephrectomy. We excluded 20 nephrectomies: 17 with indication “gain space”, two with indication malignancy, and one with removal of an autotransplant. Ultimately, 13 patients were included in the embolization group versus 31 individual patients in the nephrectomy group. One patient underwent two nephrectomies for different allografts, while another patient underwent a simultaneous nephrectomy of two allografts (counted as one procedure).

In both groups, time between re‐initiation of dialysis and the procedure was median 4 months (p = 0.99). However, time from transplantation till procedure was 7.3 years (range 0.4–41) for embolization compared to 2.6 (range 0–20) for nephrectomy (p = 0.08). For both procedures, the main treatment indication was graft intolerance syndrome (Table 1). All patients underwent embolization for graft intolerance syndrome, except for one patient (p10) with recurrent infections who underwent embolization in order to discontinue immunosuppression. Five patients had graft intolerance syndrome with concomitant infections (Table S1).

C‐reactive protein levels were median 85 before embolization compared to 45 mg/L before nephrectomy (p = 0.29). One patient was septic before embolization versus two patients before nephrectomy (8% vs. 6%, p = 1.00). One peritoneal dialysis patient (p7) remained hypotensive after treatment for peritonitis and was suspected to have concomitant graft intolerance, for which embolization was scheduled. Another peritoneal dialysis patient (p43) had a gallbladder perforation with bile‐associated peritonitis and underwent graft nephrectomy to discontinue immunosuppression. The third patient (p53) had recurrent graft infections with a septic episode and therefore underwent nephrectomy.

Surgery

Median surgery duration for nephrectomy was 63 min (IQR 38). Peri‐operative blood loss was median 225 mL (IQR 438). In six patients (19%), blood loss exceeded 500 mL (maximum 1100 mL). Main surgical technique was the intracapsular approach (n = 25, 78%), followed by extracapsular approach (n = 6, 19%), and a laparotomy for simultaneous psoas hematoma debridement in one patient.

Outcomes after Embolization and Graft Nephrectomy

Major complications (Clavien Dindo score 3–4) occurred in 3 patients after embolization versus 13 patients after nephrectomy (23% vs. 41%, p = 0.32, Table 2). One patient (p10) with ANCA vasculitis required a below‐knee amputation, and a nephrectomy for infected necrosis 36 days after embolization (Table 2). Another patient (p4) with pre‐existing pseudomonas‐infected leg wounds due to skin cancer underwent a debridement after embolization. The third patient (p12) with Clavien Dindo score 3‐4 post‐embolization underwent a subsequent graft nephrectomy after 19 days because of persisting hematuria. Complications after nephrectomy included multiple abscess drainages and drain placements, a revision of injured iliac arteries after nephrectomy, dialysis fistula revisions, debridements of the transplant fossa, and several Intensive Care Unit (ICU) admissions (Table 2).

When comparing patients with graft intolerance syndrome alone (n = 7 vs. n = 19, Table S1), they tended to have fewer complications after embolization than nephrectomy (14% vs. 37%, p = 0.38). Meanwhile, patients with graft intolerance and concomitant infection had high mortality, long hospitalization duration, and often received blood transfusions irrespective of embolization or nephrectomy.

Six‐month mortality was 15% for embolization versus 9% for nephrectomy (p = 0.62, Table 2). Two patients died 10 and 24 days after embolization. Both patients (p6 & p7) had a pre‐existing infection that did not respond to antibiotic treatment and underwent embolization for suspected graft intolerance syndrome. They died with persisting inflammation that was not resolved after embolization. For nephrectomy, three patients died after 7, 12 and 129 days, respectively. One patient (p43) died following ICU admission for biliary peritonitis. The second patient (p32) suffered from relapsing peritonitis, which relapsed shortly after nephrectomy, and subsequently received palliative treatment. The third patient (p46) died after a nephrectomy of his two allografts. In hindsight, his fever and abdominal pain that were attributed to graft intolerance were caused by posttransplant lymphoproliferative disorder.

Length of hospitalization was significantly shorter after embolization compared to nephrectomy (median 1 vs. 5 days, p = 0.001, Table 2). Readmission rate within three months was not significantly different (54% vs. 38%, p = 0.34). The majority of patients received blood transfusions (62% after embolization versus 56% after nephrectomy, p = 1.00), with median 2 packed red blood cells in both groups (p = 0.61).

Post‐embolization Syndrome

Post‐embolization syndrome occurred in 9 of 13 patients (69%). This was managed conservatively with the administration of acetaminophen, and in some patients with antibiotics, anti‐emetics, steroids, or opioids. One patient (p7) with pre‐existing sepsis could not be weaned of noradrenaline after embolization and died.

Discussion

Percutaneous embolization of the renal allograft has been introduced as a less invasive alternative for graft nephrectomy [13]. In this study, we evaluated the first series of embolizations performed in our center. Length of hospitalization was significantly shorter for embolization compared to nephrectomy.

Post‐embolization syndrome was a common finding in our study and occurred in two‐third of patients, which was in line with other studies [11]. It was managed conservatively in all cases. Compared to previous studies [11], we found a higher rate of major complications after embolization. Two of thirteen patients (15%) required a subsequent graft nephrectomy within three months, for which other studies have reported comparable rates [11, 16, 17]. Notably, a third patient (p1) developed a graft infection late after embolization (32 months), for which nephrectomy was required. Most other major complications were unlikely related to embolization and seemed related to patients’ pre‐existing conditions, including inflammation that was, in retrospect, incorrectly diagnosed as graft intolerance (p6, p7).

After nephrectomy, we observed a relatively high complication rate of 41%. Previous studies that used a similar definition (Clavien Dindo score 3 or 4) have reported lower rates between 13%–21% [4, 10, 12, 18, 19]. Of note, many studies did not define a follow‐up period for complications. In our study, many complications were deemed related to the nephrectomy, such as drain insertions, abscess drainages, surgeries and ICU admissions [4].

Importantly, two patients (15%) died within three months after embolization, while other studies have reported low mortality rates [11]. These two patients (p6, p7) had not improved upon treatment for infection, after which graft intolerance syndrome was suspected as an alternative cause for their inflammatory state and embolization was performed. We recommend caution when treating patients with suspected graft intolerance with concomitant infection. In our study, patients with infection had poor outcomes both in the embolization and nephrectomy group (Table S1). For nephrectomy, acute infection has also been reported an independent risk factor for major post‐operative complications [10]. Especially for patients with systemic inflammation, any graft intervention should be considered with caution: three of the four patients with sepsis or bacteriemia in our cohort died after embolization or nephrectomy.

Although conservative immunosuppressive therapy may delay the need for acute intervention, it may often not constitute a definitive treatment in symptomatic patients [20]. One retrospective study has reported a nephrectomy after graft failure in 81% of patients with fever with unknown origin, though not specifying whether immunosuppressive therapy was attempted first [21]. A recent study found that graft intolerance syndrome resolved in 3 of 53 patients after treatment with steroids for one week, while the others needed an intervention [22]. A prospective study reported that 9 patients with rejection after graft failure underwent a nephrectomy, while 9 others could be treated with increased immunosuppression [23]. Whether a course of steroids could reduce complications after embolization or nephrectomy is unclear: one study reported no association with prednisone or immunosuppressants use and complications after nephrectomy [4]. For embolization, steroids could be administered to avoid post‐embolization syndrome [16].

One patient (p10) who underwent embolization for recurrent pyelonephritis and multiple other infections under immunosuppressant use, developed infected necrosis of the graft and required a subsequent nephrectomy. Recent graft infections should be considered a contra‐indication for embolization. Despite extensive testing for infection, graft infection after embolization has still been reported in patients with graft intolerance [6]. Studies addressing careful patient selection by excluding patients with prior urinary tract infections, together with blood and urine cultures and antibiotic prophylaxis, reported no infectious complications post‐embolization [16, 24]. Antibiotic prophylaxis should therefore be considered for allograft embolization, to prevent infection of the necrotic tissue [25].

Another patient (p12) had hematuria as a sign of ongoing graft intolerance after embolization and hence underwent subsequent nephrectomy. In retrospect, this was the only patient in this cohort exhibiting some residual blood flow immediately after embolization. Full hemostasis, as confirmed by angiography, is necessary before considering the embolization to be successful [26].

In our study, more than half of patients received blood transfusions, which is known to increase the risk of allosensitization [27]. Current data on sensitization upon embolization is limited [28], and studies to the sensitizing effect of nephrectomy and immunosuppression withdrawal are conflicting [29, 30, 31, 32]. Future studies to the management of patients with a failed graft are needed to decipher the differential impact of embolization and nephrectomy on allosensitization, and the effect on retransplant possibilities [9, 33, 34]. Of note, a strategy of embolization with subsequently scheduled nephrectomy has been shown to reduce operating time and blood loss [35, 36]. This could be beneficial for retransplant candidates, and patients with early graft loss with higher risk of complications and bleeding [19]. Prediction models may help to identify patients with high risk of developing graft intolerance [37].

Strengths and Limitations

This study gives insights in the clinical course after embolization, a procedure that is not yet adopted in the majority of transplant centers [38]. Of note, this study has the inherent bias of a retrospective analysis in a small cohort, including bias by indication. Time after transplantation and comorbidity tended to be higher for the embolization group. Our results should be interpreted with caution, and confirmed with future, prospective studies.

Conclusion

Embolization of the failed renal allograft results in shorter hospitalization and may have fewer major complications than graft nephrectomy. Pre‐existing sepsis is a risk factor for adverse outcomes, while recent graft infections are a contra‐indication for embolization. Based on current literature and our clinical data, we propose a clinical guidance for the management of these vulnerable patients with a failed graft and symptoms of rejection or infection (Figure 1). Future studies should investigate the role of pre‐emptive embolization in asymptomatic patients prone for the development of graft intolerance, as well as the risk of allosensitization.

Clinical guidance for the management of patients with a failed allograft and symptoms of graft intolerance or infection. A (recent) graft infection should be considered a contra‐indication for embolization. Patients with acute rejection should preferably be treated with combined embolization and nephrectomy, as this might reduce blood loss and the risk of allosensitization after blood transfusion.
