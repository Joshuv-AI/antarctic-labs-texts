Identifying influential nodes through hierarchical k-shell and extended neighborhood integration
Authors: Feifei Wang, Zejun Sun, Guan Wang, Haifeng Hu, Xiaoyan Sun, Shimeng Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe identification of influential nodes has extensive applications in complex network research. To address the challenge of balancing accuracy and computational efficiency in existing methods, this paper proposes an algorithm named HKEN that integrates hierarchical k-shell decomposition with extended neighborhood information. The approach incorporates both global structural features and local topological information of nodes. First, the coarse-grained hierarchical mechanism of the k-shell algorithm is optimized, and the initial weight of nodes is dynamically computed based on their degree and k-shell values. Second, the neighborhood range of nodes is extended, and a local clustering coefficient is introduced to establish a threshold for information transmission distance. Finally, an influence aggregation strategy based on the Jaccard similarity coefficient is proposed. The performance of HKEN was validated through comparative experiments on 10 real-world networks against 12 benchmark methods. Experimental results demonstrate that the proposed method achieves higher consistency with SIR model outcomes and enhances the propagation capability of top-ranked nodes, confirming its improved identification accuracy.Keywords: Complex networks, Influential nodes, Hierarchical k-shell, Extended neighborhoodSubject terms: Mathematics and computing, Physics

Introduction

With the deepening of research on complex networks, numerous scholars have applied complex network theory to various real-world networks1–3, such as social networks4–8, power grids9,10, disease transmission networks11,12, and transportation networks13–15. In these networks, entities in the real world are often abstracted as nodes, and the relationships between entities are abstracted as edges, thereby constructing corresponding complex network models to address practical problems using complex network theory. Among the many research directions in complex networks, the identification of influential nodes holds significant importance, as such nodes exert a substantial impact on both the topological structure and functionality of networks. For example, in social networks, identifying influential individuals can facilitate the spread of beneficial information while curbing the dissemination of harmful content; in power grids, prioritizing the protection and monitoring of influential nodes helps implement timely countermeasures during failures to prevent cascading outages and avoid large-scale blackouts; In criminal networks, identifying influential nodes enables the timely implementation of control measures to contain the proliferation of illicit activities. Therefore, the identification of influential nodes in complex networks not only carries theoretical significance but also possesses substantial practical value16–19.

Given the central position of influential nodes identification in the research of complex networks, this field has continuously received extensive attention from the academic community. Existing studies have fully demonstrated that the topological structure and its basic characteristics of networks play a decisive role in the process of identifying influential nodes20–25. Early influential node identification methods can generally be divided into local-structure-based and global-structure-based approaches26–28.

Local-structure-based methods mainly rely on neighborhood information, such as degree and clustering coefficient, and are computationally efficient. However, these methods often lack sufficient discrimination ability, especially when nodes share similar local structures. Global-structure-based methods incorporate broader topological information, including shortest-path-based measures and k-shell decomposition, and usually achieve more stable identification results. Nevertheless, many of these methods suffer from high computational complexity or coarse-grained ranking. To address these limitations, recent studies have attempted to integrate local and global information through hybrid frameworks24,25. Although such methods improve performance to some extent, they may introduce additional parameters or rely heavily on specific structural assumptions, which limits their robustness across different networks. This motivates the development of a more balanced and efficient framework for influential node identification.

This paper integrates the advantages of local and global structures and proposes an influential nodes identification method that combines hierarchical k-shell and extended neighborhood (HKEN). Firstly, this method calculates the initial weight of each node according to its degree and k-shell value. Subsequently, based on the weight, a threshold for the information transmission distance is set to classify second-order neighborhood nodes, and different strategies are adopted according to different categories to calculate the initial influence of the nodes. Finally, the Jaccard similarity coefficient29 is introduced to aggregate the influence contributions of one-hop neighbor nodes. Combining the importance of the nodes themselves, the comprehensive influence of the nodes in the network is ultimately determined. The novelty of HKEN lies not in individual structural measures, but in their mechanism-driven integration. By constraining neighborhood influence propagation through k-shell hierarchy and modulating the contribution of second-order inner neighborhood nodes based on similarity and clustering-based attenuation, HKEN provides a principled balance between global and local network characteristics. In summary, the main contributions of this algorithm are as follows:

Dynamic threshold strategy. Initial node weights are computed from local (degree) and global (k-shell) attributes, and a threshold is set for information transmission. Nodes are classified based on this threshold, with low-weight nodes downweighted and high-weight nodes amplified, reflecting realistic information spread.

Related work

To clearly position the proposed method, this section first reviews influential node identification algorithms from a conceptual and structural perspective. Subsequently, it elaborates in detail on the fundamental principles of the local clustering coefficient and the Jaccard similarity coefficient adopted in the proposed method, as well as their specific mechanisms for measuring node influence, providing a theoretical basis for the subsequent method design.

Classification of influential node identification algorithms

Existing influential node identification methods are categorized into global-structure-based, local-structure-based, and hybrid algorithms according to the scope of topological information they exploit. Representative approaches are discussed with emphasis on their core ideas and shared limitations, thereby revealing the research gap addressed in this work.

Global-structure-based algorithms

Global-structure-based algorithms evaluate node influence by leveraging macroscopic network topology. Classical methods include Betweenness Centrality (BC)30, Closeness Centrality (CC)31, k-shell decomposition32, and Eigenvector Centrality (EC)33.

BC and CC assess node importance through shortest-path information, effectively capturing global connectivity. However, both methods suffer from high computational complexity and limited scalability. The k-shell decomposition algorithm efficiently identifies core–periphery structures, yet its coarse-grained shell assignment limits discrimination among nodes within the same shell. Improved variants such as Extended Local K-Shell Sum (ELKSS)34 incorporates neighborhood k-shell information, but still over-rely on neighbor contributions, neglecting intrinsic node characteristics. EC considers both node degree and neighbor importance, making it suitable for modeling long-term influence, but it tends to overemphasize high-degree nodes and requires iterative computation.

Overall, global-structure-based methods capture large-scale structural properties but generally exhibit either high computational cost or insufficient ranking resolution.

Local-structure-based algorithms

Local-structure-based algorithms focus on immediate neighborhood information and are typically more computationally efficient. Representative approaches include ProfitLeader (PL)35 and Multilayer Neighbor Node Gravity and Information Entropy(MNNGE)36.

PL evaluates node influence through resource-sharing mechanisms among neighbors, using node degree and Jaccard similarity coefficient. Although effective in certain networks, it is highly sensitive to node degree, leading to biased evaluations in the presence of hub nodes. MNNGE integrates multilayer neighborhood gravity with information entropy to aggregate influence, but the entropy component mainly reflects weight dispersion rather than actual propagation capability, limiting interpretability.

In summary, local-structure-based methods offer efficiency and fine-grained local characterization, but their limited awareness of global topology restricts their effectiveness in complex networks.

Hybrid algorithms

Hybrid algorithms aim to integrate global and local information to overcome the limitations of single-perspective approaches.

The algorithm based on adaptive adjustment of voting ability (AAVA)37 dynamically adjusts voting ability based on node similarity, enhancing local influence aggregation. The algorithm for identifying influential nodes from a global perspective (GIN)38 combines node degree with distance-weighted global influence, achieving improved performance at the expense of increased computational complexity and parameter sensitivity. The algorithm for finding influential nodes in social networks based on the neighborhood correlation coefficient (ECRM)39 refines node hierarchy using k-shell decomposition and correlation analysis, but remains strongly dependent on k-shell values. The algorithm for identifying influential nodes based on the k-shell index and neighborhood information (BM)40 and the algorithm for measuring node influence based on structural and adjacency information (IC-SNI)41 further incorporate neighborhood structure, path information, and adjacency attributes to model influence propagation.

Although these methods alleviate some limitations of traditional centrality measures, they often rely on complex fusion mechanisms, restrictive path assumptions, or normalization strategies, which may introduce bias and reduce robustness.

Research gap and motivation

From the above review, existing influential node identification methods face a persistent trade-off among global structural awareness, local discriminability, and computational efficiency. Global methods lack fine-grained resolution, local methods ignore long-range structural effects, and hybrid approaches often introduce excessive complexity or parameter dependence.

To address these issues, this paper proposes an influential node identification method that integrates hierarchical k-shell structure with extended neighborhood information, leveraging the local clustering coefficient and Jaccard similarity coefficient to enhance influence discrimination while maintaining computational efficiency. This design provides a balanced and interpretable framework for identifying influential nodes in complex networks.

Proposed algorithm

This study presents a method for identifying influential nodes, which combines the hierarchical k-shell algorithm with extended neighborhood information and mainly involves three stages. Firstly, the initial weight of each node is computed using the degree of the node, its k-shell value, and the degrees of other nodes in the network. Secondly, the threshold of information transmission distance is calculated, nodes are categorized according to their initial weights, and the clustering coefficient is utilized to calculate the initial influence of the nodes. Finally, relying on the Jaccard similarity coefficient, the contribution of neighboring nodes to the spread of the target node is calculated.

Time complexity analysis of the algorithm

The proposed HKEN algorithm primarily comprises three stages. The first stage involves computing the foundational information required by the algorithm, including the k-shell values, degree values and their averages, as well as the initial weight of each node. Since these computations entail only linear operations over the node set, the time complexity of this stage is O(n), where n denotes the total number of nodes. The second stage employs a nested loop structure to calculate the initial influence of each node. The outer loop iterates over all nodes i; for each node i, the algorithm further traverses its first-order neighboring nodes j. For each j, it continues to traverse its first-order neighbors to acquire the second-order neighboring nodes k of i. Consequently, the time complexity of this stage is closely related to the average degree of the nodes, resulting in an overall complexity of O(n < k > 2), where < k > represents the average degree of the network. The third stage is mainly dedicated to computing the final influence of the nodes. Through a double-layer loop, the influence of each node is calculated, with a time complexity of O(n < k >).

Based on the above analysis, the overall time complexity of the HKEN algorithm is O(n < k > 2). In most real-world networks, the average degree O(n < k >) is generally much smaller than the total number of nodes n; hence, the algorithm exhibits high efficiency and scalability in practical applications.

Evaluation metrics

Multiple evaluation metrics are employed to conduct a comparative analysis between the proposed HKEN algorithm, the aforementioned twelve baseline algorithms, and the experimental results derived from the SIR model, thereby enabling a comprehensive assessment of its performance.

SIR model

The SIR model is a mathematical model utilized to describe the process of infectious disease propagation and is currently widely adopted in the evaluation of influential node identification algorithms in complex networks43. In this model, nodes are categorized into three types based on their states: Susceptible (S), Infected (I), and Recovered (R). Specifically, Susceptible denotes that a node is in a state vulnerable to infection, with a certain probability of being infected; Infected indicates that a node is in an infected state and possesses the ability to transmit the disease; Recovered signifies that a node is in a recovered state and no longer participates in subsequent propagation processes. Experimental results and analysis

This section evaluates the HKEN algorithm through multiple approaches: analyzing the infection scale to examine the spread of high-impact nodes, using the Kendall’s tau correlation coefficient to verify the consistency of the ranking consistency with SIR, and employing auxiliary indicators such as the Jaccard similarity coefficient of the Top N nodes and the infection capability of the Top 10 nodes. This strategy adheres to the standards of complex network analysis45, overcomes the limitations of a single indicator, and ensures a comprehensive assessment from both static and dynamic perspectives.

Spreading ability

To validate the effectiveness of the HKEN algorithm in identifying influential nodes, the node ranking sequences obtained by the HKEN algorithm and various baseline algorithms were compared with the sequence generated by the SIR model, thereby acquiring the spreading capacity of the corresponding nodes in the SIR model. If the node sequence identified by an algorithm matches that produced by the SIR model, the recognition performance should be reflected as a smooth and monotonically decreasing curve; otherwise, fluctuations will be observed in the curve. To present algorithmic performance more clearly and intuitively, the infection probability in the SIR model was uniformly set to 0.05 in this experiment, and the probability of recovery was set to 1. Because the Ca-Astroph network is large and the running time is too long, the number of simulation iterations is set to 100, and the number of simulation iterations for the other networks is set to 1000. Jaccard similarity coefficient within the top N influential nodes

In real-world networks, the number of highly influential nodes is generally limited. These nodes possess stronger propagation capabilities compared to others and play crucial roles in the network. The ability to accurately identify such nodes is an important metric for evaluating algorithm performance. Typically, as the scale of a network increases, the absolute number of influential nodes also rises. In small-scale networks, the total number of nodes is limited, resulting in a relatively small number of influential nodes. In medium-scale networks, the number of influential nodes increases along with the growth in node count. In large-scale networks, although the proportion of influential nodes relative to the total number of nodes may be small, their absolute quantity remains significant. The top 5% of nodes play a critical role in the connectivity and functionality of the entire network, being sufficient to significantly disrupt it46. Studies have shown that while the most influential nodes typically constitute less than 10% of a network32, the critical subset for maintaining robustness is often narrower, encompassing the top 1% to 5% of nodes47,48. Therefore, when calculating the Jaccard similarity coefficient between the sets of influential nodes identified by each algorithm and those derived from the SIR model, this study considers the relatively small sizes of the Karate and Dolphins networks. For these, the computation range is set to 5 to 10 nodes. For the remaining eight datasets, the Jaccard similarity coefficient is calculated within the range of 1 to 5% of nodes.

Statistical variability analysis of SIR simulation results

To quantify the transmission variability introduced by randomness in the SIR process, four representative networks—Karate, USAir, Citeseer, and Powergrid—were selected for experimentation. In the SIR model, the transmission probability α was set within the range [0.01, 0.1] with a step size of 0.01. Each α value was independently simulated 1,000 times to calculate the mean infected scale (Mean_Infected), standard deviation of infected scale (Std), and coefficient of variation (CV). Mean_Infected represents the average number of infected nodes across 1,000 simulations, used to evaluate the average diffusion capacity of the seed node set under specific transmission conditions. Std reflects the dispersion of simulation results, where a larger value indicates greater deviation from the mean, suggesting higher instability in the transmission process. CV is defined as the ratio of standard deviation to mean infected scale (CV = Std / Mean_Infected). Normalization was applied to eliminate dimensional effects, enabling more accurate comparison of relative volatility under different α conditions.

As shown in Table 9, as α gradually increases from 0.01 to 0.1, the average infection rate of each network shows a significant upward trend. At the same time, the standard deviation and coefficient of variation also increase simultaneously, indicating that under stronger transmission intensity, the random variability of the SIR dynamics is amplified. Although dense networks (such as Citeseer) exhibit higher absolute infection scales, their relative volatility (CV: 0.14–0.20) is similar to that of sparse networks (such as Powergrid, CV: 0.09–0.20), suggesting that network topology mainly affects the breadth of transmission, while the relative random intensity of the transmission process is consistent across networks. All CV values are below 0.22, and combined with the stable statistical indicators obtained from 1,000 simulations, they confirm that the observed performance differences are not due to random fluctuations. Despite the inherent randomness of the SIR process, the coefficient of variation and standard deviation remained at low levels across different transmission probabilities and network conditions, indicating that the observed performance differences are statistically stable.

Conclusion

This paper comprehensively takes into account the local and global structural characteristics of nodes and proposes an influential nodes identification method that integrates the hierarchical k-shell algorithm and extended neighborhood information. This method calculates the initial weight of each node by combining the node degree, k-shell value, and the average degree of nodes in the network. To some extent, it alleviates the problem of insufficient node discrimination ability caused by the coarsening of the traditional k-shell algorithm. Subsequently, the scope of influence propagation of nodes is extended to the second-order neighborhood. Based on the maximum value of the initial weights of all nodes, the information transmission distance threshold is determined, and then the neighbor nodes within the second-order neighborhood are classified. On this basis, the local clustering coefficient is introduced and integrated with the idea of the gravity model to quantify the influence contribution of neighborhood nodes to the target node. Then, combined with the initial weight of the node itself, the initial influence of the node is calculated. Finally, the Jaccard similarity coefficient is used to perform weighted aggregation on the initial influence of the one-hop neighbors of the target node and fuse it with the initial influence of the node itself, thereby obtaining the final influence of the node. This stage further highlights the leading role of core nodes in the propagation process. Experimental results show that the HKEN algorithm can more accurately identify influential nodes in complex networks through a weighted hierarchical mechanism, an extended neighborhood influence range, and a multi-source influence aggregation strategy. It provides more robust and adaptable solutions for practical application scenarios such as the discovery of opinion leaders in social networks and the control of the source of major disease spread.

Despite its effectiveness, HKEN has certain limitations. In networks with near-random connectivity and weak hierarchical structure, the advantage of k-shell–based constraints may be less pronounced. In highly clustered networks, redundant neighborhood information may reduce the marginal benefit of similarity-weighted aggregation. Moreover, HKEN relies solely on static structural information and does not account for temporal or semantic factors. These limitations suggest potential directions for future extensions.
