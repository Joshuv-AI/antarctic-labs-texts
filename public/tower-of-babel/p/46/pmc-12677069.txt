Utilization of youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health care services among secondary school students in southern Ethiopia
Authors: Yonas Abebe, Daniel Yehualashet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study assesses the utilization of Youth-Friendly Sexual and Reproductive Health Care Services and associated factors among secondary school students in Southern Ethiopia, 2024.MethodsAn institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among 365 students in Dilla Town from August to December 2024. Participants were selected from all six secondary schools using a stratified random sampling method. Binary logistic regression was employed to identify factors associated with youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health services. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 26.ResultsThe prevalence of youth friendly sexual and reproductive health care utilization was 38.6 % (95 % CI: 33.6, 43.8). Factors significantly associated with utilization included urban residency (AOR = 1.96, 95 % CI: 1.03, 3.71), acceptance of service confidentiality (AOR = 5.95, 95 % CI: 3.04, 11.65), absence of stigma (AOR = 3.99, 95 % CI: 2.04,7.78), proximity of service centers (AOR = 2.58, 95 % CI: 1.27, 5.22), social media use (AOR = 3.09, 95 % CI: 1.13, 8.50), and open family communication on SRH (AOR = 5.99, 95 % CI: 2.16, 16.64).ConclusionsUtilization of youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health services was low. Strengthening confidentiality, reducing stigma, and promoting family communication are critical for improving uptake and supporting informed health choices.Keywords: Youth sexual health, Adolescent reproductive health, Youth-friendly care, Sexual health stigma, Ethiopia

Introduction

Globally, there are over 1.8 billion young people, with the vast majority (90 %) residing in developing nations, where they often constitute a significant portion of the total population (UNFPA, 2022). In Ethiopia, adolescents and youth aged 10 to 29 years represent approximately 42 % of the country's total population, and the youth age group, typically defined by the UN as 15–24, is a critical period of development due to several interlinked processes, including sexual and brain maturation, and identity formation (Ministry Of Health Ethiopia. Reproductive Health, Family Planning and Adolescent Health Desk | MINISTRY OF HEALTH - Ethiopia, 2025; United Nations Youth. Definition of youth, 2009; Arain et al., 2013).

Youth sexuality is a central component of reproductive health, shaping both physical and emotional well-being. It influences the risk of unintended pregnancies, sexually transmitted infections (STIs), and other adverse health outcomes (World Health Organization youth. WHO Recommendations On Adolescent Sexual and Reproductive Health and Rights, 2018). In Ethiopia, adolescents and youth face significant SRH challenges, including risky sexual behaviors, child marriage, early childbirth, unintended pregnancies, unsafe abortions, and complications, and STIs such as HIV. The 2016 Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey (EDHS) reports a median age at first marriage of 17.1 years for women and 23.7 years for men (Ministry Of Health Ethiopia. Reproductive Health, Family Planning and Adolescent Health Desk | MINISTRY OF HEALTH - Ethiopia, 2025; CSA. Ethiopian Demographic and Health Survey 2016: Key Indicators Report. Ethiop Water Sect Dev Progr. Published online, 2016).

Youth-Friendly Sexual and Reproductive Health Services (YFSRHS) are designed to provide accessible, acceptable, and appropriate care for young people, including sexuality counseling, voluntary HIV testing and counseling (VCT), STI management, contraception and condoms, perinatal care, and abortion/post-abortion care (Motuma et al., 2016; Tsegaw et al., 2023). Despite their importance, young people in Sub-Saharan Africa continue to face higher risks of SRH challenges compared to their peers in other regions of the world (Haile et al., 2020). Although the Ethiopian government introduced youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health services in 2015, young people continue to face higher reproductive health risks than adults (Abebe and Awoke, 2014).

Previous studies in Ethiopia have highlighted multilayered barriers to SRH care utilization, including supply and demand side constraints, fear of stigma from family and community, restrictive social norms, and limited discussion of SRH issues (Sidamo et al., 2024a; Admassu et al., 2022; Dadi et al., 2025; Habte and Dessu, 2023). Despite significant policies, programs, and initiatives aimed at improving youth reproductive health, service utilization rates remain low (Admassu et al., 2022; Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia; Ministry of Health. National Adolescents and Youth Health Strategy 2021–2025, 2021). While existing research has explored broad determinants of adolescent SRH, such as stigma, digital media use, and family communication about sexuality, there is a lack of evidence on the specific patterns of YFSRHS utilization among secondary school students in southern Ethiopia. To address this gap, this study evaluates the uptake of youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health services (YFSRHS) and its determinants among secondary school youth in Dilla Town, South Ethiopia, in 2024, focusing on perceived stigma, digital media exposure, and parent–adolescent communication.

Methods

Study design and population

An institution-based cross-sectional study was conducted among secondary school students in Dilla Town, Gedeo Zone, and Southern Ethiopia. Dilla is located approximately 356 km south of Addis Ababa and has a total population of 158,795 (80,055 males and 78,740 females). The town comprises thirteen primary and six secondary schools, namely: Dilla Secondary (1428 students), Woinshet Secondary (980), Hambule Secondary (876), Dom Bosco Secondary (1552), Koffe Secondary (562), and Dama Secondary (613), with a combined enrollment of 6011 students. Dilla was purposefully selected for its representativeness as a zonal town within Ethiopia's ethnically and culturally diverse southern region, providing an appropriate setting for assessing youth sexual and reproductive health (SRH) service utilization. Data were collected between August and December 2024.

The source population consisted of all students enrolled in secondary schools within Dilla Town, while the study population included students randomly selected from public secondary schools who were available during the data collection period. All students attending public secondary schools and present at the time of data collection were eligible for inclusion. Students who declined participation or were unable to respond due to illness were excluded.

The sample size was determined using a single population proportion formula, with the following assumptions: 95 % confidence level (Zα/2 = 1.96), estimated SRH service utilization among youth (p = 33.8 %) based on prior literature and a 5 % margin of error (d = 0.05). After accounting for a 10 % non-response rate, the final sample size was 379.

A multi-stage stratified random sampling technique was employed to ensure proportional representation of students across all six secondary schools and grades 9–12. The total sample was proportionally allocated to each school and grade according to enrollment figures. Within each grade, participants were randomly selected from official student rosters using computer-generated random numbers in Microsoft Excel. Data collection procedures and quality assurance

Data were collected using a self-administered structured questionnaire adapted from John Cleland and colleagues' illustrative questionnaire for surveys with young people, previously used in Ethiopia for similar studie (Tsegaw et al., 2023; Habte and Dessu, 2023; Cleland et al., 2005; Tlaye et al., 2018). The questionnaire was drafted in English, translated into Amharic, and back-translated into English to ensure consistency. Four BSc midwives collected the data under the supervision of two MSc-level reproductive health experts. To ensure data quality, a comprehensive protocol was implemented, including pre-testing at Hawassa Tabor secondary school, Sidama Region, Ethiopia, with 5 % of the sample one week prior to the main survey. The pre-test results informed refinements, such as restructuring complex sentences, rephrasing unfamiliar words, and confirming internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.72). All data collectors received one-day intensive training on standardized administration and data integrity, and daily supervision was maintained, with the principal investigator reviewing questionnaires for completeness and providing immediate feedback.

Study variables

Dependent variable

The dependent variable of this study was youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health (SRH) service utilization, defined as the use of at least one of the following services: SRH education; sexually transmitted infection (STI) prevention or treatment; HIV counseling and testing (VCT); modern contraceptive counseling or provision; safe abortion care (SAC); peer education; prevention of violence against women and girls (VAWG) counseling; information on harmful traditional practices (HTPs); or perinatal services (Habte and Dessu, 2023).

Independent variables

The independent variables in this study were grouped into four major categories: socio-demographic, behavioral, knowledge and communication, and health service–related factors. The operational definitions and measurement approaches are outlined as follows.

Socio-demographic factors

Socio-demographic characteristics included age, sex, residence, religion, grade level, father's and mothers educational status, marital status, and parents' living condition.

Age was recorded in completed years and categorized as 15–19 or 20–24 years. Sex was classified as male or female. Residence was categorized as urban or rural according to the student's current place of living. Religion was self-reported and grouped as Orthodox Christian, Protestant, Muslim, Catholic, or others. Grade level referred to the student's current school year (grades 9–12). Parental educational status was categorized as no formal education, elementary (enrolled or completed), secondary (enrolled), first degree, or master's and above. Marital status was coded as single, married, or divorced. Parents' living condition referred to whether both parents were alive and living together, divorced/separated, one alive, or both deceased.

Behavioral related factors

Behavioral factors included social media use, having a sexual partner, number of sexual partners, and age at first sexual intercourse. Social media use indicated whether respondents used platforms such as Facebook, Telegram, or TikTok to access or share information related to sexual and reproductive health. Having a sexual partner referred to whether a student currently had or ever had a sexual partner. The number of sexual partners was categorized as one as or more than one among those who had sexual experience. Age at first sexual intercourse referred to the age when a student first engaged in sexual activity and was grouped as 15–19 or 20–24 years.

Knowledge and communication factors

This category included knowledge about youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health (SRH) services, knowledge about sexually transmitted infection (STI) prevention, openness of family communication, and source of information about SRH services. Knowledge about youth-friendly SRH services was assessed using a structured questionnaire; participants scoring above the mean were classified as having good knowledge, while those at or below the mean were categorized as having poor knowledge (Haile et al., 2020). Knowledge about STI prevention was assessed by asking whether respondents were aware of methods to prevent STIs. Openness of family communication referred to whether students discussed SRH issues freely with their parents or guardians. Source of SRH information was identified as health workers, social media, family, or none.

Health service related factors

Health service related variables included distance to the nearest health facility, availability of youth-friendly SRH service centers, acceptance of confidentiality, and fear of stigma. Distance to the nearest health facility was measured in terms of walking time and categorized as ≤30 min or > 30 min. Availability of youth-friendly service centers referred to whether students attended schools in areas where such services were easily accessible (easy/not easy). Acceptance of confidentiality indicated whether respondents perceived that health providers maintained privacy when delivering SRH services. Fear of stigma referred to whether students felt fear of being judged or discriminated against when seeking SRH services.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics, including means, standard deviations, percentages, frequency tables, and graphs, were used to summarize the data. To identify predictors of the outcome variable, both binary and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. Variables exhibiting a p-value less than 0.25 in the binary logistic regression were considered candidates for inclusion in the multivariable model. The strength of association between predictor and outcome variables was assessed using Adjusted Odds Ratios (AORs) with 95 % confidence intervals. In the final multivariable model, statistical significance was defined as a p-value less than 0.05, with a corresponding 95 % confidence interval. Data were initially entered using EpiData version 7.2, after which they were exported to SPSS version 26 for analysis.

Factors associated with youth friendly sexual and reproductive health care service utilization among secondary school students in southern Ethiopia, 2024

This study identified several factors as predictors of youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health service (YFSRHS) utilization: place of residence, acceptance of confidentiality of service use, fear of stigma, attending schools in areas where service centers are easily available, social media use, education level (grades 11 and 12), having a sexual partner, and communication with family. The findings show that students from urban areas utilized YFSRHS approximately two times more than those from rural areas (AOR = 1.96, 95 % CI: 1.03, 3.71). Students who accepted the confidentiality of service use utilized YFSRHS about six times more than their counterparts (AOR = 5.95, 95 % CI: 3.04, 11.65). Those with no fear of stigma utilized these services four times more than their counterparts (AOR = 3.99, 95 % CI: 2.04, 7.78). Students in areas where YFSRHS centers were available utilized them more than two times compared to those in areas where they were not available (AOR = 2.58, 95 % CI: 1.27, 5.22). Social media users utilized YFSRHS three times more than non-users (AOR = 3.09, 95 % CI: 1.13, 8.50). Students in grades 11 and 12 utilized YFSRHS two times and three times more, respectively, than those in grade 9 (Grade 11: AOR = 2.52, 95 % CI: 1.01, 6.27; Grade 12: AOR = 3.28, 95 % CI: 1.29, 8.36). Students who had a sexual partner utilized YFSRHS seven times more than those who did not (AOR = 6.90, 95 % CI: 3.10, 15.22). Finally, students who openly communicated with their families regarding sexual and reproductive health issues utilized YFSRHS six times more than those who did not (AOR = 5.99, 95 % CI: 2.16, 16.64) (Table 4).

Discussion

The study found that the overall magnitude of YFSRHS utilization was 38.6 %, 95 % C·I (33.6 %, 43.8 %).This indicates that fewer than four out of ten youth in Dilla town have received these important services. When compared with other studies, this finding is consistent with those from Bahir Dar Town, Northern Ethiopia (Abebe and Awoke, 2014); Debra Berhan Town, North-Central Ethiopia (Tlaye et al., 2018); Awebal District, Northwest Ethiopia (Ayehu et al., 2016) and Boset District, Ethiopia (Agero and Bogale, 2020). This consistency may arise from a shared sociocultural context, including cultural heritage, socioeconomic conditions, and healthcare infrastructure. It may also reflect similar challenges in service delivery, such as limited resources, inadequate training of healthcare providers, and logistical difficulties in reaching adolescents in both urban and rural areas. The finding of this study was lower than those reported from Harar Town, Eastern Ethiopia (Motuma et al., 2016), and Gondar Town, North Ethiopia (Feleke et al., 2013). Differences with the Gondar study may be explained by methodological factors: that study focused solely on two components of YFSRHS family planning and voluntary counseling and testing (VCT) and employed a large, community-based sample, whereas the current study was school-based and considered a broader set of YFSRHS components. Similarly, the Harar study, also community-based, likely included a population with different characteristics, such as higher rates of child marriage (Lami et al., 2023), which can influence YFSRHS utilization due to school dropout among married youth.

The finding of this study indicates that students from urban areas utilized YFSRHS approximately twice as often as those from rural areas. Studies from Nigeria (Ilori et al., 2020) and Ghana (Okyere et al., 2022) report similar trends, with rural youth less likely to access youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health services. Disparities between urban and rural utilization are influenced by awareness, accessibility, and cultural attitudes, with rural youth facing significant barriers including a lack of knowledge about service locations and stigma (Ilori et al., 2020; Thongmixay et al., 2019). In this study, attending schools in areas where service centers are available and the fear of stigma were additional determinants of YFSRHS utilization. Students with no fear of stigma utilized YFSRHS four times more often than their counterparts, while those who perceived the services as confidential utilized YFSRHS about six times more often. These findings underscore the importance of confidential, stigma-free services. Other studies have reported similar results, highlighting that fear of stigma and lack of privacy are major barriers, whereas facilities ensuring privacy and youth involvement in decision-making achieve higher utilization rates (Haile et al., 2020; Ninsiima et al., 2021; Oyoo et al., 2024).

Furthermore, students in areas with available YFSRHS centers utilized services more than twice as often as those in areas without. Other studies from Ethiopia report similar circumstances; for example, utilization rates were significantly higher in areas with YFSRHS (33.8 %) compared to areas without (9.9 %) (Haile et al., 2020). This suggests that the presence of youth-friendly services can more than double the likelihood of utilization.

Social media users utilized youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health services three times more often than non-users. This includes users of platforms such as Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok, indicating a strong association between social media use and YFSRHS uptake. These findings are supported by other studies (Ochieng, 2023; Burns et al., 2020; Gabarron and Wynn, 2016) and suggest that social media platforms may be effective channels for information dissemination, awareness campaigns, or peer influence related to these services. Students in grades 11 and 12 utilized YFSRHS two and three times more often, respectively, than those in grade 9. This trend, also observed in other studies (Delie et al., 2024; Abate and Ayisa, 2019), may be attributed to factors such as greater knowledge, prior sexual experience, better accessibility, and stronger social influences.

Students who have a sexual partner utilized YFSRHS seven times more often than those without partners. Being sexually experienced significantly increases the likelihood of using SRH services (Binu et al., 2018; Simegn et al., 2020; Gunta et al., 2021; Elefachew et al., 2024). These findings suggest the need to provide comprehensive SRH education before students become sexually active, focus SRH promotion efforts on sexually active students, and ensure easy access to services for all students, particularly those who are sexually active.

Students who openly communicate with their families about sexual and reproductive health issues utilized YFSRHS six times more often than those who did not. This highlights the importance of family communication in promoting youth-friendly SRH service utilization (Hall et al., 2012; Sidamo et al., 2024b; Waga et al., 2023). Given that family–youth communication on SRH issues is very low in Ethiopia (Mekie et al., 2020), comprehensive strategies involving families are critical.

The generalizability of these findings should be interpreted cautiously, as this study focused on in-school youth, a structured and accessible group that may not represent out-of-school adolescents, who often face greater vulnerabilities such as poverty, limited health literacy, and poor healthcare access. Despite this, the study's strength lies in its comprehensive analysis, incorporating previously overlooked variables relevant to vulnerable populations.

Conclusions

Utilization of youth-friendly sexual and reproductive health services among secondary school students in Dilla Town was suboptimal, influenced by residence, stigma, grade level, family communication, confidentiality, and service availability. Targeted interventions are needed to reduce stigma, enhance confidentiality, and improve rural access. Policy and programmatic implications include expanding YFSRHS in underserved areas, integrating sexual and reproductive health education into schools, strengthening family engagement, and reaching out-of-school youth. These actions align with Ethiopia's National Adolescent and Youth Health Strategy (2021–2025) (Federal Democratic Republic of Ethiopia; Ministry of Health. National Adolescents and Youth Health Strategy 2021–2025, 2021) to ensure equitable and effective service delivery.

Ethics approval & consent to participate

This study received ethical approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the College of Medicine and Health Sciences at Dilla University (Reference Number: Du/IRB/097/2024). Before commencing data collection, written permission was secured from the relevant Zonal Health Office, Education offices, and the principals of each participating school. Comprehensive written informed consent was obtained from all study participants after providing a thorough explanation of the study's aims and objectives. For participants under 18 years of age, informed consent was obtained from their parents or legal guardians and assent was taken from minors themselves. Participants were clearly informed that their involvement was entirely voluntary and that they had the right to withdraw from the study at any time without any repercussions. To ensure anonymity, all data collection tools were completely anonymous. Questionnaires did not request any personal identifiers, including names, student identification numbers, or telephone numbers and etc. Furthermore, upon completion, the questionnaires were collected and stored securely, with data analysis conducted on aggregated information only. All study procedures adhered to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki.
