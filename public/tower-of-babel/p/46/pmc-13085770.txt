Kinetics of antibodies and risk of respiratory syncytial virus infection: a longitudinal cohort in Taizhou City, eastern China
Authors: Qiang Wang, David Hodgson, Bo Zheng, Hongbiao Liang, Suyi Zhang, Jiahao Xu, Kathy Leung, Adam Kucharski, Haijiang Lin, Weibing Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRespiratory syncytial virus (RSV) causes recurrent infections throughout life. Yet, the form and durability of antibody-mediated protection induced by infection remained poorly understood.MethodsWe conducted a longitudinal cohort study in Taizhou City, China. Participant age distribution approximately reflected the age structure of population in Taizhou. Blood samples were collected at baseline (March 12, 2023) and four follow-up visits (May 7–26, 2023, August 13–23, 2023, November 12, 2023, and June 2–3, 2024). Serum-specific RSV pre-fusion F (PreF) protein antibody titres were measured for all samples, and neutralising antibodies against RSV strain A2 and RSV strain B were assessed in a subset. Using a Bayesian inference framework and reversible-jump Markov Chain Monte Carlo, we recovered individual infection histories, estimated population-level RSV incidence, and characterised antibody dynamics from longitudinal PreF titres. We also estimated the correlates of protection (COP) by quantifying the relationship between PreF antibody titres and infection risk.ResultsA total of 508 individuals were included. Over the study period, two RSV epidemic waves were observed: the first wave between May and November 2023 and the second from February to May 2024. We estimated seasonal RSV infection rates of 4–7% in the community-based population. The post-infection antibody titres declined substantially, with fourfold rises sustained for an average of 128 days (95% credible interval of 21–281). Background

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a major viral pathogen responsible for respiratory infections. Nearly all children are infected with RSV by the age of two [1] and, despite the immunity generated following infection, the protection is not long-lasting, allowing for recurrent infections throughout life [2]. Given the propensity for reinfection in older children, adults, and the elderly, it is essential to evaluate RSV burden within the community. However, accurately determining the incidence of RSV infection presents challenges, as many infections are asymptomatic or subclinical [3]. Even with comprehensive active surveillance programmes that frequently collect individuals’ nasopharyngeal swabs for RSV testing, some infections may go undetected [3–5]. For instance, suboptimal sampling intervals may result in missed cases of short-duration infections [5].

Measurements of serum antibodies are valuable for identifying pathogen infections [6]. Serological surveys typically estimate either seropositivity, determined by an antibody titre threshold, or seroconversion, defined as a fourfold increase in antibody titres between paired serum samples [7, 8]. Longitudinal serological samples can be utilised to estimate the incidence of RSV infection in communities. The choice of a fourfold increase threshold has traditionally relied on heuristic rules (e.g. fourfold rise as the threshold determining influenza or RSV infection) [8–10]. Such methods, however, fail to account for individual variability in immune responses, the kinetics of antibody rise and decay, and host factors such as prior infection history, age, and immune maturation. Bayesian hierarchical modelling offers an alternative approach by incorporating multiple epidemiological processes, including history of infection, the relationship between observed and true antibody titres, and antibody dynamics [11]. This individual-level modelling enables the estimation of infection incidence without depending on fixed thresholds [12, 13].

In this study, we applied these ideas to RSV using a longitudinal, community-based cohort of 508 participants in Taizhou, eastern China, who were followed over 16 months. We analysed titres of RSV pre-fusion F protein (PreF) antibodies with serojump, a Bayesian inference framework for inferring infections and antibody kinetics from longitudinal serological data [14]. By leveraging reversible-jump Markov Chain Monte Carlo (RJ-MCMC), serojump could recover individual infection histories, estimate population-level RSV incidence, and characterise antibody dynamics. These estimates could enable us to define a continuous relationship between pre-infection antibody titres and the subsequent infection risk, therefore identifying correlates of risk and protection (COR/COP) for RSV PreF. We further investigated how these correlates vary across age groups, providing insights into the age-dependent nature of RSV immunity. By linking antibody levels to protection, our work explored evidence relevant to public health strategies, vaccine development, and immune surveillance.

Methods

Study design and participants

We conducted a longitudinal study in Taizhou City, Zhejiang Province, eastern China, from March 12,2023 (baseline visit) (Additional file 1: Fig. S1). In Taizhou, RSV circulation has historically exhibited a winter–spring pattern, with activity typically occurring between January and April [15]. However, RSV seasonality was markedly disrupted during the COVID-19 pandemic. In 2020, the spring RSV season was shortened [16]. In 2021, Taizhou experienced near year-round RSV circulation, with a peak during the summer [17]. RSV activity in Taizhou declined substantially after early 2022, with no clear epidemic observed throughout 2022. Taizhou comprised three districts, three counties, and three county-level cities. Huangyan District was randomly selected. Within Huangyan, Dongcheng Sub-district and Shabu Town were selected by random sampling, and two communities, Tayuan (Dongcheng) and Niansidu (Shabu), were subsequently included.

A total of four subsequent follow-up visits were conducted, including visit 1 (May 7–26, 2023), visit 2 (August 13–23, 2023), visit 3 (November 12, 2023), and visit 4 (June 2–3, 2024). The age distribution of the recruited participants was determined according to the age distribution of Taizhou’s population (Additional file 2: Table 1). Community administrative committee staff provided resident lists, from which participants were selected using simple random sampling. Selected individuals were contacted by committee staff and invited to attend a designated community healthcare centre for enrolment. For participants under 18 years of age, their guardians were contacted for enrolment. Individuals who had resided in the community for less than 3 months prior to enrolment were excluded. Detailed information on the study design was provided in Additional file 3: Note 1 [18].

Procedures

At each round, a venous blood sample (2 ml) was drawn from each participant. Serum-specific RSV PreF antibody titres were quantified using an indirect enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay for all collected samples. Briefly, 96-well plates were coated overnight at 4 °C with a stabilised recombinant RSV PreF trimer protein and blocked prior to incubation with serially diluted serum samples (1:1000 to 1:64,000). Horseradish peroxidase–conjugated anti-human immunoglobulin G (IgG) secondary antibody was added, followed by colorimetric detection. Antibody titres were determined by interpolation between dilution points spanning the cut-off using the FORECAST function in Microsoft Excel. For each plate, the cut-off value was defined as 2.1 times the mean optical density value of duplicate negative control wells (pooled human purified serum). A focus reduction neutralisation test (FRNT) was also conducted to assess the neutralising activity against RSV strain A2 and RSV strain B involving 30 individuals with 143 samples. Serially diluted serum samples (1:20 to 1:43,740) were incubated with live RSV prior to infection of A549 cell monolayers. Viral foci were visualised by immunostaining, and neutralising titres were defined as the serum dilution resulting in a 50% reduction in focus counts compared with virus-only controls. More details about laboratory procedure were described in Additional file 3: Note 2.

Community-level RSV activity was monitored using routine influenza-like illness (ILI) surveillance data from two national influenza sentinel hospitals in Taizhou. ILI cases were identified according to a standardised definition (fever ≥ 38 °C with cough or sore throat), and nasopharyngeal swabs were collected daily from a proportion of ILI patients. Approximately 20–50 specimens were collected per week. Real-time reverse-transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) assays were used to determine RSV positivity in the collected samples.

Descriptive statistical analyses

We reported the antibody titres translated on a log10 scale by age group for each round. The population was divided into five age groups: ≤ 5 years (young children), 6–18 years (school-aged children), 19–59 years (adults), 60–74 years (younger older adults), and ≥ 75 years (older adults). We explored the correlations between PreF antibody titre and neutralising antibody (nAb) titres using Spearman’s correlation coefficient. The 2-sided P < 0.05 was considered to be significant.

To consider the impact of age on antibody kinetics, we added hierarchical effects on r and y1. For the observational model, we assumed that antibody measurements followed a normal distribution with prior deviation, σ ~ Exponential(1). Quantifying the suitability of correlates of protection

To evaluate how well COPs discriminate between exposed but protected and exposed not protected individuals, we computed four complementary summary metrics from the fitted age-specific risk models. The first was the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), which quantifies discriminative ability. Second, we calculated coverage, defined as the proportion of individuals with titres at exposure between the 10th and 90th percentiles of the logistic curve. Third, we estimated the Average Marginal Gain (AMG) at the median titre, which represented the difference in predicted protection between the median titre and a four-fold higher titre. Finally, we calculated the AMG at log10 4, which measured the difference in protection between a titre of log10 4 and a fourfold higher titre. Together, these metrics provided complementary perspectives: AUC evaluated predictive accuracy, coverage reflected population-level attainability, and the AMG metrics captured the additional protection afforded by boosting titres above key thresholds.

Results

Study participants

The baseline cohort included 508 individuals, with a median age of 54 years (interquartile range (IQR): 39–63), and a sex distribution of 50.2% male. The distribution of recruited participants approximately reflected the age distribution of Taizhou’s population, which did not strictly achieve proportional representation. Follow-up data were collected from 485, 462, 448, and 438 participants during visits 1, 2, 3, and 4, respectively (Additional file 2: Table 2). The dropout rate of study participants was 13.8% at visit 4 (Additional file 2: Table 3). There were 46 RSV positive cases among 3082 ILI cases taking laboratory tests through routine sentinel hospital surveillance (Additional file 2: Tables 4 and 5). Over the study period, two RSV epidemic waves were observed (Fig. 1A). The first wave (wave 1) of RSV activity occurred between May and November 2023, reaching its peak in September. The second wave (wave 2) spanned from February to May 2024, with peak activity observed in March.

Descriptive evaluation of antibody levels

The average number of longitudinal samples collected from each participant was 4.6. A declining trend in the median log10 PreF antibody titres was observed from baseline (4.272, IQR: 4.118–4.455) to visit 2 (4.165, IQR: 4.040–4.349), followed by an increase at visit 3 (4.190, IQR: 4.061–4.400) and a subsequent decrease at visit 4 (4.165, IQR: 4.044–4.377) (Fig. 1B). The trend of PreF titres varied by age groups (Fig. 1C). During wave 1 (baseline to visit 3), 14 participants experienced a ≥ fourfold rise in PreF titres, including 3 young children, 9 adults, and 2 older adults. In wave 2 (visit 3 to visit 4), 9 participants showed ≥ fourfold rises, spanning various age groups (Additional file 2: Table 6). Across all five visits, median log2 titres ranged from 11.82 to 13.20 for RSV-A2 nAb and from 12.72 to 14.18 for RSV-B nAb (Additional file 1: Fig. S2). PreF titres were significantly correlated with RSV-A2 (ρ = 0.689, P < 0.001) and RSV-B nAb titres (ρ = 0.598, P < 0.001) (Additional file 1: Fig. S3). Young children exhibited lower nAb titres than other age groups (Additional file 2: Table 7).

Bayesian estimates of age-dependent antibody kinetics

The trajectory of RSV PreF antibody kinetics showed trends of variation across age groups following infection though with substantial overlap in credible intervals indicating these differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 3A). In young children, the median peak fold rise in antibody titres was 29.4 (95% CrI: 10.9–411.0) (Fig. 3B). The lowest response was observed in the oldest age group (≥ 75 years), where the median peak fold rise was 5.3 (95% CrI: 3.3–13.1). The estimated number of days that PreF antibody titres remained above a fourfold rise, which quantified the persistence of the antibody response over time, was highest in younger older adults aged 60–74 years, with a mean duration of 212 (95% CrI: 120–342) days, followed by young children, with a mean duration of 171 (95% CrI: 12–233) (Fig. 3C). Antibody persistence was lowest in older adults (≥ 75 years), exhibiting heightened (above a fourfold rise) antibody levels for 61 (95% CrI: 15–151) days post-infection.

Estimated correlates of protection against serological infection

PreF titres were inversely associated with infection risk (Fig. 4A), and the corresponding COP curve increased with titre (Fig. 4B). The COP showed good discrimination with AUC = 0.74, and reasonable calibration with coverage = 0.83. Translating the curve into operational thresholds (Fig. 4C), the posterior median titre required for ~ 75% protection was log10 4.0 (95% CrI: 3.4–4.6) PreF antibodies, whereas achieving ~ 90% protection required higher titres of log10 4.3 (95% CrI: 3.6–4.8). Framing the COP in terms of effect size, the AMG in protection given a four-fold rise in PreF titre was 11% when increasing titre relative to the median of the study population, and 18% when increasing relative to a baseline of log10 4.0.

Age-specific COP curves revealed marked heterogeneity in the relationship between baseline PreF titres and infection risk (Fig. 5). In the youngest children (≤ 5 years), infection risk declined steeply with increasing titre, with a high AUC (0.88) and complete coverage (1.00), indicating a well-defined and highly discriminative COP. By contrast, school-aged children (5–18 years) showed a flat risk curve, with a low AUC (0.50), low coverage (0.30), and minimal AMG, suggesting the COP offered poor discrimination in this group. Adults aged 19–59 years and 60–74 years showed intermediate performance, with higher AUC (0.70 and 0.82, respectively) and moderate coverage (0.81 and 0.61), indicating a reasonably well-defined COP, though many individuals remained below the threshold. The oldest adults (≥ 75 years) showed a gradual risk decline with low AUC (0.53) and coverage (0.32), suggesting weak discriminative performance. Collectively, these results indicated that using PreF antibodies was most predictive as a COP in the youngest children, and weakened in their predictive ability in other age groups.

Discussion

Our study explored the kinetics of RSV antibodies and their relationship with infection risk within a longitudinal community-based cohort in eastern China using a Bayesian modelling approach. The hierarchical Bayesian model allowed partial pooling of information across groups, stabilising estimates for smaller subgroups and accounting for varying sample sizes. The results highlighted key epidemiological patterns, including age-specific infection rates, antibody dynamics over time, and the protective role of pre-infection antibody titres in mitigating infection risk. The variability in individual-level antibody kinetics observed showed the potential influence of host factors, including age and pre-existing immunity, on immune responses.

The rapid rise and subsequent decline in antibody titres following RSV infection in this study was consistent with previous studies showing short-lived immunity post-infection [21–23]. Compared to influenza, RSV-induced immunity appeared to wane more rapidly [12, 24–26], potentially due to RSV’s ability to inhibit the development of long-lived memory B cells and diminish T-cell responses [19, 26, 27]. Our results indicated that PreF titres declined substantially over a period of 360 days, however, the timeframe for antibody titres to decline to pre-infection levels from previous studies remained unclear. For example, Falsey et al. reported that this decline may take up to 12 months [21], whereas an experimental challenge study showed a return to baseline within 6 months [22]. These discrepancies may result from differences in study populations (e.g. age, ethnicity), serological assays employed, severity of disease after infection, or sampling intervals. Moreover, the immune response elicited by natural RSV infection appeared less durable and robust compared to that induced by prophylactic agents. For instance, recipients of Ad26.RSV.preF–RSV PreF protein vaccine exhibited PreF IgG antibody levels over eightfold higher than baseline by day 15 post-inoculation, which remained > twofold at day 533 [28]. In another clinical trial, recipients of the AS01E-adjuvanted RSV PreF protein vaccine (RSVPreF3 OA) maintained PreF IgG antibody levels exceeding twofold above baseline across two consecutive RSV seasons [29]. An animal study supported that vaccination can induce stronger mucosal immune responses and greater protection against viral replication and tissue damage than natural infection [30].

We also estimated the age-specific degree of boosting of PreF titres after RSV infections, and their waning after the boost, suggesting potential age-related differences in immune response and antibody persistence. While young children aged ≤ 5 years generated the most robust initial antibody responses following RSV infection, consistent with a previous study [31], their PreF antibody titres also waned more rapidly. Faster waning antibody titres in infants compared to adults have also been observed for IgA in a previous study [32] and have been hypothesised to occur due to the immaturity of infant’s immune system [33, 34]. In our study, individuals aged 60–74 years appeared to have an antibody response compared to those aged 19–59 years and ≥ 75 years. This was in contrast to findings in a previous study which found significantly higher rates of four-fold rises in infected older adults compared to younger adults [35]. This discrepancy may stem from differences in study design (symptomatic illness vs. community-based serological inference), limited sample size in older adults, and the absence of virological confirmation in our cohort. The wide credible intervals of the boosting and waning in individuals aged 60–74 years indicated substantial within-group heterogeneity, with considerable overlap with other age groups, suggesting these kinetics were not a statistically significant observation. Without concurrent virological testing, we cannot definitively distinguish whether this pattern reflected a genuine age-related immune difference or was influenced by sampling timing relative to the acute or convalescent phase of RSV infection during blood collection. Consequently, rather than making definitive conclusions about age-specific kinetic responses between all adult age-adults, our findings primarily demonstrated clear differences between the extremes of age.

In our cohort, the highest infection rates were observed in children aged ≤ 5 years, with a rate of approximately 0.45 across two waves, consistent with the established understanding that RSV disproportionately affected younger children due to their immunological immaturity [3–5, 34]. Notably, this estimated infection rate exceeded previously reported rates of RSV-associated acute respiratory infections [5, 36, 37], highlighting the potential burden of asymptomatic or mild infections. These findings emphasised the importance of targeted vaccination strategies aimed at protecting young children, to reduce the overall community burden of RSV. Our results also showed comparable RSV infection rates in older adults to those reported in previous studies [38–40]. The comparatively lower infection rates in adults compared to infants may be attributed to presence of residual immunity providing protection from infection [12, 24]. The updated 2024 ACIP recommendations have advocated for an age-based and risk-based RSV vaccination strategy (for adults aged ≥ 75 years and adults aged 60–74 years with underlying health conditions) [41]. Our study indicated that the infection rate among adults aged 60–74 years was 6%, compared to 8% among those aged ≥ 75 years. Although RSV illness was often mild in healthy older adults [5, 39], it can be severe among those with underlying conditions such as heart failure or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, leading to hospitalisation or fatal outcomes [40, 42, 43].

Our findings demonstrated a clear inverse relationship between PreF antibody titres and subsequent RSV infection risk, supporting their potential use as a COP. This aligned with previous evidence showing that higher levels of pre-existing RSV neutralising antibodies were associated with reduced risk of infection or disease in natural infection [44, 45]. Notably, our analysis highlights marked age-related heterogeneity in the predictive performance of PreF titres. In young children, PreF titres showed a steep risk–response gradient with high discriminative ability (AUC = 0.88), suggesting a well-defined and highly predictive COP in this age group. This was consistent with recent birth-cohort and maternal immunisation studies reporting strong associations between cord or early-life neutralising/PreF titres and protection from RSV-associated illness in infants [46, 47]. By contrast, school-aged children (5–18 years) showed almost no association between titre and risk (AUC = 0.50), implying that immunity in this group may depend more on other components such as mucosal or cellular immunity, consistent with findings that repeated early-life exposures shift the balance from humoral to cellular protection [48, 49]. Predictiveness of PreF as a COP was also weak in older adults (≥ 75 years; AUC = 0.53), consistent with one previous study [50]. While immunosenescence has been reported to affect immunity response against RSV infection in older adults [51], vaccine studies have demonstrated robust and durable PreF-specific antibody responses in healthy populations aged ≥ 75 years [29]. Notably, a recent vaccine clinical trial has shown that both nAb and PreF IgG levels were predictive of protection against RSV-associated lower respiratory tract disease [52], contrasting with our study, which estimated the COP against RSV infection in a community setting. These findings suggested that the utility of PreF antibodies as a COP may be context and outcome-dependent and differ between natural infection and vaccine-induced immunity, where antibody magnitude, durability, and immune coordination may vary. Taken together, these findings suggested that while PreF titres can serve as a robust COP in the youngest children, their utility as a standalone biomarker diminished with age, highlighting the need to integrate additional immune markers—such as neutralisation titres or mucosal IgA or T-cell responses—particularly for assessing protection in older children and adults.

Despite the strengths of our study, including robust longitudinal follow-up and comprehensive serological analysis, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the reliance on serological data may lead to an underestimation of infections due to variations in individual immune responses and assay sensitivity. In particular, it is possible that immunosenescence made it challenging to infer infection rates in the elderly using serological sampling alone, as they may exhibit weakened antibody responses to infections [45]. Moreover, our approach can only detect infections that produce a measurable serological signal; infections that left little or no immunological imprint cannot be inferred in the absence of PCR confirmation or symptom reporting. Further, as our COP analysis relied on model-inferred infection status, we highlighted that the inferences are conditional on the serojumpmodelling assumptions about antibody kinetics. Incorporating virological testing (PCR) and clinical surveillance in future studies would therefore improve the accuracy of infection rate estimates and thus COP curves. Second, although the F protein was a primary target of nAbs, and a strong correlation between PreF antibody titres and nAb levels was observed in our study, PreF titres may not fully capture functional neutralisation capacity, and thus COP estimates may include more uncertainty than if neutralisation were used [53]. Third, we used the monthly RSV positivity rate among influenza-like illness cases in sentinel hospitals as a proxy for community-level RSV activity, which may influence the reliability of model-based infection estimates. Fourth, we did not collect participants’ data on chronic comorbidities or immunosuppressive conditions, which may influence antibody kinetics. Finally, although we employed a Bayesian hierarchical framework to model infection risk and antibody dynamics, the limited number of participants in certain age groups introduces additional uncertainty not only in estimates of some age-specific parameters (such as post-infection antibody fold rise), but also in estimates of infection incidence and COP. While the hierarchical structure allowed partial borrowing of information across individuals, age-specific inferences remain primarily informed by data within each age stratum and therefore cannot fully compensate for sparse representation. Expanding the cohort to include more under-represented age groups, particularly young children and school-aged children, would improve the generalisability of age-specific inferences.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study provided critical insights into the kinetics of RSV antibodies and their role in infection risk. The findings underscored the transient nature of RSV-induced immunity, the heightened vulnerability of younger age groups, and the potential utility of PreF antibody titres as a correlate of protection. These findings can inform future RSV prevention strategies, including vaccine development and deployment, to better protect at-risk populations. The observed waning of antibodies over time suggested that periodic serological surveillance could help identify populations at increased risk of infection, enabling timely interventions. Collectively, our findings supported age-specific vaccination strategies and demonstrate how serological modelling can inform the design of RSV surveillance and immunisation programmes.
