Isolated ovarian metastasis in otherwise cervix-confined adenocarcinoma of the cervix: A rare clinical conundrum
Authors: Lauren M Lim, Sarah L Speck, Yingao Zhang, Renata Urban, Margaret I Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Isolated ovarian metastasis from primary cervical cancer is rare, <5%•Ovary involvement doesn’t change cervical cancer stage but portends worse prognosis.•No guidelines for cervical cancer with isolated ovarian metastasis treatment.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Ovarian metastasis, FIGO staging

Introduction

In 2018, the International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) revised the cervical cancer staging system to incorporate imaging modalities and surgical pathologic criteria from the 2009 clinical staging guidelines (Bhatla, 2019). These guidelines state that ovarian involvement does not change staging. Although ovarian metastasis is rare, it portends worse survival (Salvo et al., 2020). The role of ovarian involvement and subsequent treatment is an open area of debate. Although most agree that otherwise early stage disease with ovarian metastasis should receive adjuvant treatment, there is no consensus if patients should be treated as locally advanced or distant metastatic disease (Bhatla, 2019, Salvo et al., 2020). Isolated ovarian metastasis is an even rarer phenomenon and not well reported in the current literature. We describe two cases of patients diagnosed with clinically early-stage invasive cervical adenocarcinoma with isolated unilateral ovarian metastasis incidentally found during primary surgical management from two tertiary care institutions, their treatment and follow up, as well as a comprehensive review of the current literature.

Case 1

The first patient was a 54-year-old postmenopausal woman who presented with cervical adenocarcinoma in situ (AIS). She had no past medical history except for human papilloma virus (HPV)-positive cervical dysplasia with loop electrical excision procedure (LEEP) four years prior.

She underwent a cold knife cone (CKC) of the cervix and endocervical curettage (ECC), with no gross lesion identified intraoperatively. Final pathology revealed a 10 mm invasive HPV-associated endocervical adenocarcinoma, with the depth of invasion of 4.6 mm out of 10 mm, and no lympho-vascular space invasion (LVSI). The endocervical margin was positive for invasive disease, while the ectocervical and radial margins were negative.

Postoperatively, pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) was negative for discrete lesions or metastatic disease. There was a 6 x 5.3 cm right adnexal cyst with internal thin septations and no other suspicious features (Fig. 1a). She was diagnosed with clinical FIGO 2018 stage IA2 disease, and underwent exploratory laparotomy, modified radical hysterectomy, bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy, and bilateral pelvic lymph node dissection after unsuccessful sentinel lymph node mapping. Pelvic exam at the time of surgery demonstrated a cervix with post-CKC changes and no clear evidence of gross tumor. The right adnexal cyst appeared to be grossly simple and was removed intact; the remainder of the intraabdominal survey was normal. Surgical pathology identified an HPV-associated grade 2 cervical adenocarcinoma with a tumor size of 1.6 cm x 1.4 cm x 0.6 cm. There was 6 mm out of 13 mm of stromal invasion, no LVSI, negative margins, and negative parametrial involvement. The right adnexal cyst measured 8.0 x 6.5 x 5.0 cm and was mostly cystic. Pathologic findings were consistent with adenocarcinoma with surface involvement. Immunostains were diffusely positive for P16 and HPV-18, confirming cervical metastasis rather than ovarian origin. Pelvic washings were also positive for adenocarcinoma. All excised lymph nodes were negative for malignancy. PD-L1 testing was negative with a combined positive score (CPS) of < 1. The patient recovered well from surgery, and postoperative positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) revealed no evidence of metastatic disease.

Given the uncertainty surrounding how ovarian metastasis may change the stage of an otherwise stage IB1 cervical adenocarcinoma, the patient received gynecologic oncology opinions at multiple institutions. Recommendations ranged from treating the cancer as a pT1B1N0M1 − either stage IVA since the metastasis was in the pelvis or stage IVB since the metastasis also involved the pelvic washings. She received adjuvant chemoradiation with cisplatin and pembrolizumab followed by pembrolizumab maintenance, per KEYNOTE A-18 for Stage IVA disease (Lorusso, 2024). The patient was disease free on maintenance therapy 15 months following surgery.

Case 2

Patient 2 was a 32-year-old who presented with a high-grade squamous intraepithelial lesion on ECC during colposcopy for cervical dysplasia and HPV positivity. Subsequent LEEP demonstrated cervical intraepithelial neoplasia (CIN) 3 and concurrent ECC showed AIS; the detached fragments were suspicious for adenocarcinoma. After negative pelvic MRI, she underwent a CKC which was negative for AIS and ECC which demonstrated focal AIS. Due to AIS on ECC, she was recommended to proceed with hysterectomy. As she desired a fertility-sparing approach, she sought a second opinion. She was recommended to have repeat CKC and ECC, which were all negative. She then underwent active surveillance. Her initial 6-month follow-up visit showed persistent AIS on ECC. She was recommended to have a repeat excisional procedure, and she underwent a third CKC showing AIS with clear margins and negative ECC. Hysterectomy was again discussed given the recurrence of AIS, and she declined. Over the next year, cytologic screenings and two additional ECC were negative. Due to pelvic discomfort, she was recommended to have a transvaginal ultrasound which showed a 4 cm left ovarian hemorrhagic cyst and no other significant findings. This cyst increased in size to 6.9 cm on an interval ultrasound, with concern for a new peripherally enhancing solid component (Fig. 1b). Labs were significant for CA125 of 45 and HE4 of 155. Given this development, the patient decided to pursue definitive surgical management.

Preoperative ultrasound of ovarian cyst in Case 2. A 6.9 cm left ovarian cyst with concern for a new peripherally enhancing solid component.

She underwent total laparoscopic hysterectomy using a V-care uterine manipulator, bilateral salpingectomy, and left ovarian cystectomy. Cystectomy was initially pursued given the absence of invasive cancer on multiple prior cone biopsies, dermoid appearance of the cyst on ultrasound, patient’s young age, and her desire for ovarian preservation. Intraoperatively, the ovarian cyst was smooth in appearance, but ruptured during cystectomy, yielding mucoid green fluid. Intraoperative frozen section of the cyst revealed adenocarcinoma; therefore, completion left salpingo-oophorectomy and omentectomy were performed. Postoperatively, CT abdomen/pelvis and PET/CT were negative for metastasis. Final pathology identified an HPV-associated invasive endocervical adenocarcinoma measuring 5 mm. There were 5 mm out of 11 mm stromal invasion, no LVSI, and negative surgical margins. The left ovary was involved by adenocarcinoma with positive HPV immunostains consistent with metastasis from the primary endocervical tumor. Pelvic washings and omentum were negative. PD-L1 testing was positive with a CPS of 12. She underwent a second surgery with laparoscopic bilateral pelvic lymph node dissection and right oophorectomy; all specimens were negative for neoplasm. The final stage was pT1A2N0M1, similar to Case 1.

The patient recovered well from surgery and received adjuvant chemoradiation with cisplatin and pembrolizumab, followed by pembrolizumab maintenance, based on KEYNOTE A-18 for Stage IVA disease (Lorusso, 2024). She discontinued immunotherapy after cycle 6 due to immunotherapy-induced toxicity (joint pain impacting quality of life), and she was without evidence of disease 21 months following surgery.

Discussion

Ovarian metastasis from primary cervical cancer is rare with estimates of < 1% for squamous cell carcinoma and < 5% for adenocarcinoma or other non-squamous cell carcinomas (Salvo et al., 2020). Furthermore, isolated ovarian metastasis in early-stage cervical cancer, without other extra-cervical involvement or LVSI, is exceedingly rare and not well represented in the current literature. In this report, we describe two patients with clinically diagnosed early-stage cervical cancer who were found to have isolated ovarian metastasis during primary surgery.

Upon identifying isolated ovarian metastasis, the staging and treatment course are not clearly defined. In the FIGO 2018 staging update, the FIGO committee concluded that ovarian involvement does not change the stage, but acknowledged that there are little data on its impact on survival as an independent risk factor (Bhatla, 2019). Ovarian involvement in patients with clinically early-stage disease undergoing surgical management has been shown to be a negative independent prognostic factor, and long-term survival outcomes have mirrored those with metastatic or recurrent disease (Table 1). However, an overwhelming majority of these patients had other sites of extra-cervical involvement, likely contributing to their poor prognosis. For example, Shimada et al. demonstrated that the 5-year survival of patients after finding ovarian metastases on comprehensive surgical staging was 46%, 37%, and 18%, for 1995 FIGO Stage IB, IIA, and IIB disease respectively. Over 96% of these patients ultimately had at least one other high risk prognostic factor such as lymph node involvement, parametrial or deep stromal invasion, or tumor size > 4 cm; two cases were described to have ovarian metastasis without these risk factors, though LVSI and vaginal involvement were not detailed (Shimada, 2006). This was the pattern seen in more contemporary cohorts as well. Zhou et al. reported 14 patients with early-stage cervical adenocarcinoma found to have ovarian metastasis after primary surgical management, of which only 4 patients were FIGO 2009 stage IB; none of the stage IA patients had ovarian metastases. No patients in the reported cohort had isolated ovarian involvement without extra-cervical disease, and the authors found that the presence of ovarian metastases was not independently associated with either relapse-free survival or overall survival after controlling for the other risk factors (Zhou et al., 2017). Conversely, in their observational cohort, Matsuo et al. did find that ovarian involvement in FIGO 2009 stage IB-IIB surgically treated patients was independently associated with worse survival, though adjuvant treatment was not described or included in the analysis. Only 4 out of 5481 patients had ovarian metastasis without LVSI, uterine extension, or lymph node involvement, but other risk factors such as parametrial status and tumor size were not reported (Matsuo, 2018).

Studies published on ovarian metastases in cervical cancer found on primary surgical staging, with either reports of adjuvant treatment or survival outcomes.

In two 2019 meta-analyses on cervical cancer outcomes based on stage, 5-year survival was approximately 40% for Stage IIIA-IIIB, approximately 60% for Stage IIIC1, and 37% for Stage IIIC2 (Matsuo et al., 2019, Wright, 2019). Five-year survival for Stage IVA cervical cancer is approximately 24% versus 14% for Stage IVB, which was similar to reported outcomes for patients with ovarian metastases (Wright, 2019). It would be reasonable to conclude that ovarian metastasis would fall under Stage IV disease. Stage IVA includes metastasis confined to the pelvis, and given that the ovaries are within the pelvis, an argument could be made for isolated ovarian metastasis to be locally advanced. However, Stage IVA is currently specific to bowel or bladder invasion. In this sense, ovarian metastasis could then be considered distant metastasis, or stage IVB. With no consensus on the impact of isolated ovarian metastasis on stage or survival outcomes independent of other prognostic factors, it is unclear whether to treat such patients as having locally advanced or distant metastatic disease (Salvo et al., 2020).

The two cases in the present study fell within an extremely rare subset of patients with ovarian metastasis without extra-cervical involvement. Kang et al. reported a case of stage IAI squamous cell carcinoma of the cervix with incidental isolated ovarian involvement found on surgical specimen (Kang, 2009). Stromal invasion was < 3 mm and there was no LVSI. However, postoperative treatment, follow up, and survival were not described for this patient. To date, there were no other published cases that detail an early-stage cervical cancer with ovarian metastases, in the absence of other negative prognostic drivers. In the two cases presented here, neither patient met traditional criteria for adjuvant radiation or chemoradiation using Sedlis or Peters criteria (Sedlis, 1999, Peters, 2000). Nonetheless, the patients could have reasonably received the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN) recommendation for stage IVB cervical cancer: cisplatin and paclitaxel and bevacizumab followed by bevacizumab maintenance, per GOG 240, plus or minus atezolizumab concurrently and as maintenance therapy, per BEATcc (Sedlis, 1999, Peters, 2000, Monk, 2023, Oaknin, 2024). Given that neither case above met Sedlis or Peters criteria, some might argue that, in the absence of other risk factors, systemic chemotherapy may be overtreatment. A reasonable approach may be to either extend Peters criteria to the ovary and counsel them toward adjuvant chemoradiation alone or to classify these patients as stage IVA with spread to “adjacent pelvic organs” (Salvo et al., 2020, Peters, 2000). Another reasonable concern is the risk of peritoneal or distant metastases with positive pelvic cytology or intraoperative rupture of malignancy, drawing from proven prognostic factors from historical ovarian cancer trials (Collinson, 2014). However, the contemporary addition of pembrolizumab under the A18 protocol may address the risk of peritoneal recurrence, especially if the primary tumor has PD-L1 CPS ≥ 1, though this potential benefit is theoretical (Lorusso, 2024). While both patients described in this report received the KEYNOTE A-18 regimen as adjuvant therapy, it is important to note that neither patient may have been eligible for the trial. Neither patient had nodal involvement and their classification as possible IVA disease was not due to local invasion into the rectum or bladder, which are the traditional criteria to assign this stage.

The cases discussed here exemplify a unique clinical scenario in which the staging, and therefore the treatments recommendations, were not clear. Much of the ambiguity in guidelines stems from the rarity of ovarian metastasis in cervical cancer, particularly with no other extra-cervical involvement, and consequently there is little data to direct clinical management of these patients. Until there is more evidence to support the addition of ovarian metastases more clearly in the next FIGO staging update, there is room for interpretation. Adjuvant treatment should be individualized to the pathology, known prognostic factors, and treatment side-effects.
