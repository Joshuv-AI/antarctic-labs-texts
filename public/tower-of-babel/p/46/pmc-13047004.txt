Temporal and Regional Trends of Foodborne Diseases in Saudi Arabia (2015–2023): A Nationwide Retrospective Analysis
Authors: Salma A. Alamri, Mohammad Alwetaid, Nasser Alshawaish, Reffal Alkharan, Moudhi Altulayhi, Imtnan Alnajashi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesFoodborne diseases (FBDs) are a significant public health burden, particularly in developing countries. This study aimed to analyze the incidence rate (IR), temporal trends, seasonal variations, and demographic and regional distribution of notifiable foodborne diseases in Saudi Arabia from 2015 to 2023.MethodsA nationwide retrospective cross-sectional study was conducted utilizing aggregate-level data obtained from the annual statistical reports published by the Saudi Ministry of Health on six notifiable foodborne and food-associated enteric diseases in Saudi Arabia from 2015 to 2023: salmonellosis, enteric fever (paratyphoid and typhoid), amoebic dysentery, bacillary dysentery, hepatitis A, and brucellosis. IRs were stratified by age, gender, year, season, and administrative areas. Linear regression was used to assess temporal trends. Percentage difference (PD) and year-on-year (YoY) changes were calculated.ResultsA total of 16,956 FBD cases were reported between 2015 and 2023, corresponding to a period cumulative incidence of 50.58 per 100,000 population over the 9-year period. Among the study population, the highest burden was observed among individuals aged 20–49 years (44.4%), and males (52%). Public food sources accounted for 68.5% of reported infections. The administrative areas with the highest IR per 100,000 were Ha’il (3.15), Bishah (2.46), and Northern Region (2.25). Seasonal peaks occurred in summer and fall. From 2015 to 2023, Salmonellosis incidence increased by 123.2%, while Amoebic Dysentery declined by 49.8%. Bacillary Dysentery and Hepatitis A showed fluctuating trends, and Brucellosis showed an overall decrease of 11.4%. However, none of the annual trends reached statistical significance (p > 0.05).ConclusionsFBDs remain a substantial health concern in Saudi Arabia, with notable regional disparities, particularly in Ha’il, Bishah, and the Northern Region, which recorded the highest average event rates over the nine-year period. Our findings highlight the need for enhanced surveillance, public education, and targeted food safety interventions, especially in high-incidence administrative areas and among vulnerable populations, including young children, and males, each of whom exhibited elevated disease IR.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s44197-025-00514-7.

Background

Foodborne diseases (FBDs) constitute a major global health concern with considerable socioeconomic impacts, affecting approximately 600 million people worldwide each year and resulting in 420,000 deaths annually [1]. Notably, children under five years old bear a disproportionate burden, accounting for 125,000 of these fatalities [2]. These diseases encompass a spectrum of illnesses caused by consumption of contaminated food or beverages, with bacterial, viral, and parasitic pathogens serving as primary etiological agents [3]. These pathogens can lead to a spectrum of health outcomes, from mild gastrointestinal discomfort to severe systemic infections and, in extreme cases, death [4, 5]. Beyond their immediate health effects, FBDs impose substantial economic burdens through healthcare costs, productivity losses, and disruptions to food trade and tourism [6].

In the Middle East region, including Saudi Arabia, FBDs present unique challenges due to the intersection of various factors including climate conditions, cultural practices, food importation patterns, and rapid urbanization [7]. Saudi Arabia’s distinctive geographical, demographic, and socioeconomic characteristics create a complex landscape for FBDs epidemiology. The country experiences extreme climate variations, hosts millions of religious pilgrims annually, maintains a large expatriate workforce, and has undergone rapid urbanization and dietary transitions in recent decades [8–10].

Environmental factors, particularly those linked to climate change, significantly influence the incidence and severity of FBDs [11]. For instance, studies have shown that extreme heat is associated with increased infection rates of certain Salmonella serovars, particularly in administrative areas with dense animal feeding operations [12]. Additionally, climatic factors such as average temperature, ocean temperature, and ocean salinity have been found to positively influence the incidence of Vibrio parahaemolyticus outbreaks [13]. In Saudi Arabia, the impact of climate change on the food system has been a growing concern [14]. The country’s food system is already under stress due to high per capita water demand, a growing population, and limited water resources [14]. Climate unpredictability and extreme weather events further exacerbate these challenges, posing significant risks to food security and public health [14]. Therefore, integrating environmental considerations into surveillance and prevention strategies is crucial for mitigating the risks associated with FBDs [15].

Despite the significant public health implications, comprehensive epidemiological analyses of FBDs in Saudi Arabia have been limited, particularly regarding regional distribution patterns, demographic vulnerabilities, seasonal variations, and temporal trends. This knowledge gap hampers the development of evidence-based interventions tailored to local contexts. Furthermore, the rising concern of antimicrobial resistance in FBDs necessitates current, locally-relevant surveillance data to guide appropriate control measures.

This study aimed to address these knowledge gaps by conducting a comprehensive analysis of six FBDs and food-associated enteric diseases in Saudi Arabia from 2015 to 2023. The primary objective was to determine the overall incidence rate (IR) and temporal trends of reported FBD and food-associated enteric cases, with particular focus on Salmonellosis, Amoebic Dysentery, Bacillary Dysentery, Hepatitis A, enteric fever (paratyphoid and typhoid), and Brucellosis. Additionally, the study sought to understand regional factors contributing to pathogen distribution, identify demographic vulnerabilities, analyze seasonal patterns, and map geographic hotspots. Specific objectives included categorizing the most common notifiable infectious diseases, analyzing temporal distributions to identify significant trends, evaluating quarterly incidence patterns across administrative areas, assessing demographic characteristics of affected populations, and investigating seasonality of outbreaks.

Methods

Study Design and Setting

This nationwide retrospective cross-sectional study utilised national surveillance data collected by the Ministry of Health (MOH) in Saudi Arabia. The dataset included all notifiable infectious diseases reported between January 2015 and December 2023 across different administrative areas of the country. The analysis focused on FBDs and food-associated enteric diseases for confirmed cases of Salmonellosis, Amoebic Dysentery, Bacillary Dysentery, Hepatitis A, enteric fever (paratyphoid and typhoid), and Brucellosis. Enteric fever was included because it is primarily transmitted via the faecal–oral route through contaminated food or water, and brucellosis was included because ingestion of unpasteurised milk/dairy is a major transmission route, despite additional non-foodborne exposure pathways. This study used publicly available, de-identified secondary data and did not require formal ethical approval. The research adhered to ethical standards for observational studies and complied with national guidelines for data handling and privacy.

Study Population and Data Sources

Data were obtained from MOH reports, which captured mandatory FBDs cases stratified by age, sex, month, season, and administrative areas. No patient-level identifiable data were used.

Variables

The primary outcome variables were the annual and monthly IRs of six major FBDs and food-associated enteric diseases. IRs were calculated per 100,000 population and stratified by demographic characteristics (age groups: <1, 1–4, 5–14, 15–44, and ≥ 45 years; and gender), time (year, month, season, and quarter), and geography. Geographical information in the MOH surveillance reports was recorded using 20 MOH administrative reporting areas, which include a mix of administrative regions/provinces and selected governorates/cities; hereafter referred to as ‘administrative areas’.

Statistical Analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using Jamovi software version 2.6.23 (The jamovi project, Sydney, Australia). Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the incidence of diseases across various strata. We calculated the period cumulative incidence (2015–2023) by dividing the total number of cases reported during 2015–2023 by the average population over the same period, then multiplying by 100,000. The average annual incidence rate across the 9-year period was calculated as total number of cases ÷ (population × number of years) × 100,000; for calendar-year rows, we calculated the annual incidence rate by dividing the number of cases in each year by the average population (without dividing by 9), then multiplying by 100,000. Linear regression analysis was used to assess monotonic linear trends in annual incidence rates over time and to assess the association between age group and the number of reported cases; beta coefficients, 95% confidence intervals (CI), and p-values were reported. Time-series and seasonal analyses were conducted to visualise monthly, quarterly, and seasonal variations in disease incidence. To calculate administrative area–level incidence, the number of reported FBD cases was divided by the corresponding annual administrative area population and expressed per 100,000 population. Population data were linearly interpolated for years with missing values to ensure continuity across the 2015–2023 study period. The nine-year average event rate and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were then calculated for each administrative area to assess regional disease burden. To evaluate temporal trends:

Among the study population, males had a slightly higher proportion of cases (51.99%, 95% CI: 51.24%–52.74%) compared to females (48.01%, 95% CI: 47.26%–48.76%), translating to IR rates of 2.92 and 2.70 per 100,000, respectively. The majority of infections were attributed to public sources (68.47%, 95% CI: 67.77%–69.17%), while home sources accounted for 31.53% of cases. Over the years, 2017 had the highest proportion of reported cases (18.51%), followed by 2015 and 2016. In contrast, the lowest proportion was recorded in 2021 (6.68%).

Administrative Area–Level Distribution of FBD Event Rates (2015–2023)

Over the nine-year surveillance period, the event rate and IR of reported FBD cases varied substantially across Saudi Arabian administrative areas. Across 16,956 reported cases, Riyadh contributed the largest proportion (2,518; 14.85%, 95% CI 14.32–15.39), followed by Ha’il (1,821; 10.74%, 95% CI 10.28–11.21) and Makkah (1,557; 9.18%, 95% CI 8.76–9.63), with other administrative areas each accounting for 1.07%–6.76% of cases. When administrative areas were stratified by IR per 100,000, administrative areas with the highest IR were Ha’il (3.15), Bishah (2.46), Northern (2.25), Al-Bahah (1.88), and Najran (1.79). On the other hand, Qurayyat (1.30), Qunfudah (1.06), Hafr Al-Baten (0.93), Tabouk (0.87), Ta’if (0.86), Jazan (0.81), Makkah (0.80), and Aseer (0.77) reported lower IR. The lowest IR per 100,000 population was reported in Medinah (0.32) and Jeddah (0.27).

Temporal and Demographic Trends of Salmonellosis Incidence in Saudi Arabia (2015–2023)

The incidence of Salmonellosis infection demonstrated considerable variation across age groups, genders, and regions over the 9-year period.

Children aged 1–4 years consistently experienced higher IRs compared to other age categories, peaking at 2.68 per 100,000 in 2019. The age group 15–44 years also exhibited elevated and steadily rising rates, reaching 2.25 per 100,000 in 2019, suggesting a substantial disease burden among working-age adults.

In terms of gender, males exhibited higher IRs across all years, with a maximum incidence of 3.82 per 100,000 in 2019, compared to 3.66 in females. The male-to-female PD showed a gradual decline from 41.5% in 2015 to 11.9% in 2023, indicating narrowing gender disparities over time.

Geographically, Eastern region, Riyadh, and Jeddah consistently reported the highest Salmonellosis IRs, with Eastern peaking at 2.22 per 100,000 in 2018, and Riyadh at 1.98 in 2019. Several peripheral regions, such as Qurayyat, Al-Jouf, and Qunfudah, showed little to no reported cases across most years. Notably, some regional spikes were observed, such as in Al-Bahah (2.04 in 2021) and Bishah (1.93 in 2021), warranting further investigation into localized outbreaks or reporting artefacts, as shown in Supp. Table 1.

Amoebic Dysentery Trends by Demographic and Regional Characteristics (2015–2023)

The incidence of Amoebic Dysentery showed notable demographic and geographic variation between 2015 and 2023. Across age groups, the 15–44 years category consistently had the highest IRs, peaking at 6.49 per 100,000 in 2017, with wide confidence intervals suggesting substantial variability. Younger age groups such as < 1 year and 1–4 years maintained lower rates throughout the study period, with the lowest in 2022 (0.11 and 0.26 per 100,000, respectively).

Males consistently had higher IRs than females across all years. For instance, in 2017, the male incidence reached 6.89 per 100,000 compared to 4.18 in females, with a corresponding PD of 64.8%. Although the absolute rates decreased over time, the male predominance persisted, with the difference remaining above 75% in recent years.

Geographic analysis revealed that the Eastern region and Jeddah were hotspots, particularly in 2017 and 2018, with Jeddah peaking at 4.38 per 100,000 and Eastern region reaching 4.91. Other regions like Riyadh, Ta’if, and Al-Ahsa demonstrated moderate but variable patterns, while several areas such as Northern, Qunfudah, and Al-Jouf consistently reported minimal or no cases throughout the surveillance period (Supp. Table 2).

Bacillary Dysentery Trends by Demographic and Regional Characteristics (2015–2023)

The incidence of Bacillary Dysentery remained consistently low across all demographics and years from 2015 to 2023. The highest rates were observed in the 15–44 years age group, with a peak of 0.082 per 100,000 in 2021, though this figure remained low overall. Younger age groups (especially < 1 year and 1–4 years) showed minimal variation, with IRs rarely exceeding 0.05 per 100,000.

Gender differences were minor and inconsistent over the years. In some years (e.g., 2016 and 2017), females had slightly higher IRs than males, while in others (e.g., 2019, 2021, and 2023), males surpassed females. However, the PD never exceeded 40.3%, suggesting a relatively balanced gender distribution with no strong trend toward either sex.

Regionally, most areas reported extremely low or zero incidence throughout the study period. The Eastern and Riyadh regions had slightly higher activity, especially in 2017–2019, with Eastern peaking at 0.047 per 100,000 in 2019 and Riyadh at 0.108. Other regions, such as Bishah, Al-Bahah, Al-Jouf, and Qunfudah, reported zero incidence in nearly all years, suggesting either very low transmission or possible underreporting (Supp. Table 3).

Epidemiological Trends of Hepatitis A (2015–2023)

The incidence of Hepatitis A in Saudi Arabia remained relatively low from 2015 to 2023, with modest fluctuations across demographic groups and regions. The highest burden was observed in the 15–44 years age group, reaching a peak incidence of 0.45 per 100,000 in 2017 and 0.4 per 100,000 in 2019. The 5–14 years group consistently reported intermediate rates, while infants (< 1 year) and the ≥ 45 years population exhibited the lowest rates, frequently approaching zero.

Gender-wise, males consistently had higher IRs than females throughout the nine-year period, with the difference most prominent in 2021 (116.4%), though this disparity was not steady across all years.

Geographic analysis revealed that the Riyadh and Eastern regions bore a consistently higher burden, particularly between 2015 and 2019, where rates in these areas ranged from 0.08 to 0.24 per 100,000. Other regions, including Makkah, Jeddah, and Medinah, showed sporadic low-level activity. Several regions such as Bishah, Al-Bahah, Al-Jouf, and Qunfudah recorded zero or near-zero incidence throughout the study period, suggesting either effective public health control or limited transmission potential (Supp. Table 4).

Discussion

This comprehensive epidemiological analysis of FBDs in Saudi Arabia from 2015 to 2023 reveals significant demographic, temporal, and geographical patterns with direct implications for policy and clinical practice.

Interpretation of Key Findings in the Saudi Context

Demographic and Spatial Distribution Patterns

The findings reveal distinctive demographic patterns of FBDs in Saudi Arabia that diverge from global trends. The highest burden was observed among adults aged 20–49 years (44.41%), followed by those aged 5–19 years (41.01%). This contrasts with global patterns where children under five typically bear the disproportionate burden of FBDs [2]. Children < 5 formed 8.49%, potentially reflecting household protection, healthcare-seeking differences, or under-diagnosis.

The slightly higher percentage of males compared to females in reported cases (51.99% versus 48.01%) aligns with findings from previous studies in the region, likely related to occupational exposures and access [18–22]. Notably, this gender disparity varied significantly across regions, with areas such as the Eastern Province showing marked male predominance in multiple years, suggesting localized occupational or behavioral risk factors that merit targeted investigation. Regional variations in disease burden were also evident, with Ha’il (3.15), Bishah (2.46), and the Northern Region (2.25) exhibiting the highest IR per 100,000 population over the nine-year period. These differences support geographically targeted interventions in addition to measures for high-volume urban centres such as Riyadh.

Temporal Trends and FBDs-Specific Patterns

Salmonellosis and bacillary dysentery increased by 123.17% and 162.5%, respectively, despite food-safety advances, possibly reflecting changing consumption patterns [23, 24]. Conversely, the decline in Amoebic dysentery (49.83%) likely reflects improvements in water and sanitation infrastructure over the study period. This divergence in trends between bacterial and parasitic pathogens suggests that current interventions may be more effective against certain transmission routes than others. The non-significant linear trends in annual IR for the studied FBDs despite apparent changes, highlight the complex, non-linear nature of FBDs epidemiology in the region.

The seasonal patterns identified, particularly the summer-fall predominance of Salmonellosis and Amoebic dysentery, align with the established understanding of temperature-dependent bacterial growth and food spoilage. In Saudi Arabia’s extreme climate, with summer temperatures frequently exceeding 45 °C, these seasonal effects may be amplified compared to more temperate regions [25]. These findings highlight the importance of seasonally-targeted interventions in food safety practices.

The incidence observed in this study is substantially lower than global and regional estimates of the overall foodborne illness burden reported in the literature [26, 27]. This discrepancy is expected because our estimates are derived from MOH passive notifiable-disease surveillance of confirmed cases, and therefore capture only a fraction of community illness, specifically, cases that (i) develop symptoms severe enough to seek care, (ii) are clinically recognised and, where relevant, tested and confirmed, and (iii) are subsequently reported through the notification system. Surveillance ‘pyramid’ frameworks and burden estimation studies consistently demonstrate major attrition between community illness and notified cases due to under-ascertainment and under-reporting [28]. In addition, this analysis covers a limited subset of FBDs and food-associated enteric diseases, and does not capture the broader spectrum of foodborne hazards that contribute substantially to community gastroenteritis, particularly viral agents and unspecified causes that are typically not notifiable or not routinely laboratory confirmed in many settings. Accordingly, our incidence estimates should be interpreted as minimum, surveillance-based incidence of selected notifiable diseases, rather than a comprehensive estimate of total foodborne illness in the Saudi population.

The declining trend in Amoebic dysentery observed in our study represents a notable departure from historical patterns in Saudi Arabia. Earlier studies from the 1990 s and early 2000 s documented higher prevalence of Entamoeba histolytica infections [29, 30]. This decline reflects improvements in water quality, sanitation infrastructure, and healthcare interventions over recent decades, similar to trends observed in other middle to high-income countries with improving water and sanitation systems [31].

The relatively low representation of children under five years in our study contrasts with global patterns and warrants careful interpretation. The World Health Organization estimates that children under five bear approximately 40% of the FBDs burden globally, yet this age group represented only about 8.49% of cases in our study [2]. This disparity may reflect protective factors within Saudi households, potential underreporting, or differences in healthcare-seeking behaviours for paediatric cases.

Brucellosis, despite showing an 11.4% decrease over the study period, remains a significant clinical challenge in Saudi Arabia due to its chronic nature and potential for misdiagnosis [32, 33]. Despite being a zoonotic disease with multiple transmission routes, including inhalation and direct contact, its inclusion in this analysis is justified because unpasteurised dairy remains a dominant source of human infection in the region [34, 35]. The spring-summer predominance observed in our study should inform diagnostic considerations during these seasons, particularly among patients with exposure to livestock or consumption of unpasteurized dairy products [32]. Unlike other FBDs in this study that are largely linked to public food sources, brucellosis is often associated with household-level consumption, which explains its different epidemiological pattern and highlights the need for targeted public health messaging on the risks of raw dairy [36].

A critical finding of this study is the high degree of YoY volatility in the incidence of several FBDs, as evidenced by the sharp fluctuations in YoY changes (Table 2). For example, Salmonellosis incidence fell by 44.8% in 2020 before rebounding by 64.5% in 2021. This substantial variability explains why the linear regression models did not yield statistically significant trends, despite large overall percentage increases for FBDs like Salmonellosis and Bacillary dysentery. The absence of a stable, linear trend is itself an important conclusion. It suggests that FBDs epidemiology in Saudi Arabia is not governed by gradual, predictable changes but is instead highly influenced by acute events, such as localized outbreaks, changes in surveillance intensity, or major societal disruptions like the COVID-19 pandemic. This volatility makes it challenging to attribute incidence changes to specific interventions or events and underscores the need for responsive, real-time surveillance systems over reliance on long-term linear trend analysis.

When situated within an international context, the FBDs burden in Saudi Arabia appears markedly lower than that reported by several developed nations between 2015 and 2023. For perspective, the United States estimates approximately 9.9 million domestically acquired illnesses annually from just six major FBDs [37], while Canada reports an estimated 4 million cases each year [38]. Similarly, national surveillance in Australia documented over 18,000 cases of salmonellosis and 24,000 cases of campylobacteriosis in a single year (2016) [39]. Data from other developed Asian countries reinforce this trend; Japan regularly reports several thousand cases of campylobacteriosis annually [40], and South Korea recorded 216 outbreaks affecting 3,028 patients in 2023 alone. This contrasts sharply with the 16,956 total cases documented across studied FBDs over a nine-year period in Saudi Arabia, corresponding to a cumulative incidence of 50.58 per 100,000 population over the study period. The disparity is particularly evident in pathogen-specific figures; for instance, the annual number of salmonellosis cases reported in countries like Australia or Canada substantially exceeds the total multi-pathogen FBD case count for the entire 2015–2023 period in Saudi Arabia. Therefore, based on official surveillance data, Saudi Arabia reports a significantly lower incidence of FBDs cases compared to these other developed nations.

Clinical Implications

The distinct epidemiological profiles of different FBDs necessitate tailored diagnostic and treatment approaches for healthcare providers in Saudi Arabia. The rising incidence of Salmonellosis and the persistent burden of Brucellosis highlight the need for clinical vigilance regarding these specific diseases.

For Salmonellosis infections, clinicians should maintain a high index of suspicion, particularly during summer months and in high-IR regions like Riyadh and Ha’il. The potential for antimicrobial resistance in Salmonellosis, though not directly assessed in our study, represents a growing global concern that may complicate treatment approaches. Previous studies in Saudi Arabia have documented concerning levels of resistance among Salmonellosis isolates, necessitating judicious use of antimicrobials and adherence to evidence-based treatment guidelines.

Brucellosis, despite showing an 11.4% decrease over the study period, remains a significant clinical challenge in Saudi Arabia due to its chronic nature and potential for misdiagnosis. The spring-summer predominance observed in our study should inform diagnostic considerations during these seasons, particularly among patients with exposure to livestock or consumption of unpasteurized dairy products.

For populations identified as vulnerable in our study, including adults aged 20–49 years, targeted clinical education regarding food safety practices may complement medical interventions. The gender disparities observed in certain regions also suggest the need for gender-sensitive approaches to prevention and treatment, particularly in areas like the Eastern Province where male predominance was pronounced.

Economic Implications

The economic burden of FBDs in Saudi Arabia, though not directly quantified in our study, is likely substantial based on the IR and distribution patterns observed. Using economic models from comparable middle to high-income countries, the annual cost of FBDs typically ranges from 0.2% to 0.5% of GDP, encompassing direct healthcare costs, productivity losses, and regulatory expenditures [41, 42]. The high IR among working-age adults (20–49 years represented 44.41% of cases) suggests significant productivity losses due to illness-related absenteeism. This age group constitutes the core of the Saudi workforce, and FBDs may therefore have disproportionate economic impacts through reduced economic output and increased healthcare utilization. For the Saudi healthcare system, the regional variations in disease burden identified in our study have important resource allocation implications. Regions with consistently high IR, such as Ha’il and Bishah, may require targeted resource investments in surveillance, laboratory capacity, and clinical services.

Beyond direct healthcare costs, FBDs impact Saudi Arabia’s food industry, tourism sector, and international trade relationships. The high proportion of cases attributed to public food sources (68.47%) highlights the economic vulnerability of the restaurant and food service sectors to outbreaks and subsequent consumer confidence declines. For a country actively developing its tourism sector, including religious tourism, maintaining food safety standards represents both a public health and economic imperative.

National Implications and Public Health Policy

Our findings have clear implications for public health policy, surveillance systems, and prevention strategies in Saudi Arabia. The comprehensive epidemiological profile creates an evidence base for targeted interventions. For policymakers, the observed regional variations in FBD event rates underscore the importance of tailored, region-specific approaches rather than uniform national strategies. The consistently high event rates in Ha’il, Bishah, and the Northern Region highlight the need for intensified food safety interventions, targeted awareness campaigns, and enhanced resource allocation. Conversely, regions with lower event rates may offer valuable insights into effective local practices that could inform broader national strategies.

The temporal trends observed, particularly the increases in Salmonellosis (123.17%) and Bacillary dysentery (162.5%), signal the need for pathogen-specific control measures. These trends contrast with the decline in Amoebic dysentery (49.83%), suggesting that current approaches may be more effective for certain diseases than others. National food safety policies should prioritize diseases with sustained or increasing trends.

Our finding that public food sources accounted for 68.47% of infections underscores the importance of strengthening regulatory oversight in the food service industry. The current regulatory framework for restaurants, catering services, and institutional food providers may require added focus to high-risk regions and seasons identified in our study.

The unique context of Saudi Arabia as host to millions of religious pilgrims annually creates both challenges and opportunities for national FBDs control [43]. Enhanced surveillance and preventive measures during Hajj and Umrah seasons, particularly in the holy cities of Makkah and Madinah, should be prioritized given the potential for international spread of FBDs [44]. Saudi Arabia demonstrates a serious commitment to public health through a robust food safety framework, centrally managed by the Saudi Food and Drug Authority (SFDA), which aligns national regulations with international standards like Codex Alimentarius and employs advanced digital platforms for enforcement and compliance [45, 46]. The comprehensive regulatory framework and proactive efforts of the SFDA have enhanced food safety governance in Saudi Arabia. Through continuous monitoring, risk assessment, the enforcement of food safety standards, and ensuring that food products available in the Kingdom meet both national and international safety requirements [47]. As part of its ongoing efforts to reduce FBDs in Saudi Arabia, the SFDA has implemented a range of targeted initiatives to enhance food safety and protect public health. These include the launch of a nationwide digital awareness campaign that provides consumers with evidence-based information on the causes of food poisoning, safe food handling practices, proper storage methods, and guidance on when to seek medical attention. Furthermore, the SFDA has released consumer-focused content addressing common FBDs, such as specific bacteria and toxins, along with best practices for handling, cooking, and storing food. In addition, the Authority conducts regular inspections and random sampling of both domestic and imported food products to ensure compliance with safety standards and microbial limits. Non-compliant products are removed from the market immediately. These measures not only support public health protection by minimizing the burden of FBDs but also align with international best practices and global health strategies such as WHO’s Global Food Safety Strategy (2022–2030) [47–49].

This stringent oversight has contributed to positive outcomes, with recent studies indicating high rates of adherence to food safety knowledge, attitudes, and practices among a majority of food handlers in major urban centres [47, 48]. Nevertheless, persistent challenges remain, including significant knowledge gaps in specific areas such as cross-contamination prevention, correct thawing procedures, and personal hygiene, particularly among certain demographic groups of food workers [47, 48]. To address these deficiencies, strategic solutions are being pursued, focusing on the implementation of enhanced, culturally and linguistically appropriate training programs, the development of specialized academic curricula, and the continued adoption of technology to strengthen surveillance and risk analysis across the food supply chain.

Future Directions

This comprehensive analysis highlights several priority areas for future research and intervention. First, the marked increases in Salmonellosis and Bacillary dysentery warrant molecular epidemiology to test for emerging subtypes or antimicrobial resistance; whole-genome sequencing (WGS) can map transmission chains and sources. Second, the regional variations calls for region-specific risk-factor studies; in high-IR areas (Ha’il, Bishah. And Northen region), focused case–control designs can pinpoint modifiable determinants. Third, the finding that 68.47% of infections were attributed to public sources highlights the need for comprehensive audits of food safety practices in the commercial food service sector. Future studies should evaluate the implementation and effectiveness of Hazard Analysis Critical Control Point (HACCP) systems in Saudi food establishments, with particular attention to high-risk settings identified through our epidemiological analysis [49]. For intervention strategies, seasonal patterns support season-specific actions, e.g., intensified food safety education and regulatory oversight during summer/fall for bacterial pathogens. Resource allocation should prioritize consistently high-IR regions while maintaining baseline surveillance elsewhere. Technology-enabled surveillance with real-time outbreak detection is a promising direction [50] to reduce reporting delays and inconsistencies and enable faster public-health action [51, 52].

Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this study. First, the study relied on officially reported passive-surveillance data, which may be subject to underreporting or misclassification biases. Regions with stronger healthcare infrastructure and laboratory capacity may report higher case numbers due to superior detection rather than true higher incidence. Nonetheless, standardized reporting criteria and laboratory-confirmed diagnoses were used by the MOH to mitigate these issues, and data completeness checks were performed prior to analysis. Second, variation in reported cases likely reflects differences in population size, food-industry density, environmental conditions, and regional food-safety practices. Relatedly, observed increases in certain FBDs over nine years may partly reflect surveillance enhancements and greater public-health vigilance, not solely true rises in incidence. Third, our study focused on six FBDs, which represent only a subset of the full spectrum of FBDs. Notably, brucellosis also has non-foodborne transmission routes, and our data do not distinguish the route of acquisition, which is a limitation when comparing it directly with exclusively FBDs. Important notifiable infections caused by other pathogens were not included, constraining the comprehensiveness of the epidemiological profile. Fourth, the study period (2015–2023) encompassed the COVID-19 pandemic, which likely influenced both disease transmission dynamics and healthcare seeking behaviours. The notable decreases observed in several FBDs during 2020–2021 may reflect pandemic-related factors rather than true epidemiological trends. Fifth, temporal trend assessment relied on simple linear regression of aggregated annual IR, which does not model the underlying case counts with population offsets, may be sensitive to overdispersion, and does not account for potential temporal autocorrelation or non-linear changes over time (including pandemic-related disruptions). Accordingly, these regression results should be interpreted as a descriptive test for monotonic linear trends; more advanced count-based or time-series approaches were beyond the scope of this study and warrant evaluation in future work.

Conclusion

This nine-year epidemiological analysis of FBDs in Saudi Arabia (2015–2023) identifies important patterns in disease distribution across demographic groups, regions, and time periods. The 9-year cumulative incidence of 50.58 per 100,000 poses a public-health challenge yet remains lower than annual rates reported in many other countries. The distinct demographic profile, with highest burden among adults 20–49 years, diverges from global patterns and requires locally tailored prevention. Rises in Salmonellosis and Bacillary dysentery, alongside a decline in Amoebic dysentery, highlight pathogen-specific temporal trends that should guide targeted control.

The regional variations identified, with the highest event rates observed in Ha’il, Bishah, and the Northern Region, provide a strategic roadmap for prioritising resource allocation and implementing intensified interventions in high-burden areas. The seasonal patterns observed, particularly the summer-fall predominance of bacterial pathogens, align with established understanding of temperature-dependent bacterial growth and offer practical guidance for timing prevention efforts. The predominance of infections linked to public food sources supports enhanced food-handler training, targeted inspections in high-burden regions, and systematic monitoring/reporting within establishments. Priorities include strengthening surveillance, probing drivers of adverse trends, implementing targeted actions in high-risk regions/seasons, and evaluating impact. Under Vision 2030, food safety and FBD reduction remain central to population health and system efficiency. These patterns inform national priorities and contribute to regional/global understanding of FBD epidemiology in rapidly developing settings with unique geographic, cultural, and demographic contexts.
