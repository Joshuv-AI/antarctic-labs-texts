High‐Performance Organic Aerogels Tailored for Versatile Recycling Approaches: Recycling‐Reforming‐Upcycling
Authors: Christos Pantazidis, Chang‐lin Wang, Željko Tomović
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractOrganic aerogels are emerging as promising materials due to their versatile properties, rendering them excellent candidates for a variety of applications in the fields of thermal insulation, energy storage, pharmaceuticals, chemical adsorption, and catalysis. However, current aerogel designs rely on cross‐linked polymer networks, which lack efficient end‐of‐use solutions, thereby hindering their overall sustainability. In this study, a facile synthesis of organic aerogels with a unique combination of imine and cyanurate moieties is presented, resulting in high‐performance, lightweight insulating materials. The aerogels’ structure, ensures mechanical robustness, thermal resistance, and hydrophobicity without additional treatments, crucial for long‐term performance. Additionally, in response to the currently unsustainable use of cross‐linked polymer materials, the molecular design offers diverse avenues of chemical recycling. These include full depolymerization back into the original monomers, partial network fragmentation producing soluble oligomers that can be promptly employed to fabricate new aerogels, and upcycling of aerogel waste into useful building blocks. This work pioneers a novel approach to material design, emphasizing recyclability as a core feature while maintaining high‐performance excellence.Keywords: aerogels, cyanurate, insulation, polyimine, recycling, sustainability, upcycling

Introduction

As a direct consequence of society's ever‐growing energy demands, we are facing a crisis that becomes more apparent and imminent as time progresses. Global energy consumption has surged to unprecedented levels, with projections indicating a further 14% increase by the year 2050.[ 1 ] Notably, the heating and cooling of residential and office spaces, amounts to approximately 48% of the global building energy consumption,[ 2 , 3 ] mostly deriving from the usage of fossil fuels. Additionally, this reliance on traditional energy sources significantly contributes to greenhouse gas emissions, vastly increasing the environmental and economic burden.[ 3 , 4 , 5 , 6 , 7 ] In order to reduce our energy consumption and proportionally decrease the carbon footprint that comes with it, research is driven to produce advanced thermal insulating materials that could minimize heat loss and energy waste, in buildings and transportation vehicles worldwide.[ 4 , 8 , 9 ] To legitimize this motivation, strong political efforts and international regulations are taking place to ensure that the aforementioned materials will play a critical role for a more sustainable future.[ 10 ]

Among several thermally insulating materials, organic aerogels exhibit strong potential to replace conventional insulation products like polystyrene foams, glass wool, polyurethane foams, etc. due to their significantly lower thermal conductivity (≤20 mW m−1 K−1).[ 8 , 11 ] Their highly porous and lightweight characteristics, combined with their open mesoporous structures (2−50 nm), provide intricate networks of high tortuosity. Within their interior, the collisions between gas molecules are significantly minimized, thereby granting the aerogels’ desirable insulating properties (i.e., the Knudsen effect).[ 12 , 13 ] Furthermore, the versatile chemical structure of organic aerogels, enables the design of diverse materials, spanning various chemistries, processing methods, and potential applications.[ 14 , 15 , 16 ] For instance, due to their high specific surface area and their ability to host various types of molecules, aerogels can be effectively used in catalysis, drug delivery systems (nano vessels), and as absorbents for water purification.[ 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 ]

However, organic aerogel design is yet to be optimized, since it consists of covalently cross‐linked polymer networks which generally do not offer the potential for sustainable end‐of‐life management. Hence, they follow a linear economy value chain, that ends with their landfill deposition or incineration, providing scarce sustainable alternatives. Thus, the primary objective of this work is to prepare aerogels that combine excellent performance traits as insulating materials, with smart molecular design that provides versatile recycling pathways to prolong their service life and add value in both economic and environmental aspects.

Herein, we report the synthesis of polyimine aerogels based on cyanurate moieties, using condensation reaction of trifunctional aldehyde, 2,4,6‐Tris(4‐formylphenoxy)−1,3,5‐triazine (TFPT) and difunctional amine, 4,4′‐Oxydianiline (ODA), in dimethylacetamide (DMAc), which were subsequently dried using supercritical CO2 (Figure 1 ). The prepared aerogels perform as great insulation materials, displaying large specific surface area, low bulk density, and thermal conductivity. Furthermore, owing to their rich aromatic and cyanurate content, the aerogels exhibit excellent mechanical performance, thermal and flame resistance, along with intrinsic hydrophobicity, which is critical for their long‐term performance. More importantly, the aerogels’ design offers multiple pathways of sustainable end‐of‐life alternatives through closed‐loop recycling, reforming via transimination and chemical upcycling (Figure 1). The reversible imine bonds are cleavable on demand under acidic conditions,[ 22 , 23 , 24 ] enabling monomer recovery in high yield and purity which can be immediately reused for the preparation of recycled aerogels (closed‐loop recycling). Second, the dynamic nature of the imine moieties fosters partial depolymerization of the cross‐linked network using transimination mechanism.[ 25 , 26 ] The produced soluble oligomers can be immediately used for the reformation of polyimine aerogels, effectively eliminating aerogel waste (reforming via transimination). Lastly, the introduction of aryloxy substituted triazine core as part of the structure, facilitates depolymerization of the cross‐linked network through aromatic substitution under basic conditions.[ 27 , 28 ] This procedure, allows for the retrieval of valuable building blocks, distinct from the original precursors (upcycling).

Schematic of organic aerogel design that facilitates versatile recycling approaches. Closed‐loop recycling, reforming via transimination and chemical upcycling.

This work demonstrates the first example of organic aerogels in which various recycling alternatives are embedded in the molecular design. With our work, we aim to inspire a paradigm shift toward holistic material design, where recyclability through multiple pathways is central, ensuring the creation of materials that meet both high standards and sustainable objectives.

Results and Discussion

Design and Preparation of Polyimine Aerogels

The samples investigated in this study were synthesized through the condensation reaction of trifunctional aromatic aldehyde, TFPT, with difunctional aromatic amine, ODA, in an organic solvent (DMAc), followed by solvent exchange with acetone and drying using supercritical CO2, yielding TFPT‐ODA aerogels (TOAs, Figure 2 ). TFPT was prepared by the reaction of cyanuric chloride and 4‐hydroxybenzaldehyde in a heterogeneous mixture of water and dichloromethane. The aromatic substitution on the triazine core took place by using NaOH, and tetrabutylammonium bromide (TBAB) as a phase transfer catalyst, resulting in the synthesis of the monomer precursor in high yield (92%) and purity (>99% assessed by NMR) (Figure S1, Supporting Information). The incorporation of cyanurate moieties in polymer networks, yields mechanically robust materials with exceptional thermal and chemical stability, high flame retardancy, and low water uptake.[ 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 ] These characteristics prolong the service life of insulating materials and are sought‐after qualities for high‐performance organic aerogels, thus serving as the primary reason for the selection of TFPT as a precursor.

The precursor solutions are mixed and allowed to gel at 80°C for 2 h. Prior to the aerogel synthesis, a model reaction was conducted using TFPT and aniline, a corresponding aromatic monoamine, structurally similar to ODA (Figure S2a, Supporting Information). The reaction demonstrated quantitative, catalyst‐free formation of imine bonds, without unwanted side reactions such as nucleophilic attack on the ether bonds, which may occur with aliphatic amines and aryloxy substituted triazines.[ 32 ] Furthermore, the model compound was treated with HCl solution to probe the hydrolysis of the imine bonds, a critical aspect for achieving chemical closed‐loop recycling of the aerogels (Figure S2b, Supporting Information). Moreover, the cyanurate core of the TFPT precursor is susceptible to nucleophilic aromatic substitution (SNAr) under basic conditions,[ 27 , 28 ] a reaction that can be harnessed to promote network depolymerization of the prepared aerogels and yield valuable upcycled building blocks. Therefore, a model reaction was conducted using the TFPT monomer in refluxing ethanol, catalyzed by K2CO3. The reaction demonstrated quantitative substitution of the triazine's core benzaldehyde groups by ethoxy ones, yielding the corresponding triethoxy triazine (Figure S3, Supporting Information). To summarize, the choice for aerogel precursors followed a comprehensive perspective of several positive attributes. The facile imine bond formation and hydrolysis, allow for an undemanding preparation and subsequent recycling of aerogels, while the highly aromatic content paired with the incorporation of cyanurate moieties, can provide mechanical strength, chemical and thermal stability as well as hydrophobic behavior (Figure 2a).

Various organic solvents (DMSO, NMP, DMAc, DMF) suitable for solubilizing the precursors were employed across different monomer concentrations (6‐12% w/v) to screen and optimize the gelation procedure (Table S1, Supporting Information). DMAc was identified as the optimal solvent to produce aerogels of low shrinkage and structural integrity, and was selected for further use (Table S2, Supporting Information). Typically, organogels were prepared by mixing precursor solutions and allowing them to react for 2 h at 80°C, followed by a 24 h aging period at room temperature. The organogels underwent solvent exchange in an acetone bath and were subsequently dried using supercritical CO2. This drying method is known to be superior in preserving the intricate porous domains of the polymeric networks, generally resulting in aerogels with minimal linear shrinkage.[ 20 ] Prior to morphological, thermal, and mechanical characterization, the dimensions and weight of the aerogels were measured to calculate their shrinkage and bulk density. Additionally, the formation of imine bonds in the samples was evaluated using FT‐IR, where the appearance of the characteristic band at 1630 cm−1 (C═N stretch) and the attenuation of the corresponding aldehyde signal at 1695 cm−1 was observed (Figure S5b, Supporting Information).

Aerogel Properties

Initially, the linear shrinkage and correlated bulk density of the aerogels were recorded. The observed values of linear shrinkage (<12%) were significantly lower than those reported in existing literature,[ 33 , 34 , 35 ] allowing for effective control of the samples’ bulk density relative to the original concentration of precursors in the reaction solution. The TOAs were lightweight materials with bulk densities in the range of 76 to 116 mg cm−3 (Table 1).

The microstructural and morphological characterization of the samples involved nitrogen physisorption porosimetry and helium pycnometry in conjunction with scanning electron microscopy (SEM). The TOAs exhibited a complex network of interconnected domains, resulting in a highly tortuous mesoporous morphology, a critical aspect for aerogel insulation materials. Specifically, their adsorption‐desorption isotherms (Figure 3a) displayed characteristics of a type IV curve, typical for mesoporous materials,[ 36 ] while the specific surface area of the samples can be extrapolated employing the Brunauer‐Emmet‐Teller theory (BET). The prepared TOAs exhibit similar range of specific surface area values (≈265 m2 g−1) showing no observable trend correlating to monomer concentration. Pore volume, on the other hand, is calculated using Barrett–Joyner–Halenda (BJH) analysis of the desorption curve and yields an upward trend (0.70 to 0.82 cm3 g−1, Table 1) favoring solutions of higher initial concentration. Essentially, varying the monomer concentration during gelation, in the range of 6 to 12% w/v does not directly affect the specific surface area of the sample but results in increasingly larger pores, which is also evident from the average pore diameter and the width distribution (Figure 3b) exhibited by the different samples. The skeletal density of the TOAs, ρs, was evaluated through helium pycnometry, and the values were employed to calculate the porosity of the samples, which exceeded 90% in each case. The intricate porous morphology of the TOAs was confirmed by SEM images, as depicted in Figure 3c.

The aerogels' intertwined mesoporous morphology, effectively impedes the collisions of air molecules, thus reducing heat transfer, which consequently leads to their low thermal conductivity values (19.3–21.8 mW m−1 K −1, Table 1). These values, although they lie in close range, show a slight increase with the rise of the sample's bulk density (Figure 3d), which is consistent with the documented aerogel insulative behavior.[ 37 ] The TOAs outperformed the commonly used insulation products,[ 9 , 11 ] highlighting their promising applications as state‐of‐the‐art materials. Thermogravimetric analysis of the samples, revealed aerogels of substantial thermal stability, in par with the standards of high‐performance insulation materials. The TOAs aromatic framework, enriched with cyanurate groups, conferred excellent resistance to thermal degradation (Td5% = 400°C), leaving a proportionally high amount of char residue, ≈35–45 wt.% at 800°C, under N2 atmosphere (Figure 3e). Next, to confirm the TOAs’ resistance to incineration, a preliminary combustion test was performed by exposing the sample to an open ethanol flame for 10 s (Figure 3f). The aerogel exhibited no signs of extensive combustion, aside from the formation of a char layer on the surface, which inhibited further burning of its interior (Figure S6a,b, Movie S1, Supporting Information). Thus, the TOAs demonstrated enhanced thermal and flame resistance without the requirement for additional additives or post‐production chemical processing (see Figure S7, Supporting Information for additional data on thermal properties).

A key attribute of high‐performance insulative aerogels is their ability to maintain structural and chemical integrity upon water contact, as moisture and water uptake can hinder their insulating performance. The TOAs demonstrated intrinsic superhydrophobicity with high water contact angles (≈140°, Figure 3i) without additional hydrophobization steps. Even after harsh water uptake tests, including complete sample immersion in water for 24 h, the TOAs exhibited no physical degradation or chemical changes, and showed only minor water uptake (8.5 wt.%).[ 22 ] The hydrolytic stability of the imine bonds, and thus the crosslinking density of the network, was ensured by FT‐IR spectra which displayed no changes after prolonged water contact (Figure 3h). Additionally, the intricate porous structures were unaffected, as evidenced by the comparison of the N2 physisorption isotherms, which remained virtually identical after water treatment (Figure S8a, Supporting Information). Dynamic water sorption data showed a minimal water uptake (4.8 wt.%), when TOA sample was exposed to high relative humidity (92%) for >30 h (Figure S8b, Supporting Information).

The mechanical properties of TOAs were measured with uniaxial compression tests. The results showed that the TOAs could withstand high compressive strains without any cracks, close to 80% vertical deformation (Figure S6c, Supporting Information). The samples’ compressive modulus increased in correlation with their bulk density, reaching a peak compressive strength of 297.3 kPa (Table 1, Figure 3g).

Recycling Approaches

The prepared TOAs are lightweight, highly porous, and mechanically robust aerogels that also demonstrate significant thermal and hydrolytic stability. These attributes, coupled with their low thermal conductivity, render them excellent insulation materials with promising potential. However, it is essential to emphasize the significance of incorporating recycling options into the material design. Herein, we demonstrate three distinct pathways (Recycling‐Reforming‐Upcycling, Figure 4 ) using TOA_DMAc_10 as an example due to its well‐balanced tradeoff between low thermal conductivity and good mechanical performance.

Closed‐Loop Recycling

The facile formation of imine bonds, coupled with their controlled hydrolysis in acidic aqueous solutions, constitutes the primary reason behind their integration in the design of the prepared TOAs. Full depolymerization of the aerogel network occurred at room temperature, in a 1N HCl solution, where the imine bonds were selectively and quantitatively cleaved. The depolymerization of aerogel pieces leads to a suspension of the insoluble TFPT monomer in the HCl solution, while the aqueous phase contained ODA in its hydrochloric salt form (Figure S9a, Supporting Information). TFPT can be separated via filtration and is further purified by dissolution in DMAc and precipitation in water, while ODA can be precipitated in a NaOH solution, rendering it insoluble upon neutralization. The precursors were isolated in high yields (96% and 81% for TFPT and ODA respectively) and high purity (>99% assessed by NMR, Figure 5a) and were subsequently used for the preparation of recycled TOA (TOA_Recycled), mirroring the experimental protocol of TOA_DMAc_10. Notably, the recycled sample, exhibited low thermal conductivity (20.9 mW m−1 K −1), identical to the original, low bulk density of 116 mg cm−3, along with high values of specific surface area (284 m2 g−1) and porosity (91.7%). Overall, the properties of the recycled TOA were comparable to those of the original sample, thus illustrating efficient closed‐loop recycling of the material while maintaining excellent performance (Figure 5b; Table S3, Figure S11, Supporting Information). Further details about the experimental procedure and the purification of recycled precursors can be found in the Supporting Information.

Reforming via Transimination

Besides the reversibility of imine bonds, their intrinsic dynamic nature offers promising pathways for recycling polymer networks through a process known as transimination.[ 25 , 38 ] This approach can be effectively employed for the repurposing of aerogel waste, bypassing the necessity for acid hydrolysis and the subsequent monomer purification steps. The method induces the partial depolymerization of the aerogel network via imine exchange reactions, and yields soluble oligomers that can be used for the preparation of new samples (aerogel‐to‐sol‐to‐aerogel, ASA process).[ 39 ] Thus, we demonstrate an alternative approach to closed‐loop recycling, effectively repurposing aerogel material by minimalizing waste. To achieve this, TOA powder was dispersed in a DMAc solution containing ODA, in a threefold excess compared to the aerogel's initial content. This excess, represents the minimum amount of ODA necessary to produce fully soluble oligomers (Figure S10a, Supporting Information). The mixture was heated and sonicated to facilitate the imine exchange reactions between the crosslinked imine aerogel network and ODA. Through the transimination process, the crosslinking density was gradually decreased and solubilized oligomers were obtained (Figure S10b, Supporting Information). Subsequently, a corresponding amount of TFPT in DMAc was added to yield an organogel with 10% w/v monomer concentration, followed by solvent exchange to acetone and supercritical CO2 drying. The reformed aerogel (TOA_Reformed) contained 25 wt.% of repurposed TOA waste material. Importantly, the reformed sample exhibited matching characteristics with the original one, maintaining low thermal conductivity (21.4 mW m−1 K −1) and bulk density of 106 mg cm−3 along with high specific surface area (223 m2 g−1) and porosity (91.8%) (Figure 5b; Table S3, Figure S11, Supporting Information). These findings legitimize the usage of TOA waste, as a valuable resource for the reformation of equally high‐performance materials, thus concluding a cycle where aerogel waste is virtually minimized.

Chemical Upcycling

In the aforementioned recycling approaches, the resultant product is a TOA, possessing characteristics comparable to the original material, thus maintaining its intended utility in insulative applications. Conversely, the strategy of designing aerogel materials with upcycling in mind, greatly enhances the options of their waste valorization into valuable resources for other uses. Herein, this concept is employed by the incorporation of a cyanurate core, susceptible to aromatic substitution, within the aerogel molecular design. This addition facilitates an alternative pathway for network depolymerization, yielding upcycled building blocks. Specifically, TOA pieces were suspended in refluxing ethanol (90°C), containing a catalytic amount of K2CO3 (1.7% w/v). The aromatic substitution on the triazine core, broke down the aryloxy triazine moieties to produce a mixture of 4,4‐{oxy‐1,4‐diphenyl bis(nitromethylidine)} diphenol (ODBD) and 2,4,6‐triethoxy‐1,3,5‐triazine (TETA). The reaction reached quantitative yield, allowing for the recovery of the upcycled building blocks in high purity (>99% assessed by NMR) and high yield (67% and 74%). Details of the experimental procedure, including ODBD and TETA separation and purification, are provided in the Supporting Information (Figure S12, Supporting Information). To highlight the value of TOA upcycling, it is noteworthy that ODBD serves as a versatile monomer, featuring terminal hydroxyl groups and imine bonds for Schiff base chemistry, thereby finding applications in epoxy[ 40 , 41 ] or polyurethane‐based materials.[ 42 ] Meanwhile, TETA, the produced ethoxy triazine, has been previously utilized as a precursor for thermosets,[ 28 ] pharmaceuticals,[ 43 ] and antioxidants for polymeric matrices[ 44 ] By carefully selecting various nucleophiles during the upcycling process, a range of substituted monomers can be prepared. Consequently, the repurposing potential of TOA waste is enriched, establishing the upcycling of prepared TOAs as a dependable alternative that complements closed‐loop recycling and reforming via transimination.

Conclusion

In this study, we present the synthesis and characterization of high‐performance organic aerogels tailored for thermal insulation applications, while simultaneously introducing diverse recycling approaches to enhance their sustainability. Schiff base chemistry is employed for the facile formation of the polymer networks through imine condensation reaction between trifunctional aldehyde, TFPT, and difunctional amine, ODA. The unique combination of imine and cyanurate moieties results in aerogels that exhibit exceptional properties, combining mechanical strength with low bulk density and low thermal conductivity, all while maintaining intrinsic hydrophobicity and hydrolytic stability. Additionally, these aerogel networks are primarily comprised of aromatic and cyanurate rich structures, which render their outstanding thermal stability and flame resistance. The design of the TOAs, not only optimizes their properties but also facilitates various recycling avenues. The dynamic imine bonds allow for complete depolymerization via acid hydrolysis and partial network fragmentation through transimination, thereby enabling closed‐loop recycling and reforming of aerogels respectively. Furthermore, our aerogel design introduces upcycling opportunities by permitting network depolymerization through aromatic substitution of aryl cyanurate moieties, yielding added value building blocks. We aspire to influence the consensus of molecular design, aiming for materials that not only fulfil multiple criteria for their desired applications, but also are inherently driven toward longevity and sustainability.
