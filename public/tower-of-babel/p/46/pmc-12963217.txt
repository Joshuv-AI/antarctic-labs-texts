Strategies to target PD-1/PD-L1 in the tumor microenvironment
Authors: Yulan Kui, Fan Tong, Ruiguang Zhang, Xiaorong Dong, Jian Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn recent years, cancer immunotherapy has become one of the most important methods in cancer treatment, which can kill tumors by activating the immune system. The PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint signaling pathway in the tumor microenvironment can impair T cell responses and thereby promote tumor immune escape. Blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway can enhance the antitumor immune response and improve the therapeutic effect. Anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies have shown antitumor effects in clinical practice and are revolutionizing the field of cancer management. Recent studies have shown that the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway in the tumor microenvironment is finely regulated, and inhibition of the PD-1/PD-L1 signal by targeting its regulatory mechanism can activate the antitumor immune response and reverse the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment. New strategies and drugs designed and developed for the PD-1/PD-L1 blockade have brought new hope for better treatment of tumors.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.

Introduction

Cancer is one of the major public health problems in the world and one of the most important factors that seriously endanger human health and life safety [1]. The creation of advanced treatment breakthroughs in basic and clinical research will surely benefit cancer patients and rapidly reduce the mortality rate of many difficult-to-treat cancers [2]. With the deepening of understanding and research on the human immune system, the development of tumor immunotherapy drugs for tumor treatment based on the basic principles of immunology has received great attention [3]. Immunotherapy plays an important role in killing tumors by activating the body’s immune system. It is a new therapeutic method after surgery, chemoradiotherapy, targeted therapy and anti-vascular therapy [4].

Tumor tissue is composed of cancer cells and many other cell types, such as fibroblasts, endothelial cells, and a variety of immune cells [5]. T cells can recognize and attack tumor cells expressing tumor-specific antigens in a double-signal mode [6]. Besides, T cell activation also induces some inhibitory pathways such as CTLA-4 (Cytotoxic T-lymphocyte-associated protein 4) and PD-1 (Programmed cell death-1). This signal will eventually weaken and terminate the T cell response. These receptors that transmit co-inhibitory signals act as immune checkpoints. Blocking these inhibitory signal pathways can enhance the antitumor immune response of T cells, which is called immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) [7]. Among them, PD-1/PD-L1 (Programmed death-ligand 1) blocking therapy stands out and is revolutionizing the field of cancer treatment [8].

PD-1 is a 288 amino acid type I transmembrane protein encoded by CD279. It consists of an immunoglobulin superfamily domain, a stem structure of ~ 20 amino acids, a transmembrane domain, and a ~ 95 residue. The intracellular domain is composed of the immunoreceptor tyrosine‑based inhibition motif (ITIM) and the immunoreceptor tyrosine‑based switch motif (ITSM) [9]. In addition to CTLA-4, PD-1 is another coinhibitory signal receptor expressed on immune cells. It is expressed on T cells, B cells, dendritic cells, natural killer cells (NK cells) and tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) and other cells [10]. At present, the function and biochemical characteristics of PD-1 have been well studied in T cells, and its expression is upregulated on TIL, which may also contribute to tumor immune escape [11].

According to reports, PD-1 has two ligands, PD-L1 and PD-L2 (Programmed cell death ligand 2, encoded by CD273) [12], which have different expression patterns [13]. Since PD-L1 is more widely expressed in normal cells and tumor cells than PD-L2, more research focuses on exploring the physiological and pathological functions of PD-1/PD-L1 and how to disrupt their interactions in cancer treatment [14]. PD-L1 is a type I transmembrane protein with 290 amino acids encoded by CD274 [9] and is a ligand of PD-1 [15], which can induce T cell apoptosis to protect tumor cells [16]. The high expression of PD-L1 is often observed on tumor cells. The combination of PD-L1 and PD-1 on T cells can cause T cell dysfunction and failure, thus preventing cytotoxic T cells from targeting tumor cells effectively and inducing the formation of the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment [17]. Therefore, the design and development of PD-1/PD-L1 blocking antibodies to disrupt the PD-1/PD-L1 interaction will reactivate T cell function to eradicate cancer cells [18]. In addition, PD-L1 can also directly transmit signals to PD-L1-expressing cells [19]. It has been reported that the PD-L1 expression is also closely related to the poor prognosis of a variety of tumors [20].

In recent years, engineered antibodies [21] have been used to develop new interventions to block the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint pathway, regulate T cell activation, and activate antitumor immune response [9]. Recently, these new therapies have become the mainstay of many cancers and have rapidly become one of the important treatment methods for many cancers and have achieved great clinical success [4]. However, the relatively low response rate and resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 blockade therapy highlight the importance of investigating the molecular regulatory mechanism of PD-1/PD-L1 [22]. This review will mainly discuss the regulatory mechanism of the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint pathway in the tumor microenvironment and the different strategies and challenges for targeted inhibition of this pathway and clinical application of targeting PD-1/PD-L1 strategies, which will help us to better develop immunotherapy drugs and improve the therapeutic effect.

Targeting the regulatory mechanism of PD-1 in T cells

Basic and clinical studies have shown that the inhibitory signal mediated by PD-1 is involved in the negative regulation of T cells, and targeting PD-1 can enhance the antitumor immune response and treat cancer [23]. Immunological studies have found that NFATc1 [23], STAT3/4 [24], CTCF [24], FoxO1 [25], IRF9 [26], Notch signaling pathway [27], etc., can promote PD-1 transcription in T cells, while T-bet [28], LSD1, and Blimp-1 [29] can inhibit the transcription of PD-1 in T cells. These results indicate that the induction and inhibition of PD-1 are controlled by fine regulatory mechanisms, and the upregulation of PD-1 is also a natural result of T cell activation, which is necessary for the termination of the immune response. However, in the tumor microenvironment, the PD-1 expression level of exhausted T cells continues to increase, which leads to T cells being unable to respond to activation signals and lose their antitumor activity, leading to tumor immune escape [30]. Targeting upregulating mechanisms of PD-1 in T cells

There are different mechanisms to upregulate PD-1 expression in T cells. For example, transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β) is a multifunctional cytokine, which can regulate many key physiological and pathological functions, such as development, tissue homeostasis, tissue regeneration, immune tolerance and so on [31]. Firstly, in T cells and TIL, TGFβ1 enhances antigen-induced PD-1 transcriptional activation through SMAD3, and mediates T cell suppression through PD-1 upregulation, thereby weakening the antitumor immune response [32]. Secondly, tumor cell-derived TGFβ1 increases the expression of SRSF3/5 in T cells through the P38/p-Ser-H3 axis, and then SRSF3 binds with NXF1 to promote the extranuclear transport of PD-1 mRNA and maintain the stability of PD-1 mRNA, which eventually leads to the increase of PD-1 expression and inhibits the antitumor response of T cells in clear cell renal cell carcinoma [33]. In the cytoplasm of TIL cells of liver cancer, the combination of TOX and PD1 promotes the endocytosis and recycling process of PD1, thereby maintaining the high expression of PD1 on the surface of CD8+ T cells and downregulation of TOX expression in CD8+ T cells can enhance the effect of anti-PD1 therapy [34]. DGKζ enhances the expression of PD-1 in T cells and inhibits the activity of CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment [35]. Also, the alternative splicing of pre-mRNA exon 3 of PD-1 leads to increased expression of soluble PD-1 on T cells via RNA splicing factor HNRNPK, resulting in promoting activation of T cells by inhibiting full-length PD-1 expression associated with inhibitory signaling of T cells [36]. High-MYC-induced lactic acid increases in tumor cells upregulate PD-1 expression in Treg cells but downregulate PD-1 in CD8 T cells. The increased lactic acid uptake by monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) enhances NFAT1 translocation into the nucleus to promote PD-1 expression in Treg cells [37]. The HIF-1α-induced Rai also increases PD-1 expression in T cells by inhibiting glycogen synthase kinase (GSK)-3 phosphorylation in colorectal cancer [38]. Rab37 mediates glycosylated PD-1 trafficking to the plasma membrane in T cells, thereby facilitating T cell exhaustion and fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment in lung cancer [39]. Furthermore, Notch1-induced noncoding RNA lncNDEPD1 also upregulates PD-1 expression in CD8 T cells by competing with miR-3619-5p in lung cancer [40].

Targeting downregulating mechanisms of PD-1 in T cells

However, in the cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTL) of colon cancer, targeting proprotein convertases can reduce the expression of PD-1 by inhibiting Notch, calcium/NFAT, and NF-κB pathways [41]. Additionally, the ubiquitination modification system is involved in the regulation of PD-1 protein degradation [42]. For example, FBXO38 is the E3 ubiquitin ligase of PD-1, which mediates the polyubiquitination linked to Lys48, promotes the ubiquitination and degradation of PD-1 protein, and regulates the level of PD-1 on the surface of T cells [43]. E3 ubiquitin ligase c-Cbl induces ubiquitination and degradation of PD-1 protein dependent on its RING finger structure [44]. The tea polyphenol epigallocatechin-3-gallate (EGCG) also decreases PD-1 expression of T cells by suppressing NF-κB nuclear translocation and phosphorylation in melanoma [45]. D-mannose, as a C-2 epimer of glucose, inhibits endocytic recycling of PD-1 and further promotes lysosomal degradation of PD-1 by increasing phosphorylated inactivation of GSK3β at Ser9 and promoting TFE3 translocation to the nucleus [46]. Methionine-induced S-adenosylmethionine (SAM) and H3K79me2 promote AMPK expression, resulting in inhibiting PD-1 expression by restraining endoplasmic reticulum stress-associated gene XBP1 in CD4 T cells but not CD8 T cells [47]. The cytokine IL-2 also inhibits PD-1 expression in T cells by transcriptional factors STAT5A competing with NFAT1 through the overlapped binding sequence [48].

Therefore, the above studies indicate that targeting the upstream regulatory mechanism of PD-1 to reduce PD-1 expression may be another strategy to inhibit the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint pathway.

Targeting the regulatory mechanism of PD-L1 in the tumor microenvironment

In the tumor microenvironment, inflammatory cytokines can induce the expression of PD-L1 on tumor cells and also promote the expression of PD-L1 in macrophages, dendritic cells, and stromal cells [49]. Among them, interferon γ (IFN-γ) is the most effective in recognizing tumors and mediating the expression of PD-L1, which ultimately leads to tumor immune escape [50]. Therefore, it is necessary to fully understand the upstream pathways regulating PD-L1 expression on tumor cells, which will help to design new strategies to regulate PD-L1 expression to improve the clinical response rate and efficacy of immunotherapy (Fig. 2). Targeting genetic and epigenetic regulation of PD-L1 expression in tumor cells

Genetic and epigenetic changes play a key role in the regulation of tumor development and immune escape [51–54]. In classic Hodgkin lymphoma [55] and small cell lung cancer [56], the increased copy number of chromosome 9p24.1, where CD274 is located, and chromosome rearrangement leads to increased PD-L1 expression. Furthermore, increased expression of PD-L1 in primary mediastinal large B-cell lymphoma is related to the translocation of adjacent promoters or enhancers [57]. Also, the amplification of PD-L1, PD-L2, and JAK2 has been identified in gastric cancer [58]. Studies have shown that structural changes in the tumor genome can destroy the 3′-UTR (untranslated region) of PD-L1 mRNA, regulate the stability of PD-L1 mRNA, and promote the increase of PD-L1 expression in tumors, thereby evading immune surveillance [59]. Tristetraprolin (TTP) reduces the stability of PD-L1 mRNA by targeting AREs (AU-rich elements) in the 3’-UTR of PD-L1 mRNA. The oncogenic RAS-MEK signaling pathway reduces TTP activity and stabilizes PD-L1 mRNA, promotes the expression of PD-L1 in tumor cells, and inhibits the antitumor immune response [60]. Bioinformatics analysis in breast cancer shows that there is a super-enhancer located between the CD274 and CD273 genes, which can promote the expression of PD-L1 and PD-L2. While the expression of PD-L1 and PD-L2 in tumors with deletion of the super-enhancer was significantly decreased, resulting in them being more sensitive to T cell-mediated killing [61]. Studies have found that the BRD4 inhibitor JQ1 downregulates the expression of PD-L1 on tumor cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages, and activates the activity of T cells to kill tumors [62]. The methyltransferase MLL1 catalyzes the formation of H3K4me3 on the PD-L1 promoter, thus activating PD-L1 transcription in pancreatic cancer cells. Inhibition of MLL1 can enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies by reducing the expression of PD-L1 [63]. Moreover, increased levels of histone acetylation of the PD-L1 gene in melanoma can upregulate PD-L1 expression in melanoma [64]. These findings indicate that genomic and epigenetic changes can promote tumor immune escape, which may be a sensitization target for PD-1/PD-L1 blockade therapy and a potential biomarker for patient selection.

Targeting transcriptional regulation of PD-L1 expression in tumor cells

More and more evidence showed that various upstream signaling pathways regulate the expression of PD-L1 at the transcriptional level by promoting the binding of key transcription factors in the promoter region of PD-L1 [65]. For example, the IFNγ-JAK-STAT signaling pathway mediates the upregulation of PD-L1 and promotes tumor immune escape [66]. Further, CDK5 is required to induce PD-L1 upregulation in medulloblastoma by IFN-γ, while inhibition of CDK5 recruits IRF1 competitive repressors IRF2 and IRF2BP2 to inhibit PD-L1 transcription, resulting in downregulation of PD-L1 expression [67]. Recent studies have shown that activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway can promote the expression of PD-L1 and induce the formation of a tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment [68]. HIF-1α binds to the hypoxia-responsive element (HRE) in the promoter of PD-L1 and activates the transcription of PD-L1 [69]. Hypoxia can induce the expression of PD-L1 in tumor cells, myeloid suppressor cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages by promoting the expression of HIF-1α. So, the hypoxic state of tumors can inhibit the antitumor immune response and promote immune escape [70]. It is worth noting that recent studies have shown that PD-L1 is the target gene of c-Myc transcription factor [71]. Inhibiting the activity of c-Myc can reduce the expression of PD-L1 and activate the antitumor immune response in synergy with ICB [72]. Many studies have shown that YAP1 [73] and TAZ [74], the components of the Hippo pathway, play an important role in the transcriptional regulation of tumor cell PD-L1, which can promote the elevation of PD-L1 and thereby inhibit the antitumor activity of T cells in the tumor microenvironment. The MAPK/ERK signaling pathway activates the transcription factor c-Jun to increase the expression of PD-L1 and promote tumor immune escape [75], while inhibiting the MAPK signaling pathway can reduce the expression of PD-L1 in tumor cells, improve the drug resistance of immunotherapy and improve the therapeutic effect [72, 75, 76]. The EGFR pathway in tumor cells mainly mediates the expression of PD-L1 through IL-6/JAK/STAT3 signaling and MAPK signaling pathways, which induces the immunosuppressive microenvironment and reduces the sensitivity of tumors to anti-PD-1 immunotherapy [77], and the expression of PD-L1 can be reduced by using EGFR inhibitors [78]. Glutamine deprivation can activate the EGFR/ERK/c-Jun signal and induce PD-L1 expression in renal cell carcinoma [79]. Epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) plays a key role in tumor immunosuppression and immune escape and is also involved in regulating the expression of immune checkpoint molecules such as PD-L1 [80]. The process of EMT regulating the expression of PD-L1 involves multiple molecules such as MUC1 [81], NF-κB [81], TGF-β [82], Snail1 [83] and ZEB1 [83, 84]. The PTEN-PI3K signaling pathway can activate STAT3-mediated PD-L1 expression in malignant pleural mesothelioma [85], and can also upregulate PD-L1 expression in glioma and squamous cell lung cancer through translational regulation [86], leading to tumor immune escape. The DNA damage signaling pathway can activate JAK-STAT-IRF1-mediated PD-L1 expression [87]. S-phase DNA damage can activate the STING pathway and induce PD-L1 expression [88]. PIM2 promotes phosphorylation of HSF1 to induce PD-L1 expression and promote tumor growth of breast cancer [89].

Non-coding RNAs in PD-L1 expression in tumor cells

Non-coding RNAs such as miRNA [90] and lncRNA [91] in tumors play an important role in regulating the expression of immune checkpoint molecules and the formation of the immune microenvironment. In NSCLC, the miR-200/ZEB1 axis regulates PD-L1 expression [83], and miR-34 directly binds to the 3’-UTR of PD-L1 mRNA to regulate PD-L1 expression [92]. LncRNA SNHG14/miR-5590-3p/ZEB1 positive feedback loop promotes immune escape in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma by regulating the expression of PD-L1 [93]. In acute myeloid leukemia, MUC1 regulates the expression of PD-L1 through miR-200c/miR-34a [94]. In pancreatic cancer, miR-142-5p inhibits PD-L1 expression by binding to the 3’-UTR of PD-L1 mRNA and activates the antitumor activity of T cells [95]. In ovarian cancer, miR-424 (322) inhibits PD-L1 expression by binding to the 3’-UTR of PD-L1 mRNA [96]. LIN28 promotes miRNA let-7 expression, which binds to the 3’-UTR of PD-L1 mRNA to inhibit PD-L1 expression [97]. lncRNA HCP5 and MIAT, as competitive endogenous RNAs, interact with miR-150-5p to upregulate the expression of PD-L1 [98]. LncRNA GATA3–AS1 regulates COPS5/CSN5 to maintain the stability of PD-L1 protein and promote the immune escape of triple-negative breast cancer [99]. In breast cancer, miR-873 inhibits PD-L1 expression by directly binding to the 3’-UTR of PD-L1 mRNA [100].

Post-translational modification in PD-L1 expression in tumor cells

Protein post-translational modification can affect the stability of PD-L1 protein and regulate the level of PD-L1 in tumor cells [42, 101]. GSK3β interacts with PD-L1 and induces phosphorylation-dependent proteasomal degradation of PD-L1 through β-TrCP. Epidermal growth factor (EGF) stabilizes tumor PD-L1 by inactivating GSK3β. In the syngeneic mouse model, the inhibition of the EGF signal by gefitinib makes PD-L1 unstable, resulting in enhanced antitumor T cell immunity and the therapeutic efficacy of the PD-1 blocker. After GSK3β-mediated phosphorylation of PD-L1, β-TrCP directly binds to PD-L1 and promotes PD-L1 polyubiquitination and degradation, while PD-L1 glycosylation can hinder its binding to GSK3β. Gefitinib inhibits EGF signaling and activates GSK3β, leading to downregulation of tumor PD-L1 expression and enhancing immunotherapy [102]. EGF signaling upregulates B3GNT3 glycosyltransferase to mediate PD-L1 glycosylation, and PD-L1 glycosylation is necessary for its binding to PD-1 and inhibiting T cells. Specific antibodies against glycosylated PD-L1 and antibody-conjugated drugs can block the interaction of PD-1/PD-L1 and promote the endocytosis and degradation of PD-L1, reactivate cytotoxic T cells, and play a bystander role, killing adjacent cancer cells without PD-L1 expression. This suggests that targeting PD-L1 glycosylation may be a potential strategy to improve the efficacy of ICB [103]. Besides, N-glycosyltransferase STT3 promotes PD-L1 glycosylation, stabilizes PD-L1 protein, and induces tumor immune escape [104]. EGF signal can induce PD-L1 monoubiquitination and polyubiquitination modification, and increase PD-L1 protein level [105]. Cullin 3SPOP is an E3 ligase that can promote the polyubiquitination and degradation of PD-L1. Cyclin D-CDK4 promotes SPOP phosphorylation and maintains its protein stability. The inactivation mutation of SPOP in tumors can inhibit ubiquitin-mediated PD-L1 degradation and induce an increase in PD-L1 levels and a decrease in the number of TIL [106]. TNF-α activates NF-kB to induce CSN5 expression. CSN5 enzyme promotes the deubiquitination of PD-L1, inhibits the degradation of PD-L1 protein, increases the level of PD-L1 in tumors, inhibits T cells, and induces tumor immune escape. Curcumin, a natural compound, inhibits CSN5-mediated PD-L1 elevation and acts synergistically with anti-CTLA-4 therapy to enhance antitumor immune response and delay tumor growth [107]. The palmitoylation of PD-L1 protein at C272 increases its stability [108, 109], while inhibition of DHHC3 weakens PD-L1 palmitoylation, reduces PD-L1 levels, and enhances antitumor immune response [109]. AMPK activated by metformin directly binds to and phosphorylates PD-L1 at S195, which results in abnormal glycosylation of PD-L1 and promotes PD-L1 degradation dependent on the ERAD pathway (ER-associated protein degradation) [110]. Inhibition of Sigma1 promotes selective autophagy-mediated degradation of PD-L1 [111]. CMTM6 binds to PD-L1 and maintains its expression in the cell membrane, which hinders lysosome-mediated PD-L1 degradation, while inhibiting CMTM6 can reduce tumor cell PD-L1 levels, improve T cell activity, and overcome tumor cell immune escape [112]. Another study also showed that CMTM6/4 binds to PD-L1 protein on the cell surface, reducing the ubiquitination of PD-L1, maintaining the stability of PD-L1 protein, and increasing the level of PD-L1 in tumor cells [113].

In summary, these studies indicate that targeting the upstream regulatory mechanism of PD-L1 to reduce tumor PD-L1 expression may be another strategy to enhance PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint blocking therapy.

Targeting the regulatory mechanism of PD-L1 in non-tumor cells

In addition to its high expression on tumor cells, PD-L1 is also widely present in various non-tumor cells, which can be primarily categorized into two major groups including different immune cells [114] and cells constituting tissue structures [115].

On the one hand, PD-L1 is mainly expressed on immune cells including macrophages, dendritic cells, B cells and regulatory T cells. For example, Diskin et al. suggested that PD-L1 expressed on T cells plays a significant role in weakening antitumor immune response, which PD-L1 on T cells could induce T cell self-tolerance via cell-intrinsic back-signaling, inhibit neighboring effector T cells through PD-1/PD-L1 axis and induce neighboring PD-1+ macrophage polarization towards an M2-like alternatively activated phenotype in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [116]. Also, PD-L1+ CD8+ T cells are associated with poor prognosis in melanoma patients [117]. The co-localization of PD-L1+ regulatory T cells and PD-1+ CD8+ T cells in tumor tissue is a supplement of PD-L1 expression on tumor cells to predict the response of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy in lung cancer [118]. Besides T cells, macrophages also express PD-L1 and have unique functions. The expression of PD-L1 on monocytes is mediated by the glycolysis-PFKFB3-NFκB axis in hepatocellular carcinoma to develop novel PD-L1 blockade strategies [119]. Progranulin also promotes PD-L1 expression on tumor-associated macrophages and M2 polarization via the STAT3 signaling pathway, resulting in CD8+ T cell exclusion and tumor immunity inhibition in breast cancer [120]. Colorectal cancer-derived small extracellular vesicles could increase PD-L1 expression on tumor-associated macrophages by miR-21-5p/miR-200a/PTEN/AKT and miR-21-5p/SCOC1/STAT1 pathways, which induces macrophage M2-like polarization and inhibits antitumor CD8+ T cells [121]. Furthermore, dendritic cells are mainly a group of important immune cells expressing PD-L1. High expression of PD-L1 on dendritic cells in tumor‑draining lymph nodes is related to metastasis and recurrent disease in oral cancer [122]. Also, IFN-γ and T cells could upregulate PD-L1 expression on type 1 dendritic cells to limit T cell responses in colon cancer [123].

On the other hand, PD-L1 is also expressed on stromal cells including cancer-associated fibroblasts, epithelial cells and endothelial cells. Cancer-associated fibroblasts are also the main sources of PD-L1 in the tumor microenvironment. In gastric cancer, FAP promotes PD-L1 expression on cancer-associated fibroblasts by inhibiting STAT1 degradation, resulting in CD8+ T cells dysfunction [115]. The gemcitabine chemotherapy induced PD-L1 expression in cancer-associated fibroblasts and prompted T cell inactivation in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [124]. Also, PD-L1+ cancer-associated fibroblasts are associated with poor prognosis in patients with esophageal cancer by inhibiting host tumor immunity [125].

Therefore, the widespread expression of PD-L1 on non-tumor cells constitutes a complex immune regulatory network, which is of significant importance for understanding the efficacy and side-effect mechanisms of immune checkpoint blockers as well as for developing novel therapeutic strategies. Also, PD-L1 derived from various cellular sources exerts heterogeneous effects on both immune evasion and the therapeutic outcomes of PD-1/PD-L1 blockade.

Targeting the regulatory mechanism of PD-1/PD-L2 in the tumor microenvironment

While the PD-1/PD-L1 axis is recognized as a pivotal pathway for tumor immune evasion and a critical target for immune checkpoint blockade (ICB) therapy, the distinct role of its alternative ligand, PD-L2, is garnering increasing attention [126]. PD-L2 not only exhibits higher affinity for PD-1 [127] but can also exert unique immunoregulatory functions by binding to repulsive guidance molecule b (RGMb) [128].

Recent studies have shown that PD-L2 is independently expressed in various tumors, with its expression patterns significantly differing from PD-L1, and is closely associated with the clinical response to ICB therapy [129]. For example, the dynamic change of PD-L2 on extracellular vesicles membrane is associated with the treatment outcome of anti-PD-1 monotherapy in melanoma patients by changing the ratio of granzyme B+ CD8T cells [130]. High expression of PD-L2 and FOXP3 is associated with poor survival in pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [131]. Also, renal clear cell carcinoma exosomes-derived PD-L2 promotes tumor cell growth by increasing regulatory T cells and decreasing cytotoxic CD8+ T cells [132]. In renal cell carcinoma and lung squamous cell carcinoma, high expression of PD-L2 is associated with suppressed antitumor immune responses, which suggests that targeting PD-1/PD-L2 is a potential immunotherapy strategy in PD-L2-expressing tumors [133]. Furthermore, matrix stiffness promotes PD-L2 expression by the SMYD3/H3K4me3 pathway, and targeting PD-L2 in combination with sorafenib and anti-PD-1 antibody inhibits hepatocellular carcinoma growth [134].

Therefore, it is important to elucidate the molecular structural features of the PD-1/PD-L2 axis, its regulatory mechanisms of expression, its immunosuppressive functions within the tumor microenvironment and potential therapeutic strategies targeting this pathway, providing a new theoretical basis for overcoming resistance to PD-1/PD-L1 monotherapy.

Immunotherapy drugs do not directly attack tumors but mobilize the immune system to treat them. The success of tumor immunotherapy, especially the immunotherapy targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, provides hope and confidence for better treatment and possible cure of cancer [14, 18, 90, 136, 137]. Monoclonal antibody

Antagonistic antibodies blocking the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint signaling pathway have proven their effectiveness in clinical trials on a variety of tumors [138–140]. For example, a large-scale phase I clinical trial of the anti-PD-1 antibody MK-3475(Pembrolizumab) showed that the response rate of patients with advanced melanoma was about 37% to 38% [141]. Subsequent studies showed that the overall response rate of advanced melanoma patients who progressed after treatment with pembrolizumab was 26% [142], so the FDA approved pembrolizumab in September 2014. Another phase III trial of nivolumab, an anti-PD-1 antibody, also showed clinical benefits in patients with metastatic melanoma. In this trial, the objective response rate of patients receiving nivolumab treatment was 40%, and the overall survival rate was 72.9%, while the objective response rate of patients receiving dacarbazine chemotherapy was 13.9%, and the overall survival rate was 42.1% [143]. Nivolumab was approved by the FDA in December 2014 for the treatment of patients with metastatic melanoma. In addition, a phase III clinical trial in non-small cell lung showed that patients treated with nivolumab had longer overall survival than docetaxel [144]. Nivolumab was approved by the FDA in March 2015 for use in patients with previously treated advanced or metastatic non-small cell lung cancer. Looking forward to the future, blocking antibodies targeting inhibitory receptors have broad application prospects, and more and more such drugs will be designed, developed, and used in clinical practice, playing an important role in cancer treatment [145]. Furthermore, PD-L1-related monoclonal antibodies including Atezolizumab, Durvalumab, Avelumab and Cosibelimab have been applied in patients with different tumors. Especially, Atezolizumab is approved by the FDA to be applied in multiple tumors including lung cancer, liver cancer, melanoma and so on. Durvalumab is also approved for lung cancer, liver cancer and biliary system cancer. Avelumab is mainly applied in urinary tumors. [146] Finally, the FDA approved Cosibelimab for metastatic or locally advanced cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma in 2024.[147]

Bispecific antibody

Recently, bispecific antibody drugs targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway have gradually gained attention in the research and development of immune drugs [148]. Among them, the bispecific antibody, which targets PD-L1 and TGF-β signals [149–151], has shown effective tumor-killing potential in preclinical experiments and phase I clinical trials. In addition, the bispecific antibody targeting PD-L1 and EGFR [152] and the bispecific antibody targeting PD-L1 and CSPG4 [153] also have good application prospects. The bispecific antibody Y126S targeting CTGF and PD-1 simultaneously has double antitumor effects by inhibiting CAFs activation-induced tumor fibrosis and promoting cytotoxic CD8+ T cells activation in pancreatic cancer [154]. In a phase Ia/Ib clinical trial, IBI318, as a bispecific antibody targeting PD-L1 and PD-1, has a well-tolerated safety profile and preliminary clinical efficacy in non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) and nasopharyngeal carcinoma (NPC) [155]. A phase I/Ib study of QL1706, a bispecific antibody engineered via the MabPair platform to contain both anti-PD-1 and anti-CTLA-4 antibodies, reported encouraging tolerability and efficacy in solid tumor patients who failed prior standard treatments [156].

Subcutaneous injection of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies

The administration of immune checkpoint blockers is undergoing a significant shift from intravenous infusion to subcutaneous injection. Subcutaneously administered PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies, facilitated by hyaluronidase-assisted technology or single-domain antibody design, significantly reduce administration time from 30 to 60 minutes to a few minutes, improve patient compliance and alleviate the burden on healthcare systems [157]. To date, four subcutaneous PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors have been approved globally, including envafolimab [158–160], atezolizumab [161], nivolumab [162] and pembrolizumab [163]. In addition, more and more subcutaneous PD‑1/PD‑L1 inhibitors are under investigation, with the majority having entered phase III or earlier stages of clinical trials. Subcutaneous PD‑1/PD‑L1 inhibitors in clinical development include toripalimab, sasanlimab, tislelizumab, SHR1901, GenSci120, and adebrelimab [157]. Therefore, subcutaneous administration offers potential advantages such as simplified operation, shorter treatment duration, improved patient comfort, and the possibility of home-based dosing, making it an important direction for the formulation improvement of PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors [164]. With the future approval of more products and the optimization of administration protocols [165], subcutaneous injection is expected to become the standard route of administration for PD‑1/PD‑L1 inhibitors, further enhancing the treatment experience for cancer patients.

Antibody-drug conjugate (ADC)

In addition, the design and development of ADC [166, 167] targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint pathway may also open up a new field of immunotherapy. ADC, as a novel chemo-immuno combination modality, could deliver precisely to cancer cells and kill tumors potently through three components including specific antibodies, stable linkers and potent cytotoxic drugs [168]. For example, U6244-051, as a PD-L1 antibody-polymer-Epirubicin conjugate, is a novel antitumor strategy through direct tumor cytotoxicity and diverse activated immune responses in ovarian cancer [169]. Also, a bispecific ADC targeting PD-L1 and B7–H3 with cytotoxic drugs monomethyl auristatin E has robust antitumor effects by promoting immunogenic cell death and activating innate and adaptive antitumor immune responses in laryngeal squamous cell carcinoma. [170] Furthermore, SLC3A2/PD-L1 BsADC is a bispecific antibody targeting PD-L1 and solute carrier family 3 member 2 (SLC3A2) conjugated with monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE), which has potent antitumor effects in solid tumors [171].

Immune-stimulating antibody conjugate（ISAC）

Although immune agonists, as immune adjuvants, are a promising application in anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies immunotherapy, they could produce serious immune-associated adverse effects [172]. Therefore, standing on the shoulders of ADC, ISAC is a wise choice to decrease adverse effects and promote precisely antitumor effects of anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies by conjugating immune agonists with anti-PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies [173]. For example, ADC HE-S2 conjugating anti-PD-L1 antibody and an immune modulator D18 could selectively target PD-L1 on tumor cells, and then release D18 to activate the antitumor immune response in colon cancer and melanoma [174]. Also, the antibody-β-glucan conjugates (AGC) composed of anti-PD-L1 antibody and β-glucan immune agonist have stronger tumor suppression effects compared to anti-PD-L1 antibody in colon cancer and melanoma by promoting antitumor function of DC cells and CD8+ T cells [175].

Other strategies to target PD-1/PD-L1 signaling

As mentioned above, inhibition of PD-1/PD-L1 signaling acquired satisfactory antitumor therapeutic effect by targeting regulatory mechanisms of PD-1 in T cells, targeting regulatory mechanisms of PD-L1 in tumor cells and interfering with the interaction of PD-1 and PD-L1 in the tumor microenvironment. Nevertheless, with the development of immunology and the update of antitumor treatment strategies, some novel strategies have been started to target PD-1/PD-L1 and attain certain antitumor effects, such as nanoparticles, small molecular inhibitors, epigenetic drugs, peptides, targeted degradation drugs, gene editing technology, tumor vaccines, oncolytic viruses and so on (Fig. 4).Fig. 4Multiple strategies to target PD-1/PD-L1 signaling. Nanoparticle-based drugs

Nanomaterials have demonstrated significant potential in tumor therapy due to their unique physicochemical properties, such as the small size effect, surface effect, and biocompatibility, which are currently being applied primarily in drug delivery, imaging diagnostics, combination therapy and immunomodulation [176]. With the development of nanotechnology, nanoparticles have great potential in tumor treatment by delivering targeted medication into tumor tissue but with less harm to healthy tissues [177]. For example, the dual drug-loaded nanoparticles were constructed to deliver doxorubicin and miR-200c simultaneously, inhibit the expression of PD-L1, trigger the immunogenic cell death (ICD), and activate the anti-tumor immune response [178]. The Boron/siPD-L1 nanoparticles combined with neutron therapy not only promote DNA damage of tumor cells and activation of CD8+ T cells in the primary tumor but also have a strong abscopal effect in distal and metastatic breast tumors [179]. A gelatinase-responsive nanoparticles effectively suppress lung tumor growth by delivering anti-PD-1 antibody and TGF-β inhibitor Galunisertib to activate antitumor immune response [180]. Also, calcium phosphate nanoparticles delivering both anti-PD-L1 antibody and CD73 inhibitor have stronger antitumor efficacy and fewer systemic side effects compared to anti-PD-L1 antibody in melanoma [181].

Small molecular inhibitor

Small-molecule inhibitors with low molecular weights (typically < 1000 Da) are a class of chemically synthesized agents that suppress tumor growth and metastasis by targeting specific molecules, such as kinases, proteins, or signaling pathways [182]. To clarify the regulatory mechanism of the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint molecules in the tumor microenvironment, and to reverse or promote the regulatory mechanism by using small molecular inhibitors [183–199], may be effective strategies to inhibit the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway and activate antitumor immunity. Also, small molecular inhibitors have higher tissue penetration, lower production costs and better oral bioavailability compared to anti-PDL1/PD1 antibodies [200]. INCB086550, as a potent and oral small-molecule PD-L1 inhibitor, could not only promote PD-L1 dimerization and internalization but also increase cytokine production by activating the NFAT pathway and decreasing SHP recruitment to PD-1 to inhibit tumor growth [201]. In a phase 1 clinical study, GS-4224, as a small molecular inhibitor of PD-L1, has potent antitumor effects with good tolerability in patients with advanced solid tumors through promoting PD-L1 dimerization [202]. Furthermore, more and more new small molecular inhibitors of PD-1/PD-L1 are being discovered, such as 5-phenylisoindoline derivatives(PD-L1) [203], Pygenic Acid A(PD-1) [204], compound GJ19(PD-L1) [205], 4-phenyl-1 H-indazole derivatives Z13(PD-L1) [206] and so on.

Epigenetic drug

Epigenetics plays a pivotal role in tumorigenesis, progression, and therapeutic interventions. Aberrant epigenetic modifications, such as DNA methylation, histone modifications, and non-coding RNA regulation, can lead to the silencing of tumor suppressor genes or activation of proto-oncogenes [207]. Consequently, drugs targeting epigenetic regulation have emerged as a major focus in anticancer research [208]. The epigenetic RNA drugs [209–211] were designed to target downregulation of PD-1/PD-L1 or hinder the interaction of PD-1/PD-L1 to activate the antitumor immune response and reverse tumor immune escape. The use of RNAi-mediated downregulation of PD-L1 enhances the antitumor immune response in pancreatic cancer [212]. At present, the PD1/PDL1 RNA drugs are delivered into the tumor microenvironment through personalized drug delivery systems. [213, 214] For example, PEI-modified elastase could deliver siPDL1 into the tumor environment and inhibit intracellular expression of PDL1, which promotes tumor infiltration of CD8+ T cells in primary and metastatic melanoma tumors [215]. Also, siPD1 delivered by attenuated Salmonella could enhance the antitumor effects of EZH2 inhibitors by improving tumor immunogenicity in colorectal cancer [216]. Furthermore, an injectable nanocomposite hydrogel system could deliver miR-214 and miR-130a into tumor cells to decrease the expression of PD-L1 and activate the function of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTL) in breast cancer [217].

Peptide-based inhibitors

Peptide-based antitumor drugs are typically composed of short-chain amino acids (generally 2–50 amino acids) and can be obtained via chemical synthesis or natural extraction. [218] By targeting PD-1/PD-L1, peptides could activate an antitumor immune response. Compared to PD-1/PD-L1 antibodies characterized by poor tissue penetration and high production costs, peptide drugs have the characteristics of high target specificity and low toxicity [219]. For example, KFMFMK, as a self-assembling hexapeptide, could inhibit PD-L1 expression on tumor cells through a ubiquitin-dependent pathway [220]. Also, cell-penetrating anti-PD-L1 peptide nanomedicine (CPPD1) could bind with PD-L1 on tumor cells and interfere with the PD1/PD-L1 interactions [221]. A 16-mer peptide named L7N from ectodomains of PD-L1 also inhibits the interaction of PD-1 and PD-L1 to reinvigorate exhausted T cells in breast cancer [222]. P-F4, as a PD-1 targeting peptide, could disturb PD-1/PD-L1 interaction to effectively increase CD8+ T cells and inhibit colon cancer growth [223]. Through glycoengineering methods, Pan et al. synthesize a PD-1 antibody-iRGD cyclic peptide conjugate (aPD-1-(iRGD)2) which effectively penetrates into tumor tissue and activates tumor-specific T cells in multiple tumors [224]. However, challenges such as stability (susceptibility to protease degradation) and short half-life must be addressed [225]. Currently, no peptide-based PD-1/PD-L1 drugs have been approved for clinical application with most candidates in preclinical or early-stage clinical development.

Fusion protein-based drug

Fusion protein-based anti-PD-1/PD-L1 drugs are a class of biologics produced through genetic engineering techniques to combine functional domains from distinct proteins, characterized by high targeting specificity and diversified mechanisms of action [226]. For example, Nb16-Sia, as a nanobody-enzyme fusion protein, simultaneously targets PD-L1 and sialic acid, resulting in promoting antitumor M1 phenotype polarization and inhibiting tumor growth [226]. A plant-made PD-1-Fc fusion protein effectively blocks PD-1/PD-L1 interaction and inhibits PD-1 signaling [227]. Also, the bispecific fusion protein MB2033 combining IL-2 variant and PD-L1 antibody effectively activates CD8+ T cells but decreases activation of regulatory T cells through higher affinity to PD-L1+ colon cancer cells compared to IL-2 Rβ γ+ Tregs [228]. Importantly, the bifunctional fusion protein targeting TGF-β and PD-L1 named bintrafusp alfa has certain anti-tumor effects in tumor patients with biliary tract cancer [229], NSCLC [230], cervical cancer [231] and urothelial carcinoma [232]. Furthermore, PD-1/PD-L1-related fusion protein drugs could combine with other anti-tumor strategies in clinical trials, such as SHR-1701(bifunctional protein targeting PD-L1 and TGF-β) combined with BP102 (a bevacizumab biosimilar) and XELOX (capecitabine plus oxaliplatin) in metastatic colorectal cancer [233] and SHR-1701 combined with famitinib in biliary tract cancer or pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma [234].

Targeted protein degradation drug

Targeted protein degradation (TPD) is an emerging anti-tumor strategy that treats cancer by selectively degrading disease-causing proteins through the ubiquitin-proteasome system and lysosomal system [235]. Unlike traditional inhibitors, TPD technology not only blocks protein function but also eliminates target proteins, overcoming drug resistance and targeting undruggable proteins [236]. PD-1/PD-L1 targeted degradation drugs designed by degradation systems such as ubiquitin-proteasome system-related proteolysis targeting chimeras (PROTAC) [237], molecular glue [238], lysosomal system-related Lysosome-Targeting Chimaeras (LYTACs) [239, 240], Chaperone-Mediated Autophagy (CMA) [241] and so on.

PROTAC is the classical targeted protein degradation technology with a relatively mature development in PD-1/PD-L1 via E3 ligase [242]. For example, a self-assembled peptide-derived PROTAC nanoparticle could degrade precisely and durably PD-L1 through direct lysosomal PD-L1 degradation and PROTAC-mediated PD-L1 degradation to both membrane and cytoplasmic PD-L1 [237]. Also, as a bispecific nanobody-PROTAC, BumPeD degrades PD-L1 and Survivin with robust antitumor efficacy in bladder cancer [243]. The carbon-dot (CD)-based PROTACs (CDTACs) also degrade PD-L1 on the surface of tumor cells through degradation of PD-L1 in lysosomes and recruit CRBE-mediated degradation of PD-L1 with proteasomes [244]. These PROTAC-based anti-PD1/PD-L1 strategies are becoming a promising antitumor approach in future clinical applications.

Other targeted protein degradation techniques are also gradually applied in PD-1/PD-L1 degradation. Like a molecular glue, a monomer of traditional Chinese medicine (baicalein) promotes CD274–LC3 interaction to facilitate PD-L1 degradation via the autophagic-lysosomal pathway [238]. As a lysosomal degradation-related strategy, a meso chimeric peptide PD-L1 in supramolecular nanospheres named CTAC-PDL1 could bind both PD-L1 and Hsc70, resulting in facilitating internalization of PD-L1 via Hsc70-mediated chaperone-mediated autophagy and further promoting PD-L1 lysosomal degradation in melanoma [245]. Also, a bifunctional LYTAC nanoplatform could degrade both extracellular proteins (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\alpha$$\end{document}-DNP) and membrane proteins (PD-L1) through the endosome-lysosome system to enhance cancer immunotherapy [239]. Furthermore, a prionoid named SAP increases PD-L1 degradation by upregulating micropinocytosis and reprogramming the active CMA to overcome IFNγ-mediated adaptive immune resistance to anti-PD1 in cutaneous melanomas [241]. Also, antibody-peptide conjugated CMA(Ab-CMA) enhances the degradation of PD-L1 through lysosomal degradation pathways [246]. Interestingly, the multivalent targeting chimeras (multi-TACs) synthesized by copolymerization of small-molecule PD-L1 inhibitor BMS-1 with acid-responsive monomers effectively increase PD-L1 degradation in tumor cells by promoting absorption-mediated endocytosis, PD-L1 internalization and lysosomal PD-L1 degradation [247]. Also, similarly to PROTAC, the deglycosylation targeting chimera (DGlyTAC) could remove N-glycans from PD-L1 leading to PD-L1 inactivation on the tumor cell surface with minimal off-target effects [248].

Gene editing technology application

Using CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing technology [249] or genetic engineering methods to design and develop tumor-killing immune cells without PD-1 or immune cells targeting without PD-L1-expressing tumor cells, such as chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T or CAR-NK [250–252], blocks the PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitory signal in the tumor microenvironment and improves the tumor immunosuppressive microenvironment. For example, knockdown of PD-L1 and KRAS by the CRISPR/Cas9 system could inhibit tumor cell proliferation, migration and promote sensitivity to chemotherapy in lung adenocarcinoma [253]. Also, a potent silk fiber-loaded CRISPR/Cas9 system with PD-L1 targeted sgRNA and engineered adenovirus effectively edits the PD-L1 genome to break PD-L1, resulting in inhibiting primary tumor growth and inducing a durable immune response [254]. Furthermore, the HSP70-promoter-driven CRISPR/Cas9 system specifically and permanently disrupts the PD-L1 gene in tumor cells and promotes antitumor immune response [255]. A nanoplatform that delivers PD-L1-targeted CRISPR/Cas9 system and chemotherapeutic drug paclitaxel could effectively inhibit PD-L1 expression in hepatocellular carcinoma cells and activate antitumor T cells in the tumor microenvironment [256].

CAR-T cells or CAR-NK cells are also a promising strategy for targeting PD-1/PD-L1 interaction. In a first-in-human phase I study, a kind of virus-free CAR-T cells termed PD1-19bbz is integrated into the PD1 locus through CRISPR/Cas9 and has a significant and durable antitumor response with a manageable toxicity profile in relapsed/refractory B cell non-Hodgkin’s lymphoma [257]. Also, hematopoietic stem cell-derived CAR-NK cells could target PD-L1+ solid tumor cells and inhibit tumor growth [258]. Interestingly, like CAR-T cells, T cell receptor fusion constructs (TRuC) T cells could potently kill tumor cells with lower cytokine release [259]. Co-expression of PD-1-CD28 fusion receptor in TRuC T cells increases the production of IFN-γ from TRuC T cells and prevents PD-L1-induced T cell hypofunction [260].

Tumor vaccine

Tumor vaccines are designed to deliver tumor-specific antigens to activate the patients’ own T cell-mediated immune response [261]. Tumor vaccines designed using the molecular characteristics of PD-1/PD-L1 pathways can activate immune surveillance and improve the effectiveness of tumor treatment [262, 263]. For example, the PKPD-L1 vaccine, as the extracellular domain of human PD-L1 with aluminum phosphate adjuvant, could increase the infiltration of lymphocytes and decrease the infiltration of CD3+CD8+PD1+high deactivated T cells to inhibit tumor growth in colon cancer [264]. Importantly, the PKPD-L1 vaccine has been applied in mice, rats, rabbits, and non-human primates without behavioral, clinical, hematological, or serum biochemical changes [265]. In a phase I first-in-man study, a dual vaccination of ARG1-derived and PD-L1-derived peptides with Montanide ISA-51 as adjuvant has a potent antitumor immune response by increasing the peripheral CD8/CD4 ratio and decreasing PD-L1 expression in myeloid cells in myeloproliferative neoplasms patients [266].

Oncolytic virus

Oncolytic virus is a class of viruses capable of selectively infecting and killing cancer cells while sparing normal cells [267]. These viruses combat cancer through direct tumor cell lysis and activation of the host immune system, representing one of the pivotal research directions in the field of cancer therapies in recent years [268]. For example, VG161, as an oncolytic herpes simplex virus with expressed IL-12, IL-15, IL-15 Rα and a PD-1/PD-L1-blocking fusion protein, could reshape the tumor immune microenvironment and have promising efficacy as a second-line therapy in tumor patients with liver cancer [269]. Also, a recombinant oncolytic adenovirus with anti-VEGF antibody and shPD-L1 could inhibit PD-L1 expression in tumor cells and reshape tumor microenvironment following downregulation of HIF-1α and CD31 and upregulation of IFN-γ, IL-6 and IL-12 [270]. Furthermore, a recombinant oncolytic influenza virus expressing a PD-L1 antibody could activate the cGAS-STING pathway in CD8+ T cells to inhibit hepatocellular carcinoma cell growth [271]. An oncolytic herpes simplex virus vector expressing PD-1 inhibitors potently suppressed tumor growth by promoting infiltration of effector CD8+ T and memory CD8+ T cells in the tumor microenvironment [272].

Clinical application of targeting PD-1/PD-L1 strategies

Targeting PD-1/PD-L1 has demonstrated remarkable clinical efficacy across a broad spectrum of hematological and solid malignancies, establishing immunotherapy as a cornerstone of modern oncology. The clinical application of these agents continues to expand through combination therapies, biomarker-guided patient selection and investigation in earlier disease stages [273]. While challenges remain including resistance mechanisms and optimal patient selection [274], ongoing research promises to further enhance the efficacy and accessibility of these transformative therapies.

Among the various approaches, antibody-dependent PD-1/PD-L1 targeting strategies currently represent the majority of clinical trials. Characteristics of responders and non-responders in targeting PD-1/PD-L1 strategies

PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors have revolutionized the treatment landscape for multiple malignancies. However, only a subset of patients achieves long-term benefit from this therapy, while the majority exhibit primary or acquired resistance. Understanding of the heterogeneous characteristics distinguishing responders from non-responders is crucial for enhancing therapeutic efficacy and achieving individualized, precision immunotherapy. Responders and non-responders to PD-1/PD-L1 targeted therapy exhibit multi-dimensional biological differences, including clinicopathological features, molecular biomarkers, the tumor microenvironment and host factors.

Characteristics of targeting PD-1/PD-L1 responders are associated with tumor-intrinsic features, tumor microenvironment features and peripheral blood biomarkers. The tumor-intrinsic features include high PD-L1 expression [286], high tumor mutational burden (TMB) [287] and microsatellite instability-high (MSI-H) [288]. Also, the changes of tumor microenvironment features include CD8+ T cell infiltration [289], mature tertiary lymphoid structures (TLS) [290] and favorable immune phenotype [291]. Finally, peripheral blood biomarkers include neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio [292], absolute lymphocyte count [293] and early ctDNA dynamics [294]. In contrast, non-responders are characterized by primary resistance mechanisms, immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and acquired resistance mechanisms. The primary resistance mechanisms are associated with T-cell-excluded tumor microenvironment [295], IFN-γ signaling pathway defects [296] and antigen presentation defects [297]. The immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment includes infiltrating suppressive immune cells and soluble immunosuppressive factors, such as myeloid-derived suppressor cells (MDSCs) [298], regulatory T cells (Tregs) [299], soluble PD-L1 [300], TGF-β and VEGF [301]. In addition, acquired resistance mechanisms are related to upregulation of alternative immune checkpoints such as TIM-3, LAG-3 and TIGIT [302].

Therefore, future research should focus on developing predictive models that integrate multi-omics data, exploring novel strategies to induce TLS formation and reverse T cell exhaustion, understanding the dynamic regulatory role of epigenetics in treatment resistance and establishing biomarker-based adaptive clinical trial designs. By deeply dissecting the heterogeneity of treatment responses, we can advance towards the precision application of targeting PD-1/PD-L1 immunotherapy, thereby maximizing patient benefit from targeting PD-1/PD-L1 strategies.

Immune-related adverse events (irAes) of targeting PD-1/PD-L1 strategies

Although PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors have become a crucial therapeutic approach for various malignancies, the non-specific activation of the immune system not only kills tumor cells but may also attack normal tissues, leading to irAEs and new challenges for clinical management [303]. irAEs can affect multiple organ systems, including the skin, gastrointestinal tract, liver, endocrine system, lungs, nervous system and cardiovascular system [304]. The pathophysiology of immune-related adverse events (irAEs) has not been fully elucidated. Current understanding suggests it involves multiple mechanisms, including T cell priming and tissue infiltration, autoantibody production, cytokine storm and gut microbiota regulation. For example, T cells recognizing tumor antigens may cross-react with antigens expressed on normal tissues and activated CD8+ T cells secrete cytokines such as IL-6, recruiting additional inflammatory cells and exacerbating tissue damage [305]. Furthermore, studies have found that autoantibodies are frequently detectable in the serum of patients who develop irAEs, indicating that B cell-mediated humoral immunity also contributes to irAE pathogenesis [306]. Some severe irAEs, such as cytokine release syndrome, are associated with a rapid and significant increase in levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6, TNF-α, and IFN-γ. IL-17 plays a crucial role in the development of immune-mediated pneumonitis [307]. The composition of the gut microbiota is closely associated with the occurrence of irAEs. Alterations in the abundance of specific microbial taxa, such as the Bacteroides genus, can influence intestinal immune homeostasis and modulate both the efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) and susceptibility to colitis [308]. Therefore, a deeper understanding of this mechanism will help optimize treatment strategies. Future research should focus on the discovery of predictive biomarkers, elucidation of the underlying pathophysiology and development of novel management strategies to achieve precision and individualization in immunotherapy.

Discussion

Strategies to target the PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoint have revolutionized the treatment landscape for multiple cancer types, offering the promise of long-term survival to numerous patients. This review systematically explores diverse strategies designed to effectively intervene in the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway within the tumor microenvironment.

Firstly, we discuss the molecular regulatory mechanisms of PD-1/PD-L1 immune checkpoints in the tumor microenvironment, including the influence of genomics and epigenetics, transcription factors, classical signaling pathways, non-coding RNA, post-translational modification of PD-L1, and mRNA stability on PD-1/PD-L1 signaling pathway. These studies are conducive to the development of tumor immune drugs targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 regulatory mechanisms to block immune checkpoint signaling, activate antitumor immune responses, and overcome therapeutic resistance. Secondly, we systematically summarize novel antibody-dependent therapeutic strategies to target PD1/PD-L1 that extend beyond traditional immune checkpoint blockade, including monoclonal antibodies, bispecific antibodies, antibody-conjugated drugs and immune-stimulating antibody conjugates. Through sophisticated engineering design, these modalities aim to target the PD-1/PD-L1 signaling axis with greater precision and efficacy. Antibody-dependent therapies targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway have brought about a profound revolution in cancer treatment. Although numerous challenges remain in clinical application, continuous research advancements and technological progress will undoubtedly enable these therapies to play an increasingly important role in future cancer therapies. Thirdly, we review the novel methods of targeted inhibition of the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway, including nanoparticle-based drugs, small molecule inhibitors, epigenetic drugs, PROTAC degradation drugs, peptides, gene-edited cell drugs, tumor vaccines, oncolytic viruses, etc. The development of effective strategies to promote tumor immunotherapy research to develop and design more effective, better specific, and less toxic PD-1/PD-L1 blocking drugs to improve the therapeutic effect of tumors. Finally, we have summarized the clinical application of PD-1/PD-L1 targeting strategies, including clinical trials, the characteristics of treatment responders and non-responders, as well as therapy-related immune adverse events.

In this review, we comprehensively explore various strategies targeting the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway in the tumor microenvironment, including the application of immune checkpoint blockers and the development of novel targeted agents. These strategies have significantly advanced the field of cancer therapy and provided survival benefits for numerous patients. However, several challenges remain to be addressed, such as variable efficacy due to individual patient differences, the management of immune-related adverse events, and the complexity of treatment resistance mechanisms. Therefore, future research should try to further accurately predict patient responses to PD-1/PD-L1 inhibitors to avoid unnecessary treatments and resource waste, effectively overcome resistance to immunotherapy to prolong treatment response duration, and better balance the efficacy and safety of immunotherapeutic interventions. In the future, with a deeper understanding of the tumor microenvironment and the PD-1/PD-L1 pathway coupled with advances in genetic testing technologies, bioinformatic analyses, and the utilization of large-scale clinical data, more personalized and precise treatment plans are anticipated to be developed. Furthermore, the continuous emergence of novel targeting PD1/PD-L1 immunotherapeutic agents and their synergistic effects with other treatment modalities are expected to provide more effective, durable, and safer treatment options for cancer patients.
