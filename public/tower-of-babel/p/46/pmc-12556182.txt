Protocol for predicting γ-tocotrienol and δ-tocotrienol binding to colorectal cancer-related proteins using Schrödinger’s Maestro and Glide
Authors: Ali Qusay Khalid, Saatheeyavaane Bhuvanendran, Kasthuri Bai Magalingam, Premdass Ramdas, Yun Khoon Liew, Ammu Kutty Radhakrishnan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe present a protocol for predicting γ-tocotrienol (γT3) and δ-tocotrienol (δT3) binding to colorectal cancer-related proteins using Schrödinger’s Maestro and Glide. The protocol describes protein preparation, ligand processing, and receptor grid generation, followed by docking simulations. It outputs binding affinities (kcal/mol) and interaction maps, with δT3 expected to bind slightly more strongly. This workflow includes checkpoints and troubleshooting, providing a reproducible framework suitable for users with intermediate to advanced molecular modeling experience.

Before you begin

This protocol details the use of Schrödinger’s Maestro and Glide software for protein-ligand docking, specifically focusing on γ-tocotrienol (γT3) and δ-tocotrienol (δT3) against colorectal cancer (CRC) targets BRAF, EGFR, JNK1, KRAS, and P38. For readers new to molecular modeling, we recommend familiarizing yourself with fundamental concepts in protein structure and ligand interactions before beginning this protocol. These include the hierarchy of protein structure (from primary to quaternary levels), key non-covalent interactions such as hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, and electrostatic interactions, the principles of molecular mechanics and force fields, and a basic understanding of how binding sites are recognized. The steps below outline the necessary software installation, hardware checks, and data acquisition required before starting the docking procedure.

Innovation

This protocol advances molecular docking workflows by adapting established Schrödinger Maestro and Glide methods to systematically evaluate phytochemicals against CRC–related targets. Unlike general docking tutorials, the workflow emphasizes reproducibility, comparability, and biological context.

First, the protocol introduces a rational protein selection strategy, focusing on five signaling proteins (BRAF, EGFR, KRAS, JNK1, and P38) with established roles in CRC progression. This disease-specific orientation ensures that docking results are biologically relevant rather than generic.

Second, ligand preparation is standardized to minimize variability between γT3 and δT3. Protonation, tautomerization, and stereochemistry are harmonized at physiological pH, enabling direct comparison of binding affinities across multiple receptors.

Third, receptor grids are generated with attention to conserved binding sites and allow flexibility in side chains or cofactors, ensuring accurate representation of the docking environment. The workflow provides decision points that let users pause after ligand or grid preparation, which reduces rerun time and improves usability.

Finally, docking results combine numerical scoring with interaction maps, creating outputs that are interpretable for both computational and experimental researchers. Clear troubleshooting links common errors to specific steps, enabling intermediate users to gain confidence while providing sufficient depth for advanced users to extend the protocol.

By merging disease-specific protein targeting with reproducible docking practices, this protocol offers an efficient and accessible platform for investigating phytochemicals interactions and supports broader applications in natural product–based drug discovery.

Background

Colorectal cancer (CRC) is one of the most prevalent malignancies worldwide, with high rates of incidence and mortality despite advances in early detection and treatment strategies.1 A key challenge in the treatment of CRC is the development of resistance to conventional chemotherapeutic agents, necessitating the discovery of novel therapeutic approaches.2,3 Targeted therapies have emerged as a promising avenue for drug development, focusing on molecular targets critical to cancer progression. Among these molecular targets, proteins such as BRAF,4 EGFR,5 JNK1,6 KRAS,5,6,7 and P388 have been extensively studied for their roles in the pathogenesis and progression of CRC. These proteins are involved in vital signalling pathways, including the MAPK/ERK and PI3K/AKT pathways, which regulate cell proliferation, survival, and apoptosis.9 Targeting these proteins through ligand-based inhibitors has become a focal point of drug discovery efforts in CRC and other cancers.

One of the key tools in modern drug discovery is protein-ligand docking, a computational method that simulates the interaction between a small-molecule ligand and a target protein. This method allows for the prediction of the binding affinity and orientation of the ligand within the protein’s active site, providing insight into potential therapeutic effects and guiding the design of new drugs. Protein-ligand docking is particularly useful in the early stages of drug development, enabling researchers to prioritize compounds for further experimental validation. With advances in computational power and software tools, docking simulations have become a cornerstone of structure-based drug design (SBDD), offering a cost-effective and efficient alternative to traditional drug screening methods.

Schrödinger’s Maestro and Glide software are among the leading platforms used in computational drug discovery for performing high-precision docking studies. Maestro provides a comprehensive suite for molecular modeling, while Glide excels in predicting accurate ligand binding poses and scoring them based on their interaction with the protein target. Together, these tools enable the optimization of protein structures, preparation of ligands, generation of receptor grids, and execution of docking simulations, streamlining the workflow for virtual screening and lead optimization. By employing crystal structures of experimentally validated protein targets, docking simulations can provide detailed insights into the molecular mechanisms of ligand binding, which is essential for rational drug design.

In the context of natural products with potential therapeutic effects, tocotrienols (T3s) have garnered significant interest due to their antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and anticancer properties.10 T3s, a subfamily of vitamin E, consist of four isomers: alpha (α), beta (β), gamma (γ), and delta (δ). Among these, γT3 and δT3 have demonstrated potent anticancer activity, particularly in the inhibition of cancer cell proliferation and induction of apoptosis.11 These effects are thought to be mediated through their interaction with key signalling proteins involved in cancer progression.12 However, the precise molecular mechanisms underlying the anticancer effects of T3s, particularly their interactions with specific protein targets in CRC, remain to be fully elucidated.

In silico docking studies provide an ideal platform to explore the potential molecular interactions between T3s and key protein targets implicated in CRC. By simulating the binding of γT3 and δT3 to proteins such as BRAF, EGFR, JNK1, KRAS, and P38, it is possible to predict their binding affinity and identify the key interactions responsible for their anticancer effects. This approach not only supports the rational design of T3-based therapeutic agents but also paves the way for the discovery of novel small-molecule inhibitors with enhanced specificity and efficacy.

Note 1: Structure prediction using AlphaFold and SWISS-MODEL

4.Using AlphaFold Models: AlphaFold represents a transformative advance in protein structure prediction, offering high-accuracy models generated through deep learning without relying on homologous templates. It is particularly valuable when no experimental structures are available. Pre-computed models can be accessed via the AlphaFold Protein Structure Database, or custom predictions can be generated using tools like ColabFold. The resulting models come with per-residue confidence scores (pLDDT), which guide users in assessing structural reliability, especially in binding site regions. While AlphaFold predictions are highly informative, they typically lack ligands or cofactors and represent the unbound state of the protein, necessitating careful validation and potential refinement before docking. When using AlphaFold models for structure-based drug design, validate predictions where possible by comparing binding site residues with experimental data (mutagenesis, SAR) or by cross-referencing with homologous proteins of known structure.5.Using SWISS-MODEL for Homology Modeling: SWISS-MODEL is a widely used web-based platform that generates protein structures through homology modeling, relying on existing high-quality structural templates from the PDB. Users input the protein sequence, and the tool automatically identifies suitable templates and builds 3D models. The output includes quality metrics such as GMQE and QMEAN, helping users evaluate model reliability. Although the accuracy of SWISS-MODEL depends on the availability and similarity of templates, it is particularly useful for modeling conserved domains or for proteins with known homologs. Before docking, models should be imported into Maestro, processed using the Protein Preparation Wizard, and validated, especially near binding sites that were modeled or inferred. Always validate SWISS-MODEL predictions against available biochemical or structural data. If multiple templates are available, cross-check binding site geometry to select the most suitable model for docking purposes.

Step-by-step method details

Protein structure preparation

Timing: 60–90 min

Timing: 60–90 min

Protein preparation is a critical step for reliable protein–ligand docking. The accuracy of predicted binding modes and affinities depends strongly on the quality of the input structure. This section describes how to prepare proteins in Maestro using the Protein Preparation Wizard, including optimization of bond orders, hydrogens, and protonation states; treatment of cofactors and water molecules; and identification of active sites.13,14 The steps below outline a systematic workflow to generate refined protein models suitable for docking studies.1.Obtain and Assess Protein Structure: Acquire the high-resolution crystal structure (ideally < 2.5 Å resolution) of the target protein from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). Assess its quality (completeness, R-factors). If necessary, use homology modeling (e.g., via Prime) but validate the model carefully.2.Import into Maestro: Launch the Maestro application and import the PDB file (File > Import Structure).3.Run Protein Preparation Wizard: Access the tool (Tasks > Protein Preparation Wizard or type prepwizard in the task tool search).a.Pre-process Structure:i.Assign Bond Orders, Add Hydrogens: The wizard usually does this automatically. Review the structure visually for correctness, especially around ligands, cofactors, or modified residues.ii.Select Chains/Assemblies: Ensure the biologically relevant unit is selected. Delete alternate conformations, unnecessary chains, or ligands not part of the binding site of interest.iii.Handle Water Molecules: Launch the Protein Preparation Wizard. Select the option “Delete waters far from ligand/residues.” Apply a distance cutoff of 3–5 Å, adjusting based on the binding site environment.CRITICAL: Retaining or deleting crystallographic waters is a key decision that directly affects docking accuracy.CRITICAL: Water molecules within 3 Å of the ligand or binding site residues often participate in direct hydrogen bonds (optimal distance 2.7–3.1 Å).15,16 Waters between 3–5 Å may act as bridging molecules, mediating ligand–protein interactions and stabilizing binding affinity.17 Waters beyond 5 Å are generally considered structurally irrelevant for most docking protocols.18EVIDENCE: Structural analyses show that conserved waters with B-factors below the average and within 3.5 Å of protein atoms are functionally important in >85% of cases.19Optional: For deeply buried binding pockets, use a lenient cutoff (up to 5 Å); for solvent-exposed sites, a stricter cutoff (∼3 Å) may suffice.Pause point: When uncertain, compare multiple crystal structures of the same protein to identify conserved waters that recur across structures, as these often play essential roles.iv.Handle Cofactors/Ions: Ensure correct parameters and bond orders for cofactors (e.g., HEME, NAD) and ions (e.g., Zn, Mg). The wizard often handles common ones, but review is crucial.v.Command-line Alternative: For reproducibility and batch processing, the Protein Preparation Wizard can be executed via command line using Schrödinger’s prepwizard utility. Below is a detailed breakdown of the “basic preprocessing” steps and their corresponding command-line parameters:CRITICAL: For reproducibility, always document the exact command used, including all parameters and their values. The log file contains important information about decisions made during preparation (e.g., added hydrogens, flipped residues) that should be preserved for method documentation (troubleshooting 1).b.Review and Modify: Use the wizard’s interface with Prime (Use Prime to fill missing side chains, Use Prime to fill missing loops) if gaps exist in the structure, especially near the binding site. Missing regions are modeled using the full amino acid sequence when available. This step can be computationally intensive (See Step 5: Handling Poor Quality Structures).Basic Syntax:```bash$SCHRODINGER/utilities/prepwizard [options] input_structure.pdb output_structure.maeCommon Options:-fillsidechains, -fillloops, -disulfides: Add missing elements-propka_pH 7.0, -epik_pH 7.0, -epik_pH_range 2.0: Protonation settings-rehtreat: Reorient Asn, Gln, His-watdist 3.0: Remove distant waters-delwater_hbond_cutoff 3: Remove waters with <3 H-bonds-noepik_hetgrp: Skip Epik for ligands-fix, -rmsd 0.3, -sidechain_only: Structure fixing/minimization-force_field OPLS4, -j N: Specify force field / CPU usageExample (with minimization):```bashprepwizard -fillsidechains -fillloops -disulfides -propka_pH 7.0 -epik_pH 7.0 -epik_pH_range 2.0 -rehtreat -watdist 3.0 -minimize -rmsd 0.3 -force_field OPLS4 -j 4 input.pdb output.maeLog Output:```bash > preparation_log.txt 2>&1c.Optimize H-bond Assignment:i.Generate Protonation States: Use Epik (Generate states at target pH) at the desired physiological pH (e.g., 7.4 ± 0.5). Consider pH sensitivity if relevant.14ii.Protonation State Considerations: Pay close attention to Histidine (His) tautomers (HID, HIE, HIP). The wizard samples states, but manual inspection and selection based on the local H-bonding environment is often necessary. Also consider Cysteine (Cys) oxidation states (thiol vs. disulfide) and Asp/Glu protonation if the pH is significantly different from neutral or if the local environment suggests altered pKa.iii.Optimize H-bond Network: Run the “Optimize H-bond assignment” function in Maestro. Manually flip terminal Asn/Gln amides or His rings if the automated assignment contradicts known interactions or the local structural context.CRITICAL: Inspect and adjust salt bridges carefully. Note 3: Five key principles for selecting effective docking criteria

The selection of proteins as potential drug targets is a critical aspect of drug discovery. The following points detail the criteria for protein selection, supported by scientific evidence.9.X-ray Crystallography Structure [www.rcsb.org]: X-ray crystallography and high-quality cryo-EM structures (especially those reaching ∼2.5–2.6 Å resolution) offer detailed 3D information on protein conformation and binding pockets. These structural insights are crucial for guiding docking accuracy and ligand design. Cryo-EM can also provide information on biologically relevant assemblies and water molecules in the active site, which are increasingly critical in modern docking workflows.10.Co-crystallized Ligand [www.rcsb.org]: The presence of a ligand, introduced either through co-crystallization or soaking, demonstrates the binding capability of the target protein. This provides direct evidence of ligand interaction, binding site geometry, and possible conformational adaptations, all of which are essential for validating docking protocols and for lead optimization.11.Resolution < 2 Å [www.rcsb.org]: A resolution of less than 2 angstroms (preferably) in X-ray crystallography is indicative of high-quality data that provides detailed atomic positions within the protein structure. High-resolution structures enable precise modeling of interactions between the protein and potential drug candidates. At this level of detail, researchers can accurately identify hydrogen bonds, van der Waals interactions, and other subtle nuances that influence binding affinity and specificity. Such precision is essential for minimizing off-target effects and optimizing pharmacological properties.12.Ramachandran Plot Statistics [www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/databases/pdbsum]: The Ramachandran plot is a graphical representation that illustrates the dihedral angles (phi and psi) of amino acid residues in a protein structure. An ideal Ramachandran plot will show that no residues occupy disallowed regions, which indicates a high-quality model with minimal steric clashes or unusual conformations (Figure 1). Proteins with residues in disallowed regions may exhibit structural instability or misfolding, leading to reduced functionality as drug targets. Therefore, ensuring that all residues fall within allowed regions enhances confidence in the structural integrity and biological relevance of the target protein.Figure 1Ramachandran plot of deoxy hemoglobin (PDB: 2DHB) as an exampleThis plot shows the distribution of φ (phi) and ψ (psi) angles for non-glycine, non-proline residues in the deoxy hemoglobin structure. The majority of residues (75.4%) are in the most favored regions, with 23.8% in additional allowed regions, and 0.4% in generously allowed regions. Leu 109 is highlighted as a residue that falls within the disallowed regions with 0.4%. This suggests that Leu 109 adopts an unusual or strained conformation, which may be due to local structural constraints, crystallographic artifacts, or functional flexibility. While such conformations can occasionally be justified by specific protein functions, residues in disallowed regions like Leu 109 should generally be approached with caution when interpreting structural data, as they might indicate inaccuracies in the model.13.Intact Protein [www.ebi.ac.uk/thornton-srv/databases/pdbsum]: A complete protein structure without breaks or fragments is essential for accurate functional analysis and drug design (Figure 2). Fragmented proteins may lack critical domains necessary for interaction with ligands or may exhibit altered conformational states that do not reflect their native function. Continuous sequences allow for better predictions regarding folding patterns and functional sites, which are crucial for effective binding interactions with therapeutic agents. Moreover, intact proteins are more likely to retain their biological activity and provide reliable targets for drug development.Figure 2Discontinuity in the SERCA structure (PDB: 4H1W) as an exampleThe structural representation reveals a notable discontinuity between residues 75 and 86 in the sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium ATPase (SERCA) complexed with sarcolipin, indicating a region of inherent flexibility that is often challenging to resolve during X-ray crystallography, suggesting that this segment may correspond to a dynamic loop critical for the conformational transitions necessary for calcium ion transport, particularly during the E1 to E2 state transitions, and highlighting the need for computational modeling approaches to predict the conformation of these missing residues and enhance our understanding of SERCA’s regulatory mechanisms in calcium homeostasis.14.Protein Side Chain Minimization: Side chain minimization is a crucial step in protein preparation that optimizes the positions of amino acid side chains while maintaining the overall protein backbone structure. This process:a.Purpose: Relieves steric clashes, optimizes hydrogen bonding networks, and improves the overall energetics of the protein structure without dramatically altering its conformation.b.Methodology in Maestro:i.After completing the preprocessing and H-bond optimization steps, proceed to the “Minimize” tab in the Protein Preparation Wizard.ii.Select “Minimize hydrogens of selected residues” to optimize only hydrogen positions, or “Optimize” to include heavy atoms in the minimization.iii.Choose “Minimize side chains only” to keep the backbone fixed (recommended for high-quality crystal structures).iv.Set RMSD convergence criteria to 0.30 Å to allow sufficient relaxation while preserving the experimental structure.c.Force Field Selection:i.Use OPLS4 for most applications as it provides improved accuracy for protein-ligand interactions.ii.The minimization employs a gradient-based approach that iteratively adjusts atomic positions to reduce the system’s potential energy.d.Constraints Application:i.Apply harmonic constraints to non-hydrogen atoms (typically 5.0 kcal/mol/Å2) to prevent large deviations from the initial structure.ii.Gradually decrease constraint strength in subsequent minimization cycles if needed for highly strained regions.e.Validation:i.After minimization, inspect Ramachandran plots to ensure side chain adjustments haven’t introduced unfavorable backbone conformations.ii.Check for improved hydrogen bonding patterns and reduced steric clashes using the “Interactions” panel.iii.Verify that binding site residues maintain biologically relevant orientations, especially those known to interact with ligands.CRITICAL: For structures with co-crystallized ligands, perform minimization with the ligand present to maintain the integrity of the binding site. If the ligand must be removed before minimization, consider applying stronger positional constraints to binding site residues.15.Identifying the Biologically Relevant Assembly: Many proteins function as multimers or as part of larger complexes, making the identification of the biologically relevant assembly crucial for accurate docking studies:a.Understanding PDB File Content:i.PDB files often Note 4: Utilizing PyMOL for protein structure repair and optimization

PyMOL is a molecular visualization system built on an open-source platform, supported by users and maintained and distributed by Schrödinger. It is a powerful tool for identifying and fixing breaks in protein structures. To identify breaks, you can load your PDB file and visually inspect the structure using various representations like cartoon or sticks, looking for gaps or missing residues. Analyzing disulfide bonds can also highlight structural issues. For fixing breaks, the Builder Tool allows you to add missing residues by selecting the appropriate amino acids, while ModLoop can refine these regions for better accuracy. If specific residues need modification, the Mutagenesis Wizard enables you to change amino acids and visualize the effects on the structure. In the context of post-docking analysis, the terms Docking Score (kcal/mol) and Glide Emodel (kcal/mol) are essential for evaluating the binding affinities of ligands to protein targets. The Docking Score and Glide Emodel are pivotal in post-docking analysis, serving multiple critical functions. Firstly, they facilitate result evaluation by enabling the systematic ranking of ligands based on their predicted binding affinities, thereby identifying the most promising candidates for subsequent experimental investigation. Secondly, these metrics are integral to rescoring and refinement, as they can be employed to enhance docking poses through sophisticated methodologies, such as Induced Fit Docking or MM-GBSA calculations, which significantly improve the accuracy of binding predictions. Lastly, they play a vital role in the validation of computational results; by juxtaposing these scores with empirical data, researchers can ascertain the reliability of their docking simulations and ensure alignment with experimentally derived binding interactions.

While docking scores vary considerably between different protein targets,34,35 we can provide general guidelines for interpreting Glide docking scores based on extensive experience and literature precedent:

The general threshold guidelines for Glide SP Scores are (a) < −9.0 kcal/mol: excellent binding potential, typically observed for high-affinity ligands (nM range or better); (b) −7.0 to −9.0 kcal/mol: good binding potential, often seen with moderate to high-affinity ligands (nM to low μM); (c) −5.0 to −7.0 kcal/mol: moderate binding potential, common for lower-affinity compounds (μM range) and (d) −5.0 kcal/mol: weak binding potential, often indicating μM to mM range or nonspecific interactions.

The Glide XP Scores can be interpreted as (a) < −10.0 kcal/mol: excellent binding potential; (b) −8.0 to −10.0 kcal/mol: good binding potential; (c) −6.0 to −8.0 kcal/mol: moderate binding potential; or (d) > −6.0 kcal/mol: weak binding potential.

The Target-Specific Calibration is highly recommended. This calibration method requires (a) docking a set of known active compounds with experimental binding data, followed by (b) plotting of docking scores against experimental pIC50 or pKi values. This step is used to (c) determine target-specific thresholds based on this calibration. Finally, (d) the thresholds are used to interpret scores for novel compounds.

The scores can be contextualized using a few methods such as (a) Relative Scoring: compare scores of test compounds to known actives rather than relying solely on absolute values; (b) Enrichment Analysis: calculate enrichment factors to assess how well docking discriminates actives from decoys; (c) Consensus Scoring: consider using multiple scoring functions and looking for consensus predictions; or (d) Score Normalization: normalize scores by ligand size (e.g., score per heavy atom) to account for molecular weight bias.

There are also some additional Quality Metrics Beyond Scores that can be used, which include (a) Ligand Strain Energy: high strain (>3 kcal/mol) suggests unrealistic binding poses; (b) Hydrogen Bond Count: more H-bonds generally indicate more specific binding; (c) Buried Surface Area: greater buried surface area often correlates with stronger binding; or (d) Interaction with Key Residues: engagement with evolutionarily conserved or known functional residues increases confidence (NOTE 5).

Some of the Common Pitfalls in Score Interpretation include (a) Molecular Weight Bias: larger molecules often score better regardless of actual affinity; (b) Charge Effects: highly charged molecules may receive artificially favorable scores; (c) Hydrophobic Interactions: some scoring functions overvalue hydrophobic contacts (Note 5); and (d) Entropy Effects: most scoring functions inadequately account for entropic penalties.CRITICAL: Docking scores should be considered as relative rather than absolute predictors of binding affinity. The most reliable approach is to establish target-specific score thresholds using compounds with known experimental binding data whenever possible.

Note 5: Understanding protein-ligand interactions

The following points detail the criteria for understanding protein-ligand interactions, focusing on binding energy, binding interactions, and binding orientation, all supported by scientific evidence. These aspects are crucial for elucidating the mechanisms of molecular recognition and the design of effective therapeutic agents.

Binding Energy: Binding energy refers to the free energy change that occurs when a ligand binds to its target protein. It is a critical parameter in understanding the affinity and stability of the protein-ligand complex. The binding free energy (ΔG) can be expressed as:ΔG=ΔH−TΔSWhere ΔH is the change in enthalpy, T is the temperature, and ΔS is the change in entropy. A more negative ΔG indicates a stronger binding affinity. Experimental studies have shown that binding energies are influenced by various factors including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and van der Waals forces. For instance, protein-ligand complexes often exhibit a trade-off between enthalpy and entropy, known as enthalpy-entropy compensation. In many cases, a favorable binding interaction (negative ΔH) may result in a decrease in entropy (negative ΔS) due to the restriction of molecular motion upon binding. Moreover, computational methods such as molecular dynamics simulations and quantum mechanical approaches have been employed to predict binding energies with high accuracy. These methods allow for a detailed analysis of the energetic landscape during ligand binding, revealing the contributions of different interaction types to the overall binding energy.

Binding Interaction: Binding interactions encompass the various non-covalent forces that facilitate the formation of a stable protein-ligand complex. These interactions include hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions, hydrophobic effects, and π-π stacking. The nature and strength of these interactions are crucial for determining the specificity and affinity of ligand binding. The initial phase of protein-ligand association is characterized by diffusional encounters that lead to the formation of an encounter complex. This phase is predominantly driven by electrostatic interactions and thermal motion. As the ligand approaches the binding site, short-range forces become significant, leading to more complex interactions that stabilize the bound state. Different models have been proposed to explain these interactions, such as the lock-and-key model and the induced fit model. The lock-and-key model suggests that both protein and ligand are rigid, fitting together perfectly. In contrast, the induced fit model posits that the binding site is flexible and undergoes conformational changes upon ligand binding, enhancing interaction specificity. Understanding these binding interactions is essential for drug design, as optimizing these non-covalent interactions can lead to improved ligand efficacy and selectivity.

Binding Orientation: Binding orientation refers to the specific spatial arrangement of a ligand within its binding site on a protein. The orientation can significantly influence both the strength of binding and the biological activity of the ligand. The orientation is determined by several factors including steric compatibility, electrostatic complementarity, and hydrophobic interactions between the ligand and protein. High-resolution structural data obtained through techniques such as X-ray crystallography or NMR spectroscopy can provide insights into preferred binding orientations. Computational methods like molecular docking simulate various potential orientations to predict how well a ligand fits into its target site. These simulations assess not only geometric compatibility but also energetic favorability based on interaction scores derived from force fields or scoring functions. The importance of optimal binding orientation cannot be overstated; even slight deviations can lead to significant differences in biological activity or efficacy. For example, certain orientations may facilitate essential interactions with key amino acid residues at the active site, while others may hinder them. Thus, understanding and optimizing binding orientation is crucial in drug design and development processes.

Alternative approaches and methodological variations

While this protocol details a standard Glide docking workflow, several variations and alternative methods can be employed depending on the specific research question and system complexity.

Induced Fit Docking (IFD): Use IFD when significant receptor conformational changes upon ligand binding are expected. IFD combines Glide docking with Prime refinement of the receptor side chains within a specified radius around the ligand pose. It is more computationally expensive than standard Glide. Workflow: Access via Tasks > Induced Fit Docking. Requires prepared receptor, grid file, and ligands. Involves initial Glide docking, Prime refinement of receptor/ligand complex, and final Glide re-docking/scoring.

Covalent Docking: For ligands that form covalent bonds with the receptor. Requires defining the reactive atoms on both the ligand and receptor and specifying the reaction type. Workflow: Access via Tasks > Covalent Docking. Requires specific setup defining the covalent reaction mechanism.

Fragment-Based Docking: Glide can be used for docking small fragments. Settings may need adjustment (e.g., smaller grid, different scoring considerations). Often coupled with fragment linking or growing strategies.

Ensemble Docking: Docking against multiple receptor conformations (e.g., from MD simulations or different crystal structures) to account for receptor flexibility. Requires generating separate grids for each conformation and combining/analyzing results.

MM-GBSA Rescoring: Use Molecular Mechanics with Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) calculations to rescore top docking poses. MM-GBSA provides a more rigorous (though still approximate) estimation of binding free energy by incorporating solvation and entropic terms, potentially improving ranking accuracy compared to docking scores alone. Workflow: Typically run on the output poses from Glide using the Prime MM-GBSA task.

Quantum Mechanics (QM) Calculations: For very high accuracy or systems where standard force fields struggle (e.g., metal interactions, charge transfer), QM calculations (e.g., using Jaguar) can be used to refine interactions or derive charges, although this is computationally very demanding.

Practical applications and result interpretation

This protocol, applied to γT3 and δT3 against CRC targets, provides insights into their potential mechanisms of action. The interpretation of results requires careful consideration of scores, interactions, and context.

Interpreting Docking Scores: Docking scores (GlideScore, Emodel) provide a relative ranking of predicted binding affinity. Lower (more negative) scores suggest stronger binding. However, absolute score values do not directly translate to experimental affinities (Ki/Kd). Differences of 1-2 kcal/mol are generally considered significant, but this varies. Compare scores to known positive and negative controls docked under identical conditions.

Analyzing Interactions: Visual inspection of binding poses and interactions (H-bonds, hydrophobic, pi-stacking) using the Ligand Interaction Diagram is crucial. Does the pose make chemical sense? Does it interact with key catalytic or binding site residues? Are there unfavorable interactions (clashes)?

Context with Experimental Data: Relate docking predictions to available experimental data. Do the predicted affinities correlate with measured activities (e.g., IC50s)? Does the predicted binding mode explain known SAR? Discrepancies may highlight limitations of the model or suggest alternative binding modes.

Negative Controls: Docking known inactive compounds or decoys is important for validation. The protocol should ideally show significantly worse scores for inactive compounds compared to active ones.

Follow-up Experimental Validation: Docking generates hypotheses. Experimental validation is essential. Recommendations include (a) Binding Assays: measure binding affinity directly (e.g., SPR, ITC, FP, MST); (b) Functional Assays: test inhibition in relevant cellular or biochemical assays; (c) Structural Biology: obtain co-crystal structures (X-ray crystallography) or NMR data to confirm the predicted binding mode; or (d) Site-Directed Mutagenesis: mutate key interacting residues identified in docking and assess the impact on binding/activity.

Limitations

Protein–ligand docking provides valuable predictive insights but carries inherent limitations that must be considered when interpreting results. Docking scores are approximations of binding affinity and may not perfectly correlate with experimental values (e.g., Ki, Kd, IC50) due to simplifications in scoring functions and force field inaccuracies. Standard docking often treats the receptor as rigid or partially flexible, limiting its ability to capture induced conformational changes, while input quality, including protein resolution and ligand preparation (e.g., protonation states, tautomers), strongly influences accuracy. In addition, solvation effects are approximated, configurational entropy is poorly accounted for, and docking predicts static binding poses rather than dynamic behavior or binding kinetics. Pharmacokinetic properties (ADME/Tox) also lie beyond the scope of docking. Therefore, computational predictions should always be interpreted with caution and complemented by experimental validation to confirm binding modes and biological activity.
