Effects of global health initiatives on the quality of care for maternal health services in sub-Saharan Africa: a scoping review report
Authors: Irene Atuhairwe, Lydia Atuhaire, Constance S Shumba, Peter Nyasulu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionGlobal health initiatives (GHIs) have played a significant role in expanding access to healthcare worldwide, particularly in maternal health. For instance, many regions in sub-Saharan Africa have seen a notable rise in the availability of skilled birth attendants. However, despite these gains in access, maternal mortality rates in low-income and middle-income countries within the African Region remain alarmingly high. Although GHIs have invested heavily in maternal health, there is limited evidence regarding their effects on the quality of maternal healthcare, an essential factor influencing maternal outcomes. The WHO has developed quality standards for maternal and newborn care to guide all stakeholders in delivery of care for mothers. These include evidence-based practices for managing childbirth complications, efficient health information systems, appropriate referral mechanisms, respectful and dignified treatment of patients, emotional support, well-trained and motivated healthcare providers and a conducive physical environment. These standards serve as a framework for improving and assessing the quality of maternal and newborn services. Despite significant funding and technical guidance aimed at enhancing care quality, there remains a lack of robust data on how GHIs have influenced the quality of maternal health services. To address this evidence gap, we conducted a scoping review to gather and analyse existing evidence on the effects of GHIs on maternal care quality in Sub-Saharan Africa. This report presents the findings from that review.MethodsThis study followed the enhanced six-stage framework for scoping reviews developed by Arksey and O’Malley. We included both peer-reviewed and grey literature such as reports and policy documents that addressed the impact of GHIs on maternal health services in sub-Saharan Africa between 2010 and 2024. Sources in all languages were considered. A defined set of inclusion and exclusion criteria guided the screening process. The selected studies underwent qualitative synthesis and descriptive analysis and were visually represented where appropriate. This review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses extension for Scoping Reviews guidelines.Key findingsThe search yielded only five quantitative studies, depicting the limited evidence on this critical subject. The studies showed that GHIs had varied effects on quality of care for mothers. These effects included improved readiness of facilities to provide care, improved prenatal-postnatal processes, increased provider knowledge, active management of labour and a decrease in mortality rates in some instances such as Uganda and Zambia. However, there was limited or no improvement in clinical practices, patient experiences and satisfaction with care. Additionally, the studies did not focus on the multidimensional aspects of quality of care as guided by WHO’s comprehensive standards for quality of maternal health services, highlighting a major gap identified throughout this review.ConclusionsWhile the limited studies available showed some improvements in specific care quality indicators, there remain significant gaps in the knowledge around how GHIs affect care quality comprehensively. Additionally, the identified studies highlighted significant challenges as a key gap to achieving the intended outcomes and sustaining the gains made during programme implementation. To have a more in-depth understanding of GHIs and their impact on quality of care, it is important to align programme implementation and assessments with comprehensive frameworks such as WHO’s multidimensional quality model. Further, there is a need to adopt iterative, context-sensitive interventions that provide a comprehensive approach to quality of care. The information gathered will be used to inform subsequent studies on the effects of GHIs on quality of care of maternal health services in Uganda and contribute to the development of maternal healthcare policies.Keywords: Health, Maternal medicine, Patient-Centered Care, Patient Satisfaction, Health Services

Introduction

As described in detail in the Scoping Review Protocol of Effects of Global Health Initiatives (GHIs) on the Quality of Care of Maternal Health Services in Sub-Saharan Africa,1 GHIs are collaborative efforts involving diverse stakeholders, including governments, international organisations, non-governmental organisations and philanthropic foundations, aimed at addressing global health challenges.2 GHIs have played a vital role in offering financial and technical assistance to enhance health systems and boost health outcomes, especially in low-income and middle-income countries. Their main areas of focus include HIV/AIDS treatment, maternal health, malaria prevention and other major health issues.3 Globally, the major GHIs include the US President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR), the Global Financing Facility (GFF), the Global Fund to end AIDS, Tuberculosis and Malaria, the Global Alliance for Vaccines and Immunisation, the Stop TB Partnership and World Bank Health Initiatives.4 In Uganda, programmes focused on maternal and women’s health have greatly benefited from initiatives like PEPFAR and the GFF, which provide support through agencies such as the United Nations Population Fund.1 5

In the Financial Year of 2018/2019, donor contributions made up 76% of the entire health sector budget with only 27% of these being direct contributions to the Ministry of Health Uganda budget (on-budget contributions). The majority of these contributions focused on HIV/AIDS (65%), Malaria (8.5%) and Reproductive, Maternal, Neonatal and Child Health (6%).6 Although external contributions have declined in recent years, donor and GHI investments remain a critical driver of health service delivery, particularly in improving maternal health outcomes. However, the potential impact of GHIs on healthcare quality is often undermined by fragmented financing, systemic weaknesses and limited institutional capacity.27,13 Since quality of care directly influences progress towards the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs) related to health and well-being, strengthening the link between funding and service performance is essential. Systematic evaluation of healthcare delivery, especially adherence to clinical guidelines, is necessary to ensure that financial investments effectively translate into improved health outcomes.14 15 Evaluating service performance and identifying gaps in adherence to clinical guidelines are essential for measuring quality and assessing the effects of GHIs on services such as maternal health.

Maternal mortality continues to be a significant concern for women of reproductive age across Africa, particularly in sub-Saharan Africa, where the situation is more severe. Africa accounts for over two-thirds (69%) of maternal deaths worldwide, with a mortality rate of 531 deaths per 100 000 live births. In 2020, countries with the highest maternal mortality rates included South Sudan (1223/100 000 live births), Chad (1064), Nigeria (1047), Central African Republic (835), Guinea Bissau (725), Liberia (652), Lesotho (566), Democratic Republic of Congo (547), Kenya (530) and Benin (523), all located in sub-Saharan Africa.16 SDG target 3.1 aims to reduce global maternal mortality to below 70 deaths per 100 000 live births, underscoring the urgent need for improved care.17 While there has been progress in maternal health, for example, in Uganda, the Maternal Mortality Ratio has reduced from 336/100 000 in 2016 to 189/100 000 in 2022, it remains unacceptably high. The institutional maternal mortality ratio remains elevated, with 82 deaths per 100 000 live births recorded in 2021/2022, a slight decrease from 92 per 100 000 in 2020/2021.18 Although these figures reflect progress, they underscore persistent gaps in the clinical management of obstetric complications.19 Similar trends are observed across sub-Saharan Africa, where increased access to institutional deliveries and antenatal care (ANC) has not consistently translated into reductions in maternal and neonatal mortality, largely due to poor quality of care.11 19

The WHO has established eight standards of care to enhance the quality of care for mothers and newborns. These standards include evidence-based care and management of childbirth complications, action-oriented health information systems, proper referral practices, effective communication, respect and dignity, emotional support, well-trained and motivated staff, and a suitable physical environment. These standards are intended to guide the implementation and evaluation of programmes focused on improving maternal care quality.20

Although significant investments have been made in maternal health across sub-Saharan Africa targeting care for mothers through GHIs, there is limited evidence on impacts of these initiatives on the quality of maternal health services. Existing research often emphasises input and output indicators, such as the number of trained health workers and institutional deliveries, while neglecting critical aspects of care quality that directly influence maternal outcomes.21 In this review, we aimed to understand the impact of GHIs on the quality of care for maternal health services quality in sub-Saharan Africa. We define ‘effect’ as encompassing both positive and negative changes that GHIs may induce on the quality of care provided in maternal health services. By elucidating both strengths and weaknesses within current GHI approaches, this review seeks to inform evidence-based policy improvements that can ultimately reduce maternal mortality rates and enhance maternal health outcomes in the region, particularly in Uganda.

Methodology and analysis

This scoping review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Extension for Scoping Reviews checklist (see online supplemental file).22 23 The enhanced six-stage methodological framework for scoping reviews of Arksey and O’Malley was used.24 This was further described in detail in the protocol for this study1 and the steps involved are given in the next sections.

Identifying the research question

As described in the scoping review protocol for this study,1 our review aimed to address the question: ‘What is the available evidence on the effects of GHIs on healthcare quality for maternal services within health facilities in Sub-Saharan Africa?’ This question emerged from the recognition of the persistently high maternal mortality in the region despite investments from GHIs, as well as consultations with key stakeholders and experts on the state of maternal health services in Uganda and sub-Saharan Africa. The review aimed to explore the complex interplay between GHIs and the quality of maternal health services, taking into account the various factors that may shape their impact.

Identifying relevant studies

A librarian supported the process of a comprehensive search across relevant databases to identify studies that were carried out from 2010 to 2024 focusing on the impact of GHIs on the quality of maternal health services. This time frame enabled us to focus on newer, up-to-date and relevant current practices and guidelines. The search was also limited to studies conducted in Sub-Saharan Africa to allow us to look at studies conducted in similar settings and contexts such as resource limitation. We performed thorough searches in various databases, including Ovid Medline, Embase, Cochrane Library, CABI Global Health (Ovid), CINAHL, Web of Science and JBI Evidence Synthesis, to gather relevant studies pertaining to the impact of GHIs on quality of maternal health services from 2010 to 2024. This search considered both published and unpublished data, for example, the WHO, Ministries of Health, Universities, partners in health and GHIs were contacted for additional grey literature where applicable. Eight broad terms were used during the search, including GHIs, maternal health, quality of health services or quality care, patient care, patient outcomes, maternal outcomes and sub-Saharan Africa. The search strategy (see online supplemental material) employed a combination of keywords and subject headings relevant to the research question, including terms such as “Effects” OR “Impact” OR “global health initiatives,” OR “Public Health” OR “International Health” AND “PEPFAR” OR “AIDSRelief” OR “Global Fund “OR “Global Financing Facility” OR “GAVI” OR “World Bank” OR “World Bank Health Initiatives” AND “maternal health” OR “Maternity” OR “Obstetric” OR “Obstetric and Newborn” AND “Quality of Care” OR “Patient Satisfaction” OR “Patient Centered Care” OR “Medical Health Quality” OR “Experiences of Care” AND " “health facilities” and “sub-Saharan Africa.” The search was conducted from September to November 2024. The process of identification of relevant studies is displayed using a Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses flow diagram.22 23 25

Study selection

As detailed in the protocol for this study,1 the review process was structured into two screening phases: an initial review of article titles/abstracts, followed by a comprehensive full-text review. The first phase involved two reviewers (IA and LA) who independently screened titles and abstracts of identified studies based on predefined eligibility criteria stated in the section below. Before screening, we went over the inclusion and exclusion criteria for the studies and then independently screened the abstracts. The abstracts were screened for relevance to the research question. In cases where there were discrepancies, they were reconciled by the team’s subject matter expert (CSS). Following this, in the second phase, the same pair of reviewers separately evaluated the full-text articles to establish whether they aligned with the inclusion/exclusion criteria. They were screened for relevance, methods used, breadth of the study and the outcomes of the study. Disagreements that arose between the reviewers were resolved through discussions and engagement of a third reviewer (CSS). Understanding of the inclusion and exclusion criteria was assessed through the evaluation of a few studies adhering strictly to the Patient Intervention Comparison Outcome (PICO) criteria.26

Data extraction

As described in the study protocol,1 data extraction form adopted from Joanna Briggs Institute’s template for data extraction27 was used. A specially designed data entry tool using Google Forms was developed by the researcher. This tool collected specific information, which included (1) first author and year of publication, (2) country, (3) study design, (4) conceptual framework used, (5) obj ectives and methods used and (6) findings on the effect of GHI on the quality of maternal health services. To ensure the validity of the process, the form was piloted and evaluated against a few studies (10%) by two reviewers (IA and CSS) and principal investigator (IA). The pair of independent reviewers (IA and LA) then extracted the data, and disagreements were resolved through consensus among the team members. Studies published in French, Arabic, Spanish and Portuguese were translated by inbuilt database translators before data extraction.

Data synthesis

This study adopted the narrative and descriptive approach to analyse the included studies. This was due to the limited studies on this topic and the fact that all included studies employed different study designs. Once we had the relevant studies, we used descriptive words to make sense of information contained in the included studies. We adopted a textural approach to tell the story of the findings from the included studies.

Quality appraisal

While not mandatory for scoping reviews, we conducted a quality appraisal of the primary studies included in the review using the Critical Appraisal Skills Programme checklist developed at Oxford University,29 for quantitative studies, and the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool developed by Pluye and Hong was used for mixed-methods studies.30 This was done to enhance transparency and provide context for interpreting the findings. Reviewers (IA and LA) independently appraised each study using the tool, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion or adjudication by a third reviewer (CSS). A total of five studies were assessed, and three were rated as high quality, two as moderate quality. The most common issue was insufficient data collection detail and reliance on secondary data. Results

Study selection and data extraction

Our comprehensive search across multiple databases and registers yielded a total of 1431 studies. During the initial screening, 13 duplicates were identified and removed using Covidence, while manual screening did not detect any additional duplicates. This left 1418 studies to be screened based on their titles and abstracts. Following this screening process, 1378 studies were excluded for not meeting the predefined inclusion criteria. A total of 26 studies proceeded with full-text assessment for eligibility. Of these, 21 studies were excluded for various reasons: 13 due to wrong outcomes, 8 for wrong interventions, 1 for wrong indication, 1 for wrong setting, 2 for not being GHI funded and 1 for wrong study design. No studies were classified as ongoing or awaiting classification. Five studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the qualitative synthesis, as summarised in figure 1.

Summary of included studies according to reporting accuracy

Five studies met the eligibility criteria for inclusion in this scoping review, each examining the effects of GHIs on the quality of maternal health services in sub-Saharan Africa. The studies were published between 2015 and 2023 and were conducted across a range of countries, including Kenya, Uganda, Zambia, Burkina Faso and a larger continental study across 36 countries. In terms of reporting accuracy, all five studies provided detailed descriptions of intervention components and outcome measures. All studies explicitly adhered to the Strengthening The Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology reporting guidelines36 and included the checklist items such as Introduction, methods, study design and setting, participant recruitment and eligibility, variables and data sources, bias and confounding, results and limitations. Limitations were acknowledged in three out of five studies. Overall, the included studies suggest that GHIs have contributed to measurable improvements in the quality of maternal health services in sub-Saharan Africa, though variability in reporting and methodological transparency limits the ability to generalise findings across contexts.

Effects of GHIs on quality of maternal health

As highlighted in table 1, we identified only five studies that fit within our inclusion criteria. The study conducted by Appel et al in Burkina Faso that was published in 2023 showed that the World Bank intervention on performance-based financing showed some positive effects in intervention facilities such as readiness scores of facilities to provide services where intervention facilities gained 0.1 points over time compared with non-intervention facilities. These were mainly in areas of infection prevention and availability of ANC supplies and drugs. However, there were no significant changes in the content of ANC care in intervention facilities compared with non-intervention facilities.31

Grépin analysed demographic health surveys of 36 sub-Saharan countries alongside data on HIV development assistance. 32 The findings indicated that there were generally no significant associations between HIV development assistance and maternal health services. However, the study found some positive effects of HIV services on specific aspects of maternal health, particularly in relation to prenatal testing. This may probably be due to the increased investment and support for laboratory testing for HIV patients that could also be used for general maternal health laboratory services data.32

Kruk et al looked at an association between HIV programmes and quality of maternal health services in Kenya which showed improved quality of maternal and newborn health service inputs.33 The presence of antiretroviral therapy (ART) programmes was associated with a 0.56 SD increase in prenatal postnatal process quality with a 0.70 SD (p<0.01) increase in prenatal–postnatal process quality in clinics, while in hospitals it was associated with a 0.47 SD (p=0.02) increase in prenatal and postnatal process quality. However, the magnitude of change was moderate. The association between ART programmes and delivery-newborn care was not significant at a p value of less than 0.05. Key limitations were that this was a cross-sectional study and could not establish causality and also did not assess the association between HIV programmes and health outcomes. The study recommended closely integrating services to achieve more gains.33

In Uganda and Zambia, Kruk et al examined the impact of the Saving Mothers Giving Life programme in Uganda and Zambia, which was funded by both PEPFAR and USAID and aimed at reducing maternal and newborn deaths. The findings showed that both countries experienced reductions in maternal and neonatal mortalities; however, they also raised key concerns on the perception of mothers of the quality of care received. Notably, there were no significant differences in mothers’ experiences or their willingness to recommend services to others between intervention and non-intervention districts. Provider obstetric knowledge was also not significantly different among health workers in intervention and non-intervention districts with values ranging from 29.7/60 to 35.3/60 in both intervention and comparison districts in both countries. Satisfaction with overall delivery experience ranged from 65% to 77.3% in both intervention and comparison districts in both countries.37

Additionally, Serbanescu et al further highlighted that there was an increase in institutional delivery from 46% to 67% in SMGL supported facilities in Uganda and from 63% to 90% in Zambia. The percentage of all SMGL facilities that reported active management of third stage of labour increased by 28% (75%–96%) in Uganda. The availability of life-saving medication significantly increased in both countries reporting no stockouts of magnesium sulphate. In Zambia, the change from baseline was 33% (72%–96%). The percentage of facilities performing audits on maternal deaths in their facilities tripled (31%–94%) and doubled in Zambia (50%–100%). Partograph use significantly improved, with 92% of facilities in both countries reporting routine partograph use, almost tripling in Uganda (33.3%–92.4%). However, use of parenteral antibiotics for mothers and newborns who needed them did not significantly increase in both countries, use of parenteral oxytocin increased in Uganda but not in Zambia. Use of parenteral Magnesium Sulphate remained significantly low in both countries.35

Together, these studies demonstrated that GHIs and other global health programmes impacted certain aspects of maternal healthcare, such as enhancing facility readiness, increasing the availability of essential commodities like life-saving drugs and promoting care practices like the use of partographs. However, they were less successful in comprehensively improving the overall quality of care, including the content of care provided, patient satisfaction and health worker practices.

Discussion

The findings of this scoping review showed the varied and multidimensional effects of GHIs on the quality of maternal health services in sub-Saharan Africa as shown in table 1. GHIs have adopted a spectrum of interventions, ranging from targeted training programmes to broader health system strengthening efforts, with demonstrable improvements in selected clinical practices yet less consistent outcomes for overarching healthcare delivery and user experience. We discuss the findings from this review according to the WHO’s standards of care for maternal care20 and later look at the broader overarching effects of GHIs.

Every Woman receives evidence-based routine care and management of complications

Although the available studies did not directly evaluate the impact of GHIs on this standard of care, this review found mixed findings with some evidence suggesting that GHIs contributed to improvements in areas such as facility readiness to deliver quality ANC, prenatal and postnatal services, active management of the third stage of labour and the availability of emergency obstetric care, among others, all being evidence-based practices. For example, in Burkina Faso, for instance, a performance-based financing intervention was successful in improving facility readiness scores of intervention facilities such as infection prevention practices and availability of ANC supplies and drugs.38 This study, however, found no improvements in content of care of ANC. The lack of significant changes in the content of care was partly attributed to a focus and heavier weighting of the tool for readiness indicators compared with those focusing on provider input by the authors.38

In Kenya, the presence of ART services showed an improvement in prenatal and postnatal services, but no significant changes were seen for delivery care. However, results from these studies also indicated efforts from the GHI initiatives did not result in significant improvement of maternal healthcare services.39 40 This aligns with broader evidence indicating that episodic training alone may be insufficient for addressing deep-rooted gaps in clinical competence, necessitating ongoing in-service training and innovative, context-specific pedagogical approaches.34 Furthermore, the findings reiterate the point that unless GHIs align financial incentives with both input and clinical practices of health workers, improvements in readiness may not translate into sustained gains in practice and quality of care provided. Additionally, a cautionary note from Grépin on the broader impact of large scale donor funding on healthcare systems in sub-Saharan Africa aligns with findings from other studies that have called for GHIs and other donors to design programmes that support overall health systems, proactively mitigating potential competition for scarce personnel and infrastructure.3 11 32

Health information enables the use of data early to ensure early appropriate interventions to improve care

In the context of this study, it is noteworthy that none of the identified research efforts assessed the impact or outcomes of Government Health Information Systems on the actual use of health information. Although there are other studies in the broader literature that have explored the influence of health systems on information or data use and found that GHIs were found to duplicate monitoring systems,11 this specific aspect is frequently overlooked in evaluations. Other studies have also found minimal investments on health information41 which may explain a lack of evidence on GHIs’ influence on this standard. This omission is concerning, especially considering that the use of health information is a key standard of care emphasised by the WHO.20

Every woman and newborn with condition(s) that cannot be dealt with effectively with the available resources is appropriately referred

This standard also lacked any supporting evidence demonstrating measurable effects or outcomes in the studies reviewed. Although a small number of targeted studies have explored referral pathways and found limited evidence around referral pathways,42 the current body of evidence included in this review did not reveal any research that reported outcomes of GHIs specifically related to this standard.

Communication with women and their families is effective and responds to their needs and preferences

None of the studies reviewed have specifically examined how GHIs influence the standard of communication between healthcare providers and pregnant women or their families. Although some research indicates that certain GHIs have contributed to training healthcare workers in effective communication and enhancing the overall quality of maternal care, these efforts have not been directly assessed in terms of their impact on patient–provider communication during pregnancy. Existing studies have primarily focused on the role of GHIs in improving care and communication for people living with HIV, where patients felt that health workers communicated to them with respect while they were in health facilities supported by PEPFAR, as compared with when they got care from public facilities.43 However, there remains a significant gap in the literature regarding the influence of these initiatives on communication practices in maternal healthcare settings.

Women and their families receive care with respect and preservation of their dignity

Similarly, there is a noticeable lack of evidence addressing this specific standard of care, particularly in terms of respect for and preservation of patient dignity and how GHIs influence or contribute to it. Despite the importance of dignity as a core principle in patient-centred care, this dimension is often neglected in evaluations and studies. Much like other standards related to patient-centredness, such as communication and emotional support, the role of GHIs in promoting dignity is either under-researched or entirely overlooked. This suggests a significant gap in the understanding and measurement of quality care from a holistic, human-centred perspective. Other findings show that PEPFAR has had an impact on how HIV patients felt that they had been treated with respect in HIV clinics compared with when they were in public facilities.43

Every woman and her family are provided with emotional support that is sensitive to their needs and strengthens the woman’s capabilities

This study did not identify any research that specifically focused on aspects such as communication, respect and dignity or emotional support within maternal healthcare. However, one partial exception was the evaluation conducted by Kruk et al as part of the Saving Mothers, Giving Life programme in Uganda and Zambia.37 Their findings indicated that women in intervention districts reported similar levels of satisfaction and likelihood to recommend health facilities as those in non-intervention districts, suggesting no significant improvement in patient-perceived experience. Notably, although there were observed improvements in healthcare providers’ obstetric knowledge, job satisfaction and clinical confidence, these gains did not translate into enhanced patient satisfaction. These findings reflect findings from Tanzania where health workers noted a need for training in patient-centred care,44 highlighting the critical need for coordinated and intentional efforts by ministries of health, GHIs and front-line health workers to strengthen communication, foster patient-centred care and provide emotional support to mothers throughout the care continuum.

For every woman and newborn, competent, motivated staff are consistently available to provide routine care and manage complications

Findings from this study indicate that GHIs have generally had an impact on this particular standard of care. For instance, an evaluation of a maternal health programme implemented in Uganda and Zambia demonstrated that healthcare workers in districts where the intervention was applied exhibited greater obstetric knowledge and clinical confidence compared with their counterparts in non-intervention districts.37

The health facility has an appropriate physical environment, with adequate water, sanitation and energy supplies, medicines, supplies and equipment for routine maternal and newborn care and management of complications.

The findings from this review indicate that GHIs have had a notable impact on the standard of care within healthcare systems. Many GHIs primarily concentrated on enhancing physical inputs such as the provision of medical supplies, essential medicines and equipment, thereby strengthening the operational capacity of health facilities. In addition to supplying critical resources, some GHIs also supported infrastructure improvements related to water, sanitation and hygiene, aiming to reduce healthcare-associated infections and improve overall patient safety.

For instance, a quasi-experimental study conducted by Appel et al on a performance-based financing programme in Burkina Faso demonstrated that the programme significantly enhanced facility readiness. This improvement was attributed to better infection prevention measures and more reliable access to essential drugs and supplies.38 Similarly, research by Serbanescu revealed that intervention districts in Uganda and Zambia experienced improved availability of emergency drugs compared with non-intervention areas, highlighting a positive differential impact.35 In summary, the review provides clear evidence that GHIs contribute to elevating the standard of care by enhancing the availability of medicines, supplies and medical equipment, as well as supporting infection prevention efforts through infrastructural and resource-based interventions.

Overall, GHIs also had system-level transformations such as increases in institutional deliveries, expansions in emergency obstetric care and impact transformation such as reductions in maternal mortality in Uganda and Zambia under the Saving Mothers, Giving Life initiative.45 However, challenges were reported in improving provider clinical practices and patient satisfaction with services.45 46 These findings mirror a wider consensus that improvements in clinical infrastructure, availability of essential supplies and motivated human resources must be continuously supported to prevent backsliding in quality of care.34 47 From our review, while facility-based indicators, such as the availability of essential medicines and supplies related to maternal neonatal care, were reportedly crucial in maximising intervention impacts, availability of these commodities did not translate into their use as seen in the Saving Mothers Giving Life Project in Uganda and Zambia. While the programme led to increased availability of drugs like Magnesium Sulphate and Oxytocin, the use of parenteral antibiotics, oxytocin and magnesium sulphate remained low.45 These findings highlight that although programmes may focus on making commodities available, additional measures such as enhanced supervision, mentorship and staff motivation are necessary to ensure that investments lead to tangible improvements in the management of mothers and quality of care provided to mothers during the delivery process.48

Despite system-level transformations such as increases in institutional deliveries, expansions in emergency obstetric care and reductions in maternal mortality in Uganda and Zambia under the Saving Mothers, Giving Life initiative, challenges were reported in ensuring sustained benefits.45 46 These findings mirror a wider consensus that improvements in clinical infrastructure, availability of essential supplies and motivated human resources must be continuously supported to prevent backsliding in quality of care.34 47 However, it is debatable if this approach is sustainable given the growing need for locally driven health systems with limited external funding. GHIs have not traditionally invested in new infrastructure and specialised clinical training. From our review, availability of facility inputs such as supplies and drugs did not lead to changes in content of care or health worker practices such as in the performance-based intervention in Burkina Faso.31 Authors attributed this to incentive schemes that prioritise structural indicators while offering insufficient motivation for health workers to consistently deliver comprehensive, high-quality ANC.

Improvements in output indicators such as increased institutional delivery rates and expanded emergency obstetric care do not always translate into improved patient satisfaction or perceived quality of care. GHIs also fell short of changing clinical practices of health workers such as administration of life-saving drugs such as parenteral antibiotics, oxytocin or magnesium sulphate. Therefore, there is a need for GHIs and recipient governments to strengthen clinical quality and health worker practices through heightened mentorship and supervision while also accounting for equity and people-centredness of care.20 49 Furthermore, maternal care-seeking behaviour is influenced by broader social, cultural and structural factors. As such, GHIs must also address patient perceptions of care, satisfaction and trust in healthcare facilities to promote sustainable service utilisation. Monitoring user experience is crucial to ensure alignment between service availability and perceived quality.33 34 49

Finally, this review highlights the limitations in scientific and programming documentation regarding the key healthcare aspect of quality of care. The limited studies that do exist focus on only a few components of care and fail to fully address the multidimensional standards of care as outlined by the WHO’s vision for quality of care. Additionally, the lack of evidence of the effects of maternal health programmes and indicators is perhaps reflective of the vertical focus on disease conditions without taking a systems approach.

Conclusions

GHIs aim to improve maternal health services, particularly through performance-based financing, training and strengthening health systems. While there have been improvements in certain care quality indicators and standards of care such as access to evidence-based care, availability of competent health workers and appropriate physical infrastructure, significant challenges remain, including difficulty in changing health worker care practices and addressing the multidimensional nature of care quality as defined by WHO standards of care. To achieve more lasting impactful results, a more integrated and context-responsive approach is needed with a focus on health worker clinical practices. GHIs should align their programmes and impact measures with comprehensive frameworks like the WHO’s multidimensional quality model and implement iterative, context-sensitive interventions that provide a more holistic approach to care quality. Future initiatives and impact assessment measures should, therefore, combine technical improvements with strategies to enhance user engagement, healthcare provider motivation, supervision and system resilience, ensuring that quality improvements are both durable and sustainable. Given the limited number of studies identified, future reviews could adopt broader search terms, thereby yielding more studies for inclusion.
