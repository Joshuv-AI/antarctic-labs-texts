Optimizing scalable approaches for early detection of cognitive impairment in primary care
Authors: Arthur H Owora, Diana Summanwar, Ambar Kulshreshtha, Zina Ben Miled, Paul R Dexter, Bowen Jiang, Yash Shah, Eric Puster, Katrina Coppedge, Shanell Disla, James E Galvin, Nicole R Fowler, Malaz Boustani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTINTRODUCTIONUnrecognized cognitive impairment remains common in primary care, delaying access to care.METHODSWe conducted a prospective cross‐sectional study to evaluate a multimodal screening approach combining a passive digital marker (PDM) from electronic health records with the Quick Dementia Rating System (QDRS).RESULTSIndividually, each method showed limited discrimination (area under the curve [AUC] 0.61–0.62). The combined model, incorporating PDM, QDRS, and comorbidity context, improved performance (AUC 0.79; sensitivity 0.75; specificity 0.71). Decision curve analysis showed a net benefit of 0.5 at a 50% risk threshold, corresponding to a modest increase in correctly identified cases relative to alternative screening strategies.DISCUSSIONThis low‐burden, scalable multimodal approach improves detection of cognitive impairment, including mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer's disease and related dementias, and may support improved risk stratification and referral prioritization in primary care.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease and related dementias, electronic health record data, mild cognitive impairment, patient‐reported outcomes tool, primary care, Quick Dementia Rating System, screening

INTRODUCTION

Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD) affect millions of Americans and impose > $12 billion in annual societal costs. 1 Currently, half of Americans living with ADRD and > 90% of those with MCI are unrecognized by health‐care systems. 2 , 3 , 4 When recognized, the diagnosis often occurs 2 to 5 years after the onset of symptoms; yet, existing treatments are more effective with earlier stages of disease detection that are more amenable to modulation. 3 For most patients early case detection remains elusive because existing MCI and ADRD case‐finding approaches (i.e., cognitive tests or biomarkers) are invasive, expensive, and difficult to implement in a very busy primary care setting. 5 Yet, early detection and treatment initiation with biologics, including monoclonal antibodies such as lecanemab and donanemab, have shown promise in slowing cognitive decline but only among patients with MCI or mild ADRD. 6

Although electronic health records (EHRs) often contain some of the markers relevant to cognitive impairment detection (e.g., cognitive decline symptoms or related comorbidities, medications, and laboratory results), primary care clinicians may overlook or fail to review this information at point of care. 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 It is important to note that EHRs may sometimes capture a transient phenotype of cognitive impairment at specific clinical encounters that may not reflect a patient's current experience. To address this challenge, routinely captured patient‐reported outcomes (PROs), such as the Quick Dementia Rating System (QDRS), can provide a patient's own perception of cognitive and functional changes. 11 Such information can be especially valuable in early stages of cognitive impairment, when a patient may recognize subtle changes in their brain function that a clinician or care partner might not observe or report. Integrating EHR data with PROs could improve early identification of at‐risk patients who can then be targeted for potentially more conclusive diagnostic evaluation and early intervention.

Therefore, this study aimed to examine the performance of two screening tools (passive digital marker [PDM] and QDRS) for the early detection of cognitive impairment (MCI or ADRD) in primary care settings. Informed by our previous studies, 11 , 12 , 13 we hypothesized that the area under the curve (AUC) for the combined approach (PDM + QRDS) would be ≥ 0.85, and the AUC for the PDM or the QDRS would be ≥ 0.75. Further, we also hypothesized that the combined approach (PDM + QDRS) would have statistically better AUC than each individual approach alone.

METHODS

Study design and population

We conducted a prospective cross‐sectional clinical validation study between July 2021 and July 2023. Patients were consecutively recruited from five Federally Qualified Health Centers (FQHC) affiliated with Eskenazi Health in Indianapolis, Indiana, and nine primary care practices/providers affiliated with the University of Miami (UHealth) in South Florida. This study was approved by the institutional review board of Indiana University (number: 2008372812).

Eligibility criteria

Eligible patients were consecutively enrolled into the study if they were ≥ 65 years, had an established primary care clinician at a participating clinic, had at least one visit within the prior year, had a minimum of 3 years of EHR data available, and were able to provide verbal or written informed consent in English. Patients were excluded if they had a documented diagnosis of cognitive impairment in the EHR (identified via the International Statistical Classification of Diseases and Related Health Problems, Tenth Revision [ICD‐10] codes), a history of cholinesterase inhibitor or memantine use, a diagnosis of a serious mental illness (e.g., schizophrenia or bipolar disorder), or resided in a long‐term care facility.

Data collection and measures

Index tests

Index tests included the PDM and QDRS. Index test positivity was determined based on optimal thresholds that maximized our study sample sensitivity and specificity as defined by Youden index. 14 No indeterminate index test results were generated based on established optimal thresholds.

The PDM is a random forest algorithm designed to predict ADRD 1 year and 3 years prior to its clinical onset using routinely collected EHR data. 15 The algorithm was developed and validated using structured and unstructured data from three EHR datasets: diagnoses (Dx), prescriptions (Rx), and clinical notes (Nx) including demographic features (i.e., age, sex, race). The algorithm was trained and tested using EHR data from incident ADRD cases and non‐ADRD controls. The PDM has previously been shown to have an overall accuracy of 85% at a cut‐off probability threshold of 0.5 with a sensitivity of 76% and specificity of 80%. 15

In this study, the QDRS was self‐completed by participants rather than informants. The QDRS is a 10‐item, multiple choice questionnaire 12 that provides a brief, valid, and reliable assessment regarding the presence and severity of cognitive impairment and demonstrates differential scoring across different ADRD etiologies. It offers a range of scores from 0 to 30, 12 , 16 with an optimal cut‐off of 1.5 for MCI (sensitivity 85.2%, specificity 96.3%) and 6 for dementia (sensitivity 90.1%, specificity 89.2%) when referenced against the Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR) as the gold standard diagnosis. 17

The workflow description for the pragmatic model‐based approach can be found in Supplemental Material I in supporting information. The EHR‐derived PDM risk score (including current patient comorbidities) and QDRS results (i.e., PROs) were combined using a predefined integration approach (multivariable logistic regression) to generate a composite risk estimate. Based on this combined output, participants were classified into two risk categories for cognitive impairment (low vs. high). This parallel, multimodal workflow enabled integration of longitudinal, passive clinical data with cross‐sectional cognitive performance (i.e., QDRS).

Reference standard (gold standard)

Blinded to the PDM and QDRS results, an interdisciplinary team consisting of a neurologist, a geriatrician, and a neuropsychologist established cognitive diagnoses through clinical consensus. The team reviewed multiple cognitive test results, clinical diagnoses, and medical records to categorize patients as having normal cognition or cognitive impairment. The cognitive testing included a comprehensive neuropsychological battery derived from the Uniform Data Set (UDS) 3.0 developed by the National Institute on Aging Alzheimer's Disease Centers. 18 , 19 The battery of tests included the 15‐item Multilingual Naming Test (MINT) for naming, category fluency (animals) for verbal fluency, number span (forward and backward) for attention and working memory, Craft Story for episodic memory (immediate and delayed recall), 20 and Trail Making Test Parts A and B for processing speed and executive attention. 21 Mood was assessed using the Geriatric Depression Scale (GDS). 22 In addition, informant‐based tools, including the CDR, 23 Neuropsychiatric Inventory (NPI) questionnaire, 24 and Functional Activities Questionnaire (FAQ), 25 were used to evaluate cognitive, behavioral, and functional domains.

Statistical analysis

We planned to recruit 400 patients from primary care practices in Indiana and Florida to achieve 81% power to compare population accuracy values of 75% versus 85% for the PDM or QDRS alone versus PDM + QDRS combined (Supplemental Material II in supporting information).

Descriptive statistics (including proportions, means with standard deviations, and medians) summarized participants’ demographic characteristics, clinical parameters, and cognitive status measures by specialists’ consensus diagnosis (normal vs. cognitive impairment).

Performance of index tests

We summarized the diagnostic performance of each screening approach using discrimination and calibration metrics. Comparative accuracy was evaluated using the DeLong test for correlated receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves (see Supplemental Material III in supporting information). 26

Pragmatic model‐based screening approach—feature selection and model estimation

We also investigated the performance of a pragmatic model‐based screening approach that combines the PDM and QDRS using a repeated testing framework for feature (predictor) selection and evaluation to improve prognostic value of the pragmatic model (see Supplemental Material IV in supporting information). 27 , 28 We selected additional prediction features/variables based on statistical (multivariable logistic regression with least absolute shrinkage and selection operator [LASSO] approach) and clinical criteria.

Internal validation (Indiana)

We used 10‐fold cross‐validation to assess the pragmatic model's generalizability by repeatedly training and testing on 10 different data partitions. Bootstrap resampling (1000 resamples) was used to examine sampling variability and assess our model's optimism. 29

External validation (Florida)

We performed an independent external validation of the pragmatic model using data from the Florida site to evaluate its diagnostic performance with and without recalibration (Supplemental Material V in supporting information).

Clinical utility

We evaluated clinical utility using a decision curve analysis (DCA), 30 , 31 which quantified the net benefit 30 of different screening schemas across a range of threshold probabilities (see Supplemental Material VI in supporting information).

We constructed a nomogram, a visual representation of the pragmatic logistic model, to provide clinicians with a quick and intuitive way to calculate an individual patient's probability of cognitive impairment based on the PDM score, QDRS score, and demographic and clinical characteristics.

We conducted subgroup analysis to examine whether the pragmatic model's diagnostic performance differed by age, sex, and race/ethnicity for both the internal and external validation samples.

All analyses were carried out in R version 4.5.0. The study follows the Standards for Reporting Diagnostic Accuracy (STARD) guidelines to ensure transparency and completeness in the reporting of index test performance, reference standard methods, and diagnostic outcomes. 32

The prevalence of previously undetected cognitive impairment was 59.5%; 166 (51.7%) patients were diagnosed with MCI and 25 (7.8%) were diagnosed with ADRD. As expected, patients with cognitive impairment (MCI or ADRD) had fewer years of education, higher CDR scores, and a lower score on the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA; Table 1). A higher proportion of patients with cognitive impairment were identified in Indiana (74.1%) than in Florida (35.0%).

At Indiana, compared to the individual tests (PDM or QDRS alone), the pragmatic model‐based approach implemented via a logistic model had the highest discrimination (Figure 2A), calibration (Figure 2B), and net benefit (Figure 3) for detecting of patients with cognitive impairment (MCI or ADRD). Across both the individual and pragmatic screening strategies (Figure 2), differentiating between patients without cognitive impairment versus ADRD (blue lines) had higher accuracy than differentiating between patients without cognitive impairment versus MCI (red lines). Accuracy was lowest when differentiating between patients without versus with cognitive impairment (MCI or ADRD; black lines). External validation (Florida site) showed similar results but with lower discrimination/calibration (Figure S1 in supporting information) and net benefit (Figure S2 in supporting information).

Table 2 summarizes the diagnostic accuracy of the index tests for cognitive impairment at optimal (based on Youden index) thresholds. For both internal and external validation, the AUC for the individual index tests and pragmatic model were below our hypothesized thresholds of 0.75 and 0.85. Compared to the PDM (AUC = 0.61) and QDRS (AUC = 0.62), the pragmatic model, which combined both tests with patient characteristics (age, sex, race, and comorbidities [cerebrovascular disease, diabetes, depression, and hypertension]) showed higher accuracy (AUC = 0.79) with Brier scores < 0.25 (i.e., indicating that the predicted probabilities were close to the actual cognitive impairment outcomes). Overall, the pragmatic model correctly classified 74% patients in Indiana (internal validation) and 70% in Florida (external validation), respectively. Pragmatic model internal validation using 10‐fold cross‐validation (Figure S3 in supporting information) and bootstrap re‐sampling (Figure S4 in supporting information) showed robust generalizability and stability (i.e., sampling variability). Pragmatic model diagnostic performance varied by sex and race in the internal validation sample and by sex and ethnicity in the external validation sample (Table S2 in supporting information). The highest diagnostic performance was observed among male (AUC = 0.86) and Hispanic (AUC = 0.90) patients in the internal and external validation samples, respectively. The nomogram (Figure S5 in supporting information) provides a visual representation of the pragmatic logistic model to provide clinicians with a quick and intuitive tool to calculate an individual patient's probability of cognitive impairment based on a patient's PDM score, QDRS scores, and characteristics.

For internal validation, DCA showed that the pragmatic model had higher clinical utility (net benefit) than the individual index tests above a 50% risk threshold (Figure 3). A net benefit of 0.5 at a threshold of 50% (i.e., probability level at which a patient/clinician would choose a full diagnostic work‐up) indicates that using the model is equivalent to finding 50 additional true positives per 100 patients while not increasing the number of false positives compared to a scenario in which no full‐diagnostic workups are performed. Similar results were observed for external validation but with a comparatively lower magnitude of net benefit at higher risk thresholds (Figure S2).

DISCUSSION

Overall, our findings suggest that combining a PDM derived from EHR data with the QDRS offers modest advantages over single‐modality screening for cognitive impairment case finding. This complementary approach provides a more clinically relevant assessment of risk by integrating longitudinal, real‐world clinical patterns with patient‐reported cognitive and functional changes while remaining potentially scalable for primary care settings, in which time and resources are limited. Although performance did not fully meet prespecified targets for a multimodal model, the observed improvements in risk stratification and clinical utility support its role as a pragmatic strategy to enhance identification of at‐risk individuals and streamline referral for confirmatory evaluation and early intervention.

To put our findings (i.e., the pragmatic strategy had a higher net benefit vs. other screening strategies above a risk threshold 50% with a net benefit of 0.5) in context, prior literature suggests that net benefits > 0.2 represent meaningful clinical impact. 30 Early detection of cognitive impairment offers substantial benefits in terms of improved cognitive outcomes with early treatment initiation, but it also carries potential harms including overdiagnosis and psychological distress. Therefore, it may be unreasonable to opt for a full diagnostic workup if the risk of cognitive impairment was < 50% or, alternatively, to refuse a full diagnostic workup if a patient had a risk of cognitive impairment of ≥ 50%. The relatively higher net benefit of the pragmatic screening approach (vs. other strategies) across a wider range of risk thresholds (> 50%) underscores its usefulness in informing targeted diagnostic workup in real‐world settings, in which early detection must be weighed against the risks and costs of overtesting, unnecessary specialist referrals, and overdiagnosis. However, it is important to note that net benefit is inherently dependent on outcome prevalence and the choice of decision threshold. Given the relatively high prevalence of cognitive impairment in our Indiana sample, the estimated magnitude of benefit, including the implied number of additional cases detected per 100 screened, may not directly translate to settings with lower prevalence or different clinical thresholds for action. Accordingly, interpretation of decision‐curve results should consider local population characteristics and practice patterns, and further evaluation in diverse clinical settings is needed to determine the generalizability of these estimates.

The potential utility of the pragmatic screening approach is demonstrated in our recent pragmatic cluster randomized trial that showed higher referral rates and increased detection of incident cognitive impairment using the PDM + QDRS compared to usual care (based on pre‐determined positivity threshold with lower diagnostic accuracy). 4 Future randomized controlled trials are needed to evaluate the potential incremental benefits of our pragmatic screening approach, which demonstrates comparatively higher diagnostic accuracy.

Using our pragmatic screening approach has several strengths. First, it ensures a balanced approach that minimizes the risks of over‐pathologizing based on EHR‐based phenotypes, which may involve transient markers of cognitive decline (PDM), while addressing the critical needs of patients who report concerns of cognitive decline through PROs (QDRS). Second, it allows for further optimization and personalization as demonstrated by the increase in diagnostic accuracy when age, sex, self‐reported race (a social construct that is an imperfect proxy for socioeconomic status), and comorbidities known to be associated with increased risk of cognitive decline were added to the screening model. This is particularly relevant in primary care, in which routine cognitive screening is often limited by time, reimbursement constraints, and restricted access to specialty evaluation. In such contexts, model‐guided triage may support clinicians in prioritizing patients who are most likely to benefit from comprehensive diagnostic assessment or specialist referral.

The relatively low accuracy of the individual screening approaches (PDM, QDRS) in primary care settings serves as a “cautionary tale” and is consistent with previous studies 33 , 34 , 35 demonstrating that single screening strategies based on the EHR phenotypes/risk classification or brief screeners, while useful (necessary) might still be insufficient as standalone screening strategies. Moreover, as standalone strategies, the PDM/QDRS may be confounded by other behavioral variables (e.g., depression, apathy) that complicate their interpretation. 33 , 34

Combining a brief cognitive screener with passive EHR‐based tools offer a scalable and clinically practical alternative to more resource‐intensive multimodal strategies. Brief digital screeners efficiently detect core cognitive deficits at the point of care, while passive EHR‐derived markers—such as missed appointments, medication adherence, comorbidity burden, health‐care use, and documented functional decline provide longitudinal, real‐world context without additional patient effort. This integration links cross‐sectional cognitive performance with routine clinical trajectories, improving early detection. For example, digital screening studies such as BioCog report 85% accuracy, increasing to 90% when combined with complementary measures, outperforming standard care (70%). 36 Similarly, EHR‐based machine learning models have demonstrated utility in identifying undiagnosed dementia and predicting risk using routinely collected data. 37 Compared to biomarker‐ or imaging‐heavy models, 38 , 39 , 40 , 41 our pragmatic model‐based approach has lower cost and complexity while maintaining improved sensitivity, representing a feasible intermediate strategy for large‐scale screening and risk stratification pending further validation.

The differences in the diagnostic accuracy of the PDM/QDRS between the Indiana (internal validation site) and Florida (external validation site) highlight the need for the recalibration of the PDM or re‐norming the QDRS when used in populations with a different case mix or spectrum of cognitive impairment (MCI or ADRD) presentation. This need is highlighted by existing studies that have shown that brief screeners such as the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) have a wide range of sensitivity (71%–92%) and specificity (56%–96%) across primary care populations. 35 In addition, the heterogeneity of reference standards (clinical diagnostic criteria vs. comprehensive neuropsychological and/or clinical assessments vs. biomarkers) and study samples (i.e., case mix) used to validate existing brief screening tools makes it challenging to predict how any single tool may perform in real‐world clinical settings. 35 , 42

Transportability of our pragmatic diagnostic approach across care systems warrants careful consideration. Because the PDM is derived from routinely collected EHR data, its behavior is inherently sensitive to differences in data generation processes, including coding practices, documentation incentives, visit frequency, comorbidity capture, and access to specialty care. Variations in referral pathways and health‐care use patterns particularly between US‐based FQHC or academic‐affiliated settings and public or universal health‐care systems may influence both the distribution of PDM features and the calibration of the combined model. Differences in patient mix may further affect model performance and threshold selection as demonstrated in our external validation sample. As such, while the multimodal framework is conceptually scalable, its performance cannot be assumed to generalize without adaptation. Future work should prioritize external validation and recalibration across diverse health‐care environments, including systems with differing payer structures and documentation norms, to ensure robustness, fairness, and clinical utility prior to broad implementation.

This study has some limitations. First, the PDM scores are dependent on the quality and completeness of EHR data and therefore may be prone to known limitations of EHR‐based screening. 43 Challenges such as variable data quality, difficulty differentiating between transient and persistent markers of cognitive decline, and algorithmic bias, can present a significant hurdle that may create inefficiencies (linked to false positives) in referral practices. 44 , 45 However, improvements in EHR data quality and adoption of interoperable EHR systems may help mitigate some of these limitations. Second, as with any screening strategy, some patients with MCI may still be missed, in part because there are no perfect criteria to fully distinguish between mild and transient symptoms of cognitive impairment. Therefore, patient literacy and clinical judgment remain essential, as is the case for all screening approaches. Third, our results may be affected by selection and participation bias, as consenters and test completers may differ from non‐participants in cognitive status and health engagement, potentially attenuating diagnostic performance estimates and limiting generalizability.

In conclusion, a pragmatic (multi‐modal) screening approach that leverages existing EHR data (as the PDM) and PROs (using the QDRS) may offer a low‐cost and scalable approach with modest accuracy for identifying patients at risk of cognitive impairment (MCI or ADRD) in primary care settings. This strategy has the potential to reduce the strain on specialty services while mitigating the psychological impact associated with over‐pathologizing solely based on EHR‐based phenotypes of cognitive impairment.
