Association between Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic Syndrome and Depressive Symptoms in Middle-Aged and Older Chinese Adults: A Cross-Sectional and Longitudinal Analysis
Authors: Wenjin Pei, Jinhao Shi, Chenyi Liu, Lu Chen, Yonghan Liu, Jialiang Hui, Siyuan Ma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionCardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome and depressive disorder represent major public health burdens. While associations between depression and individual components of CKM syndrome have been established, the relationship between depression and CKM syndrome as an integrated clinical construct remains poorly understood.MethodsData from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) were used, including 8,088 participants in cross-sectional analyses and 1,803 in longitudinal analyses. CKM syndrome was classified according to the American Heart Association (AHA) staging criteria, and depressive symptoms were assessed using the 10-item Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10). The association between CKM stage and depressive symptoms was evaluated using ordinary least squares regression models with five levels of covariate adjustment. Subgroup analyses and interaction tests were conducted to investigate interaction effects across different populations.ResultsHigher CKM stages were significantly associated with increased depressive symptom severity. In the cross-sectional analysis (2011), mean CES-D-10 scores were 8.6 ± 6.4, 8.6 ± 6.3, 8.1 ± 6.2, and 10.7 ± 6.9 for the total population and CKM stages 0–1, 2–3, and 4, respectively. Scores in stage 4 exceeded the threshold for severe depressive symptoms and were significantly higher than those in other stages (p < 0.001). Subgroup analyses further confirmed the robustness of these findings. In longitudinal analyses, progression in CKM stage over a 4-year period was independently associated with an increase in CES-D-10 scores in the fully adjusted model (β = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.07–0.54).ConclusionsCKM syndrome is significantly associated with depressive symptoms, especially in stage 4. There is a positive relationship between CKM progression and depressive symptom severity, highlighting the need for integrated physical and mental health care for CKM patients.

Introduction

Cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic (CKM) syndrome has emerged as a significant health concern, characterized by the complex interplay between metabolic disorders, chronic kidney disease (CKD), and cardiovascular diseases (CVD) [1]. This syndrome is characterized by a systemic dysfunction where metabolic abnormalities, such as diabetes and obesity, not only exacerbate renal and cardiovascular conditions but also interact with other comorbidities [2]. The prevalence of CKM syndrome is increasing, posing significant challenges to public health due to its association with elevated morbidity and mortality rates [3, 4]. Research has shown that CKM syndrome is closely linked to other diseases, such as dementia, which can further complicate its clinical manifestations and outcomes [5, 6]. Understanding the complex relationships between CKM syndrome and other comorbidities is crucial for developing comprehensive management strategies.

Depressive disorder is a leading contributor to global disability-adjusted life years, with an age-standardized prevalence of 3.4% in 2019 [7]. In China, depressive disorder is the second-highest contributor to disability-adjusted life years nationwide, characterized by marked gender disparities and placing substantial pressure on the healthcare system [8, 9]. Population aging, a significant risk factor for depressive disorder [10], is particularly pronounced in China. The prevalence of depressive disorder among older adults in China is as high as 40% [11], yet the treatment rate is extremely low [9]. The situation exacerbates clinical outcomes of multiple chronic conditions and exerts substantial pressure on healthcare systems due to elevated disability and mortality [12], highlighting the urgent need to develop targeted prevention and identification strategies.

Although existing evidence has demonstrated bidirectional associations between depression and individual cardiovascular, renal, and metabolic pathologies [13–16], the syndemic interplay of depression with CKM syndrome, a unified clinical entity, remains unexplored. This gap represents a critical limitation in understanding their shared pathophysiological mechanisms and integrated management strategies.

This study aimed to elucidate the relationship between CKM syndrome and depressive symptoms, with a focus on two key aspects: their epidemiological overlap and disease progression trajectories. Based on the existing evidence from comorbidity research, we hypothesize that CKM syndrome has a significant impact on depressive symptoms.

Method

Data Source and Study Population

This study used data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), a nationally representative longitudinal survey of Chinese adults aged 45 and above [17]. CHARLS aims to comprehensively assess the social, economic, and health status of community residents, with a particular focus on the effects of population aging [17]. The baseline survey (wave 1) was conducted from 2011 to 2012, enrolling 17,708 participants from 450 urban communities and rural areas across 28 provinces of China. Follow-up interviews were carried out biennially using face-to-face computer-assisted interviews. Physical measurements were taken every 2-year follow-up, and blood samples were collected every two follow-up periods. To date, data from four follow-up waves (wave 2: 2013–2014; wave 3: 2015–2016; wave 4: 2017–2018; wave 5: 2019–2020) have been released, with blood samples collected again in 2015. The CHARLS study has been approved by the Ethics Review Committee of Peking University, Beijing (IRB00001052-11015), and all participants provided informed consent.

For this study, we selected data from two waves of CHARLS with available blood samples: wave 1 (2011) and wave 3 (2015). Participants who were lost to follow-up in wave 3, those who could not be accurately assessed for CKM health status and depressive symptoms in both waves, and those with missing covariate data in wave 1 were excluded. After these exclusions, a total of 8,088 participants from wave 1 were included in the baseline cross-sectional analysis, and 1,803 participants with changes in CKM staging were included in the longitudinal analysis. Assessment of CKM Syndrome

According to the American Heart Association (AHA) Presidential Advisory Statement on CKM syndrome, CKM syndrome is categorized into five stages: stage 0 (no CKM risk factors), stage 1 (excess or dysfunctional adiposity), stage 2 (metabolic risk factors and CKD), stage 3 (subclinical CVD), and stage 4 (clinical CVD) (showed in online suppl. Table S1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000552261) [1]. In this study, we used the estimated glomerular filtration rate calculated by the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration equation (CKD-EPI) creatinine equation to diagnose CKD stages according to the Kidney Disease Improvement Global Outcomes (KDIGO) [18, 19]. To assess subclinical CVD, we used risk equivalents, including very high-risk CKD (stage G4 or G5 CKD) and a high predicted 10-year CVD risk based on the Framingham Risk Score [20]. The definitions of CKM stages in this study are detailed in online supplementary Tables S2 and S3.

Depressive Symptoms

The 10-item short form of the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D-10) is a validated self-report tool for screening depressive symptoms in community-dwelling older adults, with high internal consistency and construct validity [21]. In this study, CES-D-10 was used to measure depressive symptoms. It includes items such as being bothered by unusual things, trouble concentrating, feeling depressed, and feeling happy. Respondents reported the frequency of each item during the past week on a four-point scale (0: rarely or none of the time; 3: most or all of the time). Items 5 (“feeling hopeful about the future”) and 8 (“feeling happy”) were reverse-scored before analysis. The summed scores range from 0 to 30, with higher scores indicating more severe depressive symptoms. A cutoff score of ≥10 was used to indicate an increased likelihood of clinically relevant depressive symptoms [22].

Covariates

Sociodemographic characteristics and health-related factors were included as covariates. These covariates encompassed age, gender, education level (categorized as illiterate, primary school, middle school, high school, bachelor’s degree or master’s degree and above), marital status (categorized as married, separated, or unmarried/divorced/widowed), smoking and alcohol use, daily sleep duration, comorbidities (including dyslipidemia, cancer, chronic lung disease, liver disease, digestive disease, memory-related disease, arthritis, and asthma), history of falls, napping habits, and social engagement.

Statistical Analysis

For cross-sectional analyses, participants were categorized into three groups based on CKM syndrome stages: stages 0–1 (reference group), stages 2–3, and stage 4. Longitudinal analyses assessed changes in CKM stages (ΔCKM stage) from 2011 to 2015. Quantitative data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) and compared using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). Categorical data were reported as percentages and analyzed using chi-square tests. Ordinary least squares (OLS) regression models were used to examine the cross-sectional associations between CKM stages and CES-D-10 scores in 2011, with results expressed as regression coefficients (β) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Similarly, longitudinal associations between ΔCKM stage and ΔCES-D-10 score (2015 score minus 2011 score) were analyzed using OLS regression models. Five models with incremental adjustments were utilized: Model 1 – unadjusted; Model 2 – adjusted for age, gender, education, and marital status; Model 3 – adjusted for variables in Model 2 plus smoking, alcohol use, and sleep duration; Model 4 – adjusted for variables in Model 3 plus comorbidities; Model 5 – adjusted for variables in Model 4 plus history of falls, napping habits, and social engagement. To evaluate potential effect modification, we performed subgroup analyses stratified by age (45–64 vs. ≥65 years) and gender. Interaction effects were formally tested by adding product terms of the CKM exposure variables with each subgroup variable to the full-sample OLS models. We supplemented our analysis with a longitudinal statistical analysis that re-included the sample showing no changes in CKM staging, as well as a cross-sectional data analysis examining whether baseline depressive symptoms were associated with more severe CKM syndrome. The methods and results of these analyses are described in the online supplementary materials. All statistical analyses were conducted using STATA 17.0, with statistical significance set at p < 0.05.

Cross-Sectional Association between CKM Stage and Depressive Symptoms

The mean CES-D-10 scores for the overall study population and participants categorized into CKM stages 0–1, 2–3, and 4 were 8.6 ± 6.4, 8.6 ± 6.3, 8.1 ± 6.2, and 10.7 ± 6.9, respectively (Table 2). Notably, the mean score for individuals at CKM stage 4 exceeded the established threshold for likely depressive symptoms (≥10), with a statistically significant difference observed when compared to other CKM stages (p < 0.001).

As presented in Table 3, in the crude models (Model 1), compared with participants in CKM stages 0–1, those in stages 2–3 showed a marginally lower CES-D-10 score (β = −0.49, 95% CI: −0.83 to −0.15), whereas participants in stage 4 had a significantly higher score (β = 2.09, 95% CI: 1.62–2.55). Overall, the results suggest that advanced CKM stages, particularly stage 4, are associated with increased depressive symptoms. Subgroup analyses based on gender revealed that the association trends between CKM grouping and depressive symptoms in male and female populations were highly consistent with those observed in the overall population across all models (online suppl. Tables S8). In age-based subgroup analyses, after adjusting for covariates, results for the 45–64 age group were highly consistent with and robustly significant compared to the overall population. However, in the ≥65 age group, CKM stage 4 showed a statistically significant association with depressive symptoms only in Model 2 (β = 1.079, 95% CI: 0.085–2.074, p = 0.033), absent in other models. CKM stages 2–3 consistently showed a significant negative correlation with depressive symptoms, consistent with the overall sample (online suppl. Tables S9). Additionally, interaction tests revealed no statistically significant interaction terms between CKM grouping and either gender or age (p for interaction >0.05), indicating no significant gender or age-related heterogeneity in the association between CKM syndrome and depressive symptoms.

Longitudinal Association between CKM Stage and Depressive Symptoms

Among the 1,803 participants with follow-up data from 2011 to 2015, a positive association was observed between ΔCKM stage and ΔCES-D-10 score in the crude model (β = 0.25, 95% CI: 0.02–0.48, p = 0.032). This association remained statistically significant after full adjustment for potential confounders in all multivariable models except Model 2, which did not show overall statistical significance in the F test (Prob > F >0.05). The strongest associations were observed in Models 3 and 5, with β coefficients of 0.31 (95% CI: 0.08–0.55) and 0.31 (95% CI: 0.07–0.54), respectively. These findings suggested that progression in CKM stage over time was associated with an increase in depressive symptoms, independent of baseline characteristics and other covariates (Table 4). In addition, we re-included samples with ΔCKM = 0 and conducted longitudinal analyses based on the direction of CKM syndrome changes, with results presented in online supplementary Table S4.

Discussion

This study provided the first comprehensive analysis of the association between CKM syndrome and depressive symptoms in a nationally representative sample of middle-aged and older Chinese adults. We found that higher CKM stages were significantly associated with increased depressive symptoms at baseline, particularly among individuals classified as CKM stage 4, whose mean CES-D-10 score exceeded the threshold for severe depressive symptoms. Moreover, longitudinal analyses revealed that progression in CKM stage over a 4-year period was independently associated with an increase in depressive symptom severity, even after adjusting for multiple sociodemographic and health-related confounders. These findings highlight CKM syndrome not only as a marker of multisystem physiological dysfunction but also as a potential contributor to mental health deterioration.

Our cross-sectional results aligned with previous evidence indicating that individual components of CKM syndrome – such as CVD, CKD, and metabolic disorders – were each independently associated with elevated risks of depression [14–16, 23–25]. However, our findings extended this understanding by demonstrating that when these conditions coexist within the unified construct of CKM syndrome, their collective impact on mental health may be amplified. The strongest association was observed in CKM stage 4, which included individuals with established clinical CVD and CKD, suggesting that advanced systemic vascular and metabolic pathology may exert greater influence on neurobiological pathways implicated in depressive symptoms. Given that similar studies in other populations have yielded different association patterns [5, 26], the negative results for CKM stages 2–3 observed in the OLS regression of cross-sectional analysis may stem from limitations in the data sample. The specific population of middle-aged and elderly Chinese individuals in CHARLS, particularly in wave 1 (2011), may exhibit distributional biases that could explain the negative results.

Subgroup analysis further confirmed the robustness of the cross-sectional findings. In the elderly subgroup, the varying associations between CKM stage 4 and depressive symptoms across different models suggest that depressive symptoms are susceptible to confounding factors such as lifestyle and comorbid conditions. Concurrently, reduced sample size may also impact statistical significance. The significantly strengthened association between CKM stage 4 and depressive symptoms in the younger subgroup may be attributed to the psychosocial pressures, disease awareness levels, and healthcare-seeking behaviors characteristic of this population – younger participants may be more sensitive to CKM-related somatic symptoms and more prone to negative emotions arising from disease-related quality-of-life impacts.

Longitudinal analyses further supported a feed-forward mechanism: worsening CKM health over time predicted increases in depressive symptom severity. This suggested that CKM progression may serve as a modifiable risk factor for mental health decline, or that addressing CKM syndrome could potentially slow the deterioration of depression symptoms. Such relationships underscore the importance of integrated care models that simultaneously address both physical and psychological health.

Potential mechanisms underlying this association included shared pathophysiological pathways such as chronic low-grade inflammation, oxidative stress, dysregulation of the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, and insulin resistance – all of which were hallmarks of both CKM syndrome and major depressive disorder [5, 27–29]. For example, metabolic disturbances such as hyperglycemia, hypertriglyceridemia, and hypoglycemia may lead to insulin resistance, which in turn affects neurotransmitter synthesis and signaling in the brain [27]. Psychosocial factors, including social isolation, caregiving stress, and reduced health literacy, may further exacerbate this comorbidity [5], particularly among older adults in China, where stigma surrounding mental health remains high, and treatment rates remain low [30, 31].

Our findings are consistent with recent studies using other large-scale datasets. For instance, Huang et al. [5] using the UK Biobank, reported that deteriorating CKM health was associated with increased odds of clinically diagnosed depression. Similarly, Zhou et al. [32] found that higher cardiometabolic indices were linked to greater depressive symptom burden in the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), whereas Yu et al. [33] found an elevated risk of depression in patients with cardiorenal syndrome. Our results reinforced these observations in a non-Western setting and extended them to a longitudinal design, thereby strengthening causal inference.

These findings have important clinical and public health implications. They suggest that depression screening should be routinely incorporated into the management of patients with CKM syndrome, especially those in more advanced stages. Conversely, interventions targeting lifestyle modification, inflammation, and metabolic homeostasis may have dual benefits in improving both physical and mental health outcomes. Given the rising prevalence of CKM syndrome in aging populations worldwide, integrating mental health care into existing chronic disease management frameworks may help reduce the overall burden of disability and mortality associated with this syndrome.

The innovation of this study lies in three key aspects. First, it is the first to apply the AHA-defined CKM staging framework to investigate its relationship with mental health outcomes in an Asian population. Most prior studies have focused on individual components of CKM syndrome, such as diabetes or CKD, rather than examining their combined impact within a unified clinical construct. Second, we utilized data from the CHARLS, a high-quality, nationally representative cohort of aging adults, enabling both cross-sectional and longitudinal analyses with rigorous control for confounding variables. Third, we introduced a dynamic perspective by examining how changes in CKM health status over time were linked to trajectories of depressive symptoms – offering novel insights into the interplay between physical and mental health within the context of multimorbidity.

There are several limitations to this study. First, although we adjusted for multiple potential confounders, some important factors – such as physical activity and dietary habits – were not included in our analysis, which may result in residual confounding. Second, in longitudinal analyses, we used changes in CKM stage as the main independent variable. However, equal numerical changes (e.g., from stage 0 to 1 vs. stage 3 to 4) may represent different clinical implications, limiting the precision of this approach. Third, data were derived from the CHARLS database, which relies on self-reported information and is, therefore, subject to recall bias. We used a CES-D-10 score ≥10 to indicate elevated depressive symptoms; although this cutoff has demonstrated good sensitivity, it does not replace a clinical diagnosis and may lead to misclassification. Additionally, proteinuria data were unavailable in the dataset, so kidney function was assessed solely based on estimated glomerular filtration rate, which may limit the accuracy of renal staging. Finally, the CHARLS cohort mainly included middle-aged and older adults, limiting generalizability to younger populations or the broader Chinese population. Participants with missing data on key variables were also excluded, potentially introducing selection bias. Despite these limitations, our findings offered meaningful insights into the association between CKM syndrome and depressive symptoms in a nationally representative sample of Chinese adults.

Conclusion

Our study demonstrated a significant association between CKM syndrome and increased depressive symptoms among middle-aged and older Chinese adults, with the strongest effect observed in CKM stage 4. Longitudinal findings suggested that worsening CKM health was associated with greater depressive symptom severity. These findings support the need for integrated care approaches that address both physical and mental health in patients with CKM syndrome to improve overall outcomes.
