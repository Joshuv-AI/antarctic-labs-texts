Research progress on the molecular mechanism and clinical evidence of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists in improving sarcopenia
Authors: Qun Yang, Yuting Wang, Ge Gao, Haitao Li, Yu Gao, Jianqiu He
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Sarcopenia is an age-related syndrome characterized by a progressive and extensive decline in skeletal muscle mass and function, leading to serious impairment of the quality of life and poor health prognosis in older adults. As the global population ages, sarcopenia has become a serious public health challenge. Currently, effective treatments for sarcopenia are still very limited. Glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) are gaining increasing attention as first-line agents for the treatment of diabetes and obesity, and with their potential to improve the muscle mass and function, they provide new ideas for the treatment of sarcopenia. This study aims to review the latest research progress in the application of GLP-1RAs in the management of sarcopenia, analyze its key molecular pathways, and explore its role in insulin resistance, mitochondrial function, inflammation, protein metabolism balance, autophagy, and the activation of related signaling pathways. Meanwhile, this article focuses on evaluating the evidence of the current effects of GLP-1RAs on patients’ body composition, muscle mass and function in clinical trials, deeply explores the “dual effects” and heterogeneity of its efficacy, and analyzes the translational dilemma existing between preclinical positive discoveries and clinical outcomes.Furthermore, for the elderly population, this article further discusses the tolerance and nutritional risks faced during the treatment of GLP-1RAs. Further combining nutrition and exercise intervention, the strategies of GLP-1RAs in the comprehensive management of sarcopenia were explored.Finally, this paper points out the future research directions in this field, emphasizing the need for more well-designed and high-quality clinical studies to clarify the exact role, suitable population and long-term safety of GLP-1RAs in the prevention and treatment of sarcopenia.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is currently mainly defined based on the international consensus (EWGSOP2), and it is an age-related, progressive systemic skeletal muscle disease characterized by a decline in muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical function [1]. According to the EWGSOP2 consensus, the diagnostic pathway for sarcopenia begins with screening for muscle strength (such as grip strength tests). For those with low strength, further assessment of muscle mass (such as bioelectrical impedance analysis and dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry) is conducted, and the severity is defined by physical manifestations (such as walking speed and the Brief Physical Condition Scale) [2, 3]. Furthermore, sarcopenia must be distinguished from the two related but distinct concepts of “frailty” and “cachexia”. Frailty is a more extensive clinical syndrome involving a decline in physiological reserves in multiple systems, and sarcopenia is regarded as one of the key pathophysiological components constituting its phenotype [4]; Cachexia is a multifactorial metabolic syndrome, characterized by weight loss and skeletal muscle loss caused by underlying diseases such as cancer, heart failure and renal failure [5]. Sarcopenia may coexist with cachexia (such as in some patients with heart failure), but it can also occur as an independent disease entity [6], which emphasizes the necessity of specialized identification and assessment of sarcopenia. Sarcopenia, a disease associated with old age, is characterized by a progressive loss of muscle mass and function and is strongly associated with adverse health outcomes such as fractures, decreased function, and death. Although sarcopenia affects mainly older adults, it is also common in patients with cancer, metabolic disorders, and liver and kidney diseases [7]. The prevalence rate of sarcopenia is affected by race, sex, lifestyle, and environmental factors. Epidemiology shows that the incidence rate of sarcopenia increases continuously with age. In recent years, epidemiological studies of sarcopenia in European populations have shown that 10%-16% of older adults worldwide suffer from myopenia [8]. Muscle health problems are particularly prominent among Asian patients with type 2 diabetes, with 61% of the patients showing early signs of sarcopenia, 23% being diagnosed with sarcopenia, and 12.1% progressing to severe sarcopenia [9], placing a heavy financial burden on individuals, families, and public health systems. At present, the main treatment methods for myopenia include nutritional intervention, resistance training, drug therapy, and comprehensive management strategies [10]. These treatments have several limitations regarding their clinical application.

In recent years, glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists (GLP-1RA) have garnered interest as being essentially insulin-stimulating hormone analogues. Their core mechanism of action is to inhibit glucagon secretion and regulate body weight. Notably, the clinical application of GLP-1RAs has gradually expanded beyond its original scope. Currently, it is frequently used in the treatment of obesity and type 2 diabetes, and has also started to be involved in intervention research on related diseases such as sarcopenia [11]. GLP-1RAs exert key physiological effects by activating the GLP-1 receptor. An increasing number of studies have found that these drugs not only regulate blood sugar level and appetite, but may also have a positive effect on muscle health by regulating the dynamic balance between muscle protein synthesis and degradation. Preclinical studies have confirmed that GLP-1RAs can significantly reduce body fat accumulation and improve insulin sensitivity, which provides a new potential strategy for the treatment of sarcopenia [12]. Furthermore, Some preclinical studies have shown that GLP-1RAs can significantly improve muscle mass and increase muscle strength, thereby enhancing the quality of life of mice with muscle atrophy. These research results provide strong support for the application of GLP-1RAs in the clinical transformation of sarcopenia [13]. However, the clinical application of GLP-1RAs still requires a cautious attitude. Some published studies have pointed out that while such drugs help patients lose weight, there is a risk of loss of muscle mass. Therefore, particularly in older patients with sarcopenia who already have muscle atrophy problems, pretreatment risk assessment and the formulation of personalized treatment plans are crucial [14].

The pathogenesis of sarcopenia is complex and diverse, involving the interaction of multiple pathophysiological processes. Its mechanism encompasses insulin resistance (IR), chronic inflammation, oxidative stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and protein metabolism imbalance(see Figure 1). This study aims to review the molecular mechanisms and clinical application of GLP-1RAs in improving sarcopenia, so as to provide references for future research and clinical practice.

Molecular mechanism of GLP-1RAs in improving sarcopenia

Alleviation of insulin resistance

IR is one of the core mechanisms in the pathogenesis of type 2 diabetes mellitus, which leads to decreased glucose uptake and utilization in skeletal muscle. In the state of IR, the phosphatidylinositol-3-kinase/protein kinase B (PI3K/AKT) pathway is inhibited, which reduces signaling to mammalian target of rapamycin (mTOR), thus decreasing muscle protein synthesis; whereas, the ubiquitin-proteasome system (UPS) and autophagy-lysosomal pathways are activated, accelerating muscle protein degradation [15].

GLP-1RAs improve IR primarily by enhancing the muscle cell sensitivity to insulin. Preclinical studies have shown that liraglutide and semaglutide protect skeletal muscles from obesity-induced muscle atrophy via the sirtuin 1 (SIRT1) pathway in muscle tissue and myotubes of C2C12 cells and are associated with improvement of impaired glucose tolerance and IR [16]. Both exenatide and metformin in streptozotocin-induced diabetes models were effective in relieving hyperglycemia in diabetic mice and antagonizing diabetes-induced muscle weight loss, grip weakness, and changes in muscle fiber cross-sectional area distribution [17]. In vitro studies have shown that liraglutide intervention can dose-dependently increase the expression of insulin signaling pathway-related proteins such as GLUT-4 and p-Akt after the induction of IR in L6 muscle cells in a high lipid environment simulated by palmitic acid (PA), thus effectively improving the insulin sensitivity of L6 cells [18]. Studies in animals have found that GLP-1 mediates 5’ adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) phosphorylation by activating GLP-1R signaling, thereby regulating skeletal muscle remodeling and enhancing exercise tolerance, while glycogen synthesis, glucose uptake, type I muscle fiber proportion, and mitochondrial biosynthesis are enhanced in the skeletal muscles of GLP-1-AAV treated mice [19].

Inhibition of the inflammatory reaction

An increase in the levels of inflammatory factors such as cytokines (tumor necrosis factor-alpha [TNF-α], interleukin [IL]-6, and IL-1β) and chemokines (CCL2 and CXCL1) reflects the chronic inflammatory state of the body, and directly participates in the metabolic regulation of muscles and development of sarcopenia. A cross-sectional observational study has shown that high levels of IL-6 in the elderly are significantly associated with the occurrence of sarcopenia, especially under high inflammatory conditions, where the concentration of IL-6 is positively correlated with the loss of muscle strength and mass [20]. A meta-analysis showed that TNF-α levels were significantly associated with declined muscle strength (grip strength, gait speed) and reduced muscle mass in older patients with sarcopenia. Furthermore, a strong correlation was observed between elevated TNF-α values and the deterioration of muscle function in the population aged ≥ 75 years, indicating its direct involvement as an inflammatory driver in the progression of sarcopenia [21]. In addition, the increased levels of IL-1β were also associated with loss of muscle mass, and its mechanism could be achieved by regulating the apoptosis of muscle cells and inhibiting the proliferation of muscle cells [22].

These inflammatory cytokines can activate multiple signaling pathways, including the nuclear factor (NF)-κB, Janus activated kinase/signal transducer and activator of transcription (JAK/STAT), and p38 mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK) pathways; disrupt the balance between skeletal muscle protein synthesis and degradation; enhance skeletal muscle proteolysis; and inhibit protein synthesis, ultimately leading to skeletal muscle atrophy [23]. Inflammatory signaling pathways (such as NF-κB and JAK/STAT) play a central role in the pathogenesis of muscular atrophy. The NF-κB pathway is activated by pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α and IL-6 [24], enhancing transcriptional activity through receptor-mediated downstream signaling cascades, inducing the expression of E3 ubiquitin ligase genes atrogin-1 and MuRF1, thereby driving muscle protein degradation via the UPS [25].

Existing relevant studies have confirmed that GLP-1RAs can inhibit the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway through a variety of mechanisms, thereby exerting their anti-inflammatory effects. Specifically, these mechanisms of action cover multiple aspects, such as activation of the cAMP-PKA signaling pathway, inhibition of pro-inflammatory cytokine release, and also activation of the AMPK signaling pathway, promoting the expression of anti-inflammatory genes, and regulation of macrophage function [26]. Consequently, in the animal model studies of polymyositis and C protein-induced myositis, GLP-1RA PF1801 demonstrated significant anti-inflammatory effects. It could effectively reduce the levels of key pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6, and high mobility group box protein 1, thereby effectively alleviating the inflammatory response in muscle tissue [27]. Further research has revealed that GLP-1RAs can significantly reduce the expression of various inflammatory factors associated with the occurrence of diabetic sarcopenia. For instance, in the db/db mouse model, the levels of inflammatory factors such as IL-1β, IL-6, CCL2, and CXCL1 in the muscle tissue of mice all decreased significantly after treatment with GLP-1RA dulaglutide. The reduction of these factors is considered to be one of the key mechanisms by which GLP-1RAs exert a protective effect by inhibiting inflammatory responses [12]. Meanwhile, animal experiments have shown that duraglutide reduces the infiltration of F4/80 + macrophages and MPO+ neutrophils in muscle tissue and improves its microenvironment [12]. In the high-fat diet-induced sarcopenic obesity model, the intervention of semaglutide showed significant anti-inflammatory effects. Semaglutide significantly downregulates the expression levels of key pro-inflammatory factors such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β in the gastrocnemius muscle, while effectively improving IR, as evidenced by a significant reduction in the Homeostatic Model Assessment of Insulin Resistance index [28]. Similarly, semaglutide inhibits the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation mediated by NF-κB/myostatin in the gastrocnemius tissue of diabetic mice with chronic liver disease models, and enhances myogenesis mediated by heat shock factor-1 (HSCF-1). This improves gastrocnemius atrophy in chronic liver disease combined with the diabetes mouse model. Specifically, the drug effectively inhibits the activation of the NF-κB signaling pathway, leading to significant reduction in the mRNA expression levels of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, ultimately delaying the progression of muscle atrophy [29].

Improvement of mitochondrial dysfunction

In the pathophysiological process of sarcopenia, mitochondrial homeostasis imbalance is a core link, and its manifestations are mainly reflected in the following aspects. Firstly, in terms of mitochondrial biogenesis, the mitochondrial function of patients with sarcopenia is significantly inhibited. Specifically, it is manifested as a decrease in the mitochondrial DNA content, a reduction in the activity of electron transport chain complexes (such as complexes I and II), and a weakened ability to generate ATP, a key product of cellular energy metabolism [30]. This series of changes is mainly driven by a significant reduction in the expression of the key transcriptional coactivator, peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor γ coactivator − 1α (PGC-1α). The down-regulation of PGC-1α further inhibits the expression of downstream factors such as nuclear respiratory factor 1 and mitochondrial transcription factor A, thereby damaging the mitochondrial biosynthesis process, which is often accompanied by an increase in the oxidative stress levels [31]. Moreover, the mitochondrial dynamic balance is disrupted, manifested as an imbalance between mitochondrial fusion and mitochondrial division processes. Relevant studies have found that in sarcopenia, the expression of mitochondrial fusion proteins (such as MFN1/2 and OPA1) decreases, while the expression of mitochondrial fission proteins (such as DRP1) relatively increases. This alteration in the protein expression profile leads to an excessive fragmentation of the mitochondrial network, damaging its normal function [32]. In addition, once the mitochondria are damaged, the oxidative stress within the cell increases, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) rise. These ROS can damage the cell structure, cause cell death and dysfunction, and ultimately exacerbate muscle atrophy [33]. Moreover, mitochondrial dysfunction can affect the function of the endogenous antioxidant system, reducing the cell’s ability to resist oxidative stress, thus forming a vicious cycle and accelerating muscle atrophy [34]. Preclinical studies have also pointed out that AMPK activates SIRT1 by up-regulating the NAD+ levels, which in turn promotes the deacetylation of SIRT1 and activates PGC-1α, thereby forming the AMPK-SIRT1-PGC-1α signaling axis. Mitochondrial dysfunction plays a key role in the occurrence and development of skeletal muscle atrophy mainly through the molecular mechanism mediated by this signaling axis [35].

GLP-1RAs has demonstrated multi-target regulatory effects in improving sarcopenia-related mitochondrial dysfunction. One of its core mechanisms lies in the activation of the AMPK/PGC-1α signaling pathway. GLP-1RAs can effectively enhance the activity of AMP kinase, thereby upregulating the expression of PGC-1α which is the main regulatory factor of mitochondrial biosynthesis. GLP-1RAs comprehensively enhance the efficiency of energy metabolism in skeletal muscle cells by increasing the quantity and area of mitochondria and improving their morphology (such as reducing swelling) [36]. Particularly, GLP-1 activates the AMPK signaling cascade by binding to skeletal muscle GLP-1R, thereby promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and enhancing mitochondrial function. This is specifically manifested by increased mitochondrial DNA content, upregulation of related gene expression, improvement of the oxidative metabolism, and elevation of the expression levels of key proteins involved in oxidative phosphorylation [26]. These changes significantly improved the energy metabolism efficiency of muscle tissue, ultimately enhancing exercise endurance. In addition, the GLP-1R-mediated AMPK phosphorylation pathway can also optimize the muscle fiber types, improve glycogen storage and glucose uptake, further synergistically enhancing the athletic performance of skeletal muscle [19]. In a high-fat diet-induced model of lean obesity, semaglutide showed significant improvement in gastrocnemius pathomorphology. A histological analysis showed that the drug not only increased the number and area of mitochondria, but also increased the ratio of type I/type II muscle fibers, total muscle fiber area, and muscle fiber density [28]. The optimization of mitochondrial function and structure is consistent with the common mechanism by which GLP-1RAs improve sarcopenia by increasing mitochondrial area and quantity, and optimizing their morphology [36].

Regulation of protein metabolism balance

In sarcopenia, the imbalance between protein synthesis and degradation is a core mechanism. Synthesis is primarily hindered due to the inhibition of the mTOR signaling pathway (manifested as downregulation of PI3K/Akt/mTORc1 activity), leading to reduced protein synthesis [37]. Degradation enhancement is caused mainly by the activation of the UPS (such as the increased expression of key factors MuRF1 and atrogin-1) and the autophagy-lysosome system (ALS), which together accelerate muscle protein degradation under oxidative stress [38]. Therefore, the downregulation of mTOR limits synthesis, while the upregulation of UPS and ALS exacerbates degradation, collectively leading to muscle loss.

GLP-1RAs play a key role in regulating muscle protein synthesis and improving muscle atrophy by activating the Akt/mTOR signaling pathway. This pathway is the core hub for regulating cell growth, proliferation, and survival. Specifically, GLP-1RAs activate the downstream Akt signal by binding to the GLP-1 receptor. On the one hand, activated Akt activates downstream mTOR, directly promoting protein synthesis; on the other hand, Akt signaling downregulates the expression of myostatin by inhibiting the binding of NF-κB to the myostatin promoter. In addition, GLP-1RAs can effectively upregulate the expression of myogenic regulatory factors MyoD and MyoG, while inhibiting the transcription of key ubiquitin-ligases MAFbx and MuRF1 in muscle atrophy. Through the above-mentioned multi-target and multi-pathway synergistic effects, GLP-1RAs ultimately promote the growth and repair of muscle tissue and effectively alleviate muscle atrophy [39].

In the dexamethasone-induced muscle atrophy model, exenatide had a significant anti-atrophy effect, and its core function is the activation of the Akt signaling pathway. Specifically, this activation effect is mainly reflected in two aspects: it further activates the downstream mTOR molecules through the Akt-mTOR axis, directly promoting the synthesis of muscle proteins and inhibits the binding of the myostatin promoter, thereby reducing the expression of myostatin and ultimately alleviating muscle atrophy. The combined effect of these two aspects ultimately alleviates the phenomenon of muscle atrophy [40]. In the muscle atrophy model induced by a high-fat diet (HFD), the two GLP-1RAs liraglutide and semaglutide, effectively improved the state of muscle atrophy by regulating the balance of muscle protein metabolism. At the catabolic level, they directly inhibit the myoprotein degradation process mediated by the ubiquitin-proteasome pathway by downregulating the expression of two core E3 ubiquitin ligases, atrogin-1 and MuRF-1. At the anabolic level, it provides a strong impetus for muscle protein synthesis by activating the AKT/mTOR pathway and its downstream effector molecules (such as p70S6K). In conclusion, these two drugs precisely maintain the homeostasis balance of muscle proteins through a dual regulatory mechanism of inhibiting decomposition and promoting synthesis, thereby exerting significant anti-atrophy effects [16]. Further research has more deeply revealed the network of action of semaglutide. By inducing the cAMP-mediated activation of PKA and AKT, semaglutide not only inhibits the ubiquitin-proteasome degradation pathway mediated by myostatin, but also enhances the myogenesis process mediated by HSCF-1 [29].

Promotion of autophagy mechanism

The basic activity level of autophagy in muscle tissue is directly related to the maintenance of muscle health, since autophagy is one of the core protective mechanisms for cells to maintain their own homeostasis. In fact, basal autophagy provides an important guarantee for the normal physiological functions of muscle cells by continuously clearing damaged protein molecules and organelles within the cells. In disease conditions, such as sarcopenia, the regulatory role of autophagy is particularly prominent. Research has found that autophagy can exert protective effects through multiple pathways, such as enhancing the regenerative capacity of satellite cells, reducing oxidative stress damage, and inhibiting abnormal inflammatory responses, etc. These effects work together to combat muscle atrophy [41]. For example, the autophagy regulatory factor TP53INP2 can effectively promote the mitochondrial autophagy process, thereby reducing the accumulation of ROS within the cells and ultimately improving the related pathological phenotypes associated with sarcopenia [42]. Autophagy provides multiple protective mechanisms against sarcopenia by enhancing the regenerative potential of muscle stem cells and myoblasts, alleviating oxidative damage, regulating immune cells, and inhibiting inflammatory responses [43].

The ULK1 complex acts as a key signaling hub, mediating stress-induced AMPK signaling transduction, regulating the mitochondrial autophagy process through phosphorylation [44]. In skeletal muscle tissue, mitochondrial autophagy is mainly regulated through two independent but possibly synergistic pathways: one is the classical ubiquitination pathway mediated by PINK1/Parkin; the other is the receptor-mediated non-ubiquitination pathway [44]. AMPK initiates autophagy by directly activating ULK1, and promotes the autophagy process by inhibiting mTORC1 to relieve its negative regulation on autophagy [45]. In the animal model of sarcopenic obesity (SO), activating the AMPK/PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy pathway can effectively regulate glucolipid metabolism and improve skeletal muscle atrophy, thereby repairing the mitochondrial structure and function in muscle tissue [46]. In the animal model of denervation muscular atrophy, Sestrin2 (SESN2) effectively promotes mitochondrial autophagy by facilitating the formation of autophagosomes and accumulation of p62 on the mitochondria. Its mechanism of action is reflected in two aspects: first, SESN2 regulates the AMPK/mTORC1 pathway; second, the knockdown of SESN2 leads to a reduction in the translocation of p62 to the mitochondria and decreases its phosphorylation level at critical sites (such as Ser403), which is necessary for the encapsulation of the mitochondria by autophagosomes. In summary, SESN2 plays a key role in promoting mitochondrial autophagy [47].

The FoxO transcription factor family plays a prominent role in muscle physiological regulation, especially the FoxO3a subtype, which plays a key role in muscle autophagy, metabolic regulation, and sarcopenia occurrence by regulating the expression of autophagy-related genes (such as LC3 and BNIP3) [48]. Notably, FoxO1 is mainly responsible for regulating the expression of genes related to energy metabolism, while FoxO3a is more inclined to dominate the regulation of the genes related to autophagy and cell survival. There is an antagonistic interaction between FoxO1 and FoxO3a, which is crucial for the fine regulation of the balance between autophagy and protein degradation. This interaction prevents excessive autophagy from causing cell damage and further coordinates metabolic processes when the body is under stress. Through such synergistic cooperation, the two jointly enhance the autophagic activity of the cells [49]. Preclinical studies have confirmed that in the PA-induced L6 cell muscular atrophy model, liragutide plays a key role in promoting autophagy through the SESN2-mediated AMPK/Akt/mTORC1 pathway. Silencing SESN2 expression can lead to the downregulation of autophagy-related protein expression in L6 myotube cells. Liraglutide treatment can upregulate SESN2 expression and activate autophagy, thereby effectively preventing PA-induced myotube atrophy in L6 cells [18].

In summary, different GLP-1RAs improve skeletal muscle atrophy through multiple molecular mechanisms, involving multiple pathways such as insulin resistance regulation, inflammation suppression, and mitochondrial function improvement (see Table 1), providing a theoretical basis for the clinical treatment of sarcopenia with GLP-1RAs.

Clinical evidence of the effect of GLP-1RAs on skeletal muscle mass

Clinical effects of GLP-1RAs on body composition and lean body mass

Multiple clinical studies have confirmed that glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists have significant effects in reducing overall weight and fat mass. However, during the weight loss process, lean body mass (including muscle) shows heterogeneity of increase, decrease, and remain unchanged in different clinical studies. It is worth noting that lean body mass is a comprehensive indicator that includes water in muscle, organs, bone, body fluids and adipose tissue. Therefore, its changes cannot precisely reflect the changes in skeletal muscle mass [50]. The reduction in lean body mass does not entirely equate to a decrease in skeletal muscle mass. This conceptual distinction to some extent explains the differences among various research results [50].

In clinical practice, the influence of GLP-1RA on sarcopenia-related indicators shows a “dual effect” and high heterogeneity. On the one hand, some clinical evidence supports that GLP-1RA can improve body composition and muscle function. For instance, the SEMALEAN study evaluated the impact of semaglutide on obese patients. The results showed that after 12 months of treatment, the total fat mass of the patients decreased by 18%, the decline in lean body mass stabilized after the initial appearance of treatment, and grip strength improved significantly. The prevalence of sarcopenic obesity dropped from 49% at baseline to 33% [51]. Observational evidence suggests that although absolute lean body mass has decreased, relative lean body mass (as a percentage) may be maintained, and muscle mass (such as reduced intramuscular fat infiltration and improved insulin sensitivity) may be enhanced. This is regarded as an adaptive process of optimizing body composition [50]. A pilot study on elderly obese diabetic patients also showed that semaglutide effectively reduced body fat while maintaining the percentage of skeletal muscle and the proportion of limb muscle [52]. A 26-week prospective study demonstrated that oral semaglutide can reduce fat mass while preserving fat-free body mass and skeletal muscle mass [53].

On the other hand, concerns about the reduction of lean body mass (including muscle) caused by GLP-1RA still exist, and there is significant heterogeneity in the results among different studies. A longitudinal and retrospective study on elderly patients with type 2 diabetes reported that long-term use of semaglupeptide was associated with decreased grip strength and accelerated sarcopenia, raising concerns that GLP-1RAs may have adverse effects on neuromuscular health [54]. Multiple meta-analyses further revealed this complexity: A meta-analysis on the body component modification effect of GLP-1 receptor agonists showed that compared with the control group, GLP-1RA users not only had a significant reduction in fat body mass, but also a more obvious reduction in lean body mass (weighted mean difference was − 1.02 kg) [55]; A systematic review and network meta-analysis found that GLP-1RAs, while reducing total weight and fat mass, led to a decrease in lean body mass (mean difference − 0.86 kg), with the loss accounting for approximately 25% of the total weight loss [14]; However, there are also studies that have reached different conclusions. A meta-analysis of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus complicated with sarcopenia indicated that GLP-1RA had no significant effect on lean body mass and skeletal muscle index [56]; Another meta-analysis of patients with type 2 diabetes, overweight or obesity also found that when oral hypoglycemic drugs, insulin or lifestyle interventions were used as controls, there was no significant difference in the effect of GLP-1RAs on lean mass and lean mass percentage [57]. Although GLP-1 receptor agonists often lead to a decrease in fat mass and lean body mass during weight loss, recent clinical trials have confirmed that this does not necessarily have a negative impact on functional capacity. The results of the STEP-HFpEF trial showed that for obese patients with HFpEF, although treatment with Semaglutide led to a reduction in lean body mass, the 6-minute walk distance was significantly improved [58].

Influencing factors and transformation dilemmas of GLP-1RAs skeletal muscle efficacy differences

The reasons for this difference in efficacy are complex, mainly involving two key dimensions: the dose effect of the drug and the baseline status of the patients. First, there is the dose effect; existing evidence indicates that there is a clear dose-response relationship for the weight loss effects of GLP-1RAs. Multiple studies have confirmed that six drugs, including liraglutide and semaglutide, have all demonstrated significant dose-dependent weight loss effects [59]. This relationship is also reflected in its impact on muscle mass. Some hold the view that low and medium doses of GLP-1RAs may have muscle-protective potential, while high doses are more often accompanied by a decrease in muscle mass. However, the drug-specific mechanism of action and the precise dose-response relationship still need to be further clarified [60]. Secondly, there is the influence of the underlying disease status and combined medication. For instance, a retrospective observational study on hospitalized elderly patients with type 2 diabetes found that patients treated with GLP-1 receptor agonists combined with basal insulin had an increase in limb skeletal muscle mass, while those treated only with basal insulin showed a slight decrease, suggesting that under specific clinical management conditions, GLP-1 receptor agonists may help maintain muscle mass [61]; However, another meta-analysis of patients with type 2 diabetes mellitus complicated with sarcopenia showed that GLP-1 receptor agonists had no significant effect on lean body mass and skeletal muscle index [56]. Overall, the effect of GLP-1RAs on muscle mass shows a high degree of heterogeneity in clinical trials: some studies report that lean body mass reduction accounts for 40% to 60% of total weight loss, while others indicate that the proportion of lean body mass reduction is approximately 15% or lower [50]. The potential causes of this heterogeneity mainly include population characteristics, drug specificity, the intensity of lifestyle intervention, and the impact of comorbidities [50].

Preclinical studies have revealed the positive effects of GLP-1RAs on muscles. A meta-analysis showed that GLP-1RAs could increase lean body mass, cross-sectional area of muscle fibers and grip strength in animal models, enhance the expression of muscle growth factors, and inhibit genes related to muscle atrophy and inflammatory factors [62]. However, in clinical trials, the significant weight loss brought about by GLP-1RAs is usually accompanied by an absolute decrease in fat-free body mass or skeletal muscle mass [55]. The main reason for this difference lies in the disparity between preclinical research and clinical research, that is, the contradiction that GLP-1 receptor agonists show muscle-protective effects in animal models but are accompanied by fat-free body weight loss in humans. This contradiction mainly stems from the essential differences in research subjects, intervention conditions and physiological backgrounds [60]. First, the heterogeneity between the model and the population is a key factor: preclinical studies are often conducted in animal models of a single disease (such as Diabetes Mellitus or senility), and the intervention dosage and duration are often optimized to highlight the muscle-preserving effect [60]; In contrast, the subjects of clinical trials are complex patient populations, particularly elderly individuals with comorbid sarcopenia, obesity, or multiple metabolic diseases, whose muscle metabolic foundation is more fragile [63]. Secondly, the focus of clinical assessment varies. Animal experiments can directly measure grip strength, muscle cross-sectional area, and specific molecular markers. The primary focus of human studies lies in overall body weight and overall metabolic indicators. Changes in body composition (such as absolute reduction in fat-free body mass) are usually regarded as secondary outcomes, and in clinical practice, they may not be adequately matched with muscle-preserving support measures such as protein intake or resistance exercise. The above factors jointly lead to the transformation predicament of GLP-1RAs in the clinical application of improving sarcopenia.

The challenges and potential risks faced by GLP-1RAs in clinical practice

With the rapid expansion of the clinical application scope of GLP-1RAs, especially among the elderly and those at risk of sarcopenia, its potential limitations and risks are receiving increasing attention. Firstly, the issue of tolerance cannot be ignored. Elderly patients are more sensitive to the common gastrointestinal side effects of GLP-1RAs, such as nausea and vomiting. This not only may lead to treatment interruption but also may increase the risk of malnutrition and dehydration caused by reduced intake [64]. It is worth noting that for elderly people who are not obese or on the verge of sarcopenia, excessive weight loss may accelerate the progression of sarcopenia and increase the risk of falls and poor prognosis. At the same time, drug-induced appetite suppression may lead to insufficient intake of protein and key trace elements, further deteriorating the muscle metabolic environment. At present, for high-risk groups of sarcopenia with complex conditions such as chronic kidney disease, liver disease or inflammatory bowel disease, there is still a lack of relevant evidence-based evidence, which makes it difficult for clinicians to precisely balance the significant metabolic benefits and potential risks to muscle health and tolerance when prescriming [65]. Therefore, future clinical studies need to include more elderly patients, with a focus on outcomes such as body composition, muscle function, and long-term quality of life. In current clinical practice, emphasizing the combination of adequate protein intake and regular resistance exercise during treatment is a key strategy for monitoring and reducing lean body mass loss and maximizing treatment benefits.

The strategy of GLP-1RAs combined with non-pharmacological therapy for improving sarcopenia

Given that the weight loss effect induced by GLP-1RAs is often accompanied by the risk of muscle loss, and this influence is regulated by multiple factors such as age, underlying diseases, and the duration of treatment, in order to retain muscle mass to the greatest extent, the clinical consensus suggests that GLP-1RA treatment must be combined with active non-pharmacological intervention. GLP-1RAs reduces energy and protein intake by suppressing appetite, which may lead to malnutrition and insufficient muscle protein synthesis. This is particularly dangerous for elderly people who already have a reduced appetite and may exacerbate muscle loss [65]. Observational data show that GLP-1RA users often experience nutritional deficiencies within 12 months. The most common one is vitamin D deficiency, followed by thiamine and other B vitamins, as well as deficiencies of iron, calcium, magnesium and potassium [66]. A cross-sectional study of GLP-1RA users also found that the protein intake of the participants was significantly lower than the daily requirement [67]. Therefore, an enhanced nutritional support strategy must be implemented. Research suggests that a high-protein diet is a key measure to prevent GLP-1RA-related muscle loss [65]. For patients with poor baseline nutritional status or sarcopenia, it is necessary to ensure adequate energy and high-quality protein intake. The recommended protein target is 1.2–1.5 g /kg/d, and protein supplements should be considered when necessary [68].

In addition to nutritional intervention, regular resistance training is equally crucial. Observational data indicate that a comprehensive intervention strategy combining resistance training and aerobic exercise has a significant protective effect on muscle function [69]. A 12-week multicenter randomized controlled trial provided strong evidence that for elderly people with sarcopenia or potential sarcopenia, the intervention of combined muscular-targeted oral nutritional supplements (rich in protein, HMB and vitamin D3) and structured exercise programs (including twin-weekly resistance training and interday chair exercises) compared with the control group It can more effectively improve the lean tissue of the limbs, skeletal muscle index, grip strength, walking speed, chair standing performance and quality of life [69]. This multidisciplinary comprehensive management is of great significance for optimizing muscle health outcomes in patients treated with GLP-1RA.

In conclusion, in the treatment process of improving sarcopenia with GLP-1RAs, clinicians should formulate individualized plans, closely monitor the patient’s weight, nutritional intake and changes in body composition, and regularly assess muscle mass using tools such as ultrasound and bioelectrical impedance analysis, so as to adjust the plan in a timely manner [70, 71]. Future research urgently requires rigorously designed, well-sample-sized and longer follow-up period randomized double-blind trials, especially in the elderly population. These trials should integrate muscle strength outcome indicators into the design and adopt more accurate assessment methods to clearly define the drug dose range suitable for this population and ensure that the weight loss strategy based on GLP-1RA does not harm muscle strength [50, 54].

Conclusions and prospects

In summary, GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) have expanded from being merely metabolic regulation drugs to potential interventions for improving sarcopenia. Existing evidence indicates the potential of GLP-1RAs to improve muscle mass and function through multiple molecular mechanisms. These mechanisms not only involve promoting muscle protein synthesis and inhibiting catabolism, but also cover the regulation of energy metabolism, the improvement of mitochondrial function, the alleviation of inflammatory responses, the regulation of autophagy processes, and the influence on muscle stem cell function. This series of effects jointly promote the improvement of muscle mass and functional recovery, providing a solid theoretical basis and new hope for GLP-1RAs to improve sarcopenia. However, the transformation from molecular mechanisms to clinical applications still faces significant challenges.

Preclinical studies consistently show that GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) have a protective effect on muscles, but in human experiments, their efficacy presents a high degree of “dual effects” and heterogeneity. Although the drugs can effectively reduce weight and improve some metabolic indicators, they are often accompanied by a decrease in fat-free mass, and this contradiction mainly arises from the inherent differences in research models, physiological backgrounds, and intervention conditions. It is worth noting that recent clinical trials (such as STEP-HFpEF) suggest that despite a reduction in lean body mass, patients’ physical functioning (such as exercise endurance) may still significantly improve, indicating that we need to reassess the clinical balance between “muscle mass decline” and “functional benefits.“In clinical practice, the potential risks of GLP-1RAs cannot be ignored, especially in the elderly and those already suffering from sarcopenia. Poor gastrointestinal tolerance and malnutrition caused by appetite suppression, as well as micronutrient deficiencies, may exacerbate muscle loss; For high-risk patients with complex conditions such as concomitant chronic kidney disease and liver diseases, there is currently a lack of sufficient evidence-based data to guide clinical decision-making. Therefore, single-drug therapy may be insufficient to address complex sarcopenia. Combining GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) with adequate protein nutritional support and regular resistance training is a key strategy for maximizing muscle mass retention and offsetting drug side effects through multidisciplinary comprehensive management.

In response to the significant evidence gap in the current field, there is an urgent need to conduct more large-scale, multi-center, long-term follow-up randomized controlled trials (RCT) targeting the elderly and sarcopenia populations. Future research designs should go beyond simple body composition assessments, taking muscle strength, physical performance, and quality of life as core clinical endpoints to clarify the long-term effects of GLP-1RAs on different subgroups. At the same time, it is necessary to deeply analyze the differentiated molecular mechanisms of drugs under different pathophysiological conditions, focusing on exploring the optimal dosing regimen suitable for sarcopenia patients and optimizing combination therapy strategies to maximize muscle protection benefits. At the clinical practice level, it is crucial to develop standardized guidelines for nutrition and physical activity interventions to effectively prevent muscle loss through multidisciplinary comprehensive management. Overall, GLP-1 receptor agonists (GLP-1RAs) have broad application prospects in the management of sarcopenia and related metabolic diseases. The key in the future lies in precisely balancing their dual effects of weight loss and muscle protection, promoting their safe and effective benefits to more patients through individualized treatment.
