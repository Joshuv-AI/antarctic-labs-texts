Assessment of smoking exposure by urine cotinine levels in severe COVID-19 patients: a case-control study
Authors: Medeni Arpa, Bayram Şen, Leyla Kazancıoğlu, Hülya Kılıç
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Coronavirus Disease 2019 (COVID-19), caused by SARS-CoV-2, has posed a significant global public health challenge, with long-term sequelae such as post-COVID-19 syndrome continuing to burden health systems. Tobacco use, a major preventable cause of morbidity and mortality, impairs the immune response and exacerbates respiratory diseases, including COVID-19. Passive smoking, an important but often overlooked public health problem, exposes non-smokers to harmful health risks and may contribute to worse outcomes in respiratory disease. This study aims to investigate the relationship between smoking exposure, including passive smoking, and the severity of COVID-19 using urinary cotinine levels to provide objective insights into a critical public health issue. This prospective observational study was conducted over six months in 2021 in the ICU of a tertiary care hospital. Forty-two patients were divided into four groups based on smoking and COVID-19 status: non-smoker/non-COVID-19, smoker/non-COVID-19, non-smoker/COVID-19, and smoker/COVID-19. COVID-19 diagnosis was confirmed by RT-PCR and/or imaging. Data collected included demographic information, clinical scores [APACHE II, mSOFA, GCS], respiratory function [stPaO2/FiO2 ratio], arterial blood gas parameters and routine laboratory findings. Urinary cotinine levels were measured by ELISA and normalised to spot urine creatinine levels to ensure accuracy. Urine cotinine levels were highest in the smoker/COVID-19 group and significantly higher than in the smoker/non-COVID-19 and non-smoker/COVID-19 groups (p = 0.010 and p = 0.002, respectively). Surprisingly, non-smoker/non-COVID-19 patients also had elevated cotinine levels, suggesting exposure to passive smoking. The stPaO2/FiO2 ratio was significantly lower in smoker/COVID-19 patients (p < 0.001), indicating impaired lung function. This study highlights the detrimental effects of smoking exposure, including passive smoking, on the severity of COVID-19. Elevated cotinine levels in non-smokers/non-COVID-19 patients emphasize the widespread public health impact of passive smoking and highlight the need for stronger policies and interventions to minimize tobacco exposure. Addressing both active and passive smoking is critical to improving public health outcomes and reducing the burden of respiratory diseases such as COVID-19. Further research is essential to validate these findings and to inform evidence-based public health strategies.

Background

COVID-19, caused by the novel coronavirus SARS-CoV-2, was first identified in late 2019 in Wuhan, China. Due to its rapid global spread and significant impact on public health, the World Health Organization (WHO) declared it a pandemic in March 20201. While WHO announced in May 2023 that COVID-19 no longer fits the definition of a Public Health Emergency of International Concern, the pandemic remains a critical public health issue, with long-lasting effects as post-COVID-19 syndrome that continue to challenge healthcare systems worldwide2. Post-COVID-19 syndrome, also referred to as long COVID-19, encompasses a range of symptoms that persist or appear after the acute phase of the disease has resolved. These symptoms include fatigue, respiratory problems, and neurological complications, among others, significantly impacting the quality of life and health of affected individuals. Understanding the factors that exacerbate COVID-19 progression and its long-term effects is vital for improving patient outcomes. Tobacco use is a leading cause of preventable death globally, accounting for more than 8 million deaths annually3. Smoking impairs the immune response, increases susceptibility to respiratory infections, and has been associated with severe outcomes in diseases such as COVID-194,5. Despite evidence linking smoking to worse COVID-19 prognosis, the specific mechanisms remain incompletely understood, highlighting the need for further investigation6,7. Passive smoking, or involuntary exposure to tobacco smoke, poses significant health risks to non-smokers, including increased rates of respiratory diseases and cardiovascular problems. Detecting passive smoking exposure is challenging because of reliance on self-reported data and lack of awareness among exposed individuals. Its role in exacerbating COVID-19 progression is not yet fully elucidated, underscoring the importance of objective measurement methods and deeper investigation8.

Nicotine, the primary addictive substance in tobacco, is metabolized in the liver by the enzyme CYP2A6 to form cotinine, a stable metabolite. Cotinine levels in biological fluids such as blood, saliva, and urine provide a reliable marker for assessing tobacco exposure. Urinary cotinine, in particular, is preferred for detecting low-concentration exposures, including passive smoking, due to its higher concentration compared to other body fluids9,10.

Considering the complex interplay between smoking, passive smoking, and COVID-19 severity, our study aims to evaluate urinary cotinine levels in patients with severe COVID-19 who were hospitalized in the intensive care unit (ICU). This approach seeks to provide robust evidence on the relationship between smoking exposure and COVID-19 outcomes, contributing to a better understanding of the factors influencing disease progression.

Methods

This prospective observational study was conducted over a six-month period in 2021 at the ICU of a tertiary care hospital. A total of 42 patients were included in the study. Patients were categorized into four groups based on their smoking status and COVID-19 status: non-smoker and non-COVID-19, smoker and non-COVID-19, non-smoker and COVID-19, and smoker and COVID-19. Due to the unpredictable and urgent nature of the pandemic, it was not possible to conduct a priori power analysis during the study design phase. However, a posteriori power analysis was performed retrospectively to evaluate the statistical adequacy of the sample size, based on a comparison of urinary cotinine levels among the four subgroups. This analysis yielded a power of 0.857 and an effect size of 0.686, showing that the sample size was sufficient to detect moderate-to-large effects for the primary outcome.

Study Design:

COVID-19 diagnosis was confirmed using reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) positivity and/or computed tomography (CT) imaging findings consistent with COVID-19 pneumonia. Demo-graphic data, including age, gender, and smoking status, were recorded for all participants. Clinical assessments on the day of ICU admission included the APACHE II, mSOFA, and Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) scores. Respiratory function was assessed using the standardized PaO2/FiO2 (stPaO2/FiO2) ratio, calculated with the formula (PaO2+(1.66×PaCO2 − 66.4))/FiO211. Arterial blood gas parameters, including pH, partial pressure of carbon dioxide (pCO2), oxygen saturation (sO2), bicarbonate, and lactate levels, were measured. Urine samples collected on the day of ICU admission were analyzed for cotinine levels using an ELISA kit (Cat. No: E2043Hu, Bioassay Technology Laboratory, Shanghai, China). Cotinine results were normalized using spot urine creatinine values to correct for variations in urinary concentration. Routine laboratory parameters, including urea, estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR), albumin, total bilirubin, direct bilirubin, sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, amylase, and C-reactive protein (CRP), were also analyzed. Complete blood count (CBC) parameters recorded included white blood cell count (WBC), lymphocyte count (LY), monocyte count (MO), neutrophil count (NEU), eosinophil count (EO), basophil count (BA), neutrophil-to-lymphocyte ratio (NLR), red blood cell count (RBC), hemoglobin (HGB), hematocrit (HCT), mean corpuscular volume (MCV), mean corpuscular hemoglobin (MCH), mean corpuscular hemoglobin concentration (MCHC), platelet count (PLT), platelecrit (PCT), platelet distribution width (PDW), and red cell distribution width standard deviation (RDW-SD). The cotinine levels, clinical scores, arterial blood gas parameters, and laboratory findings were compared across the four study groups to evaluate the relationship between smoking exposure and COVID-19 severity.

Statistical analyses were conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics, Version 25.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA). Descriptive statistics were presented as frequencies and percentages (n, %). Continuous numerical variables were reported as mean ± standard deviation or median (min–max) according to their distribution. For comparisons between COVID-19 and non-COVID-19 groups, the Student’s t-test was used for variables with normal distribution, while the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for variables with non-normal distribution. Chi-square test used for categorical variables. ANCOVA analyses followed paired comparisons with Bonferroni correction were used for comparisons between four groups. The presence of any comorbidity was utilized as a confounding variable. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 42 patients were included in the study, 26 (61.9%) with COVID-19 and 16 (38.1%) without COVID-19. The primary hospital diagnoses of the patients are summarized in Table 1. The mean age was 60.9 ± 16.5 years in the COVID-19 group and 69.3 ± 16.5 years in the non-COVID-19 group, with no statistically significant difference between the groups (p = 0.117). Smoking exposure was reported by 10 (38.5%) COVID-19 patients and 6 (37.5%) non-COVID-19 patients, with a similar distribution between groups (p = 0.950). The male/female ratio was 14:12 in the COVID-19 group and 8:8 in the non-COVID-19 group, with no significant differences in gender distribution (p = 0.808).

Discussion

Nicotine, the main addictive substance in tobacco, affects the body by binding to nicotinic acetylcholine receptors (nAChRs), particularly the α7 subtype, which is found in the respiratory tract. When nicotine binds to nAChRs, particularly the α7 subtype found in respiratory epithelial cells, it activates intracellular signalling pathways, such as MAPK/ERK. Activation of this pathway has been shown to increase the expression of ACE2 receptors — the main entry point for SARS-CoV-2 — on the surface of bronchial cells, which might increase susceptibility to viral infection12,13. Additionally, nicotine may contribute to disease progression by promoting abnormal cell growth and reducing energy production in cells14.

Studies have suggested that nicotine may play a role in the progression and severity of COVID-19. It has been shown to enhance viral replication, transcription of viral proteins and the cytopathic effects of SARS-CoV-2 by up regulating ACE2 expression15. In addition, nicotine may contribute to the cytokine storm, a key feature of severe COVID-19, by facilitating viral entry into host cells. Epidemiological evidence also supports these mechanisms; for example, Zheng et al.16 and Baskaran et al.17 reported that smokers, including ex-smokers, had worse clinical outcomes and were more likely to develop pneumonia. Similarly, meta-analyses18,19 have shown that the prognosis of COVID-19 is worse in patients with smoking-related diseases such as COPD, especially when smoking and COPD coexist. Taken together, these findings suggest that nicotine exposure may exacerbate COVID-19 progression.

In contrast, other studies have provided conflicting evidence, suggesting no association or even a potential protective role of nicotine against severe COVID-1920–26. Hypotheses from these studies suggest that nicotine may reduce inflammation via its interaction with nAChRs or inhibit viral binding to ACE2 receptors. Some have even speculated on the therapeutic potential of cotinine, a nicotine metabolite, due to its anti-inflammatory properties and ability to modulate ACE2 expression27,28. However, given the well-established role of nicotine in increasing cardiopulmonary disease and potentially exacerbating viral infections, its therapeutic benefit in COVID-19 remains highly controversial. Our study showed that urinary cotinine levels were the highest in smoker/COVID-19 patients, confirming higher nicotine exposure in this group.

It is important to consider inter-individual and inter-ethnic differences in nicotine metabolism when interpreting serum cotinine levels. The primary metabolic pathway of nicotine is its oxidation to cotinine, predominantly catalysed by the hepatic enzyme CYP2A629. Genetic polymorphisms in the CYP2A6 gene contribute substantially to the variability in enzyme activity, resulting in differences in nicotine clearance rates and, consequently, serum cotinine concentrations independent of smoking intensity30. A comparative study by [reference to study] showed that the distribution of CYP2A6 genotypes and enzyme activity differed significantly between metabolizer genotypes in the population. This is particularly true for carriers of the rapid genotypes, suggesting additional genetic or environmental modifiers31.

In the context of our study population - critically ill COVID-19 patients - metabolic activity may be further altered by systemic inflammation, liver dysfunction or the use of drugs known to inhibit or induce cytochrome P450 enzymes. These factors may obscure the relationship between reported smoking status and measured cotinine levels, making interpretation difficult. Although our study did not include metabolic phenotyping or genotyping, these findings highlight the need to consider genetic and physiological variability in nicotine metabolism, particularly in heterogeneous or critically ill populations.

Interestingly, non-smoker/non-COVID-19 patients also had elevated urinary cotinine levels, comparable with those in the smoker groups. This unexpected finding highlights the potential role of passive smoking as a significant public health problem. Previous studies have highlighted the limitations of relying solely on self-reported smoking status, as this may lead to under-reporting32. Urinary cotinine measurements provide a more objective and reliable assessment of nicotine exposure, which is particularly important when evaluating the effects of smoking or passive smoking on disease progression. While passive smoking remains a plausible explanation, other factors may also contribute to elevated cotinine levels in self-reported non-smokers. Dietary sources, such as vegetables from the Solanaceae family (e.g., tomatoes, potatoes, eggplants), naturally contain small amounts of nicotine, potentially leading to detectable cotinine concentrations33. Environmental contamination, including exposure to residual tobacco smoke on surfaces and clothing (“third-hand smoke”), can also result in measurable levels without direct inhalation34. The elevated cotinine levels in non-smokers/non-COVID-19 patients therefore underscore the importance of considering multiple potential sources of nicotine exposure in both clinical and epidemiological contexts. Public health interventions should aim to reduce all forms of tobacco and nicotine exposure, including second- and third-hand smoke, to minimize their potential impact on respiratory and systemic health outcomes.

In our study group, urinary cotinine levels were significantly higher in self-reported smokers than in self-reported non-smokers, which is consistent with the concentration ranges reported in previous validation studies. Large-scale evaluations have demonstrated that cotinine is a reliable biochemical indicator of smoking status. Chiu et al. found excellent agreement between self-reported smoking status and urinary cotinine concentrations among military conscripts35. Similarly, Chan et al. found biochemical verification among female volunteer soldiers revealed an underestimation of smoking prevalence by 7% compared with self-reporting36. These findings highlight the general reliability of self-reporting while emphasising that under-reporting can occur in certain subgroups. While formal concordance testing between self-reported smoking status and cotinine levels was not the primary aim of our study, the distribution of cotinine concentrations in our cohort mirrors the patterns described in these studies. This provides indirect validation of the self-reported data. This supports the robustness of our classification while acknowledging the limitations of self-reported data.

Consistent with previous research, eosinophil counts were lower in COVID-19 patients compared to non-COVID-19 groups, particularly in smoker/COVID-19 patients. Eosinopenia, possibly due to decreased bone marrow production or increased apoptosis during infection, has been proposed as a marker of severe COVID-1937,38. Although eosinophils have antiviral properties, their precise role in respiratory viral infections remains unclear39,40. Further studies are needed to elucidate their function in the pathophysiology of COVID-19. In COVID-19 patients, metabolic capacity may be further modulated by acute systemic inflammation, liver dysfunction or pharmacological interventions commonly used in intensive care. These factors may lead to either accelerated or impaired cotinine clearance, potentially affecting the accuracy of biomarkers related to smoking exposure.

In accordance with previous findings, our study observed that NLR was higher in COVID-19 patients, although this difference was not statistically significant. Increased NLR has been associated with severe COVID-19 and mortality in meta-analyses41,42. While the results of our study are consistent with these trends, the variability of NLR by age and sex underscores the need for cautious interpretation43. Similarly, CRP levels, which are considered useful markers of bacterial co-infection in COVID-1944, were elevated in the COVID-19 groups but showed no significant differences when stratified by smoking status.

In terms of pulmonary oxygenation, the stPaO₂/FiO₂ ratio was significantly lower in smokers with COVID-19, indicating impaired lung function in this group. This parameter is critical for assessing oxygenation in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) and predicting mortality45–47, and a significant reduction in this parameter suggests substantial impairment in gas exchange efficiency. While the conventional arterial blood gas parameters (pH, pCO₂, sO₂, bicarbonate and lactate) did not differ significantly between groups, smokers — particularly those with SARS-CoV-2 infection — tended to exhibit lower median oxygen saturation and bicarbonate levels, as well as higher lactate levels, compared to non-smokers. While these trends did not reach statistical significance, they may reflect early or subclinical disturbances in pulmonary gas exchange capacity, ventilation–perfusion mismatch or altered metabolic compensation in the context of severe lung injury. Some studies examine the effects of smoking exposure on pulmonary function and exercise capacity. Öztürk et al.48 highlight the negative impact of passive smoking on pulmonary function tests and increased urine cotinine levels, indicating exposure to secondhand smoke. Adatia et al.49 conducted a large retrospective cohort study of 29,441 subjects, finding that long-term smoking exposure is associated with reduced expiratory flow rates, impaired diffusion capacity (DLCO), and decreased maximum power output during exercise. The dissociation observed between the markedly reduced stPaO₂/FiO₂ ratio and the relatively preserved arterial blood gas values can be explained by the standardisation method used for stPaO₂, which adjusts PaO₂ for PaCO₂, thereby revealing subtle deficits in oxygen transfer that PaO₂ or sO₂ values alone may not detect. In patients with COVID-19, particularly smokers, microvascular endothelial injury, alveolar-capillary membrane thickening and intrapulmonary shunting can impair oxygen diffusion without necessarily causing immediate or proportional changes in pH, PaCO₂ or lactate. This pathophysiological pattern highlights the stPaO₂/FiO₂ ratio’s sensitivity in detecting clinically relevant, yet otherwise concealed, hypoxaemia in this population.

Comorbidities and other confounding factors can have a significant impact on outcomes, particularly in critically ill COVID-19 patients. These patients often have multiple health problems that can affect disease progression and response to treatment. For example, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), hypertension, diabetes and cardiovascular disease can all exacerbate the severity of COVID-19 and lead to a poorer prognosis50. Multiple studies have demonstrated a significant association between cotinine levels, reflecting active or passive tobacco exposure, and DM risk. In never-smokers, elevated serum cotinine levels have been linked to a higher prevalence of DM51. Large Korean cohort studies have also confirmed that smoking, as verified by cotinine levels, increases the risk of developing type 2 DM, with the risk increasing in proportion to the amount smoked52. Secondhand smoke exposure, particularly among women, has also been associated with an increased risk of DM, proportional to the duration and frequency of exposure. Pathophysiological studies suggest that there is ethnic variation in the link between cotinine and insulin secretion, with high cotinine levels being associated with reduced β-cell function in white and black populations, but not in Hispanic populations. Cotinine-verified smoking status provides more accurate risk estimates than self-reporting, thereby reducing misclassification bias53–55. In diabetic populations, serum cotinine has been correlated with lipid atherogenic indices, suggesting broader metabolic implications56. In adolescents, however, the association between cotinine and HbA1c was inconsistent, although male-specific effects were observed. Biomarkers such as urinary cotinine N-oxide have been linked to the accumulation of advanced glycation end products, thereby reinforcing the mechanistic ties between nicotine exposure and glycemic and vascular damage57,58. Overall, the evidence supports the use of cotinine as a robust biomarker for assessing tobacco-related metabolic risk, highlighting the importance of both active and passive smoking in the pathogenesis of DM.

Cotinine-verified smoking status showed an inverse association with hypertension prevalence in Korean adults, particularly in men59,60. However, female smokers were more likely to have hypertension61. Secondhand smoke exposure, measured by urinary cotinine levels, was positively associated with increased blood pressure in females62. New and continuous secondhand smoke exposure increased the risk of new-onset hypertension in never-smokers63. Vitamin D deficiency and tobacco smoke exposure were independently associated with hypertension risk, with a potential synergistic effect64. Higher cotinine levels were associated with worse cognitive performance, particularly in the Digit Symbol Substitution Test, regardless of hypertension or diabetes status65. These findings highlight the complex interplay between smoking, hypertension, and cognitive function.

Recent studies have highlighted the detrimental effects of smoking and nicotine exposure on cardiovascular health. Urine cotinine levels can predict coronary artery calcification, encouraging smokers to quit66. Smoking activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, contributing to atherosclerosis through increased inflammation and oxidative stress67. The Multi-Ethnic Study of Atherosclerosis found associations between smoking and increased inflammatory markers, carotid intima-media thickness, and abnormal ankle-brachial index68. Serum cotinine, a biomarker for nicotine exposure, is linked to adverse cardiovascular outcomes in reproductive-aged women, including chronic hypertension, metabolic syndrome, and dyslipidemia69. Nicotine and its metabolite cotinine may exacerbate atherosclerosis by increasing MAPK signaling, inflammation, and oxidative stress, leading to vascular smooth muscle cell senescence and unstable plaque formation70. Collectively, these findings emphasise the multifaceted role of nicotine exposure, as indicated by cotinine levels, in causing vascular injury and atherosclerotic disease. This highlights the importance of targeted interventions to reduce both active and passive smoking in order to mitigate cardiovascular risk.

Recent studies have highlighted the significant association between smoking and chronic kidney disease (CKD). Tobacco metabolites such as serum cotinine and hydroxycotinine show stronger correlations with CKD risk than self-reported smoking status71–73. Elevated levels of these metabolites have been linked to a decreased glomerular filtration rate, increased albuminuria and a higher risk of CKD in smokers74. The relationship between smoking and renal function appears to be dose-dependent, with a greater risk of CKD associated with heavier smoking75. However, age and ethnicity may influence this relationship, with younger individuals and certain ethnic groups showing stronger associations (74). Smoking cessation has been proposed as an effective way to prevent kidney disease progression, especially among younger people74,75. COPD and smoking are closely linked, with cotinine serving as a reliable biomarker for tobacco exposure76,77. Studies have shown that higher cotinine levels are associated with decreased fractional exhaled nitric oxide (FeNO) in COPD patients78. Incentive-based interventions have shown potential in promoting smoking abstinence among COPD patients79. Genetic factors play a role in smoking behavior and COPD, with variants in genes like UGT2B10 and CHRNA3 associated with cotinine levels and exhaled carbon monoxide, respectively80. Cotinine measurements provide additional risk-discriminative information for lung cancer compared to self-reported smoking alone81. Nicotine, acting through nicotinic receptors, contributes to addiction, COPD, and lung cancer development82. Establishing reliable cut-off values for smoking biomarkers in COPD patients remains an area for further research83. These findings emphasize the multifactorial role of nicotine exposure, as measured by cotinine, in the development and progression of COPD and its associated comorbidities. Integrating biomarker-based assessments into clinical practice could improve risk stratification, inform personalised treatment plans and ultimately enhance patient outcomes.

In addition, medications used to treat these comorbidities can alter metabolic processes, such as drugs that inhibit or induce CYP450 enzymes, which can affect nicotine metabolism and complicate accurate interpretation of cotinine levels. It is therefore important to consider these health and treatment factors when interpreting the results of our study. In the future, a more detailed analysis of how comorbidities and treatment regimens affect cotinine levels and other biomarkers will be important for a clearer understanding of the observed effects. This would increase the precision and generalisability of the results. And also, given the growing awareness of post-COVID-19 syndrome as a long-term consequence, future studies should examine the link between nicotine exposure and post-COVID-19 outcomes to improve our understanding of the long-term effects of smoking and passive smoking on recover.

Limitations and public health implications.

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was relatively small, which may limit the generalizability of our findings. Second, comorbidities and the use of different treatments in hospitalized patients may have influenced the results. Third, a notable limitation of our study is the lack of detailed data on smoking history (e.g. pack-years, daily cigarette consumption). As most participants were critically ill ICU patients, direct self-report was not possible and information on smoking status had to be obtained from family members or close relatives. Consequently, the available data did not allow a reliable assessment of the extent of smoking exposure or correlation analyses with parameters, such as cotinine levels or oxygen saturation. This limitation may have affected the depth of interpretation of smoking-related biomarkers. The elevated urinary cotinine levels in non-smokers/non-COVID-19 patients highlight the importance of considering passive smoking exposure, which was not directly assessed in this study.

From a public health perspective, our findings underscore the need for stricter regulations and interventions to reduce passive smoking exposure, especially in health care settings and homes. The observed discrepancies between self-reported smoking status and urinary cotinine levels underline the importance of objective biomarkers in assessing nicotine exposure. Future studies should prioritize larger sample sizes and provide a detailed assessment of both active and passive smoking exposure to better understand their impact on COVID-19 progression and outcomes.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our study highlights the significant impact of nicotine exposure on COVID-19 progression, with elevated urinary cotinine levels in smoker/COVID-19 patients indicating increased nicotine exposure. Despite conflicting evidence in the literature, our findings suggest that nicotine may exacerbate the severity of COVID-19 through its effects on ACE2 expression and the cytokine storm. In addition, the unexpected cotinine levels in non-smokers/non-COVID-19 patients point to the risks of passive smoking, which should be addressed by public health interventions.

Given the limitations of the study, including the lack of detailed smoking history, future research should focus on larger cohorts and better assessment of both active and passive smoking exposure. Objective biomarkers, such as urinary cotinine, may provide more accurate data and should be prioritised in future studies to better understand the relationship between smoking and COVID-19 outcomes.
