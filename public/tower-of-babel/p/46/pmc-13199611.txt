Eosinophilic Depletion with Benralizumab, Mepolizumab, and Depemokimab: A Modeling Study
Authors: Pradeep B Lukka, Robert C Penland, Ari Brekkan, Rami Ayoun Alsoud, Sherri So, David W Boulton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeAnti-interleukin (IL)-5/IL-5 receptor therapies are an integral part of the management of patients with severe eosinophilic asthma. We modeled and compared the blood eosinophil (bEOS) depletion response following indicated dosing regimens with benralizumab, mepolizumab, and depemokimab.MethodsPharmacokinetic (PK)/pharmacodynamic (PD) models were developed to describe antibody concentration and bEOS count in adults. Two PK/PD models were developed using digitized bEOS data reported for depemokimab and mepolizumab, respectively, and one PK/PD model was based on individual PK parameters and predicted bEOS data with benralizumab. Subjects were assumed to receive either benralizumab 30 mg every 4 weeks (Q4W) for the first three doses, then every 8 weeks, mepolizumab 100 mg Q4W, or depemokimab 100 mg every 6 months.ResultsAt 12 weeks, the predicted median bEOS depletion from baseline was 99.5% (90% prediction interval [PI]: 98.3–99.8) for benralizumab, 76.1% (90% PI: 47.6–89.0) for mepolizumab, and 82.0% (90% PI: 65.0–91.6) for depemokimab. Similar median bEOS depletion rates were predicted at 24 weeks: 99.3% (90% PI: 97.2–99.8) for benralizumab, 76.3% (90% PI: 48.3–89.3) for mepolizumab, and 80.7% (90% PI: 63.2–90.2) for depemokimab. The greater reduction in bEOS count achieved with benralizumab, compared with mepolizumab and depemokimab, was predicted to be sustained through 48 weeks of continued treatment.ConclusionThese PK/PD model simulations predict that benralizumab results in consistently higher bEOS depletion than mepolizumab or depemokimab at 12 and 24 weeks. These results are dependent on the quality of the models and digitized data utilized; other models and parameter estimates may produce different results.Keywords: asthma, human, interleukin-5, monoclonal antibodies, pharmacodynamics, pharmacokinetics

Introduction

Anti-interleukin-5 (IL-5)/IL-5 receptor (IL-5R) therapies are an integral part of managing severe eosinophilic asthma (SEA). Benralizumab and mepolizumab are currently approved as add-on maintenance treatments for SEA;1 these are humanized monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) that target the IL-5 pathway, inhibiting downstream signaling cascades, albeit through differing mechanisms of action.2 Benralizumab acts as a competitive inhibitor of IL-5 by binding with high affinity to the α subunit of the IL-5R (IL-5Rα); this interaction leads to apoptosis through antibody-dependent cell-mediated cytotoxicity, resulting in rapid and near-complete blood eosinophil (bEOS) depletion,2 as well as depletion of eosinophil precursors in the bone marrow.1 Mepolizumab binds with high affinity to circulating IL-5, preventing its interaction with the IL-5Rα on eosinophils and leading to their reduced production, activation, and survival.

Benralizumab and mepolizumab are approved as add-on maintenance treatments for patients aged ≥6 years with SEA in the USA; in the European Union area, mepolizumab is approved for the same age group, whereas benralizumab is approved for adults only.1 Additional indications for benralizumab (https://www.fasenrahcp.com/) include treatment of eosinophilic granulomatosis with polyangiitis (EGPA) in adults,3 and it is also in development for the treatment of hypereosinophilic syndrome in those aged ≥12 years.4 For mepolizumab, additional indications include treatment for chronic rhinosinusitis with nasal polyps, eosinophilic chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or EGPA in adults, and hypereosinophilic syndrome in patients aged ≥12 years (https://www.nucala.com/).5 In addition, depemokimab, an ultra-long-acting anti-IL-5 mAb, has recently been approved as an add-on maintenance treatment, with dosing every 6 months (Q6M), for SEA in patients aged ≥12 years.6

The population pharmacokinetics (PK) and pharmacodynamic (PD) activity of benralizumab and mepolizumab have been described in pediatric and adult patients with SEA.7,8 However, head-to-head comparisons of the reduction in bEOS counts achieved with these therapies are lacking. Depemokimab has been shown to have substantially higher potency than mepolizumab for inhibition of IL-5-mediated proliferation in a human cell line (expressing IL-5R) and basophil degranulation.9 These anti-IL-5/IL-5R therapies warrant comparison at their indicated therapeutic dosing regimens in terms of the eosinophil depletion response. Here, we compared the bEOS depletion response following indicated dosing regimens with benralizumab, mepolizumab, and depemokimab.

Methods

PK/PD models were developed to describe the mAb concentrations and reductions in bEOS counts, respectively. Benralizumab PK was described by a two-compartment model that included a first-order absorption and elimination pathways from the central volume of distribution, and the PD model was a semi-mechanistic bEOS count model that included a proliferation compartment, four transit compartments, and a circulating bEOS compartment. The benralizumab PK/PD model was based on individual PK parameters and bEOS data of benralizumab.10 The depemokimab PK model was a one-compartment model with first-order absorption and elimination, as described in a Phase 1 study;11 the PK/PD model was developed using digitized, dose-dependent geometric mean bEOS counts from that study. The mepolizumab PK model was a two-compartment PK model with first-order absorption and elimination; the PK/PD model was developed using digitized mean data from clinical study reports available online and published information.7,12,13 The final models for both mepolizumab and depemokimab were indirect response, one-compartment models, with the drug effect described with an inhibitory sigmoidal-maximum effect function. We excluded reslizumab from our analysis, as it is less commonly used in clinical practice due to intravenous administration requirements.

Adult body weights were simulated from an empirical normal distribution with a 78.79 (standard deviation [SD] 19.64) kg mean but truncated at 40 kg to avoid unrealistically low weights. A total of 1000 body weights were sampled from the resulting distribution. The sampled body weight distribution was combined with individual parameter estimates from the developed population PK models for the three therapies; subsequently, individual predicted plasma concentrations were simulated for all subjects. Subjects were assumed to receive either benralizumab 30 mg every 4 weeks (Q4W) for the first three doses, then every 8 weeks (Q8W); mepolizumab 100 mg Q4W; or depemokimab 100 mg Q6M. Individual predicted bEOS counts were simulated for all subjects using the developed PK/PD models.

Results

Predicted reductions in bEOS count profiles with benralizumab, mepolizumab, and depemokimab treatment are summarized in Figure 1. In general, considerable variation in bEOS geometric means were predicted post-treatment initiation with mepolizumab, unlike with benralizumab and depemokimab. However, the bEOS geometric mean with benralizumab was predicted to be maintained with continued treatment with very minimal rebound between doses, in contrast to mepolizumab and depemokimab.

Predicted bEOS count profiles of 1000 subjects after standard dosing regimens of benralizumab (30 mg Q4W for the first 3 doses, then Q8W), mepolizumab (100 mg Q4W), and depemokimab (100 mg Q6M), with placebo included for reference. The shaded regions are the 90% prediction intervals, and the lines are the geometric means of the data.

Abbreviations: bEOS, blood eosinophil; PI, prediction interval; Q4W, every 4 weeks; Q6M, every 6 months; Q8W, every 8 weeks.

At 12 weeks, the predicted median bEOS depletion from baseline was 99.5% (90% prediction interval [PI] 98.3−99.8%) for benralizumab, 76.1% (90% PI 47.6−89.0%) for mepolizumab, and 82.0% (90% PI 65.0−91.6%) for depemokimab. Similar median bEOS depletion rates were predicted at 24 weeks: 99.3% (90% PI 97.2−99.8%) for benralizumab, 76.3% (90% PI 48.3−89.3%) for mepolizumab, and 80.7% (90% PI 63.2−90.2%) for depemokimab. The greater reduction in bEOS count achieved with benralizumab, compared with mepolizumab and depemokimab, was predicted to be sustained through 48 weeks’ continued treatment.

Discussion

Our modeling analysis predicts that the dosing regimen for benralizumab (30 mg Q4W for the first three doses, then Q8W) would result in rapid and near-complete bEOS depletion within the first 4 weeks of treatment, with bEOS depletion maintained with continued treatment and very minimal rebound in bEOS counts between Q8W doses, in contrast to mepolizumab (100 mg Q4W) and depemokimab (100 mg Q6M). These predictions are consistent with previous studies that showed benralizumab depleted bEOS counts to undetectable levels and to a greater extent than mepolizumab.14 It should be noted, however, that these simulations were limited to adults and may therefore not be fully representative of the pediatric population or adults with very low body weight, as body weight simulations were truncated at 40 kg.

The rapid and near-complete depletion of bEOS with benralizumab is likely due to its different mechanism of action compared with mepolizumab and depemokimab; specifically, benralizumab directly targets the IL-5Rα on eosinophils rather than targeting circulating IL-5.1,14 Thus, some rebound in bEOS counts between mepolizumab doses, continuing with each cycle of treatment, is predicted. Similarly, some rebound in bEOS counts between depemokimab doses over the 6-month dosing interval is predicted, although to a lesser extent than that seen between mepolizumab doses. These predictions are also consistent with previous observations that depemokimab has higher potency than mepolizumab for inhibition of IL-5-mediated proliferation.9

The clinical implications of the predicted differences in bEOS depletion between benralizumab, mepolizumab, and depemokimab observed in our analysis are unclear. Studies comparing the clinical benefits of benralizumab and mepolizumab are conflicting. Some studies suggest that benralizumab is more effective in reducing exacerbations and improving forced expiratory volume in 1 second,15 whereas others suggest that mepolizumab is associated with greater reductions in the rate of clinically significant exacerbations in patients with similar bEOS counts.16 Another study, utilizing a Bayesian network meta-analysis approach, suggested minimal differences in efficacy and safety between the two treatments in SEA.17

The correlation between the level of bEOS reduction and control of SEA remains to be fully determined, despite the generally accepted association between eosinophilic airway inflammation and the risk of exacerbations.14 However, data from a previous study with mepolizumab showed that about half of the exacerbations while on treatment were eosinophilic, which may be due to incomplete eosinophil depletion contributing to ongoing eosinophilic inflammation.18 In contrast, another study demonstrated that exacerbations while on benralizumab treatment were not eosinophilic, possibly due to its near complete depletion of eosinophils in blood and airways.19 Prospective head-to-head trials would be required to assess whether the difference in mechanisms and eosinophil reduction translate into meaningful differences in clinical outcomes.

Conclusion

In conclusion, these PK/PD simulations predict that treatment with benralizumab results in consistently higher bEOS depletion than treatment with mepolizumab or depemokimab. These results are dependent on the quality of the models and digitized data utilized; other models and parameter estimates may produce different results.
