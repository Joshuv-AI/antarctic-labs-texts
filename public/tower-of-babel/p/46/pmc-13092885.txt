How Are Deprescribing Clinical Practice Guidelines Disseminated and Implemented? A Cross‐Sectional Study of International Organisational Stakeholders
Authors: Chun Hei Justin Cheng, Danijela Gnjidic, Stephanie Mathieson, Barbara J. Farrell, Lisa M. McCarthy, Wade Thompson, Carl R. Schneider
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundDeprescribing guidelines support safe medication cessation and complement clinical practice guidelines. However, limited evidence exists on the strategies used by organisations and policy bodies to disseminate and implement such guidelines and their impact. The study aimed to identify dissemination and implementation practices of deprescribing guidelines across international organisations.MethodsAn explanatory mixed‐methods design was used, comprising (a) a content‐validated 44‐item survey comprising components based on the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance framework and (b) qualitative interviews with survey respondents that provided deeper insights.ResultsOf the 75 unique visitors, 47 respondents from 14 countries provided analysable data (24 completed responses); five participated in a follow‐up interview. Whereas 12 out of 27 respondents reported organisational tailoring of guidelines for local context, 18 out of 24 reported a lack of planning for implementation, dissemination and/or evaluation. Nineteen out of 24 respondents reported no monitoring of implementation outcomes. Although only three respondents reported provision of ongoing training and support, 25 out of 32 believed that guideline uptake could be improved.DiscussionAlthough deprescribing guidelines have gained international attraction, organisations frequently overlook the need for implementation evaluation and the sustainment of implementation. Without attention to these aspects, organisations risk undermining their efforts to implement deprescribing guidelines.

Background

Polypharmacy, the concurrent use of multiple medications, is a growing concern globally. It is particularly prevalent among older adults, with studies indicating that the global prevalence of polypharmacy in the general population is 37% and even higher among older individuals [1]. This increase in medication use raises the complexity of treatment and the risk of adverse events and harms [2]. Studies show that 20% of all unplanned hospital admissions result from medication‐related adverse effects, with half being potentially preventable [3, 4]. Clinical practice guidelines (CPGs) are tools to support effective clinical practice through guiding clinicians in optimising patient care by providing evidence‐based recommendations [5]. While the development of CPGs aims to mitigate low‐value care and overtreatment, the increasing prevalence of multimorbidity has complicated therapeutic treatments. Conventional CPGs are also typically developed with a focus on a single disease state or condition, and the combined effect of multimorbidity and adhering to individually rational recommendations from multiple guidelines can potentially negatively impact both healthcare professionals and patients [6]. Well‐intentioned clinicians relying on conventional CPG recommendations to make clinical decisions may be inadvertently hindered and may lead to suboptimal care [7].

One significant barrier to reducing or stopping medications is that conventional CPGs often lack detailed recommendations on when and how to safely withdraw inappropriate medications. To address this challenge, evidence‐based deprescribing guidelines have been developed. Deprescribing is the process of dose reduction or withdrawal of an inappropriate medication with the goal of managing polypharmacy and improving outcomes [8]. These guidelines provide detailed recommendations for safely reducing and withdrawing inappropriate medications and are designed to complement conventional CPGs, particularly with respect to minimising polypharmacy and enhancing the quality use of medicine. Over the last decade, six medication‐class–specific deprescribing guidelines, as listed on the Deprescribing Guidelines Repository (deprescribing.org), have been published in Australia and Canada [9, 10, 11, 12, 13, 14].

To facilitate the implementation of these guidelines into practice, a variety of knowledge mobilisation tools have been created to accompany them. These include summary algorithms, patient pamphlets, infographics, whiteboard YouTube videos and a mobile app [15, 16]. These tools aim to support clinicians in making informed decisions about deprescribing and to enhance the uptake of the guidelines in clinical practice. Despite these efforts, the uptake of CPGs in practice often faces barriers, leading to discrepancies between evidence‐based recommendations and clinical practice [17]. Recent research has explored the factors impacting the effectiveness of conventional CPGs. One proposed explanation for why guidelines are not well adopted in practice stems from the failure of guidelines reaching or being adopted by their intended end‐users [18].

Deprescribing guideline development has gained considerable attention as clinicians and policymakers recognise the harms associated with overuse of medications that may no longer be required or may be causing harm [19]. Whereas deprescribing guidelines have been developed to support clinicians in safely reducing and discontinuing medications, the strategies for disseminating and implementing these guidelines have not been evaluated. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate current dissemination and implementation practices for deprescribing guidelines across international organisations.

Methods and Materials

Study Design

An explanatory sequential mixed‐methods design and approach was adopted and employed, including (a) a survey questionnaire to explore the dissemination and implementation efforts led by organisations for deprescribing guidelines and to determine their impact [20, 21, 22] and (b) follow‐up interviews with survey participants to further explore their personal experience and opinions on the dissemination and implementation process of specific deprescribing guidelines. The study was conducted in accordance with the Basic & Clinical Pharmacology & Toxicology policy for experimental and clinical studies [23], and reporting of the survey was carried out in accordance with the Consensus‐Based Checklist for Reporting of Survey Studies (CROSS).

Survey Questionnaire

An anonymous online survey was created using Research Data Electronic Capture (REDCap version 14.1.4) software hosted by The University of Sydney. Survey data collection was conducted over 12 months (27 November 2023 to 27 November 2024). We administered a previously content‐validated survey (see File S1: Appendix B) [20], containing 39 items spanning the five components of the Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, and Maintenance (RE‐AIM) framework and 5 items capturing respondent characteristics [20, 21]. The RE‐AIM framework is a widely used implementation model designed to facilitate the translation of research into practice [24]. The framework outlines five key dimensions for translating research into practice: reaching the intended population, assessing effectiveness, promoting adoption by staff and systems, ensuring consistent and cost‐effective implementation with necessary adaptations and sustaining intervention effects over time at both the individual and organisational levels [21]. The RE‐AIM framework was used to facilitate a deeper understanding of organisational implementation and dissemination practices and their impact [20]. The validated survey instrument was developed through two rounds of content validation performed by a panel of clinicians, researchers and implementation experts (n = 19). A panel of experts rated relevance and importance for individual survey items; 39 items (75%) were met in the first round, and 44 out of 48 items (92%) were met in the second round [20].

The targeted sampling frame consisted of organisations involved in endorsement, dissemination, implementation, modification or translation of the six currently published deprescribing guidelines. The recruitment inclusion criteria consisted of individuals who were part of 42 organisations that had previously engaged with deprescribing.org with respect to translation and/or use of deprescribing guidelines (list can be found in File S1: Appendix C) [9, 10, 11, 13, 14, 25]. A purposive sampling technique was utilised. The survey was initially distributed via targeted email invitations by the research team, and broader outreach was conducted via newsletters and social media shared through international deprescribing networks and researcher accounts on X (formerly Twitter). A final‐week reminder was posted on social media to conclude recruitment.

Ethics approval for this study was granted by The University of Sydney's Human Research Ethics Committee, Sydney, Australia (Project Number 2021/660). All data collection was voluntary, de‐identified and managed securely. No incentives were offered to participants. Survey data were collected through REDCap and analysed using descriptive statistics in SPSS (version: 29.0.1.0 [171]). Both complete and incomplete responses were eligible for descriptive data analysis, with incomplete responses treated as missing data and reported with survey data in File S1 (Appendix D: Complete Survey Responses). As only descriptive data analysis was completed, all data captured were analysed, regardless of the status of completion for any entries. Results are reported for prespecified primary survey items aligned with the study aim and RE‐AIM domains; full survey branching and conditional logic are documented in File S1 (Appendix E).

Follow‐Up Interviews

A follow‐up interview guide (see File S1: Appendix F) was developed by the research team in collaboration with experienced health professionals and researchers, prior to recruitment for interviews. Upon conclusion of the online survey data collection, participants who had indicated interest and provided contact details for a follow‐up interview were approached. Individual semistructured interviews were conducted between 1 August 2024 and 31 August 2024. The semistructured interview questions were informed by the original survey questions and refined using insights from survey responses. We obtained informed consent from all interview participants prior to the interview commencement. There were no relationships established between the interviewer (CHJC) and the participants prior to study commencement. Participants were informed that CHJC conducted this study in his position as a Doctor of Philosophy (PhD) student from The University of Sydney.

Interviews were arranged individually with respondents and lasted up to 30 min over conferencing software (Zoom) and were audio recorded, transcribed verbatim and de‐identified. Transcripts were analysed using inductive content analysis, as outlined by Elo and Kyngäs [6]. The analysis was conducted by CHJC and reviewed by the broader research team to reach a general consensus. The process began with open coding, and initial codes were then transferred and categorised as per RE‐AIM to further understand the survey responses.

Results

Of the 75 unique visitors recorded, a total of 47 participants provided analysable data (24 complete responses and 23 incomplete responses), giving a response rate for completing the questionnaire of 32%, as shown in Figure 1. These countries spanned multiple WHO regions, including the Americas (e.g., Canada, the United States, Brazil and Colombia), Europe (e.g., the United Kingdom, Belgium, France, Germany, Ireland, Italy, Netherlands, Portugal, Spain and Switzerland) and the Western Pacific (Australia). Key survey findings presented in the main text were those that directly addressed the study aim of identifying dissemination and implementation practices of deprescribing guidelines across international organisations and were most relevant to the RE‐AIM domains used to structure the analysis. All other item‐level responses are provided in File S1 (Appendix D: Complete Survey Responses). All eight respondents who indicated interest in a follow‐up interview were contacted. Three participants declined the interview or did not respond. Five respondents went on to complete the interview.

Perceived opportunities to improve participation in the uptake of deprescribing guidelines.

The majority of survey respondents identified themselves as holding positions in the organisations as either a practitioner/clinician (21 out of 47) or a researcher/educator (16 out of 47). When asked about the types of organisations involved in endorsing, disseminating, modifying or translating deprescribing guidelines, respondents identified that healthcare services (20 out of 47) and universities (17 out of 47) were the two predominant contributors, with government and other organisations playing a lesser role (shown in Table 1).

From the survey findings, many (25 out of 32) respondents believed that participation in the update of deprescribing guidelines could have been improved. However, interviewees raised concerns that health professionals face common barriers, such as limited formal support, fragmented interprofessional communication and insufficient time to assimilate the guidelines into practice. Adoption

Survey respondents indicated that all six deprescribing guidelines had been adopted and disseminated within their respective organisations. The most frequently adopted guidelines were deprescribing guidelines for benzodiazepine receptor agonists (18 out of 28), followed by proton pump inhibitors (16 out of 28) and antipsychotics (12 out of 28). Overall, 19 out of 27 respondents described the overall success of deprescribing guideline dissemination and implementation within their organisation as either positive or acceptable, whereas five respondents reported negative experiences and three were unsure.

Implementation

When survey participants were asked who the target population for the deprescribing guidelines dissemination/implementation efforts was, 26 out of 34 respondents stated that physicians were the primary audience, followed by pharmacists, with 22 out of 34 respondents selecting them. Only 9 out of 34 respondents believed that health service users were the primary target population, as shown in Figure 2. Other potential deprescribing guideline stakeholders (e.g., nurses, carers and care staff and allied health professionals) were perceived to be less important target populations, as rated by respondents. This aligns with the feedback received during interviews, where respondents highlighted the critical role of primary care, viewing it as best positioned to manage long‐term patient care effectively.

Interviewees expressed the need for systematic methods, such as digital tracking, to assess the effectiveness of guideline dissemination, implementation and usage. Interviewees commented that there were potential informal tracking systems but noted the need for policy changes and dedicated personnel.

Maintenance

Fourteen out of 24 survey respondents reported a lack of planning for implementation, dissemination or evaluation by their organisation. Further, six respondents stated that their organisation had a plan. The survey revealed significant gaps in monitoring and evaluation of deprescribing guidelines within organisations. Specifically, 19 out of 24 respondents reported that their organisation did not monitor the outcomes of the strategies used. Despite this, there was a notable commitment to ongoing education and support, with three respondents reporting that their organisations provided continuous training and support on deprescribing guidelines. The survey also highlighted a shortfall in monitoring of guideline uptake at the individual level, with only 2 out of 24 respondents reporting that their organisations monitored guideline uptake.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first study to use the RE‐AIM framework to gain an understanding of real‐world dissemination, implementation practices and evaluation of deprescribing guidelines. The indicated engagement across 17 different countries suggests that the guidelines have been well perceived and are considered relevant across various contexts, despite being primarily developed by Australia and Canada, as reflected in the literature [26]. This global uptake aligns with what both survey respondents and interview participants reported, who consistently described active efforts to implement deprescribing guidelines within their organisations and to adapt them to their local context. Survey findings identify physicians as the primary target audience for deprescribing efforts. This aligns with interview findings collected after survey collection that emphasise the key role of primary care physicians in managing long‐term medication use. In contrast, only a quarter of respondents identified health service users as a target audience, reflecting a missed opportunity to engage patients and informal carers in deprescribing efforts. Greater patient involvement, such as empowering individuals to participate in medication‐related decisions, was perceived by participants to improve guideline adherence and outcomes [27]. By actively involving patients in their medication‐related decisions, healthcare providers can foster a sense of ownership and responsibility among patients, leading to better adherence to deprescribing guidelines.

The findings revealed that healthcare services (e.g., primary care health professional organisations) and universities were the primary organisations endorsing and disseminating deprescribing guidelines. These sectors play a critical role in increasing the reach of deprescribing efforts, consistent with existing literature that highlights their influence in promoting clinical guidelines [28]. However, the limited involvement of government and other stakeholders suggests untapped potential for expanding reach through the public sectors and policymakers. For example, interviewees reflected on the importance of government endorsement in improving the integration of deprescribing into national policies, with one participant explicitly calling for the government to endorse deprescribing guidelines.

The findings indicate that respondents reported a range of dissemination and implementation practices, although the extent to which these varied across organisations could not be accurately determined from the available data. Common methods include electronic distribution and seminars (i.e., structured educational sessions or training opportunities); however, the broader literature favours multifaceted approaches, which tend to be more effective strategies [29]. Interviewees also described limited educational resources, fragmented interprofessional communication and insufficient time as frequent barriers to implementation. They reiterated the importance of continuous professional development and interprofessional collaboration as potential solutions for overcoming these challenges. Creating a supportive organisational culture that prioritises deprescribing was identified as a key enabler, and this aligns with findings from a recent umbrella review [30]. For example, fostering interprofessional discussions and providing easy‐to‐access deprescribing training programmes can enhance implementation efforts. In light of recent implementation trials evaluating medication‐specific deprescribing guidelines, such as those reported by Baas et al. [31, 32], these studies similarly highlight the importance of organisational support, practical tools and coordinated system‐level strategies. Furthermore, government involvement can play a key role in enabling guideline implementation. For example, a Brazilian national childbirth guideline initiative demonstrated the impact of coordinated policy, resource allocation and supportive infrastructure to facilitate implementation [33]. Additional approaches described in prior studies include embedding deprescribing recommendations or algorithms into digital clinical decision‐support systems to be used in practice to aid clinicians.

Current survey and interview findings affirm that guideline algorithms need to be tailored to local needs. It is important to note that requests for modification were only approved primarily for knowledge mobilisation tools; changes to the guideline recommendations themselves were not permitted, to preserve the integrity of the original evidence‐based guidance. Approximately half of the respondents reported adapting guidelines to align with the specific healthcare contexts of their regions and language. These adaptations are pertinent, as cultural and organisational differences between countries can lead to legitimate variations in recommendations, even if the evidence supporting them is the same [34]. For example, existing deprescribing algorithms were adapted, particularly for consumer materials, to suit the French medical context, as noted by a general practitioner interviewee. Adjustments included translation into the French language, medication names and tailoring the consumer materials to improve patient engagement. Literature also acknowledges and recommends adapting guidelines to the local context [30]. However, the lack of systematic evaluation mechanisms limits the ability to measure the effectiveness of efforts in disseminating and implementing deprescribing guidelines in clinical practice. Only a small proportion of organisations reported tracking guideline adherence or assessing clinical outcomes. This gap underscores the need for robust monitoring systems to evaluate the impact of deprescribing initiatives and identify any areas for improvement.

The long‐term sustainability of implementing the use of deprescribing guidelines remains a significant challenge. A significant absence of formal plans for maintaining and evaluating ongoing deprescribing practices was evident. The lack of organisational planning undermines the ability to sustain deprescribing practices over time. Leadership support emerged as a factor in maintaining deprescribing initiatives. Interviewees emphasised that leadership commitment is essential for fostering a culture of deprescribing and motivating clinicians to prioritise medication reviews. Formal tracking of guideline adherence and patient outcomes is necessary to ensure sustained integration of deprescribing guidelines into routine clinical practice. Organisational culture is relatively rarely addressed in the literature, but the data available in support of this intervention is promising [35]. Digital platforms and tools could also play a pivotal role in enabling these evaluations.

A key strength of this research was the use of a mixed‐methods approach, which facilitated a more holistic understanding of current implementation practices. The utilisation of a content‐validated survey that adopted the RE‐AIM framework strengthened the reliability and validity of the survey findings. Responses were received from a diverse range of countries across both the Anglo and non‐Anglo spheres, contributing to the breadth of perspectives captured. However, the use of a purposive sampling approach may limit the generalisability of findings beyond organisations already engaged with deprescribing initiatives. Although the survey underwent content validation, self‐reported data remain subject to response and recall biases, which may affect the accuracy of the reported dissemination and implementation efforts. Furthermore, participant representation was predominantly from high‐income countries, with no representation from Asian countries, thereby limiting the global applicability of the results. The deprescribing guidelines included in this study were focused on guidelines registered on the deprescribing.org Deprescribing Guidelines Repository (DEPOT); other deprescribing guidelines may exist internationally and were not included in this analysis. We also acknowledge a high rate of incomplete survey responses, which may introduce selection bias, as individuals who did not complete the survey may differ from those who completed it. Nevertheless, all data captured were included and analysed to maximise data utility, regardless of the status of completion. Follow‐up interviews were conducted with all willing survey respondents; however, the small and self‐selected sample may have introduced volunteer bias, as those with strong opinions or experiences might have been more likely to participate. As such, the qualitative findings are only presented in this paper to supplement the quantitative survey findings.

Conclusions

The findings of this study provide insights into dissemination and implementation efforts of deprescribing guidelines within organisations focused on implementing change and adopting the latest advancements. Organisations frequently overlook the need for careful evaluation and ongoing maintenance of deprescribing guideline implementation. Without attention to these aspects, organisations risk undermining their efforts. Addressing these challenges requires better organisational planning, increased government involvement and integration of deprescribing guideline recommendations into digital decision‐support systems.

Future efforts should focus on developing robust monitoring mechanisms for guideline implementation within organisations, fostering patient engagement and promoting interdisciplinary communication to advance the global deprescribing agenda. By addressing these gaps, evidence‐based deprescribing recommendations can be effectively adopted and sustainably integrated into clinical workflows, ultimately improving patient outcomes and the quality use of medication worldwide.
