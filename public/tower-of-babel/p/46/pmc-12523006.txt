Investigating the presence of phospholipase and secreted aspartyle proteinase gene family in different genotypes of Candida albicans species
Authors: Mozhgan Mahmoodi, Hasti Nouraei, Reza Nasr, Neda Amirzadeh, Kamiar Zomorodian, Hossein Khodadadi, Keyvan Pakshir
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesCandida albicans can become an opportunistic pathogen that causes several infections that can be fatal in immunocompromised patients. Factors contributing to Candida’s pathogenicity, including enzyme production, are crucial for tissue invasion and infection. The aim of this study was to investigate the genomic presence of secreted aspartyl proteinases (SAP) gene families and phospholipases B (PLB) genes in different genotypes of C. albicans species isolated from candidiasis patients and healthy people.ResultsIn oral isolates, the predominant genotype was A (14/20, 70%). In the blood and vaginal isolates, genotype A (12/20, 60%) and genotype C (10/20, 50%) were predominant genotypes, respectively. There was a ubiquitous detection of SAP1 and SAP8 genes sequences (20/20, 100%) in oral isolates. Blood isolates exhibited a notable predominance of the SAP4 gene (18/20, 90%). In all vaginal isolates, the SAP2, SAP4, and SAP7 genes were presented (20/20, 100%). Additionally, the PLB2 gene was detected in all blood and vaginal isolates (20/20, 100%), whereas the PLB1 gene was predominantly identified in oral isolates (19/20, 95%).

Introduction

Candida albicans is a member of the normal human microbiome that colonizes the mucosa (vagina, mouth, and gastrointestinal tract) and skin in healthy individuals [1]. However, Candida can become an opportunistic pathogen, particularly in those with compromised immune systems, leading to severe fungal infections such as candidemia, which are known for their high mortality rates [2]. C. albicans is the predominant species responsible for these infections, contributing to mortality rates that can reach up to 50% in vulnerable populations. Candidemia, a life-threatening disease with high morbidity and mortality, has increased significantly in recent years [3, 4]. Vaginal candidiasis represents another prevalent form of Candida infection, affecting around 75% of women of reproductive age [5]. This highlights the widespread prevalence and potential severity of health complications associated with this fungus. Molecular typing of an infectious agent is important for epidemiological studies, including identifying the prevalence of infection, determining the source of infection, identifying pathogenic and drug-resistant strains, biodiversity of species, investigating the population structure of the pathogenic agent, and developing appropriate strategies to control infections [6, 7]. C. albicans has different genotypic forms named A, B and C, of which genotype A was reported as the predominant genotype among different forms of candidiasis. The ABC genotyping method has been shown to be able to classify C. albicans strains [8]. Several factors contribute to the pathogenic potential of this yeast, such as biofilm formation, phenotypic switching, and the secretion of hydrolase enzymes (phospholipase, proteinase, esterase, and hemolysin) [9, 10]. Production of phospholipases and aspartyl proteinases plays a significant role in damaging cell membranes, which facilitate adhesion of the microorganism and invading the host cells [11]. The secreted aspartyl proteinases (SAPs) are encoded by 10 SAP genes that have different expression during different stages of infection and different locations of the host [12]. In addition to the type and stage of infection caused by C. albicans, the expression and regulation of SAP genes mostly depend on pH conditions and substrate availability in the environment. SAP1-SAP8 are extracellular and released into the environment, while SAP9 and SAP10 have the C-terminal region for glycophosphatidyl inositol (GPI) proteins and bind to the cell surface. SAPs differ in their pH optima, so that SAP1-SAP3 (yeast form) have more activity at a lower pH of 3 to 5 and SAP4-SAP6 (pseudohyphal form) have activity at a higher pH of 5 to 7 [13, 14]. Four types of phospholipase (A, B, C, and D) have been classified in C. albicans, which depend on the hydrolysis site of phospholipid and ester bonds. Among the types of phospholipases, only PLB1 and PLB2 have been identified extracellularly. According to the studies, the highest activity of phospholipase B in C. albicans is related to PLB1, while type PLB2 has less activity [15, 16]. Some studies have mentioned the relationship between genotype and severity of C. albicans pathogenicity. In some studies, genotype A was reported as the dominant genotype and genotypes B and C as genotypes with a lower frequency [17–19]. In another study, the presence of the secreted aspartyl proteinase gene family in different genotypes of vaginal C. albicans isolates was evaluated [20]. These studies showed that there is a relationship between the dominant genotype of C. albicans in healthy people and patients. Since there is less information about PLB and SAP gene profiles in different genotypes of C. albicans species, this study was designed to investigate the presence of SAP and PLB genes and the potential relationships among various genotypes of C. albicans species isolated from candidiasis patients and healthy people.

Material and method

Isolates

The study was performed on 60 clinical isolates of C. albicans species, comprising vaginal candidiasis (n = 20), candidemia (n = 20) and normal microbiota of the oral cavity in healthy people (n = 20). All samples were randomly selected from the stock that have been verified as C. albicans species in previous studies. Vaginal isolates were identified by PCR amplification of the HWP1 gene using the primers CR-f (5′-GCTACCACTTCAGAATCATCATC-3′) and CR-r (5′-GCACCTTCAGTCGTAGAGACG-3′) [21], and also the normal microbiota of the oral cavity was confirmed as C. albicans by using conventional methods along with PCR amplification targeting the ITS region using specific primers [22], and blood isolates were previously identified using MALDI-TOF methods [23]. The research was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shiraz University of Medical Science (ethical approval number: IR.SUMS.REC.1400.464).

Amplification of genes

The genomic DNA of Candida isolates was prepared as described by Makimura et al. [24]. A small portion of yeast grown on Sabouraud dextrose agar media (SDA) was suspended in 100 µl of lysis buffer and (100 mM Tris-HCl [pH = 7.5], 0.5% [w/v] SDS, and 30 mM EDTA [pH = 7.5]). The tubes were incubated in a water bath at 100 °C for 10 min. Following this, 100 µl of 2.5 M potassium acetate buffer (pH 4.8) was added to each tube. The tubes were vigorously shaken using a vortex mixer and subsequently frozen for an hour. Precipitated proteins and cellular debris were separated by centrifugation at >12,000 g for 5 min. The supernatant was transferred to another 1.5 mL tube and centrifuged again as above. The supernatant was transferred to a new Eppendorf tube, then mixed with an equal volume of isopropyl alcohol. The tube was placed in the freezer for 30 min. The tube was mixed briefly by inversion, centrifuged at >10,000 g for 15 min and the supernatant was discarded. The DNA pellet was washed in 300 µl of 70% (v/v) ethanol. After centrifuging at 10,000 g for 5 min, the supernatant was discarded. The extracted DNA was air-dried and dissolved in 50 µl of distilled water, and stored at -20° C prior to PCR.

Genotyping of Candida albicans strains

The genotyping of C. albicans isolates was performed according to the method described in McCullough et al. [8], which includes the use of C. albicans ATCC strains as controls for genotypes A, B, and C. The banding patterns of the clinical isolates were compared to these control strains to ensure accurate genotype identification. The pair primers CA-INT-L (5′-ATA AAG GGA AGT CGG CAA ATA GAT CCG TAA-3′) and CA-INT-R (5′-CCT TGG CTG TGG TTT CGC TAG ATA GTA GAT-3′) were used for the discrimination of C. albicans genotypes [8] (Table 1). The criteria for C. albicans genotypes were based on amplifying the 25 S rRNA gene region and length pattern demonstrated after electrophoresis: genotype A (approximately 450 bp product), genotype B (approximately 840 bp product), and genotype C (both 450 and 840 bp products).

SAP gene family and PLB gene

Eight primers were used for the amplification the of SAP1-8 genes. Also, two specific primers were used for amplification of the PLB1 and PLB2 genes [25–29]. More details about the primers are presented in Table 1.

Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)

To amplify PLB genes, the PCR was carried out using a commercial 2X PCR Master Mix (Ampliqon, Denmark) containing Taq DNA polymerase, dNTPs, and 1.5 mM MgCl₂. A standardized volume of 3 µL of template DNA was used per 20 µL reaction. The PCR conditions used were as follows: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min; 32 cycles of denaturation at 95 °C for 45 s; annealing at 63 °C for 45s; and extension at 72 °C for 45s. Amplified products were electrophoresed on a 1% agarose gel stained with ethidium bromide and visualized under UV light. The PCR reaction for the SAP gene family was achieved after using gradient annealing temperatures as follows: (SAP1:57 °C, SAP2,4, PLB1:58 °C, SAP3,7:55 °C, SAP5:61 °C, SAP6, PLB2:59 °C, SAP8:60 °C, CA-INT:63 °C).

Statistical analysis

Data analysis was assessed in SPSS software version 20. Chi-squared and Fisher exact (2-tailed) tests were used.

More details about these genotypes were described in our previous study [30]. Genotype A was predominant in normal microbiota and blood isolates, and genotype C was predominant in vaginal isolates (Table 2).

Discussion

In the last decade’s candidiasis, there has been a considerable increase in immunocompromised patients and healthy populations. C. albicans is responsible for about 70% of fungal infections around the world [31, 32]. Candidemia is the fourth and seventh cause of bloodstream infections in the USA and Europe, respectively, that may invade the lungs, heart, digestive system, and other organs [33–35]. C. albicans is the causative agent of vaginal candidiasis in more than 90% of cases [5]. Molecular typing of an infectious agent is important for epidemiological studies, including identifying the prevalence of infection, determining the source of infection, identifying pathogenic and drug-resistant strains, biodiversity of species, investigating the population structure of the pathogenic agent, and developing appropriate strategies to control infections [7, 36] Based on the presence or absence of an intron in the 25s rDNA region, C. albicans could be grouped into three genotypes: A, B, and C [8].

In most studies, genotype A was reported as the dominant genotype, and genotypes B and C had a lower prevalence [37]. In this study, genotype A was the most common genotype among blood and normal microbiota isolates, but the predominant genotype in vaginal isolates was genotype C. Fornari et al. reported that genotype A was the predominant genotype [17], and Karahan et al. described that genotype A had more frequency in noninvasive and invasive isolates of C. albicans; also, one study by Millar et al. found that genotype A was the prevalent genotype [38, 39]. Similar to our study, Gharaghani et al. described genotype C as the most common genotype in vulvovaginal samples [40]. Types of clinical specimens, different populations of patients, and geographic location are the main causes of these differences [41, 42]. C. albicans can secrete extracellular hydrolytic enzymes that facilitate adherence and tissue penetration as a result of invasion of the host [43]. Several studies document a correlation between the secretion of different enzymes, such as SAP and PLB and the virulence of Candida species [9, 44]. In this study, which assessed the genomic presence of virulence genes, we observed that the SAP4 gene was detected most frequently in blood isolates, followed by SAP1 and SAP8. The genes SAP2, SAP4, and SAP7 were 100% in vaginal isolates, but all normal microbiota isolates had SAP1 and SAP8 genes.

The variable distribution of SAP genes among different isolate populations underscores their niche-specific roles in commensalism and pathogenesis. In our study, the consistent detection of SAP2, SAP4, and SAP7 (100%) in vaginal isolates suggests these genes are particularly important for establishing and maintaining vaginal infections. This finding partially aligns with previous reports; for instance, Monroy-Pérez et al. also found a high prevalence of the SAP4-6 subgroup [45], while Naglik et al. reported SAP2 and SAP5 as most frequent [28]. These discrepancies could stem from geographic variations in strain prevalence, differences in patient populations, or the disease status (acute infection vs. chronic carriage) of the subjects studied.

The biological implications of this specific gene profile are significant. SAP2 is a key enzyme for nutrient acquisition by degrading host proteins, which is crucial in the acidic environment of the vagina where it exhibits optimal activity [46]. SAP4-6, often associated with hyphal formation and systemic infection, might be pivotal for tissue invasion and immune evasion in the vaginal mucosa [47]. The consistent presence of SAP7, whose role is less defined but often linked to virulence in animal models, further points to a coordinated strategy for vaginal pathogenicity [48].

Conversely, the ubiquitous presence of SAP1 and SAP8 in commensal oral isolates hints at a role tailored for persistence in the oral niche. SAP1 is often associated with adherence and colonization in the early stages of biofilm formation, while SAP8 may play a role in surface protein remodeling for maintaining the commensal state. This distinct genetic arsenal suggests that C. albicans finely tunes its SAP gene repertoire to suit the specific environmental pressures and ecological demands of different host niches, facilitating a seamless transition from a harmless commensal to an opportunistic pathogen [46].

In the present study, both PLB1 and PLB2 genes were present in more than 85% of all the isolates. Similar to our study, Naglik et al. reported the frequency of PLB1 (58%) and PLB2 (48%) genes in oral infection and PLB1 (14%) and PLB2 (34%) genes in their carriers, while the frequency of PLB1 and PLB2 genes was reported at 63% in vaginal infection, and PLB1 (43%) and PLB2 (50%) genes were in their carriers [28]. Conversely, in our study, Ilkhanizadeh et al. reported that the frequency of PLB1 was 9.3% and PLB2 was 3.1% in oropharyngeal candidiasis isolates [49]. These findings suggest that differences in risk factors contributing to infection could significantly influence the activation or inhibition of gene functions. In several studies, the role of C. albicans genotypes and the intensity of production of these enzymes have been investigated [28, 40]. For example, Gharaghani et al. reported the highest activity of phospholipase and proteinase in genotypes B and C of vaginal isolates, respectively, which indicates the difference in enzyme activity among different genotypes [39]. In the study of Sugita et al., genotype B of Candida isolates from blood had higher activity of phospholipase and proteinase than other genotypes [50]. Jafarian et al. reported that genotype A of the isolates from the cancer patient’s esophageal mucosa was active on the phospholipase enzyme more than other genotypes, and that these studies document the significant difference between the intensity of enzyme production and the different sources of the isolates [51]. However, none of these studies specifically examined these genes across various genotypes. Different environmental conditions can play a role in the different activities of these enzymes. Conditions such as pH, association and coexistence with bacteria, the amount of sugar, as well as other nutritional factors and minerals such as iron, magnesium, calcium, etc [52]. For example, pH in the blood and vaginal area is neutral and acidic, respectively, while in the normal microbiota of the oral cavity, pH is variable depending on personal diet. As you can see in the relationship between proteinase secretion and environmental acidity, acidity often leads to the production of more specific SAPs [47]. The presence of bacteria such as Lactobacillus in the vaginal area may play a role in stimulating the secretion of phospholipases and proteinases. In the oral cavity, this condition could be generalized in relation to oral bacteria such as cocci and bacilli that exist in the oral mucosa. The concentration of minerals and sugars may also influence the secretion of enzymes. Factors such as the levels of minerals, sugars, or ketones in the blood, the quantity of sugar or nutrients in the oral cavity resulting from food intake, or the elevated levels of iron in the vaginal area during menstruation could all significantly impact this process. Therefore, the discrimination of genotypes could be useful to identify the role of different factors in the pathogenicity of the species isolated from different parts of the body. The differential expression of SAP and PLB genes in isolates from healthy individuals compared to those from infection sites may also provide valuable insights into the mechanisms underlying the transition from commensalism to pathogenicity.

Limitations

The primary limitations of this research include its focus solely on the genomic detection of SAP and PLB genes via PCR, rather than an investigation into their actual mRNA expression levels or corresponding enzymatic activity. Consequently, the findings indicate genetic potential but not necessarily functional protein production. Furthermore, this study was conducted on isolates from a single geographic region (Shiraz, Iran) with a moderate sample size, which may limit the generalizability of our findings to other populations and geographic areas.

Conclusion

In the analyzed samples, the genes SAP4 and PLB2 consistently demonstrated the highest prevalence across the board. Specifically, in vaginitis samples, genotype C emerged as the most prevalent, while genes SAP2, SAP4, SAP7, and PLB2 were notably common in these cases. The predominance of genotype A in oral and blood isolates contrasts with the prevalence of genotype C in vaginal samples, suggesting a possible niche-specific adaptation or selective pressure exerted by the host environment. The widespread genomic detection of certain SAP genes in oral and vaginal isolates suggests a potential for enzymatic versatility in C. albicans and its capacity to express virulence factors suited to different anatomical sites. Specifically, the consistent detection of SAP1 and SAP8 in oral isolates and SAP2, SAP4, and SAP7 in vaginal isolates, alongside the prevalence of the PLB2 gene in blood and vaginal samples, highlights the complex interplay between the organism’s genetic background and its environment. Consequently, these observations on the distribution of virulence gene sequences contribute to mapping the genetic landscape of C. albicans and form a basis for future functional studies into its role in human health and disease, paving the way for future research aimed at mitigating the risks associated with Candida infections.
