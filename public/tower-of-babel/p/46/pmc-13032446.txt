Assessing differences in physician associate/assistant program admissions: observing the influence of demographic characteristics on likelihood of acceptance
Authors: Nicole Wen, Mary L Warner
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPatient and clinician racial concordance has been shown to improve therapeutic trust and health outcomes. Like other health professions, the Physician Associate/Assistant (PA) profession has struggled to improve the diversity of the profession compared to the United States population. By analyzing the applicant pool from application to acceptance, this study aimed to apply a novel assessment methodology to evaluate the association between admissions policies and procedures and the diversity of the PA entering class.MethodsDe-identified demographic and academic performance data were culled from 2022 to 2023 admissions applications to the George Washington University PA program from the Centralized Application Service for Physician Assistants (CASPA). Descriptive statistics were used to characterize applicants. Logistic regression models were used to identify applicant characteristics associated with the odds of being interviewed and/or accepted.ResultsA total of 2518 applications were analyzed, describing 8% of the CASPA applicant pool from 2022 to 2023. Bivariate analysis showed that race/ethnicity and overall GPA were significant predictors of being interviewed (G2(4) = 18.92, p < 0.001; OR = 1.66, 95% CI [1.54, 1.79]) and being accepted (G2(4) = 11.29, p = 0.02; OR = 1.41, 95% CI [1.26, 1.59]). Prior to controlling for covariates, Black applicants showed significantly lower odds of being accepted into the PA program compared to their White counterparts. After adjusting for overall GPA, the association between race/ethnicity and the odds of interview and acceptance became non-significant. Overall GPA remained strongly associated with odds of interview and acceptance after controlling for other covariates. Strong associations between Asian, Black and Hispanic applicants and the following backgrounds: (1) first-generation college student, (2) living in a health professions shortage area, (3) school district where 50% or less attend college and (4) economically disadvantaged family income were identified when compared to White students.ConclusionsThis study proposes a unique method for evaluating the effects of admissions policies on accepted students from diverse backgrounds. Given some student background factors are associated with race/ethnicity, they may be useful to healthcare educational programs during the admissions cycle to facilitate increasing the diversity of the PA student body.Keywords: PA education, Medical education, Professional phase admissions, Diversity, Outcome evaluation, Cohort analysis, Race, Race/ethnicity, Admissions policies, Bias

Background

Despite the increasing racial and ethnic diversity of the U.S. population in the past 50 years, the demographic composition of the Physician Associate/Assistant (PA) workforce fails to reflect this shift and lacks diversity when compared with the demographics of today’s patients. From 2020 to 2024, the PA profession grew 27.8% and has been increasing steadily by no less than 6% annually since 2014 [1, 2]. Despite this growth, when comparing the demographic composition of the U.S. population to the PA workforce, we note considerable underrepresentation of Hispanic, Black, and American Indian racial/ethnic groups [2] This is a critical issue as the patient-clinician relationship is more effective when patients and providers share similar demographic backgrounds and experiences [3–6]. In addition, patient-clinician racial concordance is associated with improved trust, better access to timely appointments, more effective communication, and higher levels of patient satisfaction overall [4–7].

Previous studies have reported program factors that increase the likelihood of matriculation by diverse students. Female, Asian and underrepresented minority (URM) applicants valued PA program diversity as an important feature of PA programs and named it as a critical consideration when choosing where to matriculate [8]. PA programs with larger applicant pools, those that do not require a standardized entrance exam, and provisional accreditation status were positively associated with increased diversity in the applicant pool, when diversity was measured by the proportion of students from underrepresented minorities (URM) and underrepresented in medicine (URiM) backgrounds using the Simpson’s Diversity Index (SDI) [9]. Other program factors such as those with longer curricula, and those with proportionally greater URM faculty and staff were associated with increased racial/ethnic diversity among the student populations [10]. Importantly, the ability to offer scholarship funding was not associated with higher rates of matriculation among URM students [10]. Applicants from underrepresented racial and ethnic groups who applied to PA programs that utilized holistic admissions practices [11] had higher odds of matriculation [12].

Student factors associated with matriculation of a diverse student cohort have also been studied. After adjusting for academic achievement variables (standardized entrance exam scores and GPA), the odds of matriculation between URM and non-URM applicants did not differ significantly, suggesting that academic performance has a confounding effect on the odds of matriculation [13]. This study found a decreased odds of matriculation as applicant age increased, and this negative association remained when controlling for the aforementioned covariates [13]. The intersectional relationship between students’ socioeconomic backgrounds and race/ethnicity has been studied in medicine [14] supporting the College Board best practices recommendations for recruiting a diverse student cohort [15, 16]. Such recommendations include inclusive outreach to prospective students, test-optional policies for standardized entrance exams, holistic review policies focused on understanding applicants’ earlier life experiences, and investment in pre-program preparatory programs. Despite the growing research about diversity in the PA profession, PA programs continue to grapple with self-assessment tools to evaluate their admissions processes.

Our study aimed to investigate the association between race/ethnicity, sex, and age and the odds of being interviewed and accepted by evaluating demographic and academic differences in The George Washington University (GWU) PA program applicants throughout the stages of the admissions process. As a secondary aim, we assessed whether these variables (race/ethnicity, age, sex, and overall GPA) were associated with the Health Services and Resource Administration (HRSA) indicators of disadvantaged status. We conducted this observational study to help us identify whether the GWU PA program disproportionately accepts students of certain backgrounds. This study defines relationships between HRSA socioeconomic factors and demographic characteristics that may be useful in supporting the goal of diversifying the PA and other health professions. The methods used in this study can be adapted by other health professions educational programs to examine admission policies and outcomes for the purposes of improving the diversity of the healthcare workforce.

Methods

This study was deemed not human subjects research by the George Washington University Institutional Review Board, which means subject consent was deemed unnecessary. De-identified data associated with individual-level applicant data (N = 2518) for those who applied to the GWU program were obtained through the Centralized Application Service for Physician Assistants (CASPA) portal for 2022–2023. Variables of interest included: (1) applicant demographic characteristics (age, sex, race/ethnicity), (2) measures of previous academic performance (overall grade point average (OGPA) and science GPA (SGPA), (3) application status (applied, interviewed, accepted), and (4) self-reported indicators of socioeconomic background (first generation to attend college (first gen), permanent address in a health professions shortage area (HPSA), 50% or less of high school class attended college, and/or household income is economically disadvantaged). OGPA, SGPA, and age were numerical variables. Interview and accepted statuses, sex, first gen, HPSA, 50% or less go to college, and household income is economically disadvantaged were binary variables.

Applicant records that contained missing values for variables of interest were excluded from analysis (N = 76). A total of 2442 applicant records were used in logistic regression modeling. Overall GPA was rescaled to represent a 0.1-point increase across all models. Race/ethnicity was compiled from CASPA into one variable for analysis from 6 distinct binary race variables (Hispanic, American Indian, Asian, Black, Pacific Islander, and White) [14]. We defined the multiracial category as an applicant who designated more than one racial category, excluding those of Hispanic background [14]. If the subject was from a Hispanic background, they were coded as Hispanic regardless of sharing multiple racial profiles to avoid double counting with the multiracial category. No American Indian or Pacific Islander applicants were interviewed or accepted, therefore, bivariate analyses and logistic regression models used a 5-level categorical variable for race/ethnicity with White applicants as the reference group.

Descriptive statistics were used to characterize PA program applicant demographics (race/ethnicity, age, sex, academic performance, admission status, and disadvantaged status) stratified by total applicant, interviewed and accepted pools. Bivariate analysis of interviewed status and accepted status by race/ethnicity, OGPA, SGPA, age, sex, and HRSA indicators was conducted using simple logistic regression and the likelihood ratio test (Tables 2 and 3). Assumptions for logistic regression modeling were met. OGPA and SGPA were highly correlated with each other (Pearson’s correlation coefficient r = 0.91), resulting in collinearity when both predictors are in the same model. Since OGPA had been slightly more predictive of admissions outcomes from previous analyses for the GWU PA program, we chose to use OGPA as the academic performance predictor across all models.

Results

Cohort Characteristics by admission status

Table 1 displays the demographic and academic characteristics of GWU PA program applicants for the 2022–2023 cycle stratified by admission status (applied, interviewed, accepted). A total of 2518 students applied to the PA program. Of those, 268 (10.6%) were interviewed and 73 (2.9%) were accepted. Mean overall and science GPAs were higher for those interviewed and those accepted compared to the total GW applicant pool. Average age across applied, interviewed, and accepted pools were comparable, at around 24. The proportion of female to male students across admission status pools were similar, with female students outnumbering male students by approximately 5-fold.

Association between applicant characteristics and interviewed status

Table 2 reports the bivariate relationship between interviewed status and various PA program applicant characteristics. Race/ethnicity was positively associated with interviewed status G2(4) = 18.92, p = 0.0008. For each additional 0.1 point in OGPA, the odds of being interviewed increased by 66% and for each additional 0.1 point in SGPA, the odds of being interviewed increased by 54%. Age and sex were not significantly associated with the odds of being interviewed, and neither were the four HRSA disadvantaged status indicators.

Figure 1 shows a series of nested logistic regression models for interviewed status by race/ethnicity, OGPA, age, and sex predictors. In Model 1 (race/ethnicity only), Asian applicants had 34% lower odds and Black applicants had 65% lower odds of being interviewed compared to White applicants. In Models 2,3,4, with the addition of OGPA, age, and sex in a stepped fashion, we saw significant associations between OGPA and age with interviewed status. The odds of being interviewed increased for each 0.1-point increase in OGPA and for each additional year of age. No substantial changes were observed across the OR estimates from Models 2, 3 and 4 after adjustment for age and sex. Race/ethnicity and sex were not significantly associated with the odds of being interviewed in the multivariable models. OGPA appeared to be the most important factor in interviewing students across all adjusted models.

Logistic Regression Models for Interviewed Status by PA Program Applicant Characteristics

The Hosmer-Lemeshow tests suggested no evidence for a lack of fit for models 2, 3, and 4 (Model 2: χ²(8) = 11.08, p = 0.20; Model 3: χ²(8) = 14.24, p = 0.076; Model 4: χ²(8) = 10.98, p = 0.20). ROC curves for all 3 models showed fair discrimination between interviewed vs. not interviewed (Model 2: AUC = 0.785; Model 3: AUC = 0.792; Model 4: AUC = 0.793).

Association between applicant characteristics and accepted status

Bivariate associations between accepted status and PA program applicant characteristics were reported in Table 3. Race/ethnicity was overall significantly associated with accepted status, (G2(4) = 11.29, p = 0.02). OGPA was positively associated with accepted status, with the odds of being accepted increasing by 41% for each additional 0.1 point in OGPA. Age and sex were not significantly associated with accepted status. Applicants who come from an income disadvantaged family had approximately 60% lower odds of being accepted compared to those who do not.

Figure 2 depicts the logistic regression models for accepted status by PA applicant characteristics. In Model 1, Black applicants had statistically significant lower odds of acceptance (~ 88% reduction) compared to White applicants. OGPA and accepted status were positively associated, with the odds of being accepted increasing by 40% for each additional 0.1 point in OGPA (Model 2), and this association remained statistically significant across all models. Age was a significant predictor, with the odds of being accepted 7% higher for each additional year of age (Models 3 & 4). Overall GPA remained the most important factor in accepting students in the PA program.

Hosmer-Lemeshow GOF tests suggested no evidence of a lack of fit for models 2, 3, and 4 (Model 2: χ²(8) = 9.64, p = 0.29; Model 3: χ²(8) = 8.6, p = 0.38; Model 4: χ²(8) = 8.43, p = 0.39). ROC curves for all 3 models showed acceptable discrimination between accepted vs. not accepted classes (Model 2: AUC = 0.720; Model 3: AUC = 0.732; Model 4: AUC = 0.731).

Association between applicant characteristics and HRSA socioeconomic status indicators

Examination of the HRSA socioeconomic status indicators and GWU PA program demographic characteristics using multivariable logistic regression (N = 2442) are presented in Table 4.

First generation to attend college

The proportion of applicants that identified as the first generation to attend college was 24.2% (N = 591). Asian, Black, and Hispanic applicants exhibited greater odds of being First Gen compared to White applicants, with Hispanic applicants showing the strongest association. For each additional year of age, the odds of being First Gen increases by 3%, adjusting for other predictors. For each additional 0.1 point in overall GPA, the odds of being First Gen decreases by 5%, adjusting for other predictors.

Health Professional Shortage Area (HPSA)

The proportion of applicants whose permanent address is designated in a HRSA-defined health professions shortage area was 14.9%(N = 365). Compared with White applicants, Black applicants were about 3.2 times more likely, and Hispanic applicants were about 2.8 times more likely to have an address in a HPSA, adjusting for other variables. Age was a significant predictor of coming from a HPSA area, with older individuals showing an increased odds when holding other variables constant.

School district where 50% or less of graduates go to college

The proportion of applicants from a school district where 50% or less of graduates went to college was 9.1% (N = 223). Black applicants were about 4.1 times more likely to come from a school district where 50% or less of graduates attend college compared to White applicants, and Hispanic applicants were about 3.9 times more likely, adjusting for other variables. For each additional year of age, the odds coming from a school where 50% or less went to college increased by 5%, holding other variables constant.

Family income is economically disadvantaged

The proportion of students coming from a family with a disadvantaged income was 20.5% (N = 500). Asian, Black, and Hispanic applicants were more likely to come from a family with a disadvantaged income compared to White applicants, with Hispanic applicants showing the strongest association. For each additional year of age, the odds of coming from an income disadvantaged family increases by 4%, holding other variables constant.

GWU applicant characteristics compared to CASPA

Comparing GWU’s applicant demographics to the CASPA applicant pool, GWU exhibited lower proportions of White (52.2% vs. 57.2%) and Hispanic (13.7% vs. 15.6%) applicants and higher proportions of Asian (20.6% vs. 14.7%) and Black (9.4% vs. 8.0%) applicants. The GWU applicant pool consisted of a higher proportion of female applicants and a lower proportion of applicants identified as socioeconomically disadvantaged by HRSA. In general, differences in race/ethnicity (χ²(5) = 86.0, p < 0.001), sex (χ2(1) = 80.4, p < 0.001), and three of the HRSA indicators: first generation to attend college (χ²(1) = 18.6, p < 0.001), health professions shortage area (χ²(1) = 20.5, p < 0.001), and 50% or less go to college (χ²(1) = 23.4, p < 0.001) were proportionally different from CASPA.

Discussion

This study is among the first to follow PA program applicant characteristics from application to interview to acceptance. It is unique in that we observe the association between applicant characteristics (race/ethnicity, age, sex, and overall GPA) and the odds of interviewing and accepting applicants into a PA program while also assessing the relationship between these predictors and HRSA disadvantaged status indicators. Logistic regression models suggested that GPA was the most important factor in interviewing and accepting students into the PA program, consistent with other studies [13]. In particular, Asian, Black, and Hispanic applicants saw a smaller difference in odds of being interviewed and accepted while multiracial applicants showed a greater difference in odds compared to White applicants after adjusting for overall GPA. Indeed, overall GPA appeared to be a confounder of the race/ethnicity–interviewed status and race/ethnicity–accepted status relationships as differences between crude measures of association and GPA-adjusted measures of association for each racial/ethnic category were greater than 10%. These findings indicate that once GPA differences were factored in, race/ethnicity explained less of the difference in admissions. Additionally, age was an effect modifier of the relationship between race/ethnicity and program acceptance, meaning that at different ages, each racial group had varying odds of being accepted, (G2(4) = 9.64, p = 0.047).

Our results concur with findings of others that note after controlling for overall GPA, race/ethnicity were not a significant predictor of interviewed status and accepted status [9, 13]. Overall GPA and age were positively associated with the odds of being interviewed and being accepted, adjusting for race/ethnicity and sex.

Our study showed that the proportion of applicants who indicated they had one or more of the HRSA indicators decreased from application to interviewed to accepted stages, with the largest reduction in percentage occurring in the family income indicator (-10.9%). Consensus building in holistic admissions that valued students from families with lower economic backgrounds effectively increased admission of diverse students from geographic regions with a shortage of health professionals and/or from migrant backgrounds [18]. Race/ethnicity and age were significantly associated with the four indicators of HRSA disadvantaged status, and overall GPA was negatively associated with the odds of being a first-generation college student. Two previous studies reported the association of low income and first-generation students with race/ethnicity12,14, but age has not previously been described as an associated factor. Additionally, in our study the odds of being accepted were lower if the applicant came from an income disadvantaged family consistent with a medical school admissions study [16]. Our study analyzed the association of HRSA indicators and accepted status, and with race/ethnicity. These findings may help us to further evaluate the use of HRSA indicators in the admissions process.

Additionally, studies have shown that PA programs with larger applicant pools, no GRE requirements, and larger proportions of URM faculty tended to attract a more diverse student cohort [9, 10, 19, 20]. The GW faculty and staff is comprised of more individuals from diverse backgrounds than some PA programs and while the program removed the GRE requirement in 2022–2023, our study contributes new knowledge by identifying the HRSA indicators associations with age, race/ethnicity and first-generation college students. Furthermore, our study reports on evaluation of admissions processes throughout the admissions cycle rather than the sole outcome of matriculation.

Our study was limited by the sample size representing one program’s application cycle, albeit it corresponds to 8% of the CASPA applications in 2022–2023. Secondly, responses to the HRSA socioeconomic status questions are self-reported making them subject to both misclassification and recall biases. The fact that we used acceptance to the program rather than matriculation may make the results of this study less comparable to other studies [9, 12, 14, 15]. Whereas acceptance is a program decision, student matriculation depends on multifactorial considerations including finances, geography, personal and family situations, and other factors that extend beyond the admissions cycle.

Additionally, comparison between available CASPA national applicant trends and GWU’s PA program applicant data showed statistically different distributions for race/ethnicity, sex, and three HRSA disadvantaged status indicator variables suggesting the results should be interpreted with caution as the CASPA trend numbers may not be generalizable to individual PA programs [17]. Nevertheless, the somewhat large sample sizes on which analyses were conducted (N = 2518 for exploratory analysis and N = 2442 for logistic regression modeling) provide some support for the findings. Finally, we controlled for known confounders (GPA) and other covariates (age and sex).

A strength of this study is that we used simple logistic regression modelling and a stepwise model building procedure which can be easily adapted by other health professions schools or programs. This study is one of the first to assess the relationship of applicant demographics (race/ethnicity, age, sex) and disadvantaged status factors (HRSA) with the odds of being interviewed and accepted into a competitive PA program, while controlling for academic performance.

Conclusions

The study found that after adjusting for academic performance, different racial groups did not show significantly different odds of being interviewed and accepted. Retrospective analysis of cohort applicant demographics, academic success, and socioeconomic factors at application, interview, and acceptance confers the opportunity to examine differences in health professions admissions. The association between the HRSA indicators, race/ethnicity, and age may be useful information for allowing us to promote diversity in the PA student pool and therefore the PA workforce.

Future directions

As the findings represent applications to one PA program in the United States, these methods should be utilized and studied in other health professions educational settings to inform effectiveness of admissions policies and procedures. Different samples of health professions programs may improve the external validity (generalizability) of the inferences drawn and reduce threats to the internal validity of the study (bias, confounding, effect modification). The relationship between other student related factors, including patient care hours and the likelihood of acceptance or matriculation remains an open question.
