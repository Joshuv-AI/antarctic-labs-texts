Pulse Doppler ultrasound measurement of central artery elasticity: an exploratory study
Authors: Trevor Tucker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundArterial stiffness, as a biomarker of cardiovascular diseases, is most often described by a surrogate global stiffness index, pulse pressure wave velocity (PWV), a measure of the time for pressure pulses to transit to sensors normally placed on different arteries. However, local arterial elasticity also influences the transient response of an artery to an impulse of flow, as characterized by the local pulsatile flow velocity waveform. Such transient response to pulsatile flow has not previously been analyzed as an indicator of arterial stiffness and a biomarker of cardiovascular disease.ObjectivesThe objectives of this exploratory study were to identify physical relationships between arteries’ pulse flow velocity (PFV) transient response characteristics and the elasticity of central arteries, and to provide preliminary evidence of a match between measured arterial pulse flow response characteristics and those predicted by physical relationships.MethodA transmission line model is applied to the analysis of transient blood flow velocity waveforms, a new analytic approach, offering a new measure of arterial elasticity, that of natural frequency, which is shown to be directly related to arterial stiffness. Specific flow velocity values, as measured at specific points on damped oscillation transient flow velocity waveforms, enable the determination of the natural frequency associated with the artery’s transient response to an impulse of flow from the left ventricle.ResultsThe damped oscillation transient flow velocity waveforms, as measured by pulse Doppler ultrasound, is shown to match the damped oscillation flow velocity waveforms measured on the thoracic aorta and on the femoral artery. Such matching includes Bland–Altman analysis of waveform match using zero, one and two reflection sites. Also, the local stiffnesses of central arteries determined from the pulse Doppler ultrasound measurement of natural frequency are shown to match global stiffness reported for the same arteries using established PWV measurement approaches, providing an initial indicator of the validity of this new sonographic arterial elasticity measurement technique.Discussion and conclusionsThis new arterial elasticity measurement technique, Doppler ultrasound measurement of vascular natural frequency, potentially offers advantages over established pulse pressure wave velocity measurement techniques as follows:aThe measurement of elasticity at specific arterial locations;bThe measurement of the elasticity of deeply set central arteries, and;cThe potential for measuring central artery elasticity in clinical settings.This study is exploratory in that the application of pulse flow transient velocity waveform analysis to the determination of arterial elasticity is a novel departure from established pulse pressure wave analysis approaches. The study’s preliminary findings, specifically the match between predicted transient flow velocity waveforms and those measured, and also the match between predicted PWVs to those measured using established pulse transit time techniques, are both sufficiently close that further independent verification and more extensive formal validation initiatives are indicated and justifiable.

Background

Arterial stiffness is a recognized biomarker of cardiovascular diseases [1–4] and predictor of all-cause mortality [5–7]. Pulse wave velocity (PWV), an average velocity of pressure pulses propagating in arteries, is a widely accepted surrogate index of arterial stiffness [8–10]. In most arterial stiffness test configurations, PWV is measured by the time required for a pressure pulse to transit between two pressure or arterial wall displacement sensors, each applied over different arteries which are located close to the skin surface. The current “Gold Standard” surrogate index of arterial stiffness is the carotid-to-femoral pulse wave velocity (cfPWV) [11–13] in which one sensor is placed over the carotid artery and a second sensor is placed over the femoral artery. Various specific sensor techniques and various pressure waveform analysis techniques have been previously studied and reported [14–16], each of which offer different strengths and weaknesses. The measurement of PWV is a specialized and time-consuming process, hence, even though arterial stiffness is important in medical diagnosis and prognostication, PWV is rarely measured in clinical practice [17–19]. However, considerable research has been, and is currently being, expended to bring PWV measurement into the realm of clinical practice.

While PWV, as defined by the Moens-Korteweg equation, is widely accepted as a measure of arterial stiffness, there is a relative paucity of research [23–25] which formally validates the equation. The few Moens-Korteweg validation studies readily available [23–25] offer test results that are limited to arterial segments of large diameter, particularly straight aortic arteries, or phantoms of such arteries. These studies either did not discuss wave reflection from the arterial load or took initiatives to minimize such reflection.

A primary physics assumption underlying the derivation of the Moens-Korteweg equation is that blood is inviscid, meaning that viscous resistance to blood flow is negligible. The macrocirculation central region of the arterial tree, wherein viscous resistance is negligible, is that for which arterial diameter has been described as greater than 6 mm [28] (see Appendix A). The only region for which the Moens-Korteweg equation may be reasonably valid, from a physics perspective, is that for which the luminal diameter is greater than about 6 mm. PWV has dependence on three variables, “E”, “h” and “d”, each of which may vary along the length of the compliant artery, implying that PWV also varies along the length of the artery. The Moens-Korteweg equation, however, is based on the condition that “E,” “h” and “d,” are constant. The implication is, therefore, that PWV is an indicator of elasticity or stiffness, not an absolute measure.

In medical practice, PWV is widely used as a measure of arterial stiffness irrespective of the relatively few segments of the arterial tree that fully meet the physics morphology validity conditions of the Moens-Korteweg equation. Arterial PWV stiffness values, for example, those reported in PWV databases [14], for various healthy and diseased arterial segments, are standardized in the medical community, irrespective of formal Moens-Korteweg morphology validity.

In common language usage “stiffness” and “elasticity” are related but distinct concepts. Stiffness, in common usage, normally refers to an object’s resistance to deformation when an external force is applied, while elasticity normally refers to the recovery of a deformed object after an external deforming force is removed. With the common usage meaning of stiffness, the flow velocity’s rate of increase from the foot of the waveform to the systolic peak, as shown in Fig. 1, may be associated with the artery’s stiffness resistant response to an impulse of input flow, while the transient damped oscillation of flow velocity after systole and after the impulse of input flow, may be associated with the artery’s elastic response.

While arterial stiffness has been researched, characterized, and documented extensively using pressure pulse propagation and pulse wave analysis [10, 19], little research attention has been focused on pulse flow analysis as a means for assessing arterial elasticity. The pulse flow velocity (PFV) waveform, as measured using pulse Doppler ultrasound, and as represented in Fig. 1, however, also possesses transient flow response characteristics that reflect the elasticity of arteries and that may also complement pressure response characteristics as determined using pulse pressure wave analysis and transit time measurement.

Aim, premise and approach

The aim of the study was to analyze arterial elasticity and stiffness using the characteristics of the blood’s transient pulse flow velocity waveform. The approach described, characterizing arterial elasticity using pulse flow wave analysis, is an original approach, currently unaddressed in the literature and is complementary to that of pulse pressure wave analysis which is currently in wide-spread use.

This study is premised on the concept that a healthy central artery’s transient pulse flow response, after cessation of an impulse of input flow, as represented by the waveform plot of Fig. 1, is that of a damped oscillating wave, implying a damping factor and a frequency of oscillation. Systems which display such damped oscillating waveforms are common in engineering, physics and biology and are most simply represented and modelled by a linear second order differential equation. Damped oscillation in various biological systems occurs at microscopic, cellular levels and at macroscopic, whole-body levels. The study of damped oscillatory behaviour in biological and biochemical systems is a discipline unto itself [30–32], with pulsatile flow in compliant arteries, potentially a specific example.

The approach taken in this study is that of applying the scientific method, in which a hypothesis is presented (describing or explaining an observed phenomenon), predictions based on the hypothesis are presented, experiments are researched and referenced that test the hypothesis, test results are then compared to those predicted, then conclusions regarding the correctness of the hypothesis are presented.

The basic hypothesis of this study is that a second order differential equation, as derived from a transmission line [33] model of blood flow in arteries, describes a transient blood flow velocity waveform, as represented in Fig. 2. The transmission line model is a linear second order differential equation which produces a damped oscillating waveform consisting of an exponential decay component (dash-dot line), and an oscillating component (dashed line), whose frequency of oscillation is determined by blood and artery attributes.Fig. 2Representative transient flow velocity waveform response to an impulse of input flow

The damped oscillation pulse flow velocity (PFV) waveform predicted by the model is described by just two independent parameters, the damping coefficient (DC), and the natural frequency (NF). The predicted PFV waveforms are compared in this study to PFV waveforms measured in-vivo on central arteries with pulse Doppler ultrasound. The natural frequency which characterizes the damped oscillation flow waveform is proposed as a new index of local arterial elasticity and is shown in the analysis (Appendix A) to be directly related to the well-established pulse wave velocity as a measure of arterial stiffness.

Superimposed on the measured waveform is an “ideal” damped oscillating waveform which has been calculated using the analysis methodology described here (labelled “Ideal Flow Velocity Wave” in Fig. 1). Visual inspection shows a good match between the measured and calculated waveforms, indicative of the potential validity of the hypothesis that the transient elastic response of a large diameter central artery may be represented by a damped oscillation waveform.

The body of this manuscript focuses on the match between “ideal” calculated waveform characteristics and those measured in healthy, large diameter, straight arteries. The transmission line [33] flow wave model, on which the analysis is based, is described in detail in Appendix A. The approach taken in this analysis is fundamentally different from that of previous analyses of arterial stiffness, in that the focus of the analysis is on the arteries’ transient response to pulsatile flow velocity waves, not the transit time of pulse pressure waves, the focus of other more established analysis approaches. Given the novelty of the pulse flow waveform analysis approach taken, and the divergence of this approach from traditional pulse pressure wave analysis approaches, the basic approach is exploratory in nature, and subject to substantial independent verification and validation.

The perceived limitations of this novel analytic approach are discussed in more detail in the “Limitations” section below. The perceived advantages of the flow velocity analysis approach over the current pulse pressure propagation approach are described in the section entitled “Discussion.”

Arterial elasticity measurement methodology

Framework

This study applies a transmission-line formulation of pulsatile blood flow to analyze the transient flow velocity waveform measured by pulse Doppler ultrasound. The central premise is that the transient response of a large, compliant artery to an impulse of flow from the left ventricle can be represented as a damped oscillatory system governed by a second-order linear differential equation of a one-dimensional transmission line model. Under conditions of negligible viscous resistance—characteristic of macrocirculation arteries with luminal diameters greater than approximately 6 mm—the governing equation reduces to a form in which the damping coefficient (DC), damped frequency (DF), and natural frequency (NF) fully characterize the transient flow response.

With the current “gold standard” index of central artery stiffness being PWV, with associated PWV measurement standards [11–14] and databases [14] the relationship between PWV and NF is of potential medical importance. Pulse Doppler ultrasound measurement of flow velocity waveforms

Flow velocity waveforms were obtained by Hashimoto et al. [34, 35] using standard clinical pulse Doppler ultrasound systems. A narrow Doppler sample gate was positioned at the center of the artery to capture the region of maximal laminar flow and minimal turbulence. This configuration ensures that the measured waveform reflects the intrinsic elastic response of the arterial wall rather than flow disturbances near the vessel boundary.

The arteries of interest—descending thoracic aorta and femoral artery—were selected because their diameters (> 6 mm) satisfy the physical assumptions underlying the transmission-line model, including negligible viscous resistance and dominance of elastic and inertial terms.

Natural frequency – pulse wave analysis distinction

Natural Frequency values obtained from the transient waveform analysis were converted to local values of PWV using Eq. (3). The effective compliant arterial length L was set to 0.5 m, consistent with the anatomical extent of the negligible-resistance macrocirculation. NF-derived PWV values were compared with cfPWV values reported in the same populations with consistent results.

NF is distinct from PWV in that PWV is a measurement of the “global or average” velocity for pulse pressure transit between two sensors, normally on different arteries, while NF is a measurement of elasticity at a single discrete location. While these two measurements should produce results of a similar order of magnitude, they are directly comparable only if the two sensors in the PWV measurement are close together on the same artery.

The model analysis, as described in detail in Appendix A, is based on the pulse Doppler ultrasound measurement of flow velocity waveforms. However, such waveforms may also be measured using phase contrast MRI and other blood flow velocimetry approaches. However, pulse Doppler ultrasound is currently widely used in clinics and in medical practitioner’s offices worldwide, and the described analytic approach, if further verified and validated, opens a pathway to the measurement of arterial stiffness, as a biomarker of cardiovascular diseases, in clinical settings, measurements not currently practical in normal clinical and medical office settings.

Validation under multiple reflection conditions

Additional validation of the transmission-line–based predictive model was performed by incorporating both a proximal reflection site and a distal reflection site into the simulated flow velocity waveform. This analysis extends the single-reflection comparisons shown in Figs. 3 and 4 by evaluating the robustness of the predicted transient waveform under physiologically realistic multi-site reflection conditions.

The predicted waveform was compared to the ensemble-averaged measured Doppler waveform using Bland–Altman analysis. The resulting agreement was excellent, with a Bias of 0.05 cm/s and a standard deviation of 4.53 cm/s, values that fall well within the range of Doppler-to-Doppler validation studies and are consistent with the SD values reported earlier.

Clinical implications

The proposed PFV measurement and analysis methodology holds the potential for several positive clinical implications. The pulse Doppler ultrasound implementation of the methodology, with the widespread clinical use of Doppler ultrasound scanners, has the potential for making a very significant contribution to the measurement of arterial stiffness, as a predictor of all-cause mortality, in clinical settings worldwide. Currently, the measurement of arterial stiffness using PWV pressure pulse propagation techniques, with the complexity and time-consuming nature of such approaches, is limited primarily to research laboratory settings [11, 17, 18]. The measurement of arterial stiffness in clinical settings has been an objective of PWV research programs for several decades. This new PFV waveform analysis method, using pulse Doppler ultrasound, holds the potential to transition the measurement of arterial stiffness, as a predictor of all-cause mortality, from the laboratory into clinics globally.

Most current PWV pulse pressure measurement techniques involve the use of two sensors placed over two arterial segments some distance apart. As a result, the PWV obtained with the technique produces a “global” measure of arterial stiffness which does not characterize any specific location in the arteries. PWV measurement techniques do not, therefore, localize and characterize specific regions of morphology abnormality, such as regions of sclerotic plaque development. In comparison, the described new PFV measurement technique uses a single sensor which measures the flow velocity waveform at a single measurement location, enabling the elastic characterization of specific arterial locations, including regions of arterial morphology abnormality.

The measurement of PFV waveforms on either side of arterial bifurcations or other morphology changes enables the determination of the gradient of elasticity across the bifurcation or morphology change, thus enabling the determination of a measure of pulse wave reflection occurring at the measurement location [28] (see Appendix A for the relationship between reflection coefficient and arterial morphology in macrocirculation arteries). Quantifying pulse wave reflection and removing its influence in elasticity analyses of PFV waveforms, is perceived to be a future significant factor in applying the PFV analysis methodology to the mesocirculation arteries and arterial morphology abnormalities which inherently cause wave reflections.

Arterial stiffness gradient between central and peripheral arteries has also been proposed as a more accurate predictor of all-cause morality than central arterial stiffness alone [36–40]. The wide-spread use of PFV waveform measurements to assess elasticity and stiffness gradient at morphology abnormalities, made possible by Doppler ultrasound, enables the wide-spread testing of the premise that arterial stiffness gradient is a better predictor of all-cause morality, than central artery stiffness alone.

Current PWV pulse pressure measurement techniques involve the placement of sensors over arterial locations for which the artery is close to the skin surface, locations for which pressure pulsatility is measurable. In comparison, Doppler ultrasound can penetrate the body and measure flow pulsatility in internal arteries which are some distance from the skin surface. This enables the measurement of arterial stiffness in such arteries as the thoracic and abdominal aorta, the coronary, the renal and the iliac arteries, potentially substantially extending, and providing more detail, to databases of arterial stiffness.

Discussion

This exploratory study demonstrates that the transient pulse flow velocity waveform measured by Doppler ultrasound contains a rich elastic signature that can be modeled as a damped oscillatory response governed by a second-order differential equation. The resulting natural frequency (NF) provides a local, reflection-resistant, and clinically accessible index of arterial elasticity.

Unlike carotid-to-femoral PWV (cfPWV), which provides a global average stiffness across multiple arterial segments, NF is derived from the local transient response at a single measurement site. This distinction is clinically meaningful: localized stiffness abnormalities—such as focal plaque, early aortic stiffening, or post-surgical graft changes—are not detectable using global PWV methods. The NF approach enables site-specific elasticity assessment, including in deep central arteries that are inaccessible to tonometry.

A major limitation of pressure-based PWV methods is their susceptibility to wave reflection, which alters the timing and morphology of the pressure waveform. In contrast, the NF method relies on the high-frequency, early-diastolic oscillatory component of the flow waveform, which is minimally affected by reflections.

The new two-reflection validation presented here provides strong evidence of this robustness. Even with both proximal and distal reflection sites included, the predicted waveform maintained excellent agreement with the measured waveform (Bias = 0.06 cm/s; SD = 4.5 cm/s). This demonstrates that:the underlying damped-oscillator behavior of the artery is preserved despite reflection complexity,NF remains stable even when reflection amplitude and timing vary, andNF is inherently less sensitive to reflection artifacts than cfPWV.

This property positions NF as a more physiologically faithful measure of local arterial stiffness.

The assessment of arterial elasticity using pulse Doppler ultrasound measurements of transient flow velocity waveforms in the determination of natural frequency, is novel and here-to-for unexplored by the medical community. The phenomenon measured to assess arterial elasticity is the transient response of blood flow, at a central arterial location, to an impulse of flow from the left ventricle. This measurement is fundamentally different from that of measuring the transit time of pressure pulses to two spatially separate locations. The analysis process associated with this novel measurement approach is not contained within the established arterial stiffness paradigm, a paradigm calling for measurement of pulse pressures and arterial distention. This novel approach is likely to stimulate controversy within some segments of the artery research community.

The “triphasic” flow velocity waveforms of pulsatile blood flow in the central arteries are demonstrated, in this analysis, to display damped oscillatory transient behaviour. Such behaviour is consistent with the transmission line model of pulsatile flow, and with the physics of second order damped oscillating systems. The combination of Doppler ultrasound measured transient pulse flow velocity waveforms with the damped oscillatory behaviour predicted by the transmission line model of pulsatile flow, produces a simple approach to arterial elasticity evaluation. An approach which may potentially be realized in medical practice and clinical settings by widely used Doppler ultrasound systems.

Arterial elasticity, hence, impedance [41–44] to pulsatile flow, varies greatly throughout the arterial tree. The current established methodologies of arterial stiffness evaluation, using the transit time of pressure pulses, inherently limits the measurement of stiffness to an average value for a length of artery or through multiple arteries. In comparison the novel methodology described here, using Doppler ultrasound waveform measurement of transient flow velocity waveforms at specific arterial locations, enables arterial elasticity evaluation at singular locations. Single location measurement capabilities enable the evaluation of elasticity at locations of specific medical interest, such as at bifurcations, stenoses and regions of sclerotic plaque development.

Appendix A defines those arteries of diameter greater than 6 mm as “macrocirculation”, arteries in which resistive impedance to pulsatile flow is negligible. Arteries of diameter less than 1 mm have been defined [28] previously as “microcirculation”, in which the impedance to pulsatile flow is constituted by resistance and inertial reactance. The arterial region with diameters between 1 and 6 mm has been defined [28] as “mesocirculation” in which the impedance transitions from primarily reactive (negligible viscous resistance) to resistance and reactance primarily. While the terms of macrocirculation and microcirculation have been used in the literature to provide general descriptions of different areas of the arterial tree, they had not previously been defined, specifically, in relation to arterial dimensions. The above definitions of macrocirculation, mesocirculation and microcirculation represent new contributions to the medical community.

The ultrasound flow velocity measurement of arterial elasticity at two locations which bracket bifurcations and morphology changes enables the determination of elasticity gradient associated with such localized bifurcations, stenoses and plaques. Arterial stiffness gradient has been associated with increases in both pulse wave reflection, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease [45–47]. Some recent studies [36–40] indicate that peripheral to central PWV gradient is a better predictor of all-cause mortality than central PWV alone. PWV gradient has also been proposed [36] as a new “Gold Standard” for all-cause mortality prediction. The new pulse Doppler ultrasound measurement of NF on either side of arterial morphology discontinuities and bifurcations enables the relatively simple measurement of arterial stiffness and elasticity gradients across such local discontinuities.

Limitations

A major limitation of the current analysis is that the analysis is valid only to the macrocirculation region of the arterial tree (luminal diameter greater than 6 mm), a region in which viscous resistance is negligible. This is the same validity limitation applied to the physics validity of the Moens-Korteweg equation.

In this analysis the arterial tree is also assumed to be healthy, with impedance match throughout, and with negligible reflected pulse wave flow. This assumption is reasonable for healthy, large macrocirculation arteries, as reflected by the condition for zero reflection as shown in Fig. 3 and as identified by Eq. (6). However, bifurcations in the mesocirculation, have been predicted to inherently be mismatched and reflective [28]. While inherent mesocirculation reflection has been predicted [28] to be as little as 5% for an individual bifurcation in the diameter range of 3 to 4 mm, the accumulation of reflected waves from multiple closely spaced bifurcations in the mesocirculation has been predicted [28] to result in an aggregate reflection coefficient in the range of 30%, even in healthy mesocirculation arteries. While, at this stage in the analytic approach’s development, pulse reflection is assumed to be negligible, the general approach is extendable to include pulse wave reflection as discussed below in the “Future Work” section.

The current level of analysis is sufficiently complex, however, that the impact of wave reflection on measured flow waves is deferred until after the current level (non-reflective macrocirculation) is more fully validated. Also, another level of analytic complexity is feasible and is projected to be necessary for analyzing the more complex flow wave patterns associated with narrower arteries (mesocirculation and microcirculation) and arterial morphology abnormalities. Such further additional level of analysis complexity may have analogies to established pulse pressure wave analyses [51–55] for the separation of forward and reverse flowing waves. An outline for inclusion of wave reflection and mesocirculation PFV analysis is included in the section “Future Work”.

The current level of validation of this flow velocity wave analysis approach offered in this study is, by its fundamental nature, exploratory and preliminary. The match between flow wave velocity waveforms as predicted by the transmission line model, and as measuring using Doppler ultrasound, on two different arteries, while promising from a visual inspection perspective, is perceived to be an initial and preliminary indication of the potential validity and viability of the approach. An indicator of the potential usefulness of the pulse Doppler PFV measurement approach is that the study indicates that the thoracic aorta is more elastic than the femoral artery, as widely accepted, while the pulse transit time cfPWV does not provide that indication.

Another limitation of this analysis is that the analysis uses constant values for blood viscosity and elastic modulus parameters, neglecting any effect that pressure or flow may have on these parameters. These are the same linear approximations used by Womersley [26, 27] and others [41, 42]. As discussed by Nichols [42], the impact of nonlinearities has been assessed [43, 44] to be relatively insignificant, particularly in relation to the potential for the nonlinearities giving rise to inter-modulation products of the Fourier harmonics of the heart rate.

Standardization of probe pressure, placement protocol, and Doppler insonation angle, consistent with established vascular sonography guidelines, will therefore be a necessary requirement in any future formal validation study of this methodology. The use of ensemble-averaged waveforms, as employed in the reference datasets of Hashimoto et al. [34, 35] used in this exploratory study, partially mitigates the influence of beat-to-beat and operator-induced measurement variability; however, operator training and standardized acquisition protocols will remain important sources of variability to be addressed in future independent validation initiatives.

Conclusions

The analysis described in this study was based on the application of the scientific method to the analysis of transient blood flow in central arteries. The hypothesis of this analysis was that the transient blood flow response of a central artery to an impulse of flow from the left ventricle may be described, mathematically and approximately, by a transmission line model of pulsatile flow. The transmission line model is that of a linear second order differential equation, the solution for which is a damped oscillating flow velocity waveform. In an artery without wave reflection the flow velocity waveform is described by two independent parameters, a damped frequency and a damping coefficient. These two parameters can be derived from the measurement of the systolic peak flow velocity and the peak reverse overshoot flow velocity in the transient response. The transmission line model predicts transient flow velocity waveforms which are then compared to measured flow velocity waveforms. In this study two sets of measured flow velocity waveforms, one set taken in the femoral artery and the other set taken in the thoracic aorta, were used for comparison to waveforms predicted by the model. The match between predicted and measured results in this study was found to be good by visual inspection, however, the number of available calibrated measured waveforms available was limited, (an ensemble-average on 360 human subjects). Hence the demonstrated validity of the analytic approach is very preliminary. A greater number of clinical pulse Doppler measurements of PFV waveforms need to be taken and compared with predicted PFVs, preferably by more than one independent clinic or laboratory, to further test the validity of the analytic approach.

The analysis provided for the definition of a dependent parameter, natural frequency, which is calculated from the independent parameters, damped frequency and damping coefficient. The analysis derived a direct relationship between natural frequency and an established index of arterial stiffness, pulse wave velocity. The analysis demonstrated that the match between pulse wave velocity, as calculated from the natural frequency, and that measured using an established two sensor transit time measurement approach was also good, providing further preliminary validation of the analytic approach. While the match between predicted and measured pulse wave velocities is also preliminary, the predicted pulse wave velocity was localized at specific arterial locations, while the two-sensor measured pulse wave velocity was non-localized.

The overall conclusion of this study is that the approach of using a transmission line model to analyze pulse flow transient response waveforms in determining arterial elasticity, which is described by “natural frequency”, provides a sufficiently close preliminary match to measured pulse flow transient waveforms, to merit additional and independent study.

Future work

Based on the work to date the primary preliminary observation is that the initial matches between predicted and measured transient pulse flow waveforms are, by visual inspection and Bland -Altman analysis, sufficiently good that further work is merited. Such future work includes verifying that the derivation of the flow waveform, as derived from the transmission line model, is sufficient for arterial elasticity determination. Future work also includes the independent validation of the analysis methodology through comparison of other measured transient flow velocity waveforms, in a variety of central arteries, with those predicted by the analysis method and model.
