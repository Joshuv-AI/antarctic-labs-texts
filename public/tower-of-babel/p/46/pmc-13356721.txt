Digital Analysis of Upper Airway Morphology in Obstructive Sleep Apnea Syndrome Using Cone Beam Computed Tomography
Authors: Yiyu Zeng, Zhengrong Gao, Yingjun Zhang, Qian Yang, Wenhui Ge, Mengmeng Li, Xinying Wang, Yi Yu, Xiaoyan Xie, Shisheng Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Introduction and aimsObstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) is one of the most common sleep disorders, which poses a serious burden on the quality of life of the population. The incidence rate of OSAS in the population is very high and most cases have not been confirmed in time. In addition, many patients do not fully alleviate the symptoms of upper respiratory tract stenosis after receiving treatment. Therefore, OSAS patients need timely diagnosis and effective intervention.MethodsBasic clinical data and sleep breathing parameters were collected from OSAS patients diagnosed in the otolaryngology department. Subsequently, these patients underwent cone beam computed tomography (CBCT) examination in the stomatology department to screen upper airway parameters. Correlation analyses between awake upper airway characteristics and sleep breathing events were performed, culminating in the construction of a numerical upper airway assessment model for OSAS patients.ResultsThree-dimensional (3D) reconstruction of CBCT images enabled direct visualization of upper airway anatomical morphology. The upper airway in OSAS patients demonstrated anatomical stenosis compared to healthy individuals, which correlated with alterations in sleep breathing parameters. The digital model, integrating sleep breathing parameters and upper airway measurements, effectively explained these variations.ConclusionThis numerical model, based on sleep breathing and upper airway characteristics, is primarily for early screening and risk stratification of OSAS. Validated in an otolaryngology setting, it guides referral for definitive diagnosis through polysomnography (PSG) and detailed clinical evaluation, within which its predictions must be interpreted.

Introduction

According to statistics, nearly 425 million people worldwide are affected by sleep apnea and require treatment,1 making it a global health burden. This aligns with a growing body of evidence linking oral and craniofacial conditions to systemic health, as evidenced by the association between Dento-Skeletal Malformation, periodontal disease, and various medical conditions including sleep disorders and diabetes.2,3 OSAS is one of the most common sleep disorders, characterized by snoring, apnea, and daytime sleepiness during sleep.4 The prevalence of OSAS in the adult population has been reported to be high, ranging from 9% to 38%.5 However, more than 80% of OSAS cases remain undiagnosed due to the lack of proper knowledge and obvious early symptoms in some patients.6 OSAS is usually considered to be an anatomical problem of the upper airway, typically characterized by recurrent narrowing and collapse of the upper airway during sleep.7 Specific craniofacial skeletal patterns, particularly Class II retrognathism, have been shown by a systematic review with network meta-analysis to be significantly and clinically associated with reduced upper airway volume.8 OSAS associated with upper airway stenosis can lead to symptoms such as cognitive impairment9 and cardiovascular disease.10 Therefore, timely detection of OSAS patients and early intervention are necessary to prevent long-term adverse effects.

PSG is considered the gold standard for the diagnosis of various sleep disorders.11 It records the human body’s nighttime sleep condition in an all-round way, which helps physicians to fully understand and correctly diagnose patients’ sleep disorders. However, the testing process has strict requirements for patients, is time-consuming, and requires a professional management team, so PSG tests cannot be widely carried out in primary hospitals. In addition, clinics usually use various types of sleep scales to assist in screening and stratifying patients with suspected OSAS.12,13 However, these questionnaires are susceptible to patient subjectivity, which leads to their low specificity in detecting OSAS, only 10%-36%.14 More importantly, these questionnaires do not accurately reflect abnormal changes in the anatomy of the upper airway, which may cause some difficulty in determining the patient’s condition. Therefore, there is a need for more convenient and accurate tools to screen for OSAS.

CBCT is widely used in dentistry and has completely solved the problem of image overlap and distortion in conventional dental X-ray equipment. With its high isotropic spatial resolution, it can not only reconstruct the entire craniofacial region and measure the maxillofacial bone structure from cross-sectional views, tilted and multiview perspectives, but also reconstruct fine anatomical details.15 In the latest clinical practice, CBCT has been used to visualize the external contours of peri-implant soft tissues.16 In addition to soft and hard tissue measurements, an increasing number of researchers have found that gases presenting a different hypodense image from that of soft and hard tissues can be clearly observed in CBCT images and have attempted to further investigate this by 3D reconstruction. Wen et al. used CBCT images to assess the aerodynamic and morphological characteristics of the anatomical upper airway in patients with OSAS.17 The study showed that the upper airways of patients with OSAS exhibited characteristic 3D morphological and aerodynamic findings such as higher shear stress, greater airway narrowing, faster airflow, higher static pressure, airway resistance, and the images visualized a collapsed pattern of the anatomical upper airway. It is crucial to acknowledge that these CBCT-derived findings reflect static anatomy during wakefulness, distincting from the dynamic collapse occurring during sleep.18 The CBCT device provides patients with a low-dose filming protocol, which greatly reduces the radiation dose to which they are exposed. In addition, since the time to acquire images is greatly reduced, this helps to acquire images of patients who have difficulty remaining still.19 Importantly, CBCT scans frequently reveal incidental findings in the upper airway region, such as adenoid or tonsillar hypertrophy, which are recognized as clinically significant and warranting referral, underscoring the clinical relevance of detailed airway assessment in dental and maxillofacial imaging.20

Based on the above, CBCT is widely used to characterize the static anatomical morphology of the upper airway. In this study, the term “narrowing” or “stenosis” is used descriptively to refer to morphological features observed on CBCT images, and we investigate the association between these anatomical characteristics and OSAS. Specifically, we utilize CBCT to obtain objective measurements of the upper airway anatomy in awake patients. This allows us to identify and characterize sites of anatomical narrowing that are potentially implicated in OSAS, and to explore the correlations between these static anatomical parameters and clinical symptoms. Furthermore, we aim to construct a digital model for assessing the static anatomical configuration of the upper airway using CBCT data. The primary goal of this model is to provide detailed and accurate anatomical data.

Materials and methods

Research subject

Selection of study subjects

Participants were recruited from the Otolaryngology outpatient clinic of the Second Xiangya Hospital to access the necessary specialist diagnostic equipment and to target the moderate-to-severe OSAS patient population central to our study. After obtaining the informed consent of the patients, 75 patients diagnosed with OSAS by PSG were selected, and the basic clinical information of the patients, including name, gender, age, height, weight, neck circumference (NC), chest circumference (CC), abdominal circumference (AC), hip circumference (HC), living habits, and examination results, were collected to construct a clinical sample database. At the same time, 42 healthy volunteers were recruited as the control group.

PSG monitoring

PSG was performed by certified technicians from the Department of Otorhinolaryngology, and all patients underwent approximately 7 hours of PSG in a comfortable and quiet environment in the hospital. Sleep breathing related indices were collected: apnea-hypopnea index (AHI); percutaneous arterial oxygen saturation (SpO2), which was recorded in this study in OSAS patients with mean SpO2 (MSpO2) and minimum SpO2 (LSpO2); oxygen desaturation index (ODI); ct90 (%).

CBCT examination and airway 3D imaging

The patient signed an informed consent form. The patient was placed in a seated position with the head kept fixed and the upper and lower jaws in a resting jaw position. The CBCT scan showed a field of view sufficient to cover the nasopharynx, oropharynx and upper airway (Figure 1). After acquisition of the CBCT scan, the digital imaging and communications in medicine data was imported into Invivo 7 software for further analysis. The 3D reconstruction of the upper airway is shown in Figure 2. The applied airway tool was used to define the measurement limits between the upper airways, ranging from the volume between the luminal apex and the end of the cricoid cartilage. Cephalometric measurements

Lateral cephalograms were reconstructed from the pretreatment CBCT scans for all subjects using dedicated imaging software. Cephalometric analysis was performed on these standardized lateral images. To ensure measurement reliability, all tracings and landmark identifications were performed by a single experienced examiner. Intraexaminer reliability was assessed by re-measuring a random subset of 30 images after a 2-week interval. The method error was calculated using Dahlberg’s formula and intraclass correlation coefficients (ICC), demonstrating high reproducibility (Dahlberg error <0.5° for angular measurements; ICC > 0.95).

Key cephalometric measurements were recorded to define the skeletal pattern and describe craniofacial morphology. Sagittal skeletal measurements included: Sella-Nasion-A point angle (SNA), Sella-Nasion-B point angle (SNB), and the ANB difference (ANB angle, A point–Nasion–B point angle, the angle formed by the subspinale point (A), nasion point (N), and supramental point (B)). Statistical methods

SPSS 26.0 statistical software was applied for statistical analysis of the data. Comparisons of continuous variables between the 2 groups were performed using the t test, and comparisons of categorical variables were performed using the χ2 test. Correlations between parameters were assessed using the Spearman rank correlation test. Finally, linear regression analysis was performed to correct the goodness-of-fit and finally construct a model for digital assessment of airway in OSAS patients. The subject work characteristic curve (ROC) was used to assess the early assessment role of the digital model in OSAS. All significance tests were 2-sided and P < .05 was considered statistically significant.

Consistency test

To improve the accuracy of the experiment, the oral radiologist and the experimental operator jointly measured the upper airway after 3D reconstruction of the same CBCT image. The consistency of the measurements within the 2 examiners is now tested. The intragroup correlation coefficient >0.90 confirmed the strong consistency and high reproducibility of the CBCT measurements.

The results of χ2 test showed that there was a statistically significant difference in gender between the 2 groups, with OSAS being more prevalent in males (Table 3). Analysis of PSG monitoring

PSG is a standard means of diagnosing OSAS, and patients with OSAS have characteristic manifestations in PSG monitoring. t tests of PSG monitoring results between the OSAS group and the normal population group showed statistically significant differences in PSG monitoring indices, such as AHI, ODI, MSpO2, LSpO2, and CT90 (%), between the 2 groups (P < .05) (Table 5). Compared with the normal population, the OSAS population had more nocturnal sleep apneas, more oxygen decreases occurring on average per hour throughout sleep, and significantly lower mean and minimum oxygen saturation, as well as a higher percentage of time with SpO2 <90% of the entire sleep period. In the normal healthy population, SpO2 was consistently maintained above 90% during sleep despite occasional oxygen-reducing events. Constructing a digital assessment model for OSAS

Correlation analysis between basic clinical information and PSG results in OSAS patients

We first analysed the correlation between clinical data and AHI in OSAS patients to clarify the extent to which these factors influence AHI. Linear regression results (Table 7) showed that BMI explained 10.5% of the variation in AHI, NC explained 23.7% of the variation in AHI, CC explained 13.0% of the variation in AHI, and AC explained 17.0% of the variation in AHI. Increased BMI, NC, CC, and AC led to an increase in the number of sleep apnoeas per hour at night in patients with OSAS. The effect of HC on AHI was not statistically significant (P > .05). In addition, BMI, NC, CC, AC, and HC can be used to explain changes in ODI, and their enlargement could increase the occurrence of oxygen reduction events during sleep in OSAS patients (Support Table 1). BMI, neck circumference and abdominal circumference could be used to explain changes in MSpO2. Their increase can lead to a decrease in mean blood oxygen content. Discussion

OSAS is a common yet underdiagnosed condition, with less than 15% of patients with clinical signs being diagnosed in a timely manner. It increases the risk of vascular and metabolic diseases.10,22 Patients with OSAS have a reduced risk of stroke after treatment.23 People at moderate to severe risk of OSAS are more likely to have severe COVID-19 symptoms compared to those at mild risk of OSAS.24 Thus, the early detection of OSAS patients not only mitigates the risk of related adverse events, but also facilitates the subsequent management of OSAS.

Given that OSAS can induce the aforementioned adverse events, experts have gradually explored the relevant causative factors and risk factors for sleep disorders in clinical practice. Weight gain and obesity are the factors most closely associated with the development of OSAS.25 BMI is an indicator closely related to the total amount of body fat. The average BMI of OSAS patients in this study was 29.06, which was considerably higher than that of the normal population. Meanwhile, patients with OSAS had larger neck, chest, abdominal, and hip circumferences. Fat accumulation in the neck leads to “pharyngeal obesity.” The flaccid state of the muscles during sleep cannot support the weight gained from fat accumulation, resulting in pressure ptosis, which in turn compresses the upper airway, causing narrowing and collapse of the upper airway. The results of the correlation analysis in this study further demonstrated that changes in BMI, neck circumference, chest circumference, abdominal circumference, and hip circumference in patients with OSAS can have an impact on sleep breathing events. OSAS has a gender difference and is more likely to be induced in male patients, and our study corroborates this result. The reason for this may lie in the longer length of the upper airway in male patients and the fact that males experience more fluid shifts during sleep, leading to soft tissue edema in the neck. Estrogen protects sleep respiratory activity in women by modulating the chin and tongue muscles to dilate the upper airway.26 Chronic smoking and alcohol consumption can lead to the development of OSAS. In the present study, the number of people with smoking and drinking habits in the OSAS group was significantly higher than that in the normal healthy population. Alcohol causes collapse of the upper airway during sleep by relaxing the neck muscles,27 and smoke makes the airway narrow by increasing the retention of fluid in the upper airway.28 Therefore, smoking and alcohol cessation should be advocated as a preventive measure for OSAS in the subsequent therapeutic management of patients.29

A sample size of one hundred seventeen cases was included in this study, which is a larger number when compared to existing related studies.30 Our study included an experimental group of 75 patients, which contributed to the reliability and precision of the data. We also included a control group in our study, which consisted of 42 normal individuals without anatomical upper airway stenosis and sleep apnea as confirmed by PSG monitoring and CBCT photography. This control group provided valuable measurements of airway development in a healthy population under natural conditions, allowing us to observe OSAS associated with upper airway anatomical factors. We compared the upper airway morphology of patients with OSAS with that of the normal healthy population in the awake state. The results of these two aspects suggest that VT, Smin, and (AP/RL)min of the upper airway are important factors in the assessment of the severity of OSAS. The results of the aerodynamic analysis of the upper airway in OSAS showed that there were significant differences in the maximum upper airway velocities, maximum wall shear stresses, maximum wall hydrostatic pressures, and resistances in patients with different degrees of OSAS severity. There was a direct correlation between upper airway resistance and the anatomical characteristics of the upper airway in patients with OSAS, which was negatively correlated with VT and Smin of the upper airway. Meanwhile, the results of the correlation analysis showed that VT, Smin, and (AP/RL)min could be used to explain the changes in AHI, ODI, MSpO2, and CT90 (%). Therefore, we used VT, Smin, and (AP/RL)min as the main parameters for constructing the digital model.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, OSAS is a lifelong disease for most patients. This study was provisionally followed up for 3 months, and differences in the long-term results of surgical treatment may occur. Therefore, the follow-up of patients in this study is still ongoing. Second, despite its advantages in widespread availability, cost-effectiveness, and patient tolerance, the static CBCT imaging which captures anatomy in an awake, nonsleeping state, has inherent limitations in directly reflecting the dynamic pathophysiology of airway collapse during sleep. The generalizability of our findings may be constrained by the study cohort, which was recruited from a single tertiary otorhinolaryngology hospital. Such a clinical setting may lead to an overrepresentation of patients with moderate to severe OSA who are referred for specialized care. Consequently, our results may not be fully applicable to individuals with mild OSA or to broader community-based screening populations. The cross-sectional design of this study limits causal inference regarding the relationships between anatomical parameters and OSA status. The observed associations are correlational. In addition to soft tissues, jawbone tissues can also affect upper airway volume. The volumes of the oral cavity and upper airway differed significantly between class I and class III craniomaxillofacial types, thus serving as a possible risk indicator for the development of OSAS.31 A significant relationship was found between the width and height of the hard palate and the total volume of the upper airway.32 We cannot rule out the influence of unmeasured confounding variables (eg, detailed neuromuscular tone, specific fat distribution patterns) or the possibility of reverse causality.

To address these limitations, future research should aim to: (1) establish prospective, multicenter cohorts that include participants from various healthcare settings (eg, community clinics, secondary, and tertiary hospitals) or population-based screenings to enhance representativeness; (2) employ longitudinal designs to better elucidate causal pathways; and (3) integrate static CBCT anatomical assessments with dynamic functional evaluations, such as drug-induced sleep endoscopy or dynamic MRI, to construct more comprehensive diagnostic models that account for both structural predisposition and dynamic collapse risk.

The digital model constructed in this study demonstrated good sensitivity in the early screening of OSAS patients, but it still needs to be optimized by including more samples in future studies to improve the sensitivity and specificity of the model so that it can be better utilized in the early detection of OSAS. We reiterate that the clinical diagnosis and management of OSAS must be grounded in a comprehensive evaluation, primarily based on PSG and thorough clinical assessment. The anatomical data provided by CBCT and derived from this model should be considered a contributory element within this integrated, multimodal diagnostic framework.
