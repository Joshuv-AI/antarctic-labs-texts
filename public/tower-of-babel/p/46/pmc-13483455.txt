Wafer-scale 2D MoS2 transistors with self-aligned angstrom gate length and nanometer channel length
Authors: Ziming Wang, Anhan Liu, Hengbin Ding, Fan Wu, Caifang Gao, Yizhe Guo, Ruifang Xue, Peigen Zhang, Yanming Liu, Tao Deng, Tian-Ling Ren, He Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As conventional silicon scaling approaches fundamental physical limits, two-dimensional semiconductors offer a promising pathway for continued transistor miniaturization. Here we show an ultra-scaled vertical MoS2 transistor that combines a sub-1 nm gate and a 10 nm channel length, realized through a dual-self-aligned fabrication strategy. In this architecture, the exposed edge of a monolayer graphene at etched sidewalls serves as an atomically thin gate electrode, while the sidewalls also act as self-aligned masks for defining an ultra-short MoS2 channel through oblique-angle deposition. This co-scaling strategy yields an on-state current density of 23 μA/μm and on/off current ratio exceeding 10⁶. Systematic device simulations further elucidate the electrostatic control, effective gate length, and tunneling-limited scaling behavior of this vertical architecture. Beyond individual transistors, we demonstrate wafer-scale arrays and their integration into logic circuits (inverters, NAND, NOR gates), underscoring the scalability and integration potential of this platform.

Introduction

Continued advances in integrated circuits have been driven by the downscaling of silicon-based field-effect transistors (FETs), enabling improvements in performance, circuit-level integration density and energy efficiency1,2. Over the decades, device architectures have evolved from traditional planar transistors to FinFETs, and are now approaching gate-all-around FET configurations3,4. However, as transistors advance toward 1 nm technology nodes and gate lengths shrink below 12 nm, silicon-based FET scaling faces intrinsic material limits4,5. Overcoming these barriers demands innovations across materials, architectures, and integration5–7. Among promising beyond-silicon technologies, low-dimensional materials, particularly two-dimensional (2D) transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDs), offer compelling avenues for continued transistor scaling8–10. Their atomically thin bodies ensure superior electrostatic control, suppressing short-channel effects. Nevertheless, simultaneously reducing both gate and channel lengths to 10 nm remains a challenge, limited by lithography resolution limits, stringent alignment requirements, and the absence of architectures that can intrinsically achieve such precision11.

While various strategies have exploited the atomic thickness of low-dimensional materials to scale either the gate length or the channel length individually12,13, concurrent scaling of both dimensions remains challenging. For example, single-walled carbon nanotube (SWCNT) gates have demonstrated physical gate lengths near 1 nm14, and sidewall MoS2 transistors using monolayer graphene as a gate have reduced gate lengths to 0.34 nm15. Nonetheless, these approaches suffer from alignment challenges between the gate and source/drain electrodes, resulting in channel lengths exceeding 500 nm. Liu et al. demonstrated vertical transistors with channel lengths down to 0.65 nm using vdW integration, but global gating prevented full co-integration of scaled gate and channel dimensions16. Achieving sub-10 nm gate alignment between source and drain is essential for concurrent scaling17. While recent work has demonstrated co-scaled gate and channel lengths through mechanical exfoliation and folding of van der Waals heterostructures18, a wafer-scalable, top-down fabrication route to concurrent sub-dimensional scaling remains a key technical obstacle. Self-aligned fabrication offers a solution to the challenges of transistor scaling by employing the internal gate structure as a mask to define the source and drain, inherently aligning the gate and channel regions19.

In this work, we introduce a dual-self-aligned vertical architecture in which monolayer graphene sidewalls define the sub-1 nm gate, and oblique-angle deposition forms a 10 nm MoS2 channel precisely aligned to the gate edge. Despite aggressive scaling, the device exhibits strong electrostatic control, with an on-state current density of 23 μA/μm, an on/off current ratio exceeding 106 under a 1 V bias. Quantum transport simulation confirms that this MoS2 architecture inherently suppresses direct source-drain tunneling, operating reliably down to a 5 nm channel limit. Furthermore, the framework’s versatility was demonstrated by combining a conformal channel with an alternative dielectric hardmask, expanding the electrostatic field to maximize gate control and significantly enhance drive performance. Finally, the self-aligned approach with circuit-level integration was demonstrated by implementing wafer-scale inverters, NAND, and NOR gates, highlighting the potential of this method for extending the physical scaling limits of two-dimensional logic circuits. These results establish a practical, scalable pathway toward transistor miniaturization, providing a feasible route to extend Moore’s law into the post-silicon era.

Results

Fabrication of dual-self-aligned vertical transistors

Figure 1c illustrates the dual-self-aligned fabrication process and schematic of the ultra-scaled MoS2 transistors. Monolayer graphene was first wet-transferred to a silicon substrate with 45 nm HfO2. To realize vertical sidewalls, HfO2 was selected as the bottom dielectric of the Si-based substrate instead of SiO2. The all-HfO2 dielectric stack eliminates differential etching, yielding uniform vertical profiles. A comparison between SiO2 and HfO2 as back-gate dielectrics on Si substrates confirms that Si-based HfO2 significantly improves the etch uniformity (Supplementary Fig. 1a, b). In addition, the Raman spectrum of graphene is shown in Supplementary Fig. 2. The as-fabricated graphene transistor with an HfO2 back-gate substrate was measured (Supplementary Fig. 3), exhibiting good electrical conductivity. A Cr/Pd electrode was then deposited on one side to serve as the graphene gate contact. Subsequently, a dense high-k HfO2 was deposited on the graphene surface. As graphene lacks the dangling bonds required for high-k nucleation20, a 3 nm Al seed layer was deposited by thermal evaporation and naturally oxidized to Al2O3, promoting uniform HfO2 growth. Next, a Cr/Pd metal layer was deposited and patterned on top of the blocking dielectric to serve as the vertical electric field screening layer, which simultaneously functions as a self-aligned hard mask for the subsequent vertical etching process. After etching to form the vertical step structure (Fig. 1d), the atomically thin sidewall edge of the monolayer graphene is fully exposed, definitively establishing the sub-1 nm physical gate length defined by the monolayer graphene thickness. The profile of the graphene gate was further verified by EDS elemental mapping (Supplementary Fig. 4). Following the etching process to form the sidewall structure, a HfO2 layer with a thickness of 8 nm was deposited on the sidewall surface as a dielectric. Subsequently, CVD-grown monolayer MoS2 was wet-transferred and patterned, where precise control of the etching height is critical to ensure the adhesion and continuity of wet-transferred MoS2. Insufficient adhesion can introduce interfacial gaps, leading to degraded electrostatic control and device-to-device variability. Finally, the source and drain contacts are fabricated using oblique-angle deposition. A window pattern covering the MoS2 channel was defined, and a thin Bi/Pd bilayer was evaporated at an oblique angle (Fig. 1e). This angled deposition defines the ultra-short channel via shadow masking, self-aligning to the graphene gate edge. By combining high-resolution electron-beam lithography with this self-aligned oblique-angle deposition, the approach achieves highly scalable contact lengths down to ~60 nm, explicitly verifying that the lateral contact pitch can be deterministically co-scaled with the vertical channel dimensions (Supplementary Fig. 5). The broad architectural versatility of this self-aligned methodology was further demonstrated through two structural modifications. First, the metallic screening layer was replaced by a SiO2 dielectric spacer without adding gate complexity (Supplementary Fig. 6). In this structure, the gate electrode consists of graphene at the sidewall edge, with its thickness directly defining the gate dimension. Accurate sidewall etching height is critical for precise gate-channel alignment. Excessive etching height can cause mechanical fracture of MoS2 during the subsequent transfer process, as illustrated in the cross-sectional transmission electron microscopy (TEM) imaging (top panel of Fig. 1f). By carefully tuning the thicknesses of each layer in the stack and adjusting the etching time (with an etching rate of approximately 1.1 nm/s), we determined the optimal etching depth to be 50 nm (bottom panel of Fig. 1f). This etching height balances the need for structural integrity during MoS2 adhesion while maintaining compatibility with oblique-angle deposition processes. The channel material is a monolayer of MoS2 adhered to the sidewall structure, where the channel length is defined by utilizing the sidewall as a mask. By modulating the evaporation angle α, the projection length of the channel can be systematically tuned. Figure 1g shows the scanning electron microscope (SEM) images of different channels fabricated with varying evaporation angle α. The planar projected spacing between the self-aligned channel regions can be reduced from 65 nm to 1 nm. This demonstrates the effectiveness of oblique-angle deposition in defining ultra-short channels. Structural and electrical characterization

To validate the structural and electrical characteristics of the proposed transistor, we conducted detailed microscopic analyses and electrical measurements. A false-colorized scanning electron microscope (SEM) image of a representative scaled MoS₂ transistor is shown in Fig. 2a, featuring four electrical terminals: source (S), drain (D), metal screening layer (Sc), and a graphene contact electrode (G). In baseline operation, the top Bi/Pd electrode serves as a source, the bottom Bi/Pd contact as a drain, and the graphene edge as the sub-1 nm physical gate length. During intrinsic gating evaluations, the screening layer is held at 0 V to terminate vertical electric fields, ensuring exclusive electrostatic control by the atomically thin graphene gate consistent with established operating principles15. Alternatively, this metal terminal can be actively biased to function as an auxiliary gate to modulate the access region resistance (Supplementary Fig. 8). Supplementary Fig. 9a illustrates the cross-sectional TEM image of a representative dual-self-aligned MoS2 FET, with its constituent elemental distribution further confirmed by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping in Supplementary Fig. 9b. The corresponding device (Lg ~ 0.34nm, Lch ~35 nm) achieves an off-state current as low as 1 pA, an on-state current density above 20 μA/μm, and an on/off current ratio exceeding 108 at a drain bias of 1 V. Building upon this validated foundation, the geometric limits of the integration scheme were systematically extended to explore the scaling limits. As directly demonstrated in Fig. 2b and the corresponding EDS elemental maps in Fig. 2c, this dual-self-aligned process successfully yields an extreme scaled MoS2 transistor with a sub-nanometer gate length (Lg) co-integrated with a 10 nm channel length (Lch). The distance between the upper and lower terrace metals naturally defines the ultra-short vertical channel, with the effective channel length strictly determined by the step height and the angular profile of the metal deposition. Figure 2d presents the transfer characteristics of the ultra-scaled MoS2 transistor measured at varying drain biases, showing consistent gate modulation across all bias conditions. Even in this extreme scaling regime, the devices also exhibit robust switching capabilities, including high on/off current ratios of 106, large on-state current density of ~23 μA/μm under a drain bias of 1 V without any auxiliary gating (Fig. 2d). Furthermore, the output characteristics demonstrate current modulation under varying gate voltages (Fig. 2e). Driven by the enhanced electrostatic matching inherent to simultaneous gate and channel co-scaling, this current density is higher than carbon nanotube or graphene-edge gated architectures15,22. Key short-channel metrics extracted from these curves reveal a subthreshold swing (SS) of ~412 mV/dec and a drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL) of ~1000 mV/V. The observed electrical characteristics are presently limited by extrinsic process-induced non-idealities, which encompass a relatively thick gate dielectric, a finite interface trap density, and parasitic series resistance introduced by the partially ungated access region (Supplementary Fig. 11, Supplementary Table 1). Alongside the analysis of these structural factors, a quantitative extraction of the contact resistance was performed to thoroughly evaluate the interfacial limitations (Supplementary Fig. 12). To confirm the high reproducibility and operational stability of this self-aligned process, statistical transfer characteristics of multiple fabricated MoS2 devices are presented in Fig. 2f. In addition, while 10 nm is presented as the representative channel length, the inherent process variations of the oblique-angle deposition naturally yield functional devices at the sub-10 nm scale within these measured arrays. Wafer-scale statistics indicate a functional yield of ~60% for devices with a ~65 nm channel length fabricated via a 60° deposition angle, while ultra-scaled ~10 nm channel devices maintain a yield of ~15% (Supplementary Fig. 13). The successful fabrication of multiple device batches with tunable channel lengths further validates the fabrication stability and dimensional versatility of the process (Supplementary Fig. 14).

The broad architectural versatility of this dual-self-aligned method is demonstrated through a combined structural and material reconfiguration. Specifically, the conventional metal screening layer was structurally replaced by a SiO2 dielectric hardmask to eliminate the ungated access regions and maximize the fringing electrostatic coverage (Supplementary Fig. 15), while integrating a conformal In2O3 channel facilitates the formation of low-contact-resistance interfaces (device TEM image shown in the inset of Fig. 2g). The transistor delivers an on-state current density reaching 172 μA/μm and a SS of 127 mV/dec (Fig. 2g). This result directly confirms that the vertical architecture inherently supports competitive on-state currents once extrinsic interfacial limitations are mitigated. Finally, a benchmarking analysis of Lg versus channel length Lch against state-of-the-art ultra-scaled transistors based on other works is presented in Fig. 2h6,14–17,22–51. While most prior works remain focused on single-dimension scaling, several recent studies using high-resolution electron-beam lithography have achieved co-scaling down to the 20 nm regime; however, those approaches are still challenged by strict overlay accuracy requirements6,45. In contrast, our dual-self-aligned strategy enables the co-integration of sub-1 nm gate electrodes and 10 nm channels within a single device, demonstrating the shortest gate length and channel length co-scaling reported to date. These results demonstrate that the self-aligned strategy offers a pathway for the extreme miniaturization of future 2D electronic devices, while also establishing a foundational framework for their comprehensive scaling in integrated circuits.

Device simulation

To investigate the electrostatic behavior, scaling limits, and transport physics of the proposed ultra-scaled vertical transistors, we performed comprehensive TCAD simulations. A semi-classical drift-diffusion framework was employed52, with model parameters rigorously calibrated against the experimental transfer characteristics of the fabricated devices (Supplementary Table 2). As illustrated by the cross-sectional electric field distribution and carrier density profiles in Fig. 3a, the atomically thin graphene gate edge delivers effective electrostatic control over the vertical MoS₂ channel through fringing field extension. The effective gate-controlled length was extracted following established methodologies15. Specifically, an off-state current criterion of 10-9 A/μm was established, corresponding to an extracted threshold voltage of −2.73 V. By subsequently defining the off-state carrier density boundary at 1012 cm−3, an effective gate length of approximately 4.85 nm was quantitatively determined. Alongside this fringing modulation, the metallic shielding layer positioned beneath the source contact successfully suppresses field penetration into the MoS₂ region, thereby preventing unintended gate coupling. The spatial energy band evolution under experimental bias conditions was visualized by fixing the graphene gate and drain terminals at 1 V and progressively shifting the source position toward the drain, effectively emulating channel length scaling (Fig. 3b). At the longer channel lengths of 20 nm, the limited electrostatic reach of the graphene gate results in a sizeable potential barrier near the source, suppressing carrier injection and resulting in low on-state current. As the channel is progressively shortened, the barrier is gradually flattened. Once the barrier is eliminated, the current saturates. To further explore the intrinsic device transport limits at extreme scaling, we performed quantum transport simulations within a non-equilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) framework53. Figure 3c presents the energy band diagram and energy-resolved current spectrum along the vertical channel for a device with a 3 nm channel length. The current spectrum exhibits clear contributions from energies below the channel potential barrier, directly confirming the onset of direct source-drain tunneling in this deeply scaled regime. This result is consistent with the transistor scaling framework, where tunneling-induced leakage defines the fundamental limit. This transport degradation is directly reflected in Fig. 3d, which plots the simulated transfer characteristics for devices with channel lengths scaling from 16 nm down to 2 nm. Both the off-state and on-state currents increase as the channel length decreases, highlighting an intrinsic trade-off between maximizing the drive capability and suppressing the off-state leakage. Notably, as the channel length scales below 5 nm, the leakage current increases sharply while the enhancement in on-state current becomes marginal, indicating a severe degradation in the overall switching characteristics of the device. These results confirm that our experimentally demonstrated 10 nm channel length devices operate above the critical tunneling limit, maintaining well-defined switching characteristics without significant tunneling-induced degradation. Finally, we assess the performance of this architecture in comparison with silicon transistor technology. Figure 3e compares the off-state leakage current of the scaled MoS₂ transistors with identical silicon geometries across various channel lengths. Owing to the larger transport effective mass of monolayer MoS2 (mt = 0.48), the MoS2 transistors exhibit an intrinsic suppression of direct source-drain tunneling. This material property results in leakage currents two to three orders of magnitude lower than their silicon counterparts at identical physical dimensions. This material-specific advantage enables more robust electrostatic control at deeply scaled dimensions, highlighting the inherent suitability of 2D semiconductors for transistor scaling beyond the physical limits of silicon54. Beyond the effective mass, the physical thickness of the channel material itself plays a critical role in preserving electrostatic integrity. As demonstrated in Fig. 3f, the SS of the transistors is highly dependent on the channel thickness (Tch). The atomically thin nature of monolayer MoS2 ensures an ultra-confined electrostatic transport plane, allowing the device to maintain a significantly steeper SS compared to thicker silicon channels as physical dimensions are aggressively reduced.

Wafer-scale logic circuit integration

Following the validation of the structural design and electrical characteristics, we further explored the integration compatibility of this device architecture at the circuit level. Figure 4a shows the 2-inch wafer-scale integration of logic gates, including inverters, NAND, and NOR gates, all fabricated via a vertical MoS2 architecture and self-aligned process. The pseudo-NMOS circuit topology used for all logic gates is illustrated in Fig. 4b, with corresponding optical images provided in Supplementary Fig. 16. Specifically, the inverter comprises a depletion-mode load transistor and an enhancement-mode pull-down transistor connected via a zero-gate-voltage (zero-VGS) configuration55. By tailoring the width-to-length (W/L) ratios, we balanced performance between output swing and drive capability. Figure 4c, d present the inverter’s static voltage transfer curve and dynamic response. At VDD = 2 V, the inverter exhibits nearly full-swing switching with output voltages of 1 V (high) and 0 V (low), yielding a voltage gain of ~8 (inset, Fig. 4c). The NAND and NOR logic gates in Fig. 4e, f exhibit the expected logic behavior. The NAND gate outputs high unless both inputs are high, while the NOR gate outputs high only when both inputs are low, confirming the functionality of the two logic gates56. The observed output voltage exceeding VDD arises from the FNMV (Force Nothing/Measure Voltage) test mode’s open-circuit offset combined with finite off-state current, rather than intrinsic amplification (Supplementary Fig. 17). To date, research on scaled MoS2 transistors has mainly focused on the performance of individual devices, while the realization of wafer-level logic circuits built with sub-1 nm gate or sub-20 nm channel transistors remains rare. While our logic circuits exhibit performance variations and modest yield rates, the approach demonstrates process compatibility and scalability. Discussion

We have developed a dual-self-aligned vertical fabrication strategy for ultra-scaled MoS2 transistors, enabling the concurrent scaling of gate and channel lengths to ~0.34 nm and ~10 nm, respectively. This lithography-independent strategy uses the atomic edge of monolayer graphene to define the gate, and self-aligned oblique-angle deposition to form the channel, thereby effectively circumventing the stringent resolution and overlay accuracy constraints typically imposed by conventional lithographic scaling. The resulting transistor delivers competitive electrical performance in this extreme scaling regime. Guided by physical de-embedding analyses, the architectural versatility of this framework was further demonstrated by integrating a SiO2 hardmask and an In2O3 channel, exhibiting an on-state current density of 172 μA/μm. Furthermore, quantum transport simulations indicate that this MoS2 vertical architecture suppresses direct source-drain tunneling, suggesting the functional scaling limit into the sub-5 nm regime. Beyond single-transistor performance, we fabricated wafer-integrated 2D logic circuits using sidewall-defined architecture. While oblique-angle deposition here serves as a proof-of-concept demonstration of the dual-self-aligned strategy, the underlying structural blueprint could be adapted to established fab-compatible techniques. Replacing the physical shadowing approach with conformal atomic layer deposition and anisotropic spacer etching could provide the precise dimensional control and wafer-level uniformity required for seamless integration with mainstream silicon-based CMOS processes, offering a practical pathway toward scalable manufacturing of ultra-scaled 2D transistor arrays.

Methods

Wet transfer of graphene and MoS2

Monolayer graphene grown on copper foil (3 × 3 cm²) via chemical vapor deposition (CVD) was purchased from Hefei Vigon Material Technology Co., Ltd., with a monolayer coverage exceeding 99%. To mitigate the inherent structural sensitivity of the atomically thin graphene during device fabrication, stringent process control was rigorously maintained throughout the wet-transfer and patterning procedures to preserve optimal gate integrity. Monolayer MoS2 grown on a 2-inch sapphire substrate was obtained from Shenzhen 6Carbon Technology Co., Ltd., also exhibiting >99% monolayer coverage. For graphene transfer, a 400 K molecular weight poly (methyl methacrylate) (PMMA) layer was spin-coated onto the graphene/Cu/graphene stack at 3000 rpm for 60 s. The graphene layer on the back side of the Cu foil was removed by O2 plasma (300 sccm, 300 W, 4 min) using an Alpha Plasma AL18 system. The underlying copper was then etched using a CuSO4: H2O: HCl (1:5:5) solution. After complete removal of the copper, the PMMA/graphene film was rinsed six times in fresh deionized (DI) water to eliminate etchant residues and subsequently transferred onto the target substrate. The sample was air-dried at room temperature for over 12 h, followed by annealing at 90–110 °C for 30 min to enhance adhesion. PMMA was removed by soaking in fresh acetone for 30 min, repeated at least twice. For MoS2 transfer, a PMMA layer was spin-coated onto the MoS2/sapphire sample at 1500 rpm for 60 s, followed by annealing at 100 °C for 3 min. The MoS2/PMMA film was lifted off using a 3 wt% KOH solution at 120 °C. After detachment, the film was rinsed six times in fresh DI water to remove residual KOH and transferred to the target substrate. The sample was naturally dried for over 12 h and annealed at 85 °C for 30 min to promote adhesion. PMMA was subsequently removed with fresh acetone as described above.

Fabrication of dual-self-aligned vertical transistors

A 2-inch, heavily p-doped silicon wafer coated with a 45 nm HfO2 dielectric layer was used as the substrate. Subsequently, a 3 × 3 cm2 CVD-grown monolayer graphene film was wet-transferred to the central region of the wafer. Photolithography using AZ601 photoresist was then employed to pattern the graphene, and the unprotected graphene regions were removed by oxygen plasma etching (150 sccm O2, 150 W, 6 min) using an Alpha Plasma AL18 system. Following this, a Cr/Pd (5 nm/15 nm) contact layer was deposited via e-beam evaporation to establish electrical contact with the graphene, using a standard lithography process with NR9-1000PY photoresist and RD6 as the developer. This was followed by the sequential deposition of a 3 nm Al seeding layer (via e-beam evaporation) and a 10 nm HfO2 layer using atomic layer deposition (ALD, MNT system). Electron-beam lithography (EBL) was performed using a 70 K molecular weight PMMA resist to define the nanoscale patterns. A 10 nm Pd screening layer was then deposited through e-beam evaporation onto the Al2O3/HfO2 stack. This Pd layer functioned as a self-aligned etch mask for subsequent processing. An inductively coupled plasma (ICP) etching process (BCl3/Cl2 chemistry, NE-550H system) was used to etch the exposed Al2O3/HfO2 layers, the underlying CVD graphene, and the 20 nm recessed HfO2 layer, thereby forming the desired sidewall structure. Following the etching step, an additional 8 nm HfO2 layer was conformally deposited via ALD (200 °C, MNT system). Afterward, a conventional photolithography process using AZ601 photoresist was performed to open the probe regions. The 8 nm HfO2 covering the graphene contacts and Pd screening layer in the probe area was selectively removed via reactive ion etching (CF4/Ar plasma). A 2 × 2 cm2 monolayer MoS2 film was then wet-transferred onto the central region of the wafer. Another AZ601-based photolithography step was used to define the MoS2 channel area, and excess material was etched away using oxygen plasma (150sccm O2, 150 W, 7 min, Alpha Plasma AL18). Finally, using NR9-1000 PY photoresist, a window was opened across the MoS2 channel region, and Bi/Pd (5 nm/15 nm) source and drain electrodes were deposited at an oblique angle via electron beam evaporation equipment. This oblique deposition allowed for the formation of a 10 nm scaled channel length via a self-aligned process. To demonstrate the structural versatility of this framework, an alternative configuration was fabricated utilizing the identical baseline method with two specific modifications. The conventional metallic Pd screening layer was replaced by an e-beam evaporated SiO2 dielectric hardmask, and the transferred MoS2 was substituted with a conformal In2O3 channel grown via ALD and subsequently patterned using dilute HCl etching. The identical oblique-angle deposition of Bi/Pd electrodes was then performed, leveraging the SiO2 step structure to seamlessly self-align the ultra-short In2O3 channel to the graphene gate.

Device characterization and electrical measurements

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images were acquired using a GEMINI SEM 500 field-emission microscope at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV to visualize the MoS₂ channel and metal contacts. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) and energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) were performed using a Themis Z (Thermo Scientific) equipped with a Super-X EDS detector, operated at 200 kV. TEM measurements used an electron beam with a 23.8 mrad convergence angle, and the collection angle was set to 90–370 mrad. Raman spectroscopy was conducted using a high-resolution confocal Raman microscope (LabRAM HR, HORIBA Jobin Yvon) with a 532 nm excitation laser. Electrical measurements were carried out in a vacuum probe station (Lakeshore) under a pressure below 10−3 Pa, using an Agilent B1500A semiconductor device analyzer. For circuit module testing, the input and output signals were recorded by ST2516 test equipment, and the sample was connected using the Cascade Summit 12000B-M probe station with a 28-port pin card and a printed circuit board (PCB) adapter board.

Transistor simulation

A two-layer simulation framework was implemented within the commercial Sentaurus TCAD platform. This framework comprises a semi-classical drift-diffusion model for reproducing the experimental geometries and a non-equilibrium Green’s function quantum transport model for projecting the fundamental scaling limits.

The semi-classical simulations were conducted using the Sentaurus device tool. This model explicitly incorporates crucial physical mechanisms, including Fermi-level pinning and localized defect models. Furthermore, quantum correction models were integrated to account for quasi-ballistic transport and source-drain tunneling effects present in the sub-10 nm experimental devices. Using this calibrated model, the effective gate length was extracted following established methodologies, and the corresponding energy band profiles along the channel direction were systematically analyzed.

Non-equilibrium Green’s function (NEGF) quantum transport simulations were carried out in Sentaurus QTX to elucidate the intrinsic quantum transport physics of ultra-scaled MoS2 devices. The Hamiltonian was solved using the effective mass approximation (EMA). Specifically, to capture the channel transport properties of monolayer MoS2 along the (100) crystal direction, the transport mass (mt) was set to 0.48 m0 (where m0 is the free-electron mass), while the mode effective mass was applied to the direction subjected to quantum confinement. Within the QTX NEGF framework, carrier transport in the channel was modeled as purely ballistic to evaluate the intrinsic drive capability and quantum switching characteristics of the devices in the absence of scattering.
