Clinical utility of a cerebrospinal fluid multiplex PCR meningitis/encephalitis panel in children: a retrospective review of positive results
Authors: Supatida Tengsupakul, Madhuri Mulekar, Bibek Raj Bista, Paul Maertens, Kamal Sharma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMeningitis has significant clinical morbidity and mortality in children. Mortality in children, especially neonates, is high due to diagnostic difficulty. Diagnosis of pediatric meningitis is traditionally made using CSF parameters and cultures. The performance of cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) multiplex polymerase chain reaction (PCR) meningitis/encephalitis (ME) panel needs further evaluation in the pediatric population.MethodsMedical records of children with a positive CSF ME panel (June 1, 2016 to August 31, 2018) were reviewed retrospectively. We extracted data about antimicrobials used, laboratory and culture results, duration of antibiotic treatment, hospital stay, and follow-up. Long term outcome was evaluated.ResultsA total of 79 children (age, 1 d to 12 y) were identified, including 58 children with viral (73%) and 21 children (27%) with bacterial meningitis. The most frequent viruses were enterovirus in all age groups. Empiric antibiotics were discontinued within 24 h in 14 of 40 patients (35%) positive for viral meningitis. Specific antiviral therapy was rapidly initiated in 6 out of 58 patients (10%). Antibiotics were stopped or never initiated when viral etiology was identified. Even when the CSF sample was inadequate for analysis, the pathogenic organism was identified which is particularly helpful in the infants. In antibiotic pretreated patients, the organism was identified even when the cultures were negative. CSF ME panel identified 13 patients who had bacterial meningitis despite negative CSF cultures. Twelve of these 13 patients (92%) were pretreated with antibiotics. In bacterial meningitis cases, modification of antibiotic therapy was not based solely on the result of CSF ME panel. Duration of antibiotic treatment and hospital stay were substantially shorter in patients with viral as opposed to bacterial meningitis.ConclusionsDelay in diagnosis and targeted antimicrobial therapy may worsen neurologic outcomes. CSF ME panel may enable earlier targeted therapy and reduce unnecessary antimicrobial exposure as it is rapid, specific, and highly sensitive. It is expected to reduce healthcare cost, and its use is recommended in a pediatric population.Keywords: Pediatrics, Lumbar puncture, Pretreatment, Viral meningitis

Background

The incidence of bacterial meningitis in children varies by age group and is highest in infants, especially younger than two months. During 2010–2019, the annual incidence of meningitis in all children less than 18 yrs was 17.2 per 100,000 [1]. In children with or without disseminated illness, meningitis has significant clinical morbidity and mortality [2]. Mortality is multifactorial and can vary with the causative pathogen. Despite the availability of effective antibiotics to treat bacterial meningitis, 50% of children may experience neurologic sequelae, and mortality is high (5% to 30%), especially in neonates, in part because of diagnostic difficulty [3, 4].

In Pediatrics, the incidence of meningitis is highest under 6 months of age [5]. High index of suspicion is critical for prompt investigation and early recognition of meningitis, as recommended by the American Academy of Pediatrics [6]. Clinical examination is not sufficient for diagnosis as symptoms and signs of meningitis in children are variable and nonspecific [7]. In neonates, clinical presentation varies even more. The most common symptoms prompting diagnostic lumbar puncture in the emergency room include fever, seizures, and/or unexplained altered mental status [4]. Laboratory confirmation of bacterial meningitis is ascertained by cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) Gram stain and culture. Treatment with empiric antibiotics before obtaining CSF studies can affect the sensitivity of culture and Gram stain [8]. The sensitivity of CSF Gram stain is 60% to 80% in patients without antibiotic pretreatment and decreases by 20% with antibiotic pretreatment [9]. CSF bacterial culture is positive in 8% of patients who have meningitis [10] but is a useful diagnostic test even though results may not be available for two days, because it provides information about the pathogen and antimicrobial sensitivity [11]. Parameters such as CSF protein level, glucose level, and white blood cell (WBC) count may be inconsistent in differentiating viral from bacterial infections and nonspecific to provide treatment direction [9].

The purpose of the present study is to evaluate utility of positive CSF PCR results.

Materials & methods

Study design

In this single-center retrospective study, we included all children under 18 years of age who had a positive CSF multiplex PCR panel (FilmArray Meningitis/Encephalitis (ME) Panel, BioFire Diagnostics, Salt Lake City, UT) and were admitted from June 1, 2016 to August 31, 2018. The beginning of study corresponds to the start of using CSF ME panel in our institution. Extending the sample size was considered but rejected as it would decrease long term follow-up. In the case of multiple positive CSF specimens for the same patient, only the first result was included in the study to avoid double entry of the same patient which would skew the data. Patients were grouped by age (< 30 d; 30 to 90 d; >90 d) into three categories. Clinical data was extracted from the electronic medical records of patients after institutional board approval. The results of the CSF ME panel were deidentified. Laboratory data included serum WBC count, C-reactive protein (CRP), CSF WBC count, protein level, and glucose level. Antibiotic pretreatment was defined as antibiotic treatment received within three days before lumbar puncture. Single dose intravenous antibiotics and oral prophylaxis were also included as pretreatment. Respiratory distress was defined as tachypnea for age, retractions, or grunting [12].

The CSF data was categorized into normal and abnormal groups as follows. Normal CSF WBC was defined as < 20 cells/mm3 for age ≤ 28 d, < 10 cells/mm3 for age 29–60 d and < 5 cells/mm3 for age > 60 d [13]. Normal CSF protein level was defined as < 130 mg/dL for age ≤ 28 d, < 100 mg/dL for age 29–60 d, or < 70 mg/dL for age > 60 d. Normal CSF glucose level was defined as 37 to 75 mg/dL for all ages [13]. Traumatic lumbar puncture was defined by CSF RBC count > 10,000 cells/mm3. CSF cell count and protein were adjusted according to the correction formula [14, 15]. Post-discharge follow-up was reported if available. Deaths were reported when a causal link to the infection was present.

Statistical analysis

Data was entered into a spreadsheet (Excel, Microsoft, Redmond, WA) and analyzed with statistical software (JMP Pro v 14.2.0-16.2.0 and 18.0.2, SAS Inc., Cary, NC). Numerical variables such as age and duration of hospital stay were summarized using mean and range. Categorical variables such as pathogen and gender were summarized using count and percent. Mean outcomes for numerical variables were compared for bacterial/viral meningitis patients using a 2-sample t-test and that for categorical variables using Fisher’s or Chi-square test. Odds ratios and their 95% confidence intervals (CI) were computed to measure the association between bacterial/viral infection and factors such as normal CSF protein, glucose, and WBC counts. Comparisons within the age groups were not performed because of small sample sizes. Statistical significance was defined as P < 0.05.

Results

There were 79 patients aged 1 d to 12 y (0.7 ± 2 y) who had CSF ME panel showing at least one pathogen. No samples showed multiple pathogen detections. Most patients (73%) had viral meningitis, and the mean age did not differ significantly between patients with viral versus bacterial meningitis. Fever was the presenting symptom in most patients (65%), and 4 neonates with viral meningitis had hypothermia. Respiratory distress was less frequent in patients with viral than with bacterial (7% vs. 48%) meningitis (Table 1). Comparison of serum WBC and CRP levels for patients with bacterial/viral meningitis showed that the median CRP value was higher among those with bacterial infection than viral (1.25 vs. 0.9). Serum WBC was slightly lower in bacterial infection than viral (10.87 vs. 11.05) (Table 2). The most frequently observed viral pathogen was enterovirus in patients from all age groups, and the most frequently observed bacterial pathogens were Streptococcus agalactiae for patients < 30 d old, Escherichia coli for patients 30–90 d old, and Haemophilus influenzae for patients > 90 d old (Table 3). Some pathogens in the CSF ME panel were not identified in any patient, such as Listeria monocytogenes, Neisseria meningitidis, Cryptococcus neoformans, and Cryptococcus gattii.

Antibiotic stewardship impact with the use of CSF multiplex panel

The study of glucose, protein, and cell count was not performed in 4 patients due to the small volume of CSF available for analysis. One infant was a 12-month-old boy who presented with seizures. CSF ME panel was positive for HHV-6. Three remaining infants were less than 30 days old. The CSF ME panel was positive for viral meningitis in all 3 infants. Infant 1 was a two-day-old newborn male with respiratory distress who received empiric antibiotics for 24 h and underwent a spinal tap due to progressive elevation of CRP (8.4 mg/dL) on the second day of life. CSF ME panel was positive for cytomegalovirus (CMV). CMV culture was positive in CSF and urine. There were no obvious signs of congenital CMV such as petechiae, jaundice, hepatosplenomegaly, microcephaly, sensorineural hearing loss or retinopathy. The infant received oral valganciclovir for 6 months. At 6 years of age, he still had normal hearing, vision, learning, and head growth. Infant 2 is a 17-day-old male who presented with decreased oral intake, fussiness, and subjective fever. Initial spinal tap was unsuccessful, and he was given empiric antibiotics, however acyclovir was not started. After 2 days, due to onset of seizures, spinal tap was repeated yielding 1 ml of CSF. CSF ME panel was positive for herpes simplex virus 2 (HSV-2) and antibiotic therapy was discontinued when CSF bacterial culture was negative. He was treated with acyclovir for 6 months. He required a ventriculoperitoneal shunt and only suffers from a mild global developmental delay. At the age of 8, he functions like a 6-year-old child. Infant 3 is a 30-day-old female who presented with seizure and had a CSF ME panel positive for HHV-6. The patient was treated with empiric antibiotics for three days until bacterial cultures were negative. Long term outcome was normal.

Data about CSF WBC, protein, and glucose was available for 75 patients when patients from all three age groups were combined into one. As expected, normal CSF protein (80% vs. 33%, Chi sq test, p < 0.0001) and glucose (89% vs. 57%, Chi sq test, p = 0.01) levels were noted more frequently in children with viral meningitis than with bacterial meningitis. CSF cell count, however, was not significantly different (P = 0.46) in patients with viral or bacterial meningitis. Comparisons within three different age groups were not performed due to small sample sizes (Table 3). The odds of normal CSF protein and glucose were respectively 7.71 (p < 0.0001) and 6 (p = 0.01) times higher among patients with viral meningitis than with bacterial meningitis.

Among 54 infants with positive viral CSF ME panel, 44 (81%) were less than 90 d old, 38 (86%) had normal CSF glucose, and 36 (82%) had normal CSF protein. Among these infants, one infant of interest was a 42-day-old male presenting with worsening diffuse papular rash which became vesicular and pustular. He had cold symptoms and fever and underwent a septic workup. CSF was normal except for CSF ME panel which was positive for varicella zoster virus (VZV). The infant was treated with acyclovir. In two infants less than 90 days old, CSF protein was elevated while CSF glucose and WBC were normal. One infant was extremely premature, 24 + 5/7 weeks small for a gestational age female, born to a G1P0000 mother with sickle cell disease. At 49 days of life, recurrent episodes of apnea/ bradycardia were evaluated with spinal tap. CSF WBC count was 3 cells/mm3, red blood was 510 cells/mm3 and CSF protein 169 mg/dL. CSF ME panel showed HSV-1. Patient was treated with intravenous acyclovir for 21 days and oral acyclovir for 6 months. At a 2 year and 4 months follow-up, the patient was noted to have short stature and mild language developmental delay. At 6 years of age, she improved with only mild residual cognitive impairment. Low CSF glucose was seen in 6 infants less than 90 d old with viral meningitis. Among those 6 less than 90 d old infants, 4 had all abnormal CSF parameters including protein and WBC. All these infants had CSF ME panel positive for enterovirus meningitis and negative HSV workup. Infant 1, a 9-day-old male newborn, received acyclovir for two days until full HSV workup was negative and empiric antibiotics for three days until bacterial culture was negative. Infants 2 and 3 were a set of female twins who presented with hypothermia at the age of 17 days, and Infant 4 was a 39-day-old male who developed fever. The acyclovir was not given to these three infants.

Acyclovir was used in 17 out of 79 patients (22%). Five patients (29%) had HSV/ VZV infection and 12 (71%) had non-HSV/VZV infection. Acyclovir duration varied based on clinical suspicion, neonatal age, and availability of CSF, surface, and blood PCR results. Ten of 17 (59%) patients were less than 30 days old. CSF PCR results usually come back in one hour; however, blood HSV PCR takes few days in our institution. Acyclovir was discontinued after one day in 3 patients, after 2 days in 3 patients, and after 3 days in 5 patients. One patient with enteroviral sepsis received acyclovir for 5 days. One patient with VZV infection and four patients with HSV infection received prolonged therapy in accordance with standard of care. Our CMV patient was only treated with valganciclovir as antiviral therapy.

In all 10 over 90 d old children with viral meningitis, normal CSF glucose was reported (Table 3). In 7 of 10 (70%) patients, CSF protein was normal and elevated in the remaining 3 patients. One of them is an 8-year-old female with fever, behavioral changes, status epilepticus who required intubation and mechanical ventilation. Her CSF protein and glucose were normal while CSF WBC (23 cells/mm3) and RBC (50 cells/mm3) were elevated. She was found to have HSV-1 meningitis by CSF ME panel and as a result she was given intravenous acyclovir and empiric antibiotics for meningitis. Antibiotics were not stopped as she was pretreated before the spinal tap. She was intubated for status epilepticus and acute respiratory failure and successfully extubated after 13 days of mechanical ventilation. She was treated with intravenous acyclovir for 21 days and oral acyclovir for 5 months. Patient was discharged after 27 days of hospitalization. She received occupational therapy.

In our cohort, 11 patients had HHV-6 detected by CSF ME panel (Fig. 1). Age ranged from 1 d old to 1 year old. The majority had a benign and transient clinical course. Blood HHV-6 PCR was not performed in 6 patients. One infant of interest is a 30-day-old female who presented with a seizure. CSF sample volume was not sufficient for other analysis. She was discharged after three days, and antiepileptic therapy was stopped on follow-up without recurrence of seizure. Blood HHV-6 PCR was performed in five patients. It was negative in a 1 year-old-male, who presented in extremis with status epilepticus and Group A streptococcal septic shock. Blood HHV-6 PCR was positive in the remaining four patients. Infant 1, a one-day-old male, underwent spinal tap for persistent tachypnea to rule out sepsis and received three days of IV antibiotics. Infant 2, a 53-day-old male, infant 3, a 68-day-male and infant 4, a 7-month-old male underwent spinal tap for fever workup. All four infants had an uneventful clinical course. Long term outcome of the 11 infants was normal in all, except one who died from septic shock.

Twenty-one patients had bacterial meningitis identified by the CSF ME panel, and CSF culture results were available for all of them. In 8 (38%) of them, CSF culture results confirmed the pathogen identified by the CSF panel. CSF cultures showed no growth in the remaining 13 (62%) patients who had bacterial pathogens identified by CSF ME panel (Table 5). A negative CSF culture was the result of antibiotic pretreatment in the majority (92%) of these 13 patients. The antibiotic pretreatments were ampicillin and gentamicin in the neonatal age group and ceftriaxone in children older than one month. Specific antibiotic pretreatments for all patients are listed in Table 6. In two patients, CSF culture was negative, but blood culture confirmed the pathogen identified by the CSF ME panel (Table 6). All other patients with negative CSF culture had negative blood cultures. Furthermore, in these 13 patients, a wide range of serum and CSF biomarker results were observed, and most of them had blood and urine cultures either not available or showing no growth (Table 6).

When the antibiotic duration and hospital stay were analyzed (Table 4), as expected, patients with bacterial meningitis had significantly longer treatment durations and longer hospital stays. Mean duration of antibiotic treatment was 2 d (range, 0–6 d) in children with viral meningitis and 18 d (range, 9–28 d) in children with bacterial meningitis (P = 0.0015). Empiric antibiotic treatment depended on the age of the patient, patient-specific factors, local epidemiology, local resistance patterns and possibility of a hospital acquired infection. Neonates were commonly treated with ampicillin and cefotaxime while older children were treated with ceftriaxone and vancomycin. Three patients with E. coli meningitis were escalated to meropenem based on CSF ME panel. Rifampin was also used in some cases of E. coli, S. pneumoniae and S. agalactiae depending on clinical complexities. The antibiotic regimens were deescalated based on clinical improvement and a culture sensitivity report. Mean duration of hospital stay was 4 d (range 1–23 d) in children with viral meningitis and 22 d (range 16–27 d) in children with bacterial meningitis (P < 0.0001).

There were 10 patients older than 2 months with bacterial meningitis. S. pneumoniae meningitis was diagnosed in an unimmunized 3-month-old infant and in a 4-month-old who received only one dose of pneumococcal conjugate vaccine. H. influenzae meningitis was diagnosed in three completely immunized patients (age 6 m, 3 y, and 3 y). H. influenzae serotype A was detected in 2 patients (a 6-month-old and a 3-year-old). H. influenzae serotype was unknown in the third patient due to negative culture from blood and CSF. E. coli meningitis was detected in 4 infants and S. agalactiae meningitis in 1 infant. None of our patients with bacterial meningitis were in the age group qualifying for routine meningococcal conjugate vaccine. Meningococcal meningitis was also not encountered.

Post-discharge follow-up was reported if available. Most of the children with enterovirus, HHV-6 and human parechovirus had a mild clinical course and did not require long-term follow-up in our specialty clinics. Deaths were listed when a causal link to the infection was present. Our follow-up duration ranged from 2 to 8 years. The outcome in children with viral etiologies was benign, except in children with HSV-1/ HSV-2 (Table 4). In the eight-year-old with HSV-1, post-discharge follow-up at 2 years revealed full recovery with normal cognition. The 49-day-old with neonatal HSV-1 had mild developmental delay at 6 years of age. Treatment delay due to late presentation was the most likely cause of poor outcome in the other two patients with HSV. Neuroimaging in the 8-month-old infant presenting in status epilepticus was already abnormal and showed diffuse necrotizing encephalopathy. CSF ME panel was positive for HSV-2. In this patient, severe neonatal HSV-2 encephalopathy preceded the study period. He was a 19-day-old when he presented initially. Neuroimaging was already consistent with diffuse encephalopathy. Although treated appropriately with acyclovir, he developed microcephaly, quadriparesis, epilepsy and a minimally conscious state. Outcome of bacterial meningitis varies with pathogenic organisms and was most severe with E. coli meningitis. One neonate with E. coli meningitis passed away. Cognitive impairment was seen in 3 patients with E. coli, 2 with S. agalactiae and 2 with S. pneumoniae meningitis (Table 4).

Discussion

Real-time polymerase chain reaction (PCR) amplifying a single target pathogen has been in use in commercial laboratories in the United States for detecting pathogens since the 1990s [16]. In 2015, the U.S. Food and Drug Administration approved the first CSF ME panel for detection of CNS infections [17]. The CSF ME panel detects 14 common pathogens, including six bacteria, seven viruses, and one fungus. The pathogens included in CSF ME panel are CMV, enterovirus, HSV-1 and 2, HHV-6, human parechovirus, VZV, Escherichia coli K1, Haemophilus influenzae, Listeria monocytogenes, Neisseria meningitidis, Streptococcus agalactiae, Streptococcus pneumoniae, and Cryptococcus neoformans/Cryptococcus gattii. CSF ME panel decreases the need for single pathogen testing, allowing labs to provide more comprehensive results with less resources. Studies about the efficacy of this CSF ME panel have shown high sensitivity (86%-100%), specificity (92.8%-100%), positive predictive value (85%), and negative predictive value (98%) [18].

In our institution, prompt investigation and early recognition of meningitis is achieved following the guidelines of the American Academy of Pediatrics [6]. CSF parameters can sometimes distinguish viral and bacterial meningitis. In our study, normal CSF protein and glucose are in favor of a viral etiology. However, CSF pleocytosis, a sensitive marker for inflammation, is less predictive (Table 3). CSF parameters are a poor predictor of the organism causing meningitis and normal CSF parameters could be misleading. Some patients in our study suffered from serious, treatable meningitis that would have been missed if CSF ME had not been performed. In our study, 4 infants with positive bacterial CSF ME had all normal CSF parameters. All of them were neonates. In neonates, bacterial meningitis confirmed with positive cultures is possible with normal CSF parameters including CSF WBC count [19].

The present study confirmed that CSF ME panel was useful in the early diagnosis and treatment of pediatric meningitis. First, CSF ME panel is rapid compared to slow conventional culture methods. FilmArray CSF ME panel analyzes multiple target sequences simultaneously, enabling the testing for multiple pathogens in a single test with a turnaround time of about 1 h [20, 21]. By quickly identifying the cause of meningitis, clinicians can make more informed decisions. In our study, CSF ME panel enabled rapid identification of a treatable viral meningitis in 6 out of 58 children with viral meningitis (10%). HSV was identified by CSF ME panel in 4 children, two with HSV-1 and two with HSV-2. All children received 21 days of intravenous acyclovir followed by the intent of oral suppressive therapy for 6 months. Two HSV infants were less than 90 days old. First one is a 19-day-old male infant with positive HSV-2. No CSF parameter was available. Treatment was initiated without delay. Second one is a 49-day-old female infant with HSV-1 who had normal CSF glucose and cell count but high CSF protein. At age 6, she had a mild developmental delay. The third infant had a past-history of neonatal HSV-2 at 19 days of age, leading to microcephaly, quadriparesis, epilepsy and minimally conscious state. He presented in status epilepticus at 8 months of age and CSF ME panel was positive for HSV-2. CSF WBC and CSF glucose were normal, but CSF protein was high. Fourth patient is an 8-year-old female with HSV-1 who had CSF WBC abnormal but had normal CSF protein and glucose. Fifth patient is a 2-day-old male for whom CSF ME panel identified CMV meningitis but CSF sample was not sufficient for analysis. Sixth patient is a 42-day-old male with VZV identified by CSF ME panel who had all CSF parameters normal. The above-mentioned patients would have been missed had there been no CSF ME panel available showing the vital importance of the CSF ME panel. Specific antiviral therapy was initiated without delay. In addition, CSF ME panel identified non-treatable pathogens, such as enterovirus, HHV-6, and parechovirus in 52 out of 58 patients with viral meningitis (90%). When non-treatable viral pathogens were identified by CSF ME panel, acyclovir, which is nephrotoxic and vesicant, was discontinued. In addition, discovery of non-treatable viral pathogens prevented unnecessary antimicrobial use. In our study, all non-treatable viral pathogens detected in CSF ME panel were never associated with other CSF pathogens. Antibiotics were never started in 25% of children with non-treatable viral pathogens and were only given for 24 h or less in 35%. Because bacterial meningitis is a life-threatening medical emergency, patients have historically and currently been started on broad-spectrum antimicrobial therapy as a precaution while awaiting CSF parameters and cultures. Unnecessary antibiotic use increases the risk of developing multidrug-resistant organisms and leading to serious side effects and complications, including C. difficile infection, allergic reactions, and organ toxicity [22]. CSF ME panel identified bacterial meningitis in 27% of patients. In our study, CSF ME panel did not help in simplifying antibiotic regimen in bacterial meningitis and in each case antibiotic therapy was only modified after obtaining culture and sensitivity results. Continuing the use of Gram stain and bacterial cultures is still a valid recommendation to confirm the results of the CSF ME panel [23] and guide antimicrobial therapy based on susceptibility results. Contrary to a previous report [24], we found that the CSF ME panel does not necessarily simplify antibiotic management in bacterial meningitis. Currently CSF ME panel does not provide information about antibiotic susceptibility [11]. None of our patients had meningococcal meningitis. Rapid identification of such pathogens would not only improve patient outcomes but also would allow the implementation of necessary public health measures such as antibiotic prophylaxis needed within 24 h to prevent outbreaks [25].

Second, CSF ME panel is highly sensitive. CSF ME panel can identify bacterial pathogens that are missed by Gram stain and culture [26]. The sensitivity of culture and Gram stain is variable according to type of organisms and demographics, but was reported to be 8% and 60%, respectively [10]. Sample volume can be small and still provide quick identification of CSF pathogens in an infected patient. In our study, the four patients with inadequate CSF samples, too small for full analysis, were less than 12 months of age. Two infants, one infant with HSV meningitis and one with congenital CMV, were treated without knowledge of CSF parameters. These cases highlight the importance of CSF ME panel, as diagnosis would have been delayed or missed. Furthermore, CSF ME panel can detect a pathogen’s genetic material (DNA or RNA) even if the organism has been killed or suppressed by antibiotics. Pretreatment with antibiotics significantly decreases the sensitivity of traditional culture methods, making them more likely to produce false-negative results [27]. Antibiotic pretreatment > 12 h before lumbar puncture altered CSF glucose and protein but not CSF WBC count [28]. Antibiotic pretreatment decreases the reliability of CSF cultures, and CSF sterilization has been previously observed after starting antibiotic therapy for N. meningitidis within 2 h and S. pneumoniae within 4 h [20]. In our study, bacterial pathogens were identified by CSF ME panel in 13 patients with negative CSF culture, the majority of whom had been treated with antibiotics (Table 4). We conclude that a CSF ME panel is a crucial diagnostic tool allowing detection of pathogen genetic material in pretreated patients. Third, CSF ME panel can identify slow growing organisms such as Cryptococcus. None of our patients had cryptococcal meningitis.

CSF ME panel is expected to reduce healthcare cost. Simultaneous testing of various common pathogens significantly reduces the need for manpower in the lab. In children with viral meningitis, the antibiotic duration is decreased, shortening their stay in the hospital. Delays in diagnosis and targeted therapy may worsen neurologic outcomes. Early identification of treatable pathogens is expected to decrease morbidity. Long term sequelae of viral meningitis are decreased by CSF ME panel (Table 4). Antiviral therapy was initiated shortly after presentation in all cases. The infant with VZV meningitis had a full recovery. Our CMV patient also had a normal outcome. The 8-year-old with HSV-1 had normal outcome. The 49-day-old with neonatal HSV-1 had a good outcome considering her extreme prematurity. Treatment delay due to late presentation was the most likely cause of poor outcome in the other two patients with HSV. This conclusion is suggested by brain imaging on presentation (Data not shown). Morbidity and mortality in bacterial meningitis remain a challenge. A randomized prospective study comparing outcomes in patients undergoing or not undergoing CSF ME panel is not ethically feasible.

CSF ME panel is an essential adjunctive test but has some limitations and is not an all-inclusive library of pathogens. Many viruses such as West Nile virus and Eastern Equine Encephalitis virus are not covered by standard CSF ME panel. CSF ME panel does not cover certain tropical pathogens because the panel is designed for the most common etiologies of meningitis in our regions. Pathogens causing subacute and chronic infections, such as syphilis, Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Leptospira species, most parasites and fungi, just to name a few, are not covered by CSF ME panel. Finally, CSF ME panel is not recommended to be performed in the CSF from shunt as it does not include Staphylococcus species, coagulase-negative staphylococci, Cutibacterium acnes, or Corynebacterium species, which are common causes of shunt infection [11]. Therefore, a negative CSF ME panel does not exclude meningitis. We must be mindful that the result of the CSF ME panel must be considered alongside the patient’s clinical picture, overall laboratory data, CSF analysis, CSF culture, and imaging studies. Our study shows that the serum CRP level is useful as an adjunct test, higher in bacterial meningitis compared to viral meningitis, and this finding is consistent with existing literature [29, 30]. If there is suspicion based on clinical presentation and CSF parameters, further evaluation is needed.

Of 356 CSF ME panel ordered during the study period, 79 were positive, corresponding to a test positivity rate of 22%. Our study has limitations. It is a single center retrospective study with a small sample size. It assesses only the positive results of CSF ME panel. We are unable to evaluate the sensitivity of CSF ME panel as assessment of false negatives is not performed. HSV positivity does not always mean that the patient has acute fulminating encephalitis. HSV can reactivate from a latent state. There was no CSF pleocytosis in our 8-month-old with history of neonatal HSV-2. Treatment with acyclovir was given as he was symptomatic. HHV-6 positivity in CSF can represent reactivation or chromosomally integrated HHV-6 instead of a true central nervous system (CNS) disease, especially in the pediatric population. Blood HHV-6 PCR was negative in the 1 year-old-male, who presented in extremis with status epilepticus and Group A streptococcal septic shock. His death was not related to HHV-6 positivity in CSF ME panel.

Conclusions

In summary, this study has shown that the use of CSF ME panel may enable earlier targeted therapy and reduce unnecessary antimicrobial exposure as it is rapid, specific and highly sensitive. CSF ME panel detects the causative pathogen in viral or bacterial meningitis, even when CSF parameters are normal or inadequate. It prevents the use of unnecessary antiviral agents and optimizes the use of specific antiviral agents. It detects bacterial organisms even when Gram stain and culture are negative, especially in pretreated patients. Finally, it rapidly identifies slow growing organisms. Overall, the benefits of CSF ME panel are multifaceted.
