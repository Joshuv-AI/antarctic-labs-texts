Cerebral oxygenation responses to obstructive sleep apnea in cognitively normal older adults: a study using simultaneous polysomnography and functional near-infrared spectroscopy
Authors: Chang Hyun Park, Hwan Wook Shim, Hwidon Lee, Chang-Seok Kim, Young-Min Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObstructive sleep apnea (OSA) can cause intermittent cerebral hypoxia that may impair brain health. However, widely used clinical indicators such as the Apnea-Hypopnea Index (AHI) and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂) may not accurately capture cerebral oxygen dynamics. In this study, we recorded frontal lobe cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) while concurrently acquiring overnight polysomnography (PSG) in 23 cognitively normal older adults (13 with OSA and 10 controls). Our results demonstrated that rSO₂ was negatively associated with apnea indices but showed no association with hypopnea indices. In contrast, mean SpO₂ was associated only with hypopnea indices, and minimum SpO₂ was associated with all indices. Applying increasingly stringent exclusion criteria to improve fNIRS signal quality strengthened the association between rSO₂ and apnea indices, but rSO₂ was not associated with hypopnea indices. These findings suggest that apneas cause cerebral desaturation, whereas hypopneas are mitigated by cerebral autoregulation, revealing a dissociation between cerebral oxygenation and SpO₂. Accordingly, SpO₂ alone may not fully reflect cerebral hypoxia. Thus, integrating high-quality fNIRS with PSG could provide clinically meaningful insights into both the physiological burden of OSA and its potential impact on cognitive function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-21068-5.Keywords: Obstructive sleep apnea, Older adults, Sleep, Functional near-infrared spectroscopy, PolysomnographySubject terms: Diseases, Medical research, Neurology, Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a sleep disorder characterized by repeated upper airway obstruction during sleep, leading to intermittent breathing cessation. Its prevalence increases with age1–4, and many outcomes associated with OSA in older adults overlap with physiological changes observed during aging, highlighting the need for research targeting the elderly population5–8. OSA is associated with depression3,9,10 and metabolic syndrome3,11–13 and is considered a fundamental contributor to cardiovascular disease3,14,15. Furthermore, chronic disruption of sleep architecture and intermittent hypoxemia associated with OSA have been identified as key risk factors for cognitive decline10,16–20.

In current clinical practice, the diagnosis and severity assessment of OSA primarily relies on the apnea–hypopnea index (AHI) and peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂), both measured through polysomnography (PSG)2,3,21,22. However, several studies have indicated that these indices alone may be insufficient for explaining the pathophysiology of OSA8,22–24. In particular, SpO₂ is a peripheral measurement that provides only indirect information regarding systemic oxygen delivery. It does not accurately reflect the actual oxygen supply to specific organs, such as the brain, which possesses unique autoregulatory mechanisms and high sensitivity to hypoxia25–29. Therefore, to accurately assess cerebral oxygenation, it is necessary to measure brain oxygen saturation directly, rather than relying on peripheral SpO₂.

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) is a non-invasive technique that enables real-time measurement of regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rSO₂) in various cortical areas, including the prefrontal cortex, and is widely utilized in both clinical and research settings30–34. The discrepancy between SpO₂ and rSO₂ may serve as a physiologically meaningful indicator of differential oxygen delivery regulation between peripheral and cerebral circulation35. Therefore, studies related to OSA have been conducted to directly monitor cerebral oxygen saturation36–40. However, previous studies have primarily focused on infants or the general adult population, resulting in a lack of data on the elderly population5,20,41,42.

This study aimed to investigate the effects of OSA on cerebral hemodynamic changes during sleep in cognitively normal older adults by simultaneously performing fNIRS and PSG. An fNIRS device continuously recorded rSO₂ in the prefrontal cortex. Simultaneously, PSG was used to monitor respiratory parameters and SpO₂ throughout sleep. This study investigated the physiological differences between cerebral and peripheral oxygen saturation by comparing their responses during OSA episodes in cognitively normal older adults. Focusing on this specific demographic, this study aimed to assess the clinical significance of direct cerebral oxygen monitoring in aging individuals susceptible to hypoxic stress.

Method

Study design

We conducted a cross-sectional study to evaluate the effects of OSA on cerebral hemodynamic regulation in older adults with normal cognitive function. All participants underwent a standardized evaluation process that included cognitive assessments, such as a clinical interview, a review of cardiovascular risk factors and current medications, laboratory tests, neurological examination, the Seoul Neuropsychological Screening Battery (SNSB)43, the Seoul-Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Scale (S-IADL)44, brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and amyloid positron emission tomography (PET). The diagnosis and severity of OSA and measurement of SpO₂ were assessed using PSG. The mean rSO₂ during sleep was measured using fNIRS to evaluate cerebral hemodynamic changes. All participants underwent simultaneous PSG and fNIRS during sleep to analyze the associations and differences between rSO₂, SpO₂, and various OSA parameters. The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and all applicable guidelines and regulations. Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Pusan National University Hospital (IRB No. 2209-016-119). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants or their legal guardians.

Subject

The participants in this study were cognitively normal volunteers recruited through community advertising. The study inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) older than 65 years of age, (2) no subjective memory complaints, (3) normal cognitive performance (above − 1.0 standard deviation of norms) on all subtests of SNSB43, (4) normal activities of daily living, based on clinical findings and S-IADL44, (5) Clinical Dementia Rating score of 0, and (6) amyloid-β (Aβ)-negative based on visual assessment by a nuclear physician blinded to clinical data with a previously described method45. Subjects were excluded from participation in this study if they met any of the following criteria: (1) presence of significant psychiatric conditions (e.g., delirium, bipolar disorder, major depressive disorder, schizophrenia, other psychotic disorder, and substance abuse), (2) presence of significant cerebrovascular condition (e.g., multiple lacunes and severe white matter hyperintensities with Fazekas scale, 3) presence of other neurodegenerative diseases (e.g., dementia with Lewy bodies, frontotemporal dementia, and Parkinson’s disease), 4) presence of significant medical conditions (e.g., severe organ failure, metabolic or hematologic disorders, and clinically significant abnormal laboratory findings), and 5) a history of taking sedative or psychotropic drugs in the past year. Additionally, participants were excluded from the analysis if no analyzable fNIRS data with acceptable signal quality were present throughout the entire sleep period.

Polysomnography measurement

PSG is the gold-standard diagnostic tool for simultaneously monitoring and analyzing various physiological signals during sleep2,21. In this study, PSG was performed to include the following parameters. Electroencephalography (EEG) was performed using the international 10–20 system at the F4–M1, C4–M1, O2–M1, F3–M2, C3–M2, and O1–M2 derivations. Electrooculography (EOG) was used to assess eye movements, and electromyography (EMG) was performed on the chin and bilateral anterior tibialis muscles. Electrocardiography (ECG), peripheral oxygen saturation (SpO₂), snoring, and body position were recorded simultaneously. An oronasal thermistor and nasal pressure transducer were used to evaluate apnea and hypopnea events. Respiratory effort was measured using respiratory inductance plethysmography belts placed around the chest and abdomen. The PSG results were scored according to the guidelines of the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM), and the definitions and diagnostic criteria for apnea and hypopnea followed the International Classification of Sleep Disorders, Third Edition (ICSD-3)46. Apnea was defined as a ≥ 90% reduction in airflow lasting at least 10 s, and hypopnea was defined as a 50–90% reduction in airflow for at least 10 s accompanied by a ≥ 4% drop in SpO₂. To quantify the severity of sleep-disordered breathing, three indices were calculated: Apnea–Hypopnea Index (AHI), Apnea Index (AI), and Hypopnea Index (HI). AHI represents the total number of apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep, whereas AI and HI denote the numbers of apneas and hypopneas per hour of sleep, respectively. These indices are commonly used in clinical and research settings to assess the frequency and pattern of respiratory disturbances during sleep.

OSA is generally diagnosed when apnea or hypopnea events lasting at least 10 s occurred ≥ 5 times per hour despite continued respiratory effort from the chest and abdomen (AHI ≥ 5)47. However, considering the study population consisted of older adults, participants with AHI < 15 were classified into the non-OSA group, while those with AHI ≥ 15 were classified into the OSA group48,49.

Additionally, SpO₂-derived parameters were analyzed. The mean SpO₂, a widely used indicator, was extracted along with the minimum SpO₂ and cumulative percentage of sleep time with SpO₂ below 90% (CT90), which are complementary measures of hypoxemia-related physiological stress and risk23. We also included conventional respiratory indices calculated over the entire sleep period, including AHI-total, AI-total, and HI-total. To further investigate the contribution of respiratory events to hypoxemia, apneas and hypopneas were rescored specifically during periods when SpO₂ dropped below 90%. The resulting indices were defined as AHI-90, AI-90, and HI-90.

fNIRS measurement

fNIRS is a noninvasive optical technique for monitoring cerebral hemodynamics. Conventional continuous-wave fNIRS estimates relative changes in oxygenated and deoxygenated hemoglobin concentrations (Δ[HbO], Δ[HbR]) via the modified Beer–Lambert law, whereas spatially resolved spectroscopy (SRS) fNIRS provides an absolute index of rSO₂27,50–54.

This study used a commercial SRS-based fNIRS device (Nirsit ON, OBELAB, Korea) to measure rSO₂ in the frontal cortex during sleep. This device utilizes patches that emit near-infrared light at wavelengths of 780 and 850 nm towards the cerebral cortex. Each patch contains two light sources and four detectors, forming measurement channels with source-detector distances of 2, 2.25, 3, and 3.35 cm55. rSO₂ was derived from multi-distance measurements using the diffusion approximation of spatially resolved spectroscopy, based on the distance dependence of diffuse reflectance53,54,56,57.

The fNIRS patches were attached to the left and right forehead regions, and rSO₂ signals from the frontal cortex were continuously recorded at a sampling rate of approximately 1 Hz. The device also provides a real-time attachment quality score ranging from 0 (lowest) to 5 (highest), which indicates the reliability of the recorded signals.

Data analysis

Participants simultaneously wore PSG and fNIRS devices, and data from both systems were recorded independently throughout the measurement period. Sleep stages were scored in 30-second epochs. fNIRS signals, acquired at 1-second intervals, were downsampled to 30-second averages for temporal alignment with PSG-derived sleep stages. Mean rSO₂ values were computed exclusively during total sleep time (TST), with data from wake stages excluded. The fNIRS patch attachment quality score was likewise averaged across 30-second epochs. To ensure data quality, only epochs with an attachment quality score ≥ 4, corresponding to a signal-to-noise ratio ≥ 25 dB, were retained for analysis. No additional filtering or artifact rejection procedures were applied. In this study, three grades of exclusion criteria were applied to the participants’ data:

Grade 1

To select data unaffected by moving artifacts or other external noise in the fNIRS device, we utilized a SNR metric provided by the Nirsit ON device. Only data with an average patch attachment score of 4 or higher (SNR ≥ 25 dB) over 30 s were included in the analysis. One non-OSA participant was excluded from the study because no data with an attachment score of 4 or higher were available.

Grade 2

Among the data with an average patch attachment score of 4 or higher, participants with less than 4 h of total sleep time were excluded, as this was deemed a failure of the PSG measurement. Consequently, the data from four non-OSA participants and one OSA participant were excluded58,59.

Grade 3

In addition to the grade 2 criteria, participants with periodic limb movements (PLMS), which could influence cerebral hemodynamic changes, were excluded. Consequently, seven participants (5 non-OSA participants and two OSA participants) were excluded from the final analysis. Demographic information of the participants who remained after applying the Grade 2 and Grade 3 exclusion criteria is provided in supplementary table S1.

Statistical analysis

A total of 23 cognitively normal individuals aged 65 years or older participated in the study, and those with an AHI of 15 or more were classified as having OSA48,49. The differences in baseline demographic and sleep characteristics between the groups were compared using the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables and the Chi-square test for categorical variables. Linear regression models were applied, with mean rSO₂, mean SpO₂, and minimum SpO₂ as dependent variables, sleep apnea indices as predictors, and body mass index (BMI) as a covariate. Although age, sex, and total sleep time (TST) can also influence OSA indices, only BMI was included as a covariate due to the limited sample size and its significant correlation with the dependent mean SpO₂ and TST (as shown in supplementary table S2). All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 27 (IBM Corporation, USA).

Discussion

This study focused on cognitively normal older adults to examine how OSA affects cerebral oxygenation changes during sleep. In this study, we found that cerebral (rSO₂) and peripheral (SpO₂) oxygenation showed distinct association patterns with OSA indices. The analyses revealed different association patterns, suggesting that cerebral and peripheral oxygenation may not vary in the same way during sleep-disordered breathing. These findings indicate that OSA-related hypoxemia may be accompanied by cerebral oxygenation responses that differ from those observed in peripheral oxygenation responses. Our results are consistent with the possibility that cognitively normal older adults retain regulatory mechanisms, such as cerebral autoregulation, that can partially preserve rSO₂ even when peripheral desaturation is present. This observation aligns with previous research on cerebral autoregulation28,29,35,40 and highlights the potential resilience of the brain to milder forms of respiratory disturbance. Furthermore, we found that the strength of the association between rSO₂ and OSA indices varied depending on the exclusion criteria applied, underscoring the importance of appropriate data quality standards to ensure the reliability of fNIRS-based measurements in sleep research.

Specifically, as shown in Table 2, min SpO₂ was significantly negatively associated with all OSA-related indices. In contrast, mean SpO₂ showed no significant association with apnea indices (AI-Total and AI-90) but demonstrated negative associations with hypopnea indices (HI-Total and HI-90). In contrast, rSO₂ exhibited significant negative associations with apnea-related indices (AHI-Total, AI-Total, AHI-90, and AI-90) but not with hypopnea indices (HI and HI-90). This discrepancy may be explained by previously proposed mechanisms of cerebral autoregulation, in which the brain retains some capacity to buffer oxygen desaturation during partial obstruction (hypopnea) but is less able to compensate during more severe events, such as apnea28,29. Meanwhile, CT90, a SpO₂-based index, showed a significant negative association with mean and min SpO₂. However, it was not significantly associated with rSO₂, indicating that cerebral oxygen saturation does not necessarily decrease even when SpO₂ falls below 90% during sleep. This suggests that rSO₂ is preserved by cerebral autoregulatory mechanisms, even with a drop in SpO₂28,29. These findings highlight the limitations of using only SpO₂-based indices or AHI to assess cerebral hypoxic stress in OSA22–25,27. Because SpO₂ may be insufficient to indicate cerebral hypoxia, the direct measurement of cerebral oxygen saturation through fNIRS has become increasingly significant. However, fNIRS is susceptible to motion artifacts and physiological noise, and several methods have been proposed to address these limitations60–62. In this study, we applied a stepwise exclusion criterion (grades 1–3) to reduce the impact of external noise on the fNIRS measurements. This was based on the SNR of the raw data, total sleep time, and level of movement during sleep59,63,64.

As shown in Table 3, stricter exclusion criteria led to stronger associations between apnea-related indices and rSO₂, whereas no consistent association was observed with hypopnea indices. In addition, a significant negative association between CT90 and rSO₂ was observed in grades 2 and 3, but not in grade 1. This suggests that noise reduction may enhance the quality and sensitivity of the fNIRS signal. Alternatively, stricter exclusion criteria may have disproportionately removed participants from the non-OSA group, thereby increasing the relative proportion of individuals with more severe OSA. This shift in sample characteristics may have elevated the contribution of apnea events to overall CT90, potentially amplifying the observed association, given that both rSO₂ and CT90 are closely associated with apnea severity.

Beyond these analytical considerations, this study had several limitations. First, the relatively small sample size, further reduced by the exclusion of Grade 3 participants, may have weakened the statistical power, and the lack of control for sex differences also limits the generalizability of the findings. Second, rSO₂ was measured solely in the prefrontal cortex using fNIRS, which limits the generalizability of the findings to the entire brain. Third, sedative and psychotropic drug use was controlled, but antihypertensives and other cardiovascular drugs were not. Some participants had hypertension or cardiovascular disease. Therefore, these factors may have influenced cerebral hemodynamics, oxygenation, and autoregulation. Fourth, hypopneas defined by airflow reduction accompanied only by arousals (without oxygen desaturation) were not considered, which may have led to an underestimation of the AHI.

Nevertheless, the results of this study provide foundational insights into the cerebral hemodynamic impact of sleep apnea in cognitively normal elderly individuals and underscore the necessity and clinical potential of directly measuring cerebral oxygen saturation. Future studies should include cognitively impaired older adults and larger, more diverse samples to further elucidate the interaction between cerebral autoregulatory mechanisms and OSA severity, ultimately contributing to the development of effective sleep-related intervention strategies for the elderly population.

Conclusion

This study is one of the first to utilize fNIRS and PSG to investigate cerebral oxygen saturation responses to OSA in cognitively normal older adults. rSO₂ and SpO₂ showed different association profiles with OSA indices. These findings suggest that PSG indices such as SpO₂ and AHI may be insufficient to fully reflect cerebral oxygenation during sleep. Given that older adults may be particularly vulnerable to cognitive decline associated with hypoxic stress, direct monitoring of cerebral oxygen saturation may provide clinically valuable insights into both the physiological and cognitive aspects of OSA. These insights, in turn, offer an important basis for the early detection of OSA-related cerebral hypoxic stress, the development of personalized treatment strategies, and the preservation of long-term cognitive health. Nevertheless, this study did not include direct, event-level comparisons between measures. Therefore, further work should incorporate event duration and employ statistical approaches such as the Inter-Class Correlation Coefficient (ICC) to characterize the relationship between cerebral and peripheral oxygenation more precisely. In addition, Larger and more diverse cohorts, particularly those including individuals with cognitive impairment, are needed to validate these findings and to clarify how OSA severity influences cerebral oxygenation. Finally, integrating fNIRS with EEG or cognitive performance assessments may provide a more comprehensive and clinically meaningful understanding of OSA-related brain alterations.
