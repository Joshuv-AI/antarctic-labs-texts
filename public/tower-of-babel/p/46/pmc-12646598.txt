Identification of Acute Respiratory Failure Phenotypes With Electronic Health Record Data
Authors: Charles R. Terry, Daniel L. Brinton, Katie G. Kirchoff, Andrew J. Goodwin, Dee W. Ford
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:Secondary analysis of clinical trial data and highly selected observational cohorts have identified 2 subphenotypes in acute respiratory failure, but have not been reported previously using only real-world electronic health record (EHR) data.RESEARCH QUESTION:Are subphenotypes of acute ventilator-dependent respiratory failure identifiable using readily available EHR data?STUDY DESIGN AND METHODS:This multicenter retrospective cohort study used patient encounters from the Medical University of South Carolina (n = 4,233 between 2016 and 2023) and the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (n = 8,313 between 2001 and 2012) to train and validate K-means models with multiply imputed cluster analysis at 24 and 48 hours after intubation.RESULTS:Clustering models identified 2 clusters for 24-hour and 48-hour models in both training and test cohorts with clusters separating on variables related to pulmonary physiology, perfusion, organ dysfunction, and metabolic dysregulation. Cluster 2 showed higher 90-day mortality after discharge and more ventilator days compared with cluster 1 that persisted despite multivariable adjustment for age, illness severity, and comorbidities. Cluster models and clusters were stable in 0- to 24-hour and 25- to 48-hour models with crossover (29.2% and 25.9% of the test and training cohorts) from the higher-acuity cluster 2 to the lower-acuity cluster 1 subphenotype occurring by 48 hours after intubation.INTERPRETATION:Our results suggest that acute ventilator-dependent respiratory failure has 2 subphenotypes that are discernible using readily available data from EHRs with identifiable differences in pulmonary physiologic features, perfusion, organ dysfunction, and metabolic dysregulation at 24 and 48 hours after intubation. This may enable future EHR tools to identify particularly vulnerable patients.

ARDS remains a heterogeneous and poorly recognized clinical syndrome with disappointingly few proven therapies beyond lung protective ventilation, conservative fluid management, and early prone ventilation.1–3 Two reproducible clinical subphenotypes identified with unsupervised machine learning methods have been identified consistently using secondary data from clinical trials and retrospective cohorts in ARDS and non-ARDS subgroups of acute ventilator-dependent respiratory failure (VDRF).4–6 These 2 subphenotypes demonstrate different clinical outcomes and respond differently to disease-modifying therapies, such as steroids, fluid strategies, statins, and high positive end-expiratory pressure ventilation strategies.4,7–9 Phenotypic approaches improve homogeneity within clinically defined syndromes, thereby improving prognostication and decreasing heterogeneity of treatment effects. (For consistency, the term subphenotype is defined as “a distinct subgroup of a phenotype based on a shared set or pattern of observable or measurable properties, which can be reliably discriminated from other sub-phenotypes” as detailed in Gordon et al.10)

Implementation of machine learning models for phenotyping in clinical care must account for time-dependent variables, diagnostic imprecision in clinical practice, data missingness, and construct validity of clinical diagnoses and syndromes. Barriers to using subphenotypes in clinical care and research include model complexity, inaccessibility of biomarker testing, and data missingness in routine clinical practice. Although parsimonious models using 3 and 4 variables have identified ARDS subphenotypes successfully, they still rely on interleukin testing that is not available clinically in most hospitals.11 Readily available electronic health record (EHR) data have been used with machine learning classifiers to identify hyperinflammatory subphenotypes originally identified by latent classes with reasonable accuracy, suggesting that phenotyping using readily available clinical data is feasible.12 However, these models used supervised machine learning methods (as opposed to unsupervised methods) in prelabelled observational cohorts with ARDS with low rates of missingness in validation data sets using extreme values for input data, which is not reflective of data available or diagnostic uncertainty for most clinical encounters in real time.13 Although these models represent an important proof of concept, EHR data often have unreliable diagnostic labels, and prospective, just-in-time analysis precludes using extreme values for prospective modelling strategies (because extreme values for a given period can be known only after the period of interest has passed), limiting their applicability for ongoing subphenotype identification. Moreover, evidence from the Epidemiology, Patterns of Care, and Mortality for Patients with Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome in Intensive Care Units in 50 Countries (LUNG-SAFE) study suggests that ARDS remains underdiagnosed and poorly recognized in clinical practice, limiting its usefulness as a diagnostic label for embedded machine learning models.14 Recent evidence that non-ARDS acute VDRF also can be classified as 2 subphenotypes suggests that a phenotypic model including ARDS and non-ARDS causes of acute VDRF may be more generalizable and may avoid the diagnostic uncertainty inherent in ARDS.6

To examine this possibility, we trained and externally validated 2 unsupervised K-means clustering models with predictor variables obtained entirely from structured EHR data and compared the masked clinical outcomes of each cluster and the prevalence of critical illness syndromes as identified from administrative coding data. We hypothesized that patients with acute VDRF (1) would have latent physiologic subphenotypes that are identifiable with readily available data from routine clinical activity, (2) would have differentiable clinical outcomes, and (3) would have temporally stable cluster assignments. These models extend the field of subphenotyping by using real-world EHR data and represent an important step toward prospective implementation.

Study Design and Methods

Study Design and Ethical Considerations

This was a multicenter observational cohort study using EHR data collected for clinical care. Its design and procedures were approved by the Medical University of South Carolina (MUSC) institutional review board (identifier: Pro00126191; approval date, January 26, 2023) with a waiver of informed consent. All procedures were followed in accordance with the ethical standards of the responsible committee on human experimentation and with the tenets of the Helsinki Declaration of 1975.

Data Source and Patient Population

Adults 18 years of age or older who received invasive mechanical ventilation in an ICU location for ≥ 48 hours and ≤ 30 days were included for both training and validation cohorts. EHR hospitalization data from MUSC hospitals from 2016 through 2023 comprised the training cohort, whereas the validation cohort was derived using patient encounters from the Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care III (MIMIC-III) (Figs 1, 2).15 Encounters were screened for inclusion using Current Procedural Terminology codes for invasive mechanical ventilation for 24 to 96 hours and > 96 hours, followed by identifying the total duration of mechanical ventilation using ventilator-specific variables from respiratory therapy clinical flowsheets. Diagnoses for each encounter were derived from administrative billing codes for MUSC and MIMIC cohorts (e-Table 1). Summative variables from the MIMIC cohort were obtained using standard query language queries available from the MIMIC coding repository (e-Table 2). Initiation of mechanical ventilation was defined by the time stamp of the first invasive ventilator-specific variable. Ventilator data was mostly missing from 2,159 encounters in the MUSC cohort. These encounters were missing minute ventilation, positive end-expiratory pressure, and exhaled tidal volumes, but had recorded plateau pressure (Pplat). Given the importance of ventilator data in the analysis, these encounters were excluded. The missing cohort was slightly younger and showed slightly worsened hypoxemia, but demonstrate similar Pplat compared with the analyzed cohort. Although statistically significant differences were noted in the predictor variables, median values did not identify any obvious clinically meaningful differences in predictor variables. Likewise, although statistically different, the median and interquartile ranges between analyzed and missing groups for ventilator days and hospital length of stay (LOS) were similar, and hospital mortality was not statistically different (e-Table 3).

Predictor Variable Selection

Predictor variables selected for clustering models were chosen based on known associations from prior literature in similar populations, their availability in the EHR, and independence from other predictor variables. Predictor variables for clustering included indicators of pulmonary physiologic characteristics (Pplat, minute ventilation, positive end-expiratory pressure, oxygen saturation in peripheral blood [Spo2] to FIO2 ratio, and exhaled tidal volume adjusted for ideal body weight), inflammation (WBC count, temperature, and platelet count), perfusion (mean arterial pressure, pulse, weight-adjusted norepinephrine dosing, and lactic acid), organ dysfunction (serum creatinine, alkaline phosphatase, and alanine transferase), and metabolic dysregulation (albumin, calcium, serum bicarbonate, hemoglobin, sodium, potassium, glucose, and BMI). These variables were chosen based on their class-defining features in previous analysis of ARDS phenotypes and representativeness of other class-defining variables measured with more esoteric biomarker testing.4–6,11–13 Variables with > 50% missingness were excluded from model training to ensure representativeness of the underlying distribution of predictor variables. Cluster models were masked to clinical outcome variables. No predictor variables from the MIMIC cohort were used in model training.

K-Means Clustering With Multiply Imputed Cluster Analysis

Cluster analysis for 0- to 24-hour and 25- to 48-hour models used multiply imputed cluster analysis, as described by Lee and Harel.17 Training and testing data sets were multiply imputed 20 times for missing variables using an expectation-maximization algorithm with boot-strapping.18 Imputed median values for predictor variables from the training and validation cohorts and their missingness fractions are presented in the data supplement (e-Tables 4–6). Each imputed data set was found to have a high clustering tendency (e-Table 7). Correlational analyses was used to identify and remove colinear predictor variables (e-Figures 1–4). Stage I clustering created 2 K-means models for 0- to 24-hour and 25- to 48-hour variables using the imputed data sets. A matrix of the variable centers from each cluster from the imputed data sets was clustered again using a K-medoids algorithm for stage II clustering to assign pooled center estimates for both models. Finally, we assigned clusters for encounters without missing predictor variables using a K-means cluster model with pooled center estimates, whereas encounters with missing data were assigned to their most frequently assigned cluster among imputed data sets. All predictor variables were scaled to a normal distribution. Optimal cluster number was evaluated by reduction of within-cluster sum of squares, the average silhouette width per cluster, Gap statistic, partition instability, and the NBClust procedure19 (e-Figures 5–9). Prinicipal components of the predictor variables from the 0-24 and 25-48 hour models with training and validation cohorts and alternative clustering and imputation methods were examined to ensure the robustness of cluster analysis and results (e-Figures 10–13).

Clinical Outcomes by Cluster for 0- to 24-Hour and 25- to 48-Hour Models

Pooled cluster assignments were evaluated by masked clinical outcomes and cluster stability. Because all cluster modelling was unsupervised analysis, external validity and generalizability were assessed by prognostic features from masked clinical outcomes applied to the MIMIC-III cohort, which was not used in any aspect of model training. Encounters for both models in the test and training cohorts were stratified by cluster to compare differences in the ventilator days, hospital LOS, hospital mortality, and 90-day mortality after hospital discharge. Cluster assignment stability across both models was evaluated for hospital mortality.

All data were reported as medians with interquartile ranges or frequencies and percentages. Differences between groups were analyzed by Wilcoxon rank-sum test or χ2 test, as appropriate. All analyses were performed using R version 4.4.0 software (R Foundation for Statistical Computing)20 and the RStudio Integrated Development Environment version 2024.09.0+375 (Posit Software). Additional detail on the methods is provided in e-Appendix 1.

Results

Patient Characteristics

We analyzed 4,233 encounters from 4,037 patients and 8,313 encounters from 7,583 patients meeting acute VDRF criteria from MUSC and the MIMIC-III databases, respectively. Both cohorts demonstrated similar ventilator days, although the MIMIC-III cohort showed longer hospital LOS. Hospital mortality and 90-day mortality after discharge were higher in the MUSC cohort compared with the MIMIC-III cohort, reflective of the higher acuity and comorbidities present (Table 1, e-Table 6). The MUSC cohort showed a higher prevalence of sepsis and ARDS billing diagnoses, whereas the MIMIC-III cohort showed a slightly higher rate of pneumonia (Table 1).21 Predictor variables among both cohorts were notable for worse hypoxemia and higher exhaled tidal volumes in the MIMIC-III cohort, whereas the MUSC cohort showed higher serum creatinine consistent with high prevalence of comorbid renal failure (Table 1, e-Table 6).

K-Means Clustering Model Training

Based on a consensus of cluster number diagnostics, a 2-cluster model was chosen for both 0- to 24-hour and 25- to 48-hour models (e-Appendix 1). The 0- to 24-hour model identified 2,533 encounters (60.4%) and 1,700 encounters (39.6%) in the training cohort in clusters 1 and 2, respectively; similarly, the 25- to 48-hour model identified 3,046 encounters (72.0%) and 1,187 encounters (28.0%) for each cluster, respectively (Table 2). The MIMIC-III validation cohort was divided into 5,123 encounters (61.6%) and 3,190 encounters (38.4%) and into 5,949 encounters (71.6%) and 2,364 encounters (28.4%) for clusters 1 and 2 in each respective model, as well.

Clusters from both the earlier and later models mostly differed from each other on variables related to pulmonary physiologic features (eg, Spo2 to FIO2 ratio, positive end-expiratory pressure, Pplat) and organ perfusion (eg, vasopressors, mean arterial pressure, serum lactate) (Figs 3, 4, e-Tables 9–16). Cluster 1 at both time points showed less dispersion than cluster 2, suggesting more consistency with respect to predictor variables. Cluster 2 showed more dispersion in the 25- to 48-hour model compared with the 0- to 24-hour model, suggesting dynamic changes in acuity and organ failure as their clinical course progresses after intubation. Interestingly, cluster differences in predictor variables related to perfusion and organ dysfunction were attenuated in the 25- to 48-hour model, whereas the pulmonary physiologic variables were persistent. Principal component analysis corroborated the influence of these predictors with respect to the variance in the cohort (e-Figs 10, 11).

Clinical Outcomes by Cluster

For each period and in each cohort, cluster 2 experienced worse adjusted in-hospital mortality and 90-day mortality after discharge than cluster 1 (Tables 2, 3). Differences in mortality outcomes between clusters were robust to different metrics of comorbidities and severity of illness (e-Tables 17–21). MUSC cluster 2 showed a higher frequency of sepsis-, pneumonia-, and ARDS-related administrative diagnostic codes than MUSC cluster 1 (e-Table 17). MIMIC-III clusters showed similar differences in sepsis and ARDS; however, no differences in the frequency of pneumonia-related codes were found between the 2 clusters (e-Table 19). Differences in sepsis were consistent and robust to different administrative coding definitions of sepsis, as well (e-Table 8).22,23 Cluster assignments were consistent between both time points, with the most significant crossover (29.2% and 25.9% of the MUSC and MIMIC-III cohorts) occurring from cluster 2 to cluster 1 subphenotypes occurring by 48 hours after intubation (Fig 5).

The crossover group from 0- to 24-hour cluster 2 to 25- to 48-hour cluster 1 showed fewer ventilator days, shorter hospital LOS, and marginally lower rates of hospital mortality and 90-day mortality after discharge than encounters that remained in cluster 2 in both models. Similarly, the crossover group from 0- to 24-hour cluster 1 to 25- to 48-hour cluster 2 showed more ventilator days, longer hospital LOS, and rates of hospital mortality and 90-day mortality after discharge that were slightly higher or similar to those of cluster 1 (e-Tables 22, 23). Sepsis and ARDS were more prevalent in cluster 2 and the crossover group from 0- to 24-hour cluster 2 to 25- to 48-hour cluster 1 with similar relative prevalence ratios, but different absolute prevalences, between cohorts (e-Tables 24, 25).

Discussion

This study demonstrated the ability of machine learning to identify subphenotypes of patients with acute VDRF using real-world EHR data and despite the presence of significant missingness. Our results are consistent with previous results in secondary analyses of randomized clinical trials and observational cohorts in ARDS and non-ARDS populations4–9,11–13 and advance the field by supporting the feasibility of clustering methods to identify latent subphenotypes in patients with acute VDRF using routinely available clinical data. This work extends the existing literature of cluster analysis and phenotyping in acute VDRF by using EHR data with unselected patient encounters and new methodologies to address challenges of subphenotype prediction using EHR data from clinical encounters.

We identified 2 clusters in our training and testing VDRF cohorts, consisting of higher- and lower-acuity subphenotypes differing on clinical outcomes. These subphenotypes differed by their prevalence of sepsis and ARDS and masked clinical outcomes, which remained different after statistical adjustment for comorbidities and illness severity. Cluster differences in each of our models supported the importance of predictor variables related to lung compliance, hypoxemia, shock, and organ dysfunction for separating each subphenotype. Pplat, positive end-expiratory pressure, and SpO2 to FIO2 ratio remained among the most important contributors to the differences between both clusters at both time points.

Although the phenotypic approach to reducing heterogeneity in VDRF is a promising strategy for advancing precision medicine in critical care, clinical subphenotypes must correlate with differences in the pathophysiologic features and mechanisms of disease present in the syndromes under study. A key limitation of our findings and an area for further research is identifying whether subphenotypes in acute VDRF predict treatment response for current interventions in respiratory failure. Because of the pragmatic nature of our study question and data, we did not have corroborating biomarker data to inform our cluster phenotypes or to determine how closely our clinically derived clusters aligned with existing models for inflammatory or noninflammatory subphenotypes. However, our models identified similar associations with predictor variables as other more comprehensive phenotyping studies in patients with VDRF with and without ARDS.6,24 These similar associations offer the intriguing hypothesis of mechanism-focused classification of patients with acute VDRF using EHR-derived clinical variables alone, which, if validated, would reduce the cost and logistical investment further for implementation of subphenotype-driven clinical trials and care. Phenotyping with EHR data offers the additional dimension of using clinical variable trajectories from repeated measurements, which can reflect important inflammatory phenotypes and differences in immune and biomarker profiles, as seen in sepsis temperature trajectories.25 Our models and predictor variable associations are consistent with previous findings in acute VDRF and ARDS and support a modelling approach using EHR data, with minimal preprocessing embedded in existing care processes. Future work will need to correlate our clinically derived clusters with biomarkers or agreement with previous models in secondary data analysis of existing clinical trials.

Data missingness was a frequent occurrence in both the training and validation cohort, which we addressed using multiple imputation and a novel clustering strategy adapted from Lee and Harel17 that accounts for incomplete data while estimating the overall predictor centroids for each cluster. This approach addresses a key limitation of K-means clustering that typically requires complete cases for analysis. We excluded 2,159 encounters from our training cohort because of important ventilatory variables such as exhaled tidal volume, PEEP, and minute ventilation were missing entirely from respiratory flowsheets available from our clinical data warehouse. The missing data encounters seem to have occurred primarily in our academic hospital early during the period included in our sample, suggesting that the missing data may be related to changes in EHR flowsheet documentation or data storage practices from our clinical data warehouse. Nevertheless, although statistically significant differences were noted in predictor and outcome variables between the 2 groups, these did not seem so different from the analyzed cohort as to bias the results from our cluster models significantly.

The temporal stability of subphenotypes is important for ensuring robust predictions and determining the frequency and timing of assessments. Our models used the 0- to 24-hour and 25- to 48-hour periods after intubation to identify subphenotypes based on prior data suggesting that early intervention with lung protective ventilation in mechanical ventilation influences clinical outcomes.26 Cluster assignments for cluster 1 were largely stable between 24 and 48 hours; however, 25.9% and 29.2% of the MIMIC-III and MUSC cluster 2 groups transitioned to cluster 1 in the 48-hour model. These findings contrast with prior work by Delucchi et al,27 who found low probabilities of transition between nonreactive and inflammatory subphenotypes in the Ventilation with Lower Tidal Volumes as Compared with Traditional Tidal Volumes for Acute Lung Injury and the Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ARMA) and Higher Versus Lower Positive End-Expiratory Pressures in Patients with the Acute Respiratory Distress Syndrome (ALVEOLI) clinical trial cohorts. Similarly, their models and ours found relatively consistent importance of the clinical predictors in both models. Clinical outcomes for clusters that crossed over from cluster 1 to cluster 2 and from cluster 2 to cluster 1 from the 0- to 24-hour and 25- to 48-hour models were worse (longer duration receiving ventilation, longer hospital LOS, and higher mortality) than the patient encounters that remained in cluster 1 and better than the outcomes for patients that remained in cluster 2. The prevalence of sepsis in groups that crossed over to different clusters in the 25- to 48-hour models seem mostly to reflect the same prevalence of sepsis as their original cluster groups in the 0- to 24-hour model. These results suggest that clinical subphenotypes are not static from the time of admission and may affect clinical outcomes. Similarly, relative differences in feature importance between ventilatory and perfusion parameters in the 0- to 24-hour and 25- to 48-hour models were persistent for ventilatory variables, but were more attenuated for perfusion parameters, suggesting that certain cluster-defining features for VDRF subphenotypes are time dependent and time independent with respect to duration of mechanical ventilation. Future studies looking at the stability of reactive and noninflammatory biomarker profiles are warranted to ensure generalizability of these models to all patients with acute VDRF.

As discussed earlier, limitations of our models include an inability to test for differential treatment responses between both subphenotypes because of the observational and retrospective nature of this study. Although future observational studies supporting causal inference, such as propensity score adjustment or trial emulation, may be useful in identifying differential treatment responses between both subphenotypes, a prospective, randomized clinical trial with stratification by subphenotype would be the ideal way of addressing this important clinical question. Further, although all our data were obtained from EHRs, extensive preprocessing was still necessary before analysis. Unlocking the potential of embedded clinical research, learning health systems, and precision medicine in critical care depend on developing robust and versatile data pipelines that can clean data for real-time or near real-time analysis rapidly and reliably for research and clinical care.

Interpretation

Our findings support the feasibility of subphenotyping patients with acute VDRF using data created from routine clinical activity. Although subphenotype assignment has been assumed to be static, we found evidence of crossover, particularly for the reactive cluster, between subphenotypes, suggesting that subphenotypes are not static in the early intubation period. Future work should explore biomarker signature differences of crossover patients, differential treatment effects by cluster, and external or temporal validation of cluster models with additional data.
