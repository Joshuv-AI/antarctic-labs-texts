Arthroscopic Bicortical Biceps Anchorage: An Arthroscopic‐Assisted Technique for Subpectoral Biceps Tenodesis
Authors: Gert‐Jan Opsomer, Ludwig Anné, Olivier Verborgt
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractLong head of the biceps tendon pathology is a frequent cause of anterior shoulder pain. Tenotomy is technically simple but may lead to cosmetic deformity and muscle cramping, whereas tenodesis better preserves the biceps contour but involves a more complex procedure. Multiple surgical techniques and fixation methods are available. No single technique has emerged as clinically superior, and no gold standard has been established. Among the various techniques and fixation sites, subpectoral placement within cancellous bone has been proposed as a favorable option for long head of the biceps tendon tenodesis. This technical note describes a surgical approach—arthroscopic bicortical biceps anchorage—which combines subpectoral positioning, arthroscopic execution, and inlay fixation using a bicortical button. Nevertheless, the surgical technique is associated with a substantial learning curve and may involve a theoretical risk of neurovascular injury related to bicortical drilling. These considerations underscore the need for further biomechanical and clinical evaluation.

This video shows an arthroscopic‐assisted, subpectoral biceps tenodesis based on 3 core principles: fixation in the subpectoral region to allow complete removal of diseased tendon from the bicipital groove, an arthroscopically feasible technique, and bicortical fixation within cancellous bone to promote biologically favorable tendon‐to‐bone healing. These principles form the conceptual basis of the arthroscopic bicortical biceps anchorage technique. Video content can be viewed at https://doi.org/10.1002/atn2.70089.

Pathology of the long head of the biceps tendon is a frequent contributor to anterior shoulder pain, particularly in aging populations and patients with coexisting rotator cuff injuries. 1 Two main surgical treatments are available. Tenotomy represents a technically straightforward and time‐efficient procedure. Nevertheless, this approach is associated with a higher incidence of cosmetic deformity as well as potential muscle cramping. 2 , 3 In contrast, tenodesis aims to maintain the anatomic contour and functional strength of the biceps muscle. 4 However, this technique typically requires a more prolonged rehabilitation period and carries an increased risk of complications related to the fixation site. 5 , 6

A biceps tenodesis can be executed through an arthroscopic or an open technique. Arthroscopy offers the advantage of minimally invasive surgery and the ability to address concomitant intra‐articular pathology, whereas the open technique may be technically less demanding. 7 , 8

Various implant types are available, including interference screws, suture anchors, and cortical buttons. Each has distinct biomechanical and clinical profiles. Despite differences, current evidence shows no clear superiority of 1 implant type over another. 9 , 10

The insertion of the tenodesis can be performed at different levels along the humerus, each with specific benefits and limitations. 11 Intra‐articular or proximal groove fixation requires minimal dissection, but diseased tendon segments may remain, potentially causing persistent pain. 12 Suprapectoral tenodesis, located just above the pectoralis major tendon, provides good visualization and a balance between efficiency and accuracy, whereas subpectoral tenodesis, distal to the cranial border of the pectoralis major insertion, removes all diseased tendon and is often favored. 13 , 14 , 15

Tendon fixation limited to the cortical surface results in relatively poor biological integration. 16 By extending fixation into the intramedullary canal, the tendon benefits from a larger contact area and enhanced osseointegration. 4 , 17

From a conceptual point of view, the ideal biceps tenodesis should integrate 3 core principles. First, the tenodesis should be performed in the subpectoral region, allowing for the complete removal of the diseased tendon out of the bicipital groove. Second, the technique should be arthroscopically feasible. Third, the procedure must enable fixation within cancellous bone, rather than against cortical surfaces, to support biologically favorable tendon‐to‐bone healing. This triad—subpectoral location, arthroscopic access, and cancellous integration—forms the conceptual basis for an arthroscopic‐assisted, bicortical biceps tenodesis strategy. Therefore, we describe a surgical technique: arthroscopic bicortical biceps anchorage.

SURGICAL TECHNIQUE

The patient is positioned in a beach chair position. The arm is mounted on a Trimano positioning system (Arthrex, Naples, FL). The arm is placed in approximately 45° of forward flexion and 45° of abduction.

For this procedure, we need 4 portals (Figure 1). It begins with a standard posterior viewing portal (Portal P). Through this portal, a comprehensive diagnostic arthroscopy is performed to assess the glenohumeral joint. If the decision is made to do a biceps tenodesis, a standard anterior working portal (Portal A) is created through the rotator interval. A suture is passed through the biceps tendon and retrieved to the anterior portal. After this tagging of the long head of the biceps tendon, a tenotomy is performed (Figure 2).

Posterior viewing portal in beach position. Arthroscopic intra‐articular view of the shoulder showing suture passage through the biceps tendon (A,B), followed by arthroscopic tenotomy (C). (LHBT, long head of the biceps tendon.)

Subsequently, the scope is moved to the subacromial space using the same posterior portal. After evaluation of subacromial structures, a classic lateral portal (Portal L) is established to address any subacromial pathology as needed. Once subacromial work is completed, the camera is shifted to the lateral portal (Portal L), which now serves as the main viewing portal until the end of the procedure. An accessory axillary working portal (Portal AX) is then made 18 (Figure 3). Under direct arthroscopic visualization through the lateral portal, a spinal needle is used to localize the ideal entry site. The goal is to target the anteromedial humeral cortex, ensuring alignment over the bicipital groove, just at the superior border of the pectoralis major tendon insertion. A landmark for this portal is the axillary fold (Figure 3). The entry point is 1 cm lateral and superior to this fold. Through this axillary portal, a complete anterior bursectomy is performed, allowing for arthroscopic identification of the pectoralis major tendon insertion (Figure 4). Knowing that the biceps tendon lies medial to this insertion, the sheath is incised longitudinally to expose the extra‐articular portion of the biceps tendon. The tendon is then retrieved through the axillary portal and temporarily secured with a clamp outside the body for later preparation and fixation (Figure 5).

Once the tendon is exteriorized through the axillary portal, it is secured to a TightRope implant: Type II RT (Arthrex, Naples, FL) (Figure 6). The biceps tendon is then looped through the loop of the implant and then secured with 5 Krakow stitches using a No. 2 FiberWire (Figure 7). During fixation, special attention should be given in applying steady traction to the biceps tendon. Maintaining proper tension at this stage is essential to reproduce the anatomic length‐tension relationship of the biceps muscle and tendon. Failure to do so may result in inadequate tensioning and the occurrence of a postoperative Popeye deformity. Following fixation, the diseased remnant of the tendon is resected at a level proximal to the tendon‐implant interface. Next, the biceps tendon is gauged for diameter, and the measurement should be used to select a tunnel size that allows for a slightly firm fit (Figure 8).

Extracorporeal measurement of the looped LHBT diameter for humeral tunnel sizing prior to fixation in the ABBA technique. Viewing from right‐sided shoulder in beach position. (ABBA, arthroscopic bicortical biceps anchorage; LHBT, long head of the biceps tendon.)

Arthroscopic visualization is resumed through the lateral viewing portal, where the insertion of the pectoralis major tendon is again identified. Using the axillary working portal, a guide pin is introduced and drilled through both the anterior and posterior humeral cortex. The entry point is just inferior to the superior border of the pectoralis major insertion (Figure 9). The pin is advanced from anterior to posterior across the humerus, with a slight inferior inclination (less risk of neurovascular damage: further research is done, but not yet published). After the guide pin has traversed the humerus and exited posteriorly through the skin, the anterior cortex is then drilled with the correct drill size unicortically, matching the measured tendon diameter (Figure 10). The passing suture of the implant is then shuttled along the guide pin from anterior to posterior. The cortical button is drawn through the posterior exit point of the humerus and securely flipped against the posterior cortex under direct tensioning (Figure 11). Finally, the biceps tendon is then pulled into the prepared tunnel from the anterior aspect until it is seated on the intramedullary side of the posterior cortex (Figure 12). It is then seated within the cancellous bone channel. An example of a postoperative X‐ray shows the positioning of the cortical button (Figure 13).

DISCUSSION

The management of the long head of the biceps tendon pathology remains a subject of ongoing debate, with both tenotomy and tenodesis showing favorable results in different patient populations. 6 , 19 , 20 In cases where a biceps tenodesis is indicated, there remains significant debate regarding the preferred surgical technique. 21

Although the relative merits of different techniques may vary among surgeons, with 1 approach being better suited to a given surgeon than another, the current technique shows several general advantages that are widely applicable. First, this technique has an arthroscopic nature, yet the technically demanding step of tendon fixation to the implant is conducted extracorporeally. This not only simplifies the procedure but also enhances the strength of the fixation. The second advantage of this technique is that it mitigates the problem of residual pain frequently encountered when the biceps tenodesis is performed in zone 1 or 2 of the bicipital tunnel. By utilizing a zone 3 fixation, distal to the cranial border of the pectoral major tendon, it further minimizes the likelihood of bottleneck constriction at the superior border of the pectoralis major. 22 Third, it is an inlay configuration technique, which may promote a more favorable biological environment for tendon healing. 16 , 23 , 24 The advantages and disadvantages of the technique are summarized in Table 3.

Despite its conceptual advantages, the arthroscopic bicortical biceps anchorage technique is not without limitations. The procedure requires advanced arthroscopic skills. Furthermore, bicortical drilling of the humerus carries a theoretical risk of neurovascular injury to posterior structures. Ongoing research about neurovascular safety is being conducted.
