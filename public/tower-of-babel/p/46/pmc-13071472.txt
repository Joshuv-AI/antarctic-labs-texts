Size Disparity in Putative Primate Adaptive Radiations and Other Mammalian Clades
Authors: Jeremiah E. Scott
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionAdaptive radiation is commonly viewed as the rapid production of phenotypic disparity and taxonomic diversity. Among primates, two clades have been identified as potential adaptive radiations: the lemurs of Madagascar and the platyrrhines of the Americas. This study examines these clades for one of the proposed signals of adaptive radiation: exceptional disparity in an ecologically important trait.Materials and MethodsI sliced a time‐scaled molecular phylogeny of extant and recently extinct mammals at the Eocene–Oligocene and mid‐Oligocene boundaries, creating samples of clades that are comparable in age to or younger than Lemuriformes and Platyrrhini. For each clade, phenotypic disparity was quantified as the variance of species means for body mass. Differences in disparity attributable to clade age were controlled using phylogenetic regression.ResultsBoth primate clades have high age‐adjusted disparity: lemuriforms are among the most size‐disparate, ranking in the 95th percentile among clades originating after the Eocene–Oligocene boundary; platyrrhines are in the 84th percentile for clades originating after the mid‐Oligocene boundary. None of the high‐disparity clades in the samples are outliers, and the distributions of disparity are neither positively skewed nor bimodal.ConclusionsIf adaptive radiation has influenced size evolution in mammals, the effect is not apparent in the distributions of size disparity. Without outliers, positive skew, or bimodality, there are no criteria for identifying exceptional clades. The magnitudes of disparity found in lemuriforms and platyrrhines can be interpreted as consistent with adaptive radiation, but size disparity does not provide decisive support for recognizing them as such.

Introduction

Adaptive radiation is an important concept in macroevolutionary biology, which aims to understand the origin of large‐scale patterns of taxonomic, phenotypic, and ecological diversity (Stanley 1975; Erwin 2000; Jablonski 2017a, 2017b). Henry Fairfield Osborn introduced the term adaptive radiation and visualized it as lineages (“radii”) diverging from a plesiomorphic ancestor in various directions of ecological and morphological specialization (Osborn 1902). The concept was later updated and integrated into the Modern Synthesis (e.g., Huxley 1942; Simpson 1944; Lack 1947; reviewed by Givnish 1997), with George Gaylord Simpson's formulation in The Major Features of Evolution being particularly influential in guiding subsequent research (Simpson 1953, 222–230; Schluter 2000). Under the Simpsonian model, adaptive radiation is triggered by ecological opportunity in the form of empty niches or underutilized resources. Rates of phenotypic evolution and speciation accelerate in the initial stages, driven by divergent natural selection and ecological speciation, and then slow down as niches are filled, resulting in an early‐burst pattern of phenotypic divergence and lineage diversification (Schluter 2000; Rabosky and Lovette 2008a; Glor 2010; Harmon et al. 2010). Thus, adaptive radiation is commonly viewed as the rapid production of taxonomic and phenotypic diversity in response to ecological opportunity.

There is disagreement over which aspects of the Simpsonian model are necessary for recognizing a clade as an adaptive radiation (Olson and Arroyo‐Santos 2009; Givnish 1997, 2015). Ecological opportunity is widely considered to be an essential component, given its role as a causal mechanism (Simpson 1953; Schluter 2000; Yoder et al. 2010; Gillespie et al. 2020). Four types of opportunity have been recognized: extinction of antagonists (competitors or predators), dispersal to a region without antagonists, appearance of a new resource, and the evolution of a trait that allows exploitation of previously inaccessible resources (i.e., a key innovation) (Losos 2010; Wellborn and Langerhans 2014; Stroud and Losos 2016). Ecological opportunity is difficult to quantify and is most often recognized retrospectively. That is, a clade is identified as a potential adaptive radiation based morphological, functional, and taxonomic criteria, and then a plausible, spatiotemporally proximate opportunity is identified (Losos 2010; Stroud and Losos 2016). The challenge of operationalizing ecological opportunity is most evident in cases where a lineage has failed to radiate when it might have been expected to do so. Such cases raise questions about our ability to accurately assess ecological opportunity and suggest that opportunity may not always be sufficient to trigger adaptive radiation (Losos 2010; Stroud and Losos 2016).

Among extant primates, two clades have attracted attention as potential adaptive radiations: the lemurs of Madagascar (Martin 1972; Tattersall 1973; Jungers 1980; Baab et al. 2014; Herrera 2017; Everson et al. 2025) and the platyrrhine monkeys of the Americas (Rosenberger 1980, 1992; Marroig and Cheverud 2005; Hodgson et al. 2009; Aristide et al. 2015). The ancestors of both groups are thought to have dispersed from Africa across water barriers during the Paleogene. In the case of lemurs, a single dispersal across the Mozambique Channel in the late Paleocene or early Eocene is the prevailing view (Yoder et al. 1996; Kistler et al. 2015), but fossils from eastern and northern Africa have been interpreted by Gunnell et al. (2018) as evidence for separate dispersals by the aye‐aye ( Daubentonia madagascariensis , infraorder Chiromyiformes) and the lineage leading to all other extant lemurs (infraorder Lemuriformes; I use the informal term lemurs to refer to the clade defined by the last common ancestor of Lemuriformes and Chiromyiformes). Whether representatives from other primate clades dispersed to Madagascar but failed to persist to the present is unknown owing to the nearly complete absence of a fossil record on the island between the end of the Cretaceous and the late Pleistocene (Godfrey et al. 2020). In South America, on the other hand, there is some evidence for multiple dispersals across the Atlantic Ocean by separate African anthropoid lineages during the late Eocene or early Oligocene (Bond et al. 2015; Seiffert et al. 2020; Marivaux et al. 2023). Only the platyrrhine lineage appears to have become established, however, as all known fossil primates from the early Miocene onward have been reconstructed as stem or crown platyrrhines (Silvestro et al. 2019; Beck et al. 2023; Marivaux et al. 2023). Although important questions remain, the current evidence suggests that extant lemurs and platyrrhines are likely to have diversified largely or entirely in the absence of competition with other primate clades. Thus, the origins of these clades are associated with plausible ecological opportunities.

Tests for the signals of Simpsonian adaptive radiation in lemurs and platyrrhines have produced mixed results. Both clades are ecologically diverse and exhibit an early burst in the evolution of body size, but neither appears to have been characterized by an early burst in speciation rate (Aristide et al. 2015; Herrera 2017; Everson et al. 2025). The implication of the latter finding for the hypothesis of adaptive radiation is unclear. The temporal dynamics of speciation in both clades were inferred using molecular phylogenies, but speciation and extinction rates reconstructed in deep time using this approach are unreliable (Rabosky and Lovette 2008b; Louca and Pennell 2020; O'Meara and Beaulieu 2021). The absence of an early‐burst pattern of speciation in a molecular phylogeny cannot, therefore, be used to reject adaptive radiation, and the presence of the pattern cannot be considered strong support. Aristide et al. (2015) argued that the platyrrhine fossil record is consistent with an increased rate of speciation early in the clade's history followed by a slowdown, but fossil‐based rate estimates lack the precision needed to distinguish among different scenarios (Silvestro et al. 2019). Beyond these methodological challenges, there is debate over whether an early burst in speciation is necessary for recognizing adaptive radiation (Losos and Mahler 2010; Glor 2010; Givnish 1997, 2015), especially given that subsequent events might obscure the initial burst (Everson et al. 2025).

The early‐burst signals of adaptive radiation are intrinsic properties. That is, the patterns can be identified without comparison to other clades. For example, the tests of adaptive radiation conducted by Aristide et al. (2015) in platyrrhines did not involve any other primates or mammals. Extrinsic criteria are also important, and some approaches to identifying adaptive radiation prioritize comparisons with other clades (Losos and Miles 2002; Losos and Mahler 2010; Givnish 2015; Moen et al. 2021; Miles et al. 2023; Morinaga et al. 2023). Losos and colleagues, in particular, have advocated for a definition of adaptive radiation that centers exceptional disparity in ecomorphological traits, arguing that, for the concept to be meaningful and useful, adaptive radiation must distinguish between mere adaptive divergence and the high levels of phenotypic variation that are associated with classic adaptive radiations (Losos and Miles 2002; Gavrilets and Losos 2009; Losos and Mahler 2010).

Developing criteria for recognizing unusually high morphological and ecological variation presents a challenge: What is the most relevant comparative context and what is the threshold for “unusually high” (Losos and Miles 2002; Moen et al. 2021; Miles et al. 2023; Morinaga et al. 2023)? One of the tests of adaptive radiation conducted by Herrera (2017) for lemurs was a comparison of the amount of ecospace occupied by lemurs and their sister clade, the Lorisiformes (lorises and galagos). The logic of a sister‐clade comparison is that it holds constant the effect of time on the accumulation of morphological, ecological, and taxonomic diversity (Mitter et al. 1988; Zeh et al. 1989; Losos and Miles 2002). All else being equal, we expect older clades to be more diverse than younger clades simply by virtue of the greater time older clades have had to diversify and diverge phenotypically. Lemurs certainly exhibit greater ecological and morphological variety than lorisiforms (Fleagle and Reed 1996; Fleagle 2013; Herrera 2017), but a single pairwise comparison has the potential to be misleading because it is not clear how representative the sister is of a typical clade (Losos and Miles 2002).

The present study evaluates whether body‐size disparity in lemurs and platyrrhines is exceptional by comparing these putative primate adaptive radiations to other mammalian clades. Body size only captures some aspects of adaptive diversity, but it has the advantage of being available for a large number of mammals and directly comparable across clades with highly divergent morphologies that might otherwise complicate large‐scale comparisons. Moreover, body size is an ecologically important variable linked with diet and locomotion in primates (e.g., Kay and Simons 1980; Jungers Fleagle 1985; Ford and Davis 1992), and it has played an important role in previous studies of adaptive radiation in lemurs (Herrera 2017), platyrrhines (Aristide et al. 2015), and other mammals (Alroy 1999; Slater et al. 2010; García‐Navas et al. 2018). Although unusually high phenotypic disparity is not a universally accepted criterion for recognizing a clade as an adaptive radiation, it is an important consideration and is often what researchers are interested in explaining when invoking adaptive radiation (Losos and Mahler 2010).

Materials and Methods

Phylogenetic Framework and Body‐Mass Data

The analyses conducted for this study are based on the dated molecular phylogenetic tree of mammals reconstructed by Álvarez‐Carretero et al. (2022). The tree includes 4705 extant and recently extinct (Holocene or late Pleistocene) species. Body‐mass data are from the PHYLACINE 1.2.1 database (Faurby et al. 2018), which is an expansion of the database of mean adult body masses compiled by Smith et al. (2003) from a variety of literature sources (see also Jones et al. 2009). The database also includes skeletal estimates of adult body mass for recently extinct taxa. Only values identified as “reported” were used in the analysis; data labeled otherwise (e.g., “imputed” or “assumed”) were excluded. The PHYLACINE data were matched with the species in the phylogenetic tree. Data for species that were not present in the tree were dropped, resulting in a sample size of 3346 extant species (Table S1), representing about half of the currently recognized diversity of living mammals (Burgin et al. 2025). Species means for body mass were log10‐transformed for analysis.

Given the importance of the giant subfossil lemurs in discussions of lemurs as an adaptive radiation (Martin 1972; Tattersall 1973; Jungers 1980; Baab et al. 2014; Herrera 2017), I included data for mammalian species that went extinct in the late Pleistocene or Holocene. There are 272 such species in the PHYLACINE database (Table S2), including 14 subfossil lemurs and five extinct platyrrhines. The subfossil lemurs increase the range of size variation found in extant lemurs by an order of magnitude, and the late Pleistocene megafaunal members of several other clades have a similar effect. For platyrrhines, the extinct species Protopithecus brasiliensis and Caipora bambuiorum extend the maximum body mass for the clade by 10–20 kg. A few recently extinct species are included in the molecular tree, most notably the subfossil lemurs Palaeopropithecus ingens , Megaladapis edwardsi , and Pachylemur jullyi . For the remaining extinct species, I inserted as many as possible into the analysis using the procedures described in Section 2.3.

Although many mammalian species exhibit substantial sexual dimorphism in body size (Tombak et al. 2024), macroevolutionary studies of size evolution typically use species means from pooled‐sex samples as the units of analysis (e.g., Cooper and Purvis 2010; Slater 2013; Baker et al. 2015; Price and Hopkins 2015). This procedure has also been used in recent studies focusing on primate clades with size‐dimorphic members (Aristide et al. 2015; Grabowski and Jungers 2017; Silvestro et al. 2019). A sex‐specific analysis can provide different signals than those found in a sex‐pooled analysis (Grabowski and Jungers 2017), but sex‐specific analyses were not possible in the present study for two reasons. First, PHYLACINE and other large databases such as PanTHERIA (Jones et al. 2009) do not report separate male and female values. Second, the individuals in the fossil samples cannot be assigned to sex with the same confidence as extant individuals.

Defining the Comparative Sample of Clades

The approach taken here to defining the comparative sample emphasizes clade age over named clades and taxonomic ranks. Clades have two ages: a crown age and a stem age (Magallón and Sanderson 2001). A crown clade is a clade that contains an ancestor and all of its extant descendants, along with the extinct lineages that are contained within those genealogical boundaries. A clade's crown age is simply the age of its last common ancestor. A clade's stem age is the age of the last common ancestor that the clade shares with its sister clade. In the context of adaptive radiation, unusually high disparity typically means disparity that is much greater than expected given the amount of time that a clade has been accumulating phenotypic variation (Losos and Miles 2002; Miles et al. 2023). Because this study deals with extant clades (crown clades), crown age is the most appropriate standard by which to judge disparity.

Using the treeSlice function in the phytools 2.0 package (Revell 2024) for R (R Core Team 2024), I sliced the tree at two time‐points. The first slice occurred at 33.9 Ma, the boundary between the Eocene and Oligocene epochs, creating 151 separate clades of early Oligocene age or younger. I retained 125 of those clades for analysis (Table S3); the other 26 were discarded because they contained only one species or they lacked sufficient data for estimating size disparity. The second slice occurred at 27.82 Ma, the boundary between early (Rupelian age) and late (Chattian age) Oligocene. This slice resulted in 248 clades of late Oligocene age or younger, 190 of which were retained for analysis (Table S4). These time‐points were chosen because crown Lemuriformes and crown Platyrrhini originated in the early Oligocene (30.35 Ma) and late Oligocene (24.67 Ma), respectively, according to the divergence dates estimated by Álvarez‐Carretero et al. (2022). Those dates are consistent with the results of primate‐specific phylogenetic analyses (e.g., Springer et al. 2012; Kistler et al. 2015; Beck et al. 2023).

The analysis of lemurs focused on Lemuriformes instead of all lemurs because molecular phylogenetic evidence indicates that the aye‐aye diverged from other lemurs 17–20 million years before the origin of crown Lemuriformes (Springer et al. 2012; Kistler et al. 2015; Álvarez‐Carretero et al. 2022). Thus, nearly all of the diversity found in extant and recently extinct lemurs has accumulated over the last 30 million years. If Holocene lemur diversity is the result of adaptive radiation, then that radiation was triggered in the early Oligocene (Kistler et al. 2015; Godfrey et al. 2020). The focus on Lemuriformes emphasizes that the initial stages of adaptive radiation are spatiotemporally localized. The aye‐aye is too distantly related to the other lemurs for its divergence at circa 47 Ma (Álvarez‐Carretero et al. 2022) to be considered part of the same ecological opportunity that may have driven the Lemuriformes to radiate in the early Oligocene.

Quantifying Disparity and Adjusting for Crown Age

The terms diversity and disparity have distinct meanings in the macroevolutionary literature. Diversity in the strict macroevolutionary sense refers to species richness, whereas disparity refers to phenotypic differences among species. I follow this distinction hereafter. Several measures of morphological disparity are available (Wills 2001). Variance, the one used in this analysis, captures two aspects of dispersion in morphospace: the amount of space occupied by a clade and, to a lesser extent, the density of points (species) within that space (Guillerme et al. 2020; Smith et al. 2021). Variance is less sensitive than range to outliers and sample size, and range contains no information about the distribution of species between the extremes (Wills 2001). Because disparity was computed as the variance of the sample of species contained within each clade, the extinct species present in the PHYLACINE database could be included in the analysis without precise knowledge of their phylogenetic position as long as they could be confidently assigned to one of the crown clades created by slicing the tree (Table S2). In the case of the Eocene–Oligocene time slice, 207 of the recently extinct species could be placed in a crown clade with reasonable certainty. For the mid‐Oligocene time slice, that number was reduced to 171.

Slicing the tree at 33.9 Ma and 27.82 Ma created many clades with crown ages that are comparable to those for Lemuriformes and Platyrrhini, respectively, but also many clades that are much younger. As noted above, younger clades are expected to have less disparity, on average, than older clades because they have had less time to explore morphospace. Instead of excluding young clades from the analysis, I adjusted the variances for all clades by crown age using residuals from a phylogenetic generalized least squares (PGLS) regression. The PGLS analysis was conducted in the nlme package for R (Pinheiro et al. 2023) following the procedures described by Revell and Harmon (2022). Clade variances were logged for the PGLS analysis, and I accounted for heteroscedasticity using the varExp function in nlme to model residual variance as an exponential function of crown age.

Although older clades will tend to have more variance than younger clades, disparity does not necessarily accumulate linearly over time. Disparity can fluctuate, and a clade will eventually begin losing disparity when it enters the decline phase of its history. The oldest clades examined in this study are likely to be a mix of clades that radiated to varying degrees, those that failed to radiate, and those that are in decline. On the other hand, the youngest clades have not had enough time to generate the same amount of variation in evolutionary history and are, perhaps, more likely to be in an expanding phase or near their peaks. Variance is, therefore, a function of accumulation and persistence, but persistence may be more important in older clades, especially if clades tend to reach peak disparity early in their histories (Hughes et al. 2013). Accumulation and persistence cannot be teased apart in this study, but one way to get at this issue indirectly is to examine the cohorts of clades that originated close in time to Lemuriformes and Platyrrhini, such that all of the clades in the cohort have been exploring morphospace and diversifying for a similar amount of time. I defined these cohorts as all clades with a crown age within 3.5 million years (older and younger) of the crown ages of Lemuriformes (30.35 Ma) and Platyrrhini (24.67 Ma). This threshold for inclusion is arbitrary, but it ensures that all of the oldest clades created by each time slice are part of the lemuriform and platyrrhine cohorts.

I evaluated Lemuriformes and Platyrrhini using the distributions of age‐adjusted variance for the entire sample and for their age cohorts. If disparity is exceptional in lemuriforms and platyrrhines, then they should fall outside the main part of the distributions formed by other mammals, perhaps as part of a small subset of other clades with unusually high disparity. I used the tools available in nlme to detect potential outliers. I also tested the distributions for multimodality using the excess‐mass test (Ameijeiras‐Alonso et al. 2019) and for skewness using D'Agostino's skewness test (D'Agostino 1970). If adaptive radiation generates exceptional disparity, then bimodality or positive skew is expected if there are several adaptive radiations in the sample (Figure 1). The excess‐mass test was conducted in the multimode package for R (Ameijeiras‐Alonso et al. 2021), with 10,000 bootstrap iterations used to determine the significance of deviations from unimodality. D'Agostino's skewness test was conducted in the moments package for R (Komsta and Novomestky 2022).

Some possible distributions of size disparity in mammals. (A) A symmetric unimodal distribution: Adaptive radiation has not generated exceptional clades. (B) A positively skewed distribution: Adaptive radiation has generated exceptional clades, but the distribution is continuous, without a clear break between clades that radiated and those that did not. (C) A bimodal distribution: Adaptive radiation has generated a subset of exceptional clades that are distinct from other clades.

A Comparison of Species‐ and Genus‐Level Estimates of Clade Disparity

Sample variance does not account for the phylogenetic structure within each clade. Some clades may contain one or two genera that are particularly speciose but with little morphological differentiation. The effect of such phylogenetic pseudoreplicates (sensu Maddison and FitzJohn 2015) on estimates of disparity depends on their position relative to the clade's mean phenotype (Foote 1993a). If a lineage that is very close to its clade's mean begins to speciate rapidly but with only minimal phenotypic divergence, the effect of the newly evolved species will be to lower the clade's disparity because all of the new species will have short distances from the clade's mean. If, on the other hand, the rapidly speciating lineage is at the periphery of the clade's morphospace, then disparity will increase because the lineage will be producing species that are far from the clade's mean.

To evaluate whether phylogenetic pseudoreplication is an issue for estimates of disparity in primates, I examined how disparity changes when variance is computed using genus means instead of species means in select primate clades. I used all of the genera recognized by Estrada et al. (2017) for extant primates as a framework for this analysis, and I included recently extinct genera where appropriate. To facilitate comparisons among this small sample of clades, I used the bootstrap to generate 95% confidence intervals for each estimate of variance (Foote 1993a). I resampled each primate clade with replacement 10,000 times at a sample size equal to that of the original sample and used the percentile method to generate confidence intervals. The bootstrap analysis was conducted using the simpleboot package for R (Peng 2019).

Results

Eocene–Oligocene Time Slice

The median age of the 125 clades created by the Eocene–Oligocene time slice is 27.1 million years. The distribution of crown ages is negatively skewed and extends well into the Pleistocene, with one clade (two species of the pangolin genus Manis) being less than a million years old (Figure 2). A total of 64 clades, or 51% of the comparative sample, originated within 3.5 million years of Lemuriformes. As predicted, there is a significant positive relationship between crown age and disparity among the clades, with log(variance) increasing by 0.0379 units per million years. There is no evidence of clades with unusually high variance in the bivariate scatter. Two chiropteran genera—Melonycteris and Myzopoda—appear to be outliers with unusually low variance, but each genus contains only two species.

Three chiropteran clades with very low variances are labeled: Melonycteris (Mel), Myzopoda (Myz), and Harpyionycteris–Boneia (Harpy).

The distribution of age‐adjusted variances for the entire sample confirms the impression from the bivariate plot: the highest values are not clearly separated from the main part of the distribution, indicating that none of the high‐variance clades can be confidently described as exceptional (Figure 3, all clades in the Eocene–Oligocene time slice). The excess‐mass test indicates that unimodality cannot be rejected for the distribution (p = 0.7832), and the distribution is negatively skewed (p < 0.0001), not positively skewed as predicted. Lemuriforms have one of the highest age‐adjusted variances in the sample, ranking seventh out of all 125 clades (95th percentile). Platyrrhines also have relatively high variance (83rd percentile). Other major primate clades have variances that are more typical of mammals: lorisiforms are above the median but unremarkable (65th percentile) and catarrhines are close to the median (48th percentile).

The distribution for the cohort of clades that originated within 3.5 million years of the lemuriform crown age is similar to the distribution for the full sample (Figure 3, lemuriform cohort). Unimodality cannot be rejected (p = 0.8434), and the distribution is not significantly skewed (p = 0.6079). Lemuriforms rank second out of the 64 clades (98th percentile) in this subset of the data, exceeded only by the Folivora (sloths). Mid‐Oligocene Time Slice

The general pattern of results for the mid‐Oligocene time slice is similar to the one for the Eocene–Oligocene time slice. The relationship between variance and crown age is significantly positive, with the PGLS slope being similar in magnitude to the one for the Eocene–Oligocene sample (Figure 2). None of the clades stand out in the bivariate scatter as potential high‐variance outliers, but Melonycteris and Myzopoda once again have unusually low variance, along with a third chiropteran lineage, the clade formed by the genera Harpyionycteris and Boneia (three extant species but represented in the sample by only two).

The median age for the 190 clades in this sample is 22.1 million years, with 101 clades (53% of the comparative sample) originating within 3.5 million years of the Platyrrhini. The mid‐Oligocene time slice separates Catarrhini, Lorisiformes, and Lemuriformes into major subclades. In the case of Lemuriformes, three subclades are part of the sample: Cheirogaleidae–Lepilemuridae (25.64 Ma), Lemuridae–Megaladapidae (25.03 Ma), and Indriidae–Palaeopropithecidae (22.60 Ma). Note that Hadropithecus or Archaeolemur are not included in this time slice because they are not present in the molecular tree, and they fall outside the clade containing Indriidae and Palaeopropithecidae (Kistler et al. 2015).

Unimodality cannot be rejected for the mid‐Oligocene distribution of age‐adjusted variances (p = 0.9873), and the distribution is negatively skewed (p < 0.0001) (Figure 3). Platyrrhines have high age‐adjusted variance, ranking 30th out of the 190 clades in the full mid‐Oligocene sample, and are in nearly the same percentile (84th) as in the Eocene–Oligocene sample. The Hominoidea and the three lemuriform subclades all rank higher: Hominoidea are in the 85th percentile, Cheirogaleidae–Lepilemuridae are in the 87th, Lemuridae–Megaladapidae are in the 88th, and Indriidae–Palaeopropithecidae, the most disparate primate clade in the mid‐Oligocene sample, are in the 93rd. Limiting the comparative sample for the mid‐Oligocene time slice to the cohort of clades that originated within 3.5 million years of the Platyrrhini has little effect on the distribution (excess‐mass test: p = 0.8134, indicating unimodality; skewness test: p < 0.0001, indicating negative skew). The rankings of the primate clades increase slightly in this more restricted sample: platyrrhines are in the 86th percentile, Indriidae–Palaeopropithecidae are in 94th percentile, and the clades in between move up 2–3 percentiles (Table 1).

Species‐ and Genus‐Level Disparity in Primates

Using genus means instead of species means to compute variance has only a small effect on the overall pattern of differences among primate clades (Figure 4; note that the variances are not age‐adjusted). A paired t‐test for the nine primate clades shown in Figure 4 is not significant (p = 0.4570 using a permutation test with 10,000 iterations in the R package jmuOutlier; Garren 2019). The rank‐order correlation between species‐ and genus‐based variances is r s = 0.87 (p = 0.0023; one‐tailed permutation test with 10,000 iterations in jmuOutlier). Regardless of how variance is computed, lemuriforms stand out in comparison to other primates, as do cercopithecoids, but at the lower end of the distribution. The rest of the clades are distributed more or less continuously with some differences in ordering. The clades that show the biggest difference between species‐ and genus‐based variances are the Cheirogaleidae–Lepilemuridae and the Lorisiformes. Using genus means also increases uncertainty in the estimates of variance for all clades, as expected based on the decrease in sample size. Given the uncertainty in the variance estimates, many of the differences among platyrrhines, hominoids, and the three lemuriform subclades noted in the analysis of the mid‐Oligocene time slice are unlikely to be very meaningful.

A comparison between variance in log(body mass) computed using species means and genus means for several primate clades. Vertical bars are the 95% bootstrap confidence intervals. The variances are not adjusted for crown age.

Discussion

Size Disparity in the Lemuriformes and Platyrrhini

Although Lemuriformes and Platyrrhini are not exceptional among mammals, or part of a subset of clades that are exceptional, they are characterized by high size disparity. Lemuriformes are particularly impressive, ranking seventh in the complete set of clades from the Eocene–Oligocene time slice and second in their age cohort. Moreover, lemuriform subclades also exhibit high disparity, especially the clade containing the extant indriids and their subfossil sister clade, the Palaeopropithecidae. Platyrrhines, on the other hand, have high size disparity but are less impressive, falling well within the mammalian distribution and ranking no higher than the 86th percentile across the various subsets of the data. Among primates, platyrrhines are rivaled or exceeded by the lemuriform subclades and the Hominoidea.

In evaluating disparity in primate clades, it is worth emphasizing that no single measure of disparity captures all aspects of how species within a clade occupy morphospace (Wills 2001; Guillerme et al. 2020; Smith et al. 2021). Variance is useful because it summarizes information about the size and density of a clade's morphospace, but different patterns of dispersion can produce similar estimates of variance, potentially obscuring important differences among clades (Wills 2001; Smith et al. 2021). This issue is most salient in the platyrrhine cohort of clades, where 5 of the 14 clades ranked ahead of platyrrhines have low extant diversity and are represented by fewer than 10 species in the samples used for the analysis (Table 1 and Figure 5). Two of the top three clades in the platyrrhine cohort are depauperate: the physeteroid cetaceans with three extant species (sperm whale, pygmy sperm whale, and dwarf sperm whale) and the thryonomyoid rodents (cane rats and the dassie rat), also with three extant species but only two in the sample used for this analysis. The only way for such clades to achieve high variance is to occupy morphospace at very low density, whereas clades with high diversity and high disparity, like platyrrhines, are characterized by higher densities and more continuous variation.

Jitter plots showing the distribution of individual species for the clades with the highest age‐adjusted variance in the lemuriform and platyrrhine cohorts (Table 1). Clades are arranged from left to right in descending order of age‐adjusted log(variance).

From the perspective of using high disparity as a tool for identifying adaptive radiation, high variance in a depauperate clade would seem to be a false signal for the sort of morphological variety evolutionary biologists are typically looking for. The fact that such clades are among those with the highest age‐adjusted variance in the sample created by the mid‐Oligocene time slice raises questions about how they affect assessments of clades with greater diversity. Excluding clades with sample sizes less than n = 10 from the platyrrhine cohort changes the rank of Platyrrhini from 15th out of 101 clades (86th percentile) to 10th out of 55 clades (83rd percentile). Thus, inclusion of the high‐variance depauperate clades does not seem to have much of an impact on how variance in Platyrrhini is perceived.

Depauperate clades with high variance are interesting because some of them may be clades in decline that have lost diversity more rapidly than disparity. This possibility raises two issues that are difficult to address from the perspective of extant and recently extinct species: When did a clade reach maximum disparity and how was it achieved? It is unlikely that all of the clades created by slicing the mammalian tree at the Eocene–Oligocene and mid‐Oligocene boundaries were at maximum disparity and diversity in the late Pleistocene and early Holocene. As a clade's diversity waxes and wanes, disparity increases, fluctuates, and then decreases, but not necessarily in sync with diversity (Foote 1993b; Hughes et al. 2013). The effects of extinction on disparity are complicated and depend on how much diversity is lost, whether extinction is random or selective, and how disparity is quantified (Foote 1993b; Korn et al. 2013; Puttick et al. 2020). In the case of random extinction, variance may decrease, be unaffected, or even increase under some conditions (Puttick et al. 2020). Selective extinction, where extinction is trait‐dependent, decreases variance when it targets one or more of the margins of a clade's morphospace, as in the megafaunal extinctions of the late Pleistocene and Holocene that affected lemurs and several other clades. Selective extinction can also increase variance when it targets species in the middle of a clade's distribution (Foote 1993b; Puttick et al. 2020). Thus, although extinction is expected to eventually reduce disparity when a clade is in decline, the clade may maintain a high level of disparity for some period of time. The unlinking of disparity and diversity creates interpretive challenges that are most obvious when considering depauperate clades with high variance, but these challenges apply to clades with greater diversity as well. A notable example from primates is the Hominoidea, which has been in decline since the late Miocene (Fleagle 2013; Urciuoli and Alba 2023) but is characterized by high size disparity in comparison to other mammals (Table 1), perhaps because of bimodality resulting from the extinction of species that were intermediate between Hominidae and Hylobatidae (the two clusters evident in Figure 5; other examples include the Folivora and Phalangeroidea).

Ultimately, the ideal framework for evaluating disparity in Lemuriformes and Platyrrhini would include densely sampled and precisely dated fossil records for a large sample of mammalian clades. Such a sample would provide information on when clades reached maximum variance in body size and how it was achieved in each case (i.e., by expansion of morphospace through phenotypic evolution or by extinction of species in the intermediate size range). This approach is unlikely to ever be applied to lemurs, which lack a fossil record on Madagascar older than the late Pleistocene. Platyrrhines, on the other hand, have a fossil record extending back to the Oligocene. The platyrrhine record is not densely sampled but may be sufficient for a paleontologically focused study. One analysis of size evolution in Platyrrhini that included fossil data suggests that platyrrhines achieved maximum size disparity in the Pleistocene (Silvestro et al. 2019), indicating that a fossil‐based analysis that includes other mammalian clades is unlikely to alter the conclusion that size disparity in platyrrhines is high but not high enough to place the clade among the top 5% or 10% of the mammalian distribution, given the amount of time that has elapsed since the clade's origin.

Does Adaptive Radiation Generate Exceptional Disparity?

The design of this study is based on the framework developed by Miles et al. (2023) to examine whether classic avian adaptive radiations are characterized by exceptional disparity in body size and shape. As a starting point for their analysis, Miles et al. reasoned that the usual (i.e., background) processes of clade disparification might be expected to generate a normal or lognormal distribution of clade variances. Departures from normality in the form of positive skew or bimodality driven by a small subset of clades with unusually high variance might then be interpreted as a potential signature of adaptive radiation. Although the avian distributions deviated from lognormality, they were close enough for Miles et al. (2023) to conclude that there was little evidence that classic examples of avian adaptive radiation are the outcome of a process that is qualitatively distinct from background disparification. Some avian adaptive radiations were located in the extreme right tail of the size or shape distributions (above the 95th percentile: birds‐of‐paradise, Hawaiian honeycreepers, vangas of Madagascar), whereas others, most notably Darwin's finches, were located above the median but below the 90th percentile.

The results of the present study are consistent with those of Miles et al. (2023) in showing that adaptive radiation has failed to generate a subset of mammalian clades with unusually high size disparity. The distributions shown in Figure 3 indicate that variation in size disparity is continuous without a long right tail or a second mode to the right of the main part of the distribution. The Folivora exhibit some separation from the main part of the distribution when comparison is limited to clades that originated within 3.5 million years of Lemuriformes (Figure 3, lemuriform cohort; Table 1), but they are not distinctive when all clades in the Eocene–Oligocene time slice are considered, and they are not distinct enough in the lemuriform cohort to suggest the presence of a second mode or positive skew.

One possibility worth considering is that adaptive radiation generates exceptional disparity but that the effect is usually transient. Adaptive radiation is thought to trigger an acceleration in phenotypic evolution, followed by a slowdown as niches fill (i.e., the early‐burst pattern; Simpson 1953; Schluter 2000; Glor 2010; Harmon et al. 2010). Given a long enough timeframe, clades that are accumulating disparity at a more gradual pace may catch up to those that radiated, obscuring the signal of adaptive radiation in age‐adjusted disparities. The key period of time to capture the signal under this scenario would be early in an adaptive radiation's history. To test this possibility, I sliced the mammalian tree at the beginning of the late Miocene (Tortonian age, 11.63 Ma) and the beginning of the Pliocene (5.333 Ma) to create samples of very young clades, and I analyzed these samples using the same procedures that were applied to the Eocene–Oligocene and mid‐Oligocene time slices. If adaptive radiation generates exceptional disparity but only for a limited period of time, then the effect is most likely to be detected over these short timeframes, before other clades have had sufficient time to catch up (assuming that adaptive radiation has occurred in at least some mammalian clades that have originated since the late Miocene or Pliocene). The distributions of age‐adjusted log(variance) for these younger time slices support the conclusions based on the results for the older time slices (Figure 6): unimodality cannot be rejected for either time slice (late Miocene: p = 0.9519; Pliocene: p = 0.5460) and neither distribution is positively skewed (both are negatively skewed; p < 0.0001 for each time slice).

Distribution of age‐adjusted log(variance) for the clades created by slicing the tree at the beginning of the late Miocene (11.63 Ma) and beginning of the Pliocene (5.333 Ma). A clade from the Pliocene time slice with a value of −4.66 is not shown.

A major limitation of this study is that it examined only one aspect of the mammalian phenotype. Miles et al. (2023), in addition to analyzing body size, also examined size‐adjusted features (i.e., shape) of the beak, wing, lower limb, and tail and found that a multivariate description of these traits provided the same signal as body size. Whether the pattern of size disparity found in mammals applies to other traits is an open question. Body size is an ecologically important variable but is probably only a crude indicator of adaptive divergence in many clades. Traits related to trophic, sensory, and locomotor systems can exhibit contrasting macroevolutionary signals (e.g., Slater and Friscia 2019; Slater 2022), so it is possible that analysis of a different system, or all of the systems combined, might reveal distributions of disparity with clear outlier clades or groups of outlier clades. The present study focused on body size for three reasons. First, recent tests for signals of adaptive radiation in lemurs and platyrrhines used body size to demonstrate early bursts in phenotypic evolution in these two clades (Aristide et al. 2015; Herrera 2017). Second, size is correlated with diet, locomotion, and other aspects of ecology in primates (Kay and Simons 1980; Fleagle 1985; Ford and Davis 1992). Third, size data are available for a large number of mammalian species, providing a robust comparative framework for evaluating size disparity in the two primate clades. However, there is no necessary link between size evolution and adaptive radiation. A definitive answer to the question of whether adaptive radiation has generated mammalian clades with exceptional phenotypic disparity will require the analysis of other types of traits.

Searching for Discontinuity: Disparity, Early Bursts, and Ecological Opportunity

The distributions of disparity found here and by Miles et al. (2023) align with the argument raised by Olson and Arroyo‐Santos (2009) that the sorts of properties that evolutionary biologists use to identify adaptive radiations—ecomorphological disparity, rates of phenotypic evolution, and rates of speciation—are likely to be continuously distributed across clades, making thresholds for recognizing adaptive radiation arbitrary and context‐dependent. Olson and Arroyo‐Santos concluded that such arbitrariness undermines adaptive radiation as a description of a real thing in nature, as opposed to a human‐defined segment of a continuum. On the basis of that conclusion, they argued that the concept should be replaced with concepts of disparification and diversification that provide a more accurate framework for capturing the quantitative reality. The two putative primate adaptive radiations illustrate the problem from the perspective of size disparity. The lemuriforms' position as one of the most size‐disparate mammalian clades to have originated since the end of the Eocene is consistent with previous arguments in favor of recognizing them as an adaptive radiation based on other lines of evidence, but the platyrrhines have a more ambiguous magnitude of disparity: it is high, but there are no principled criteria for determining whether it is high enough. In other words, size disparity does not provide a rigorous test of the hypothesis of adaptive radiation.

A larger question is what the continuous distribution of size disparity means for the distinctiveness of adaptive radiation relative to other processes of disparification. A useful framework for thinking about this issue was introduced by Wang (2003) in his discussion of whether mass extinction is qualitatively different from background extinction or simply the extreme of a continuum of extinction intensities. Wang proposed three types of continuity: continuity of cause, continuity of effect, and continuity of magnitude. He argued that mass extinction can be considered a distinct phenomenon if at least one type of continuity between mass extinction and background extinction can be rejected. The present study and the one conducted by Miles et al. (2023) tested for continuity of magnitude in disparity between adaptive radiation and background disparification and failed to reject it. To the extent that these results can be generalized to other traits and other clades, a major question for the conceptual viability of adaptive radiation is whether there is discontinuity of cause or effect.

With regard to effect, many recent studies of adaptive radiation have focused on detecting early bursts of phenotypic evolution, where the rate of evolution in a clade is initially high and then decelerates as the radiation unfolds (Harmon et al. 2010; Slater et al. 2010; Slater and Friscia 2019; Grossnickle et al. 2024). This approach is explicitly grounded in the Simpsonian model of a lineage encountering ecological opportunity, rapidly exploiting it, and then reaching an equilibrium as the clade approaches an ecological limit (Simpson 1944, 1953). The qualitative distinctiveness of the early‐burst pattern relative to other models of evolution makes it an unambiguous way of sorting adaptive radiations from other clades. Under this criterion, the case for recognizing lemuriforms and platyrrhines is compelling because both exhibit early bursts in size evolution (Aristide et al. 2015; Herrera 2017). However, there are important questions about the connection between early bursts and adaptive radiation. First, there is disagreement over whether the early‐burst pattern is necessary for recognizing a clade as an adaptive radiation (Losos and Mahler 2010; Slater and Friscia 2019; Gillespie et al. 2020; Moen et al. 2021). For example, the history of disparification in many clades, especially older ones, is likely to be more complex than a simple decelerating pattern of trait evolution, whereas younger clades may not exhibit decelerations because their phases of rapid evolution extend to the present (Erwin 2015; Quintero 2025). Second, it is worth asking whether the early‐burst pattern is synonymous with adaptive radiation. Tests for early bursts are typically applied to clades that have impressed us with their ecological and morphological variety, but the extent to which early bursts have occurred in clades that are less impressive is unknown. Should clades with low or moderate disparity be recognized as adaptive radiations if they exhibit the early‐burst pattern in at least one ecomorphological trait? The answer is unclear because high disparity has guided the selection of clades to test for adaptive radiation. Finally, the most common way of detecting early bursts is to test for decelerating trait evolution in the focal clade (Harmon et al. 2010), but whether such decelerations are preceded by accelerations is not usually examined, and decelerations may occur for reasons other than ecological opportunity (Moen et al. 2021).

With regard to cause, ecological opportunity—thought to be the driver of adaptive radiation—is not an all‐or‐nothing phenomenon (Yoder et al. 2010; Stroud and Losos 2016; Gillespie et al. 2020). For example, if we accept that Lemuriformes and Platyrrhini are adaptive radiations, then the difference between them in age‐adjusted disparity suggests a difference in ecological opportunity. In fact, ecological opportunity probably plays a role in the macroevolutionary dynamics of most clades, but there may be discontinuities in its distribution, with some opportunities being exceptional (e.g., the aftermath of mass extinction or dispersal to a competitor‐free land mass). If magnitudes of disparity do not allow us to confidently identify clades that require special explanation, then the importance of adaptive radiation within the apparatus of evolutionary theory will depend on understanding ecological opportunity, the organismal factors that facilitate exploitation of opportunity, and the evolutionary processes that drive adaptive divergence when opportunity is encountered (Schluter 2000; Gillespie et al. 2020). These issues are challenging to address even under the best circumstances but will be especially difficult for clades, such as Lemuriformes and Platyrrhini, in which the key events occurred tens of millions of years ago.

Conclusions

This study evaluated disparity in body size in the putative primate adaptive radiations Lemuriformes and Platyrrhini relative to other mammals. Both of these primate clades have high size disparity, and Lemuriformes are one of the most size‐disparate lineages to have originated since the beginning of the Oligocene. Although high disparity is consistent with the hypothesis of adaptive radiation, distributions of disparity for mammalian clades are continuous, without obvious gaps that might be used to detect clades that are the outcome of a macroevolutionary process that is qualitatively distinct from the processes that affect all clades. If adaptive radiation does not generate exceptional size disparity, then magnitudes of size disparity do not provide a rigorous test for adaptive radiation. The continuity of magnitudes of size disparity also aligns with the idea that the clades identified as adaptive radiations are an arbitrary segment of a continuum. Whether adaptive divergence in Lemuriformes, Platyrrhini, and other proposed mammalian adaptive radiations can be considered qualitatively distinct from adaptive divergence in other clades will depend on a better understanding of ecological opportunity and its effects on the macroevolutionary dynamics of trait evolution.
