Maternal Choline Supplementation in a Mouse Model of Down Syndrome and Alzheimer’s Disease Generates Unique Expression Profile Mosaics Within Three Hippocampal Excitatory Neuronal Populations
Authors: Melissa J Alldred, Kyrillos W Ibrahim, Harshitha Pidikiti, Sang Han Lee, Adriana Heguy, Gabriela Chiosis, Elliott J Mufson, Grace E Stutzmann, Stephen D Ginsberg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIndividuals with Down syndrome (DS) are at risk for early‐onset Alzheimer's disease (AD), marked by neurodegeneration in hippocampal and basal forebrain circuits. Early‐life interventions offer therapeutic potential, including maternal choline supplementation (MCS). MCS improves cognitive outcomes and neuroplasticity in rodent models of neurodevelopmental and neurodegenerative disorders, yet cell‐type specific molecular effects remain unknown. We investigated the effect of MCS upon the onset of septohippocampal degeneration at 6 months of age in the Ts65Dn mouse model of DS/AD. Using laser capture microdissection and single population RNA‐sequencing, transcriptomic changes were profiled within hippocampal CA1 and CA3 pyramidal neurons and dentate gyrus granule cells comparing trisomic and disomic offspring. Bioinformatic analysis revealed MCS‐mediated downregulation of apoptotic pathways and upregulation of cognition‐related functions across all populations, alongside cell‐specific responses. These findings highlight MCS as a promising strategy for modulating disease‐relevant pathways in a hippocampal cell‐type–specific manner during early neurodegeneration in DS/AD.Keywords: Alzheimer's disease, bioinformatics, Down syndrome, laser capture microdissection, maternal choline supplementation, RNA‐sequencing, Ts65Dn mouse

Introduction

Down syndrome (DS), caused by triplication of human chromosome 21 (HSA21), is the most common genetic disorder of intellectual disability [1, 2, 3], and a risk for early‐onset Alzheimer's disease (AD) [4, 5]. Due to the increased life expectancy in individuals with DS, the prevalence of age‐related cognitive decline and AD pathology has become a prescient concern. AD pathology presents in the DS brain decades earlier than in the onset of sporadic AD, with amyloid‐beta peptide (Aβ) containing senile plaques and tau‐positive neurofibrillary tangles (NFTs) appearing in early middle age, combined with age‐associated cortical thinning and brain atrophy [6, 7, 8, 9]. Furthermore, degeneration of the cholinergic basal forebrain neurons located within the medial septum that innervate the hippocampus [10, 11, 12] occurs prior to the onset of Aβ plaques and tau‐positive NFTs in individuals with DS who later in life develop AD hallmark pathology and dementia [13]. The early loss of cholinergic innervation of the septohippocampal connectome underscores an urgent need for early interventions targeting this highly vulnerable neural circuit. Several studies suggest that increased dietary choline during the perinatal period improves cognition and has neuroprotective benefits in preclinical models of aging and neurodevelopmental disorders including DS [14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19, 20].

Choline is an essential nutrient with limited endogenous production [21, 22, 23]. Choline is utilized as a precursor to acetylcholine, a key neurotransmitter required during development for neuronal differentiation, proliferation, plasticity, and synapse formation [24, 25, 26]. Choline is comprised of an ethanolamine molecule with three methyl groups attached to the nitrogen atom, which is utilized as a substrate of the phosphatidylethanolamine N‐methyltransferase (PEMT) pathway for neuronal membrane components phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin [27, 28, 29]. Choline is also the primary dietary methyl donor, playing a role in the epigenetic regulation of gene expression [26, 30, 31]. Choline requirements for pregnant mothers are thought to be significantly underestimated, with normative choline consumption studies in rodents and humans showing low or diminished concentrations of choline metabolites in plasma [23, 32, 33]. Indeed, studies of pregnant women who consumed twice the recommended allowance of choline, which is water soluble and the excess is secreted through urine, continue to exhibit lower urinary levels of choline metabolites compared to non‐pregnant women [34]. Although human and preclinical studies indicate choline requirements during gestation are extremely high, current daily requirements likely remain inadequate for mother and fetus.

Maternal choline supplementation (MCS) is a treatment used to increase dietary choline during the perinatal period (gestation through weaning), with preclinical early choline delivery in relevant animal models intended to mimic choline supplementation clinical trials in pregnant women [28, 34]. MCS has been shown in rodent models under normative and aging conditions to improve multiple memory and biobehavioral assessments [35, 36, 37, 38]. MCS also improves hippocampal responses to cholinergic inputs in adult offspring [39] and provides benefits in terms of learning and memory and related behaviors to offspring in rodent models of developmental and neurological impairments, including DS [17, 19, 40, 41], Rett syndrome [42, 43], and Fragile X [44], along with human MCS clinical trials for fetal alcohol syndrome [45, 46, 47].

The Ts65Dn model of DS and AD was generated in the early 1990s [48, 49] with a freely segregating mini‐chromosome encompassing a partial triplication of HSA21 orthologs on mouse chromosome 16 (Mmu16; ~90 protein‐coding genes), along with triplication of a non‐orthologous centromeric region of mouse chromosome 17 (Mmu17; segment 17q1a) [50, 51, 52]. Ts65Dn mice recapitulate many human DS + AD brain phenotypes, including early endosomal abnormalities [53], age‐associated behavioral deficits in multiple cognitive domains [41, 54, 55, 56], and frank neuronal loss [56, 57, 58], specifically basal forebrain cholinergic neuron (BFCN) degeneration [59, 60, 61]. BFCNs that reside in the medial septum project to the hippocampus, forming a bidirectional septohippocampal circuit [62, 63, 64]. Hippocampal neurons also comprise the trisynaptic circuit, with entorhinal cortex input to dentate gyrus granule cells (DGCs), which project to CA3 pyramidal neurons (PNs), which in turn project to CA1 PNs [65]. The septohippocampal and trisynaptic circuits degenerate along with profuse amyloid and tau pathology in sporadic AD and aged DS individuals [66, 67, 68, 69]. In the Ts65Dn model, BFCN degeneration includes decreased cholinergic tone, aberrant activity found in cholinergic projection neurons and age‐related loss of cholinergic neurons [14, 19, 70, 71]. Further, the hippocampal trisynaptic circuit displays synaptic structural and functional alterations including excitatory and inhibitory current alterations and defects in long‐term potentiation (LTP) and long‐term depression [72, 73, 74, 75, 76, 77, 78, 79, 80, 81, 82, 83]. Importantly, MCS has shown lifelong mechanistic alterations in choline metabolite distribution and consumption in the Ts65Dn mouse model, likely caused by epigenetic reprogramming [29]. Epigenetic reprogramming is a key requirement for use as a treatment for a developmental disability such as DS.

Previous studies demonstrated that MCS improves behavioral outcomes in Ts65Dn mice [14, 17, 18, 19, 28]. MCS improves responses to hippocampal‐dependent spatial learning paradigms including novel object recognition and attentional behaviors as assessed in the 5 choice serial reaction time task in Ts65Dn mice up to ~16 months of age (MO) [14, 17, 18, 19, 28]. MCS also positively alters BFCN gene expression [84, 85], as well as hippocampal CA1 PN gene expression [86, 87, 88], likely via epigenetic mechanisms [29]. However, how MCS impacts excitatory hippocampal neurons within the entire trisynaptic circuit, a region integral to learning and memory and vulnerable in both DS and AD remains a major knowledge gap. Here, we examine the effects of MCS on CA1 and CA3 PNs and DGCs at 6 MO in Ts65Dn mice, which corresponds to the onset of BFCN degeneration. We used single population RNA‐sequencing (RNA‐seq), to define changes in hippocampal subtype cell‐type–specific expression and identify convergent and divergent pathways influenced by MCS in this trisomic mouse model. We report MCS results in neuron specific gene expression alterations in all three hippocampal neuron populations examined. In addition, several genes, pathways, and processes were identified that are convergently rescued by MCS within hippocampal excitatory neurons, including apoptosis, transcription of DNA, and LTP. Pathways and processes uniquely altered via MCS within CA1 PNs, CA3 PNs, and DGCs were also identified. We suggest that targeting genes underlying these neuron specific alterations are approaches for therapeutic intervention for learning and memory disabilities in DS and may inform AD therapeutics during the onset of degeneration.

Methods

Mouse Cohorts and MCS Delivery

Animal protocols were approved by the Nathan Kline Institute/New York University Grossman School of Medicine (NYUGSOM) IACUC in accordance with NIH guidelines. We utilized this mouse cohort for BFCN analysis via single population RNA‐seq [84, 85], as well as genotype alterations in trisynaptic circuit neurons [89], allowing for direct analysis and comparison of MCS within the septohippocampal and trisynaptic circuits. Briefly, Ts65Dn breeder pairs were assigned to receive one of two choline‐controlled experimental diets: (i) control rodent diet containing 1.1 g/kg choline chloride (AIN‐76A; Dyets Inc., Bethlehem, PA), or (ii) choline‐supplemented diet containing 5.0 g/kg choline chloride (AIN‐76A; Dyets Inc.). Choline levels were chosen based upon previous rodent studies employing MCS in Ts65Dn mice [17, 28, 90]. Based on a dam's respective diet, E0‐P21 pups were either choline normal (2N, disomic control; n = 6 and Ts, trisomic; n = 6) or choline supplemented (2N+, n = 7 and Ts+, n = 9). At weaning (P21) all offspring were placed on control diet, had ad libitum access to water and were aged to ~6 MO [84, 85] (Figure 1A).

Tissue Preparation

Brain tissues were accessed after transcardial perfusion with ice‐cold 0.15 M phosphate buffer as previously described [84, 85, 89]. Rostral hippocampal sections were cut at 20 μm (−25°C) in the coronal plane on a cryostat (CM1860UV, Leica, Buffalo Grove, IL) and mounted on polyethylene naphthalate (PEN) membrane slides (Leica) [89]. Slides were kept under desiccant at −80°C. RNase‐free precautions were employed, and solutions were made with 18.2 M Ohm RNase‐free water (Nanopure Diamond, Barnstead, Dubuque, IA).

Nissl Staining and Neuron Collection

PEN membrane slides containing the rostral hippocampus were equilibrated to room temperature (RT) under desiccant (−20°C for 5 min, 4°C for 10 min, RT for 5 min) followed by a rapid Nissl staining protocol to preserve intact RNA in unfixed tissue [91]. Three hippocampal excitatory neuron populations (CA1 PNs, CA3 PNs, and DGCs) were isolated in parallel from the same section (Figure 1A) from ~6 hippocampal sections per brain via laser capture microdissection (LCM) [89] using the LMD7000 system (Fluotar 20×/0.40; Leica Microsystems, Waltham, MA).

RNA Purification

RNA from CA1 PNs, CA3 PNs, and DGCs were purified using the miRNeasy Micro kit (Qiagen, Germantown, MD) according to manufacturers' specifications. A DNase digestion was performed twice sequentially before the final washes and RNA purification. RNA quality control (QC) was performed via bioanalysis (Tapestation, Agilent Technologies, Santa Clara, CA) using the high sensitivity RNA screen tape (Agilent). Although RNA Integrity Number (RIN) was used when available for individual samples, RIN values were not generated for samples that did not meet criteria (< ~100 pg/μL) [89]. In these cases, DV200 values were utilized to quantify sample quality before library preparation [91]. No samples were excluded for quality control at this stage.

Library Preparation and RNA‐Seq

The SMARTer Stranded Total RNA‐Seq kit‐Pico input Mammalian v3 (Takara Bio, Mountain View, CA) was employed with unique indexes for each sample, with minor modifications to normalize the RNA sample input as previously described [89]. Samples were quantified using the D1000 DNA assay (Tapestation, Agilent). One sample failed QC, so LCM was re‐performed for all three neuron populations, which were processed and passed QC. Samples were pooled in equimolar concentrations and assayed on the NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina, San Diego, CA) using an S1 100 cycle flow cell (v.1.5) by the New York University Grossman School of Medicine Genome Technology Center (NYUGSOM GTC). Although unsupplemented (e.g., normal choline diet) trisomic (Ts) and disomic (2N) RNA‐seq profiles were previously generated [89], all samples were prepared and run in the same batch. Herein, we use previously published Ts and 2N profiles [89] to compare expression profiles with offspring from dams exposed to MCS for trisomic (Ts+) and disomic (2N+) mice.

RNA‐Seq Processing

FastQ files were utilized for all 4 conditions (Ts+, Ts, 2N+ and 2N) in all three cell types (CA1 PNs, CA3 PNs, and DGCs) to analyze data in parallel. FastQ files were generated and QC of the raw reads was performed by FastQC v.0.11.9 [92]. Samples were removed from analysis if sequence counts were low (< 100 000 bases). One CA1 PN and two CA3 PN samples were removed (< 6500 aligned reads) at this step. Read trimming was performed as necessary by Trimmomatic 0.39 [93]. If QC passed and showed no adapter contamination, this step was skipped. Sequence reads were indexed and aligned to the reference genome (Gencode GRCm39‐mm39) using STAR Aligner (2.7.1a) [94]. Quantification was performed on alignments using Picard (2.26.2) (2019 [95]) for different measures and RSEM (1.3.1) for output [96]. QC was performed on alignments using RSeQC (v.5.0.2) [97]. Differential gene expression was performed in R (version4.4.0/RStudio v.1 + 494) using gene results obtained from RSEM (Figure 1C and Figure S1).

Statistical Analysis

Gene Count matrix obtained from RSEM was analyzed. Genes with over 0.1 counts per million for at least 10 samples were retained and TMM normalization was implemented by edgeR [98] for downstream analysis. This step removes lowly expressed genes as they provide little evidence of differential expression and increase statistical errors and false discovery rate [98, 99, 100]. Analyses were performed using the DREAM pipeline [101] built using the limma‐voom framework from the VariancePartition (v.1.34.0) package. DREAM computes moderated t‐statistics and corresponding p‐values improved by empirical Bayes shrinkage [102]. In addition to Group and RNA concentration, the following variables were included as covariates: Intergenic percentage, Intronic percentage, mRNA base percentage, Usable base percentage, and Correct strand reads percentage. The covariates were computed from RNA‐seq reads by Picard, except for Group and RNA concentration. Multidimensional Scaling (MDS) was used to visualize the distribution of points and if necessary, identify the presence of outliers. TopTable (edgeR; v.4.2.1) extracts genes that are present for all comparisons. Gene expression differences at (p < 0.05) were considered statistically significant. Protein coding genes were extracted using the R Bioconductor package AnnotationDbi [103]. Multiple testing correction was performed using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure [104], and false discovery rate (FDR)–adjusted p‐values were calculated for all genes. To ensure microisolated cells were excitatory CA1 PNs, CA3 PNs, and DGCs, statistical analysis was performed in R using the lmer package to normalized cell counts whereupon excitatory neuronal markers were compared to all other cell specific markers [105] modeled as a function of group or maternal diet (Figure S1A–C) as previously described [106, 107]. Significance was judged at the level α = 0.05, two‐sided. Graphpad prism (v.10.6.1) was used to examine gene expression levels of all triplicated genes for each cell type individually by genotype (Ts vs. 2N) and disease diet (Ts+ vs. Ts) using a one sample Wilcoxon t‐test with the hypothetical mean at zero for log‐fold change (LFC). Triplicated expression was considered significant at the level α = 0.05.

Pathway Analyses

Pathway analyses consisted of Ingenuity Pathway Analysis (IPA; Qiagen) [108, 109] and Gene Ontology (GO) [110, 111]. Genes were filtered using a nominal p‐value threshold (p < 0.05) for both packages. IPA comparisons were performed comparing differentially expressed genes (DEGs; p < 0.05) to the IPA reference dataset (Qiagen). Shiny package (v.1.8.1.1) was utilized to create a web‐based app to run GO analysis using R version 4.4.0/RStudio v.1 + 494. This app was also used to filter keyword targets to identify classes of processes affected by genotype and region [85, 89, 106, 107]. “Rescue” of MCS was defined if the following criteria were met: (i) significant dysregulation was seen in Ts versus 2N, (ii) significant alteration in expression was seen in Ts+ versus Ts, and (iii) activation scores showed increased activity in Ts versus 2N and decreased activity in Ts+ versus Ts (or vice versa). GO analysis could not replicate activation and were therefore classified as “MCS responsive” by criteria (i) significant dysregulation was seen in Ts, and (ii) classification of each GO process was performed using keyword targets in 15 categories, with unidentified GO processes binned to “other/non‐neuronal” and not examined further. The hippocampal cell types showed process dependent effects, with the processes depicted as a percentage of the total processes in the demarcated 15 categories. GO gene network plots were produced in R (v.4.4.1) using the clusterprofiler (v.4.14.6) package. Venn diagrams were generated using theInteractiVenn program [112].

Results

MCS Modulation of Hippocampal Excitatory Neuronal Gene Expression

Using LCM, single population RNA‐seq was performed on three hippocampal neuron populations from disomic and trisomic offspring exposed to MCS (5.0 g/kg; 2N+ and Ts+) or a control choline diet (1.1 g/kg; 2N and Ts) and aged to ~6 MO. CA1 and CA3 PNs along with DGCs were microisolated from each respective cohort for RNA‐seq analysis followed by bioinformatic inquiry to determine the effect of MCS on these hippocampal neurons at the start of BFCN degeneration (Figure 1A). MDS plots show each mouse has a unique gene expression profile for CA1 PNs, CA3 PNs, and DGCs (Figure 1B). Both 2N and Ts offspring exhibit DEGs due to MCS in all three neuron populations (Figure 1C). The trisomic comparison (Ts+ vs. Ts; termed disease diet; all dietary conditions as defined in our previous publication [85]) DEGs for CA1 PNs, CA3 PNs and DGCs are listed in Table S1 (worksheets A–C). MCS in CA1 PNs generated approximately equal upregulated and downregulated DEGs for the normal disomic diet condition (2N+ vs. 2N) but more upregulated than downregulated DEGs in disease diet (Ts+ vs. Ts). MCS caused greater upregulation of DEGs than downregulation in CA3 PNs in the disease diet comparison as well, while the reverse was found in the disomic diet comparison. MCS in DGCs resulted in slightly more downregulated DEGs than upregulated DEGs for both the disease diet and disomic diet comparisons. The number of DEGs are shown in Table 1 with DEGs binned by LFC for both disomic diet and disease diet (Figure S2).

Impact of MCS on Triplicated Gene Expression Within Hippocampal Neurons

Genotype dependent gene expression revealed overall significant upregulation of expressed triplicated genes in trisomic mice compared to 2N mice for all three cell types (Figure 3A), consistent with our previous findings [89]. However, MCS displayed little effect on trisomic gene expression levels evidenced by disease diet LFC mean expression in all three neuron populations (Figure 3A). There were a few triplicated DEGs rescued by MCS in CA1 PNs, CA3 PNs, and DGCs (Figure 3B and Figure S3). Within CA1 PNs, 2 of 27 triplicated DEGs orthologous to HSA21 were rescued by MCS, including solute carrier family 5 (inositol transporters), member 3 (Slc5a3) and ADAM metallopeptidase with thrombospondin type 1 motif 1 (Adamts1, Figure 3B, left). CA3 PNs displayed 2 of 51 DEGs rescued by MCS, downstream neighbor of SON (Donson), and carbonyl reductase 1 (Cbr1, Figure 3B, middle). DGCs exhibited slightly more triplicated DEGs rescued by MCS (4 of 35) including Adamts1, Son DNA binding protein (SON), Purkinje cell protein 4 (Pcp4), and oligodendrocyte transcription factor 1 (Olig1, Figure 3B, right). Additionally, 6 triplicated DEGs on the non‐homologous DS segment Mmu17q1A were differentially regulated by genotype and/or disease diet, although none of these DEGs were rescued by MCS (Figure S3). Together, this suggests, at onset of dysfunction, MCS does not reduce triplicated gene expression upregulation in these 3 hippocampal populations. Therefore, we hypothesize that MCS effects are mainly driven by unique alterations within CA1 PNs, CA3 PNs, and DGCs within this trisomic mouse model.

Pathway Analysis of Hippocampal Neurons Identifies Molecular Mechanisms in Response to MCS in DS

IPA and GO analysis were performed on the disease diet cohort of DEGs within CA1 and CA3 PNs and DGCs. In IPA, due to the addition of z‐score which imparts a regulatory direction of activity, resultant pathways were compared to genotype (Ts vs. 2N) [89] to determine rescue effects of MCS on canonical pathways as well as Disease and Function (D/F) analysis. One canonical pathway was common to all cell types, “Mitotic Roles of Polo‐Like Kinases”. However, in the context of these single population RNA‐seq studies, the minimum data requirement for IPA to assign a z‐score for this pathway was not sufficient to determine rescue. Canonical pathways exhibiting significant alterations in both genotype and disease diet comparisons were overwhelmingly neuron population specific (Table S2). In CA1 PNs, few pathways were rescued by MCS, including Mitotic prometaphase, Trans‐Golgi network vesicle budding, and Translocation of SLC2A4 (GLUT4) to the plasma membrane (Figure 4A and Table S2). In contrast, CA3 PNs had more rescued pathways, including upregulation of the Glutaminergic receptor signaling pathway, multiple Salvage pathways, along with concomitant downregulation in Senescence, Signaling by EGFR, Glycerophospholipid biosynthesis, and Mitochondrial dysfunction (Figure 4B and Table S2). DGCs also displayed multiple rescued pathways including downregulation of the CLEAR signaling pathway, MicroRNA biogenesis signaling pathway, and mTOR signaling along with upregulation in Synaptic long‐term depression (Figure 4C and Table S2). In both CA3 PNs (Figure 4B) and DGCs (Figure 4C), select disease diet MCS responsive pathways were not rescued, rather they showed additive effects (e.g., Opioid signaling in CA3 PNs and Calcium signaling in DGCs), which may indicate compensatory mechanism activation, although more information is required to identify in specific pathway regulators that might drive these effects.

D/F analysis was performed in IPA to examine biofunctions modulated by MCS. Unique and overlapping pathways within excitatory hippocampal neuron populations were rescued by MCS (Table S3). Unlike canonical pathways, many D/Fs showed disease diet rescue of genotype dysfunction in all three hippocampal excitatory neuron populations (Figure 5A and Table S3), including downregulation of Apoptosis, and Movement Disorders, along with upregulation of Cognition and Microtubule Dynamics. D/Fs also exhibit unique cell‐type dependent modulation of dysregulation. Within CA1 PNs, unique rescued pathways included Anxiety, Cell survival, and Repair of DNA (Figure 5B and Table S3). Within CA3 PNs, MCS uniquely impacted dysregulation in several behaviors, including contextual and cued conditioning, emotional and hyperactive behavior, among other D/Fs (Figure 5C and Table S3). Within DGCs, MCS rescued pathways include Excitation of neurons, Transcription, Synthesis of lipid, and Ubiquitination of protein, among others (Figure 5D and Table S3). Several hippocampal cell‐type specific MCS responsive pathways showed dysregulation by genotype but were not rescued by MCS, indicating MCS responsiveness is highly cell‐type specific (Figure S4). MCS benefits all three hippocampal neuronal populations, but mechanisms are mainly cell‐type specific, indicating degeneration status of individual cell types may underlie the effects of MCS at onset of disease pathology.

GO Analysis of MCS Responsiveness in DS Hippocampal Neurons

To examine biological processes, molecular functions, and cellular components that are MCS responsive (as GO analysis does not impart a regulatory score), significant disease diet GO processes were compared across hippocampal neuron populations (Table S4) and compared to significant genotype GO processes (Table S5). Most disease diet comparison processes were cell‐type specific, with unique dysregulation in 59.63% of CA1 PN processes, 60.82% of CA3 PN processes, and 64.54% of DGC processes (Figure 6A), with convergent or partially overlapping processes accounting for the minority of disease diet processes. CA1 PNs showed the highest relative percentage for “Metabolism”, “RNA” and “Signaling” processes affected by disease diet, whereas CA3 PNs showed higher relative percentages associated with “Activity”, “Neurotransmitters, Ions and Receptors (NIR)” and “Synaptic” categories that were disease diet dependent, and DGCs DEGs altered the most processes in “Actin”, “Development” and “Protein” categories (Figure 6B). To determine whether GO, like IPA, shows MCS‐driven rescue of genotype effects, GO pathways were compared to the genotype GO processes. Contrary to IPA, GO analysis does not provide activation status. Therefore, if processes overlapped, this was considered MCS responsive, with each category scored as a percentage of the total processes in each category as genotype processes [85]. CA1 PNs exhibited the highest percentage of MCS responsiveness in behavioral categories (Figure 6C), while CA3 PNs had higher percentages of MCS responsiveness in both behavioral and synaptic process categories (Figure 6D). In contrast, DGC MCS responsiveness was highest in Endo/Exocytosis and ROS signaling (Figure 6E and Table S5). To examine MCS responsive DEGs, violin plots and gene network analysis were performed for select processes. We examined two MCS responsive behavioral processes in all three cell types. Although the GO process of Cognition (Figure 6F) and Learning or Memory (Figure S5A) exhibited key alterations in DEGs, few DEGs overlapped between cell types. These included Neural precursor cell expressed, developmentally downregulated gene 9 (Nedd9) and family with sequence similarity 107, member A (Fam107a aka Drr1/Tu3a) overlapping in CA1 PNs and DGCs. Other DEGs were specifically reversed in terms of directionality in a neuron population, including nerve growth factor (Ngf) and RIC8 guanine nucleotide exchange factor A (Ric8a) in CA3 PNs and neurotrophic tyrosine kinase, receptor, type 2 (Ntrk2 aka TrkB), which showed additive effects in DGCs (Figure S5A).

Gene network analysis for driver hubs included metabolic processes within CA1 PNs (Figure S5B), synaptic processes within CA3 PNs (Figure S5C), and actin processes within DGCs (Figure S5D). Moreover, NIR processes in CA3 PNs elucidated several target DEGs, including Fam107a, modulated in disease diet for these key processes, suggesting potential drivers of MCS modulation in the context of these processes. Although IPA and GO analyses delineate potential MCS driven beneficial mechanisms of behavior, specific gene expression alterations are hippocampal cell‐type specific.

Discussion

The impact of MCS was assessed within three distinct excitatory hippocampal neuron populations via single population RNA‐seq and downstream bioinformatic inquiry. Paralleling genotype dependent alterations, MCS has effects on unique DEGs within each hippocampal excitatory neuron population. Results indicate disease diet DEGs had an MCS effect independent of the alterations seen in the genotype comparison (e.g., unique disease diet genes) in CA1 PNs, CA3 PNs, and DGCs as previously observed within BFCNs [85]. Importantly, several key functions found to be dysregulated by genotype are either fully and/or partially rescued by MCS and displayed unique responsive DEGs, suggesting early choline delivery is a beneficial therapeutic for ameliorating dysregulation observed in DS/AD mice. Notably, single population RNA‐seq revealed HSA21 orthologs triplicated in DS/AD mice were significantly upregulated within septohippocampal neuronal cell types while overall disease diet triplicated DEGs were not significantly changed (Figure 3A; [89]). Genotype alterations parallelled findings in postmortem human brain [107]. Few triplicated HSA21 orthologs were MCS responsive within the hippocampus (Figure 3B), with changes due to disease diet limited to 3 HSA21 orthologs in CA1 PNs, 2 HSA21 orthologs in CA3 PNs, and 4 HSA21 orthologs in DGCs. None of the rescued triplicated genes overlap between cell types, indicating the effects of MCS are highly cell‐type specific. The overall muted response to MCS suggests the DS genetic signature cannot be wholly overcome by MCS. Commensurate with our findings within BFCNs and independent observations, triplicated HSA21 orthologs represent a small subset of the DEGs in the trisomic mouse models [85, 89, 106, 114] and postmortem human DS neurons [115, 116], indicating therapeutic strategies may be beneficial without solely targeting triplicated transcripts.

Dysregulation was most pronounced within CA3 PNs [89]. Likewise, CA3 PNs were considered the most MCS responsive cell type interrogated in this study followed by CA1 PNs and DGCs, based on the number of DEGs attenuated in the disease diet comparison, combined with the number of IPA canonical pathways rescued by MCS in Ts65Dn mice, along with the relative percentages of overlapping GO processes. Previous studies demonstrate MCS has beneficial effects during the onset of neurodegeneration in DS mouse models, with effects declining in older mice [15, 19, 37, 41, 113, 117]. In normative rodent models, MCS shows beneficial effects in the context of aging, effectively extending the cognitive healthspan [16, 118], indicating divergence of the impact of MCS based upon normative versus neurodevelopmental and/or neurodegenerative conditions [18]. Similarly, MCS does not appear to have profound effects prior to, or in the absence of, neurodegenerative pathology [70, 113, 119]. These behavioral findings complement the current transcriptomic profiling, suggesting MCS benefits are mild in DGCs, the cell type whereby the fewest genotype dependent DEGs are found and the fewest MCS responsive DEGs are also observed (Figure 2C). By contrast, CA3 PNs display the most genotype dependent DEGs and MCS responsive genes (Figure 2B). CA3 PNs also displayed the highest MCS related rescue of uniquely dysregulated canonical pathways (Figure 4B) and behavioral D/Fs (Figure 5C). Benefits of MCS in all three hippocampal cells types are linked to memory and attentional tasks, indicating a correlation of genetic alterations in expression and behavioral assessments suggesting MCS offspring outperform those raised on a normal choline diet [18, 19, 41, 113].

In all three hippocampal neuronal populations, MCS responsive behavioral mechanisms were identified by IPA and GO pathway analyses, although behavioral assessments were not conducted on these cohorts. These included attenuation of the genotype dependent upregulation of “Cognitive Impairment”, genotype dependent downregulation of “Learning”, “Memory”, and “Cognition” (Figure 5A). While GO analysis indicated CA1 PNs displayed the highest percentage of behavioral processes affected by MCS (Figure 6C), CA3 PNs had more total behavioral processes affected, with a similarly high number of synaptic processes that were MCS responsive in GO (Figure 6D). In the GO pathway Cognition, MCS attenuated downregulation of Fam107a in trisomic CA1 PNs and DGCs (Figure 6F), commensurate with observations of a genotype dependent Fam107a hippocampal downregulation in the 5XFAD mouse model of AD at 7 MO [120]. Overexpression of Fam107a is associated with reduced spine density on apical dendrites in mature neurons, resulting in reduced LTP magnitude and decreased field excitatory postsynaptic potential amplitudes [121]. In humans with DS + AD, FAM107A gene expression in cortical layer III and layer V PNs is upregulated compared to age matched controls [107], similar to findings in postmortem cortical tissue from individuals with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder [122]. This suggests gene expression modulation either spatially or during the neurodegenerative time course. Alternatively, currently employed mouse models may not replicate human disease at this level of resolution. Further studies examining aging in trisomic models are needed to elucidate this conundrum, although BFCNs evaluated in the same mice as analyzed herein do not show significant dysregulation or MCS recovery of Fam107a expression [85], suggesting spatial distribution may be critical for expression level regulation.

In GO pathways of Cognition (GO: 0050890) and Learning or Memory (GO: 0007611) Nedd9 showed MCS responsiveness within CA1 PNs and compensatory upregulation in DGCs. Nedd9 is an adhesion protein member of the Crk‐associated substrate family, linked to various biological processes involved in neurite outgrowth, apoptosis, and multiple cancers [123]. Unlike Fam107a, dysregulation of Nedd9 mimics results from postmortem human DS frontal cortical laminae III and V PNs [107]. MCS benefits on Nedd9 expression, linked to the GO processes of Cognition and Learning or Memory (Figure 6A and Figure S5A), and in IPA D/F pathways are associated with rescue of Apoptosis and Microtubule dynamics (Figure 5A), among others. Neuroprotective benefits of the single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) rs760678 of NEDD9 have been reported in late‐onset AD and Parkinson's disease, with an inverse correlation to ApoE allele status [123, 124, 125]. However, these studies require additional replication with well‐powered cohorts.

The cognate BDNF receptor TrkB (Ntrk2) was upregulated in disease diet CA1 PNs and DGCs. Ntrk2 is associated with neuroprotection, with decreased levels reported in DS and AD during disease progression [126, 127, 128]. Ntrk2 expression is MCS responsive within CA1 PNs and DGCs (Table S1) and is associated with GO processes of Cognition and Learning or Memory (Figure 6F and Figure S5A), along with upregulation of IPA D/Fs Cognition, Learning, Memory and Long‐Term Potentiation, among others (Figure 5A). The present single population RNA‐seq findings support previous microarray analysis of CA1 PNs demonstrating Ntrk2 is MCS responsive in trisomic mice [129, 130]. In DGCs, Ntrk2 expression is upregulated by genotype and is also MCS responsive. NTRK2 is also downregulated within postmortem lamina V PNs in human DS/AD [107, 115], providing translational relevance for MCS trials in DS and across the AD spectrum. Interestingly, “Cognition” also has unique MCS responsive genes in CA3 PNs, including Ngf. Decreased Ngf has long been associated with DS and AD pathobiology [131, 132, 133]. During MCI, NGF levels are conversely preserved or increased [134, 135], indicating that there may be compensatory mechanisms driving NGF expression. However, rescue by MCS is a novel finding for DS. This suggests MCS is driving compensatory increased Ngf in CA3 PNs during the onset of degeneration. These select MCS responsive DS and AD relevant genes provide a molecular and cellular framework for preservation of learning and memory in trisomic mice that are cell‐type specific but appear to benefit the septohippocampal connectome. Therefore, key DEGs provide target mechanisms and drivers of cellular functions critical for this increased behavioral healthspan that could be utilized to develop targeted therapies in at‐risk persons. This is essential for individuals with DS, either prior or proximate to the onset of neurodegeneration associated with cognitive decline, most notable memory and executive function.

A limitation to this study is the lack of a female cohort. However, we have collected hippocampal tissues from ~6 MO female trisomic and 2N mice in the context of MCS to examine sex differences, with an emphasis on estrogen and estrus cycle differences. Another caveat is that the Ts65Dn mouse partially recapitulates human DS HSA21 orthologs and contains a non‐orthologous Mmu17q1A segment, which also exhibits altered select gene expression levels within CA1 and CA3 PNs and DGCs [89]. Moreover, Ts65Dn mice lack amyloid plaques and NFTs. However, the Ts65Dn mouse model recapitulates septohippocampal pathology, including BFCN degeneration, an enlarged endosomal phenotype, cognitive decline and synaptic pathology [58, 72, 136, 137]. In the future, we may consider using other mouse models of DS including TcMac21 mice, which have an entire triplication of human HSA21 [138], or the Ts66YAH mouse model, which has a CRISPR insertion of a copy of HSA21, creating a transgenic model that contains both the mouse chromosome and the triplicated human chromosome [139]. To date, neither of these trisomic models have demonstrated septohippocampal defects mimicking the human DS condition, which is fully replicated in the Ts65Dn mouse used in the present study. No studies have shown long‐term negative sequelae associated with MCS. Overdose of choline, a water‐soluble nutrient, is unlikely. However, it is possible that MCS has side effects which have been undetected. Choline and/or choline metabolite levels (e.g., betaine; [23, 33]), in biofluids (e.g., urine and/or plasma) could be measured in future studies within trisomic models to monitor potential externalities during the perinatal period and beyond.

In conclusion, the present findings suggest MCS exerts effects through both shared and cell‐type specific mechanisms across the hippocampal trisynaptic circuit in the Ts65Dn murine model of DS/AD. Notably, CA3 PNs showed the most robust transcriptional rescue, aligning with greater vulnerability in DS/AD and highlighting this population as particularly responsive to early‐life intervention prior to or at the onset of BFCN degeneration. Across all hippocampal neuron populations, MCS consistently attenuated pathways related to apoptosis and enhanced those linked to cognition and synaptic plasticity, supporting prior behavioral and BFCN transcriptional findings. The identification of convergent targets in hippocampal neurons, such as Ntrk2 and Fam107a, as well as specific targets including Ngf in CA3 PNs, genes known to have roles in neurotrophic signaling and synaptic structure, provides a molecular framework for understanding how early dietary interventions modulate disease progression. Aggregating our findings, we posit that targeting BFCN degeneration prior to onset of genotoxicity in hippocampus may provide a more useful framework for intervention and increase normative mechanisms within hippocampal neuron populations in DS. We postulate MCS would slow septohippocampal neurodegeneration, resulting in a longer healthspan, rather than benefitting a longer lifespan. Overall, current findings suggest that timely, cell‐specific modulation of neurodegenerative trajectories is possible, and nominate a set of candidate genes and pathways for future translational validation in human DS/AD cohorts.
