A direct and relatively simple method to cut down computational runtime in forecast software by up to 86 %
Authors: Bafnoti G. Gabra, Bichoy G. Gabra
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Software runtime poses significant challenges across multiple industries. In particular forecast applications such HVAC, cooling, and heating are time consuming due to their intricate input requirements and the need for repeated simulations to arrive at viable solutions. To address this, we present an a method implemented in the form of a Python software suit. This suite streamlines iterations and minimizes the mathematical operations necessary to generate cumulative output profiles for software solutions related to the aforementioned applications. Our method which deals directly and efficiently modifies achieves an impressive reduction in simulation runtime, up to 86 %.Here we demonstrate:•Software runtime in the simulation/CAD industry is an ongoing challenge applying simple problem-specific (in our case study it’s HVAC energy consumption) algorithm show promising results.•We used an average “representative day” of a short reference year on a reduced input file, when the deviation was considerable, the PATE method was triggered to adjust the error between the real software generated results and the results generated from the natural full simulation.•The method was applied to several buildings on different locations in several continents.

Background

Despite the increasing computational capacities [1], software runtime remains a persistent challenge across various industries [2]. In the realm of building HVAC simulations, the computational burden is particularly pronounced due to the intricate input requirements and the multitude of variables involved. Additionally, the complexity of building geometry further exacerbates these computational demands. Previous research [3] proposed the use of a reduced sequence of days known as the Short Reference Year (SRY), where only a subset of days (often 12 days) is employed instead of the full 365 days for simulations. However, the same researchers acknowledged that their approach based on clustering algorithms had several limitation when used to reduce the required computational time.

In this paper, we present the PATÉ (Partially Adjusted Thermal Enhancement)/SRY hybrid method. This novel approach simplifies the underlying algorithm and directly addresses the computation process in heating and cooling applications. By doing so, we aim to significantly enhance computational efficiency and streamline the simulation workflow.

SRY optimization - introducing the PATÉ method

There’s always a need for getting better results, that’s why although the result of the average SRY method without any further optimizations were relatively satisfying, The following SRY optimization method were created.

The SRY method has always provided a low RSQ error (often less than 5 %) but in very few cases has produced significant errors exceeding even 20 %, that means that, at least, the SRY method has introduced a good “Skeleton” to work on.

PATÉ stands for Partially Adjusted Temperature Enhancement

The PATÉ Algorithm is only triggered if the relative cumulative error is higher than 5 %.

We found that it’s enough to remedy the high-error affected segments in the results of the shortened sequence by “extending” or “shirking” their respective weather file parts, basically we’re creating a counter-effect action in the input of the Affected segments (Fig. 7) which are those who have a significantly differently slope than their counterparts produced by the real data.Fig. 7Cooling error - affected segment.Fig 7

Cooling error - affected segment.

For example, as in the figure below, we can see that the fake curve is falling off on cooling, in that case the respective weather file temperature will be extended up and up only creating a heating effect which in turn will increase the respective cooling power, to respond to this significant error and thus decrease the difference in slope between the real and software produced slope and error.

Basically if we found that the cooling power resulting from the shortened sequence is less than the cooling power produced by the real data, we take a counter action which is raising the temperature of the corresponding temperature segments in the input file. And similarly if the error was found in the heating signal, we decrease the temperature of the respective parts in the temperature file.

HOW IS THE PATÉ MADE? One simple linear equation is created

Ta=A*Tb+B

(Where Ta is the temperature after adjustment and Tb is the temperature before adjustment)

The equation is solved twice to find the 2 unknowns A and B, once on the maximum temperature value (Tbmaz) of the affected segment and another on the lower temperature value(Tbmin).

Once the A and B coefficient found, the equation is applied to the whole points of the affected segment creating anew wholly Adjusted and Enhanced Temperature-segment (Fig. 9). Just like any optimization algorithm, more than one iteration will often be necessary.

How are the (TA)S found?

TaTb=G, Where G is initially guessed by being the ratio between the real and adjusted temperature corresponding to the maximum error in the affected segment as in the Fig. 8 belowFig. 8. How to find the G factor.Fig 8Fig. 9Extending and shrinking affected SRY Segments by PATE.Fig 9

. How to find the G factor.

Extending and shrinking affected SRY Segments by PATE.

Next iterations of G will be calculated as the desired error multiplied by the previously used value of G divided by the obtained error.Gn=Gn−1*desirederrorerrorn−1

For example a first iteration with a G of 6 yielded to an error of 15 % while the desired error is 5 %. The new G will thus equals to 6 × 5/15=2.

Shrinking (decreasing) the temperature is used when the heating power is found to be not enough and extending (increasing) will be used when the cooling power is found to be not sufficient.

A simple PATÉ algorithm

We found that optimizing the cooling and heating independently is better and simpler.

First the error is scanned then the PATE method is employed proportionally so for example if we get negative heating error it means that more heating is required and thus lowering the corresponding temperature signal and vice versa, a negative cooling error means that more cooling is required, thus increasing the temperature of the corresponding temperature signal and vice versa, optimization is complete when the desired error rate is reached or when simply the algorithm is taking too much time. The optimization algorithm is rather simple compared to other optimization algorithm found in [5,6] and [7].

Method validation

The mean-based SRY method and the PATE Optimized SRY method have successfully addressed the runtime challenges specific to heating and cooling applications in most cases. The SRY Calculator software generates an SRY sequence in a mere 3 s for a single field, resulting in a total processing time of 15 s (considering 3 fields to be optimized, 5 s each). The cooling error was found to be 5.5 % (with data magnification taking approximately 0.01 s per field).

While previous researches use well known algorithms such Evolutionary Algorithms [6] and specifically genetic algorithms [5] or the Stochastic Gradient Estimation [7]. the combined SRY/PATE algorithm was simple to code and implement and its simplicity arises from the fact that it’s based on creating a set of representative days each would consists of being the average of its corresponding set of days, then taking a counter action in the input file based on the error location in the output file we may briefly summarize previous work as follows:

Uhlig et al. (2013) [5] address the problem of manufacturing system design (material flow simulation). To reduce runtime, they relied on a Genetic Algorithm, applying fast approximations in early generations and more accurate models only to the best candidate solutions. As reported, “The experiment consumed 3296 CPU hours … on average one simulation with profile fast needed 14.7 min, with profile medium 50.3 min and with profile accurate 6.7 h. So a repetition without simulation profiles would need approximately 38,000 CPU hours”, which means a time reduction of (38,000–3296)/(38,000)=91 %. However, the approach remains highly problem-specific, since defining profiles requires domain expertise.

In contrast, Fu (2008) [7] situates his work in the broader field of stochastic simulation optimization, surveying algorithms such as stochastic gradients, nested partitioning, and, in particular, Optimal Computing Budget Allocation (OCBA). The paper highlights a speed up factor of 19.8 for a number of alternatives of 100.

Hachicha and Ammeri (2010) [6] take a different perspective, presenting a comprehensive classification of simulation–optimization methods across domains, including Discrete input parameter methods, Continuous input parameter methods and Global search methods. The aim was to minimize the resources spent while maximizing the information obtained in a simulation experiment. Some limitations were that each survey concentrates on only few classification criteria and that simulation output is subject to random errors and requires proper statistical analysis, unlike Uhlig (2013) and Fu (2008), this contribution does not provide direct runtime benchmarks, but instead highlights methodological directions and their expected impact.

For broader applicability, the PATE Optimized SRY method achieves remarkable results, reducing errors to just 6 % within three iterations. Leveraging real weather file data, the SRY method significantly shortens calculation times. Moreover, by directly addressing heating and cooling issues through corrective actions, its impact is immediate, also when optimized data from a building were used on another building, moderate errors range was obtained, while the method wasn’t tested to cover all sort of changes the obtained results does seem promising.

Lastly, the simplicity of the equations employed in the PATE method, involving minimal variables, contributed to its effectiveness.

Limitations

While the method was used on buildings from 3 continents (North America, Europe and Africa) Maybe further applications may show limitations. The last few years (2023-now) has shown an explosion in discussions/applications related to the use of AI a lot of algorithms were applied to forecast software, such as the use of genetic algorithms, neutral networks etc.… But as we’ve proven above this isn’t always necessary since IF the phenomena to be forecast has a well-known behavior this behavior should be addressed directly (or fed into the algorithm’s code) instead of wandering aimlessly through more complicated evolutionary and machine learning algorithms, in other words “there's no such thing as a free lunch” [8], This concept has been proven in [9]. So the method may not be suitable for phenomena with unpredictable/unknown behavior.
