Exploring LTB-mediated T cell differentiation as a prognostic marker in triple-negative breast cancer
Authors: Xuekai Wang, Yingxiao Ni, Yang Shi, Xiaofeng Yu, Chenchen Wu, Zhuo Xiang, Wei Lv, Qiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractImmune infiltration in triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is associated with patient prognosis. However, the precise phenotypic classifications of immune infiltration in TNBC remain unclear, and the key regulatory mechanisms underlying this process require further investigation. In this study, we employed transcriptomic and single-cell data analysis, utilizing unsupervised clustering to classify the immune microenvironment of TNBC. We constructed a prognostic model based on immune phenotypes and further validated the regulatory role of key genes in immune cell modulation. Through unsupervised clustering analysis of the GEO and METABRIC databases, we identified two distinct immune infiltration phenotypes in TNBC tumor tissues. Notably, patients with high immune infiltration exhibited significantly prolonged overall survival (OS). Subsequent analysis of tumor tissues from high/low immune infiltration groups revealed five differentially expressed genes (DEGs), which were significantly correlated with patient prognosis: lymphotoxin β (LTB), interferon regulatory factor 8 (IRF8), indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase 1 (IDO1), integral membrane protein 2A (ITM2A), and lymphocyte cytosolic protein 1 (LCP1). A prognostic model for TNBC was developed based on the five key genes. Furthermore, we demonstrated a significant association between LTB expression and enhanced immune effects in TNBC tissues. Single-cell sequencing data revealed that LTB expression was predominantly localized to immune-infiltrating T cells, which tended to differentiate toward cytotoxic T cells. We also confirmed that LTB expression improved T cell proliferation and enhanced the tumoricidal effect by modulating T cell differentiation pathways. In conclusion, immune infiltration phenotype is a crucial prognostic biomarker for TNBC patients. Introduction

TNBC is the most prognostically unfavorable subtype of breast cancer [1]. Due to the absence of specific molecular therapeutic targets, chemotherapy remains the primary treatment modality for TNBC [2, 3]. In recent years, immunotherapy has made significant strides in TNBC treatment. Compared to other breast cancer subtypes, TNBC typically exhibits higher levels of PD-L1 expression [4] as well as elevated tumor mutational burden [5, 6]. Immunotherapy exerts its effects by targeting infiltrating immune cells, and TNBC is often associated with a higher presence of tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) [7]. However, the efficacy of immunotherapy shows considerable inter-individual variability. Thus, understanding the types of immune infiltration in TNBC is crucial for optimizing treatment strategies and predicting patient prognosis with immunotherapy.

Previous studies have explored the morphological classification of immune cell infiltration in TNBC [8, 9], revealing that these immune infiltrates are closely linked to patient outcomes. High levels of lymphocytic infiltration are generally associated with a favorable prognosis in early-stage TNBC patients [10]. However, certain TNBC subtypes exhibit elevated levels of immunosuppressive cells, which is correlated with poor prognosis [11]. Furthermore, the immunological characteristics of TNBC tumors are closely intertwined with their metabolic phenotypes, which, in turn, influence the functionality and infiltration of immune cells [12]. These findings underscore the critical importance of immune infiltration subtypes in the development of personalized therapeutic strategies for TNBC. Therefore, this study aims to analyze the differences in the immune phenotypes of TNBC, construct a prognostic estimation model based on immune characteristics, and investigate key molecules involved in the regulation of TNBC immune infiltration.

Materials and methods

Acquisition and preprocessing of the TNBC dataset

Gene chip data from the GEO cohorts were obtained from the Gene Expression Omnibus (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). We selected three TNBC datasets: GSE58812, GSE167213, and GSE76124. These datasets were merged using the R package InSilicoMerging, and batch effects were removed using the method described by Johnson WE et al. Box plots, density plots, and Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) plots were used to compare the data before and after processing (Figure S1), resulting in a batch-effect-corrected matrix containing gene expression data from 429 patients. Gene chip data and patient survival data (including survival time, survival status, and ER, PR, and HER2 assessments) for the METABRIC cohort were downloaded from cBioPortal (http://www.cbioportal.org/). Redundant or duplicate columns in the gene matrix were removed to ensure data uniqueness and consistency. Missing values in the gene expression data were imputed using the K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN) algorithm from the impute R package. Subsequently, based on ER, PR, and HER2 expression status, a totally of 319 TNBC patients with complete survival and gene expression data were selected from a cohort of 2, 509 patients.

Immune subpopulation identification and validation

To evaluate the immune status of each sample, 29 immune-related gene sets were selected from previously published literature (Supplemental Table 1) [13]. We performed unsupervised clustering of TNBC based on ssGSEA scores from 29 immune-related gene sets, and evaluated the optimal number of clusters using consensus clustering. Based on the cleanness of color blocks in the clustering heatmap, the gradual rise and sudden steepness of the CDF curve, and the inflection point of the delta area, we ultimately determined and identified two distinct immune subtypes (Figure S2). ssGSEA scores were calculated using the GSVA package in R. Immune scores, Stromal scores, ESTIMATE scores, and Tumor purity were analyzed using the ESTIMATE algorithm [14].

KEGG enrichment and gene set variation analysis (GSVA)

KEGG enrichment analysis was performed using the R package clusterProfiler (version 3.14.3) to obtain gene set enrichment results. GSVA using the GSVA package was conducted to assess pathway variation between different immune subgroups. The gene sets “c2.cp.kegg.v6.2.symbols” and “h.all.v2023.1.Hs.symbols.gmt” were downloaded from the Molecular Signatures Database (MSigDB) (http://www.gsea-msigdb.org) for GSVA analysis.

Comparison of immune cell infiltration scores

Using the CIBERSORT deconvolution method, we estimated the abundance of 22 immune cell types based on gene expression profiles and selected significant samples (p < 0.05 by CIBERSORT) for further comparison. The Mann–Whitney U test was used to compare the proportion differences of 22 immune cell types between two immune subtypes.

Construction of a prognostic model

Data source and processing

Data source and processing

The data for this study were obtained from the TNBC cohort of the METABRIC database. Using the caret package in R, we randomly split the dataset into a training set and a validation set at a 1:1 ratio, ensuring a balanced distribution of survival statuses between the two sets.

(2)Screening of prognosis-related genes and model construction

Screening of prognosis-related genes and model construction

In the training set, we first performed univariate Cox regression analysis to identify genes significantly associated with OS (p < 0.05). Subsequently, to prevent model overfitting, we applied LASSO-Cox regression analysis to reduce the dimensionality of these genes. Fifteen-fold cross-validation using the glmnet R package was performed to determine the optimal penalty parameter (lambda.min) and identify core prognostic genes: LTB (coefficient − 0.1707), IRF8 (coefficient − 0.0969), IDO1 (coefficient − 0.0182), ITM2A (coefficient − 0.0110), and LCP1 (coefficient − 0.0095).

(3)Evaluation and validation of the prognostic model

Evaluation and validation of the prognostic model

We stratified patients in the training and validation sets into high-risk and low-risk groups based on the optimal risk score cutoff. Kaplan–Meier survival curves and Log-rank tests were used to compare survival differences between the two groups. Furthermore, we plotted time-dependent Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) curves and calculated the area under the curve (AUC) at 1, 2, and 3 years for both the training and validation sets to evaluate the model's predictive performance.

(4)Screening and integration of alternative feature genes

Screening and integration of alternative feature genes

We utilized the random forest algorithm (using the randomForestSRC package) to identify another set of important prognostic genes based on their variable importance (VIP) scores. Finally, we integrated the genes selected by both LASSO and random forest analyses using a Venn diagram to determine the final set of feature genes.

(5)Statistical analysis and visualization

Statistical analysis and visualization

All analyses and visualizations were performed in R. We used forest plots, risk score distribution plots, survival status scatter plots, and heatmaps to intuitively present the results. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests.

Univariate and multivariate cox regression analysis

To identify potential prognostic factors, univariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was performed for each candidate variable, including LTB and relevant clinicopathological features, to independently evaluate their association with OS. For each univariate analysis, cases with missing data for the specific variable were excluded to maintain statistical integrity, and hazard ratios (HRs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and Wald test p-values were calculated. Subsequently, multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analysis was executed using the R package survival. To ensure consistency in sample size during the stepwise selection procedure, samples with missing values were excluded prior to model construction. A bidirectional stepwise regression method based on the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) was then implemented to optimize the prognostic model, integrating both clinical variables (e.g., tumor size and grade) and genetic features. The prognostic Risk Score for each patient was calculated as a linear combination of the expression levels of the selected genes, weighted by their respective multivariate Cox regression coefficients, defined as Risk Score=. Finally, HRs and 95% CIs were calculated to evaluate the independent contribution of each variable to OS.

Prognostic model evaluation and benchmarking

To comprehensively evaluate and compare the predictive performance and clinical utility of the 5-gene prognostic signature (5-GPS) against previously reported models (the 6-gene model and 12-gene model) [15, 16], three independent prognostic frameworks were integrated with patient clinical follow-up data across three statistical dimensions. First, the discrimination capability was assessed by calculating the Concordance Index (C-index) using the R package survival, and pairwise statistical comparisons of C-indices between the 5-GPS and the alternative models were performed using the compareC package to determine statistical significance. Second, time-dependent predictive accuracy was evaluated through time-dependent ROC curves and the corresponding AUCs for 1, 3, and 5-year OS, computed via the timeROC package to assess temporal predictive accuracy. Finally, clinical utility was appraised using Decision Curve Analysis (DCA) conducted via the ggDCA package at a 3-year survival threshold to quantify and compare the net clinical benefit derived from each model.

Survival validation of TNBC patients in TCGA dataset

We downloaded the transcriptome data and clinical information files of all breast cancer patients from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) database. A total of 114 patients with TNBC were screened according to the status of progesterone receptor (PR), estrogen receptor (ER) and human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) in clinical data (Supplemental Table 2). The Kaplan–Meier method was used to plot survival curves via the survival and survminer packages in R. The log-rank test was applied to assess survival differences between groups.

Single-cell data preprocessing

The single-cell RNA sequencing data, including the GEO dataset GSE176078, was generated using the 10 × Genomics platform. The data was processed in R using Seurat (v4.1.0). Low-quality cells were filtered out based on the number of expressed genes, UMI counts, and the percentage of mitochondrial genes. The data were then normalized using the “NormalizeData” function, and the top 2000 highly variable features were identified with FindVariableFeatures. The Canonical Correlation Analysis (CCA) integration method (FindIntegrationAnchors and IntegrateData) was used to correct for batch effects and create a unified expression matrix. Cells were clustered using “FindClusters” and visualized through UMAP for dimensionality reduction.

Cell cluster annotation

Single-cell RNA sequencing data was annotated by cell cluster using SingleR (2.4.1) in R (v4.3.0), identifying cell identities by comparison with reference datasets. The results were integrated into Seurat (v4.1.0) metadata and visualized using DimPlot. Cell classification was manually validated using CellMarker (v2.0) and corrected based on marker genes (Figure S3).

CytoTRACE analysis

Cellular differentiation potential was assessed using CytoTRACE (v1.0) in R (v4.3.0). The normalized expression matrix was extracted from Seurat (v4.1.0) and used as input. CytoTRACE algorithm calculated a differentiation potential score (CytoTRACE score) for each cell based on gene expression complexity, which includes the number of genes expressed and transcriptional diversity. A higher score indicated a more stem-like or undifferentiated state. The analysis generated a differentiation trajectory at the single-cell level, which was subsequently integrated with clustering annotations. Stem-like cell populations were further validated using known stemness marker genes. Finally, the results were presented as trajectory plots and heatmaps, where cells were ordered and colored by their CytoTRACE score or pseudotime. The expression patterns of key genes were also highlighted to reveal the differentiation direction and potential developmental relationships among cell populations.

Monocle 2 pseudotime analysis

Using R (v4.3.0) and Monocle 2 (v2.28.0) for pseudotime analysis, a Monocle object was constructed by extracting the normalized matrix and highly variable genes (2000, selected using FindVariableFeatures) from Seurat (v4.1.0). Dimensionality reduction was performed using reduceDimension (DDRTree, max_components = 2) to construct the pseudotime space. The starting point was defined as the stem cell population (confirmed by marker genes), and consistency was validated using CytoTRACE (1.0). Differential gene analysis was performed using differentialGeneTest (q < 0.01, Benjamini–Hochberg correction). The results were presented as a trajectory plot, with cells colored by pseudotime and significant gene expression highlighted.

GeneSwitches analysis

Using GeneSwitches (v0.1.0) to analyze gene switching events during T cell cytotoxic activation. Input data consisted of a normalized single-cell RNA sequencing expression matrix and a pre-computed pseudotime ordering (generated by Monocle). Logistic regression models were fitted using the “fit_switching_genes” function (family = binomial) to identify genes that switch significantly along the pseudotime trajectory, setting a threshold of q < 0.05 (Benjamini–Hochberg correction). The analysis focused on the activation order of cytotoxicity-related genes, and a trajectory plot was generated using “plot_switching_pathway” to display the cell pseudotime distribution and the switching time points of key genes.

T lymphocyte culture, lentiviral infection, and proliferation assay

Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were incubated in RPMI-1640 medium (C11875500bt, Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS (C04001-500, Vivacell) for 24 h. Subsequently, cells were transferred to 24-well plates coated with anti-CD3 antibody (1 µg /ml, 130-122-282, Miltenyi) and anti-CD28 antibody (0.5 µg /ml, 130-122-350, Miltenyi) for 48 h of activation. The medium was then replaced with complete 1640 medium containing 300 IU/mL IL-2 (200-02-50UG, Peprotech) for expansion culture. LTB-overexpressing lentivirus and Polybrene reagent (40804ES76, Yeasen) were added to the activated T lymphocytes. The plate was centrifuged at 32 °C, 4000 rpm for 1 h, and then incubated in a 5% CO2, 37 °C cell culture incubator for an additional 48 h for subsequent proliferation and cytokine detection.

Western blotting and real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR) analysis

Protein was extracted using RIPA lysis buffer (P0013C, Beyotime) supplemented with protease inhibitors (P1005, Beyotime). Protein concentration was determined using the BCA assay (ZJ101L, Epizyme). Protein molecular weight markers (WJ103L, Epizyme) were included on all blots. Equal amounts of protein (20 μg) were separated by electrophoresis on a 10% polyacrylamide gel (PG112, Epizyme) and transferred to NC membranes (10,600,001, Cytiva). Membranes were blocked with 5% non-fat milk for 1 h. FLAG monoclonal antibody (F1804, Sigma-Aldrich) and β-ACTIN (A5441, Sigma-Aldrich) were incubated overnight at 4 °C. Subsequently, membranes were incubated with secondary antibody for 2 h at room temperature, and immunoreactivity was detected using ECL detection reagent (SQ201L, Epizyme).

Total RNA was extracted using Trizol reagent (9109, Takara), and mRNA was reverse transcribed using a reverse transcription kit (11141ES10, Yeasen). cDNA was analyzed by quantitative PCR in a 20 µl reaction system (11201ES03, Yeasen) containing 10 μM primers. Expression data were analyzed using the delta-delta CT method (2-ΔΔCT) and normalized to the housekeeping gene glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH). The specific primer sequences used for quantification were as follows: LTB-F 5'-GAGGACTGGTAACGGAGACG-3'; LTB-R 5'-AGAAACGCCTGTTCCTTCGT-3'; GAPDH-F 5'-GTCTCCTCTGACTTCAACAGCG-3'; GAPDH-R 5'-ACCACCCTGTTGCTGTAGCCAA-3'.

Flow cytometry

For FACS analysis, T cells were resuspended in PBS containing 2% FBS. Cells were incubated with antibodies against CD3 (317,322, BioLegend), CD4 (300,546, BioLegend), CD8 (344,723, BioLegend), PD-1 (12-9969-42, Invitrogen), LAG-3 (17-2239-42, Invitrogen), and TIM-3 (345,014, BioLegend) cell surface markers at room temperature for 30 min. After washing twice, cells were resuspended in PBS containing 2% FBS and analyzed by flow cytometry. FlowJo software was used to quantify the proportion of positively stained cells or MFI within specific cell populations.

EdU cell proliferation assay

Cell proliferation was assessed using an EdU cell proliferation assay kit on day 7 after lentiviral transduction of T cells. Cells were stained to label newly synthesized DNA according to the manufacturer's instructions (C0071S, Beyotime). Following staining, proliferation was analyzed by flow cytometry.

T cell cytotoxicity assay

T cell isolation, activation, and culture were performed as described above. An appropriate number of T cells and tumor cells (MDA-MB-231) were mixed and co-cultured at specific ratios (1:1, 1:5, 1:10). Separate control groups were included: tumor cells alone, T cells alone, tumor cells with complete lysis, and a background control. Each group had at least three replicates. After 6 h of co-culture, the LDH working solution, prepared according to the manufacturer's instructions (C0018S, Beyotime), was added, mixed well, and incubated at room temperature in the dark for a specific period. Absorbance values were then measured at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Cytokine release assay

Conditioned media from both control and overexpressing T cell groups were collected. Cytokine levels were then measured using a ten-plex cytokine assay kit (Raisecare) according to the manufacturer's instructions, and quantified by flow cytometry.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8.0. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance between two groups was determined using unpaired t-tests or Mann–Whitney tests, as appropriate. Comparisons between groups were performed using one-way or two-way ANOVA, followed by Bonferroni post-hoc tests to determine relevant p-values. p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Results

Unsupervised cluster analysis and identification of immune infiltration phenotypes in TNBC

First, we performed unsupervised cluster analysis on a merged TNBC cohort (n = 429) from the GEO database to identify immune infiltration phenotypes. Based on ssGSEA scores of 29 validated immune-related gene sets, we identified two significantly different immune subgroups: Immunity_Low (n = 357) and Immunity_High (n = 72). Compared to the Immunity_L group, the Immunity_H group exhibited higher levels of enrichment in immune-related gene sets and significantly lower tumor purity scores (Fig. 1A).

Two immune subgroups were identified based on 29 immune-related gene sets. These two subgroups are defined as Immunity_H and Immunity_L; B Comparison of Stromal score, Immune score, and ESTIMATE score between the two immune subtypes (Immunity_H: 72, Immunity_L: 357), *p ≤ 0.05, **p ≤ 0.01, ***p ≤ 0.001, ns, not significant; C PCA analysis of the two immune subgroups in the GEO dataset, with different colored dots representing each subgroup: immunity_H (red) and immunity_L (blue); D PCA analysis of the two immune subgroups in the METABRIC dataset; E Kaplan–Meier survival analysis of OS for the two immune subgroups

Furthermore, we used the ESTIMATE algorithm to evaluate their immune infiltration characteristics. The results showed that the ESTIMATE, ImmuneScore, and StromaScore of the Immunity_H subtype were significantly higher than those of the Immunity_L subtype (Fig. 1B, all p < 0.001). These results suggest that patients in the Immunity_H group have a higher proportion of immune cells in their tumor tissue. We performed three-dimensional principal component analysis (PCA) on the two groups of samples (Fig. 1C), showing the distribution of Immunity_H (red dots) and Immunity_L (blue dots) subtypes based on PC1, PC2, and PC3. Immunity_H subtype samples were more concentrated on the PC1 and PC2 axes, reflecting their higher consistency in gene expression patterns, while Immunity_L subtype samples were more dispersed, suggesting greater heterogeneity.

Similarly, we performed the same analysis on TNBC samples (n = 319) from the METABRIC database, and the results showed similar classification (Fig. 1D), validating the reliability of this classification method. Importantly, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis based on the METABRIC database showed that patients with the Immunity_H subtype had significantly longer survival expectancy than those with the Immunity_L subtype (Fig. 1E , p < 0.0001). In summary, these results confirm that immune infiltration-based subtyping via unsupervised cluster analysis is an important indicator for prognostic analysis in TNBC patients.

Molecular pathway enrichment and immune cell infiltration characteristics analysis of Immunity_H/L subgroups

Next, we analyzed the immune characteristics of the Immunity_H and Immunity_L subgroups. First, we performed GSVA on the GEO cohort to assess the enrichment of Hallmark and KEGG gene sets in the two subtypes. In the Hallmark gene set analysis, the Immunity_H subgroup was primarily enriched in immune-related pathways such as interferon gamma response, interferon alpha response, and the IL6-JAK-STAT3 signaling pathway (Fig. 2A). The activation of these pathways suggests that the Immunity_H subgroup may have a stronger anti-tumor immune response and inflammatory response.

KEGG pathway analysis further validated. In the Immunity_H subgroup, the top three enriched pathways were Primary immunodeficiency, Autoimmune thyroid disease, and another autoimmune-related disease (Fig. 2B). These results suggest a high level of immune system activity in the Immunity_H subgroup, potentially indicating a stronger anti-tumor immune response. We also performed GSVA analysis in the METABRIC cohort. The results showed a high degree of consistency, confirming the significant enrichment of the Immunity_H subgroup in immune-related pathways (Figure S4A, B).

In addition, we performed a comparative analysis of the expression levels of Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) molecules. The results showed that the expression of both MHC class I molecules (HLA-A, HLA-B, HLA-C) and MHC class II molecules (HLA-DP, HLA-DQ, HLA-DR) was significantly higher in the Immunity_H subgroup than Immunity_L subgroup (Fig. 2C, all p-values < 0.001). These results suggest that the upregulation of MHC class I molecule expression may enhance the ability of cytotoxic T cells to recognize and kill tumor cells. Meanwhile, the high expression of MHC class II molecules indicates that antigen-presenting cells (APCs) (such as B cells, dendritic cells, and macrophages) may enhance the immune response by more efficiently presenting exogenous antigens to CD4+ T cells. To further quantify the degree of immune cell infiltration, we used the CIBERSORT algorithm to compare the abundance of key immune cell subsets in the two subgroups. As shown in Fig. 2D, the proportion of naive B cells, activated memory CD4+ T cells, and γδ T cells was significantly higher in the Immunity_H group than in the Immunity_L group (p < 0.01). In contrast, the Immunity_L group had a higher proportion of plasma cells, regulatory T cells (Tregs), and M0 macrophages (p < 0.05). Notably, the high expression of Tregs, which are important immunosuppressive cells, in the Immunity_L group partially explains the suppressed anti-tumor immune response in this subgroup. Similar results were observed in METABRIC database (Figure S4C, D).

Differential expression analysis of immune subgroups

To investigate the key genes responsible for differential immune induction, we analyzed DEGs between Immunity_H and Immunity_L subgroups. We identified 874 and 568 DEGs (|logFC|> 0.585 and FDR < 0.05) from the GEO and METABRIC cohorts, respectively (Fig. 3A, B). Using Venn diagram analysis, we screened the up and down-regulated genes from the GEO cohort against those from the METABRIC cohort, identifying 391 and 11 overlapping DEGs, respectively (Fig. 3C). KEGG pathway analysis revealed that these DEGs were mainly enriched in immune-related pathways and diseases (Fig. 3D), with upregulated genes significantly enriched in Cytokine-cytokine receptor interaction, Cell adhesion molecules, and Epstein-Barr virus infection pathways. GO biological process analysis also indicated that these DEGs play a role in multiple immune-related pathways such as T cell activation, leukocyte mediated immunity, and regulation of T cell activation (Fig. 3E)

Construction of a prognostic model based on the immunophenotype of TNBC

In this study, the LASSO regression algorithm was employed to screen for genes to construct a simplified and efficient prognostic model. The study subjects (METABRIC cohort) were randomly divided into training and validation sets at a 1:1 ratio. Through 15-fold cross-validation, we determined the optimal penalty parameter, λ (Fig. 4A, left panel). Based on this, five genes crucial for survival prognosis were identified from numerous candidates: LTB (HR = 0.593. p ≤ 0.00013), IRF8 (HR = 0.544, p ≤ 0.00025), IDO1 (HR = 0.726, p ≤ 0.00213), ITM2A (HR = 0.554, p ≤ 0.00035), and LCP1 (HR = 0.639. p ≤ 0.00084) listed in descending order of importance. The LASSO coefficient path plot (Fig. 4A, right panel) clearly illustrates the changes in the coefficients of each gene at different λ values, marking the five core genes retained at the optimal λ. The random forest algorithm, based on its built-in feature importance evaluation mechanism, screened 80 potential prognosis-related genes from 402 DEGs (Fig. 4B), overlapping with the 5 genes obtained by LASSO regression. To validate the effectiveness of LASSO prognostic model, we conducted a series of evaluations in both the training and validation cohorts. First, a gene expression heatmap revealed that the downregulation of the five model genes was closely associated with an increased risk of mortality (Fig. 4C, D). Subsequently, Kaplan–Meier survival analysis demonstrated a highly significant difference in survival outcomes between the high-risk and low-risk groups, as stratified by the model's risk score. This difference was confirmed in both the training cohort (log-rank p < 0.0001) and the validation cohort (log-rank p = 0.0081) (Fig. 4E). Finally, time-dependent ROC analysis indicated that the model possesses excellent predictive accuracy. In the training cohort, the AUC values for predicting 1, 2, and 3 year survival rates all exceeded 0.6. In the validation cohort, the corresponding AUC values were also all above 0.5, confirming the stability and validity of the prognostic model (Fig. 4F).

To evaluate the independent prognostic value of the 5-GPS, univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression analyses were performed. Univariate analysis revealed that all five-constituent immune-related genes (LTB, IDO1, ITM2A, LCP1, and IRF8) served as significant protective factors (all HR < 1, p < 0.05; Figure S5A), whereas clinical features including Tumor size, Advanced Stage, Chemotherapy and Immune Score were associated with poor survival outcomes (all HR > 1, p < 0.05). These significant variables were subsequently integrated into the multivariate Cox regression model (Figure S5B). Notably, both tumor stage (HR = 1.823, 95% CI: 1.244–2.670, p = 0.002) and the 5-GPS risk score (HR = 1.226, 95% CI: 1.010–1.489, p = 0.039) remained prominent independent predictors, confirming that the 5-GPS possesses independent prognostic value for TNBC. To further demonstrate its clinical superiority, the performance of the 5-GPS was rigorously benchmarked against previously reported 6-gene and 12-gene prognostic models in TNBC [15, 16]. Multi-dimensional comparative evaluations based on C-indices, time-dependent ROC curves, and DCA consistently demonstrated that the 5-GPS exhibited the most robust predictive performance, yielding significantly higher C-indices, larger AUC values across 1, 3, and 5 year survival thresholds, and the highest net clinical benefit (Figure S5C-E). Furthermore, the prognostic efficacy of the five signature genes was successfully validated in the independent TCGA cohort, among which LTB was underscored as a pivotal protective marker. Survival analysis clearly indicated that elevated LTB expression was significantly associated with a more favorable prognosis, corresponding to a 35% reduction in the risk of death for OS (HR = 0.65, 95% CI: 0.49–0.86, p = 0.00041) and a 42% reduction in the risk of disease progression (HR = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.42–0.80, p = 0.00086) (Fig. 4G and Figure S6).

High LTB expression is associated with an enhanced immune response

We investigated the relationship between LTB gene expression and immunotherapeutic potential. Patients in the TCGA database were divided into LTB high-expression and low-expression groups. Significant differences in immune cell composition were observed between the two groups. The LTB high-expression group was enriched in immune-promoting cells such as naive B cells, CD8 T+ cells, activated CD4 memory T cells, follicular helper T cells, gamma delta T cells, and activated NK cells (Fig. 5A). Correlation analysis revealed a positive correlation between LTB expression levels and CD8 + T cells and activated CD4+ memory T cells, but a negative correlation with resting CD4+ memory T cells and Tregs. (Fig. 5B). Moreover, LTB expression showed a positive correlation with the expression of multiple immune checkpoint molecules, including CD274 (PD-L1), PDCD1 (PD-1), CTLA4, TIM-3 (HAVCR2), and TIGIT (Fig. 5C). This finding suggests that patients with high LTB expression may benefit more from immune checkpoint therapy.

Expression pattern analysis of LTB in T cells

Systematic analysis of single-cell RNA sequencing data was performed to investigate the LTB expression patterns. UMAP analysis showed the distribution of all cells in nine different cell subpopulations (Fig. 6A). Our research shows that LTB is mainly expressed by activated T cells (LTB-positive cells (nPos = 8362) accounted for 26.16% of the total number of cells) (Fig. 6B), while LTβR is widely expressed in cancer cells, myeloid cells (dendritic cells, macrophages), etc. (Fig. 6C). We further explored the expression of LTB within the T cell subpopulation, finding low LTB expression in exhausted T cells (T_cells_c8_CD8+_LAG3) (Fig. 6D, E). Pseudotime analysis using the Monocle2 algorithm depicted the developmental trajectory of CD8+ T cells, including c4, c6, c7, c8 (Fig. 6F). The results showed that T cell subpopulations differentiated from a common starting point (c4) into two main branch paths (c6 or c8) (Fig. 6G). Analysis of LTB expression dynamics using the GeneSwitches algorithm revealed a gradual increase in LTB expression during early differentiation (c4), peaking in the mid-stage (c6), and a significant decrease in the late stage (c8) (Fig. 6H). LTB expression was primarily localized to the T_cells_c6_IFIT1 subpopulation. Furthermore, CytoTRACE analysis indicated high LTB expression in cytotoxic T cells with high differentiation potential and a marked downregulation in exhausted T cells (Figure S7). In summary, our results suggested that LTB may be involved in the differentiation process of T cells, promoting the generation of cytotoxic T cells and reducing T cell exhaustion.

LTB is a key gene affecting T cell function

We investigated the impact of LTB expression on T cell biological functions. First, we examined the expression changes within 96 h after T cell activation and observed that LTB levels decreased by approximately 50% post-activation, returning to pre-activation levels after 72 h and remaining stable until 96 h and beyond (Fig. 7A, left, Figure S9A). A similar trend of change was observed at the mRNA level (Fig. 7A, right). Therefore, we overexpressed LTB protein in T cells (Fig. 7B, Figure S9B) and analyzed its effect on T cell function. Initially, the LTB overexpression group significantly increased the proportion of CD8-positive T cells (Fig. 7C), consistent with previous single-cell pseudotime analysis results. Secondly, the levels of LAG-3 and PD-1 proteins on the surface of total T lymphocytes, CD4 T cells, and CD8 T cells in the LTB overexpression group were significantly lower than those in the control group from day 3 to day 7 (Fig. 7D, E). While the protein level of TIM-3 in total T lymphocytes in the LTB overexpression group was slightly higher than the control group on day 3, it began to decrease from day 7, and CD4 T cells were lower than the control group on both day 3 and day 7 (Fig. 7F). The results indicate that LTB expression inhibits T cell exhaustion. The EdU proliferation experiment also showed that, among all types of T cells, the LTB overexpression group had a higher proliferation rate than the control group on day seven, particularly with a more significant difference observed in CD8+ T cells (Fig. 7G). Further analysis indicated that the efficiency of LTB overexpression in promoting the proliferation of CD8+ T cells was significantly higher than that of CD4+T cells (Figure S8). Additionally, we found that LTB overexpression enhanced the levels of secreted IFN-γ and TNF-α cytokines on day 3 (Fig. 7H). The cytotoxic activity of LTB-overexpressing T cells against TNBC cells was enhanced, resulting in approximately 35.45% specific lysis after 6 h at a 1:10 E:T ratio, compared to about 10.87% in the control group (Fig. 7I). In summary, in vitro experiments confirmed that LTB overexpression can promote T cell proliferation, reduce T cell exhaustion, and induce T cell differentiation towards cytotoxic T lymphocytes.

Discussion

This study comprehensively delineates the characteristics of the tumor immune microenvironment in TNBC and its association with patient prognosis through an integrated analysis of transcriptomic and single-cell RNA sequencing data. Our analysis revealed that TNBC patients exhibiting a high immune infiltration phenotype experienced significantly prolonged OS. This favorable prognosis was characterized by a substantial increase in the proportions of naïve B cells, activated memory CD4⁺ T cells, and γδ T cells, coupled with a marked enrichment of immune activation-related signaling pathways [17–19]. Building upon these findings, we successfully constructed and validated a prognostic model comprising five pivotal genes: LTB, IRF8, IDO1, ITM2A, and LCP1. This model demonstrated consistent and favorable predictive performance across multiple independent datasets, thereby offering a novel tool for risk stratification in TNBC patients. Furthermore, we identified LTB as a potential therapeutic target in TNBC, given its critical role in T cell activation and differentiation.

Within the 5-GPS prognostic model established based on distinct immune infiltration phenotypes, LTB emerged as a central hub gene. Previous investigations have demonstrated that LTB expression is closely associated with the abundance of CD8⁺ T cells and activated CD4⁺ memory T cells, and exhibits positive correlations with the expression of various immune checkpoint molecules [20, 21]. The remaining four genes in our model are also critical predictive components. IRF8, which is highly expressed in the basal-like TNBC subtype, participates in immunoregulatory processes [22]. IDO1 influences prognosis by mediating immune escape and represents a potential immunotherapeutic target [23, 24]. ITM2A displays aberrant expression in TNBC and promotes tumor progression, although its prognostic value warrants further validation [25]. LCP1 is positively correlated with adverse prognosis and the extent of immune infiltration, suggesting its potential as a prognostic biomarker [26]. Interestingly, although the individual genes in our signature (e.g., LTB, IDO1) were identified as protective factors (HR < 1), the composite risk score acted as a risk factor (HR > 1). This is statistically consistent; since the signature comprises protective genes with negative weights, a higher risk score denotes a cumulative decrease in the expression of these protective elements, thereby characterizing a poorer prognosis. The biological functions of these genes in TNBC and their implications for clinical translation merit further in-depth investigation.

In this study, we discovered that LTB overexpression enhances antitumor immunity by modulating intercellular communication within PBMCs, thereby promoting T cell proliferation, delaying T cell exhaustion, and augmenting the expression of effector cytokines such as IFN-γ and TNF-α. Mechanistically, LTB primarily binds to its receptor, LTβR, activating multiple signaling pathways including NF-κB, MAPK, and STAT, which collectively form a complex immunoregulatory network. Specifically, the LTB-LTβR axis signaling through the non-canonical NF-κB pathway induces the expression of chemokines such as CXCL13 and CCL19/CCL21, thereby fostering the formation of tertiary lymphoid structures [27, 28]. This process involves TRAF3 ubiquitination and degradation, NIK stabilization, and IKKα activation, culminating in the nuclear translocation of the RelB:p52 dimer to initiate the transcription of target genes [29, 30]. Concurrently, LTB regulates reactive oxygen species generation and cytokine secretion via the MAPK pathway, driving the differentiation of naïve T cells into cytotoxic T cells [31], and counteracts STAT3-mediated immunosuppression to optimize the tumor microenvironment [32]. Our experimental data further indicate that LTB modulates T cell function through distinct pathways. We demonstrate that LTB is predominantly expressed by activated immune cells such as T and NK cells, whereas LTβR is widely expressed on tumor cells and myeloid cells (e.g., dendritic cells, macrophages). As T cells are the principal “producers” of LTB rather than the primary “responders” via LTβR, we posit that LTB likely functions as an “immune modulator” or “adjuvant.” Upon binding to LTβR on APCs, such as monocytes and dendritic cells, LTB activates the NF-κB pathway, upregulating the expression of co-stimulatory molecules (e.g., CD80, CD86, ICAM-1). Consequently, LTB educates APCs within the PBMC population, creating a microenvironment enriched with co-stimulatory signals and Th1-type cytokines (IL-12, IFN-I) [33–36]. Within this optimized milieu, T cells undergo more robust activation and polarization, leading to enhanced cytotoxic activity and a significantly potentiated anti-tumor immune response [37].

In recent years, therapeutic strategies targeting the LTB/LTβR axis have presented a landscape of concurrent promise and challenge. Although LTβR blockade can inhibit the non-canonical NF-κB (RelB/p52) pathway, thereby suppressing TNBC cell proliferation and viability [38, 39], it may concomitantly disrupt high endothelial venule structure and T cell recruitment, potentially impairing immune surveillance [39, 40]. This dichotomy underscores the limitations of a simple blockade strategy and suggests that LTβR agonists, particularly in combination with immune checkpoint inhibitors, may hold greater therapeutic promise [41]. Furthermore, the feasibility and risks of targeting LTB require careful consideration. Its differential expression in TNBC presents a therapeutic opportunity [42], yet the dual nature of its signaling may also confer pro-tumorigenic effects [43, 44]. Novel delivery systems, such as nanoparticles, could enhance targeting efficiency and reduce toxicity [45, 46], while robust patient molecular subtyping and biomarker identification are paramount for personalized therapy [47]. Given the beneficial impact of LTB on T cell biology elucidated in our study, its application in the realm of CAR-T cell immunotherapy warrants further exploration.

Despite the predictive value of the LTB-mediated prognostic model, several limitations remain to be addressed. First, the molecular signature was validated using retrospective public cohorts, lacking prospective, multi-center clinical verification; furthermore, the transcriptomic analyses lack spatial resolution, necessitating future integration of spatial transcriptomics and multiplexed immunofluorescence to map the in situ topological distribution of the LTβR axis. Second, the functional mechanisms were primarily validated in vitro, which cannot fully recapitulate the complex, immunosuppressive in vivo tumor microenvironment. Thus, syngeneic or patient-derived xenograft models and deep multi-omics profiling are required to fully elucidate downstream signaling pathways. Finally, systemic therapeutic targeting of the LTβR axis poses off-target toxicity risks due to its pleiotropic nature; consequently, future research should focus on precision delivery systems, such as tumor-localized CAR-T cells or lipid nanoparticles, as well as rational combinatorial designs with existing immune checkpoint blockades to optimize clinical translation.

Conclusion

In this study, we used unsupervised clustering analysis to identify TNBC patients with different immune infiltration, which were associated with prognoses estimation. Furthermore, we developed a prognostic prediction model based on immune infiltration characteristics. Through the study of the key immune infiltration gene-LTB, we illuminated its biological functions and mechanisms related to enhancing T cell cytotoxicity. In conclusion, this study demonstrates the critical role of immune infiltration in the prognosis of TNBC patients and provides important evidence and potential targets for improving immunotherapy outcomes in TNBC.
