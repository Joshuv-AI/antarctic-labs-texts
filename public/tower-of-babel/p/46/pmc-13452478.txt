DeepACPred: an integrated multistage framework for anticancer peptide discovery and activity prediction
Authors: Bo Zhang, Ruifang Li, Kedong Yin, Yufeng Yang, Jinhua Zhang, Mengwan Jiang, Huijie Wang, Shiyu Li, Lujing Jia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractArtificial intelligence accelerates anticancer peptides (ACPs) discovery. However, existing computational methods lack integration of identification with activity-based candidate prioritization. Here, we present DeepACPred, a three-stage pipeline encompassing ACP binary classification model, ACP multilabel classification model, and ACP IC50 prediction model, leveraging multimodal features from ESM2 protein language model embeddings, AAindex physicochemical descriptors, and sequence composition. On 5712 benchmark sequences, the binary classifier achieved 95.10% accuracy (AUC = 0.9913), with performance remaining stable under CD-HIT cluster-aware splitting at 40%–90% identity thresholds. Multilabel cancer-type prediction yielded macro-F1 = 0.9124 across seven cancer types, and log10(IC50) regression achieved Spearman ρ = 0.8602 under 5-fold cross-validation. Ablation experiments showed task-dependent feature contributions rather than uniformly additive multimodal effects. Applied to 260 000 motif-enriched 18-mer candidates, DeepACPred selected 12 peptides predicted to be active against breast cancer cells, all of which showed measurable in vitro cytotoxic activity against murine 4T1 cells in OD-derived dose–response assays (IC50: 0.88–36.83 μg/ml). Although prospective IC50 ranking showed limited fine-grained resolution, these results support the use of the regression module for coarse candidate enrichment. In conclusion, DeepACPred provides a systematic framework for ACP candidate enrichment and prioritization.Keywords: anticancer peptides, multistage prediction, ESM2 protein language model, multimodal feature integration, activity-based candidate screening

Introduction

Cancer remains a leading cause of mortality worldwide, with steadily increasing incidence rates. Conventional anticancer therapies are often limited by adverse side effects and the emergence of drug resistance [1, 2]. These challenges have motivated the exploration of alternative treatment modalities, among which anticancer peptides (ACPs) have attracted considerable attention [3]. ACPs are typically short (10–50 amino acids), amphipathic, and cationic peptides that can interact with cancer-cell membranes through physicochemical properties such as charge, hydrophobicity, and amphipathicity [4, 5]. However, identifying highly active ACPs from vast sequence spaces remains challenging, owing to the cost and low throughput of experimental screening [6–8].

Artificial intelligence has established efficient pathways for ACP discovery. Early predictors relied on single-modality features, including mACPpred [9] (88.72% accuracy) and ACPred-FL [10] (91.4% accuracy), while Akbar et al. [11] explored genetic algorithm–based ensemble strategies. Subsequent efforts expanded feature spaces and architectures: AntiCP 2.0 [12] adopted multifeature fusion, ACP-MHCNN [13] employed multiheaded CNNs, Le et al. [14] combined fuzzy neural networks with feature selection, and Yuan et al. [15] integrated ordinal encoding with BiLSTM/CNN/LightGBM ensembles. Manavalan et al. [16] proposed MLACP for multilabel cancer-type prediction, and Schaduangrat et al. [17] reviewed computational ACP methods, highlighting the need for integrated multitask frameworks. Despite these advances, most approaches remain confined to binary classification, and none integrates ACP identification, multilabel cancer-type prediction, and quantitative activity regression within a unified pipeline.

Beyond binary classification, several methodological gaps persist. First, no existing framework bridges the ACP binary classification model, ACP multilabel classification model, and ACP IC50 prediction model within a single pipeline [18–20]. Second, evaluation rigor remains insufficient: most studies apply permissive identity thresholds for deduplication—inadequate for short peptides where minor motif variations can preserve near-identical sequences across data splits—and frequently fit preprocessing operations on entire datasets prior to cross-validation, risking information leakage [21]. Third, claims of multimodal fusion benefits are rarely substantiated by systematic ablation experiments, leaving unclear whether performance gains reflect genuine feature complementarity or dataset artifacts.

Protein language models (PLMs) such as ESM2 [22], ProtTrans [23], and PeptideBERT [24] offer a promising avenue to address these gaps by generating contextual embeddings encoding evolutionary and structural constraints. However, integrating PLM embeddings with established physicochemical and compositional descriptors remains challenging [25–28]: naive concatenation of modalities with vastly different dimensionalities risks allowing higher-dimensional features to dominate biologically informative contributions. A principled multimodal fusion strategy with per-modality feature selection, combined with homology-aware data splitting and leakage-free nested cross-validation, is essential for reliable multitask ACP prediction.

Here, we present DeepACPred, a multistage framework integrating an ACP binary classification model, an ACP multilabel cancer-type prediction model, and an ACP IC50 prediction model (Fig. 1). The framework integrates ESM2 embeddings [22], AAindex physicochemical descriptors [29], and sequence composition features (SeqComp) [30–34], with per-modality feature selection to prevent dimensional dominance. All preprocessing is strictly nested within cross-validation folds, and robustness is validated through CD-HIT cluster-aware splitting at 40%–90% identity thresholds [43, 44]. Systematic ablation experiments quantify each modality’s contribution. On 5712 benchmark sequences, the binary classifier achieved 95.10% accuracy (AUC = 0.9913), stable at the strictest 40% threshold. Multilabel cancer-type prediction yielded macro-F1 = 0.9124 across seven cancer types, and log10(IC50) regression achieved cross-validation Spearman ρ = 0.8602. Applied to 260 000 candidate octadecapeptides from conserved motif-based enrichment [21, 35–39], the pipeline selected 12 candidates predicted to be active against breast cancer cells, all of which showed measurable in vitro cytotoxic activity against murine 4 T1 cells (IC50: 0.88–36.83 μg/ml). However, prospective IC50 ranking among these 12 peptides was not statistically significant, supporting the use of the regression module primarily as a coarse enrichment tool.

Materials and methods

Data collection and preprocessing

Positive samples comprised ACPs with experimentally determined IC50 values from the CancerPPD database [40]. Negative samples were compiled from three sources: antimicrobial peptides from UniProt [41] (physicochemically similar to ACPs but lacking anticancer activity), experimentally validated non-ACPs from the AntiCP 2.0 benchmark [12], and randomly selected Swiss-Prot sequences (general background). This three-source design, analogous to strategies in AntiCP 2.0 [12] and ACPScanner [19], increases the biological realism of the decision boundary. Model robustness was evaluated using CD-HIT cluster-aware data splitting at progressively tighter thresholds (90%, 70%, and 40% identity), and all sequences were deduplicated at a 90% sequence identity threshold. Three task-specific datasets were derived: (i) a binary classification dataset containing 5712 sequences, including 2856 positives samples (Data S1) and 2856 negatives samples (Data S2); (ii) a multilabel classification dataset comprising 389 ACPs annotated with seven cancer-type labels—breast, lung, skin, prostate, colorectal, cervical, and leukemia—with each peptide associated with two to three cancer types on average (Data S3); and (iii) an activity regression dataset of 453 ACPs with experimentally measured IC50 values ranging from 0.02 to 655 μg/ml (Data S4). All sequences underwent a unified preprocessing pipeline, including letter-case standardization and replacement of nonstandard amino acids with “X” [26].

To assess external generalizability of the binary ACP identification model, we used an external test set derived from the NITA-LGBM-ACp study [42], containing 90 experimentally verified ACPs and 500 real non-ACP sequences. This set was not used during model development. Exact sequence matching confirmed no duplicate sequences between the external test set and the model-development datasets. The set was therefore evaluated only once as an external benchmark; the absence of additional CD-HIT high-identity filtering is acknowledged as a limitation.

To prevent homology-based information leakage, cluster-aware data splitting was implemented using CD-HIT [43, 44]. At each identity threshold (90%, 70%, 40%), sequences were clustered (word size: −n = 5 for ≥70%; −n = 2 for 40%), and clusters—not individual sequences—were assigned to training (80%) and held-out test (20%) sets. Within the training set, cluster-aware K-fold cross-validation ensured all sequences from a given cluster appear in the same fold. Cluster statistics are reported in Table 1.

Multimodal feature extraction and integration

A multimodal feature representation was established by integrating sequence-based, physicochemical, and deep learning–derived descriptors, resulting in an initial feature space of 6800 dimensions. At the sequence level, a 426-dimensional feature vector was constructed, incorporating amino acid composition, dipeptide composition, physicochemical group distribution, and sequence length to encode fundamental structural characteristics of peptides [45]. The physicochemical profile was derived from 566 indices in the AAindex database [29], which were transformed using nine statistical descriptors into a 5094-dimensional feature matrix. Nine statistical descriptors—mean, standard deviation, minimum, maximum, median, Q1, Q3, skewness, and kurtosis—were computed as sequence-level summaries from per-residue physicochemical values. AAindex indices with >5 missing amino acid values (out of 20) were excluded; remaining missing values were imputed with the mean of known values. The pretrained ESM2 model (ESM2-t33-650M-UR50D) generated 1280-dimensional embeddings by extracting layer-33 residue-level representations and applying mean pooling over nonspecial tokens (excluding BOS/EOS). Sequences exceeding 1024 tokens were truncated; nonstandard residues (“X”) were processed using the ESM2 tokenizer’s default encoding. To prevent higher-dimensional modalities from dominating the fused representation, per-modality feature selection was applied independently to each modality before concatenation. For binary classification, mutual information (MI)–based selection with a total budget of 500 features (minimum 50 per modality) yielded 518 features. For multilabel classification, 600 MI features were selected per modality, followed by PCA compression to 100 dimensions. For log10(IC50) regression, Random Forest–based importance selection was applied per modality, followed by PCA reduction to 30 components [46, 47]. The nesting of all preprocessing within cross-validation folds is detailed in the Model Training and Evaluation section.

Deep learning architecture

Task-specific machine learning architectures were used to construct the three-stage predictive pipeline. For binary classification, we constructed a Deep Neural Network (DNN) with three fully connected hidden layers (512, 256, and 128 units), with batch normalization, dropout (P = 0.5), and L2 kernel regularization (λ = 0.0005). The model was trained using the Adam optimizer (initial learning rate = 1 × 10−4) for up to 100 epochs with a batch size of 64, early stopping (patience = 10, monitored by validation AUC), and a reduce-on-plateau learning rate scheduler (factor = 0.5, patience = 5) [48, 49]. For multilabel cancer-type prediction, four classifiers were evaluated under a One-vs-Rest strategy with inverse frequency weighting: Random Forest (300 trees, maximum depth 10) [50], XGBoost (300 estimators, learning rate 0.05) [51], linear SVM (C = 1.0), and L2-regularized Logistic Regression (C = 1.0) [52, 53]. The best model in each fold was selected by macro-F1, and per-label thresholds were optimized on the validation partition. For IC50 regression, CatBoost was used as the activity-prediction model [54]. Z-score-normalized IC50 and log10(IC50) targets were evaluated under matched data splits and preprocessing. The log10(IC50) model was used for candidate prioritization because it reduced the dynamic range of IC50 values and allowed back-transformation to μg/ml. CatBoost hyperparameters were optimized using Optuna with 50 trials and 3-fold inner cross-validation on the training data only [55, 56].

Model training and evaluation

All models were evaluated using leakage-free protocols. Stratified K-fold cross-validation was used for binary classification, whereas MultilabelStratifiedKFold was used for multilabel classification to preserve label distributions across folds. All preprocessing steps, including mutual information feature selection, Random Forest–based feature selection, StandardScaler normalization, and PCA dimensionality reduction, were fitted only on the training partition of each fold and then applied to the corresponding validation partition. No validation or held-out test information was used for preprocessing, feature selection, dimensionality reduction, threshold selection, or hyperparameter optimization. For IC50 regression, the dataset was split into a model-development training set (80%) and an isolated held-out test set (20%). Within the training set, 5-fold cross-validation was used for model development and stability assessment, while Optuna hyperparameter search used an independent 3-fold inner cross-validation. The held-out test set was evaluated once after model selection [57, 58]. Task-specific metrics were used, with the Matthews Correlation Coefficient (MCC) as the primary metric for binary classification [59].

Spearman ρ and Kendall τ with two-sided P-values were calculated for this prospective analysis. The experimental IC50 values were not used for model training, hyperparameter tuning, or candidate selection.

Computational resources

All experiments were conducted on a Linux server (96 CPU cores; Python 3.10.8; TensorFlow 2.20.0; PyTorch 2.1.2) in CPU-only mode. Sequential execution of the three independent stages required ~217 min; parallel execution completed in ~84 min.

Results

Dataset construction and analysis

Dataset composition and statistical properties are summarized in Fig. 2. Sequence length distribution (Fig. 2B) confirmed the characteristic short length of ACPs, with positive peptides exhibiting a median length of ~15 amino acids compared to ~25 for negatives [63]. Cancer-type annotation analysis (Fig. 2C) showed an average of two to three labels per peptide, with prevalence for breast, colorectal, and skin cancers (15%–20% each), and lower representation for cervical cancer and leukemia (~10%). The IC50 values (Fig. 2D) spanned a wide dynamic range (0.02–655 μg/ml); Z-score normalization successfully handled this range, producing a distribution centered near zero. For log10(IC50) regression, two target transformation approaches were compared: Z-score normalization (preserving relative ordering on a standardized scale) and log10(IC50) transformation (operating directly in biologically interpretable log-concentration space, range: −1.70 to 2.82). Predictions from the log10 model can be back-transformed via 10y to yield IC50 values in μg/ml. Both formulations were evaluated; log10(IC50) was adopted as the primary model given its superior cross-validation stability (Spearman = 0.8602 ± 0.0529 versus 0.8478 ± 0.0646 for Z-score).

Multimodal feature fusion and feature contribution analysis

Using the multimodal feature extraction framework described in Materials and Methods (426 sequence-level features, 5094 AAindex features, and 1280 ESM2 embeddings; total 6800 dimensions), task-specific per-modality feature selection was applied to each predictive task. For binary classification, MI-based selection with a total budget of 500 features yielded 95.10% accuracy; for multilabel classification, 600 MI features followed by PCA compression to 100 dimensions yielded macro-F1 = 0.9124 ± 0.0089; for log10(IC50) regression, Random Forest–based selection followed by PCA reduction to 30 components was applied.

The contribution of different feature modalities to prediction performance was quantified using systematic ablation experiments under matched evaluation settings (Fig. 3). These experiments included single-modality models and leave-one-modality-out pairwise combinations. Specifically, ESM2 + AAindex removes SeqComp, ESM2 + SeqComp removes AAindex, and AAindex + SeqComp removes ESM2. Within each pipeline stage, all ablation configurations used the same train/test splits or cross-validation folds, preprocessing procedures, feature-selection strategy, dimensionality-reduction method, model-training protocol, and evaluation metrics.

ESM2 embeddings achieved the highest unimodal performance in Stage 1 (AUC = 0.994, MCC = 0.920) and Stage 2 (macro-F1 = 0.904), while AAindex showed comparable or superior performance in Stage 3 regression (R2 = 0.502 versus 0.466 for ESM2). The sequence-composition feature had the lowest unimodal performance on most metrics but remained competitive in the second stage (Macro-F1 = 0.906). Therefore, the full trimodal representation should not be interpreted as universally optimal across all stages. SHAP-based feature importance visualization was also performed; however, attributions computed after PCA cannot be mapped to individual original features, and fold-wise stability was not assessed [62].

ACPs binary classification model

Training dynamics demonstrated rapid convergence and effective feature learning (Fig. 4A and B). On a stratified held-out test set (80% training/20% test), the model attained an overall accuracy of 95.10% (Fig. 4C). Comprehensive evaluation (Fig. 4D) confirmed robust performance across metrics: 93.52% sensitivity, 96.68% specificity, an F1-score of 0.9502, a Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) of 0.9025, and AUC-ROC of 0.9913. Under 5-fold cross-validation, key metrics showed high reproducibility: AUC = 0.9907 ± 0.0017, MCC = 0.9006 ± 0.0106, and accuracy = 0.9501 ± 0.0053.

To verify that performance does not rely on near-homolog memorization, robustness was evaluated under progressively tighter CD-HIT identity thresholds (Table 1). Performance remained stable across all thresholds (AUC > 0.988 at 40% identity), confirming that the model does not depend on memorization of near-duplicate sequences.

To further assess cross-dataset generalizability, we evaluated the binary classifier on the external NITA-LGBM-ACp test set, which was not used during model development, feature selection, hyperparameter tuning, or threshold selection. Exact sequence matching confirmed that none of the external test sequences appeared in the training/model-development datasets. On 90 external positive ACPs, the model correctly identified 78 (sensitivity = 86.7%); on 500 external non-ACP sequences, specificity was 89.2% (false-positive rate = 10.8%). These results provide additional evidence that the binary classifier can generalize to an external dataset beyond the model-development distribution.

For comparative context, three representative ACP predictors—AntiCP 2.0 [12], MLACP 2.0 [65], and ACP-MHCNN [13]—were evaluated alongside DeepACPred on the independent external NITA-LGBM-ACp test set (90 ACPs, 500 non-ACPs). Predictions from AntiCP 2.0 and MLACP 2.0 were obtained via their publicly available web servers; ACP-MHCNN was executed using its released code with default parameters. As shown in Table 2, DeepACPred achieved the highest sensitivity (86.7%) and specificity (89.2%).

ACP multilabel classification model

The multilabel cancer-type prediction model was trained on 389 ACPs annotated with seven cancer-type labels (breast, lung, skin, prostate, colorectal, cervical, and leukemia) using multimodal features, per-modality MI selection, and a One-vs-Rest classification strategy, with PCA compression to 100 dimensions. Under 5-fold MultilabelStratifiedKFold cross-validation, Random Forest was selected as the best-performing model in all five folds based on macro-F1. For the final deployed model trained on the complete dataset, XGBoost achieved the highest macro-F1 and was therefore adopted for inference [66].

As shown in Fig. 5A–D, the multilabel classification model performed effectively across all evaluation criteria. Under 5-fold cross-validation, key metrics indicated high reproducibility and stability. The best-selected model per fold achieved a macro F1-score of 0.9124 ± 0.0089, macro-precision of 0.9029 ± 0.0134, macro-recall of 0.9260 ± 0.0148, macro-AUC of 0.9530 ± 0.0089, exact match ratio of 0.6505 ± 0.0355, and Hamming loss of 0.0867 ± 0.0096—a notable result given the 128 possible label combinations among the seven cancer types. Label-by-label precision, recall, and F1-score are summarized in Table 3, with cervical cancer showing the lowest F1 (0.834) and skin cancer achieving the highest (0.949).

Among the four classifiers, Random Forest demonstrated the highest stability, selected as best in all five folds (Fig. 5A). Tree-based methods (RF and XGBoost) substantially outperformed linear models (SVM and LR). For final deployment on the complete dataset, XGBoost achieved the highest macro-F1 (Fig. 5B) and was therefore adopted for inference, likely benefiting from its stronger optimization capacity with more training data. Furthermore, predictive performance varied considerably across cancer types (Fig. 5E). Quantitative evaluation revealed the highest per-label F1-score for skin cancer (0.949), while cervical cancer (0.834) and leukemia (0.897) showed comparatively lower performance (Fig. 5E). These systematic disparities suggest that peptides annotated with different cancer-type labels may contain partially distinct sequence patterns, although experimental validation across multiple cancer and matched normal cell lines would be required to establish true cancer-type selectivity.

Analysis of feature importance in the best-selected models reveals a balanced contribution pattern among AAindex physicochemical properties, sequence composition features (SeqComp), and ESM2 embeddings. This relatively even distribution across the three feature modalities suggests that multilabel cancer-type prediction may benefit from integrating multidimensional information, although the current multilabel model should not be interpreted as experimental evidence of cancer-type selectivity. In contrast to the pronounced dominance of AAindex features observed in binary classification, this balanced profile underscores distinct feature dependencies across different prediction tasks.

ACP IC50 prediction model

For activity-based candidate prioritization, we established a CatBoost regression framework based on 453 ACPs with experimentally measured IC50 values across five cancer types (breast cancer, colorectal cancer, leukemia, lung cancer, and skin cancer), with IC50 values modeled using both Z-score normalization and log10 transformation. Hyperparameters were optimized using Optuna Bayesian search (50 trials, 3-fold inner CV). To improve clarity, regression performance is reported separately for internal cross-validation, held-out test evaluation, target-transformation comparison, and prospective wet-lab validation.

In internal 5-fold cross-validation, the log10(IC50) model achieved Spearman ρ = 0.8602 ± 0.0529 and R2 = 0.6706 ± 0.1776. On the held-out test set, the same model achieved MSE = 0.1931, R2 = 0.4494, Spearman ρ = 0.7333, and Kendall τ = 0.5765 (Fig. 6A). Training MSE versus test MSE comparison indicated a moderate generalization gap consistent with the limited sample size (n = 453). Residual analysis (Fig. 6B) confirmed the reliability of the general model, with residuals randomly distributed around zero and following a normal distribution, indicating absence of systematic bias across the peptide sequence space.

Cancer-specific IC50 regression models were evaluated as exploratory analyses because of limited sample sizes: breast cancer (n = 99), colorectal cancer (n = 80), leukemia (n = 87), lung cancer (n = 111), and skin cancer (n = 76). Although some cancer-specific models showed low MSE values, these results should be interpreted cautiously because small subsets and train/test MSE gaps may indicate overfitting rather than robust cancer-specific quantitative prediction. Feature-importance and ablation analyses suggested that ESM2 embeddings provided useful sequence representations, consistent with prior evidence that protein language models capture biological sequence information [67]. However, no single modality combination consistently dominated across all regression metrics, indicating that IC50 prediction remains challenging at the current data scale.

Ablation-based quantification of modality contributions in pipeline stages

To quantify the independent and combined contribution of each feature modality, systematic ablation experiments were conducted under identical data splits, preprocessing procedures, feature-selection strategies, dimensionality-reduction settings, model-training protocols, and evaluation metrics within each pipeline stage. Three single-modality models (ESM2-only, AAindex-only, and SeqComp-only), three leave-one-modality-out pairwise combinations, and the full trimodal model were evaluated. Specifically, ESM2 + AAindex removes SeqComp, ESM2 + SeqComp removes AAindex, and AAindex + SeqComp removes ESM2.

In Stage 1, ESM2-only achieved the highest unimodal performance (AUC = 0.9936, accuracy = 0.9598), establishing it as the primary discriminative source (Table 5). Among the leave-one-modality-out combinations, ESM2 + SeqComp slightly outperformed the full trimodal model on the held-out test set (MCC: 0.9160 versus 0.9075). This result indicates that adding all modalities does not necessarily improve Stage 1 binary classification.

High-activity ACP screening

Analysis of active ACPs in CancerPPD revealed that 18-mers represent the highest proportion (Fig. S5) and provide sufficient context for stable feature extraction; the candidate library length was therefore fixed at 18 residues. A total of 260 000 candidate octadeca-peptide sequences (Data S5) were generated by applying a multiscale conserved pattern mining algorithm (Fig. 1A). This approach leveraged existing peptide records with reported breast cancer-cell activity to construct length-aligned position-specific amino acid matrices, combined with single-residue and dipeptide frequency analyses to extract functional conserved peptide motifs [68]. It should be noted that this candidate library generation strategy constitutes targeted enrichment based on known peptide motifs associated with reported breast cancer-cell activity, rather than true de novo discovery, which introduces a priori bias toward known motif neighborhoods and increases the expected hit rate relative to a random library. Using the DeepACPred three-stage screening pipeline, the binary classification model (probability threshold: 0.95) identified 41 107 sequences as putative ACPs (Data S6). The multilabel classifier subsequently narrowed this set to 1228 peptides predicted to be active against breast cancer cells (Data S7). Finally, the regression model ranked candidates by predicted IC50 values, and the 12 candidates with the lowest predicted IC50 values (predicted IC50: 11.85–14.97 μg/ml) were selected for experimental testing (Table 8).

Predicted IC50 values were narrowly clustered (11.85–14.97 μg/ml), whereas experimentally measured IC50 values for the 12 wet-lab validated peptides spanned a wider range (0.88–36.83 μg/ml), indicating predictive compression characteristic of regression dilution. To distinguish this prospective wet-lab validation from the internal cross-validation and held-out test evaluations, we separately calculated rank correlations between predicted and experimentally measured IC50 values for the 12 tested peptides. This prospective analysis showed no significant association between predicted and experimental IC50 values (Spearman ρ = −0.1156, P = 0.7206; Kendall τ = −0.0763, P = 0.7311). Therefore, the regression module should not be interpreted as a precise quantitative IC50 predictor in the prospective setting. Nevertheless, all 12 selected peptides exhibited measurable activity against murine 4T1 cells, supporting the practical value of the regression module as a coarse enrichment and prioritization tool for selecting active candidates from a 260 000-peptide library.

Collectively, our framework implemented a sequential pipeline from ACP identification through multilabel cancer-type prediction to activity-based prioritization, demonstrating its practical value through experimental validation on murine 4T1 cells.

Discussion

DeepACPred establishes an integrated computational pipeline spanning ACP binary classification, multilabel cancer-type prediction, and IC50-based candidate prioritization. Systematic ablation experiments revealed task-dependent modality contribution patterns rather than uniformly additive effects (Tables 5–7). In binary classification, ESM2 embeddings served as the primary discriminative source, while SeqComp and AAindex provided limited or task-specific complementary information. For multilabel cancer-type prediction, the full trimodal model achieved the best overall macro-F1, suggesting that this task may benefit from integrating contextual embeddings, physicochemical descriptors, and sequence-composition information. In IC50 prediction, no single modality combination consistently dominated all metrics. Notably, AAindex + SeqComp outperformed the full trimodal model on Spearman correlation (0.7772 versus 0.7544) and R2 (0.5786 versus 0.5081), suggesting that high-dimensional ESM2 embeddings (1280 dimensions) may introduce noise under the limited regression sample size (n = 453). These results indicate that multimodal fusion does not universally guarantee improvement and that task-adaptive feature representations are required for peptide function prediction [69].

The primary contribution of DeepACPred is integrating ACP identification, multilabel cancer-type prediction, and activity value prediction within a single pipeline—a capability absent in AntiCP 2.0 [12], MLACP 2.0 [65] (both binary only), and ACP-MHCNN [13] (lacking quantitative capability). On the external NITA-LGBM-ACp test set, which showed no exact sequence overlap with the model-development data, DeepACPred outperformed AntiCP 2.0, MLACP 2.0, and ACP-MHCNN in both sensitivity (86.7%) and specificity (89.2%), as detailed in Table 2. Internal ablation experiments further provide the primary evidence for the effectiveness of our multimodal strategy under identical evaluation conditions.

Here, we demonstrated the practical utility of the three-stage framework using peptides predicted to be active against breast cancer cells as an example. All 12 selected candidates showed measurable in vitro cytotoxic activity against murine 4T1 cells in OD-derived dose–response assays, with final SPSS-fitted IC50 values ranging from 0.88 to 36.83 μg/ml. However, the predicted IC50 values were narrowly clustered compared with the wider experimental range, and prospective ranking correlation was not statistically significant across these 12 peptides (Spearman ρ = −0.1156, p = 0.7206; Kendall τ = −0.0763, P = 0.7311). Therefore, the regression module should be positioned as a coarse screening and candidate-enrichment tool for large-scale peptide libraries rather than as a precise quantitative IC50 predictor. Final activity quantification still requires experimental dose–response titration. Future work will incorporate additional experimentally measured IC50 values into iterative model refinement.

Notwithstanding these advances, several limitations should be acknowledged. The multilabel and regression datasets remain limited in scale, and the negative sample set may not fully represent true non-ACP distributions. The One-vs-Rest strategy does not explicitly model interlabel dependencies, and the IC50 regression model showed limited fine-grained prospective ranking ability in the 12-peptide validation set. The cancer-specific regression models were based on small subsets and should be considered exploratory. Although the external NITA-LGBM-ACp test set showed no exact sequence overlap with the model-development data, additional CD-HIT high-identity filtering was not performed. The absence of explicit three-dimensional structural information limits deeper mechanistic characterization [70]. The candidate library was generated by targeted motif-based enrichment, which may increase the expected hit rate relative to random libraries. Experimentally, validation was performed on murine 4T1 cells only; therefore, the current results do not establish cancer-type selectivity or normal-cell safety. Validation on human cancer cell lines with matched normal-cell comparators remains an essential future step.
