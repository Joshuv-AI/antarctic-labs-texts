Gut dysbiosis in people with epilepsy: a dual-site cross-sectional study of enterobacteriaceae patterns and clinical correlates in peri-urban and urban Ghana
Authors: Kingsley Essel Arthur, Ransford Amoah, John Danquah, Priscilla Kolibea Mante
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTo compare recoverable Enterobacteriaceae patterns in adults with epilepsy and controls across peri-urban and urban Ghana, and to explore associations with comorbidity and antiseizure medication (ASM) regimen.MethodsThis dual-site cross-sectional study enrolled 120 adults (70 people with epilepsy [PWE], 50 controls) from Tarkwa Municipal Hospital (peri-urban) and KNUST Hospital, Kumasi (urban). Stool samples were subjected to enrichment in Brilliant Green Bile Broth followed by selective/differential culture for Enterobacteriaceae. Recoverable CFU/g-equivalents were defined as colony-forming units recovered after enrichment and selective culture, back-calculated to the gram of stool input, and interpreted as a standardized recoverability measure (not absolute stool bacterial load). Isolates were identified by MALDI-TOF MS (Bruker) using manufacturer score thresholds (≥ 2.0 species-level; 1.70–1.99 genus-level). Richness was defined as the number of distinct MALDI-TOF–confirmed Enterobacteriaceae taxa recovered per participant.ResultsIn the peri-urban cohort, PWE had lower setting-dependent recoverable burden than controls (11.20 ± 2.44 vs. 20.95 ± 3.95 [recoverable CFU/g-equivalents, post-enrichment]; p < 0.0001). In the urban cohort, PWE had higher setting-dependent recoverable burden than controls (17.48 ± 4.00 vs. 11.29 ± 1.86 [recoverable CFU/g-equivalents, post-enrichment]; p = 0.002). Site-dependent differences were observed for Citrobacter freundii, Proteus mirabilis, and Enterobacter hormaechei. Comorbidities were more frequent in urban PWE (50%) than peri-urban PWE (15%); among peri-urban PWE, E. hormaechei recoverable abundance was higher in comorbid versus non-comorbid participants (p = 0.03). In urban PWE, ASM polytherapy was associated with higher psychosis-related symptom scores than ASM monotherapy (p < 0.0001).ConclusionRecoverable Enterobacteriaceae-associated patterns in epilepsy differed by setting under enrichment and selective culture conditions, supporting context-specific dysbiosis hypotheses in Ghana and motivating larger longitudinal, sequencing-based studies with environmental and standardized clinical phenotyping.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-04901-3.

Background

Epilepsy is a chronic neurological disorder characterized by recurrent unprovoked seizures and remains a major contributor to disability worldwide, with a disproportionate burden in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) [1, 2]. In sub-Saharan Africa, preventable and treatable causes (including infections, trauma, and perinatal insults), limited specialist access, medication stock-outs, and stigma contribute to delayed care and persistent treatment gaps [3, 4]. In Ghana, epilepsy prevalence is commonly reported around 1–2% [5], and clinically important comorbidities including depression, psychosis-spectrum symptoms, cognitive impairment, and cardiometabolic conditions contribute to poorer quality of life, reduced adherence, and increased health service utilization [6]. Beyond these clinical and health-system challenges, emerging biological pathways may also modulate seizure susceptibility and neurobehavioral outcomes.

Growing evidence implicates the microbiota–gut–brain axis as a potential modifier of seizure susceptibility and neurobehavioral outcomes [7, 8]. Gut microbial signals can influence host immune tone, barrier integrity, and circulating metabolites with neuromodulatory potential. Inflammatory signaling linked to Gram-negative bacteria and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) has been proposed as one pathway through which dysbiosis could contribute to neuroinflammation and altered neuronal excitability [9, 10]. However, most epilepsy–microbiome studies have been conducted in high-income settings and use sequencing-based designs, and evidence from sub-Saharan Africa remains limited [11, 12].

Enterobacteriaceae are a clinically relevant family of facultative Gram-negative organisms that frequently expand under ecological disturbance and have been associated with inflammatory dysbiosis signatures. Species such as Escherichia coli, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Citrobacter freundii, Enterobacter hormaechei, and Proteus mirabilis are LPS-bearing taxa that can be assessed pragmatically in resource-constrained settings using targeted culture-based recovery/enumeration under enrichment and selective culture conditions, followed by MALDI-TOF confirmation [13, 14]. This targeted approach does not characterize the full gut microbiome but can generate species-resolved evidence on a biologically plausible inflammatory family. Importantly, the detectability and relative recovery of these taxa may vary with local exposures and care patterns.

In Ghana, environmental and socioeconomic gradients may shape enteric microbial exposures and yield context-specific dysbiosis patterns. Peri-urban communities may experience dietary constraints, variable sanitation, and intermittent healthcare access, while urban populations may experience dietary transitions, greater medication access (including antibiotics), environmental pollutants, and psychosocial stressors. These exposures may influence Enterobacteriaceae carriage and abundance and potentially relate to epilepsy comorbidities and treatment burden. Antiseizure medication (ASM) regimen intensity adds complexity: polytherapy is often used in more severe or refractory epilepsy and is associated with a higher adverse-effect burden, including neuropsychiatric symptoms, but whether regimen intensity co-segregates with Enterobacteriaceae patterns within Ghanaian settings is not well characterized [15].

Despite increasing interest in microbiome–epilepsy relationships, few studies in Africa have compared microbial patterns across differing socioeconomic contexts within the same country. Addressing this gap is important in Ghana, where comorbidity burden is substantial and exposures likely to shape enteric ecology vary across peri-urban and urban settings. Therefore, this study profiled Enterobacteriaceae-associated patterns among adults with epilepsy and matched controls across peri-urban (Tarkwa) and urban (Kumasi) Ghana using targeted culture-based recovery/enumeration under enrichment and selective culture conditions, followed by MALDI-TOF confirmation. We hypothesized that Enterobacteriaceae patterns in epilepsy would differ by setting, consistent with context-dependent dysbiosis, and that polytherapy would be associated with higher neurobehavioral symptom burden, particularly in the urban cohort. Materials and methods

Study design and setting

This dual-site cross-sectional comparative study was conducted in Ghana at Tarkwa Municipal Hospital (peri-urban) and KNUST Hospital, Kumasi (urban). Tarkwa Municipal Hospital and KNUST Hospital, Kumasi, were purposively selected to represent peri-urban and urban populations, respectively. These sites were chosen due to their distinct environmental and socioeconomic characteristics, availability of established epilepsy care services, adequate patient populations, and feasibility of sample and clinical data collection. Participant recruitment and clinical data collection occurred at both sites. Microbiological processing, culture-based recovery, and enumeration were performed at the KNUST Microbiology Laboratory, while isolate identification was completed at the Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research using MALDI-TOF mass spectrometry. The study used a targeted culture-based approach focused on Enterobacteriaceae and was not designed to characterize the full gut microbiome.

Participants, eligibility criteria, and grouping

A total of 120 adults aged 18–60 years were enrolled, including 70 people with epilepsy (PWE) and 50 controls. Controls had no known neurological, psychiatric, or gastrointestinal conditions and were selected to be broadly comparable to PWE by age and sex. Participants were recruited from peri-urban Tarkwa (20 PWE, 20 controls) and urban Kumasi (50 PWE, 30 controls). Epilepsy diagnosis and enrollment were conducted by a trained epilepsy specialist nurse. PWE were eligible if they had been on a stable antiseizure medication (ASM) regimen for ≥ 6 months. Exclusion criteria for all participants included antibiotic or probiotic use within 30 days, current gastrointestinal infection, pregnancy, or lactation. Samples were anonymized as P1–P70 (PWE) and C1–C50 (controls). Comorbidity categories were defined a priori (psychosis, depression, hypertension, cognitive impairment) and recorded from clinical notes where available, otherwise self-report; in the peri-urban PWE cohort, three participants (P1, P7, P17) had documented neuropsychiatric comorbidities (psychosis/schizophrenia).

Clinical and treatment data collection

Demographic and clinical information was collected using a structured questionnaire, including age, sex, epilepsy history, self-reported seizure type(s), current ASMs and dosages, and history of other neurological/psychiatric diagnoses and medications. ASM regimen was categorized as monotherapy (one ASM) or polytherapy (two or more ASMs).

Psychosis-related symptom assessment

Neurobehavioral symptoms were assessed in PWE using a symptom-frequency questionnaire with a two-week recall window. Items were scored on a 5-point frequency scale (0 = never, 1 = less than once weekly, 2 = once or twice weekly, 3 = several times weekly, 4 = nearly every day). Psychosis-related symptom burden was operationalized as the summed score of three items assessing auditory hallucinations, visual hallucinations, and paranoia/suspiciousness (range 0–12), with higher scores indicating more frequent symptoms.

Stool sample collection, transport, and storage

Participants provided approximately 1–2 g of stool in sterile wide-mouthed containers. Samples were transported on ice to the KNUST Microbiology Laboratory within one hour of collection. Specimens were processed aseptically, aliquoted, and stored at − 80 °C until microbiological analysis.

Sample preparation and serial dilution

For culture-based analysis, 1 g of stool was homogenized in 9 mL sterile peptone water (1:10 dilution). Serial 10-fold dilutions were prepared as needed to obtain countable plates for enumeration following the study’s enrichment and selective culture workflow. Because samples underwent enrichment prior to plating, enumeration was interpreted as recoverable CFU/g-equivalents (i.e., CFU recovered under the study pipeline, referenced to gram stool input) rather than absolute stool CFU/g.

Selective culture and recovery of Enterobacteriaceae

To recover Enterobacteriaceae, samples underwent enrichment in Brilliant Green Bile Broth at 35 °C for 18 h prior to subculture onto selective and differential media, including MacConkey agar, Xylose Lysine Deoxycholate (XLD) agar, Bismuth Sulphite agar, and Brilliance™ UTI agar (Oxoid, Thermo Fisher Scientific, UK). Media were prepared according to manufacturer instructions and incubated at 35 °C for 18–24 h. Abundance was expressed as recoverable CFU/g-equivalents under the enrichment selective culture workflow.

Enumeration and presumptive differentiation

Following enrichment and subculture onto selective/differential media, colonies were enumerated using a magnified digital colony counter and recorded as recoverable CFU/g-equivalents. Morphologically distinct colonies were subcultured onto Brilliance™ UTI chromogenic agar for presumptive differentiation prior to MALDI-TOF identification. From each participant, colonies were selected for MALDI-TOF to represent distinct morphotypes (e.g., one colony per morphotype, up to a pre-specified maximum per sample).

For each sample, colony counts were taken from plates within an interpretable counting range (where available) and back-calculated to CFU/g-equivalents using the plated volume and dilution factor, referenced to the original stool input mass (1 g). CFU/g-equivalent = (colonies counted ÷ volume plated [mL]) × dilution factor × (1 ÷ stool input [g]).

MALDI-TOF MS identification and interpretation criteria

Presumptive isolates were transported under cold chain to the Noguchi Memorial Institute for Medical Research and identified using the Bruker MALDI Biotyper system following the manufacturer’s protocol. Spectra were matched against the Bruker reference library to generate log(score) values. Identification confidence was interpreted using manufacturer thresholds: scores ≥ 2.0 were considered high-confidence species-level identification; 1.70–1.99 were considered genus-level identification (probable species); and scores < 1.70 or “no peaks found” were treated as non-identifiable under study conditions. For analyses requiring species-level assignment, only isolates meeting the pre-specified threshold were retained; lower-confidence identifications were excluded or reported at genus level as appropriate.

Outcome definitions

Primary microbiological outcomes were: (i) recoverable Enterobacteriaceae abundance, quantified after enrichment in Brilliant Green Bile Broth and selective/differential culture and reported as recoverable CFU/g-equivalents, defined as colony-forming units recovered after enrichment and selective culture, back-calculated to the gram of stool input, and interpreted as a standardized recoverability measure (not absolute stool bacterial load or absolute stool CFU/g); and (ii) Enterobacteriaceae species richness, defined as the number of distinct MALDI-TOF–confirmed Enterobacteriaceae taxa recovered per participant under the study’s morphotype-based sampling rule. Secondary microbiological outcomes included species-level recoverable abundances (reported as recoverable CFU/g-equivalents) of confirmed taxa. Clinical outcomes included questionnaire-derived psychosis-related symptom scores and documented comorbidity status.

Questionnaire development and validation

The questionnaire used in the study was developed specifically for the present study based on the study objectives and a review of relevant clinical and epidemiological literature. It was reviewed by clinicians and researchers with experience in epilepsy care and public health research to ensure clarity, relevance, and content validity. The questionnaire was pilot-tested among a small group of participants (30) prior to the main study, and minor revisions were made to improve comprehension and consistency. Only items relevant to the study objectives were retained for data collection.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism v 9.0 (GraphPad Software, USA). Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were performed using two-tailed independent-samples t-tests. Associations between microbiological measures and clinical indices were evaluated using Pearson’s correlation. Logistic regression was used to explore species-level predictors of comorbidity in the urban cohort; peri-urban regression was not estimable due to small comorbidity counts and collinearity. Analyses were conducted on complete cases without imputation, with sample sizes reported per analysis. Sensitivity analyses excluding participants with neuropsychiatric comorbidities and restricting to high-confidence species identifications yielded qualitatively similar results. Where multiple species were compared within a defined family of tests, multiplicity control was applied as appropriate (e.g., Bonferroni correction). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval was obtained from the Committee on Human Research, Publications and Ethics (CHRPE), KNUST (CHRPE/AP/669/19). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results

Participant demographic and clinical characteristics

Participant demographic and clinical characteristics by site and epilepsy status are summarized in Table 1. The peri-urban (Tarkwa) cohort comprised 20 PWE and 20 controls, while the urban (KNUST) cohort comprised 50 PWE and 30 controls (total n = 120). Across sites, sex distribution was comparable between PWE and controls (Tarkwa: 65% female in both groups; KNUST: 62.0% female among PWE and 63.3% among controls). Age distribution was broadly similar between groups within sites (Tarkwa: 18–40 years, 75% of PWE vs. 70% of controls; KNUST: 18–40 years, 52% of PWE vs. 50% of controls). Mean systolic blood pressure was higher in PWE than controls at both sites (Tarkwa: 127.2 ± 9.7 vs. 119.2 ± 10.9 mmHg; KNUST: 126.4 ± 11.3 vs. 120.2 ± 9.7 mmHg), with a higher proportion of PWE exceeding 130 mmHg (Tarkwa: 35% vs. 15%; KNUST: 38.0% vs. 16.7%). Among PWE, polytherapy was common in both Tarkwa (90%) and KNUST (80%), and documented comorbidity was more frequent in urban PWE (50%) than peri-urban PWE (15%). Duration on the current ASM regimen was longer in the urban cohort (507.4 ± 233.1 days) than the peri-urban cohort (409.5 ± 46.9days), and mean psychosis-related symptom scores were slightly higher in urban PWE than peri-urban PWE (5.5 ± 1.9 vs. 4.6 ± 2.1).

Setting-dependent recoverable enterobacteriaceae abundance in PWE versus controls differs by site context

A total of 120 adults were analyzed (peri-urban: 20 PWE and 20 controls; urban: 50 PWE and 30 controls). Under the enrichment → selective/differential culture workflow with MALDI-TOF confirmation, recoverable Enterobacteriaceae abundance differed by epilepsy status in a site-dependent manner. In the peri-urban cohort, PWE exhibited significantly lower recoverable abundance than controls (11.20 ± 2.44 vs. 20.95 ± 3.95 recoverable CFU/g-equivalents; p < 0.0001). In contrast, in the urban cohort, PWE exhibited significantly higher recoverable abundance than controls (17.48 ± 4.00 vs. 11.29 ± 1.86 recoverable CFU/g-equivalents; p = 0.002) (Fig. 2A).

Among urban PWE (n = 50), culture-confirmed Enterobacteriaceae species richness was generally high, while species-level recoverable abundances varied markedly across individuals (Fig. 3A). Citrobacter freundii and Klebsiella pneumoniae were recovered from most participants and showed comparatively higher recoverable abundance, whereas Enterobacter hormaechei and Proteus mirabilis were absent in a subset, indicating heterogeneous carriage patterns.

ASM polytherapy is associated with higher psychosis-related symptom scores in urban PWE

Psychosis-related symptom burden was quantified as the summed score of three items (auditory hallucinations, visual hallucinations, paranoia/suspiciousness; range 0–12). In peri-urban PWE, scores did not differ between polytherapy (n = 18) and monotherapy (n = 2) (4.50 ± 2.15 vs. 6.00 ± 2.83; p = 0.37), noting the limited inference due to the very small monotherapy stratum. In urban PWE, polytherapy users (n = 40) had significantly higher psychosis-related symptom scores than monotherapy users (n = 10) (6.10 ± 1.45 vs. 2.90 ± 0.74; p = 1.83 × 10⁻⁸) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

This dual-site cross-sectional study provides culture-based evidence that Enterobacteriaceae-associated signals in epilepsy are setting-dependent within Ghana when assessed using an enrichment → selective/differential culture pipeline with MALDI-TOF confirmation. Specifically, peri-urban PWE demonstrated lower recoverable Enterobacteriaceae abundance than matched controls, whereas urban PWE demonstrated higher recoverable abundance than controls; among PWE, recoverable abundance was also greater in the urban site than the peri-urban site. Species-level contrasts consistently implicated Citrobacter freundii, Proteus mirabilis, and Enterobacter hormaechei, supporting the possibility that epilepsy-associated Gram-negative recoverability patterns vary across exposure contexts even within the same country. Enumeration followed enrichment, therefore, the CFU readout should be interpreted as recoverable CFU/g-equivalents (post-enrichment), a standardized recoverability signal under the study workflow rather than an estimate of absolute stool bacterial load necessitating careful triangulation with sequencing-based microbiome evidence and mechanistic gut–brain axis frameworks [8, 10, 14, 15].

Accordingly, Enterobacteriaceae were used as a pragmatic, biologically relevant, and technically feasible indicator family, rather than a comprehensive proxy for whole-community dysbiosis. While this approach enables species-resolved recovery of inflammatory Gram-negative taxa, it does not capture obligate anaerobes or SCFA-producing taxa that play important roles in gut–brain axis signaling.

Our findings are broadly consistent with prior sequencing-based studies reporting enrichment of Gram-negative and pro-inflammatory taxa in epilepsy. For example [1] and [11], reported increased abundance of inflammatory-associated taxa and altered microbial composition in patients with epilepsy, supporting a potential role of microbiota-associated inflammatory signaling [16]. Similarly, experimental and clinical evidence suggests that Gram-negative bacteria and LPS-mediated pathways may influence neuroinflammation and neuronal excitability [10, 16]. Our identification of Enterobacteriaceae-associated signals aligns with these observations and provides species-resolved evidence from an underrepresented geographic context.

The opposite direction of group differences by site suggests that epilepsy-associated Enterobacteriaceae signals may be context-dependent within the same country, plausibly shaped by exposure gradients (dietary patterns, sanitation/infectious burden, and medication access), host factors, and treatment-related effects that influence which taxa preferentially survive enrichment and grow on selective media. This interpretation is consistent with broader evidence that microbiome composition varies substantially by geography and environment, limiting portability of single “reference” dysbiosis signatures across settings [17, 18]. In addition, medication exposures can restructure gut ecology: large-scale evidence indicates that non-antibiotic drugs can exert measurable and sometimes extensive impacts on gut bacterial taxa, and antibiotic exposure alters both community structure and resistome trajectories, factors that could plausibly influence “recoverability” under enrichment-based workflows [19, 20].

However, in contrast to prior studies that generally report unidirectional expansion of inflammatory taxa in epilepsy, our findings demonstrate context-dependent directional differences, with lower recoverability in peri-urban PWE and higher recoverability in urban PWE compared with controls. This contrast suggests that microbiota–epilepsy relationships may be strongly influenced by environmental, treatment, and socioeconomic context, rather than representing a uniform biological signature. This extends prior work by demonstrating that microbiome-associated signals in epilepsy may not be universally consistent across populations and highlights the importance of contextual investigation, particularly in LMIC settings.

Beyond aggregate recoverable abundance and richness, the species-level patterns suggest that specific Enterobacterales may differentiate groups in a setting-dependent manner.

This aligns with prior microbiome studies demonstrating that species-level variation within Enterobacteriaceae and related Enterobacterales may provide greater discriminatory power than family-level abundance measures, particularly in inflammatory and neurological conditions [21, 22].

The peri-urban comorbidity subgroup showed higher recoverable E. hormaechei among comorbid versus non-comorbid PWE, whereas the urban cohort showed no robust species-level separation by comorbidity. This may reflect limited power in the peri-urban comorbidity subgroup (n = 3), heterogeneity in comorbidity documentation, or the possibility that broader elevation of recoverable Enterobacteriaceae in the urban setting reduces separability between clinical subgroups.

Such context-dependent species-level variability has been observed in prior microbiome studies, where environmental exposures, medication use, and host inflammatory status influence species-specific expansion within Enterobacterales [23, 24].

E. hormaechei is increasingly recognized as a clinically important opportunistic Enterobacterales species, and the signal observed here should be treated as hypothesis-generating pending replication in larger samples and sequencing-based validation [25]. Similarly, given experimental evidence that P. mirabilis can induce neurobiological and behavioral effects in model systems, species-resolved follow-up may be informative when paired with mechanistic biomarkers [26].

Urban PWE receiving polytherapy had substantially higher psychosis-related symptom scores than those receiving monotherapy. Several non-mutually exclusive explanations are plausible. Polytherapy can act as a proxy for greater epilepsy severity or drug resistance (confounding by indication), consistent with ILAE concepts around treatment response [4]. Polytherapy may also increase adverse-effect burden and neuropsychiatric symptoms independent of microbiological measures, aligning with broader work on psychiatric comorbidity in epilepsy and regimen-related burden [6, 15, 27]. Finally, ASM exposure could plausibly co-segregate with gut ecological shifts (directly or indirectly through healthcare access patterns), but the present cross-sectional design cannot establish directionality and requires longitudinal follow-up [19].

In interpreting these setting-dependent microbiological patterns, it is important to consider the clinical profile captured in Table 1. PWE at both sites had higher mean systolic blood pressure and a greater proportion with systolic BP > 130 mmHg than controls, suggesting cardiometabolic differences that may co-vary with medication exposure, stress, diet, and other unmeasured factors.

Cardiometabolic comorbidities, including hypertension, have been increasingly recognized in epilepsy populations and may reflect shared inflammatory pathways, medication effects, and lifestyle-related risk factors [28, 29]. Certain antiseizure medications have been associated with metabolic alterations, vascular risk profiles, and systemic physiological effects that may indirectly influence host–microbiome interactions and inflammatory tone [30, 31].

Urban PWE also had a substantially higher documented comorbidity burden and longer duration on their current ASM regimen, and polytherapy remained common across both sites. These clinical differences may contribute to residual confounding and reinforce the need for longitudinal designs with standardized seizure severity, psychiatric phenotyping, and detailed exposure measurement.

The conceptual models (Figures S6–S7) synthesize these findings into testable pathways rather than mechanistic conclusions. The peri-urban pattern is framed as a “microbial scarcity” signal (lower recoverable Enterobacteriaceae under study conditions), while the urban pattern is framed as “pathogenic dominance” (higher recoverable Enterobacteriaceae under the same workflow). These models align with established microbiota–gut–brain axis frameworks implicating immune activation, barrier integrity, and metabolite-mediated signaling in neurobehavioral outcomes [7, 8, 32–37]. In the urban “pathogenic dominance” framing, an LPS-associated inflammatory pathway is biologically plausible given the endotoxin hypothesis literature and the known capacity of Gram-negative signals to engage innate immune cascades [10, 35–37]. In the peri-urban “microbial scarcity” framing, SCFA-related signaling remains a plausible pathway to evaluate, given evidence that SCFAs influence host immunometabolic tone and gut–brain signaling [38, 39].

However, these conceptual models are hypothesis-generating rather than mechanistically confirmed. Integration of sequencing-based microbiome profiling, inflammatory biomarker measurement, and longitudinal designs will be necessary to validate these proposed pathways and establish causal relationships.

Because epilepsy-relevant neuroinflammation can be mediated at the blood–brain barrier (BBB), and gut microbes/metabolites can influence BBB integrity, future work should incorporate barrier and inflammatory biomarkers to test whether setting-linked recoverability patterns track with barrier disruption or systemic inflammatory tone [16, 40–42]. Importantly, cytokines, permeability indices, and microbial metabolites were not directly measured in this study; therefore, these pathways remain speculative and are presented to guide hypothesis-driven validation [7, 32, 33].

If confirmed, the setting-dependent nature of recoverable Enterobacteriaceae patterns suggests that microbiome-informed epilepsy research and interventions in Ghana may need to account for contextual exposures rather than assuming a uniform dysbiosis profile. This is particularly relevant in LMIC settings where epilepsy burden and treatment gaps remain substantial and where environmental and healthcare-access gradients can shape both epilepsy care and enteric ecology [2, 3, 43]. Given these contextual gradients and our facility-based sampling approach, the extent to which these results generalize beyond the study sites warrants careful consideration.

Accordingly, interpretation and external validity should be approached with caution. This study used a facility-based, dual-site Ghanaian sample (peri-urban Tarkwa and urban Kumasi) and a targeted enrichment → selective/differential culture workflow focused on Enterobacteriaceae; therefore, the results are most directly applicable to adults receiving routine epilepsy care in similar peri-urban and urban Ghanaian settings under comparable exposure and treatment patterns. Participants were recruited from health facilities and key contextual determinants (dietary intake, sanitation, antibiotic exposure history beyond exclusion windows, seizure severity/frequency, and socioeconomic/environmental indicators) were not comprehensively quantified. Therefore, the observed site-dependent patterns may not extrapolate to rural populations, other regions, or settings with different environmental and healthcare-access profiles. These considerations also highlight several important limitations.

Collectively, these findings extend existing epilepsy–microbiome literature by demonstrating that microbiota-associated signals may be context-dependent even within the same country. To our knowledge, this is the first study in sub-Saharan Africa to demonstrate setting-dependent Enterobacteriaceae recoverability patterns in epilepsy using species-resolved culture and MALDI-TOF identification. These results highlight the importance of contextual environmental and treatment factors and underscore the need for geographically diverse microbiome research.

Limitations

Several limitations should be noted. First, the cross-sectional design precludes causal inference and cannot distinguish whether microbial recoverability patterns precede epilepsy-related outcomes or reflect disease and treatment effects. Second, the microbiology workflow relied on enrichment and selective culture targeting Enterobacteriaceae; thus, the results represent recoverable Enterobacteriaceae under the study pipeline rather than whole-community composition. Third, small subgroup sizes (notably peri-urban comorbidity and monotherapy) constrain statistical power and increase uncertainty. Fourth, key confounders (dietary intake, antibiotic exposure beyond exclusion windows, seizure frequency/severity, adherence, and socioeconomic/environmental indicators) were not comprehensively quantified, which may contribute to residual confounding [17, 18, 20]. Additionally, inflammatory cytokines, gut permeability markers, and microbial metabolites such as SCFAs were not measured, limiting mechanistic interpretation of the observed recoverability patterns. As a result, the conceptual microbial scarcity and pathogenic dominance models should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating frameworks rather than confirmed biological mechanisms.

Finally, Psychosis-related symptom assessment relied on a study-specific structured questionnaire rather than standardized diagnostic psychiatric instruments. Although the tool underwent pilot testing and expert review, it was designed for exploratory phenotyping rather than clinical diagnosis. Future studies should incorporate validated psychiatric assessment tools administered by trained clinicians to enable more precise characterization of neuropsychiatric comorbidities.

Future directions

Future studies should prioritize (i) larger multi-site cohorts with standardized psychiatric phenotyping and seizure severity measures; (ii) sequencing-based profiling (16 S and/or metagenomics) to capture anaerobes and functional pathways; (iii) longitudinal sampling to clarify temporal relationships among microbiome signals, ASM exposure, seizure control, and symptom trajectories; and (iv) integration of biomarkers aligned with the conceptual models (e.g., inflammatory mediators, LPS-related indices, BBB/barrier integrity markers, and SCFA/metabolite panels) [32–34]. Replication of the site-dependent species-level signals particularly the peri-urban E. hormaechei association should be pre-specified to reduce false-positive risk [22]. Additionally, future work should extend beyond targeted Enterobacteriaceae recovery to incorporate sequencing-based profiling of obligate anaerobes and SCFA-producing taxa, particularly within Firmicutes and related functional groups. This will enable comprehensive assessment of microbiome structure, functional metabolic pathways, and their relationship to epilepsy phenotypes across contextual environments.

Conclusions

This dual-site study suggests that recoverable Enterobacteriaceae patterns in Ghanaian adults with epilepsy differ by setting under enrichment and selective culture conditions. In the peri-urban cohort, PWE had lower recoverable Enterobacteriaceae abundance than controls, whereas in the urban cohort PWE had higher recoverable abundance than controls. Urban PWE also had a higher documented comorbidity burden, and ASM polytherapy was associated with higher psychosis-related symptom scores in the urban site. Collectively, these findings support the possibility of context-dependent dysbiosis signatures in epilepsy and motivate larger, longitudinal, sequencing-based studies integrating standardized clinical phenotyping and environmental exposure measurement to clarify causality and separate microbial associations from treatment- and context-related confounding.
