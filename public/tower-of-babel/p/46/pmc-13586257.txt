Recent advances in the development of GRK2 inhibitors: blocking the interaction of GRK2 with its partners
Authors: Chen-chen Han, Hao-zhou Guo, Fu-yuan Guo, Hong-zheng Tan, Hua-zhan Sun, Jin-tao Gu, Shu-jun Zuo, Wei-kang Wang, Yang Ma, Wei Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
G protein-coupled receptor kinase 2 (GRK2) is a key player in signal transduction and contains an N-terminus, a kinase domain (KD) and a C-terminus. Changes in the configuration of GRK2 regulate its allosteric effects, including KD closure and protein binding, and the interaction between the KD and the pleckstrin homology (PH) domain modulates the kinase activity of GRK2. GRK2 functions as a central molecule within a complex interactome, interacting with various cytoplasmic or membrane proteins in addition to G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs), and plays a significant role in several GRK2 dysfunction-related diseases, including arthritis and cardiovascular disorders. Recently, GRK2 inhibitors—including peptide-based inhibitors, RNA aptamers, and small-molecule compounds—have been developed on the basis of targeted blockade of the GRK2–Gαq interface, GRK2–Gβγ interaction, and GRK2 KD–substrate binding, offering hope for the treatment of GRK2 dysfunction-related diseases. However, owing to the unfavourable properties of some small-molecule inhibitors and rapid degradation of peptides, the entry of these GRK2 inhibitors into clinical trials is limited. Understanding the regulation, activity, levels, or specific interactions of GRK2 is crucial for overcoming these challenges and optimizing the clinical use of GRK2 inhibitors. Our current review discusses the structure and function of GRK2, the research progress on GRK2 inhibitors and the regulatory role of GRK2 inhibitors in GRK2 dysfunction-related diseases to provide a significant theoretical basis for extensive research on GRK2-targeted therapies.

Introduction

G protein-coupled receptor kinase 2 (GRK2), a significant Ser/Thr kinase, regulates various biological effects in novel ways and plays a pivotal role in multiple GRK2 dysfunction-related diseases [1]. The N-terminal domain of GRK2 is modestly conserved and plays a general role in receptor recognition. This domain comprises an N-terminal α-helix (αN) and a regulator of G protein signalling (RGS) homology (RH) domain. The kinase domain (KD) of GRK2 is a highly conserved central domain containing an essential amino acid, Lys220, which determines kinase activity. The RH domain and KD of GRK2 are in close contact, establishing a structural basis for allosteric effects, including KD closure and protein binding. The C-terminal domain is highly variable and is responsible for subcellular localization and agonist-dependent translocation to the plasma membrane [2]. GRK2 adopts two major conformations, an open (inactive) state and a closed (active) state, which are allosterically regulated by the binding of distinct partner proteins, such as Gαq to the RH domain [3], Gβγ to the pleckstrin homology (PH) domain of the C-terminus [4], and substrates to the KD [5, 6]. This selective molecular recognition pattern not only links the open/closed conformational states to specific protein interactions but also provides a structural basis for targeting various GRK2 interaction interfaces, such as the GRK2–Gαq interface, the GRK2–Gβγ interaction, and the KD–substrate interface [1, 7].

Given these distinct interaction interfaces, the ability of GRK2 to engage multiple partners is central to its pathophysiological role in certain diseases. GRK2 is ubiquitously expressed in different cells and remains inactive in the cytosol. Growing evidence indicates that GRK2 can act as a central molecule in an intricate interactome, interacting with multiple cytoplasmic or membrane proteins in addition to G-protein coupled receptors (GPCRs) and having broader regulatory effects on various signalling networks and cellular functions [8]. Allosteric regulation of GRK2 is closely linked to its subcellular localization and binding partners. Therefore, targeting the interaction between GRK2 and its partners represents a promising and novel strategy for treating GRK2 dysfunction-related diseases. Such inhibitors include cyclic peptides, peptides, RNA aptamers, and small-molecule compounds. They regulate cell functions and alleviate disease by modulating Gαq- and Gβγ-mediated signalling pathways and inhibiting the interaction of GRK2 with its partners [9, 10]. However, owing to the adverse properties of certain small-molecule inhibitors and the rapid degradation of peptides, their entry into clinical trials has been hindered. A thorough analysis of the opportunities and challenges associated with the use of GRK2 inhibitors could effectively translate these findings from research laboratories to clinical settings.

Therefore, this review aimed to provide a comprehensive analysis of the structure and function of GRK2, the research progress on GRK2 inhibitors and the regulatory role of GRK2 inhibitors in GRK2 dysfunction-related diseases. This analysis could offer a significant theoretical basis for extensive research on GRK2-targeted therapies.

Atomic structure and dynamic conformational states of GRK2

GRK2 (also known as βARK1, β-adrenergic receptor kinase 1) was originally purified from the bovine brain [11]. In mammals, GRK2 is located on chromosome 11, making the gene encoding this protein a promising candidate gene for human diseases linked to the 11q13.2 locus. The 3’ untranslated region of the human GRK2 clone contains approximately 125 bp, and the 3’ localization and high degree of conservation of this region suggest that it may play a regulatory role in the translation of GRK2 [12]. GRK2 cDNA encodes a protein of 689 amino acids and consists of a single 80 kDa subunit with a protein kinase catalytic domain that has the greatest sequence similarity to protein kinase C (PKC) and cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)-dependent protein kinase [13].

Crystal structure analysis revealed that the GRK2 protein consists of N-terminal, kinase, and C-terminal domains, which play pivotal roles in the open (inactive) and closed (active) states of the GRK2 kinase (Fig. 1). The N-terminal domain (Met1–Ile184) is crucial for receptor recognition and intracellular membrane anchoring. This domain contains an N-terminal 20-residue segment with a helical structure that is essential for receptor phosphorylation and the homologous region of the RGS family of proteins, known as the RH domain [14, 15] (Fig. 1a). The RH domain specifically localizes GRK2 to the membrane and is characterized by a bilobal architecture. One lobe comprises helices α4–α7 arranged in an antiparallel manner to form the GRK2 RH bundle subdomain, while the other lobe consists of helices α1–α3 and α8–α9, forming the terminal subdomain [3] (Fig. 1b). Together, these subdomains create a typical V-shaped notch with two ends, forming contact surfaces with the adjacent small and large lobes of the KD [16]. The KD (His185–Ile513) is located within a loop between the α9 and α10 helices of the RH domain (Fig. 1a). This domain contains key amino acid sites (Lys220) that determine kinase activity and several general functional domains that serve as the main binding sites of GRK2 inhibitors [17]. The KD possesses a bilobal fold with a small lobe (186–272 aa and 496–513 aa) and a large lobe (273–475 aa), extending into the conserved core called the C-terminal tail (C-tail). The small lobe features a canonical five-strand antiparallel β-sheet (designated β1–β5) and three α-helices (αB, αC, and αK). The β1–β2 turn forms the phosphate-binding glycine-rich loop (P-loop) of the small lobe, which directly binds to the triphosphate tail of ATP when the kinase is in its active state (fully closed KD), stabilizing the phosphorylation transition state. The larger lobe of GRK2 consists of a four-strand antiparallel β-sheet (β6–β9) situated under the interlobe hinge region (272–275 aa) and seven α-helices (αD–αJ). The C-tail is inserted between the αJ and αK helices of the KD, particularly in the AST region (474–498 aa) of the C-tail, which contributes to the nucleotide and phosphoacceptor binding sites, and controls the entry and exit of nucleotides in the kinase active site, a feature also known as the nucleotide gate. It plays a crucial role in regulating kinase activity (Fig. 1b). In the absence of nucleotides, the C-tail is usually disordered; however, when nucleotides are bound and the KD is closed, the C-tail becomes integral to the ATP-binding site [16, 18].

The C-terminal domain (Ser514–Leu689) facilitates subcellular localization and agonist-dependent translocation through binding to lipids and other proteins (Fig. 1a). This domain includes a PH domain with binding sites for membrane-binding molecules that directly influence the intrinsic kinase activity and translocation of GRK2 [19, 20]. The PH domain (553–661 aa) consists of a canonical seven-strand antiparallel β-sheet (designated β1–β7) and α helices of the C-terminal (αCT) domain (637–659 aa), which is an extended region of the α9 and α10 helices of the RH domain (514–549 aa) (Fig. 1b). Studies have shown that the αCT domain alone is sufficient for membrane association, while the N-terminal domain aids in targeting GRK2 to the cell membrane, enhancing receptor phosphorylation. The β1 sheet and αC helices of the PH domain form a domain interface with the α1 and α9 helices of the RH terminal subdomain, establishing a close relationship between these two domains. This interaction provides a structural basis for allosteric regulation, including receptor binding and KD closure [4].

GRK2 adopts at least two major conformations: an open (inactive) state and a closed (active) state (Fig. 1c). In the absence of agonist stimulation, GRK2 predominantly exists in an open conformation, characterized by a disordered N-terminus, conformational flexibility of the PH domain, and a poorly ordered AST region. After ATP binding, the KD of GRK2 becomes partially closed, and the AST region becomes partially ordered, but the N-terminal domain remains disordered [5]. Furthermore, binding of substrate proteins (G proteins, GPCRs, cytosolic proteins, etc.) to GRK2 allosterically induces kinase closure. For instance, Gαq specifically binds to the RH domain of GRK2. This interaction can occur when GRK2 is in the open conformation, thereby achieving kinase activity-independent negative feedback inhibition (Fig. 1c) [3]. The binding of the Gβγ subunit to the PH domain of GRK2 favours the closed conformation of the KD, acts as a potent allosteric activator of GRK2, and is required for its translocation from the cytosol to the plasma membrane (Fig. 1c) [4]. Substrate protein binding involves both the RH domain and the KD of GRK2. This induces large-scale domain rearrangements, including the interaction of the N-terminal region with the KD; the RH and PH domains moving closer together and promoting closure of the KD around ATP and the substrate; and the full ordering of the AST region coupled with closure of the KD (Fig. 1c) [6]. This selective molecular recognition pattern based on the distinct domains of GRK2 establishes a link between the open/closed conformational states of GRK2 and its selective interactions with different binding molecules and provides a structural basis for the elucidation of how different classes of inhibitors target these specific interaction interfaces, including the GRK2–Gαq interface, the GRK2–Gβγ interaction and the GRK2 KD–substrate interaction [1, 7].

Binding partners and functional roles of GRK2

The allosteric regulation of GRK2 relies on its interaction with multiple proteins, including membrane-binding proteins, cytosolic proteins, transcription factors, and nuclear proteins. The different functional domains of GRK2 can activate or inhibit signal transduction in cells by binding to these proteins, which may then regulate the expression, activity, and degradation of GRK2.

GRK2 as a feedback inhibitor in Gαq-coupled signalling

The RH domain of GRK2 interacts specifically with Gαq family members, such as Gαq, Gα11, and Gα14 [21]. Gαq signalling involves various signalling molecules on the cell membrane and cytoplasm, including receptors, GRK2, free Gβγ subunits, activated Gαq subunits, phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate (PIP2), PKC, calmodulin (CaM), and c-Src. Regulating Gαq signalling is essential for the GPCR crosstalk mediated by GRKs and the secretion of numerous cytokines, chemokines, growth factors, hormones, and neurotransmitters [22]. GRK2 is a negative feedback protein that inhibits the Gq-mediated pathway by interacting with several signalling molecules (Fig. 2). GRK2 binds to and phosphorylates specific serine and threonine residues on Gαq-coupled receptors and recruits β-arrestin (β-arr) and Src, thereby initiating extracellular signal-regulated kinase (ERK) signalling through the scaffolding of mitogen-activated protein kinase (MAPK), leading to the targeting of the signalling complex to endosomal vesicles [23]. Additionally, GRK2 binds to and isolates the activated Gαq subunit independent of its kinase activity, thereby inhibiting PLCβ activity [24]. GRK2 and PIP2 also participate in an early negative feedback loop by activating GRK2, which binds to activated Gαq-coupled receptors, thereby shutting down Gαq protein signalling and inhibiting receptor activity [25]. CaM binds and sequesters GRK2, thereby competing with receptors for GRK2 binding, with Ca2+ increasing this interaction [26]. PKC activates GRK2 by phosphorylating serine 29, thereby counteracting the inhibitory effect of Ca2+/CaM on GRK2 activity and inhibiting Gαq-dependent PLCβ1 activity [27]. c-Src phosphorylates Tyr13, 86, and 92 of GRK2, increasing its kinase activity towards GPCRs and increasing interactions between the RH domain and Gαq subunits, thus leading to a rapid switch-off of Gαq-mediated signalling and a consequent decrease in Gαq signalling to PLCβ [28]. Numerous signalling molecules provide feedback to GRK2, which subsequently deactivates the receptor and Gαq. Translocation of GRK2 to the plasma membrane by the Gβγ protein

As previously mentioned, the N-terminal RH region of GRK2 mediates Gq signal transduction and plays a crucial role in receptor recognition and intracellular anchoring. The Gβγ subunit may also be linked to the plasma membrane through cysteine residues at the C-terminus. Furthermore, upon receptor activation, free Gβγ recruits GRK2 from the cytoplasm to the plasma membrane by binding to the PH domain of GRK2 (Fig. 3). Therefore, GRK2 is considered an essential protein for the allosteric activation of receptors. Additionally, Gβγ, PIP2, and phosphatidylserine (PS) are known as membrane-binding molecules. PIP2/PS cooperate synergistically with Gβγ by binding to the PH domain of GRK2, promoting its activation and localization to the plasma membrane upon receptor activation [25]. This cooperation promotes receptor phosphorylation and desensitization. Further research has indicated that multiple proteins can influence Gβγ-mediated receptor phosphorylation and desensitization. For instance, protein kinase A (PKA) phosphorylates GRK2 at Ser685, increasing the interaction between Gβγ and GRK2. This leads to increased GPCR phosphorylation, homologous desensitization, and internalization [29]. AKAP79, a PKA-anchoring protein, regulates the formation of a complex involving PKA and its receptor at the membrane. AKAP79 also regulates the ability of GRK2 to phosphorylate agonist-occupied receptors by interacting with PKA, thereby contributing to increased receptor phosphorylation and desensitization [30]. Phosphodiesterase-4 (PDE4) also modulates the effect of PKA on GRK2, preventing inappropriate membrane translocation of GRK2 in unstimulated cells by controlling PKA phosphorylation due to fluctuations in basal levels of cAMP [31]. GRK2 regulates the phosphorylation-dependent and phosphorylation-independent desensitization of GPCRs through direct interactions

GRK2 is a commonly expressed enzyme that is primarily responsible for phosphorylating GPCRs in the presence of appropriate agonists. The amino acids in the third intracellular loop (ICL3) and C-tail, especially serine/threonine residues, are essential for GRK2-mediated phosphorylation [33]. Receptor phosphorylation by GRK2 induces conformational changes and dissociation of the heterotrimeric G protein, promoting arrestin binding. This process switches off G protein-mediated signalling and mediates GPCR desensitization, internalization, and downregulation. GRK2 plays a pivotal role in the phosphorylation-dependent desensitization of various GPCRs, such as β2-adrenergic receptor (β2-AR), C-C chemokine receptors 2/4 (CCR2/4), μ-opioid receptor (MOR), M2 muscarinic cholinergic receptor (mAChR), and metabotropic glutamate receptor (mGluR) 3 [34].

Additionally, GRK2 mediates the phosphorylation-independent desensitization of several GPCRs, including thromboxane A2 receptor (TXA2R), M1 and M3 muscarinic receptors, parathyroid hormone (PTH) receptors, thyrotropin-releasing hormone (TRH) receptors, and angiotensin Ⅱ (Ang Ⅱ) receptors, as well as mGluR1 and β3-adrenergic receptor (β3-AR) [34]. This regulation depends mainly on the interaction of GRK2 with receptors and Gαq, particularly the RH domain in the GRK2 N-terminus. The N-terminal helix forms an interdomain bridge crucial for maintaining GRK2 in a catalytically competent state [35]. GRK2 also participates in the phosphorylation-dependent and phosphorylation-independent desensitization of various GPCRs, including mGluR5 and endothelin A receptors (ETAR) [36–39].

GRK2 phosphorylates non-GPCR substrates and engages in a protein-binding network

Phosphorylation of diverse non-GPCR substrates by GRK2

GRK2 not only regulates the functions of GPCRs but also mediates the phosphorylation of other membrane receptor families and nonplasma membrane receptor substrates. GRK2 modulates signalling mediated by other membrane receptor families, such as insulin-like growth factor-1 receptor (IGF-1R), epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR), platelet-derived growth factor receptor-β (PDGFRβ), and epithelial Na+ channels (ENaC) [40–43]. In addition, GRK2 phosphorylates smoothened (Smo) and cluster of differentiation 36 (CD36), thereby regulating embryonic development and metabolic homoeostasis in the failing heart [44, 45]. GRK2 binds to and phosphorylates these membrane receptors, influencing receptor signalling, desensitization, and degradation, thereby affecting cell chemotaxis and proliferation. GRK2 acts as a multifunctional signalling regulator that binds to and phosphorylates a variety of nonplasma membrane receptor substrates, including cytoplasmic and nuclear proteins, and participates in multiple physiological and pathological processes, including cardiovascular disease pathogenesis, metabolism, inflammation, fibrosis, cancer, and mitochondrial homoeostasis (Table 1 and Fig. 4). First, GRK2-mediated phosphorylation of neural precursor cell expressed, developmentally down-regulated protein 4-2 (Nedd4-2) at Thr466 activates ENaC, thereby enhancing sodium reabsorption and blood pressure control [46], and GRK2 participates in the occurrence of human atherosclerosis through the phosphorylation and stabilization of DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1) [47]. Second, GRK2 directly modulates inflammatory signalling by phosphorylating nuclear factor-kappaB (NF‑κB) inhibitor alpha (IκBα), which increases NF‑κB activity and drives proinflammatory cytokine production [48]. GRK2 phosphorylates and inactivates p38MAPK at Thr123 to limit inflammatory responses [49]. It also phosphorylates pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2) at Ser406 to inhibit aerobic glycolysis [50]. Third, GRK2 regulates key cancer-related pathways through the phosphorylation and activation of histone deacetylase 6 (HDAC6) [51, 52], phosphorylation of human antigen R (HuR) at Thr142 to promote hypoxia-inducible factor 1-alpha (HIF‑1α) nuclear accumulation under hypoxic conditions [53], phosphorylation of tumour protein 53 (p53) at Thr55 to promote its degradation [54], and phosphorylation of snail family transcriptional repressor 1 (Snail1) at Ser90 to weaken its function and inhibit epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition [55]. It also phosphorylates adenomatous polyposis coli protein (APC) to inhibit Wnt signalling and phosphorylates mammalian STE20-like protein kinase 2 (MST2) to mediate centrosome disjunction, regulating the cell cycle and suppressing tumour growth [56, 57]. Fourth, GRK2 phosphorylates Smad2/3 to negatively regulate transforming growth factor-beta (TGF-β) signalling, thereby influencing fibrosis and cancer progression [58]. Fifth, GRK2 phosphorylates substrate proteins involved in metabolic regulation: it phosphorylates insulin receptor substrate 1 (IRS1) at Ser307, impairing insulin signalling and glucose transporter type 4 (GLUT4) translocation [59], whereas phosphorylation of adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (AMPK) at Ser485 leads to its inactivation, promoting hepatic gluconeogenesis [60]. Phosphorylation-independent protein interaction network of GRK2

In addition to the phosphorylation-dependent processes mentioned above, GRK2 regulates cellular responses and the development of various GRK2 dysfunction-related diseases in a phosphorylation-independent manner by binding to several membrane proteins, cytoplasmic proteins, nuclear proteins and mitochondrial proteins. GRK2 is involved in an extensive protein–protein interaction network that operates independently of its kinase activity. These phosphorylation-independent interactions establish GRK2 as a versatile hub that integrates signals from diverse cellular compartments to regulate cardiovascular diseases, inflammation, fibrosis, tumour progression, and mitochondrial homoeostasis (Table 2 and Fig. 4). First, GRK2 regulates GPCR trafficking and cardiovascular function through interactions with endocytic and signalling proteins. GRK2 binds to clathrin and caveolin via its C-terminus to promote or inhibit receptor internalization, respectively [62, 63]. It also interacts with PKCβ1 to downregulate its kinase activity, with protein kinase B (AKT) to inhibit AKT activation and nitric oxide (NO) production and with phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) to promote receptor internalization, all of which play important roles in cardiovascular disease, insulin resistance, and cancer [64–66]. Moreover, GRK2 interacts with poly(A) binding protein cytoplasmic 1 (PABPC1) to influence ADH1 mRNA stability under oxidative stress [67]. Second, GRK2 modulates immune and inflammatory signalling through interactions with multiple membrane and cytoplasmic proteins. GRK2 binds to the T cell receptor (TCR) to promote T-cell activation and cytokine production and interacts with tumour necrosis factor receptor-associated factor 2 (TRAF2) to induce prostaglandin E2 receptor 4 (EP4) desensitization, reducing cAMP production and thereby influencing inflammatory responses [68, 69]. GRK2 also binds to Hedgehog receptor patched 1 (PTCH1), alleviating its inhibition of Cyclin1 nuclear translocation, which affects Hedgehog signalling and developmental processes [70]. GRK2 forms a complex with peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma (PPARγ) to inhibit Flt-1-mediated angiogenesis, thereby affecting rheumatoid arthritis (RA) pannus formation and tumour angiogenesis [71]. It interacts with NF‑κB1 p105 to negatively regulate ERK activation, thereby achieving context-dependent modulation of cell proliferation and migration [72], and with salvador homologue-1 (SAV1) to promote its degradation, leading to synovial hyperplasia in RA [73]. GRK2 binds to Janus kinase 1 (JAK1) to inhibit JAK1–STAT3 signalling, reducing keratinocyte hyperproliferation in psoriasis [74]. It also binds to Zinc finger protein 36-like 1 (ZFP36L1) to facilitate antibody-secreting cell migration [75], and its expression is negatively regulated by Zinc finger and BTB domain containing 16 (ZBTB16) binding to the GRK2 promoter, influencing osteoarthritis (OA) and inflammatory diseases [76]. Third, GRK2 interacts with the long noncoding RNA (lncRNA) antisense non-coding RNA in the INK4 locus (ANRIL) to activate the PI3K/AKT/BCL-2 pathway, promoting cell proliferation and inhibiting apoptosis in gastric cancer (GC) and leukaemia [77], and with the paracaspase mucosa-associated lymphoid tissue lymphoma translocation protein 1 (MALT1), suppressing its protease activity and thereby influencing lymphomagenesis and autoimmune responses [78]. GRK2 fine-tunes ERK/MAPK signalling through interactions with scaffold and regulatory proteins. GRK2 interacts with MAPK/ERK kinase (MEK) to negatively regulate ERK activation and with GRK-interacting protein 1 (GIT1) to promote ERK activation, thereby achieving context-dependent modulation of cell proliferation and migration [79, 80]. Fourth, GRK2 is involved in oxidative stress, fibrosis, and tissue remodelling. GRK2 directly associates with NADPH oxidase 4 (NOX4), promoting reactive oxygen species production and contributing to renal fibrosis and cisplatin-induced injury [81]. GRK2 interacts with TGF-β1 to synergistically promote cardiac hypertrophy and fibrosis [82]. Fifth, GRK2 influences mitochondrial function and dynamics. GRK2 deletion reduces dynamin related protein 1 (Drp1) expression and improves flap function and mitochondrial function after ischaemia-reperfusion injury [83]. GRK2 also interacts with synaptojanin 2 binding protein (SYNJ2BP) to promote receptor phosphorylation, affecting cell signalling [84]. Additionally, GRK2 binds to heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) to regulate embryonic cleavage and AKT activation during development [85], and interacts with encephalopsin and melanopsin (Opn3/4) to modulate photorelaxation responses in vascular tissue [86]. Regulation of GRK2 activation, expression, and degradation

The functional interactions of GRK2 with various proteins regulate its activation, expression, and degradation. As stated above, the phosphorylation of GRK2 at serine (S29, S685) and tyrosine (Y13, Y86, Y92, Y553, Y651) residues by PKC, c-Src, PKA, PDGFRβ, and EGFR increases its catalytic activity [28, 30, 42, 88, 89]. S-nitrosylation of GRK2 at Cys340 by NO and S-nitrosothiols (SNOs) represents a critical mechanism through which GRK2 activity is inhibited [90]. The regulation of GRK2 stability may play a crucial role in modulating its expression levels, with the mechanism of GRK2 degradation being the most widely studied. Recent studies have shown that GRK2 degradation requires kinase activity and is facilitated by GPCR activation and Mdm2-mediated ubiquitination [91]. Eukaryotic initiation factor 3 d (EIF3d) and the lncRNA urothelial carcinoma-associated 1 (UCA1) promote GRK2 ubiquitination and degradation [92, 93].

Development of GRK2 inhibitors

The activity of GRK2, a crucial member of the AGC kinase family, is regulated by conformational changes. These changes are influenced by dynamic combinations of its αN-helix, RH, and PH domains and activator partners, such as agonist-induced GPCRs, Gβγ subunits, and PIP2. This process ultimately leads to allosteric rearrangements affecting the function of the relevant AST loop and KD closure [17, 94]. Dysregulated GRK2 activity and expression may be involved in the development of GRK2 dysfunction-related diseases. Therefore, various potential strategies to inhibit GRK2 function are being developed on the basis of its structural features and regulatory mechanisms. GRK2 inhibitors include peptide-based inhibitors, RNA aptamers, and small-molecule compounds. They act via distinct mechanisms, including blockade of the GRK2–Gαq interface, GRK2–Gβγ interaction, and GRK2 KD–substrate binding. In addition to their distinct mechanisms of action, GRK2 inhibitors also have key parameters, such as potency (IC₅₀) and development phase. Targeted inhibition of the GRK2–Gαq interface

As detailed above, the N-terminal domain of GRK2 not only mediates the inhibition of Gαq signalling but also plays a crucial role in regulating kinase activity. The extreme amino terminus is essential for efficient receptor phosphorylation and may facilitate the structural transition from the inactive state to the active state of the kinase. Therefore, targeting the RGS domain to attenuate pathological Gαq signalling is a promising approach for GRK2-targeted therapy. The overexpression of peptide βARKrgs (50–145 aa) derived from the RGS domain in the N-terminus of GRK2 indicates that its beneficial effect is mediated by the inhibition of Gαq rather than the direct inhibition of GRK2. The peptide βARKrgs may serve as a prototype for developing effective drugs to prevent Gαq-mediated cardiac hypertrophy in animal models [95]. However, as a peptide-based agent, its clinical translation faces substantial hurdles, including poor oral bioavailability, rapid proteolytic degradation, and the requirement for gene delivery approaches (Table 3) [95–97].

Targeted inhibition of the GRK2–Gβγ interaction

Gβγ subunits modulate most signalling pathways, especially GRK2 translocation; thus, an inhibitor that selectively targets a specific subset of Gβγ interactions is needed for Gβγ–GRK2 targeting [98]. The first reported approach involved the utilization of a recombinant C-terminal domain of GRK2 (βARKct), comprising 195 amino acid residues (495–689 aa), including the Gβγ binding domain, which presumably serves as an inhibitor of Gβγ–GRK2 interactions. Therefore, the peptide βARKct can block the membrane translocation and activation of GRK2 [99]. Although βARKct gene therapy has demonstrated cardiac protection and functional recovery in multiple animal models of heart failure (HF), its reliance on viral vector-mediated delivery (e.g., AAV6-βARKct) represents a major limitation for clinical application [100, 101]. Both βARKct and βARKrgs remain at the preclinical stage, with no approved human applications to date (Table 3) [10].

Virtual screening was performed to identify small molecules capable of binding the Gβγ subunit, one of which, termed M119, demonstrated high apparent affinity for the Gβγ subunit. M119 acts as a specific inhibitor of a particular subset of Gβγ-mediated signalling, and its highly homologous and more stable analogue, gallein, interferes with Gβγ binding to GRK2, thereby inhibiting GRK2 membrane localization and Gβγ-mediated GPCR phosphorylation [98, 102]. These interventions play a vital role in the recovery of failed myocyte function, the prevention of cardiac dysfunction [103, 104], and the alleviation of OA [105]. However, because Gβγ subunits are involved in numerous signalling pathways in addition to GRK2, these inhibitors lack selectivity for the Gβγ–GRK2 interaction. Consequently, off-target effects remain a significant concern [106]. Both M119 and gallein are currently at the preclinical stage (Table 3) [10, 103].

Targeted inhibition of GRK2-substrate binding

Studies have reported that the use of a small peptide encompassing parts of the αF–αG helix (383-KLLRGHSP-390) in the KD of GRK2 (referred to as the HJ loop) represents a novel strategy for inhibiting GRK2. This peptide can prevent the conformational shifts required for kinase–substrate binding in several protein kinases, resulting in reduced substrate phosphorylation and selective inhibition of the kinase [107]. New cyclic peptides, including KRX-C7 and KRX-683, were developed through side chain cyclization on the basis of the HJ loop of linear peptides. Compared with their parent linear peptides, these cyclic peptides exhibit greater stability, selectivity, and inhibitory activity in cellular models. Further research has indicated that these cyclic peptides have hypoglycaemic effects in animal models of type 2 diabetes [108]. Despite their improved pharmacological properties, these cyclic peptide inhibitors are still in the preclinical stage (Table 3) [10, 107]

A novel class of inhibitors called RNA aptamers may be established to inhibit GRK2; in particular, the C13 aptamer selectively inhibits the kinase activity of GRK2. The RNA aptamer C13 binds to the large lobe of the KD and remodels the basic regions of GRK2, increasing its interaction with the polyanionic phosphodiester backbone of RNA [109]. An adenine nucleotide in a hairpin loop of the RNA molecule C13 is localized to the ATP binding pocket of GRK2, whereas other regions of C13 interact with the basic αF–αG helicoidal regions in the large lobe of the KD to exert inhibitory effects on GRK2 [110]. Therefore, the binding mechanism of the RNA aptamer to the large lobe of GRK2 represents the mechanism of inhibition by polyanions. The RNA aptamer C13 inhibits GRK2 with an IC₅₀ of approximately 4.1 nM and GRK5 with an IC₅₀ of approximately 79 nM [109]. Despite its exceptional potency and selectivity in vitro, C13 has not yet been tested in vivo in animal models, and the practical challenges associated with RNA-based therapeutics—including stability, delivery, and cost—hinder its clinical translation (Table 3) [109, 111].

Additionally, certain small-molecule compounds inhibit the activity of GRK2 by stabilizing its inactive conformation. This class of compounds attaches linearly, occupying the adenine, ribose, polyphosphate, and hydrophobic subsites on GRK2, thereby inhibiting GRK2 with nanomolar potency. Examples include the natural products balanol and Takeda inhibitors, such as CMPD101 and 103 A [17, 112]. Balanol, a fungal metabolite, acts as a nonselective ATP-competitive inhibitor of AGC family kinases. It was revealed to inhibit GRK2 with an IC₅₀ of approximately 42 nM but also potently inhibit GRK5 (IC₅₀ = 160 nM). The lack of selectivity limits the utility of balanol for specific GRK2 targeting [113]. In contrast, the Takeda inhibitors (CMPD101 and CMPD103A) are more selective. Both compounds are potent, highly selective, and membrane‑permeable small‑molecule inhibitors of GRK2, with IC₅₀ values of 290 nM and 54 nM, respectively. Notably, neither CMPD101 nor CMPD103A inhibited GRK5 at concentrations up to 125 μM [112]. These inhibitors can inhibit adrenergic, neurogenic, and nonadrenergic smooth muscle contractions in the human prostate [114]. However, Takeda inhibitors remain at the preclinical stage, and challenges in avoiding CYP3A4 inhibition have been noted during their optimization (Table 3) [112, 115, 116].

Paroxetine, an FDA-approved selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor (SSRI), was later identified as a GRK2 inhibitor. The inhibitory effect of paroxetine on GRK2 was weaker than that of balanol or Takeda inhibitors, with an IC₅₀ value of 20 μM, and paroxetine exhibited up to 50-fold greater selectivity for GRK2 than for other GRKs [117]. The benzodioxole ring of paroxetine forms one conventional and one carbon–oxygen hydrogen bond with the hinge, while its piperidine ring mimics the large lobe interaction seen with the azepane ring of balanol. Paroxetine binds to the active site of GRK2, thereby stabilizing its KD in a novel conformation [118]. The paroxetine analogue Compound 14as is highly potent against GRK2, with an IC₅₀ of 30 nM and significant selectivity over GRK5 (IC₅₀ = 7.09 μM) [119]. CCG258208 (also known as GRK2-IN-1), a further optimized analogue, has an IC₅₀ of 30 nM for GRK2, with 240‑fold selectivity over GRK5. CCG258747 is a selective GRK2 inhibitor with an IC₅₀ of 18 nM and is highly selective for GRK2 over GRK5 (83-fold) [120]. It has been reported that paroxetine and its analogues attenuate IgE-mediated anaphylaxis [121], alleviate cardiac hypertrophy and HF [122–124], and modulate the differentiation and activation of immune cells and abnormal proliferation of fibroblast-like synoviocytes (FLS) in patients with arthritis through the downregulation of GRK2–EP4 interactions [73, 125, 126]. Paroxetine also prevents pathological cardiac remodelling by inhibiting the GRK2-mediated IκBα/NF-κB signalling pathway [127]. Notably, a phase III clinical trial (NCT06231745) is being conducted to evaluate paroxetine as an adjunct to conventional disease-modifying antirheumatic drug (DMARD) therapy in patients with RA [128]. However, as an SSRI, paroxetine has intrinsic central nervous system effects that may limit its utility in nonpsychiatric indications [129]. Its analogues remain at the preclinical stage (Table 3).

Our group has made numerous significant contributions to the field of GRK2-targeted therapy, including the identification of a novel compound, paeoniflorin-6′-O-benzene sulphonate (CP-25), which has inhibitory effects on GRK2 (Chinese invention patent: ZL201210030616.4). CP-25 is an esterified modification of paeoniflorin, an active ingredient extracted from the roots of Paeonia lactiflora known for its anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory properties [130]. CP-25 inhibits GRK2 with an IC₅₀ of approximately 0.24 μM [131]. The benzoyl side chain of CP-25 forms hydrogen bonds with K220 in the small lobe, whereas its cyclohexane and two oxygen heterocyclic rings primarily interact with GRK2 Ala321 in the P-loop to form hydrogen bonds. Additionally, the benzene sulfonyl side chain mainly binds to GRK2 Gly201 and Asp335 in the large lobe, forming hydrogen bonds. These hydrogen bonds involve conserved backbone atoms or side chains across GRK2, likely contributing to the inhibition and selectivity of small-molecule compounds. Site-directed mutagenesis studies have confirmed that Ala321 is a critical residue for CP-25-mediated inhibition of the GRK2–EP4 interaction. CP-25 has demonstrated efficacy across a wide range of inflammatory and immune-mediated disease models. It inhibits RA FLS hyperproliferation [131, 132], ameliorates the pristane-induced mouse systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) model by suppressing plasma cell differentiation [133], alleviates cisplatin-induced acute liver injury [134] and antigen-induced experimental Sjogren’s syndrome (SS) [135], inhibits keratinocyte proliferation by restoring GRK2–JAK1 colocalization in a mouse model of psoriasis [74], and effectively alleviates renal fibrosis by regulating NOX4 and oxidative stress [136]. Currently, the use of CP-25 remains at the preclinical stage (Table 3).

In summary, the optimal GRK2-targeted strategy may differ according to the primary pathological axis driving each disease. With respect to Gαq-mediated cardiac hypertrophy, interference with the GRK2–Gαq interface (e.g., βARKrgs) appears most direct [95]. In heart failure, where Gβγ-dependent GRK2 translocation plays a critical role, preclinical studies have shown that targeting the GRK2–Gβγ interaction with βARKct, gallein, or M119 can effectively prevent and reverse cardiac dysfunction [101, 103]. In contrast, for chronic inflammatory and autoimmune diseases such as RA, psoriasis, and SLE, where GRK2 acts predominantly through substrate phosphorylation (e.g., IκBα and EP4), small-molecule inhibitors targeting the kinase domain (e.g., paroxetine analogues, CP-25) may offer greater therapeutic benefit [126, 131, 137]. Future studies should systematically compare these strategies in disease-specific animal models to guide clinical translation.

Summary and prospects

GRK2 is a crucial regulatory protein widely expressed in various cell types. Crystal structures of GRK2 are essential for understanding its function. The RH domain in the N-terminal region plays a vital role in anchoring GRK2 to the plasma membrane and modulating its activity. Additionally, the RH domain interacts with Gαq, facilitating Gαq signal transduction [3]. The KD of GRK2 exhibits different active and inactive conformations in the crystal structure. Complete closure of the KD, induced by ATP and substrate binding, is necessary for GRK2 to be catalytically active. In addition, the C-terminal PH domain interacts with the RH domain, forming an extensive interface that participates in the regulation of the allosteric activation of GRK2 through binding with phospholipids or Gβγ subunits, thereby modulating the phosphorylation of activated receptors [138]. Therefore, GRK2 activation depends on the dynamic interactions of its αN-helix, RH, and PH domains, along with activating partners, such as agonist-occupied GPCRs, Gβγ subunits, and PIP2. Increased expression and activity of GRK2 promote its interaction with a series of proteins related to cell proliferation, migration, differentiation, and mitochondrial energy metabolism, including GPCRs, Gα, Gβγ, PI3K and AKT. This results in the formation of a complex GRK2 interactome that contributes to the development of GRK2 dysfunction-related diseases.

On the basis of their ability to target specific GRK2 interaction interfaces (e.g., blocking GRK2–Gβγ, GRK2–Gαq, or GRK2–substrate binding), GRK2 inhibitors can be broadly classified into peptide-based inhibitors, RNA aptamers, and small molecules. Each class has unique advantages and limitations. Peptide‑based inhibitors (βARKrgs, βARKct, and HJ‑loop peptides) achieve high target specificity by acting on protein–protein interaction interfaces, but they face significant pharmacokinetic hurdles—poor membrane permeability, rapid proteolytic degradation, and the need for gene delivery or chemical modification for in vivo stability [96, 99, 107]. RNA aptamers (e.g., C13) exhibit exceptional selectivity and potency in vitro; however, their clinical translation is hindered by nuclease instability, delivery challenges, and high production costs [109, 139]. Small‑molecule ATP‑competitive inhibitors—including paroxetine, balanol, and their analogues (e.g., CCG258747, CCG258208, and Compound 14as)—demonstrate potent GRK2 inhibition, generally favourable membrane permeability, and oral bioavailability. Nevertheless, they often suffer from limited selectivity across the AGC kinase family and off‑target toxicity [140]. For example, paroxetine requires high micromolar concentrations (IC₅₀ = 20 μM) to inhibit GRK2, doses that inevitably engage its primary target (serotonin transporter) and elicit central nervous system effects [117]. Even the more selective paroxetine analogue (CCG258747) retained measurable activity against GRK5 (IC₅₀ = 1.5 μM) [120]. These selectivity gaps pose significant risks: inhibiting GRK5, which plays distinct roles in heart and immune cells, may cause unexpected toxicity or counterproductive effects. Moreover, extensive off‑target kinase inhibition can confound phenotypic outcomes in disease models. Although interface‑targeting peptides theoretically offer the advantage of disrupting specific GRK2–partner interactions with minimal off‑target effects, their practical application remains largely preclinical. This trade‑off between specificity and drug‑like properties underscores the need for innovative chemical strategies.

To mitigate these risks and advance the translational potential of GRK2‑targeted therapies, we propose three specific and feasible research directions for the development of highly selective GRK2 inhibitors. The first direction is the development of allosteric inhibitors. These compounds bind outside the highly conserved ATP‑binding pocket, exploiting the unique conformational states of GRK2 to achieve greater selectivity across the GRK family. Such allosteric modulators would avoid the off‑target risks associated with ATP‑competitive agents. For example, through computational docking and virtual screening, small‑molecule inhibitors targeting the RH domain of GRK2—such as C3Z392 and C5Z299—have been identified as non‑ATP‑competitive inhibitors. These GRK2 allosteric inhibitors act by inhibiting the GRK2 RH domain, thereby enhancing receptor-mediated cAMP responses, preventing GRK2 translocation to the plasma membrane, disrupting the coimmunoprecipitation of GRK2 with the Gαs subunit, and protecting against receptor desensitization [34]. The second direction is the development of rational combination therapy based on disease‑specific pathways. Given the complex and multifactorial nature of GRK2 dysfunction-related diseases, single-agent GRK2 inhibition may be insufficient to achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes. Taking RA as an example, recent studies have indicated that approximately 45%–60% of RA patients respond inadequately to methotrexate (MTX) monotherapy and that the overexpression of membrane‑associated GRK2 is negatively correlated with the efficacy of MTX. In CIA rats with high GRK2 expression, the efficacy of MTX monotherapy was 77.8%, and the efficacy of MTX combined with CP‑25 reached 88.8% [141]. CP‑25 has demonstrated favourable therapeutic effects in animal models of RA; however, no clinical trials have been initiated to date [142]. Nevertheless, these findings provide a strong rationale for exploring GRK2-targeted combination regimens in other GRK2 dysfunction-related diseases, such as SLE [133] and SS [143], which also involve GRK2 overexpression. These strategies align with the broader concept of multitarget synergistic therapy, which aims to enhance efficacy while reducing the toxicity associated with high-dose monotherapy. The third direction is using conditional genetic models for cell type-specific functional studies. GRK2 is widely expressed, but its pathogenic role in GRK2 dysfunction diseases may be cell type dependent (e.g., macrophages and FLS). Traditional knockout or systemic inhibition cannot be used to dissect these contributions. By generating macrophage-specific conditional GRK2 knockout mice, we can evaluate the therapeutic effect of GRK2 inhibition on specific macrophage populations in RA models [71]. This approach can be used to identify optimal cellular targets and avoid systemic toxicity.

In conclusion, although proof‑of‑concept research of current GRK2 inhibitors in preclinical models has been conducted successfully, the clinical translation of these inhibitors requires overcoming selectivity and pharmacokinetic hurdles. Guided by structural and cell‑type‑specific insights, prioritizing the development of allosteric inhibitors and rational combination strategies will accelerate the development of GRK2‑targeted therapies for GRK2 dysfunction‑related diseases.
