Central Message

Papillary muscle excision after head transposition is an alternative technique to treat mitral valve-induced LVOTO in hypertrophic cardiomyopathy.

Mitral valve (MV) leaflet and subvalvular apparatus abnormalities are common features in patients with hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM).1 These anomalies contribute to dynamic left ventricular outflow tract obstruction (LVOTO), particularly if septal hypertrophy is minimal.2 Anomalies in the position and/or anatomy of the papillary muscles (PMs) are recognized predisposing factors to LVOTO.2 Among the wide array of PM anomalies, anterior displacement of the antero-lateral PM and bifid PM with increased head mobility are strongly associated with systolic anterior motion (SAM) and LVOTO. Several surgical techniques to address PM anomalies have been described.3

In April 2023, a 56-year-old man with a diagnosis of HCM presented with exertional dyspnea and postprandial exacerbation despite optimal medical therapy. Transthoracic echocardiography documented HCM with 17-mm basal septal hypertrophy; a bifid, hypertrophic, and anteriorly displaced anterolateral PM; a dynamic LVOTO with an intraventricular gradient of 40 mm Hg at rest and 100 mm Hg during Valsalva maneuver; and SAM of the MV was evident, with moderate mitral regurgitation at rest and severe regurgitation during the Valsalva maneuver (Video 1). PM anomaly was confirmed with computed tomography angiogram (Video 2). After multidisciplinary evaluation, he underwent surgery. PM excision and head transposition plus limited septal myectomy was performed and LVOTO was successfully relieved. The patient is currently asymptomatic. Written informed consent for publication of study data was collected. Institutional review board approval was not required.

Surgical Technique

After transverse aortotomy, direct inspection through the aortic valve confirmed a bifid, hypertrophic, and anteriorly displaced anterolateral PM. The PM was not hypermobile under traction. PM head transposition was performed as shown in Figure 1. The anterior head and its chordal apparatus were identified, grasped, and separated from the body of the PM, which was completely excised at its base to widen the LVOT. The fibrotic head and its chordal apparatus were then reimplanted with a single 4-0 Prolene (Ethicon) pledgeted suture to the posterior head of the anterolateral PM. As a result, all the chordae originated from a single, posterior head and MV coaptation was moved posteriorly, further away from the LVOT. Finally, a limited septal myectomy was performed to address all possible involved mechanisms of LVOTO. After cardiopulmonary bypass weaning, transesophageal echocardiography showed relief of LVOTO with a peak provoked gradient of 26 mm Hg, absence of SAM, and mitral regurgitation. At 1 year follow-up high-speed computed tomography scan confirmed a preserved MV anatomy (Figure 2). Discussion

In patients with obstructive HCM without significant septal hypertrophy, the dynamic LVOTO is generally associated with MV structural abnormalities. Bifid, hypertrophic, and anteriorly displaced anterolateral PM are common anomalies in these patients. Understanding the leading mechanisms of LVOTO is crucial. In these cases, a sole extended septal myectomy might not be sufficient to relieve LVOTO due to the multiple mechanisms involved. Moreover, when extended septal myectomy is performed in low-volume centers, it might be associated with increased risk of iatrogenic septal defect or conduction system impairment.4

Therefore, specific PM interventions have been proposed to address LVOTO.3 PM head reorientation is a valid option in case of hypermobile PM.5 However, in the reported case, the bifid PM was thick and not hypermobile, hence difficult to mobilize and reorient. Moreover, even after reorientation, due to its hypertrophy, it would still obstruct the LVOT. In such cases, PM head transposition has the double advantage to completely excise the PM obstructing the LVOT and move MV coaptation posteriorly.

The reported technique has never been proven to be effective in this subpopulation of patients with HCM before this report. This technique can be an extreme option to effectively treat LVOTO, without need for MV replacement or excessive septal myectomy.
