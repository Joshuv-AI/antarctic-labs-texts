Comparative efficacy and acceptability of non-pharmacological and pharmacological treatments in post-stroke depression: protocol for a systematic review and network meta-analysis
Authors: Xinyi Yao, Juncai Pu, Yi Fan, Xuan Jiang, Haitong Zhao, Yunyan Wang, Yuge Ye, Yutian Wei, Lining Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDepression is a common complication of stroke that adversely affects functional recovery. Although a wide range of pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions are used in clinical practice, evidence regarding their comparative efficacy and acceptability remains inconclusive. Therefore, we will conduct the first systematic review and network meta-analysis (NMA) to compare and rank available interventions for post-stroke depression (PSD).Methods and analysisThe findings will be reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses Protocols guidelines. In this systematic review and network meta-analysis, we will search PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials, PsycINFO, CINAHL, ClinicalTrials.gov and the WHO International Clinical Trials Registry Platform from database inception to 1 September 2025 to identify published and unpublished randomised controlled trials (RCTs). We will include studies comparing pharmacological and non-pharmacological treatments, or any control conditions as monotherapy for the treatment of patients with PSD. We will assess the certainty of evidence using the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis framework. The primary outcomes will be the change score on depression scales from baseline to the end of treatment (efficacy) and all-cause discontinuation (acceptability). Secondary outcomes will include quality of life, cognitive and neurological function scores, anxiety and sleep quality. Two reviewers will independently screen and select eligible studies based on predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. Risk of bias in all RCTs included in the NMA will be assessed using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2). A frequentist NMA will be conducted in Stata and R. Meta-regression and sensitivity analyses will be performed to assess robustness.Ethics and disseminationThis NMA does not need ethical approval, as the data used here are based on aggregated data in the public domain. Findings from the analysis will provide an overview and information on the relative efficacy and acceptability of non-pharmacological and pharmacological treatments in PSD. The results will be disseminated through peer-reviewed publication.PROSPERO registration numberCRD420251136670

Introduction

Post-stroke depression (PSD) is a common and serious complication of stroke, affecting approximately one-third of stroke survivors.1 2 PSD negatively impacts stroke outcomes, such as impaired functional recovery, cognitive deficits and poor quality of life.3 It is also associated with increased mortality and imposes a considerable burden on families and society.4 Despite these adverse consequences, many patients remain untreated due to underdiagnosis or undertreatment of mood disorders.5 A recent study reported that only 33% of patients in acute hospitals of Australia were screened for depression, and among those identified as requiring support, only a half received appropriate care.6 In addition, both general practitioners and hospital physicians have been reported to hold negative attitudes towards treatment.7 Thus, identifying effective and acceptable management strategies for PSD is beneficial for both individuals and society.

In the past decade, research on PSD has increased substantially. A variety of pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions have been investigated, including antidepressants, psychological therapy, neuromodulation therapy, Chinese medicine and rehabilitation therapy.8 Among these, research on pharmacological therapy and psychological therapy is most extensive, with the strongest evidence base.9 Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are widely recognised as the first-line pharmacological therapy for PSD;10 other antidepressants have also significantly reduced the proportion of patients meeting diagnostic criteria for depression compared with placebo.1113 Among psychological therapies, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) has been demonstrated to alleviate depressive symptoms in PSD patients.14

Several clinical guidelines recommend antidepressants as first-line treatment, while psychological interventions are also effective.4 15 However, existing evidence remains insufficient to determine whether pharmacological treatment is safer or more effective than psychological or other non-pharmacological therapy. Furthermore, antidepressant use is associated with adverse outcomes, including suicide, recurrent stroke and falls.16 17 In recent years, an increasing number of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have explored novel interventions for PSD treatment, such as repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), traditional Chinese medicine and rehabilitation therapies. For instance, several trials have reported superior efficacy for traditional Chinese medicine compared with antidepressants in PSD patients,1821 but current research findings remain inconclusive. A recent Cochrane conventional meta-analysis suggested that pharmacological treatment, psychological therapy and rTMS may all be effective.11 However, due to the paucity of head-to-head RCTs, the conventional meta-analysis could not provide an evidence-based recommendation on which types of treatments are most preferred. This is highly problematic, particularly for patients who do not opt for or are unable to tolerate a pharmacologic treatment and need to be informed about the comparative efficacy and acceptability of currently available non-pharmacologic options.

Network meta-analysis (NMA) allows indirect comparisons between different interventions when head-to-head RCTs are lacking, by synthesising data from multiple studies that evaluate treatments against a common comparator such as pharmacological placebo or psychological therapy.2224 This methodology enables researchers to answer comparative questions that extend beyond the scope of the original trials design. NMA have been increasingly incorporated into major clinical guideline development and health policy evaluations to evaluate the relative benefits and costs across entire treatment pathways, such as the National Institute for Health and Care Excellence in the UK and the American Heart Association/American Stroke Association.25 26 Furthermore, NMA allows for the ranking of treatments across different outcomes, providing clinicians with evidence-based guidance for treatment selection.

Therefore, we plan to conduct the first NMA to systematically evaluate the efficacy and acceptability of pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions for PSD by synthesise the latest RCT evidence. Specifically, we aim to identify the most effective therapy to inform clinical decision-making for the treatment of PSD.

Methods

This protocol will be presented in accordance with the Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) Protocols guidelines.27 Our systematic review protocol was registered with the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) on 2 September 2025 (registration number CRD420251136670). The NMA will be performed in compliance with the guidelines of the Cochrane Handbook28 and findings will be reported following the guidelines of the PRISMA-NMA.29

Inclusion criteria

Types of studies

We will include only RCTs published in English from database inception to 1 September 2025. Publication types include the first phase of cross-over trials as well as cluster randomised trials. Conference proceedings, reviews, comments or studies involving animal models will be excluded.

Types of participants

We will include only individuals meeting established diagnostic criteria for PSD. Stroke will be diagnosed according to clinical criteria or neuroimaging confirmation, including cerebral infarction, intracerebral haemorrhage or stroke of uncertain pathological type. The diagnosis of depression will be based on structured psychiatric interviews, clinician assessment or validated mood scales with established thresholds.11 Acceptable diagnostic categories include the following: depressive disorder: symptom scores exceeding prespecified thresholds on standard screening tools; major depressive disorder: diagnosis according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)-III-R,30 DSM-IV,31 DSM-532 or equivalent standards; dysthymia or minor depressive disorder: defined according to DSM or other diagnostic frameworks. No restrictions will be placed on age, sex or other demographic and clinical characteristics. Studies including participants with comorbid psychiatric or somatic disorders will also be included. Studies that do not confirm depression at baseline (even if depression is measured during follow-up) and studies from which PSD subgroup data cannot be independently extracted from mixed or ambiguous populations (eg, populations combining cerebrovascular stenosis and stroke) will be excluded.

Types of interventions and controls

We will include five main categories of interventions for PSD: pharmacotherapy including SSRIs (escitalopram, fluoxetine, paroxetine, sertraline, etc.), tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline), serotonin–norepinephrine reuptake inhibitors (venlafaxine, duloxetine) and newer agents such as mirtazapine; psychological therapy including any manualised or structured therapies, such as behavioural therapy, CBT, problem-solving therapy, psychodynamic therapy and supportive therapy. No restrictions apply regarding specific delivery format (individual or group) or medium (in-person or online); neuromodulation therapy, defined as the alteration of nerve activity through targeted delivery of a stimulus, such as electrical stimulation, to specific neurological sites in the body, including rTMS, transcranial electrical stimulation and transcutaneous auricular vagus nerve stimulation; traditional Chinese medicine including acupuncture, Chinese herbal medicine and rehabilitation therapy including exercise or function rehabilitation therapy.

We plan to include all RCTs comparing any active intervention either with control conditions or with another active comparator. Control conditions for each intervention can be usual care, defined as locally accepted treatment practices for stroke. It may be provided by private or public healthcare providers and does not include active components targeting depression.33 Control conditions can also be placebo, defined as an intervention without antidepressant activity, such as starch, sugar pills or sham stimulation. Placebos in this study will include psychological, pharmacological and physical placebos.34 Especially when usual care includes structured psychotherapy or fixed-dose pharmacotherapy with a named agent, it will be considered an active intervention. Eligible interventions must be course based, delivered with a predefined frequency, dose or duration. Single-session procedures and surgical implantations will be excluded.

Change score on depression scales from baseline to the end of treatment (Efficacy), measured as the overall mean change on depression symptom scales (self-rated or clinician-rated) from baseline to the endpoint of the treatment prespecified in each study, such as different versions of the Hamilton Depression Rating Scale (HAMD),35 Montgomery–Åsberg Depression Rating Scale (MADRS),36 Beck Depression Inventory (BDI),37 Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)-Depression subscale38 and Patient Health Questionnaire-9 (PHQ-9).39

Where depression symptoms are measured using more than one depression scale in a trial, we will extract data from the depressive scales based on a hierarchy of rating scales. The hierarchy will be based on informants and for consistency of use across trials, with the clinician report first in the hierarchy, and then the patient self-report. If both self-report and clinician report, we will prioritise the scales which have been used most across the included studies. To maximise the number of eligible studies and improve comparability, we will use the endpoint of the intervention prespecified in each study as the primary assessment timepoint. If multiple follow-up time points are reported, we will prioritise data at the end of the intervention and consider follow-up outcomes in sensitivity analyses.

Data sources and search strategy

We will comprehensively search electronic databases (PubMed, EMBASE, Cochrane Library, PsycINFO, CINAHL) from inception to 1 September 2025 using combinations of Medical Subject Headings and free-text terms related to stroke or depression with RCT filters. For example, in PubMed, stroke-related terms include “stroke”, “post-stroke”, “ischemic stroke”, “hemorrhagic stroke”, “cerebrovascular accident”, “intracerebral hemorrhage” and “cerebral infarction”. Depression-related terms include “depression” and “depressive symptoms”. To ensure comprehensiveness, we will include validated depression rating scales in the search strategy, such as HAMD, PHQ-9, HADS, GDS, BDI and Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D). Study selection

All retrieved records will be imported into EndNote X9 to remove duplicates. After deduplication, two reviewers will independently screen titles and abstracts and assess full texts of potentially relevant studies. If both reviewers independently judge a study as potentially eligible based on its title and abstract, it will proceed to full-text screening. Only studies meeting the inclusion criteria described above will be included. Any disagreements will be resolved by a third reviewer or through discussion with the study authors. For repeated measures, we will also extract each timepoint for further analysis if data are sufficient. Data presented only in figures will be extracted using WebPlot Digitizer. If study data or full text cannot be obtained, we will contact the original authors. Disagreements between reviewers will be resolved through consultation with a third researcher and, if necessary, by contacting the original authors.

Risk of bias assessment

The risk of bias in RCTs will be assessed using the revised Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2).51 Two reviewers will independently assess five domains: bias arising from the randomisation process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing outcome data, bias in measurement of the outcome, bias in selection of the reported result, and overall bias. Studies will be classified as low risk of bias (all domains low risk), some concerns (at least one domain with concerns but not high risk) or high risk of bias (at least one domain high risk). Disagreements will be resolved through discussion to reach a consensus.

Data synthesis and analysis

If all selected studies report depression severity using scales with established conversion factors, we will attempt to convert them using conversion procedures (eg, http://ids-qids.org/interpretation.html) or relevant formulas,52 and we will use the mean difference between treatment groups within studies as the effect measure for the primary outcome. If depression severity is measured using self-rated scales or scales that cannot be converted, we will standardise all study-specific means and adopt the standardised mean differences (SMDs, Hedges’ g) with its 95% CIs for continuous outcomes and ORs with 95% CIs for dichotomous outcomes. According to Cohen’s standards, SMD values of 0.2, 0.5 and 0.8 represent small, medium and large effects, respectively.28

Missing dichotomous outcome data will be managed according to the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, and all the dropouts after randomisation will be considered non-responders. Missing continuous outcome data will be analysed using the completer data. If SDs are not reported directly, we will derive them from SE, 95% CIsCIs, quartiles, ranges (min–max) or baseline differences to calculate SMDs and their 95% CIs.53

We plan to group interventions into broad categories (pharmacological therapy, psychological therapy, neuromodulatory therapy, traditional Chinese medicine, rehabilitation therapy). Trials comparing the same therapy at different therapeutic doses, form or frequency will be merged in the same node in the NMA. We also plan to merge treatment as usual, pill placebo, sham stimulation, attention control and social support into an ‘active control’ condition because they exhibit similar effects in mental health disorders,34 thereby improving statistical power. To ensure consistency in this study, subsequent regression analyses and sensitivity tests will compare different control conditions; if significant discrepancies are identified, stratified reporting will be conducted.

We will first perform conventional pairwise meta-analyses of direct comparisons using Review Manager (V.5.4.1), followed by frequentist network meta-analyses using Stata (V.14.1)54 and the netmeta package in R.55 We will use random-effects models to account for heterogeneity in intervention effects.56 The network plot will be generated using Stata’s ‘network map’ command, with node size proportional to sample size and edge thickness reflecting the number of direct comparisons. League tables will be constructed too. To estimate the intervention hierarchy and ranking probability for each intervention, we will use the surface under the cumulative ranking curve (SUCRA). SUCRA percentages range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating a greater likelihood of being the best intervention. If quantitative synthesis is not feasible, we will provide a structured narrative synthesis of the findings.

Measures for transitivity assumption

Transitivity assumes that all interventions are ‘jointly randomizable’, and this assumption could be violated if interventions have different indications.57 In other words, transitivity may be violated if effect modifiers are not similarly distributed across the different comparisons in the network. We will examine the transitivity assumption using boxplots in R (ggplot2) to visually inspect the distributions of the following potential effect modifiers: publication year; patient characteristics such as stroke type, duration, depression severity and sex; and intervention characteristics such as frequency and duration.

Measures for heterogeneity

Heterogeneity will be assessed using I2 statistics, guided by recommendations from the Cochrane Statistical Methods Group: considering p values and CIs, <40% indicates low heterogeneity, 30–60% moderate heterogeneity, 50–90% substantial heterogeneity and 75–100% considerable heterogeneity.28

Measure for inconsistency

Incoherence between direct and indirect sources of evidence will be statistically assessed globally, by comparison of the fit and parsimony of consistency and inconsistency models, and locally by using node-splitting and loop-specific methods to compare differences between direct and indirect effects within closed loops of the network.58 We will evaluate the presence of local inconsistency and global inconsistency in Stata and will also be duplicated in R.

Subgroup analyses and sensitivity analyses

To determine whether the results are affected by study characteristics, we will conduct meta-regression models to explore potential sources of heterogeneity (eg, publication year, sample size, depression severity, stroke type, sex, age). To perform the meta-regressions, we will use package ‘gemtc’ in R. Sensitivity analyses will be stratified by sample size, scale type, PSD diagnostic criteria and study risk of bias (eg, excluding small-sample studies, restricting to clinician-rated scales, excluding studies where depression diagnosis relied only on screening tools, excluding studies at high risk of bias and comparing end of intervention vs most recent follow-up assessments).

Measures for publication bias

Publication bias will be assessed through visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s test.59 We will also use the comparison-adjusted funnel plots of all trials with placebo controls or treatment as usual to investigate whether results in imprecise trials differ from those in more precise trials in NMA.60

Certainty assessment

We will use the Confidence in Network Meta-Analysis web tool to assess the quality of evidence for each comparison, considering six factors that could lead to a downgrade: within-study bias, reporting bias, indirectness, imprecision, heterogeneity and incoherence.57 For imprecision, we consider a ±0.20 threshold for SMDs as clinically meaningful.40 Similarly, we will apply the same framework to heterogeneity. For incoherence, we will evaluate the variability of direct and indirect effects against a clinically meaningful threshold. Finally, the quality of evidence for each comparison will be categorised as high, moderate, low or very low.

Discussion

PSD is prevalent among patients with stroke. Currently, there are various pharmacological and non-pharmacological interventions for PSD treatment, yet their comparative effectiveness remains uncertain. We will conduct an NMA to evaluate the effectiveness and acceptability of different therapies for PSD and rank the probabilities of these therapeutic options.

The findings of this study will provide clinicians with stratified treatment recommendations based on efficacy and acceptability, guiding selection of the most appropriate therapies for patients. For example, if pharmacotherapy is found to be the most effective intervention for relieving depression while neuromodulatory therapy shows higher acceptability, clinicians can tailor treatment plans according to patients’ specific needs and preferences.

Nevertheless, there will be certain unavoidable limitations. First, the variability in study design, participant demographics, intervention strategies and outcome assessment methods may introduce heterogeneity, complicate interpretation and increase uncertainty. Furthermore, the lack of direct comparisons between specific treatments and weak evidence in some comparisons may undermine the reliability of overall conclusions. Differences in study quality further aggravate these issues, as studies with lower methodological rigour may negatively affect the accuracy of pooled results. To mitigate heterogeneity, we plan to conduct meta-regression to help identify factors that may influence variation in intervention effects. Additionally, we will conduct sensitivity analyses by excluding low-quality studies or specific populations to assess the robustness of our findings.

Ethics and dissemination

This study does not involve primary data collection and instead relies on the analysis of published data. Thus, ethical approval is not required. The findings will be shared via publication in a peer-reviewed journal or presentation at relevant scientific conferences. Any significant protocol amendments will be documented and updated on the PROSPERO registry.
