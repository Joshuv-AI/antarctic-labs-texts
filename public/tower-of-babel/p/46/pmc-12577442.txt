FAK-dependent activation of src family kinase member BLK contributed to endometrial fibrosis via endoplasmic reticulum stress
Authors: Yimeng Lu, Xudong Zhang, Shanshan Wu, Siwen Zhang, Xiaohan Qi, Jichun Tan
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhile Src family kinases (SFKs) are established mediators of some fibrotic diseases, their specific function in endometrial fibrosis is not well defined. Here, we investigated the function and underlying mechanism of B lymphoid tyrosine kinase (BLK), an SFK member, in driving endometrial fibrosis.MethodsBLK expression was analyzed in the human endometrium and mice uterus, as well as in human endometrial stromal cells (hEndoSCs). Its functional role was assessed using siBLK in TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs and in the intrauterine adhesion (IUA) mice model with Blk knockdown (IUABlk[LKD]).ResultsBLK was overexpressed and activated in the endometrium of IUA patients, the uterus of IUA mice, and TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs, and was accompanied by high expression of endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS) hallmarks GRP78 and CHOP. Inhibition of BLK significantly reduced the expression levels of GRP78, CHOP, Collagen I, and α-SMA in TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs. Endometrial fibrosis was also significantly attenuated in IUABlk[LKD] mice. Phospho-activation of BLK was found to rely on its binding with focal adhesion kinase (FAK) to form a complex, and subsequently aggravated endometrial fibrosis by regulating ERS.ConclusionsOur findings clarified the critical role and possible mechanism of BLK in endometrial fibrosis. BLK may serve as a promising target for treating endometrial fibrosis.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13578-025-01492-w.

Background

Intrauterine adhesion (IUA) is an emerging female reproductive disorder, mainly caused by uterine manipulation and infection[1]. Common clinical manifestations include amenorrhea, abnormal menstruation, infertility, and recurrent abortion[1, 2]. A key pathological feature of IUA is endometrial fibrosis, characterized by excessive deposition of avascular fibrous tissue within the uterine cavity[3, 4]. This avascular fibrous tissue, also referred to as extracellular matrix (ECM), is composed of collagen, fibronectin, elastin, and fibrillin, all of which contribute to fibrosis[5]. Myofibroblasts have been identified as the primary cell type responsible for ECM production[6]. These highly specialized, α-smooth muscle actin (α-SMA)-positive cells play a critical role in tissue repair and organ damage response[7]. Genetic lineage tracing studies across various fibrotic diseases have further demonstrated that organ-resident stromal cells serve as the predominant source of myofibroblasts[8, 9]. Myofibroblast differentiation is a complex process reliant on transforming growth factor β1 (TGF-β1) signaling, integrin-mediated activation, and ECM deposition[10, 11]. TGF-β1 is a well-defined cytokine that potently induces myofibroblast differentiation, and its pro-fibrotic effects have been extensively documented[12].

The Src family kinases (SFKs) are a group of nonreceptor tyrosine kinases that play crucial roles in various cellular processes, including migration, invasion, and growth[13]. Members of SFKs, such as SRC, HCK, LCK, YES, FYN, and LYN, have been implicated in the pathogenesis of multiple fibrotic diseases, including liver fibrosis, pulmonary fibrosis, renal fibrosis, and systemic sclerosis[14, 15]. Furthermore, targeted inhibition of SFKs has been shown to reduce ECM deposition and alleviate tissue fibrosis[14]. SFKs activation is known to occur through phosphorylation of corresponding tyrosine residues[13], with focal adhesion kinase (FAK) playing a key role in this process[16]. Among the SFKs, B lymphoid tyrosine kinase (BLK) is primarily expressed in B-lineage cells but has also been detected in non-B-lineage cells[17, 18]. RNA-sequencing analysis of endometrial tissue from IUA patients and normal controls identified BLK as the most significantly differentially expressed SFK member. Although BLK is well established as an oncogene and therapeutic target in various cancers[19], its potential role in endometrial fibrosis of IUA remains unexplored.

The endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is a large and dynamic organelle primarily responsible for calcium storage, protein folding, post-translational modifications, protein transport, and the maintenance of cellular homeostasis[20, 21]. In response to injury and stress[22], the accumulation of unfolded or misfolded proteins within the ER lumen disrupts the organelle’s internal environment, leading to endoplasmic reticulum stress (ERS). While early ERS may help restore cellular homeostasis, persistent ERS promotes inflammation, apoptosis, and autophagy[23, 24]. Current studies have confirmed that activated ERS is a crucial mechanism driving the progression of various chronic diseases[25–29], specially in fibrotic diseases [26, 30–33]. Nevertheless, the specific role of ERS in the pathogenesis of endometrial fibrosis remains poorly understood. Previous studies have implicated other members of SFKs in disease progression through the induction of ERS. Therefore, we speculate that BLK may similarly contribute to endometrial fibrosis via ERS-related mechanisms.

In this study, we investigated the role and mechanism of BLK in IUA endometrial fibrosis through in vivo and in vitro studies. Furthermore, we explored the potential relationship between ERS activation and endometrial fibrosis in IUA.

Methods

Patients enrollment

The study conformed to the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All experiments involving human specimens were approved by the Ethics Committee of Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University (2022PS011F). All patients were informed of the experimental purpose and procedures prior to the study and signed voluntarily informed consent. IUA patients were included based on criteria established in our previous study[34].

Materials

All antibodies were obtained commercially listed in Table S1. Recombinant human TGF-β1 (Cat#CA59) was purchased from Novoprotein (China). SU6656 (Cat#HY-B0789), Y15 (Cat#HY-12444), 4-Phenylbutyric Acid (4-PBA) (Cat#HY-A0281), and α-Lipoic Acid (α-LA) (Cat#HY-N0492) were obtained from MedChemExpress (China). The small interfering RNA (siRNA) targeting Blk (siBlk) and negative control (siNC) were designed and supplied by HANBIO (China). The detailed siRNA sequences are shown in Table S2.

Establishment of IUA and endometrium-specific Blk-knockdown mice models

The present study hewed to ethical regulations for animal research and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University (2022PS436K). Healthy and reproductively mature female ICR (18-20 g) mice were obtained from HFK Bioscience (China) and raised in the SPF Animal Department. The estrous cycle was monitored using vaginal smear cytology. Mice were randomly divided into two groups: the Sham group (n = 6) and the IUA group (n = 6). The IUA model was established via mechanical injury according to a previously published protocol[35]. Mice in the Sham group underwent laparotomy only.

To further investigate the role of BLK in vivo, Blk-knockdown lentivirus was designed and purchased from ABM (Wuhan, China), and the sequences are listed in Table S2. Additional mice from the Sham and IUA groups received Blk-knockdown lentivirus injections: ShamBlk[LKD] group (n = 6) and IUABlk[LKD] group (n = 6). On day 5 post-modeling, mice were anesthetized and subjected to a secondary laparotomy. The Blk-knockdown lentivirus (1 × 108 UT/mL) was injected at multiple points into the uterine along the entire length of the uterus using a microsyringe. One side uterus was injected with 10 μL lentivirus. On day 14 post-modeling, mice were sacrificed to obtain the uteruses for further analysis.

Primary human endometrial stromal cells (hEndoSCs) isolation and culture

Endometrial samples were obtained from IUA patients undergoing hysteroscopic adhesiolysis. Fresh endometrial tissues were minced and digested with 5 mg/ml collagenase type I (Sigma-Aldrich, USA) at 37 ℃ for 30 min to isolate primary hEndoSCs. Subsequently, red blood cell lysis buffer (Solarbio, China, Cat#R1010) was applied to remove residual red blood cells for 10 min at room temperature (RT). The cell suspension was then filtered through a 40 μm strainer (BKMAM, China) and centrifuged at 300 × g for 5 min at RT. The pelleted hEndoSCs were resuspended and cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium/F-12 Medium (DEME/F12) (HyClone, USA) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS) (Gibco, USA), 100 U/ml Penicillin and Streptomycin (PS) (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louise, MO), and maintained at 37 ℃ in a 5% (v/v) CO2 humidified atmosphere.

Primary human endometrial stromal cells (hEndoSCs) treatment

hEndoSCs at 70–80% confluence were treated with the corresponding reagents. The control group received an equal volume of DMSO under the same conditions. For TGF-β1 induction, hEndoSCs were stimulated with 10 ng/ml TGF-β1 in serum-free medium for 2, 4, 6, 8, 12, and 24 h. Subsequently, the cells were treated with an SFKs inhibitor (SU6656) at concentration gradient (1 μM, 2 μM, 5 μM, and 10 μM) for 24 h. Additionally, TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs were treated for 24 h with the FAK inhibitor Y15 (10 μM), the ERS inhibitor 4PBA (5 mM), or the ERS activator α-LA (100 μM).

RNA interference

Per the GP-transfect-Mate transfection reagent (GenePharma, China, Cat#G04009) instructions, the corresponding transfection procedures were conducted in this study. hEndoSCs were planted onto six-well plates (NEST) with 2 × 105 cells per well on one day prior to transfections. The transfection complexes were prepared by combining 7.5 μl GP-transfect-Mate and 10 μl of 20 μmol siBlk or siNC in serum-free DMEM/F12 (HyClone, USA), followed by incubation at RT for 20 min. The mixture was then added to the hEndoSCs and incubated for 12 h. After transfection, the medium was replaced with fresh DMEM/F12 containing 10% FBS. RNA extraction was performed 24–48 h post-transfection, and protein extraction was 48–72 h.

Tissue section & staining

Fresh human endometrium and mouse uterus from each group were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at a thickness of 3.5 μm. The sections were dewaxed, transparent, rehydrated, and then stained with hematoxylin and eosin (Solarbio, China, Cat#G1120) or Masson’s trichrome stain (Solarbio, China, Cat#G1340) following standard protocols. Morphological evaluation and gland counting were performed using an optical microscope (IX73, Olympus, Japan). The average integrated optical density (IOD) of collagen deposition was quantified from 10 randomly selected fields per sample at × 200 magnification using ImageJ software.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC)

An IHC Kit was purchased from Elabscience (Wuhan, China, Cat#E-IR-R217). The sections underwent dewaxing, transparency, rehydration, and antigen retrieval. Following that, endogenous peroxidase activity was quenched by treating the sections with 3% hydrogen peroxide. After blocking with goat serum blocking buffer, the sections were incubated with appropriately diluted primary antibodies (as indicated in Table S1) overnight at 4 ℃. The following day, the sections were washed three times with PBS and incubated with ready-to-use polyperoxidase-anti-mouse/rabbit IgG for 1 h at RT. Following three additional washes, DAB served as the chromogenic agent, and hematoxylin was applied for nuclear counterstaining. Finally, the sections were mounted with neutral resin. Positive signals were observed under an optical microscope (IX73, Olympus, Japan), and the staining intensity was quantified using ImageJ software.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative (RT-qPCR)

Total RNA from tissues or hEndoSCs was extracted using the RNA-easy isolation reagent (Vazyme, China, Cat#R701-01) according to the instructions. RNA concentration was measured with a Nanophotometer N50 (Implen, Germany). Subsequently, 1 μg RNA was reverse-transcribed into cRNA using a reverse transcription kit (Vazyme, China, Cat#R323-01). RT-qPCR was performed with ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR master mix (Vazyme, China, Cat#Q711-02) on a QuantStudio 5 Real-Time PCR Detection System (Thermo Fisher, USA). All primers used in this study were purchased from Sangon Biotech (China), and their sequences are listed in Table S2. The relative expression of each gene was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCT method.

Protein preparation and western blotting

Tissues or hEndoSCs were washed at least twice with ice-cold Phosphate Buffered Saline (PBS) (Biopico, China) and lysed on ice using RIPA buffer (Epizyme, China) supplemented with protease and phosphatase inhibitor mixtures (Solarbio, China). The lysates were centrifuged at 12,000 × g for 15 min at 4 ℃ to remove the sediment. Protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). A minimum of 20 μg of total protein per sample was separated by Sodium Dodecyl Sulfate–Polyacrylamide Gel Electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) (Epizyme, China) and transferred onto Polyvinylidene Fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Millipore, USA). After blocking with 1 × Protein-Free Rapid Blocking Buffer (Epizyme, China, Cat#PS108), the membranes were incubated with specific primary antibodies (as indicated in Table S1) at appropriate dilutions for 1.5 h at RT. Following three washes with 1 × TBST (Servicebio, China), the membranes were incubated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit/mouse IgG antibody (Abmart, China, Cat#M21003) at a 1:5000 dilution for 1 h at RT. After three additional washes with TBST, the blots were visualized using ECL western blotting detection reagents (Epizyme, China, Cat#SQ202) and detected by Amersham ImageQuant 800 (Cytiva, China).

Molecular docking

The protein sequences of BLK and FAK were retrieved from the Uniprot Database (https://www.uniprot.org/). Protein–protein docking was performed using the AlphaFold Server 3.0 (https://alphafoldserver.com/). The resulting docking model was imported into PyMOLWin, where hydrogen bonds mediating the interaction between the two proteins and the participating amino acid residues were identified and labeled. Cartoon-style representations of the complex were generated and exported for figure preparation.

Immunofluorence (IF)

Tissue sections were prepared as described previously. Cells were seeded and allowed to adhere to coverslips (BKMANM, China). The pre-treatment steps for tissue sections prior to staining followed the same protocol as above. Coverslips were rinsed twice with PBS and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at RT, then blocked with 10% goat serum (ZSGB-Bio, China, Cat#ZLI-9022) for 40 min at RT. Both sections and coverslips were incubated with appropriately diluted primary antibody (as indicated in Table S1) overnight at 4 ℃. The following day, after washing three times with PBS, the sections and coverslips were incubated with fluorescently labeled secondary antibodies (as indicated in Table S1) for 2 h at RT. Following this, they were stained with DAPI (Biosharp, China, Cat#BL105A) for 10 min, followed by sealing with a fluorescent quench agent. Fluorescence images were acquired using a fluorescence microscope (IX73, Olympus, Japan) and quantified by ImageJ software.

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

A CoIP Kit (Cat#abs955) was purchased from Absin (China). Cells were rinsed with ice-cold PBS and lysed with IP lysis buffer containing protease inhibitor for 30 min. Lysates were prepared as described in the previous section. Then, 500 μg of lysate was incubated with normal rabbit IgG (negative control), specific rabbit anti-BLK antibody, or specific rabbit anti-FAK antibody on a vertical rotator (LICHEN, China) at 4℃ overnight. The following day, 5 μl of protein A/G agarose beads were added and incubated for 12 h at 4 °C to capture the immunocomplexes. The beads were then washed at least twice with 1 × washing buffer and centrifuged at 14,000 × g for 15 min at 4℃. Finally, the immunocomplexes were resuspended in SDS sample buffer, boiled at 100 °C for 5 min, and analyzed by immunoblotting.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism 8 (San Diego, USA). All experiments were performed in triplicate with consistent results at least, and representative images are presented in this study. Data are expressed as Mean ± Standard Deviation (SD). Differences between two groups were analyzed using Student’s t-test, while comparisons among three or more groups were evaluated by One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). All tests were two-sided, and a p-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001).

BLK mediated endometrial fibrosis through stimulating ERS.

As shown in Fig. 5A-B, intracellular Ca2+ concentration was higher in TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs (p = 0.003), which indicated ERS activation during endometrial fibrosis in vitro. Given that ERS hallmarks (GRP78 and CHOP) were reduced by BLK knockdown both in vitro and in vivo, we further investigated whether BLK promoted endometrial fibrosis via ERS. In TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs, Collagen I and α-SMA expression were downregulated by BLK knockdown using siBLK, and such inhibitory effect was counteracted by the ERS agonist α-LA (Fig. 5C-D). Moreover, TGF-β1-induced upregulation of Collagen I (p = 0.027) and α-SMA (p = 0.008) was significantly suppressed by the ERS inhibitor 4PBA (Fig. 6A-B), whereas their expression was further enhanced by the ERS agonist α-LA (p = 0.037 and p = 0.043, respectively) (Fig. 6C-D). Increased FAK and p-FAK expression in human and mouse fibrotic endometrium, as well as in TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs, were also confirmed by Western blots (Fig. S3). Discussions

In the present study, we identified BLK, one of the SFKs, as a critical regulator in the development of endometrial fibrosis. As summarized in Fig. 8, BLK was overexpressed and phosphorylated in the endometrium of IUA patients, the uterus of IUA mice, and TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs. Endometrial fibrosis was alleviated by BLK knockdown both in vitro and in vivo. Phosphor-activation of BLK promoted endometrial fibrosis by regulating ERS. Furthermore, BLK activation was shown to depend on its interaction with FAK. Our findings showed a positive correlation between BLK and endometrial fibrosis progression, and demonstrated BLK, upon FAK-assisted phosphorylation, induces ERS, resulting in detrimental effects on the development of EndoSCs.Fig. 8The schematic model of BLK contributed the endometrial fibrosis through FAK-dependent activation and ERS

Up to now, nine members of the SFKs have been identified: SRC, FYN, LYN, YES, FGR, HCK, LCK, YRK, and BLK. Notably, based on our RNA-Seq analysis, BLK was identified as the only SFK member showing statistically significant upregulation in endometrium of IUA patients compared to normal patients. However, its role in endometrial fibrosis and even in fibrotic diseases has remained largely unexplored. BLK is widely recognized as an overexpressed pathologenic gene, a specific therapeutic target in oncology, since its knockdown is likely to have few side effects[19, 36]. Although previous sequencing studies have reported differential expression of BLK in certain fibrotic diseases[37–39], its functional relevance had not been experimentally verified. Our study provided substantive evidence supporting the involvement of BLK in endometrial fibrosis and shed light on its potential mechanisms. BLK was overexpressed and phosphorylated in fibrotic endometrium of both IUA patients and IUA mice, as well as in TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs. Either pharmacological inhibition or knockdown of BLK significantly suppressed the upregulation of Collagen I and α-SMA in TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs. Moreover, endometrial fibrosis was markedly attenuated in Blk-knockdown IUA model mice. Together, these findings strongly support a critical role of BLK in the pathogenesis of endometrial fibrosis.

The mechanism by which BLK contributes to endometrial fibrosis has remained unclear. Our study revealed that elevated expression of ERS hallmarks GRP78 and CHOP in both fibrotic endometrium and TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs, indicating the presence of intracellular ERS[40, 41]. Upregulation of GRP78 is identified as a well-established indicator of ERS activation[42], while CHOP is served as a central mediator across all three branches of the ERS signaling network[43]. Since the ER as a calcium reservoir, ERS leads to calcium channel activation, increasing cytoplasmic Ca2+ levels, and excess Ca2+ in turn exacerbates ERS[44]. Consistent with this, a significant rise in cytoplasmic Ca2+ concentration in TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs was observed in our study. TGF-β1 has been shown to upregulate ERS-associated proteins, including GRP78, CHOP, ATF4, and spliced XBP1[45–47], our study also was corroborated in this finding. Accumulative evidence has underscored the pivotal role of ERS in driving fibrogenesis across multiple organ systems, such as renal fibrosis[46–48], pulmonary fibrosis[49], and cardiac fibrosis[50, 51]. For instance, Roberson et al. demonstrated that influenza virus-induced ERS in lung epithelial cells promoted JNK1-mediated TGF-β1 production, which further contribute to myofibroblast activation and extracellular matrix deposition, leading to upregulation of Collagen I and α-SMA, ultimately aggravating pulmonary fibrosis [45]. PM2.5 exposure exacerbated pulmonary fibrosis through ERS amplification via regulation of the TGF-β1/Smad3 axis [49]. ERS activation had also been found in the angiotensis II-induced renal injury, which was linked to NLRP3 inflammasome [52]. Overexpression of CHOP leaded to a decrease of Bcl-2 and a activation of caspase-3, and drove pro-apoptosis signaling[53], which was closely related with cardiac fibrosis. In cholestasis-driven liver fibrosis, since α-SMA as an indicator of hepatic stellate cell (HSC) activation, its expression was significantly downregulated in CHOP-deficient mice[54]. Importantly, therapeutic targeting of ERS has been shown to significantly ameliorate fibrosis progression[55–57]. In our study, treatment with the ERS inhibitor 4PBA reduced collagen I and α-SMA expression in TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs, whereas the ERS activator α-LA further aggravated fibrotic responses. To date, only one study explored the role of ERS in endometrial fibrosis[58], and our results were consistent with its findings. Collectively, these results suggest that inhibition of ERS-related proteins such as GRP78 and CHOP, or direct suppression of ERS, may offer novel therapeutic strategies for fibrotic diseases.

Studies have shown that SRC and FYN, both members of SFKs, can induce ERS[59, 60]. For instance, FYN had been reported to drive pro-apoptosis signaling through ERS activation, leading to skeletal muscle atrophy[59]. SRC-mediated ERS activation had also been implicated in both epithelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EMT) process[60, 61] and Endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition (EndMT)[62]. Moreover, the use of an SFK inhibitor had been shown to suppress FYN-induced ERS, thereby ameliorating diabetic kidney injury[63]. However, the regulatory relationship between BLK and ERS in the context of endometrial fibrosis has not been elucidated. In the present study, we observed that inhibition of BLK expression or activation, both in vitro and in vivo, significantly reduced the levels of the ERS hallmarks GRP78 and CHOP. In contrast, treatment with either an ERS inhibitor or activator did not alter BLK expression or phosphorylation levels. Rescue experiments further confirmed that ERS acts downstream of BLK in endometrial fibrosis.

Prior studies have demonstrated that TGF-β1 triggered the activation of certain SFKs, such as SRC and FYN[64, 65]. Our results further indicated that TGF-β1 also induced BLK activation. Although SFKs generally require phosphorylation for further play its role, the upstream regulators of BLK in fibrotic microenvironments remain poorly defined. Previous studies have established that FAK is essential for TGF-β1-induced expression of Collagen I and α-SMA, as well as for myofibroblast differentiation[66, 67], which our study also observed in endometrial fibrosis. We found that FAK was overexpressed and phosphorylated in the endometrium of IUA patients, the uterus of IUA mice, as well as in TGF-β1-induced hEndoSCs. Current evidence suggest that FAK autophosphorylation at Y397 facilitates the recruitment of SRC and promotes its phosphorylation, thereby activating downstream signaling pathways [68, 69]. In line with this, inhibition of FAK phosphorylation using Y15, a potent and specific FAK inhibitor targeting the Y397 autophosphorylation site[70], prominently disrupted BLK phosphorylation without affecting total BLK levels, and subsequently suppressed TGF-β1-induced upregulation of Collagen I and α-SMA. It is well-known that activated FAK forms a complex with SRC, leading to SRC activation and initiation of multiple downstream signaling pathways involved in diverse cellular functions [71, 72]. The FAK/SRC complex plays a crucial role in ECM interaction and extracellular signals transduction[73–75]. Consistent with bioinformatic predictions, both CoIP and IF confirmed that FAK and BLK colocalize and interact physically in hEndoSCs, and such interaction was enhanced under TGF-β1 stimulation. Therefore, our findings support the hypothesis in which TGF-β1 induces FAK phosphorylation, leading to the recruitment of BLK and formation of a FAK-BLK complex that facilitates BLK phosphorylation. Phosphorylation of BLK subsequently triggers ERS, ultimately promoting endometrial fibrosis.

Conclusions

In summary, our findings proved that BLK as a critical regulator in the progression of endometrial fibrosis. We demonstrated that BLK was phosphor-activated via interaction with FAK, subsequently inducing ERS and thereby promoting the development of endometrial fibrosis. Targeting BLK may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for IUA. Further investigations are needed to elucidate the precise mechanisms through which ERS exacerbates endometrial fibrosis.
