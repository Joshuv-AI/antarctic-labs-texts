Myasthenia gravis complicated by intestinal pseudo-obstruction: a case report and literature review
Authors: Yangtao Lin, Chaoyue Zhang, Zhishan Zhong, Yufei Deng, Haocheng Luo, Xiaojun Yang, Qilong Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
PurposeMyasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disease characterized by fluctuating weakness of skeletal muscles, usually without involvement of gastrointestinal muscle groups. However, when accompanied by intestinal pseudo-obstruction (IPO), patients may present with gastrointestinal symptoms, complicating prompt diagnosis. We report two new cases of MG complicated by IPO, detailing their clinical course and management.MethodsA case report and review of the literature.ResultsWe report two new cases of young male MG complicated by IPO. Both patients were admitted with symptoms indicative of intestinal obstruction and achieved clinical remission following thymectomy. We identified a total of 14 cases of MG complicated by IPO, including 12 previously reported in the literature. Of these cases, IPO was the initial clinical manifestation in 64.3% (9/14). 12 (85.7%) of them were correlated with thymoma, and only one patient did not undergo thymectomy. Ten patients showed clinical improvement, while the remaining four succumbed to the disease progression or complications.ConclusionsIt is challenging to diagnose MG complicated by IPO at the initial presentation. We should be careful to avoid unnecessary colectomies in the treatment process.

Introduction

Myasthenia gravis (MG) is an autoimmune disorder affecting the postsynaptic membrane at neuromuscular junctions, primarily characterized by fluctuating weakness and fatigability of skeletal muscles. Oculobulbar muscle weakness is most common, and patients can also present with generalized muscle weakness, including the proximal muscles of the limbs and the trunk [1]. Gastrointestinal symptoms are usually relatively rare in MG; however, patients with thymoma may be more susceptible to intestinal pseudo-obstruction (IPO) [2], which manifests as abdominal distension and vomiting.

IPO presents with signs and symptoms of intestinal obstruction in the absence of anatomical blockages. Treatment typically focuses on nutritional support, and surgery should be avoided whenever possible [3, 4]. Although IPO has been reported in patients with thymoma-associated MG [5], it remains difficult to make a definitive diagnosis in the first instance and avoid surgery [6].

We report two MG patients, both of whom were admitted with symptoms of bloating and vomiting and subsequently diagnosed with IPO. One had a thymoma, while the other did not. However, both experienced relief after thymectomy.

Methods

We conducted a systematic literature search of PubMed, Web of Science, and CNKI databases (1980–2024) using the keywords: ("myasthenia gravis"OR"MG") AND ("pseudo-obstruction"OR"IPO"). Inclusion criteria: (1) Case reports/series of MG with IPO; (2) English or Chinese publications. Exclusion criteria: (1) No full-text article; (2) Incomplete clinical data. A total of 28 articles were screened, and 10 eligible articles were identified (Fig. 1). Case presentations

Case 1

A 28-year-old previously healthy male presented in August of 2019 with a one-week history of vomiting, abdominal pain, and distension. He also presented with three days of constipation and a weight loss of 15 kg over the preceding month. On physical examination, the patient had a distended abdomen, with hypoactive bowel sounds. Abdominal radiograph showed gastric distension and duodenal dilation. Abdominal computed tomography (CT) demonstrated marked gastric and duodenal dilation with fluid sequestration, accompanied by prominent gaseous distension of the colon, without evidence of mechanical obstruction (Fig. 2). Abdominal vascular color Doppler ultrasonography revealed a decrease angle between the superior mesenteric artery and the abdominal aorta, along with duodenum compression, suggestive of superior mesenteric artery (SMA) syndrome. The patient underwent endoscopic placement of a feeding tube to alleviate his symptoms. After nutritional support, he gained weight without further episodes of vomiting, abdominal pain, or distension, leading to the removal of the feeding tube.Fig. 2Abdominal CT axial view (a) showed gastric and duodenal dilation with fluid sequestration, and coronal view (b) revealed prominent gaseous distension within the stomach and colon

Abdominal CT axial view (a) showed gastric and duodenal dilation with fluid sequestration, and coronal view (b) revealed prominent gaseous distension within the stomach and colon

Two years later, the patient was readmitted for the same indication, complaining of newly developed lower limb weakness. He underwent endoscopic placement of a feeding tube again yet without any improvement in his condition. A repeat CT scan, including thoracic imaging, demonstrated an anterior mediastinal mass measuring 5.9 cm by 4.2 cm by 7.8 cm, indicative of a possible thymoma (Fig. 3a). The feeding tube remained in place, and gastrointestinal distension was noted. Then the patient underwent a puncture biopsy of the anterior mediastinal mass, with histological findings suggestive of a type B2 thymoma at clinical stage 3 (modified Masaoka system) (Fig. 3b). Therefore, the patient's intestinal obstruction was considered to be a paraneoplastic syndrome (PNS) due to thymoma, i.e., IPO.Fig. 3Thoracic CT (a) revealed an anterior mediastinal mass of 5.9 cm by 4.2 cm by 7.8 cm, a possible thymoma. To assess for a possible combination of myasthenia gravis, the patient had a repetitive nerve stimulation (RNS) test, showing a positive decrement. Acetylcholine receptor antibodies (AChR-Ab) testing was positive, while the ganglionic AChR antibody and the voltage-gated calcium channel (VGCC) antibodies were negative. And PNS related antibodies (including anti-GAD65, anti-Zic4, anti-Tr, anti-SOX1, anti-Ma2, anti-Ma1, anti-Amphiphysin, anti-CV2, anti-Ri, anti-Yo and anti-Hu antibodies) were negative. He was started on pyridostigmine and prednisolone, along with continuous enteral and parenteral nutrition.

After he was able to feed himself without vomiting, the feeding tube was removed. However, during this time, he developed weakness in the limbs and respiratory muscles. Following a period of treatment, he gained weight and underwent thymectomy. Since then, his myasthenia gravis has improved, and he has not experienced any further episodes of vomiting.

Case 2

A 25-year-old male, diagnosed with MG since the age of 22 years, for which he was under treatment with pyridostigmine and prednisolone, was referred to the emergency department with symptoms of intestinal obstruction in December 2021, which had commenced approximately 4 days prior to admission. He presented with a four-day history of vomiting and a two-day history of constipation. On physical examination, the patient had abdominal tenderness and distension, with an absence of bowel sounds. Abdominal radiograph showed dilated bowel loops with truncated air-fluid levels and segmental luminal dilation. Abdominal CT demonstrated edema and thickening of the duodenal intestinal wall, accompanied by peripheral inflammatory exudates, indicative of possible SMA syndrome (Fig. 4). For further evaluation, the patient was subsequently hospitalized.Fig. 4Abdominal CT axial view (a) showed edema and thickening of the duodenal intestinal wall with peripheral inflammatory exudates, and coronal view (b) showed massive gaseous distension of the gastric fundus and colon

Thoracic CT revealed an anterior mediastinal mass measuring 4.1 cm by 0.7 cm, suggestive of possible thymic hyperplasia or thymoma (Fig. 5a). Abdominal CT demonstrated duodenal stasis and submucosal edema of the duodenum and upper segment of the jejunum, with worsening compared to previous imaging. A barium meal study indicated small bowel obstruction, with possible intestinal ischemia or paralytic ileus. Given the patient's thymic condition and history of MG, a diagnosis of IPO could not be excluded. He underwent bowel decompression and endoscopic placement of a feeding tube for nutritional support.Fig. 5Thoracic CT (a) revealed an anterior mediastinal mass of 4.1 cm by 0.7 cm, possible thymic hyperplasia or thymoma. As the intestinal obstruction improved, he underwent thymectomy, with histological findings suggestive of thymic hyperplasia (Fig. 5b). The patient’s postoperative course was uneventful, and he was discharged free of symptoms, with a clear diagnosis of IPO.

Patient perspective

After both patients had been discharged for about 1 year, we contacted them for a follow-up and obtained informed consent. They reported an improvement in their quality of life, with no further symptoms of intestinal obstruction.

Discussion

Clinical features

Myasthenia gravis complicated by intestinal pseudo-obstruction represents a rare clinical entity. We report two new cases of MG complicated by IPO, both involving young male patients who were admitted with symptoms of intestinal obstruction. One patient had a prior diagnosis of MG, while the other was diagnosed during this hospitalization. Both patients experienced symptomatic relief following enteral nutrition, thereby avoiding the need for colostomy. Our review of the literature identified 12 detailed case reports of patients presenting with MG complicated by IPO (Table 1). With the addition of our two new cases, we evaluated the clinical course and characteristics of a total of 14 patients. A marked male predominance was observed (12/14 cases, 85.7%), predominantly affecting ​​individuals in young to mid-adulthood​​ with a median age of 45 years. Three (33.3%) were tested positive for ganglionic AChR-Ab. Thymoma correlation reached 85.7% (12/14), suggesting paraneoplastic autoimmunity as a potential mechanism in disease pathogenesis. IPO represented the initial clinical manifestation in 9 of 14 cases (64.3%), frequently preceding classic MG symptoms by months to years. Differential diagnosis

Intestinal pseudo-obstruction is an acute or chronic motility disorder, manifested by distension and dilation of the small or large bowel, with clinical symptoms resembling intestinal obstruction in the absence of anatomic blockage [15]. Our cases illustrate critical diagnostic challenges. In Case 1, duodenal dilation was initially misdiagnosed as SMA syndrome, resulting in a two-year delay in identifying MG and thymoma. This diagnostic pitfall aligns with published literature reporting that 57.1% (8/14) of MG complicated by IPO cases undergo unnecessary surgical interventions [5–9, 11, 12].

Additionally, Lambert-Eaton myasthenic syndrome (LEMS) requires consideration in the differential diagnosis, although it is typically characterized by detectable the VGCC antibodies and is notably associated with small cell lung cancer [16]. Autoimmune autonomic ganglionopathy (AAG) may similarly present with IPO, but characteristically lacks both MG symptoms and seropositivity for AChR-Ab [17]. However, in this review, all three patients demonstrated detectable gAChR antibody, suggesting possible pathogenic overlap between MG and AAG [5, 9, 10].

Pathogenesis

The current understanding posits that the pathogenesis of MG complicated by IPO is closely linked to autoimmune dysregulation. And the core pathology of MG involves anti-striated muscle AChR-Ab disrupting neuromuscular junction transmission [1]. IPO results from abnormal intestinal motility due to autonomic neuropathy and/or enteric neuromuscular dysfunction [4], often associated with systemic pathologies of diverse etiology [2, 10]. Neuropathy represents a primary cause of dysregulation leading to IPO. Research indicates that gAChR-Ab in some patients may impair cholinergic synaptic transmission within sympathetic and parasympathetic ganglia, thereby precipitating dysregulation of intestinal motility. Ganglionic AChR-Ab is hypothesized to contribute to this process by impairing synaptic transmission in autonomic ganglia [7].

Inflammatory triggers, notably autoimmune diseases like MG and PNS [7], can provoke lymphocytic, eosinophilic, and mast cell infiltration within the enteric neuromuscular compartment. This infiltrate causes ganglionitis and/or leiomyositis [4], disrupting signaling within the enteric nervous system (ENS). This immune abnormality appears particularly pronounced in patients with thymoma. As an immunologically active organ, thymoma potentially induces cross-reactive antibodies via molecular mimicry, simultaneously targeting both skeletal muscle AChR and enteric ganglionic receptors. This mechanism may explain the co-occurrence of MG and IPO.

Furthermore, histopathological examination reveals lymphocytic and plasmacellular infiltration within the myenteric plexus accompanied by neuronal degeneration—— findings consistent with ganglionitis [4]. This combined damage to autonomic regulation and the ENS substantiates immune-mediated enteric neural injury as a fundamental pathological basis for IPO [18].

Management

Thymectomy constitutes the cornerstone of treatment for MG complicated by IPO. Among the 14 included cases, 12 presented with thymoma, and 13 underwent thymectomy. Symptomatic improvement was achieved in 9 patients (69.2%). Notably, the patient who did not undergo thymectomy developed myasthenic crisis leading to mortality, precipitated by an upper respiratory tract infection [11]. Preoperative correction of malnutrition via enteral or parenteral nutritional support is imperative. As observed in our two cases, nutritional optimization significantly reduced surgical complications, thereby establishing a crucial foundation for subsequent therapeutic interventions.

It is critical to emphasize avoiding unnecessary colonic surgery, as MG complicated by IPO represents a functional dysmotility disorder rather than a mechanical obstruction. Clinically, more than half of such patients underwent invasive abdominal interventions—prompted by misdiagnosis, yet these procedures typically fail to resolve symptoms and may even precipitate symptom exacerbation.

Limitation

This study has several limitations. The cohort size is relatively small due to the rarity of the condition, and longer follow-up analysis is needed. Additionally, gAChR antibodies were not assessed in nearly half of the patients, potentially compromising mechanistic interpretations. Future studies requiring larger cohorts with standardized antibody panels are needed to validate these findings.

Conclusion

Myasthenia gravis rarely starts with gastrointestinal symptoms, which makes the diagnosis of MG complicated by IPO more challenging. Consequently, it is necessary to be careful during diagnosis and treatment, with an emphasis on avoiding colon resection whenever possible.
