Comments on “Efficacy and safety of low-dose naltrexone for the management of fibromyalgia: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials with trial sequential analysis”
Authors: Hoa Ngan Doan, Sang Gyu Kwak, Min Cheol Chang
Published: 2025

TO THE EDITOR

We read with interest the recent systematic review and meta-analysis by Vatvani et al. [1] on the efficacy and safety of low-dose naltrexone for the management of fibromyalgia. The meta-analysis found that low-dose naltrexone significantly reduced pain and increased the pressure-pain threshold in patients with fibromyalgia compared to a placebo. However, we would like to raise concerns regarding the meta-analytic model selection criteria. The authors state that they applied a random-effects model because diverse participant characteristics resulted in a substantial level of heterogeneity. However, we believe that the authors’ model selection approach is not sufficiently comprehensive.

Choosing the appropriate meta-analytic model, whether a fixed-effect or random-effects model, is crucial for accurately pooling effect-size estimates. The fixed-effect model assumes that all studies estimate the same true effect size, and differences in observed effects are attributed to sampling error only. Conversely, the random-effects model assumes that true effect sizes may vary between studies, and differences in observed effects therefore reflect both sampling error and heterogeneity [2,3].

In practice, choosing between fixed-effect and random-effects models in meta-analysis remains a source of debate, as there is no universally established framework or standard criteria to guide model selection in different scenarios.

Some researchers, including Vatvani et al. [1], have proposed selecting the meta-analytic model based on the study’s methodology and underlying assumptions. The fixed-effect model is appropriate when all studies included in the meta-analysis are assumed to be functionally identical. In this scenario, the objective is to estimate a common effect size that applies only to the included studies [2]. In contrast, the random-effects model is more suitable when the studies included in the meta-analysis are conducted independently with variations in participants or interventions. Here, the aim is to generalize the findings beyond a narrowly defined population [2]. However, we believe that this approach to selecting a fixed-effect or random-effects model is subjective and lacks strong empirical support.

Considering both objectivity and practicality, we support using the I² statistic and P values from Cochran’s Q test for meta-analytic model selection. When I² ≤ 50% and P ≥ 0.05, a fixed-effect model is used. When I² > 50% and P < 0.05, a random-effects model is used. In cases where I² ≤ 50% and P < 0.05 or I² > 50% and P ≥ 0.05, model selection should be based on the study’s methodology and underlying assumptions, as proposed by Vatvani et al. [1].

In addition to our concerns regarding the meta-analytic model selection, we would like to highlight a terminology issue in which the term “random-effect model” is used instead of the correct term, “random-effects model.” In meta-analysis, the random-effects model assumes that true effect sizes vary across studies; hence, the plural form “effects” is appropriate [2].

In conclusion, we believe that Vatvani et al. [1] should reconsider the criteria used to select the meta-analytic model. In addition to the conceptual assumptions regarding study heterogeneity, we recommend that they consider empirical indicators such as the I² statistic and P values from Cochran’s Q test. Adopting a more structured and transparent approach to meta-analytic model selection could enhance the methodological rigor and interpretability of their findings.
