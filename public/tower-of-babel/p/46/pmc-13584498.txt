Honghe Bunya-like virus: a novel virus identified in mosquitoes from Yunnan, China
Authors: Xinbei Li, Zhenhua Li, Xiaodong Sun, Yaqing Guo, Zheng Pang, Guoyu Niu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundArboviruses represent a persistent and escalating threat to global public health, with mosquitoes serving as the principal vectors in their natural transmission cycles and geographic dissemination. Yunnan Province, southwestern China, is a recognized hotspot for arboviral diversity, yet the full spectrum of mosquito-borne viruses circulating in this region remains incompletely characterized.ResultsA total of 3,300 female mosquitoes of four species across four genera were collected from rural areas of Honghe County, Yunnan Province in 2024, and subjected to viral metatranscriptomic sequencing. A previously undescribed bunya-like virus, designated Honghe Bunya-like virus, was identified in two locally dominant hematophagous mosquito species, with minimum infection rates of 0.2% and 0.3%, respectively. The viral genome comprises three single-stranded negative-sense RNA segments (L, M, and S) encoding the RdRp, glycoprotein, and nucleoprotein, respectively, consistent with the canonical architecture of the genus Orthobunyavirus. Phylogenetic analyses placed the virus within Orthobunyavirus across all three segments, though inter-segment topological incongruence was observed; amino acid identities to known orthobunyaviruses (49.7%–71.6%) fell below conspecific thresholds, suggesting a novel species.ConclusionsThis study expands the known genetic diversity of mosquito-associated virus in southwestern China and, given the phylogenetic affinity to pathogenic orthobunyaviruses and the hematophagous nature of the vector species, raises the possibility of vertebrate infection potential warranting further investigation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12864-026-13112-z.Keywords: Mosquito-associated virus, Metagenomic sequencing, Orthobunyavirus, Genome organization, Phylogenetic analysis

Introduction

Arboviruses are a diverse group of disease agent transmitted by arthropod vectors, characterized by broad geographic distribution, wide host ranges, and substantial capacity to cause emerging and re-emerging infectious diseases, collectively posing a persistent threat to global public health [1]. As integral components of ecosystems, arthropods harbor a remarkable diversity of viruses. Among them, hematophagous insects—including mosquitoes and ticks—are equipped with piercing–sucking mouthparts that enable direct inoculation of pathogens into host bloodstreams, rendering them the principal vectors of arboviruses [2]. Within this group, mosquitoes occupy a preeminent role in arbovirus transmission by virtue of their vast population size, cosmopolitan distribution, and strong host-feeding preference for vertebrates [3]. Beyond their capacity to acquire and transmit multiple viruses across species boundaries, mosquitoes can maintain persistent viral lineages in nature through vertical (transovarial) transmission, thereby contributing to the long-term perpetuation and geographic spread of arboviruses [4, 5].

Yunnan Province, situated along the southwestern border of China, occupies a transitional zone between the low-latitude plateau and tropical regions, characterized by pronounced altitudinal climatic gradients and a mosaic of complex ecological landscapes encompassing tropical rainforests, subtropical forests, and mountainous river valleys. The region’s warm, humid climate and abundant rainfall provide highly favorable conditions for year-round mosquito breeding and proliferation; to date, more than 130 mosquito species across 15 genera have been recorded in the province [6]. The exceptional biodiversity of wildlife and the diversity of vertebrate host communities further establish optimal ecological conditions for the natural circulation and genetic diversification of arboviruses. Accordingly, Yunnan Province is widely recognized as one of China’s primary hotspots for arboviral diversity, and is the province in which the greatest number of mosquito-associated viruses have been first isolated in China. Culex tritaeniorhynchus, the principal enzootic vector of Japanese encephalitis virus in the region, has been shown through metatranscriptomic surveillance to harbor a broad spectrum of additional mosquito-associated RNA viruses, including Culex flavivirus, Quang Binh virus, and chikungunya virus [7]. Anopheles sinensis, while predominantly recognized as the primary malaria vector in southern China, has similarly been identified as a carrier of multiple arboviruses in field collections from Yunnan, including dengue virus, Zika virus, chikungunya virus, and Mangshi virus [8]. The genus Orthobunyavirus (family Peribunyaviridae, order Bunyavirales) represents one of the largest groups of mosquito-borne RNA viruses, comprising over 170 recognized species spanning multiple serogroups worldwide, yet remains substantially undercharacterized in mosquito populations across subtropical and tropical Asia, underscoring the need for systematic surveillance in ecologically diverse regions such as Yunnan [9].

In the present study, systematic mosquito sampling and arbovirus surveillance were conducted in rural areas of Honghe County, Yunnan Province. Through viral metatranscriptomic sequencing combined with comprehensive bioinformatic analyses, a previously undescribed bunya-like virus was identified and its genome organization, phylogenetic position, and molecular characteristics were systematically characterized, providing new molecular evidence for the genetic diversity of mosquito-associated viruses circulating in this region.

Methods and materials

Mosquito collection

Systematic mosquito collection was conducted from June to September 2024 in rural areas of Chegu Township, Honghe County, Honghe Hani and Yi Autonomous Prefecture, Yunnan Province (Fig. 1). A total of six sampling sites were established, all situated at or immediately adjacent to livestock enclosures (cattle and pig pens) on the periphery of local villages. Mosquitoes were captured using ultraviolet light traps deployed from dusk until dawn; traps were inspected and serviced daily, and collected specimens were transported promptly to the laboratory for processing. Upon arrival, mosquitoes were cold-anesthetized and identified to species based on morphological characters under a stereomicroscope [10]. Morphological assignments were subsequently verified by molecular methods: a fragment of the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase subunit I (COI) gene was amplified by PCR to ensure the accuracy and reliability of species identification [11]. All specimens were catalogued and archived according to collection date, sampling site, and species, with no more than 50 individuals per pool to ensure the reliability of downstream molecular assays.

Nucleic acid extraction

Prior to nucleic acid extraction, mosquito samples were subjected to a standardized pre-processing procedure. Each batch of specimens was first surface-cleaned in an ultrasonic cleaner (laboratory-assembled) to remove environmentally derived contaminants and exogenous nucleic acids adhering to the cuticle. Samples were then immersed in 75% ethanol with gentle agitation for approximately 2 min, followed by air-drying under aseptic conditions. The dried specimens were manually homogenized under liquid nitrogen to maximize tissue disruption and ensure efficient nucleic acid release. Following homogenization, an appropriate volume of pre-chilled RPMI 1640 medium was added to each tube to prepare a tissue suspension, which was centrifuged at approximately 15,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C. The resulting supernatant was collected for downstream nucleic acid extraction. Total RNA was isolated using the QIAamp® RNA Mini Kit (QIAGEN, Germany) in strict accordance with the manufacturer’s protocol. RNA quality and concentration were assessed prior to use; samples meeting quality criteria were either processed directly for library construction and sequencing or stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Library construction and sequence alignment

Total RNA extracted from mosquito pools was quantified using a NanoDrop spectrophotometer and assessed for integrity on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer prior to library preparation. RNA quality was considered acceptable when the A260/A280 ratio was between 1.8 and 2.1, the A260/A230 ratio was ≥ 1.8, and the RIN was ≥ 5.0. Pools yielding ≥ 1 µg of total RNA were subjected to rRNA depletion using the FastSelect rRNA Removal Kit; pools below this threshold were processed directly for library construction. Sequencing libraries were constructed from 5 µL of total RNA per pool using the VAHTS® Universal V8 RNA-seq Library Prep Kit, following a standard workflow comprising RNA fragmentation, first- and second-strand cDNA synthesis, end repair and dA-tailing, adaptor ligation, and PCR amplification. Libraries passing quality control were normalized and subjected to paired-end sequencing (2 × 150 bp) on an Illumina platform [12].

Raw sequencing reads were processed in CLC Genomics Workbench, including adapter trimming, low-quality read filtering (Phred quality score < 20), and removal of reads shorter than 50 bp. Host-derived sequences were removed by aligning clean reads against the relevant host reference genome using the STAR algorithm [13]. The remaining non-host reads were assembled de novo to generate contigs, which were queried against the NCBI viral protein database and the non-redundant nucleotide database using BLASTx and BLASTn [14], respectively, to identify candidate viral sequences. Candidate virus-associated contigs were prioritized based on sequence similarity scores (E-value ≤ 1 × 10⁻⁵), percentage amino acid identity, and supporting read depth.

Genome characterization

To elucidate the genomic organization of the identified virus, open reading frame (ORF) prediction and functional annotation were performed on each assembled genomic segment. Potential protein-coding regions (including positions, lengths, and reading frame orientations) were identified using NCBI ORF Finder, and preliminary functional annotation was conducted based on known genomic features of Bunyavirales [9, 15]. Subsequently, the nucleotide and deduced amino acid sequences of each segment were compared against the GenBank database using NCBI BLAST (BLASTn/BLASTx, E-value ≤ 1 × 10⁻⁵) to retrieve representative virus strains with the highest similarity and their corresponding protein sequences, which served as references for downstream analyses.

Phylogenetic analysis

Pairwise amino acid sequence identities between Honghe Bunya-like virus and representative members of the family Peribunyaviridae included in the phylogenetic analyses were calculated using the p-distance model implemented in MEGA 7 [16, 17], in accordance with the species demarcation criteria established by the ICTV for the genus Orthobunyavirus. Multiple sequence alignments were generated using MAFFT v7 [18], followed by manual refinement to ensure accurate alignment across key conserved regions. Maximum likelihood (ML) phylogenetic trees were independently constructed for the full-length protein sequences encoded by each of the three genomic segments: the RdRp (2,250 aa, encoded by the L segment), the glycoprotein (1,451 aa, encoded by the M segment), and the partial nucleoprotein (95 aa, encoded by the S segment, corresponding to the central-to-C-terminal region). Reference sequences spanning the full taxonomic breadth of the family Peribunyaviridae were retrieved from GenBank, including members of the genera Orthobunyavirus, Pacuvirus, Gryffinivirus, Khurdivirus, Lakivirus, Herbevirus, Shangavirus, and Lambavirus, as well as unclassified Peribunyaviridae. ML trees were constructed using MEGA 7 with topological robustness assessed by 1,000 bootstrap replicates: the JTT matrix-based substitution model was applied for the L segment RdRp dataset, while the Poisson correction model was applied for both the M segment glycoprotein and S segment nucleoprotein datasets. The taxonomic position of the virus was ultimately determined through an integrative assessment of genome annotation, pairwise sequence homology data, and multi-segment phylogenetic inference.

Viral nucleic acid detection

A one-step qRT-PCR assay was developed to screen all field-collected mosquito pools for Honghe Bunya-like virus. Specific primers and a TaqMan hydrolysis probe targeting a conserved region of the viral genome were designed based on the assembled consensus sequences of Honghe Bunya-like virus (Table 1). Reactions were performed using the One Step PrimeScript™ RT-PCR Kit on a QuantStudio™ 5 Real-Time PCR System under the following conditions: reverse transcription at 42 °C for 5 min, initial denaturation at 95 °C for 10 s, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 5 s and 60 °C for 30 s. A sample was considered positive at a Ct value of ≤ 35.

Furthermore, the qRT-PCR-positive pools were further subjected to nested PCR targeting a conserved region of the M segment using virus-specific primers (Table 1) to verify genetic identity across positive pools and across mosquito species. Amplification products were resolved by 1% agarose gel electrophoresis, and high-quality bands were subjected to Sanger sequencing; pairwise nucleotide identities among sequenced amplicons were calculated in MEGA 7.

Virus isolation

Virus isolation was attempted from all five qRT-PCR-positive mosquito pools using three cell lines: C6/36 (Culex quinquefasciatus), Vero (African green monkey kidney), and BHK-21 (Baby Hamster Kidney). C6/36 cells were maintained in SF-9 medium (Gibco) supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 30 °C; Vero and BHK-21 cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C. Clarified pool homogenates filtered through 0.22 μm membranes were used to inoculate cell monolayers at 70–80% confluence; after 2 h adsorption, inocula were replaced with maintenance medium (SF-9 with 2% FBS for C6/36; DMEM with 2% FBS for Vero and BHK-21). Cells were monitored daily for cytopathic effect (CPE), and supernatants were harvested for three consecutive blind passages, with viral replication assessed by qRT-PCR at the end of each passage cycle [19]. All procedures were conducted in a BSL-2 laboratory.

Statistical and bioinformatic analyses

The minimum infection rate (MIR) of Honghe Bunya-like virus in mosquitoes was calculated as the number of positive pools divided by the total number of individual mosquitoes tested [20, 21]. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 5.00 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA); differences in positivity rates among mosquito species were evaluated by Fisher’s exact test, with a p-value of less than 0.05 considered statistically significant. The complete nucleotide sequences of all three genomic segments have been deposited in the GenBank database under accession numbers PZ013241–PZ013243.

Conserved protein domains encoded by the L, M, and S genomic segments of Honghe Bunya-like virus, Tataguine virus, and Tanga virus were identified and annotated using the NCBI Conserved Domain Search (CD-Search) tool [22], with functional annotations cross-referenced against the UniProt database [23]. Three-dimensional structural models of the encoded proteins were predicted using AlphaFold 3 and visualized in PyMOL to delineate overall folding topology and the spatial distribution of identified conserved domains [24].

Results

Sample collection and nucleic acid extraction

A total of six mosquito sampling sites were established in rural areas of Chegu Township, Honghe County, Honghe Hani and Yi Autonomous Prefecture, Yunnan Province, yielding a cumulative total of 3,300 female mosquitoes. Morphological examination assigned all specimens to four species across four genera: Culex tritaeniorhynchus, Anopheles sinensis, Culex quinquefasciatus, and Armigeres subalbatus; species assignments were subsequently corroborated by molecular analysis of the mitochondrial COI gene. Culex tritaeniorhynchus was the predominant species, comprising 1,896 individuals (57.5% of the total catch), followed by An. sinensis (21.3%) and Cx. quinquefasciatus (14.8%), with Armigeres subalbatus being the least abundant (6.4%) (Table 2). All collected mosquitoes were grouped into 68 pools based on species, with each pool containing no more than 50 individuals. RNA was subsequently extracted from these pools.

Genomic characterization of Honghe Bunya-like virus

Through metagenomic next-generation sequencing and de novo assembly of mosquito pools, we discovered a novel tripartite, single-stranded, negative-sense RNA virus, tentatively named Honghe Bunya-like virus, with three segments: large (L), medium (M), and small (S) (Fig. 2). The de novo assembly recovered 6,861 nt for the L segment, 4,492 nt for the M segment, and 631 nt for the S segment, with the L and M assemblies encompassing complete coding regions and the S assembly representing a partial internal fragment. The L segment encodes a 2,250-aa RNA-dependent RNA polymerase (RdRp) spanning nucleotide positions 32 − 6,784; the M segment encodes a 1,451-aa glycoprotein precursor at positions 33 − 4,388, predicted to be co- and post-translationally processed into envelope glycoproteins Gn and Gc together with an intervening non-structural protein NSm; and the partial S segment ORF occupies positions 178–465, yielding a deduced nucleoprotein fragment of 95 aa. The 5′ terminus of the S segment was not recovered in the present assembly; consequently, whether this segment encodes an additional NSs protein remains undetermined. This tripartite genomic architecture, with each segment encoding a single major protein, is consistent with the canonical genome organization of the genus Orthobunyavirus, family Peribunyaviridae.

Schematic illustration of the genome organization of Honghe Bunya-like virus

BLASTp searches against the non-redundant protein database identified Tataguine virus (strain H9963) and Tanga virus as the closest known relatives across all three genomic segments. The 95-aa nucleoprotein fragment recovered in this study corresponds to amino acids 82–233 of the Tataguine virus nucleoprotein (239 aa; AXS76879.1), encompassing approximately 70.6% of the Bunya_nucleocap conserved domain (pfam00952; residues 19–232), with 68% amino acid identity (104/152 identical residues) and 83% similarity (127/152 positive matches) across a 152-residue alignment span. Systematic pairwise identity analysis against all representative Peribunyaviridae members revealed that, for the RdRp, the highest identity was observed with Tanga virus (71.6%), followed by Tataguine virus (65.0%) and Okola virus (64.5%), with all remaining members ranging from 10.2% to 57.5%; for the glycoprotein, Tataguine virus ranked highest (55.7%), followed by Okola virus (49.8%) and Tanga virus (49.1%), with other members ranging from 14.1% to 37.0%; and for the nucleoprotein, Tataguine virus again ranked highest (69.5%), followed by Okola virus (56.8%) and Tanga virus (55.3%), with other members ranging from 11.6% to 49.5% (Supplementary File 2). Notably, across all three segments, the amino acid identity values fell substantially below the 96% species demarcation threshold established by the ICTV for the genus Orthobunyavirus, providing robust quantitative support for the designation of Honghe Bunya-like virus as a novel viral species.

Phylogenetic analysis

To determine the taxonomic position of Honghe Bunya-like virus, phylogenetic trees were independently constructed for the L, M, and S genomic segments based on the deduced amino acid sequences of their encoded proteins. The L segment RdRp-based phylogenetic tree (Fig. 3) demonstrated that Honghe Bunya-like virus clustered robustly within the genus Orthobunyavirus, forming a well-supported sister clade with Tanga virus. The S segment nucleoprotein-based phylogenetic tree (Fig. 4) showed that the virus grouped most closely with Tataguine virus. Similarly, the M segment glycoprotein-based phylogenetic tree (Fig. 5) displayed a comparable topology, with the virus again forming the closest evolutionary relationship with Tataguine virus. Notably, topological incongruence was observed among the three genomic segments: the L segment exhibited the closest affinity to Tanga virus, whereas both the S and M segments clustered most closely with Tataguine virus. Nevertheless, across all three segment-based phylogenies, Honghe Bunya-like virus was consistently placed within the evolutionary lineages of the genus Orthobunyavirus, confirming its overall taxonomic assignment.

For the L segment-encoded RdRp, two conserved domains were consistently identified in all three viruses (Fig. 6): an N-terminal PD-(D/E)XK endonuclease-like domain, which mediates cap-snatching to initiate viral mRNA transcription, and a central Bunya_RdRp superfamily catalytic domain harboring the conserved polymerase motifs A–F essential for viral RNA replication and transcription. The positional boundaries of both domains were nearly identical across the three viruses, with inter-virus variation of no more than 3–4 residues, indicating that the core replication machinery is structurally invariant within this phylogenetic cluster.

For the M segment-encoded glycoprotein precursor, three conserved domains recapitulating the canonical orthobunyavirus polyprotein organization were detected in all three viruses (Fig. 7): a Bunya_G2 domain, a co-localizing bunya_NS-m superfamily domain implicated in viral assembly and morphogenesis, and a C-terminal Bunya_G1 superfamily domain corresponding to the Gc glycoprotein, which mediates host cell receptor recognition and membrane fusion. Notably, while the central G2/NSm region maintained well-conserved boundaries across the three viruses, the Bunya_G1 (Gc) domain displayed greater N-terminal boundary variation, consistent with more divergent evolution of the surface-exposed glycoprotein relative to the replication machinery.

For the S segment-encoded nucleoprotein, the Bunya_nucleocap conserved domain (pfam00952) — responsible for encapsidating viral genomic RNA to form the ribonucleoprotein complex essential for genome stability, replication, and packaging — was identified in all three viruses (Fig. 8). Despite incomplete S segment recovery, the detection of this core domain in Honghe Bunya-like virus (representing approximately 70.6% coverage of the full domain) confirms that the structural framework for nucleocapsid assembly is retained.

Nucleic acid detection of Honghe virus in mosquitoes

We performed qRT-PCR to detect Honghe virus RNA in all 68 mosquito pools and identified five positive pools, all of which were derived exclusively from Cx. tritaeniorhynchus (3 pools) and An. sinensis (2 pools), corresponding to minimum infection rates of 0.2% and 0.3%, respectively. No statistically significant difference was observed between the two species (Fisher’s exact test, p > 0.05) (Table 2). Nested PCR amplification of a conserved M segment region successfully generated a ~ 923 bp fragment from three of the five positive pools (two Cx. tritaeniorhynchus, one An. sinensis); pairwise nucleotide comparison revealed 100% identity among all sequenced amplicons, whereas the remaining two pools failed to yield amplification products. The consensus sequence obtained from nested PCR verification is provided in Supplementary File 1.

Virus isolation

Virus isolation was attempted from all five qRT-PCR-positive mosquito pools using BHK-21, Vero, and C6/36 cell lines. Over three consecutive blind passages, no cytopathic effect (CPE) was observed in any of the cell lines tested, and qRT-PCR monitoring of culture supernatants at the end of each passage cycle failed to detect a consistent or increasing viral replication signal. A stably passageable viral isolate could not be established under the conditions employed in this study.

Discussion

Mosquito-borne viruses are collectively defined as viruses transmitted to vertebrate hosts, including humans, via mosquito bites. To date, more than 500 mosquito-borne viruses have been identified globally, of which at least 100 are known to infect humans and cause disease, predominantly within the families Flaviviridae, Togaviridae, and Peribunyaviridae [25]. In recent years, the widespread adoption of high-throughput sequencing technologies has substantially broadened our understanding of the viral diversity harbored by mosquitoes, leading to the discovery of numerous novel mosquito-associated viruses [26]. The majority of these are classified as insect-specific viruses (ISVs), generally considered to possess a host range restricted to arthropods and to lack the capacity to directly infect or cause disease in humans [27]. Notably, however, a subset of these newly identified viruses belongs to the families Peribunyaviridae, Phenuiviridae, or Flaviviridae—families that harbor several well-characterized pathogenic mosquito-borne viruses—and may therefore pose potential threats to public health [28]. In the present study, a previously unreported novel mosquito-associated virus was identified from field-collected specimens; phylogenetic analysis placed it within the family Peribunyaviridae, suggesting that this virus may possess pathogenic potential [29].

Under the ICTV species demarcation framework for the genus Orthobunyavirus, viruses sharing ≥ 96% amino acid identity in the RdRp are classified within the same species, with glycoprotein and nucleoprotein identities serving as supplementary criteria. As demonstrated in our pairwise identity analyses (Supplementary File 2), Honghe Bunya-like virus falls far below this threshold across all three genomic segments, providing unambiguous quantitative support for its designation as a novel species. The substantial genetic distance separating this virus from all currently recognized orthobunyaviruses further underscores the extent of uncharacterized viral diversity persisting in subtropical mosquito populations.

The three genomic segments of Honghe Bunya-like virus exhibit markedly unequal evolutionary rates, a pattern that is reflected at multiple levels of analysis. Among the three encoded proteins, the M segment glycoprotein displayed the most pronounced divergence from both Tataguine virus and Tanga virus, consistent with the well-documented pattern of accelerated evolution in surface-exposed proteins relative to the more conserved replication machinery [30]. As the principal determinant of host receptor recognition and viral entry, the glycoprotein is the most functionally consequential surface protein; divergence of this magnitude potentially indicates shifts in receptor-binding mechanisms or host cell tropism relative to known orthobunyaviruses [31, 32].

This differential evolutionary rate also manifests at the structural level. Comparative conserved domain analysis confirmed that Honghe Bunya-like virus retains the full canonical orthobunyavirus domain complement across all three segments, closely paralleling the architecture of Tataguine virus and Tanga virus despite substantial sequence divergence [33]. The RdRp endonuclease and catalytic domain boundaries were nearly identical among the three viruses, consistent with intense purifying selection on the core replication machinery [34, 35], whereas the Gc glycoprotein domain exhibited greater positional variability, reflecting diversifying selection at the vector–host interface [31, 36]. This retention of the complete domain architecture independently corroborates the taxonomic placement of Honghe Bunya-like virus within the genus Orthobunyavirus [37].

The same differential selective regime provides the most parsimonious explanation for the inter-segment phylogenetic incongruence observed in this study, whereby the L segment clusters with Tanga virus while the S and M segments affiliate with Tataguine virus. The RdRp, subject to strong purifying selection, may retain ancestral affinity to Tanga virus, while the structural proteins—more intimately involved in host–virus interactions—have diverged along a trajectory closer to Tataguine virus. An alternative explanation is genome segment reassortment during co-infection, a process facilitated by the partially overlapping geographic distributions and host ranges of these two viruses. However, no hallmark signatures of recent reassortment were detected (e.g., individual segments clustering with viruses from different serogroups), suggesting that Honghe Bunya-like virus more likely represents an independently evolved lineage derived from a common ancestor within the genus.

Unlike certain orthobunyaviruses exhibiting strict vector specificity, Honghe Bunya-like virus was detected in two ecologically distinct mosquito species—Cx. tritaeniorhynchus and An. sinensis—at statistically comparable infection rates. The 100% nucleotide identity confirmed among M segment amplicons from both species demonstrates that a single viral strain circulates across both vectors, suggesting active inter-species transmission or shared acquisition from a common environmental source [38]. These two species occupy complementary biting niches: Cx. tritaeniorhynchus predominates around human dwellings and animal shelters, whereas An. sinensis is primarily associated with peri-domestic rice paddies. This non-preferential vector association implies a broader ecological footprint than would be expected for a vector-restricted virus, potentially conferring enhanced dispersal capacity should the virus encounter novel vertebrate hosts [39]. Combined with the proximity of all sampling sites to village settlements, these findings suggest that the virus has become embedded within the rural residential and livestock-rearing environment, warranting continued surveillance to assess its outbreak potential.

The phylogenetic affinity of Honghe Bunya-like virus to two orthobunyaviruses with documented vertebrate associations has direct implications for assessing its potential host range. Tataguine virus is a confirmed human pathogen causing acute febrile illness characterized by fever, arthralgia, and rash [40], replicates productively in Vero, BHK-21, and C6/36 cells, and has elicited neutralizing antibodies in passerine birds, implicating avian species as probable amplifying hosts [41]. Tanga virus, while lacking confirmed human cases, similarly replicates in vertebrate and insect cell lines and has serological evidence of avian infection [36]. These data collectively suggest that the capacity to infect vertebrate hosts may be a conserved biological feature within this phylogenetic cluster of orthobunyaviruses. Against this backdrop, the failure of Honghe Bunya-like virus to replicate productively in Vero, BHK-21, and C6/36 cells requires cautious interpretation. Unsuccessful isolation from field specimens does not necessarily preclude vertebrate infection capacity, as several orthobunyaviruses were initially refractory to cell culture yet were subsequently confirmed as vertebrate pathogens [19]. Given the demonstrated vertebrate tropism of both closest relatives, the bridging vector capacity of Cx. tritaeniorhynchus and An. sinensis [39], and the proximity of sampling sites to human settlements, the pathogenic potential of Honghe Bunya-like virus cannot be dismissed and warrants further investigation through expanded cell line panels, experimental animal inoculation, and seroepidemiological screening.

Several limitations merit acknowledgment. First, the incomplete recovery of the S segment (631nt) precludes assessment of the full nucleoprotein sequence and the possible presence of an overlapping NSs ORF; recovery of the complete segment by 5′/3′ RACE is a clear priority [33]. Second, despite systematic isolation attempts across three cell lines over three blind passages, a stably passageable isolate could not be established, precluding experimental characterization of host range, replication kinetics, and pathogenic potential. This outcome may reflect insufficient viral titers in field specimens, absence of required host factors in the cell lines employed, or strict dependence on conditions present only in the natural mosquito host. Third, the temporal and geographic scope of sampling was restricted to a single township over a four-month period, precluding a comprehensive assessment of the geographic distribution and seasonal transmission dynamics of this virus.

Conclusions

This study reports the first identification of a novel bunya-like virus, designated Honghe Bunya-like virus, in rural areas of Honghe County, Yunnan Province, and provides a systematic characterization of its genome organization and molecular features based on currently available sequences. Phylogenetic analyses across all three genomic segments consistently support the classification of this virus within the genus Orthobunyavirus, family Peribunyaviridae, although inter-segment topological incongruence was observed, likely reflecting differential evolutionary pressures acting on distinct genomic segments. The virus was detected in two dominant local mosquito species, Culex tritaeniorhynchus and Anopheles sinensis, suggesting that its ecological distribution in nature may be broader than currently documented. Collectively, the findings of this study expand the known genetic diversity of mosquito-associated viruses in China and provide essential molecular groundwork for future investigations into the ecological dynamics, host range, and pathogenic potential of this virus.
