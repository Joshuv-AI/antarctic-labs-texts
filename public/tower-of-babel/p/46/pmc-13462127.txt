Predicting seismic-induced liquefaction potential of gravelly soils using dynamic penetration case histories
Authors: Mike Onyia, Siddharth Siddharth, Ahmed Ebid, Shadi Hanandeh, Nakkeeran Ganasen, Krishna Prakash Arunachalam, Ahmed Elsheikh, Abdelmalek H. Aljarah, Viroon Kamchoom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
As demonstrated by numerous environmental disasters worldwide, many sites have suffered from seismically induced liquefaction, resulting in substantial economic losses. Consequently, there is an urgent need for reliable prediction methods to assess vulnerability to liquefaction. In this study, the liquefaction potential (LP) of gravelly soil sites is predicted using available seismological parameters (Mw, R, t, PGA), soil parameters (G, F, D50), and site profile parameters (N’120, σ’v, Dw, Hn, Dn) through AI-based symbolic regression techniques, namely response surface methodology (RSM), genetic programming (GP), evolutionary polynomial regression (EPR), and the group method of data handling neural network (GMDH-NN). A total of 234 data records were compiled from earthquake case histories reported in the literature and divided into 80% for training and 20% for validation. RSM was employed to model the database, whereas GP, EPR, and GMDH-NN were used for liquefaction potential classification in the investigated area. Comparative evaluation of model performance indicates that the RSM yielded a statistically significant parametric LP equation, operating with a degree of variation of 0.61 and a p-value of 0.0001. For the classification models, GP, EPR, and GMDH indicate that liquefaction is expected when the predicted LP ≥ 0.5, while liquefaction is not triggered when LP < 0.5. The total misclassification cases were categorized into positive errors, where liquefaction occurred but was not predicted, and negative errors, where liquefaction was predicted but not observed, with the latter being more conservative. In practice, negative errors are not entirely definitive, as liquefaction may locally occur beneath surface layers without being visibly manifested. Although all four predictive models achieved comparable accuracy levels ranging from 88% to 90%, further analysis revealed that the GP model produced 12% positive errors, whereas both EPR and GMDH models resulted in only 6% positive errors, indicating that they are more conservative and safer than the GP model. In addition, the GMDH model is considerably more complex than the EPR model, providing EPR with a notable practical advantage. Correlation analysis further demonstrated that the vertical effective overburden stress (σ’v) and the dynamic penetration test blow count (N’120) are the most influential parameters, each exhibiting a correlation coefficient greater than 0.3g.

Introduction

Gravelly soil is composed of a combination of gravel, cobble, silt, sand, and clay particles1. Within this soil type, the gravel component, which consists of grain sizes greater than 4.75 mm, can vary from 0% to 100%. This range encompasses many subcategories of gravelly soil, such as gravel, sandy gravel, sand, silty gravel, pure gravel and clayey gravel2–5. Gravelly soil possesses numerous advantageous characteristics, such as a notable permeability coefficient, reduced compressibility, cost-effectiveness, and substantial shear strength. Consequently, it is extensively employed on a global scale for various applications, including embankment filling, dam construction, railway and highway roadbeds, as well as maritime reclamation6,7. Nevertheless, the occurrence of liquefaction in loose to medium gravelly soil has been increasingly observed in past strong seismic events5,8–10. A sequence of laboratory experiments has been undertaken to unveilthe existence of soil liquefaction in gravelly soil and to investigate the underlying mechanism11,12.Liquefaction happens when soil behaves fluidly because of a loss of shear strength brought on by an increase in pore water pressure affected by earthquake waves swift enough to encourage an undrained condition. Rapid cyclic loads tend to consolidate soil granular such as sand and gravel, leading to an increase in pore water pressure13. Even though liquefaction has been stated and studied predominantly in gravelly soils, granular soil has also experienced liquefaction. Gravelly soils are more difficult to assess for seismic liquefaction using effective and cost-effective methods than sandy soils, due to the larger particle size of the gravel, which prevent, to a certain extent, extra pore water pressure to develop during earthquakes. Costly large-scale sampling and large-scale laboratory equipment are required to ensure fast-enough cyclic loading, hence in-situ testing is considered to be more practical. The most common in situ tests, the standard penetration test (SPT) and the cone penetration test (CPT) yield inexact results due to the greater analytical value of resistance induced by larger particles in gravelly soils13,14. Although liquefaction of gravelly soil has been stated in a variety of earthquakes, there is a paucity of historical survey data available. Most previous research concentrated on constructing liquefaction evaluation models for silty or sandy soils, ignoring the opportunity of liquefaction in gravelly soils.

The latest studies about the process and performance of liquefaction in gravelly soil have shed light on the distinct activating conditions that differentiate it from sandy or silty soil liquefaction3,11,15. For instance, upon examining the similarities between soil profiles, it becomes evident that there is a substantial layer of non-liquefiable sandy gravel with a significant resistance to penetration. This characteristic suggests the presence of a dense soil deposit, which potentially served as a less-permeable covering layer16,17. This layer likely played a role in the generation of elevated excess pore water pressures, ultimately leading to liquefaction18. Additionally, the presence of this less permeable layer prevented the expulsion of sand particles to the ground surface, thereby hindering the observation of liquefaction manifestations19. Due to these inherent distinctions, the liquefaction behavior of gravel soil varies in comparison to that of sandy or silty soil. The assessment of liquefaction potential in this particular soil type is being pursued by researchers and engineers due to the frequent occurrences of seismic liquefaction in gravelly soils after significant earthquakes across the globe20. Nevertheless, there is a dearth of case studies about the liquefaction of gravelly soil, which hinders the improvement of reliable models10,21. Kang et al. examined the liquefaction potential of gravelly soils using artificial neural networks and shear wave velocity (Vs) field data22. Liquefaction cases in the Wenchuan earthquake may occur 94% of the time. Neural networks can model the intricate link between soil parameters, seismic factors, and gravelly soil liquefaction potential, according to the study. Rollins et al. established probabilistic liquefaction resistance curves using 137 data points from depositional environments and 10 earthquakes in seven nations1. A novel magnitude scaling factor (MSF) curve for gravel liquefaction was compatible with prior sand curves. Rollins et al. conducted an evaluation of liquefaction resistance in Avasinis, Italy, by employing four DPT investigations with two different hammer vitalities and measuring shear wave velocity (VS)23. The study site, which experienced liquefaction during the 1976 Friuli earthquake in Italy, consisted of gravelly sand. The DPT accurately anticipated the occurrence of liquefaction at three specific locations where liquefaction was indeed detected. However, it also forecast liquefaction in a geological profile consisting of highly stratified silty gravel and silt, where no evidence of ejecta was observed. The observed failure can be attributed to the “system response” of the profile, which hindered the expulsion of ejecta, as has been observed in comparable stratified sites in New Zealand. In their study, Roy et al. assessed liquefaction resistance at eight sites in Old Valdez and Alaska, Seward, where gravel liquefaction occurred, and at two sites in Valdez, where no liquefaction happened during the 1964 Alaska earthquake24. These results suggest that the DPT can offer cost-effective and accurate liquefaction hazard assessments for several gravelly soils. Liquefaction evaluations using currently obtainable Vs-based triggering curves established for soils frequently specified no liquefaction at sites where liquefaction was observed in Alaska. Lopez et al. concentrated on the site characterization, geotechnical exploration, and analyses conducted for the port’s peripheral wharf embankment, where clear liquefaction indications were observed25. Laboratory results revealed that the embankment was composed of sandy gravel, sand and gravelly sand, limiting the applicability of standard exploration methods such as CPTu and SPT experiments. Then, liquefaction causing assessments were conducted using the Chinese dynamic penetration test (DPT) and Vs, which allow the description of soils with high sediment content. The results of this assessment are showed and contrasted to field explanations to demonstrate that these techniques accurately recognized the liquefaction potential in the embankment.Hu developed two novel-Bayesian Network (BN) models for forecasting gravelly soil liquefaction by linking the best information coefficient with field knowledge depending on the shear wave velocity test and dynamic penetration test databases26. Two new BN models performed significantly better than existing methods or models for forecasting gravelly soil liquefaction in the testing, validation, and training sets, thereby validating the performance of the proposed hybrid approach. Hanandeh et al. presented a comparative assessment for CPT-based liquefaction data based on supervised machine learning26. This study views soil liquefaction as a binary classification problem, whether the soil is liquefied or not, and employs three supervised machine learning classifiers: support vector machine, Decision Trees, and Quadratic Discriminant Analysis. In conclusion, this study proposed straightforward and rapid methods for evaluating the susceptibility of soil to liquefaction without the need for complex calculations.

Aim and objectives

The aim of this research project is to develop reliable and conservative artificial intelligence-based predictive models for assessing the liquefaction potential of gravelly soil sites under seismic loading, using combined seismological, soil, and site profile parameters, in order to support safer and more informed geotechnical engineering decision-making. The objectives of the study are to compile a representative database of liquefaction case histories from previous earthquake records and partition it appropriately for training and validation of predictive models; to apply symbolic regression and machine learning techniques, including response surface methodology, genetic programming, evolutionary polynomial regression, and group method of data handling neural networks, for both regression and classification of liquefaction potential; to evaluate and compare the predictive accuracy, conservatism, and practical reliability of the developed models through misclassification analysis; to identify the most influential parameters governing liquefaction potential in gravelly soils through correlation analysis; and to recommend the most suitable and practical modeling approach for engineering applications based on accuracy, safety, and model simplicity.

Research gap and statement of novelty

The existing body of research on seismic-induced liquefaction has predominantly focused on sandy and silty soils, while liquefaction behaviour in gravelly soils remains comparatively underdeveloped and less accurately modelled. Although gravelly soils are increasingly reported to experience liquefaction during strong earthquake events, the prediction of this phenomenon remains challenging due to their distinct geotechnical characteristics, including high permeability, coarse particle structure, and complex fabric effects that are not adequately captured by conventional empirical formulations. Most widely used liquefaction triggering models have been derived from datasets dominated by sandy soil case histories and are often extended to gravelly soils without explicit calibration for their unique behaviour, leading to reduced reliability and increased uncertainty in practical applications. Conventional in-situ testing methods such as SPT and CPT, which form the basis of many existing liquefaction models, are known to exhibit significant limitations in gravelly soils due to particle interference, penetration resistance bias, and scale effects, resulting in inconsistent and less reliable measurements. Although alternative parameters such as shear wave velocity and dynamic penetration tests have been explored in recent studies, these approaches are still limited by sparse datasets and a lack of comprehensive integration of controlling factors. In addition, many existing studies rely on single modelling techniques or simplified empirical correlations, which do not adequately capture the nonlinear and interacting effects of seismic loading parameters, soil properties, and site profile characteristics. Furthermore, a notable limitation in the current literature is the absence of transparent and interpretable predictive models that can simultaneously provide accurate predictions and explicit parametric expressions suitable for engineering design and decision-making. The novelty of this study lies in addressing these gaps by developing a comprehensive and unified predictive framework for gravelly soil liquefaction potential assessment. This is achieved by integrating seismic parameters, soil index properties, and site profile characteristics within a symbolic regression-based artificial intelligence framework. Unlike previous studies that focus on isolated variables or single modelling approaches, this research employs and comparatively evaluates four advanced modelling techniques, namely Response Surface Methodology, Genetic Programming, Evolutionary Polynomial Regression, and Group Method of Data Handling Neural Networks, using a curated dataset of earthquake-induced liquefaction case histories. The study further strengthens existing approaches by incorporating model interpretability, parametric equation derivation, and misclassification-based safety evaluation to assess conservatism in predictions. In addition, the identification of the most influential parameters governing liquefaction behaviour enhances the physical understanding of gravelly soil response under seismic loading. Overall, this work advances current knowledge by overcoming the limitations of conventional empirical models, addressing data and modelling constraints in existing machine learning applications, and providing a robust, interpretable, and practically applicable framework for the prediction of liquefaction potential in gravelly soils.

Methods

Collected database and preliminary analyses

A global representation of 234 records was collected from observations after earthquakes for suspected liquefaction sites with gravelly soil profile28,29 deposited in a previous literature10. Each record contains the following data: Mw-moment magnitude of earthquake, R-the epicentre distance (km), t-bracketed duration (sec), PGA-the peak ground acceleration (g), G-gravel content (%), F-fines content (%), D50-average particle size (mm), N’120-overburden stress-corrected dynamic penetration test blow count (Blows), σ’v-vertical effective overburden stress (kPa), Dw-depth to the water table (m), Hn-thickness of the impermeable capping layer (m), Dn-thickness of the unsaturated zone between groundwater table and capping layer (m), and LP-liquefaction potential (liquefied or not)28,29. The collected case records were divided into ~ 80% training set (184 records) and ~ 20% validation set (50 records) in line with previous machine learning investigation30,31. The appendix includes the complete dataset. The theoretical framework is presented in Fig. 1, while Tables 1 and 2 summarize their statistical characteristics and the Pearson correlation matrix. Table 1 shows that the compiled database spans a wide and representative range of seismic, soil, and site profile conditions for gravelly soil liquefaction assessment, ensuring the robustness of the developed predictive models28,29. The moment magnitude varies from moderate to strong earthquakes, with average values around 7.7 for both training and validation sets, indicating that the dataset captures events capable of triggering liquefaction28,29. The epicentral distance and bracketed duration exhibit large standard deviations and coefficients of variation, reflecting diverse seismic scenarios from near-field to far-field events and from short to prolonged shaking, which is essential for generalizing model performance28,29. Peak ground acceleration also covers a broad range, confirming the inclusion of both low- and high-intensity ground motions. The soil-related parameters show substantial variability, with gravel content ranging from 0 to 45% and fines content extending up to 90%, highlighting the heterogeneity typical of gravelly soil deposits28,29. The wide spread in mean particle size D50 and dynamic penetration resistance N′120 indicates that the database incorporates loose to dense soil conditions28,29. Similarly, the vertical effective overburden stress and groundwater-related parameters vary significantly, capturing shallow and deep water table conditions as well as differences in impermeable capping layer thickness and unsaturated zone depth28,29. The liquefaction potential values span the full binary range from 0 to 1 in both datasets, and the close agreement between the statistical descriptors of the training and validation sets confirms that data partitioning was balanced and representative, minimizing bias and supporting reliable model training and validation28,29. Table 2 provides insight into the interrelationships among the variables and their influence on liquefaction potential. Strong correlations are observed among several seismic parameters, such as moment magnitude, epicentral distance, and shaking duration, which is consistent with earthquake source and propagation characteristics28,29. Soil gradation parameters show meaningful internal correlations, particularly between fines content and mean particle size, reflecting their inherent physical linkage28,29. The liquefaction potential exhibits its strongest negative correlations with the dynamic penetration test blow count and the vertical effective overburden stress, indicating that denser soils and higher confining stresses significantly reduce the likelihood of liquefaction, which aligns well with established geotechnical understanding28,29. Moderate negative correlations with groundwater depth and unsaturated zone thickness further suggest that deeper water tables and thicker unsaturated layers reduce liquefaction susceptibility28,29. The observed negative correlation does not imply that larger earthquakes reduce the likelihood of liquefaction in a physical sense. Rather, it reflects the characteristics and structure of the compiled case-history database as well as the interaction between seismic demand parameters. In liquefaction studies, earthquake magnitude is not an independent driver of liquefaction but is coupled with other parameters such as peak ground acceleration (PGA), distance to the source (R), and duration-related effects. Larger magnitude earthquakes are often associated with greater epicentral distances in recorded case histories, which leads to attenuation of ground motion intensity at the site. As a result, even though Mw is high, the effective cyclic stress imposed on the soil may be lower due to distance attenuation, producing a reduced liquefaction response in the dataset. Additionally, magnitude scaling effects are already indirectly embedded within parameters such as PGA and shaking duration (t), which more directly control the cyclic stress ratio responsible for triggering liquefaction. When these parameters are explicitly included in the model, Mw may exhibit an apparent inverse relationship due to multicollinearity and redistribution of influence among correlated seismic variables. This leads to a statistical negative correlation, even though the underlying physical relationship remains consistent with established liquefaction theory. Furthermore, the dataset composition may include moderate-to-large magnitude events recorded at sites with relatively dense or high-resistance soils, where liquefaction did not occur, thereby reinforcing the negative trend. This reflects the dominance of site-specific resistance parameters, such as σ’v and N’120, over magnitude alone in controlling liquefaction triggering. This clarification has been incorporated into the revised manuscript to ensure that the statistical observation is properly interpreted within the context of seismic soil behaviour and database characteristics.

In contrast, peak ground acceleration shows a positive correlation with liquefaction potential, confirming the role of seismic demand in triggering liquefaction28,29. Overall, the correlation analysis confirms the physical consistency of the database, supports the selection of input parameters, and demonstrates that liquefaction potential in gravelly soils is governed by a complex interaction between seismic loading, soil resistance, and site conditions rather than any single controlling factor28,29. Research plan

In line with previous machine learning methods18,19,30,31, four different symbolic regression techniques were used to predict the liquefaction potential (LP) of gravelly soil sites using the collected database. These techniques are the response surface methodology (RSM)18,19, genetic programming (GP)30,31, three models of group method of data handling neural network (GMDH-NN) and evolutionary polynomial regression (EPR)30,31. Flowcharts for the used RSM, GP, GMDH-NN and EPR techniques are presented in Figs. 3 and 4. All the four developed models were used to predict the liquefaction potential (LP) using seismological parameters (Mw, R, t, PGA), soil parameters (G, F, D50) and site profile parameters (N’120, σ’v, Dw, Hn, Dn) as predictors28,29. The following section discusses the results of each model. The accuracies of the proposed models were evaluated by comparing the misclassified cases (error, %) between predicted and observed values of LP.

Practical applications

The findings of this research project have direct practical applications in geotechnical earthquake engineering, particularly in the assessment and mitigation of liquefaction hazards in gravelly soil deposits. The developed AI-based predictive models provide engineers with efficient tools for evaluating liquefaction potential using commonly available seismic, soil, and site parameters, thereby reducing reliance on costly and time-consuming large-scale laboratory testing and extensive in situ investigations. This is especially beneficial for projects located in seismically active regions where gravelly soils are prevalent and traditional evaluation methods such as SPT and CPT may yield unreliable results due to particle size effects. The parametric and classification models proposed in this study can be integrated into preliminary site investigation and feasibility stages to rapidly screen large areas for liquefaction susceptibility. By identifying zones with a higher likelihood of liquefaction, engineers can prioritize detailed investigations and allocate resources more effectively. The conservative nature of selected models, particularly those with lower positive error rates, supports safer decision-making in foundation design, ground improvement selection, and seismic risk management for critical infrastructure such as bridges, ports, embankments, highways, and earth dams. In practical design scenarios, the models can assist engineers in selecting appropriate mitigation measures, including densification, drainage improvement, or reinforcement techniques, by providing quantitative estimates of liquefaction potential under different seismic loading conditions. The explicit mathematical expressions derived from symbolic regression approaches, such as EPR and GP, are particularly useful for hand calculations, spreadsheet-based design checks, and incorporation into existing geotechnical design workflows without the need for specialized software. From a sustainability perspective, the proposed models contribute to more resilient and cost-effective infrastructure by enabling early identification of liquefaction risks and reducing the likelihood of post-earthquake damage and reconstruction. By improving the accuracy and reliability of liquefaction assessments in gravelly soils, this research supports the development of safer foundations, optimized ground improvement strategies, and long-term performance of structures subjected to seismic loading, ultimately enhancing the sustainability and resilience of built environments in earthquake-prone regions.

Limitations and recommendation for future research

Despite the promising performance and interpretability of the proposed models, this study is subject to several limitations that should be acknowledged. The most notable constraint is the relatively limited size of the compiled database, which, although carefully curated from documented case histories, may not fully capture the complete variability of gravelly soil behaviour under diverse seismic and geological conditions. This limitation may influence the generalisability of the developed models when applied to regions or conditions not well represented in the dataset. In addition, the database is derived from reported case histories, which inherently include uncertainties related to measurement accuracy, reporting inconsistencies, and simplifications in recorded field parameters. Variations in testing procedures, equipment calibration, and interpretation of parameters such as penetration resistance and effective stress may introduce bias into the modelling process. Another limitation lies in the structure of the modelling approaches themselves. While symbolic regression techniques such as GP, EPR, and GMDH-NN provide interpretable equations, they may still produce mathematically valid expressions that lack strict physical constraints, occasionally resulting in non-physical outputs such as negative liquefaction potential values. Although these are addressed through classification thresholds, they highlight the absence of hard physical boundaries within the modelling framework. Furthermore, the models are developed using a predefined set of input parameters, and other potentially influential factors, such as detailed grain shape characteristics, fines plasticity, or complex stratigraphic layering effects, are not explicitly incorporated due to data unavailability. This may limit the completeness of the predictive relationships. Finally, the study focuses on model development and comparative performance evaluation rather than full-scale probabilistic or reliability-based design integration. As such, uncertainty quantification is addressed indirectly through validation strategies rather than through formal probabilistic frameworks. Future research incorporating larger datasets, expanded parameter spaces, and probabilistic analyses would further enhance the robustness and applicability of the proposed models.

Future research should focus on expanding the available database of gravelly soil liquefaction case histories by incorporating data from additional earthquakes, geological settings, and depositional environments to further enhance model generalizability and robustness. Particular attention should be given to underrepresented regions and deep gravelly deposits, where liquefaction behavior may differ due to stress history, aging effects, and complex stratigraphy. The inclusion of high-quality post-earthquake field investigations would significantly improve the reliability of future predictive models. Further studies are recommended to integrate advanced in situ testing techniques, such as seismic dilatometer tests, surface wave methods, and hybrid geophysical–penetration approaches, to overcome the limitations of conventional SPT and CPT in gravelly soils. Combining these measurements with AI-based frameworks may improve the physical interpretability of predictions and reduce uncertainty associated with indirect measurements of soil resistance. Future work should also explore the coupling of probabilistic and reliability-based approaches with machine learning models to explicitly account for uncertainty in seismic loading, soil properties, and boundary conditions. This would allow for risk-informed liquefaction assessments and performance-based design applications, which are increasingly required for critical infrastructure in seismic regions. The development of explainable and physics-informed machine learning models is another important direction for future research. Embedding geotechnical principles, stress–strain relationships, and pore pressure generation mechanisms into AI architectures could enhance transparency, improve acceptance by practicing engineers, and ensure consistency with established design concepts. Finally, future research should investigate the applicability of the proposed models within regional and national seismic hazard frameworks and design codes. Validation against independent datasets and comparison with code-based liquefaction triggering methods would support potential calibration factors or guideline development, facilitating the practical adoption of AI-assisted liquefaction prediction tools in routine engineering practice.
