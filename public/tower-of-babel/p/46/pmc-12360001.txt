A mixed-method exploratory assessment of provider-based stigma of opioid use disorder (OUD) and support for medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) in carceral settings
Authors: Katherine J McLean, Nathan E Kruis, Eric Weintraub, Heather Fitzsimons, Christopher Welsh, Natalie Spicyn, Brook Kearley, William Altekruse, Nicholas Rowland, Annabelle Belcher
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundIn 2019, the state of Maryland passed legislation requiring local detention centers to offer medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD) to all incarcerated individuals who met criteria for opioid use disorder (OUD). Four years later, many facilities remain out of compliance. Beyond issues of staffing, funding, and technical support, stigma may represent a barrier to MOUD implementation.MethodsUsing surveys and interviews among correctional professionals working in Maryland, the current exploratory study sought to identify barriers to MOUD implementation, including measures of provider-based stigma of OUD and support for MOUD.ResultsQuantitative and qualitative results revealed moderately high levels of OUD-related stigma and negative attitudes toward MOUD, with multivariable modeling revealing a statistically significant relationship between provider-based stigma of OUD and support for MOUD in correctional settings.ConclusionThese findings suggest that stigma may be a significant barrier to MOUD implementation in local detention centers, even in the presence of legal mandates. Further, these results reveal the need for educational and anti-stigma campaigns targeted at correctional professionals who are charged with implementing policies that may contradict their previous learning or correctional training.Keywords: Opioid use disorder (OUD), Medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD), Corrections, Treatment, Stigma

Background

The opioid crisis has had an unprecedented impact on American healthcare and criminal legal systems. Current data suggests that approximately 3 million Americans are living with opioid use disorder (OUD) [1]. Nationally, drug overdoses are responsible for more than 100,000 deaths annually in the United States [2]. Opioids play a role in about 75% of these deaths, which is the most of all drugs [3]. Moreover, many people who use opioids (PWUO) are also involved with the criminal-legal system. A significant number of arrests every year involve nonmedical use/possession of opioids and/or heroin, and it is estimated that between 17 and 19% of everyone sentenced to incarceration regularly use opioids [4, 5].

Medications for Opioid Use Disorder (MOUD), including methadone and buprenorphine, represent the ‘gold-standard’ treatment for OUD; a third MOUD, naltrexone, is additionally approved for the treatment of OUD in the United States [6, 7]. Unfortunately, data suggest that many individuals living with opioid use disorder do not receive or have access to these types of treatment [8]. Regarding the criminal legal system, MOUD has been significantly underutilized in carceral settings and MOUD, especially agonist treatment, is rarely offered as standard of care to criminal legal-involved individuals [9, 10]. This is despite research demonstrating MOUD in correctional settings is associated with reductions in post-release overdose deaths, and improvements in post-release reintegrative outcomes; when combined with counseling, pre-release methadone maintenance specifically may reduce self-reported illegal opioid use, injection drug use, and criminal activity following reentry [11–15]. Scholarship to date has identified multiple barriers to MOUD integration and uptake in correctional settings at the institutional, systemic, programmatic, and attitudinal levels, with stigma—held by correctional administrators, front-line staff, and criminal legal-involved individuals—suspected among the chief impediments [16–18]. criminal legalcriminal legalYet little empirical work has assessed the relationship between provider-based stigma of OUD and perceptions of MOUD in correctional settings, and specifically facilities where MOUD implementation has been legally mandated. Accordingly, using a sample of correctional personnel working in Maryland detention centers, the current study sought to help fill this gap in the literature by examining the relationship between provider-based stigma of OUD and support for various forms of MOUD treatment in a state where local detentions centered were first ordered to roll out MOUD in 2019. At the onset it is important to note that this study is exploratory in nature, an extension of our earlier pilot project at one institution, and utilized a mixed-method approach to assess these concepts.

Literature review

Medications for opioid use disorder (MOUD)

MOUD, particularly agonist pharmacotherapy, is considered to be the “gold standard” treatment for OUD [7, 8]. Currently, there are three FDA-approved medications for OUD: naltrexone, methadone, and buprenorphine [19]. Methadone and buprenorphine are characterized as agonists to the body’s opioid receptor, the μ receptor, meaning that they stimulate it in a manner similar to the effects of other medical or illegal opioids. While methadone is administered daily, as a liquid, powder, or diskette, buprenorphine may be consumed as a daily sublingual tablet (Subutex) or strip (Suboxone), or as an extended-release weekly or monthly injection (Sublocade). Naltrexone (brand-name Vivitrol), on the other hand, is an opioid receptor antagonist, meaning it blocks or prevents other opioids from stimulating the μ receptor [20]. Thus, in theory, someone with OUD who is prescribed naltrexone would have no need to take illegal opioids, as the drugs would be prevented from producing euphoric effects. It should be noted, however, that mounting research has revealed naltrexone treatment to be less effective in preventing opioid overdose and promoting treatment retention when compared to both methadone and buprenorphine [21, 22].

All three of these medications have demonstrated some degree of efficacy for treating OUD. For instance, researchers have found MOUD to be effective at increasing sobriety while decreasing risk for overdose deaths [23–26]. Further, incorporated as part of a holistic treatment protocol, MOUD has also been found to help reduce the spread of infectious diseases, such as HIV and HCV [27–30]. Moreover, research has consistently demonstrated that the use of MOUD in carceral settings is associated with improvements in post-release outcomes, including increases in treatment engagement in the community and reductions in opioid use and overdoses without compromising risk for recidivism [31]. Lack of MOUD treatment during incarceration forces individuals with OUD into a state of abstinence, lowering their opioid tolerance, and increasing their risk for overdose and reincarceration post-release [32]. Indeed, research has shown incarcerated individuals to be at an elevated risk of opioid-related mortality post institutional release, especially in the early weeks of community reentry, with one recent scoping review estimating that individuals leaving prison faced a pooled standardized mortality ratio of 27.07 from drug-related causes (compared to the general population) within the first two weeks after release [33–35]. However, effective MOUD treatment, particularly agonist treatment, in jails and prisons has been shown to decrease this risk [14, 36]. Across modalities of MOUD, partnership with community-based organizations that offer aftercare (post-release) has shown to be a best practice [37, 38].

In recent years, federal policies, as well as recent lawsuits, have affirmed the right of incarcerated individuals to continue on MOUD while in prison or jail, with the Department of Justice listing OUD as a disability covered by the Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) in 2022 [39–41]. Nevertheless, MOUD are rarely offered as standard-of care treatments to criminal legal-involved or criminal legal-referred individuals, despite initiatives like the Criminal Justice Drug Abuse Treatment Studies (CJ-DATS), which has sought to promote the integration of evidence-based treatment within the criminal legal settings since 2002 [9, 14, 16, 18, 42–44]. Policies related to the use of MOUD vary greatly by jurisdiction. There is little consistency in MOUD policy, and many courts and correctional administrators seem to make the decision on who can receive MOUD, and which formulation, on a “case-by-case” basis [45, 46]. Generally, though, research shows specialty courts (i.e., drug courts) and correctional agencies seem to have more policies toward MOUD continuation than initiation, and for antagonist treatment via naltrexone than agonist treatment via methadone or buprenorphine [45, 47–50]. This is concerning given recent research indicating naltrexone may not be as safe or effective as buprenorphine or methadone for treating OUD, and some individuals with OUD prefer, use, and/or rely on agonist medications [21, 50–52].

Criminal legal professionals’ attitudes toward MOUD

While research on criminal legal professionals’ perceptions of MOUD is limited, the available scholarly work suggests that negative attitudes toward MOUD may be pervasive in the criminal legal system and prevent access to MOUD in carceral settings [53]. Scholars have found that many criminal legal professionals view agonist treatment as a “substitute addiction” or “as substituting one addiction for another” and express support for antagonist treatment via naltrexone or non-pharmacological treatment over MOUD [47, 54–57]. Such beliefs have been recognized as significant barriers to MOUD use in carceral settings, in addition to stigma attached to drug use [16, 58]. Concerns for diversion and abuse, as well as preference for reserving MOUD treatment only for those with “severe OUD” (i.e., history of injection drug use) also seem to be prevalent in this population [57, 59–61]. Ironically, recent studies have suggested that inadequate access to MOUD to incarcerated populations may exacerbate diversion, while some studies have reported high levels of nonmedical opioid use among currently incarcerated individuals [62]. Specificially, a 2021 study of recently-incarcerated individuals New York City and Baltimore found that 63% of participants affirmed nonmedical opioid use during their period in prison or jail [63].

For instance, in their mixed-method study of “community stakeholders” working in Michigan, including criminal legal professionals, Victor et al. [57] found that participants expressed greater support for naltrexone than for buprenorphine or methadone use in criminal legal populations, and that some held skepticism toward MOUD being used as a long-term treatment option. However, training on MOUD seemed to be related to more positive perceptions of MOUD use, while stigma was found to be negatively associated with attitudes toward MOUD. Similar findings were reported by Andraka-Christou and Atkins [55] in their study of problem-solving courts in Florida and by Andraka-Christou et al. [54] in their study of court personnel in Indiana. Specifically, the former study found professional preference for naltrexone, paired counseling with MOUD, and tapering plans for MOUD patients, and concluded that “court staff beliefs about MOUD do not necessarily reflect the scientific evidence about MOUD” [55, p.754]. Likewise, in addition to finding preference for agonist over antagonist treatment in Indiana, the latter project found education and training to be associated with more positive attitudes toward medication treatment [54]. Familiarty may also play a role in shaping attitudes toward MOUD; in their multi-county analysis of jail and treatment staff attitudes toward MOUD, Blue et al. [64] found participants’ attitudes improved following implementation of MOUD treatment plans in their detention centers.

Related, in their study of law enforcement officers’ and other first responders’ perceptions of MOUD, Kruis et al. [6] found addiction attitudes, specifically, supporting the disease model of addiction, to be correlated with favorable views of medication treatment, while direct measures of stigma of OUD were related to negative perceptions of medication treatment. More recently, in their empirical examination of the relationship between stigma and criminal legal staff perceptions of MOUD, Moore et al. [65, p. 5] found that while professionals in their study generally self-reported positive views of agonist treatment, there was a significant negative relationship observed in multivariable modeling between stigma of criminal legal-involved persons (generally) and support for MOUD use (broadly) in criminal legal-involved populations [65]. Such recent findings are novel, yet characterized by methodological limitations, including the use of convenience samples comprised of “personal contacts” and self-identifying individuals working “at a criminal justice agency” collected from various social media platforms; broad conceptualization of the term “criminal legal staff” (including judges, probation officers, nurses, and administrative staff from courts, probation offices, and jails); and omission of naltrexone from their MOUD-attitude scale, precluding the generalizability of findings. Given these limitations, the authors called for future research in this area and work which examines “each type of MOUD,” including naltrexone. The current study helps to heed this call.

Current study

The prior research in this area has been scant, and to the best of our knowledge, no study to date has directly focused on exploring the attitudes of criminal legal and treatment professionals who work in correctional settings in which MOUD is—or could be—used to treat criminal legal-involved populations with OUD and the role stigma plays in shaping these attitudes. This is a glaring omission made by scholars, as many professionals in these roles are in decision-making positions that can influence the standard of care and treatment criminal legal-involved persons with OUD receive, or do not receive, while they are incarcerated. Moreover, given that prior research has found that jail staff holding negative attitudes toward MOUD is associated with negative treatment outcomes for patients, including MOUD provision, it is important to study staff attitudes as they appear to influence behaviors [53].

Thus, in an extension of the literature summarized above, the main objective of this exploratory study was to measure criminal legal professionals’ support for MOUD in correctional settings while also assessing the relationship between provider-based stigma of OUD and support for various forms of MOUD treatment in correctional settings in Maryland following the passing of Maryland Law §9–603, a state law mandating MOUD provision in all jails/detention centers in Maryland. In this context, it is important to note that for the five jails that were included in this study, determinations regarding the appropriate treatment of opioid use disorder (including initiation, maintenance and decisions regarding treatment modality) were individualized for patients. The attending medical providers have the ultimate and sole authority to determine an incarcerated patient’s status in treatment, with a focus on what is most beneficial and therapeutic for the patient, and is ideally a shared decision-making process with the patient. Specifically, this study was driven by the following research questions:

Do correctional personnel in Maryland detention facilities stigmatize (i.e., hold negative attitudes or describe in negative terms) PWUO?

Given findings from our previous work and other scholars, we anticipated finding measurable levels of provider-based stigma of OUD in our sample of correctional professionals [54, 55, 65–67]. We hypothesized that participants would favor antagonist over agonist MOUD treatment and that there would be an inverse relationship between provider-based stigma of OUD and support for MOUD in correctional settings. That is, we speculated at the onset of this project that correctional professionals who held higher levels of opioid-related stigma would be less supportive of MOUD treatment in detention centers, especially agonist MOUD treatment. We also hypothesized that those who supported the medical addiction framework would be more supportive of MOUD treatment, while those who attributed drug dependency to a lack of “will power” or “morality” would be less supportive of MOUD treatment in prisons and jails than those with different belief systems.

Methods

Sample

All research activities for this study were approved by the Pennsylvania State University Institutional Review Board (IRB) (approval no. STUDY00020800) on August 23, 2022. The sample used in this study consisted of correctional professionals (i.e., correctional officers, administrators, reentry staff including social workers, treatment staff including peer support workers, medical staff, etc.) working in five different jails and county detention centers for adults (or juveniles aged 15–17 facing adult charges) located in Maryland. All centers provided at least one form of MOUD treatment at the time of data collection. In the months before data collection, members of the study team contacted the warden at each facility, to request their participation in the study, as well as assistance in recruiting their correctional staff. A mixed-method approach was used to collect data. Specifically, an original survey developed and administered by members of the research team was used as the primary instrument of data collection. The survey consisted of Likert scale, text entry, and short response questions/statements designed to capture measures of provider-based stigma, addiction frameworks, perceptions of MOUD, and general demographic characteristics. The survey was administered via Qualtrics using a single useable link sent to professionals through their work email address by the warden at each facility. Participants were compensated with a $20 electronic gift card for completing the survey. All survey participants were then asked if they would be interested in participating in in-depth interviews via Zoom to provide greater perspective on these topics. Those who indicated “yes” (n = 29) were asked to provide their name and contact information (in a survey separated from the response data) to schedule an individual interview with a member of the research team. Interview participants were compensated with a $40 electronic gift card. In total, 81 correctional professionals clicked on the survey link and 64 submitted usable survey data, representing approximately a 79% completion rate. Overall, our survey sample represented more than 20% of all the workers employed at the five correctional centers. Further, 13 participants completed individual interviews with a member of the research team, representing approximately a 45% response rate for those who expressed interest in the individual interview. Interviews were conducted by a member of the research team trained in qualitative methods, and followed an interview guide intended to capture support for MOUD in correctional and community settings. While the interview guide was developed in collaboration with research team members currently working in Maryland detention centers, it additionally included items intended to capture variants of provider-based stigma (blame, dangerousness, desire for social distance and fatalism) that have been deployed in a previous study [68]. All interviewees stated their verbal consent to participate at the beginning of each interview, with interviews running, on average, just over one hour.

Measures

Attitude toward MOUD

The main outcome measure of interest for this study was respondents’ self-reported attitude toward MOUD. A revised measure adapted from Kruis et al. (2021) was used to capture this construct [6]. Specifically, participants were asked to indicate their level of agreement with the following 6 statements: (1) “MOUD is an effective form of substance use treatment for inmates,” (2) “Inmates with OUD should be provided with MOUD,” (3) “The use of MOUD in jails and detention centers is a good investment for society,” (4) “I support the use of Methadone as treatment for OUD in jails and detention centers,” (5) “I support the use of Buprenorphine (aka Suboxone) as treatment for OUD in jails and detention centers,” and (6) “ I support the use of Naltrexone (aka Vivitrol) as treatment for OUD in jails and detention centers.” Response options followed a standard Likert scale ranging from 1 = “Strongly Disagree” to 5 = “Strongly Agree.” Consistent with Kruis et al.’s [6] analysis, a comprehensive scale measure capturing participants’ ‘Attitude toward MOUD’ was created by summing and averaging responses to all 6 statements listed above (α = 0.959).

Addiction frameworks

The survey also captured measures of addiction frameworks, conceptualized in prior research as a participant’s endorsement of addiction being the product of a medical, moral, or will-related problem [66, 69]. To measure addiction frameworks, participants were asked to indicate their level of agreement that (1) “Drug dependency is a medical problem” (medical framework) and (2) “Lack of willpower is a major factor contributing to drug dependency” (will-related framework). Participants were also asked to indicate whether they believed that “lacking good moral values contributes to drug addiction” (morality framework). Responses to these statements were dichotomized for analysis purposes such that 1 = “Indicated Agreement” and 0 = “Did not indicate agreement.”

Stigma

Stigma is considered to be a significant barrier to MOUD implementation in carceral settings [16]. Provider-based stigma of OUD was the primary independent variable of interest in this study. A revised 18-item scale variable capturing components of dangerousness, blame, social distance, and fatalism taken from Kruis et al.’s validated scales (2021, 2024) was used to measure provider-based stigma of OUD [6, 69]. Items included, “People who misuse opioids are a threat to the safety of our community” (dangerousness), “People who regularly misuse opioids are usually responsible for their own condition” (blame), “I would be fine letting someone who had a history of opioid misuse marry into my family” (social distance), and “Treating persons addicted to opioids seems futile (incapable of producing any useful result)” (fatalism). Response options followed a Likert scale ranging from 1 = “Strongly Disagree” to 5 = “Strongly Agree.” Responses were summed and averaged to create a scale measure of provider-based OUD stigma with higher numbers indicative of greater stigma (α = 0.896).

Demographics/controls

Measures of sex (0 = “female,” 1 = “male”), race (0 = “non-White,” 1 = “White”), political conservativism (0 = “left-leaning liberal,” 10 = “ right-leaning conservative”), having a college degree (0 = “no,” 1 = “yes”), and completion of a course on substance use dependency (0 = “no,” 1 = “yes”) were captured and included as control measures in analyses.

More than a third of the sample reported having a college degree (38.7%) and most had completed at least one class on substance use dependency (61.3%). In the aggregate, participants reported moderate political beliefs (M = 5.41, SD = 2.43). Analytic strategy

The quantitative data analysis for this project was completed using IBM SPSS ver. 29. The analysis consisted of four main steps. First, data were inspected, cleaned, and coded appropriately immediately following the end of the survey data collection period. Second, preliminary analyses, consisting of calculating measures of central tendency and dispersion, as well as factor analyses (EFAs) and estimates of internal consistency, were then used to assist with construct formation and to assist in answering the first two research questions, which were primarily descriptive in nature. Third, bivariate analyses (i.e., t-tests, correlations, etc.) were used as preliminary assessments of relationships between variables of interest. Fourth, Hierarchical Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) multiple regression was used to examine the additive effects of correlates of our dependent measure at the multivariable level. Following OLS modeling, Post Hoc Power Analyses were completed using G*Power ver. 3.1.9.7. Results indicated power was not an issue in modeling. For the concurrent qualitative analysis, verbatim transcripts produced by Zoom were checked and corrected against the original audio files, and then subjected to by a team of 5 coders who met regularly to resolve questions and discuss emergent themes [70–75]. Rapid assessment, a method of qualitative analysis increasingly prevalent in health service implementation research, enables the timely understanding of qualitative data, and particularly narrative data from service providers themselves; it further facilitates a deep contextual understanding of the data, in requiring researchers to summarize and subsequently thematize all coded text. As in rapid assessment processes more generally, this study employed the interview guide to develop a set of a priori codes that reflected each question (ex. “perceptions of PWUO”), which were then applied to the full set of interview transcripts by 5 members of the study team. Unlike more traditional forms of qualitative coding, which codes or labels blocks of text within interview transcripts or other documents, rapid assessment utilizes a qualitative matrix, wherein relevant blocks of text are both copied under each code, then summarized, and finally collaboratively interpreted for emergent themes (ex. “PWUO may endanger others while under the influence”) [76]. While modest in number, thematic saturation was achieved by the final interview in our sample, a result in keeping with recent studies [77]. All names presented in the qualitative data are pseudonyms.

Results

Stigma of opioid and substance use disorder

Regarding our first research question, participants indicated moderately high levels of provider-based stigma of OUD in the survey (M = 3.37, SD = 0.740). Stigma of OUD was also present in a majority of interviews, with every dimension of provider-based stigma voiced across the sample. Most common were attributions of blameworthiness toward incarcerated individuals with OUD and substance use disorder (SUD) more generally. While few interviewees characterized PWUO as culpable for the initiation of substance use, many stated the belief that they were morally responsible for their own recovery. When asked to identify barriers to recovery among persons with OUD, a variety of participants individualized blame, while also disclaiming professional responsibility. While Nina, a social worker, cited “lack of motivation,” a Certified Peer Recovery Specialist, Samuel, identified “mindset” as a key impediment. Such thoughts were echoed by correctional staff; while Matthew, a Correctional Officer Sergeant saw “sheer willpower” as a barrier to recovery, his CO colleague in the pretrial division further emphasized the importance of individual desire in achieving abstinence:

They have to want it. You can’t help somebody that doesn’t want to be helped... I can’t make you go in, I can’t make you want to be clean. (Alyssa, Correctional Officer, Pretrial Division).

Nearly half of interviewees also described PWUO in ways that evoked dangerousness. On the one hand, persons with OUD were described as a persistent safety threat in a correctional context, due to the inherent volatility and unpredictability of their actions. While Cara, a social worker and reentry coordinator linked individuals’ “erratic behaviors” to being “high out of their minds” when arrested and booked, Sandy, a CO working in the classification department, also identified withdrawal and frustration (at being incarcerated) as predictors of dangerousness:

They’re going through a lot, so it’s kind of hard to talk to them as well. Some are angry and they’re taking that emotion out on you. So that’s a risk for safety as well. (Sandy, Correctional Officer, Classification)

Dangerousness was also invoked in statements concerning the criminality of PWUO. While most incarcerated persons with OUD were described as entering the system on controlled substance or property charges, some interviewees, like Martin, a Certified Peer Recovery Specialist, highlighted their potential for violent offenses as well.

I see a lot of people under the influence, um, get into getting more violent charges as well. Um, and a lot of those are even connected to them trying to get more drugs. (Martin, Certified Peer Recovery Specialist)

Likewise, correctional staff emphasized that PWUO were likely to commit crimes beyond those directly linked to substance use, lamenting the chronic nature of their offending.

Most of these people, they know that they’re gonna go out and commit other crimes. They might not tell their mom, their grandmother, their significant other, or us that, but if you talk to them real deep in conversation, you know, there comes a time where you’ll see it. (Daniel, Lieutenant Operations Commander)

While less common, a sense of fatalism was also expressed among a handful of interviewees, who described PWUO as enmired in their condition, regardless of treatment participation. Fatalistic descriptions of PWOU often cited the interviewees’ professional experience, which showed them that recovery was an unlikely, if not impossible outcome. Asked to gauge the potential of persons with OUD to recover, diverse personnel offered pessimistic estimates. While Kurt, a long-time CO in the Booking Department offered that “probably 10%” of PWUO achieved long-term recovery, Daniel (quoted above) guessed that “once a year maybe, or twice a year” a formerly incarcerated individual would be seen doing well in the community.

Interestingly, only one interview participant (quoted twice previously) voiced a desire for social distance from PWUO, a finding that may reflect the prevalence of personal proximity to OUD within the qualitative sample. In fact, nearly one-third of interviewees identified a close friend, partner, or family member who had struggled with opioid use. While their personal experience with OUD appeared to influence their understanding of addiction, beliefs around treatment, and support for MOUD, it did not result in a reluctance to associate with PWUO. Instead, the single participant articulating a desire for social distance referenced their professional experience. Asked to speculate on their reaction upon discovering that a close social contact used opioids nonmedically, Sandy, a CO quoted above, offered:

I wouldn’t really trust someone. That’s because I’ve seen what people do that’s on it. I’ve seen how it, especially in a jail, how it affects, like…they do all kinds of things. And my trust level would be different. I probably wouldn’t love them the same, to be honest. (Sandy, Correctional Officer, Classification)

Support for MOUD

Our second research question concerned assessing support for different forms of MOUD. Table 2 displays responses to survey items assessing support for the use of methadone, buprenorphine, and naltrexone treatments in jails and detention centers. As can be seen, most participants indicated a lack of support for buprenorphine (56%) and methadone (56%) treatment in jails and detention centers. However, a majority of participants expressed support for naltrexone treatment in jails and detention centers. Both McNemar’s and Cochran’s Q tests revealed statistically similar support for buprenorphine and methadone treatment in jails and detention centers (p >0.10), but statistically different levels of support for Naltrexone treatment compared to both Methadone and Buprenorphine treatment in jails and detention centers (p < 0.01). Thus, as hypothesized, these findings show that correctional professionals support antagonist treatment more than agonist treatment in correctional settings.

In the qualitative dataset, the vast majority of participants expressed ambivalence around the use of MOUD in correctional settings, with only one participant voicing unabashed support, and three rejecting such forms of treatment with a broad brush. As in the survey, interview results revealed more explicit support for naltrexone, as well as long-acting, injectable forms of MOUD such as Sublocade. Importantly, interviewees’ stated reasons for preferring naltrexone and Sublocade resonated with some of the stigmatizing conceptions around PWUO described above, namely their blameworthiness, dangerousness, and general untrustworthiness. First, some participants, such as Cara, a social worker quoted previously, inferred sincere motivation to recover among individuals who sought out long-acting treatments:

I find it interesting in here the people that we have request the Sublocade shot. Those are kind of the real deal to me. It’s like, okay. You don’t want to get high. You want this to be a long term thing. You know those people are at a different mind level with their addiction. (Cara, Social Worker)

Other expressions of support for injectable formulations, and naltrexone in particular, implied a mistrust of agonist treatments that may be used for the purposes of intoxication. Nearly every participant stated concerns around the diversion of oral medications, which were seen as possibly pleasurable, and thus attractive to a diverse swath of the incarcerated population. By contrast, naltrexone was praised as not only non-intoxicating, but also potentially punitive due to the presence of naloxone. Interviewees speculated that such pharmacological properties made naltrexone more effective on an intuitive level. While Nina, a social worker, likened naltrexone to an undesirable ingredient in an otherwise favorite food, her colleague Grace, a Licensed Clinical Social Worker, opined that naltrexone—by forestalling any opioid intoxication—allowed individuals to overcome their cravings and pursue other interests:

I think [the chances of recovery are] probably higher when, if you have a blocker and you’re not getting the same, response from the drugs that you’re used to. I mean, I’m not an addict, so I can’t tell you like, the craving stuff. I don’t know about all the side effects, but I guess putting something that I don’t like to eat in food that you like to eat, you know, eventually I’m gonna stop liking it. (Nina, Social Worker)

Maybe offering them something like Vivitrol, they can no longer get high, then maybe they’ll be interested in other things. (Grace, Licensed Clinical Social Worker)

These statements imply that persons with opioid use disorder cannot be trusted with a medication that offered the possibility of pleasure—or perhaps, that they did not deserve such a form of treatment. (They also might be seen as expressing a misunderstanding of how MOUD, or OUD itself, works). At the same time, other participants seemed to simply prefer the logistics of naltrexone or Sublocade administration, which didn’t require daily lockdowns or movement of incarcerated individuals throughout the jail. Instead, these long-acting medications added minimal complications to the work of correctional officers in particular. Referencing the brand name for injectable naltrexone, Kurt, an experienced CO, explained:

Vivitrol is good because it’s once a month rather than every day (Kurt, Correctional Officer).

While there were no direct throughlines connecting stigma, support for MOUD, and addiction frameworks in the qualitative data, different elements of provider-based stigma infused participants’ notions of who should be eligible to receive MOUD, and in what contexts; moreover, while nearly all interviewees endorsed the medical model of addiction in direct questioning, other statements demonstrated the way in which moralistic understandings of opioid use as a choice persisted. For example, when asked whether individuals who resumed nonmedical opioid use should continue to receive MOUD, otherwise ardent supporters waivered. Cara, the social worker and Reentry Coordinator already quoted, believed that such second chances were “wasted,” on individuals who were “choosing” to continue use:

I think they should not be giving be given MOUD services anymore, because if they’re continuing to get high and choose that, it’s like, why was this resource wasted on you when you you’re clearly not choosing to, to use that. (Cara, Social Worker/Reentry Coordinator)

Others, such as Matthew, a Correctional Officer Sergeant, believed MOUD to be an inappropriate offering in correctional facilities specifically, describing such programs as an unfair burden upon taxpayers:

I don’t like having taxpayers pay for something like that. Why do I have to pay for your your addiction? I don’t. That’s a personal standpoint. You know, it’s like, I like drinking beer. I like bourbon. I don’t ask you to pay for it, so why should I have to pay for your stuff. (Matthew, Correctional Officer Sergeant)

Moreover, individuals who did not endorse the chronic disease model of addiction were not necessarily opposed to the use MOUD in jails. All supporters of MOUD in their respective facilities, Sandy, a novice CO, described addiction as a “choice that went wrong,” while her correctional colleague Kurt characterized it as “an illness that can go away.” Samuel, a Certified Peer Recovery Specialist and ardent defender of harm reduction offered a more contextual understanding of addiction as enmired in “certain environments [and] certain mentalities.”

One predictor of MOUD skepticism unique to the qualitative dataset was correctional rank and position. Three interview participants roundly rejected the use of MOUD in jails and detentions centers, with all reporting promotion to a higher rank or a more prestigious location within their facility. While citing unique reasons and logics for their dismissal of MOUD treatment, each rejection may be seen to resonate with the responsibilities of their role. Matthew, a Correctional Officer Sergeant concerning with maintaining institutional security, bemoaned diverted buprenorphine as “probably the worst thing in our facility. It’s like a needle in a hay stack to find it.” Sandy, a CO in classification, described the pure logistical nuisance of obtaining MOUD for newly-detained individuals, whom she believed “want to get high” after getting incarcerated. At the highest level, Daniel, a Lieutenant Correctional Officer, spouted resentment at the state law that mandated the availability of MOUD, the importance of which he minimized:

I get the law. I get all this other crap. But bottom line is, you know, Subutex, Suboxone, the whole nine, methadone, whatever. It’s just a tool. It’s a small, small tool that unfortunately society is making it into this ‘oh-my-God-miracle.’ You know, and it’s not. It’s a small minute tool in the whole recovery process. (Daniel, Lieutenant Correctional Officer)

Discussion

Legislators in Maryland passed Maryland Law §9-603 in 2019 which requires all local jails and the Baltimore County Detention Center (a state-run facility incarcerating individuals pre-trial or serving short sentences) to appropriately screen individuals for OUD and offer the three-FDA approved forms of MOUD treatment. Yet, as of February 2023 fewer than half of all Maryland jails were compliant, despite the years-long mandate [78]. This finding is reflective of a national trend where American jails are underutilizing MOUD treatment as standard of care for OUD [14, 42, 48, 79]. While scholars have speculated that stigma of OUD presents a significant barrier to MOUD implementation and use in criminal legal-involved and criminal legal-referred populations, to date there has been little effort made in the published literature to empirically explore this hypothesis [8, 16, 17, 58]. To help fill this gap in the literature, the current study used a mixed-method analysis to examine professional support for MOUD in correctional settings in Maryland and assess the relationship between provider-based stigma of OUD and support for various forms of MOUD treatment. There are three main findings from analyses that warrant further discussion.

First, consistent with our first hypothesis, we found measurable levels of provider-based stigma of OUD in our sample of correctional professionals. While the mean score for our stigma measure was 3.37 on a 1 to 5 scale–suggesting moderately high levels of stigma of OUD—this descriptive finding tells us little about the nature, prevalence, and extent of stigma in our sample. The qualitative data from interviews with professionals provided a more content-rich assessment to help us answer our first research question. Perceptions of blameworthiness, dangerousness and fatalism were present in nearly every interview, with a majority of participants describing incarcerated individuals with OUD as untrustworthy, unlikely to recover, and lacking in the necessary desire and willpower to stop using (nonmedical) opioids. Such characterizations transcended participants’ specific correctional role, rank, and experience, as well as facility location.

Second, also consistent with our hypothesis and prior research, data revealed that professionals in our sample overwhelmingly favored antagonist treatment with naltrexone over agonist treatment with buprenorphine or methadone in carceral settings [54, 55, 57]. Specifically, only 44% of our sample indicated supporting the use of methadone and buprenorphine in jails and detention centers, whereas 60% supported the use of naltrexone. Quantitative analyses revealed that these differences in support were statistically significant at the .05 alpha level, suggesting preference for antagonist treatment is likely true in the population too. The qualitative data supported these findings while also providing some nuance. Overwhelmingly, interviewees expressed more support for naltrexone than for buprenorphine or methadone. Interestingly, some interviewees also seemed to favor injectable buprenorphine over others forms of MOUD (with the exception of naltrexone), citing fears of diversion and what other scholars have termed “substitute addiction,” or believing that various forms of agonist medications are used merely as tools of intoxication [c.f., 56. Naltrexone was praised by many respondents for its non-intoxicating effects and potential to “punish” nonmedical opioid use; many even (wrongfully) believed that naltrexone was more effective as a treatment modality than all other forms of MOUD. These findings are consistent with those reported by Andraka-Christou and Atkins [55] suggesting there is a “disconnect” between the scientific evidence surrounding MOUD and criminal legal professionals’ beliefs about MOUD. Given that prior research has found professional MOUD attitudes to influence professional MOUD-related policies and responses, it is not unreasonable to assume that this “research disconnect,” negative views, and misperceptions about MOUD could help explain why many correctional institutions are not currently providing all three FDA-approved formulations of MOUD, and why many who do favor naltrexone treatment [53]. From a policy perspective this is concerning, as recent research has indicated that naltrexone may not be as safe or effective as it was once thought to be, and there are many criminal legal-involved individuals who desire, need, or could benefit from agonist medications [21, 50–52]. As such, efforts need to be made to address this “research disconnect” and improve professionals’ understandings of the efficacy of agonist treatment. With prior research in mind, we suggest that education could be a crucial initial step in this process [54, 57, 64]. In keeping with prior research, our study suggests that training and education on different modes of MOUD should include not only correctional, but also reentry and treatment staff. Organizational leadership may also play a crucial role in promoting acceptance of OUD treatment using both agonist and antagonist medications [18, 80].

Third, and also consistent with our hypothesis and prior research, findings revealed a statistically significant relationship between provider-based stigma of OUD and perceptions of MOUD use in jails and detention centers [8, 65]. Interestingly, baseline statistical modeling indicated a statistically significant relationship between all of our measures of addiction frameworks and support for MOUD in jails and detention centers. Findings revealed that those who associated drug dependency with moral failure or a lack of will power were less supportive of MOUD, whereas those who associated drug dependency with the “medical model of addiction” were more supportive of MOUD. These findings are consistent with prior research on this topic [66, 67, 69]. However, with the addition of our measure of provider-based stigma into modeling these variables were no longer statistically significant, indicating that provider-based stigma appears to be a more proximal determinant of MOUD-related attitudes than other variables included in modeling. As expected, provider-based stigma of OUD was significantly and negatively related to perceptions of MOUD use in jails and detention centers. Those who placed greater stigma onto PWUO indicated less support for MOUD treatment in correctional institutions, suggesting that stigma of OUD is a barrier to successful implementation of MOUD treatment in carceral settings. These findings further support the need for educational-based interventions and stigma reduction campaigns targeted at professionals working in correctional settings.

Fortunately, recent research has demonstrated the effectiveness of several educational interventions to reduce not only social stigma around OUD, but intervention stigma around MOUD. For example, the Communities That Heal (CTH) intervention, implemented in 66 localities in New York State, took a community-collaborative approach to their communication campaigns around both OUD and evidence-based interventions to reduce overdose deaths. Involving community members and stakeholders in the planning, preparation, and implementation of educational campaigns may in turn improve interest and buy-in, ultimately leading to both attitudinal and behavior change; in fact, stakeholder participants in the CTH intervention sites showed larger decreases in stigma toward individuals with OUD and MOUD, compared to participants at control sites [81, 82]. Positive empathy interventions have also shown promise in reducing stigma around people who use drugs, and may be particularly relevant for changing attitudes within populations suffering from “compassion fatigue,” such as law enforcement or correctional staff [83]. For example, Clinton and Pollini [84] found that individuals participating in an “imagined contact exercise” with an individual who injects drugs, who were then asked a series of reflection questions designed to induce positive empathy, reported significantly lower stigma scores post-intervention, compared to individuals who were exposed to a more traditional learning campaign to reduce stigma. These findings are further in keeping with research demonstrating that stigma reduction trainings are more effective when delivered by individuals with lived experience of substance use disorders [85].

Limitations and directions for future work

There are several limitations to this exploratory study that are important to consider. First, while power analysis indicated quantitative modelling had adequate statistical power, and our sample represented more than 20% of all the workers employed at the 5 correctional centers we studied, this study used findings from a relatively ‘small’ sample of correctional professionals working in one state. As such, we were limited in what we could do in modeling and findings are not generalizable beyond these parameters. We encourage future researchers to expand this work to other jurisdictions and use larger samples for analyses when possible. Second, data are cross-sectional, and as such, temporal ordering could not be established between variables. Thus, to gain a better understanding of the relationship between addiction frameworks, stigma of OUD, and perceptions of MOUD, future work would also benefit from longitudinal data collection. This, along with larger and more diverse sample sizes, could also be useful when running mediation analysis to explore these relationships. Third, and also related to data limitations, we were limited in the number of variables that we could include in modeling. Future researchers may also benefit from including measures of job duty, familiarity with OUD/MOUD, years of job experience, and prior drug use history (i.e., lived and vicarious) in modeling as these variables may also influence perceptions of MOUD. Similarly, future qualitative research might explore in further depth how specific correctional job types might influence perceptions of and stigma around both PWUO and MOUD, an issue our qualitative analysis was unable to answer due to limitations in sample size. Fourth, both quantitative and qualitative data collected as part of this study may be skewed by several types of bias. All data presented here is self-reported; in addition to problems of recall bias or selective memory, participants may have also been hesitant to fully disclose stigmatizing beliefs around PWUO or MOUD, in keeping with social desirability bias. It is also necessary to reflect on the positionality of the researchers, especially those contributing to the qualitative analysis. Our qualitative research team brought together diverse expertise, in psychiatry, community health, social work, sociology, and criminal justice. This diversity of perspective represents a potential mitigating factor against one direction of interpretive bias in our analysis; however, qualitative research is an inherently interpretive exercise, inextricably bound up with analysts’ personal histories, positions, and beliefs. For this reason, our findings may not be generalizable.

Conclusion

Our findings show a significant association between stigmatizing beliefs around PWUO and negative perceptions of MOUD in carceral settings, suggesting that stigma represents a significant barrier to the expansion of such evidence-based programming. While correctional professionals demonstrate higher levels of support for MOUD involving opioid antagonists and/or long-acting injectables, such attitudes are not necessarily consonant with research surrounding MOUD effectiveness. Overall, our results reveal the need for more in-depth educational and anti-stigma campaigns directed at correctional professionals.
