Enhancing clinical insights in glioma grading using Bayesian Optimization and Explainable AI
Authors: Alaa M Elsayad, Omar A Elsayad
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•A novel framework for interpretable glioma grading is introduced, combining Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) with Bayesian Optimization and Explainable AI (XAI).•A Bayesian-optimized Generalized Additive Model (GAM) achieves superior or competitive performance compared to traditional machine learning models for glioma grade classification.•Permutation Feature Importance (PFI) and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) revealed the critical role of IDH1 and the contributions of Age, PTEN, ATRX, and CIC in glioma grade prediction.•This research provides clinically relevant insights into glioma grading using a transparent machine learning framework, promoting informed decision-making for clinicians.Keywords: Glioma grading, Generalized additive models (GAMs), Bayesian optimization, Explainable AI (XAI), Machine learning, Clinical decision support

Introduction

Gliomas, the most prevalent form of malignant brain tumors, originate from glial cells and present a formidable clinical challenge due to their associated morbidity and mortality1. These tumors are characterized by marked heterogeneity, encompassing a diverse spectrum of types and grades, each exhibiting distinct biological behaviors and clinical trajectories2. Consequently, accurate glioma grading is paramount for informing effective treatment decisions, precisely predicting patient prognosis, and guiding optimal clinical management strategies3. However, the current gold standard, histopathological analysis, relies on the microscopic examination of tissue samples, a process that is inherently subjective and vulnerable to significant inter-observer variability, potentially undermining diagnostic consistency and treatment standardization4. These limitations, including subjectivity, inter-pathologist variability, and the time-intensive nature of traditional histopathology, highlight the critical need for more reliable, efficient, and transparent diagnostic approaches.

Machine learning presents a promising avenue for revolutionizing glioma grading, with the potential to automate and enhance the process, substantially improving diagnostic accuracy and objectivity[5], [6]. This study investigates a range of machine learning methodologies for glioma classification, encompassing Generalized Additive Models (GAMs)7, Random Forest (RF)8, LogitBoost (LB)9, Support Vector Machines (SVM)10, and Artificial Neural Networks (ANNs)11. Although many machine learning models exhibit high predictive accuracy, their inherent lack of interpretability often hinders their effective adoption in clinical settings. In this respect, GAMs distinguish themselves as they can model complex, non-linear relationships between clinical and genetic features, while also providing inherent interpretability by showing each feature's functional relationship with the target variable12. This level of transparency is crucial, because GAMs offer clinicians with insights into feature contributions, facilitating more informed, context-aware decision-making processes. The other established methods serve as important baselines, highlighting the diverse approaches for classification and emphasizing the need to improve model interpretability for clinical use. This research particularly emphasizes the unique benefits of GAMs, highlighting their transparency, interpretability, and suitability for clinically impactful model building, in contrast to black-box models.

To maximize model performance, this study uses Bayesian Optimization13, combined with robust cross-validation, to precisely fine-tune hyperparameters across all the models. Bayesian optimization is particularly advantageous as it efficiently navigates high-dimensional hyperparameter search spaces and enhances both model accuracy and robustness. Additionally, Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) is integrated as a core component of this research to address the critical requirement of transparency in healthcare applications and also to improve model interpretability. Permutation Feature Importance (PFI)14 and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP)15 are leveraged for global feature importance, and GAM local effects and partial dependency plots (PDP) for individual explanation of data instances, with the goal to enhance model evaluation and to build more reliable and trustworthy models, specifically suited for clinical environments.

This research aims to establish a robust, accurate, and, more importantly, clinically interpretable framework for glioma grade classification. This is achieved by synergistically combining Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) with Bayesian Optimization and Explainable AI (XAI). By comparatively evaluating the proposed approach against established machine learning methods, the focus is on demonstrating the combined importance of enhanced accuracy and improved interpretability for glioma grading. The investigation includes comparing the performance metrics of GAM, RF, LB, SVM, and ANN; identifying the most impactful features using feature selection; improving GAM performance through Bayesian Optimization; and showcasing clinical implications using XAI. Ultimately, the goal is to contribute towards developing refined, objective, transparent, and clinically actionable tools that can support clinicians in making more informed treatment decisions, achieving more personalized treatment strategies, and finally improving patient outcomes. This work presents a clear and refined approach that not only improves prediction accuracy but also provides useful therapeutic information about how genetic and demographic factors, such as IDH1, PTEN, and Age, interact in nonlinear ways. Thus, the proposed methodology combines interpretability with high-performance classification, providing a new framework for reliable machine learning in glioma diagnosis.

Literature review

The application of machine learning (ML) to glioma grading has witnessed significant advancements, yet several critical challenges persist in achieving robust, interpretable, and clinically translatable results. This section reviews recent studies that have explored different machine learning approaches, with a particular emphasis on feature selection techniques, model performance, dataset utilization, and the crucial aspect of interpretability. These studies highlight the current state of the field, identify limitations, and provide a valuable context for the present study.

A hierarchical, voting-based methodology for glioma grading is presented in16, using ensemble learning and feature selection to enhance the use of clinical and molecular data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) and Chinese Glioma Genome Atlas (CGGA) datasets. These datasets also provide the foundation for this present work. This study combined four different feature selection techniques using a voting scheme and achieved a significantly improved performance using a soft-voting-based ensemble learning strategy across five different models. They also reduced the feature set considerably (35% for TCGA and 20% for CGGA). By focusing on relevant features and using robust machine learning models, their method achieved improved results as compared to previous works in the literature, while also highlighting the importance of feature selection.

A shift towards a data-centric approach is highlighted in17, which emphasizes the importance of strategic data curation and preprocessing for optimal ML model performance. Using TCGA data, this research demonstrated the impact of standardization, class balancing using oversampling, and meticulous feature selection on model performance. Through comparing six different models, this work identified Random Forest and CatBoost as top performers (accuracy of 0.869). It also shows that using only five top-ranked features (IDH1, Age, PTEN, CIC, and ATRX) yields comparable performance while reducing model complexity and training time, and highlights the importance of pre-processing and feature selection. The study also shows that oversampling can be used to mitigate the effect of class imbalance in the dataset and provides the highest performance of 0.871 by using a Logistic Regression model with these techniques.

A systematic review of 85 studies focusing on machine learning for glioma grading18 reveals a critical need for more rigorous reporting and external validation. This review highlights that while most studies show higher accuracies with methods like CNN (0.91) and SVM (0.90), most of them are limited by small dataset size, lack of external validation, and lack of uniform evaluation practices, thereby limiting the generalizability of findings. It also highlights that datasets with better curation (BraTS and TCIA) have higher mean accuracy as well, and there is need for more transparent models. This points to a major drawback in the current state of the field, and motivates the need for robust analysis.

Furthermore, a hybrid feature selection method19, integrating a rank-based weighted multivariate filter (mRMR) and embedded LASSO to enhance glioma grading (GradWise) on both TCGA and CGGA datasets, is proposed. This study utilized a combination of five supervised models and achieved the best result using SVM (0.87 on TCGA and 0.804 on CGGA). It also identified four important features: IDH1, Age, PTEN, and NF1, showcasing the power of using different techniques for achieving clinically relevant results.

Recently, Noviandy et al. in20 proposed an XAI framework for glioma grading that combined the Light Gradient Boosting Machine (LightGBM) with SHAP technique to identify key predictors such as IDH1 and Age. While their model achieved strong accuracy and post-hoc interpretability, it remained a black-box ensemble whose explanations were limited to feature importance visualization. In contrast, this current study presents an inherently interpretable GAM model created through Bayesian optimization and enhanced with multi-level XAI analysis (PFI, SHAP, and PDPs). This integration offers multi-layered global-to-local explanations and uncovers persistent, non-linear influences of IDH1, PTEN, and Age on tumor grade, enhancing clinical transparency beyond previous models.

These studies illustrate a growing body of research applying machine learning techniques to glioma grading and that there is a clear path towards clinically relevant solutions, but also underscores several challenges that must be addressed. They emphasize the critical role of robust data preprocessing, strategic feature selection, and the potential benefits of ensemble methods. Moreover, they emphasize the crucial need for interpretable models and rigorous model validation, while also highlighting the critical importance of external validations. These findings highlight the need for better validation metrics and transparent reporting practices to produce models with higher clinical validity and better use of machine learning models in real-world practices. Such findings will guide our current research towards the development of a robust, interpretable, and clinically actionable framework that can build upon the current limitations in the literature.

Material and methods

Data acquisition and description

This study utilized the publicly available 'Glioma Grading Clinical and Mutation Features' dataset, sourced from both Kaggle and the UC Irvine Machine Learning Repository (UCI). This dataset compiles clinical and molecular/mutation data from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) projects focusing on Low-Grade Gliomas (TCGA-LGG) and Glioblastomas (TCGA-GBM), two major categories of gliomas. The dataset was specifically chosen for its relevance to glioma grading research, accessibility, and inclusion of frequently mutated genes alongside pertinent clinical factors. The primary objective of this dataset is to facilitate glioma classification by distinguishing between LGG and GBM. The dataset comprises 839 patient samples, each characterized by a total of 22 features, after removing BCOR as it had a zero mRMR score during the feature selection phase. These features include:

•Clinical Features: Three demographic features associated with patients, namely gender (a binary categorical variable: male/female), age at diagnosis (a continuous numerical variable representing the patient’s age), and race (a categorical variable denoting self-reported race/ethnicity).

•Molecular/Mutation Features: Nineteen binary features (represented as 1 for “mutated” and 0 for “not mutated”) that indicate the presence or absence of mutations in the 19 most frequently mutated genes observed in TCGA-LGG and TCGA-GBM studies. This inherent complexity presents a challenge for model building, thus necessitating robust modeling and evaluation strategies. Generalized Additive Model (GAM) and benchmarks

This study employed five distinct classification models for glioma grade prediction, encompassing our core inherently interpretable model and four established benchmarks. A critical aspect of our framework was the optimization strategy applied uniformly across all models to ensure peak predictive performance and robust generalization.

The Generalized Additive Model (GAM) serves as the core methodology due to its flexible, inherently interpretable nature22, which is ideal for glioma grading. Unlike linear models, GAMs model the outcome as a sum of smooth, potentially non-linear functions of individual predictors. Its boosting architecture models individual and interaction effects through additive components. This interpretability is crucial, as GAMs allow for direct inspection and visualization of each feature's effect using partial dependence plots (PDPs) and enable the uncovering of complex interaction effects[23], [24]. While powerful, GAMs can be computationally demanding and complex to interpret in high-parameter cases, necessitating careful hyperparameter tuning. To benchmark performance, we compared GAMs with four established models:

1.Random Forest (RF): A robust ensemble that uses bagging and feature subsampling to handle high-dimensional data and mitigate overfitting[25], [26]. It offers strong predictive performance and the capacity to estimate feature importance, but its ensemble nature makes it less inherently interpretable than GAMs.

Random Forest (RF): A robust ensemble that uses bagging and feature subsampling to handle high-dimensional data and mitigate overfitting[25], [26]. It offers strong predictive performance and the capacity to estimate feature importance, but its ensemble nature makes it less inherently interpretable than GAMs.

2.LogitBoost (LB): A boosting algorithm that combines weak logistic regression learners, iteratively re-weighting misclassified samples to focus on challenging cases. It models non-linearity and provides probabilistic outputs, offering a degree of interpretability through the base learner coefficients27. However, the final combined model lacks transparency.

It models non-linearity and provides probabilistic outputs, offering a degree of interpretability through the base learner coefficients27. However, the final combined model lacks transparency.

3.Support Vector Machines (SVM): A robust algorithm that finds an optimal hyperplane to separate classes in high-dimensional space. SVMs offer strong predictive performance and limited interpretability via the identification of support vectors. Interpretation is challenging with non-linear kernels, and they require extensive hyperparameter tuning[28], [29].

Bayesian optimization for hyperparameter tuning

Bayesian optimization was employed to optimize the hyperparameters of each classification model. This method is a powerful and efficient approach for identifying the best parameter configurations by intelligently exploring the search space. Unlike traditional grid or random search methods, Bayesian optimization uses a probabilistic surrogate model, typically a Gaussian Process (GP), to approximate the objective function, which in this case is the cross-validated misclassification error. The GP acts as a surrogate function by learning the relationship between hyperparameter values and model performance, capturing the uncertainty of model performance for different parameter values. This allows the optimizer to select better parameter combinations in the hyperparameter space, aiming to minimize the misclassification error. The surrogate function enables Bayesian optimization to efficiently find optimal hyperparameters even for computationally expensive models by focusing on more promising parameter configurations.

To determine the following hyperparameter configuration to explore, the algorithm uses the 'expected-improvement-plus' acquisition function. This function strikes a balance between exploring less well-understood areas of the hyperparameter space and exploiting regions that have previously yielded good results. By striking this balance, the 'expected-improvement-plus' function helps the optimizer avoid getting stuck in a local optimum, where further performance improvements are difficult to obtain32. To further accelerate the hyperparameter tuning process, the optimization was set to leverage available parallel processing resources and to repartition the data whenever feasible. Data repartitioning during cross-validation enables more efficient use of computational resources, especially with large datasets, and enhances hyperparameter tuning efficiency by reducing data loading and transfer overheads.

Explainable AI (XAI) methods

To address the inherent interpretability challenges of many machine learning models and to gain clinically relevant insights into the glioma grading task, this study integrates XAI techniques, specifically, PFI and SHAP, which provide complementary perspectives on model behavior.

Permutation feature importance (PFI)

Permutation Feature Importance (PFI) is a model-agnostic, global explanation technique that evaluates the significance of a feature by measuring the decrease in model performance when the feature's values are randomly shuffled. This disruption of the relationship between the feature and the target variable allows PFI to quantify the model's reliance on the feature for accurate predictions. The algorithm calculates the difference between the baseline performance and the performance on the permuted dataset14. By averaging the impact of feature permutations over multiple iterations, PFI provides a robust measure of feature importance, ensuring that the results are not influenced by random fluctuations.

PFI's interpretability is crucial for clinical applications, where understanding the role of each feature can inform treatment decisions. It can reveal interactions between features by showing how the importance of one feature changes when another is permuted. This insight is particularly useful for understanding the multifactorial nature of glioma progression. Recent studies have demonstrated the effectiveness of PFI in enhancing the interpretability and performance of models used for glioma classification, highlighting its practical utility in medical research33.

Shapley additive explanations (SHAP)

SHAP provide a powerful and interpretable method for understanding the contributions of individual features in predictive models, making them particularly beneficial for glioma grade classification. SHAP values are based on cooperative game theory in a way that ensures that the contributions of all features add up to match the model's predictions, providing a clear and consistent explanation of how each feature affects the outcome.34. They are calculated by considering all possible combinations of features, ensuring that the importance measures are robust and reliable.

SHAP can reveal how features interact with each other by showing how the importance of one feature changes in the presence of another. This is particularly useful for glioma grading, where the interplay between multiple genetic mutations and clinical factors can significantly impact tumor classification. By providing detailed insights into which features are driving the model's predictions, SHAP helps clinicians understand the underlying factors influencing glioma grades. This can inform more personalized treatment strategies and improve patient outcomes.

Partial dependency plot (PDP)

PDP are tools used to interpret machine learning models by showing the relationship between a feature and the predicted outcome, while averaging out the effects of other features35. They help in understanding how changes in a feature's value influence model predictions. In glioma grading, PDPs can illustrate the impact of clinical and genetic features, such as the IDH1 gene or patient age, on the likelihood of a glioma being classified as low-grade or high-grade. PDPs enhance interpretability, transparency, and clinical relevance, making them valuable for validating model decisions and aiding clinicians in making informed decisions. Integrating PDPs with other XAI techniques like PFI and SHAP provides a comprehensive understanding of the model's decision-making process.

Experimental results

This section presents the results of experiments designed to evaluate and compare the performance of the proposed Generalized Additive Model (GAM) framework against four established machine learning techniques: Random Forest (RF), LogitBoost (LB), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN). The primary objective was to assess each model's ability to accurately and, critically, interpretably classify glioma grades, specifically distinguishing between Low-Grade Gliomas (LGG) as the positive class and Glioblastomas Multiforme (GBM) as the negative class.

Models were trained and tested using the 'Glioma Grading Clinical and Mutation Features' dataset from Kaggle. This dataset was partitioned into an 80% training subset and a 20% held-out test subset, facilitating a robust assessment of generalization performance. Prior to final evaluation, hyperparameter optimization was performed for each model utilizing Bayesian Optimization, combined with a 10-fold cross-validation strategy on the training data. This approach was specifically used to enhance the robustness and generalizability of the models and to reduce the risk of overfitting. Bayesian Optimization employed an 'expected-improvement-plus' acquisition function to explore the hyperparameter search space strategically. All optimizations were performed using MATLAB’s optimization framework, with parallel processing and re-partitioning enabled to accelerate the tuning process when applicable. The utilization of parallel Bayesian optimization accelerates the tuning process, but due to the asynchronous evaluation of hyperparameter points, strict reproducibility is not maintained across different runs. Model performance was evaluated using a comprehensive suite of metrics: accuracy (ACC), sensitivity (SEN), specificity (SPEC), F1-score, positive predictive value (PPV), negative predictive value (NPV), and the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUC). The F1-score, which is the harmonic mean of precision and recall, was of particular importance due to its ability to provide a balanced evaluation of model performance, especially given the class imbalance inherent in the dataset.

Feature importance and XAI

XAI techniques are crucial for ensuring the transparency and interpretability of complex machine learning models, particularly within sensitive domains such as healthcare. XAI not only enhances prediction accuracy but also provides crucial insights into the reasoning behind AI decisions, fostering trust, promoting informed decision-making, and facilitating collaboration between data scientists and clinicians by validating clinical relevance. To demystify the decision-making processes of the glioma grading models in this study, a focused set of XAI techniques emphasizing both global and local perspectives was employed.

Global feature importance: PFI and SHAP

Fig. 3 presents the global feature importance derived from the GAM framework. This visualization includes both PFI scores and mean absolute SHAP values. PFI scores are computed for each feature by measuring the decrease in model performance on both training and test sets when a feature is randomly permuted. This provides insight into the importance of each feature in predicting the output, while also highlighting their generalization ability. Complementarily, mean absolute SHAP values quantify the magnitude of each feature's contribution to the model’s prediction across all data points in the training and test sets respectively.

PFI and SHAP global feature importance for train and test subsets.

As depicted in Fig. 3, the IDH1 feature consistently emerges as the most influential predictor across both the PFI and SHAP analyses, having a significantly higher importance score than any other feature. This emphasizes the critical role of the IDH1 gene in glioma grading, which was found with different feature ranking techniques of previous models. Although IDH1 dominates, a few other features, namely Age, PTEN, ATRX, and CIC, exhibit a moderate level of importance, particularly according to the SHAP analysis, suggesting their potential role in influencing model predictions. Most of the remaining features display considerably low scores for both PFI and SHAP, indicating a limited individual influence on the GAM model's overall performance.

False Negative (FN): The absence of IDH1 and the presence of negative interaction terms such as IDH1-NF1, IDH1-IDH2, and IDH1-EGFR are the driving factors that led the model to falsely classify a LGG case as a GBM. Features like PTEN and Age also contribute to false negatives.

These analysis of four specific test records highlights several key insights. IDH1 is a crucial but complex indicator in glioma grading, showing positive influence in true positive (LGG) cases and negative influence in true negative (GBM) and false negative (misclassified LGG) cases. This underscores its importance and the complexity of its relationship with glioma grade, influenced by other clinical factors and interactions. The frequent appearance of interaction terms among top features emphasizes their role in capturing complex relationships and the need for accurate modeling of these effects. Misclassified cases show that while features like IDH1 and Age can aid correct classification, their dominance can also lead to misclassifications. The findings align with global insights, confirming IDH1 as a consistent predictor and validating the use of interpretable techniques to uncover complex data relationships.

Discussion and conclusion

This study introduced a novel and clinically relevant framework for glioma grade classification by strategically combining Generalized Additive Models (GAMs) with Bayesian Optimization and Explainable AI (XAI) techniques. This approach directly addresses the critical need for accurate, transparent, and interpretable models capable of guiding treatment strategies, predicting patient prognosis, and improving clinical management of glioma patients. This study uniquely integrates GAMs, Bayesian Optimization, and multi-level XAI into a single, transparent framework that achieves high accuracy while maintaining interpretability. By combining PFI, SHAP, and PDPs, it provides layered clinical insights into how key molecular and demographic features, such as IDH1, ATRX, and Age, influence glioma grading—advancing toward more trustworthy AI in neuro-oncology.

The experimental findings demonstrate that the Bayesian-optimized GAM not only matches but, in several metrics, outperforms established machine learning models, such as Random Forest (RF), LogitBoost (LB), Support Vector Machines (SVM), and Artificial Neural Networks (ANN). The performance of the five models was measured using a suite of comprehensive metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score, positive predictive value, negative predictive value, and the area under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve. Specifically, while other models such as Random Forest showed high accuracy in training, the proposed GAM model demonstrated strong generalization capabilities on unseen test data, with the highest accuracy (0.9012) and F1-score (0.8975). These results showcase the predictive power of GAMs while also highlighting that it provides a performance that is at least comparable to other traditional machine learning models.

Furthermore, by applying Explainable AI (XAI) techniques, specifically Permutation Feature Importance (PFI) and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP), we have been able to enhance the model interpretability. These techniques provided crucial insights into the influence of individual features, particularly IDH1 and Age, on the model’s predictions and the underlying biological significance, while also allowing individual feature analysis. Analysis using Partial Dependence Plots (PDPs) also shows the complex, non-linear relationships between features, particularly IDH1 and Age, and the glioma grades, giving a better understanding of how a change in a particular feature is impacting the final prediction. Specifically, while IDH1′s dominance was observed in all the models, PDPs highlight its complex and contrasting nature by highlighting both the positive and negative aspects and also the threshold for the separation of LGG and GBM. Further analysis of local feature importance shows how each feature is contributing in different cases. This framework, therefore, helps to uncover both local as well as global feature importance.

In conclusion, this study introduces a novel framework for glioma grade classification that combines Generalized Additive Models (GAMs), Bayesian Optimization, and Explainable AI (XAI) methods. This approach results in a robust, accurate, and clinically interpretable predictive system. The careful use of various XAI techniques provided a thorough understanding of the model's behavior, helping develop a transparent and reliable diagnostic tool. The proposed framework shows great promise in supporting personalized treatment plans, improving diagnostic accuracy, and offering clinicians better insights into glioma biology. However, these promising findings are subject to several limitations. Primarily, the model's generalizability is currently restricted by the lack of external validation beyond the UCI dataset. Further limitations include a relatively modest sample size (N = 839) and a limited diversity of clinical features incorporated into the analysis. Additionally, despite the intrinsic interpretability of GAMs, challenges may arise when modeling intricate interactions among numerous variables. Future work will address these limitations by prioritizing rigorous external validation using diverse, multi-institutional datasets. We also intend to explore advanced model refinement strategies and cutting-edge XAI techniques to enhance both interpretability and generalizability further.
