A heat flux sensor with both fast response and high sensitivity
Authors: Le Li, Bian Tian, Yanzhong Chen, Meng Wang, Shuai Yu, Nengchao Lu, Mingzhou He, Zhaojun Liu, Shuimin Li, Wu He, Zhongkai Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Heat flux sensors are of great significance for measuring heat transfer processes. However, due to the limitations of the thermal resistance thickness and the number of thermopile junctions, it is often difficult to combine the high sensitivity and fast response time in one heat flux sensor. In this work, we design the thermopile array based on the principle of hexagonal dense arrangement to increase the number of junctions in a limited area, and at the same time, we set up films with different functions at the hot and cold junctions to increase the temperature difference. Tests on multiple sensors show that in the range of 0–207 kW/m2, the coefficient of determination R2 of the quadratic fit between sensor output and heat flux is greater than 0.999. In the linear operating range from 114 to 201 kW/m2, the sensor exhibits a maximum heat flux sensitivity of 91.13 μV/(kW/m2); the response times of the sensors are faster than 2.85 ms under different laser powers and durations. The sensor exhibits a good response to heat fluxes such as respiration, flame, and hot air, and is expected to be applied to the measurement of high-temperature and rapidly varying heat transfer.

Introduction

Heat flux reflects the heat transfer process and is a key physical parameter in the fields of thermal process control and management1,2, aerospace thermal protection3,4, and energy saving and emission reduction5–7. Accurate measurement of heat flux can help to improve heating efficiency or enhance thermal protection. The performance of the heat flux sensor, as the core device for heat flux density measurement, directly affects the acquisition of heat flux8. Existing heat flux measurement methods include Gardon gauge9–11, coaxial thermocouples12–14, atomic layer thermopile-type heat flux sensors15–17, and thin film thermopile-type heat flux sensors18–21. Gardon gauge usually requires water cooling to achieve a constant temperature at the cold junction. Coaxial thermocouples are usually only used in transient heat flux, which is difficult to achieve stable heat flux measurements, and atomic layer thermopiles are fast responding but usually have low sensitivity and struggle to survive at high temperatures. The thermopile-type heat flux sensor has the advantages of small size, high temperature tolerance, fast response, and no additional water cooling, which is the hot spot of current research. For example, the all-ceramic heat flux sensor can work at ultra-high temperature of 1300 °C and high heat flux of 340 kW/m2 with a sensitivity of 31.5 µV/(kW/m2)22, but the response time takes 2.3 s. The heat flux sensor in ref. 23 achieves a response time of 10.18 ms, but the sensitivity is only 6.13 µV/(kW/m2); the high-temperature heat flux sensor with a composite ceramic thermal resistance layer has a response time of 1.08 ms, but the sensitivity is only 3.58 µV/(kW/m2)24. The thermopile type heat flux sensor is difficult to combine high sensitivity and fast response because when the thermal resistance is thick, the sensitivity is high but the response time is slow, and when the thermal resistance is thin, the response is fast, but the sensitivity is reduced. The existing methods need to increase the number of thermocouples, but it increases the complexity of the process, requiring high precision in alignment, uniformity in thin film deposition, and reliability at the junctions.

In this work, to resolve the contradiction that the sensitivity and response time of thermopile-based heat flux sensors are difficult to balance simultaneously, multi-dimensional optimizations were carried out. SiC film with high heat absorption rate and low thermal resistance was deposited on the hot junctions, while a film with high thermal resistance was deposited on the cold junctions, significantly increasing the temperature difference between the hot and cold junctions; a distributed junction layout was designed based on the hexagonal dense arrangement principle to increase the number of series thermocouples within a limited area; and the thermopile employs ITO and In2O3 as the thermoelectric pair respectively. They possess outstanding high-temperature stability and a large Seebeck coefficient, which can significantly improve the output signal of the sensor. On the premise of achieving high output, the thickness of the thermal resistance film can be further reduced, thereby enhancing the response speed of the sensor. Ultimately, a heat flux sensor with stable high sensitivity and fast response characteristics under high-temperature conditions was successfully developed.

We design more junctions in a limited area based on the principle of hexagonal close packing. On the other hand, the temperature difference between the hot and cold junctions of conventional heat flux sensors is formed only by the barrier of the thermal insulation layer, and there is no treatment at the hot junction. We plated high emissivity SiC film at the hot junction to increase the heat absorption rate of the film and covered the cold junction with highly insulating SiO2 to lower the temperature of the cold junction, finally forming the designed structure as shown in Fig. 1e. As shown in Fig. 1f, the temperatures of films with different emissivities were calculated under the same heat transfer conditions, and when the heat transfer was stable, the emissivity of the film with 0.9 was 1.57 K higher than that of the film with an emissivity of 0.7 and 11.52 K higher than that of the film with an emissivity of 0.1, which shows that a change in the surface temperature of the film can be realized by the adjustment of the emissivity. This is because in radiative heat transfer, the heat flux value is not linearly related to the temperature. When the thickness of the heat-absorbing layer is increased, the change of the sensor output is small, while when the thickness of the thermal-resistance layer is increased, the sensor output is increased significantly, indicating that thickening the thermal-resistance layer is advantageous for the increase of the sensor output. Sensor fabrication

In order to obtain faster response speed, thermoelectric and thermal resistance materials need to be made into micro-nanoscale films. The commonly used film preparation methods include physical vapor deposition (PVD)25–29, screen printing30–34, chemical vapor deposition (CVD)35–37, etc. Among them, PVD such as magnetron sputtering has the advantages of fast film formation and high purity, which is suitable for the preparation of thermoelectric materials; Films prepared by screen printing can withstand high temperatures, but they are usually thicker, which leads to longer sensor response times; chemical vapor deposition forms films quickly, but it can prepare only a limited variety of materials, usually used for preparing materials like SiO2; and atomic layer deposition methods typically have slower preparation speeds. Therefore, the PVD method was chosen for the preparation of the thermoelectric layer and heat-absorbing layer, while the CVD method was chosen for the fast preparation of the thermal resistance layer.

The sensor’s fabrication process is illustrated in Fig. 3, which is as follows: (1) Firstly, the substrate was cleaned under ultrasonic conditions, in acetone and ethanol solutions for 10 min each, with a water bath temperature of about 50 °C; (2) ITO thin film was grown and patterned using through magnetron sputtering and masking with parameters of 120 W, 45 sccm, and 150 min; subsequently, In2O3 was prepared under the same manner; (3) SiC thin film was prepared by masking and sputtering with parameters of 150 W, 45 sccm, and 6 h; (4) Prepare thermal-resistance layer SiO2 by PECVD; (5) The prepared heat flux sensor is put into the muffle furnace for annealing, the annealing temperature is 1000 °C and the time is 30 min; (6) The sensor is leaded using high temperature resistant wire and silver paste.Fig. 3Heat flux sensor fabrication process.a–c ITO, In2O3, SiC thin films are prepared by magnetron sputtering. d SiO2 thin film is prepared by PECVD. e The thin film is annealed. f Leads were applied to the sensor

Heat flux sensor fabrication process.

Compared to ITO and In2O3, sputtering SiC requires a larger sputtering power and time to ensure that the sensor surface color is sufficiently darkened to obtain a larger heat absorption rate. The reason for using PECVD for SiO2 films fabrication is that the films prepared by PECVD are thicker compared to methods such as magnetron sputtering, and for SiO2 films, it takes only tens of minutes to prepare micron-sized films, while magnetron sputtering often takes tens of hours to form the same thickness. Thermal resistance layers for heat flux sensors need to be of a certain thickness to form large temperature gradients, and PECVD is a suitable method for rapid fabrication of thermal resistance layers.

Result and discussion

Static performance of the sensor

As shown in Fig. 5, the calibration device of heat flux sensor mainly includes thermal source (high-temperature heat radiation lamp), power supply, standard heat flux sensor (Gardon gauge), heat flux sensor to be calibrated, and data collector. The specific calibration process is as follows: the heat flux sensor to be calibrated and a standard heat flux sensor of known accuracy are successively placed in the same area of the uniform heat flux region under the radiation field of the high temperature lamp; the electric power of the high temperature lamp is accurately controlled to produce a stable and known heat flux density; the output signals of the two sensors are recorded respectively; by comparing the output of the sensor to be calibrated with the known heat flux value measured by the standard sensor, the relationship curve between the output signal and the actual heat flux is established, and the sensitivity coefficient of the sensor to be calibrated is calculated. The heat flux density values under different radiation powers were calibrated by a standard Gardon heat flux meter (Fig. 5b), among which the heat flux of the heat source reached a maximum of 207 kW/m2. As shown in Fig. 5c, for the heat flux sensor equipped with a heat-absorbing layer, as the radiation power increases, the output voltage of the sensor to be calibrated exhibits a rapid increasing trend. At the minimum heat flux, the output voltage stabilizes at approximately 0.41 mV; with the gradual increase in heat flux, the output voltage eventually reaches 13.24 mV. For the heat flux sensor without a heat-absorbing layer, although its output also increases with the rise in heat flux, the output amplitude is significantly smaller than that of the sensor with a heat-absorbing layer. When the heat flux reaches the maximum, the output of this sensor is only 0.87 mV, demonstrating the critical role of the heat-absorbing layer in enhancing the sensor output. In Fig. 5d, three heat flux sensors are calibrated three times repeatedly. Test results show that the coefficient of determination R2 of the sensors is higher than 0.999 when fitted with a quadratic polynomial across the full measurement range. Within the high heat flux range of 114–207 kW/m2, the linear fitting R2 values are 0.997, 0.999, and 0.998, respectively. Calculations show that the repeatability errors of the three sensors are 1.5%, 0.4%, and 1.3%, respectively, and all sensors exhibit good repeatability and stability. As shown in Fig. 5e, refined tests are performed on the linear region of the heat flux sensor with the maximum output. The results show that within the heat flux range from 114 to 201 kW/m2, the linear fitting coefficient R2 of the sensor output voltage and heat flux is 0.996, and the sensitivity of the developed sensor in this linear range is 91.13 μV/(kW/m2). In Fig. 5f, thermal cycling tests are performed on the sensor under a heat flux of 114 kW/m2. In each cycle, the heat flux is applied for 2 min and then turned off for 1 min, with 10 consecutive cycles conducted. The test results show that the output curve of the sensor remains stable without obvious abnormal fluctuations during each cycle. The average output at thermal steady state is 4.34 mV, and the maximum deviation appears in the first cycle, with a relative deviation of 0.33% from the average value. Figure 5g presents the long-term operation test of the sensor. Three different radiation power levels, namely 50 W, 75 W, and 100 W, are selected and continuously applied for half an hour each. Under heat source powers of 50, 75, and 100 W, the sensor output ranges are 1.52–1.57, 2.92–2.95, and 4.24–4.36 mV, respectively. The output deviation of the sensor becomes relatively more pronounced as the applied heat flux increases. This is partly attributed to the output fluctuation of the sensor itself at high temperatures and partly to the attenuation of the radiant heat source. When the heat source power is 100 W, the surface temperature exceeds 900 °C. During prolonged heating, the lamp filament exhibits a certain degree of volatilization, leading to a gradual decrease in the effective radiant heat flux and thus a slight downward trend in the sensor output. In Fig. 5h, calibrations are conducted three times before and after the tests for the sensor subjected to long-term high-temperature loading and high-temperature cycling. The results show that at a maximum heat flux density of 207 kW/m2, the average sensor output decreases by only approximately 1.2% compared with that before the test. Within the high heat flux range of 114–207 kW/m2, the sensor sensitivity remains stable at 59.1 μV/(kW/m2) before and after the tests, with no obvious performance degradation observed.Fig. 5Schematic diagram of static calibration of heat flux sensor.a Schematic diagram. b Output of the standard heat flux sensor under different heat sources power. c Output of the developed heat flux sensor under different heat sources power. d Output curves and fitting results of three heat flux sensors after three calibrations. e Output curve of the heat flux sensor, ranging from 114 to 201 kW/m2, and the fitting results. f Output curve of the heat flux sensor under multiple heat cycles. g Output curve of the heat flux sensor under long‑term heating conditions. h Calibration curves of the sensor before and after long-term heating

Dynamic performance of the sensor

Heat flux sensor dynamic test device (Fig. 6a) includes laser generator, laser sensor, developed sensor, and high-speed data collector (oscilloscope). The testing process is as follows: Fix the heat flux sensor to be tested so that its sensitive surface is directly facing the laser beam, and adjust the optical path to ensure that the laser spot evenly covers the entire sensitive area. Install high-speed laser sensors on the side of the optical path to monitor and record the actual transient changes of the laser in real time. Start the high-speed data acquisition system to synchronously collect the output signal of the heat flux sensor and the laser signal recorded by the laser sensor, and capture the complete transient process at a sampling rate much higher than the estimated response frequency of the sensor. Through the laser controller, the laser power can be switched from zero to the set high power in an extremely short time, generating an ideal step or pulse heat flux. The actual laser application time can be confirmed by analyzing the recorded signals through the laser sensor. The moment when the heat flux sensor begins to rise is denoted as t0, with the sensor’s output at this point being E0. Once the heat flux sensor attains a steady state, its output is designated as Es. Sensor performance analysis

The developed heat flux sensor features high sensitivity and fast response at high temperatures. To test the sensor’s high-sensitivity response capability to small heat fluxes, respiratory heat flux tests were conducted using the sensor, as shown in Fig. 7a. Figure 7b, c presents the output results of the developed heat flux sensor and a commercial thermopile-based heat flux sensor (HT-50), respectively. For the developed heat flux sensor, during the initial 30 s, the test subject maintained a steady breathing rhythm. Leveraging its high sensitivity, the sensor captured 7 complete breathing cycles within this period, with its output curve exhibiting regular periodic fluctuations and a maximum output voltage peak of 570 μV. At approximately 30 s into the test, the test subject began to hold their breath, at which point heat exchange between the sensor surface and the external environment ceased, the heat flux gradually decreased, and the sensor’s output voltage entered a stable declining phase, eventually stabilizing at around 0 μV during the breath-holding state. At approximately 45 s, the test subject resumed normal breathing, and the sensor output signal exhibited periodic fluctuations consistent with the initial phase. In Fig. 7c, the commercial heat flux sensor was used to test the similar breathing states of the same subject. During breathing, the output of the commercial sensor only fluctuated within the range of −3 to 1 μV, while the maximum output voltage peak of the developed heat flux sensor was more than two orders of magnitude higher than the maximum output amplitude of the commercial sensor. Analysis and discussion

For the developed heat flux sensor, the temperature difference between the cold and hot junctions is mainly provided by the SiC heat-absorbing layer disposed at the hot junction. Compared with the conventional design that relies solely on the thermal resistance layer to generate the temperature difference, the proposed sensor exhibits a more significant output. Meanwhile, due to the enhanced output, the thermal resistance film can be fabricated thinner, thereby remarkably improving the sensor’s response speed. According to the calibration results, as the heat flux increases, the sensor’s output sensitivity is enhanced, which is conducive to the measurement of high-temperature heat flux. In the low heat flux range, the output performance of the proposed sensor is also superior to that of sensors without a heat-absorbing layer. Further improvement of the output in the low heat flux range can be achieved by employing films with higher thermal absorptivity and higher thermal conductivity.

Conclusion

In this work, to achieve high sensitivity and fast response heat flux sensors at high temperatures, a hexagonal arrangement of thermopile was designed to increase the density, setting up 112 pairs of thermocouples within a 1 cm × 1 cm area; additionally, different functional films were set at the hot and cold junctions, where high heat-absorption rate and high-thermal conductivity SiC films were placed at the hot junction, and low thermal conductivity films were placed at the cold junction to enhance the temperature difference between the hot and cold junctions, thereby increasing the sensitivity under high heat flux. Multiple sensors demonstrate a good quadratic relationship between output and heat flux. The sensor has a maximum sensitivity of 91.13 μV/(kW/m2) within the range of 114 to 201 kW/m2, and the sensor’s response time was faster than 2.85 ms under different laser powers and pulse widths. The developed sensor is expected to be used for measuring rapidly changing high-temperature thermal flux.
