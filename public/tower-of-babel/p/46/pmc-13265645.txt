A sophisticated predictive framework for identifying obstructive sleep apnea syndrome in women with obesity during pregnancy
Authors: Chen Jiang, Hong Zhou, Jiao Yi, Xianxia Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo develop a risk prediction model of obstructive sleep apnea syndrome (OSAS) in obese pregnant women in order to facilitate early intervention.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed polysomnography confirmed OSAS cases (n = 828) from Hefei Maternal and Child Health Hospital (Jan 1st,2023-Jan 1st,2025). Cases were classified according to the onset of the OSAS during pregnancy: non-occurrence (n = 526) and occurrence (n = 302). Predictive factors were determined using univariate logistic regression (P < 0.1), LASSO regularization (λ = 0.007) and multivariate logistic regression.ResultsFive independent predictors were identified: tongue size, sleep position, neck circumference, Berlin Questionnaire score and Epworth Sleepiness Scale score. The model was found to have an area under the curve (AUC) of 0.811, good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow P = 0.275), low average absolute error (0.017), and net clinical benefit across risk thresholds (0.04–0.95).ConclusionThis five-variable model is a reliable predictor of the risk of OSAS in obese pregnant women and is useful for timely prenatal screening, risk stratification, and early management.

Introduction

Obesity is one of the key international health issues that have an influential impact on maternal and fetal health. There is a high risk of poor outcomes in gestational obesity, including gestational diabetes mellitus, hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, and macrosomia, and long-term risks to the offspring, including congenital anomalies, metabolic disorders, asthma, and neurodevelopmental impairments [1], [2], [3], [4], [5], [6], [7]. According to the latest epidemiological information, 30% −51% of pregnant women around the globe fit the definition of gestational obesity, which is a significant and increasing healthcare liability [8].

Obstructive sleep apnea (OSA) is a prevalent though under-identified comorbidity during pregnancy, which is defined by periodic upper airway obstruction, hypoxia, and sleep fragmentation. The physiological alterations during pregnancy such as fluid retention, diaphragmatic rise, and hormonal adjustment to respiratory activity precondition the emergence or aggravation of OSA in women [9], [10]. As many as 25% of pregnant women can be affected by the third trimester [11]. Obesity also enhances this risk by encouraging the narrowing of upper airways and decreasing neuromuscular stability [12], [13].

Guidelines on the screening and management of OSA in pregnancy have been published in 2023 by the Society of Anesthesiology and Sleep Medicine and the Society of Obstetric Anesthesia and Perinatal Medicine [14]. Nevertheless, these recommendations are silent concerning the determination of the critical risk factors and prediction of OSA among pregnant women with obesity. To fill this gap, the current study is a systematic analysis of clinical data of pregnant women with obesity to determine independent predictors of OSA and to come up with a practical prediction model. This model will support the early stratification of risks and direct specific diagnostic and preventive actions in clinical practice.

Data and methods

Clinical information

We retrospectively analyzed 828 pregnant women with obesity who were treated at Hefei Maternal and Child Health Hospital between January 1, 2023 and January 1, 2025. All participants were anonymized during data collection, and no personal identifiers were accessible after data extraction. Based on polysomnography (PSG), participants were divided into a non-OSA group (n = 526) and an OSA group (n = 302).

Inclusion criteria were: (1) pre-pregnancy obesity (BMI ≥ 30 kg/m²) and/or gestational weight gain exceeding the thresholds recommended by the U.S. Institute of Medicine [15]; (2) singleton pregnancy; (3) term delivery (≥37 weeks of gestation), ensuring that all participants were in the third trimester, when pregnancy-related physiological changes, including upper airway changes, gestational weight gain, and fluid retention, are most pronounced and OSA symptoms are most directly attributable to pregnancy; (4) complete prenatal and intrapartum records; and (5) provision of informed consent for the use of de-identified clinical data and postpartum follow-up.

Exclusion criteria were: (1) pre-existing OSA diagnosed before pregnancy; (2) craniofacial abnormalities affecting the upper airway; (3) current CPAP therapy; (4) neuromuscular disorders affecting respiration or airway patency; (5) chronic obstructive pulmonary disease; (6) chronic opioid use for ≥ 90 days; (7) severe refractory insomnia; (8) previous stroke with residual neurological deficits; (9) coexisting primary sleep disorders; (10) pregestational diabetes mellitus; (11) active or recently treated malignancy; (12) chronic catabolic diseases such as advanced heart failure, end-stage renal disease, or uncontrolled autoimmune disease; (13) oropharyngeal dysphagia or eating disorders; (14) intestinal malabsorption syndromes; (15) moderate-to-severe neurocognitive or communication impairment; (16) incomplete or inconsistent medical records; and (17) loss to follow-up.

The study was conducted in line with the Declaration of Helsinki, approved by the Ethics Committee and supported by written informed consent of patients and their families.

Candidate predictors

Clinical data and follow-up information were obtained from electronic medical records and validated patient questionnaires. Candidate predictors included:

Demographic and socioeconomic variables: maternal age, pre-pregnancy BMI, gravidity, parity, education level, place of residence, household income, employment status, and family relationship status.

Clinical and behavioral history: family history of cardiometabolic or respiratory disease; smoking and alcohol use; conception method; pregestational hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, and dyslipidemia; and pregnancy complications including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, gestational diabetes mellitus, thyroid dysfunction, iron-deficiency anemia, gestational thrombocytopenia, and hyperemesis gravidarum.

Anatomical and physiological variables: tonsillar hypertrophy grade, Mallampati classification, habitual sleep position, daily physical activity duration, gestational weight gain, estimated fetal weight, serial antenatal blood pressure, gestational age, fundal height, abdominal circumference, chest circumference, and neck circumference.

Laboratory and functional indicators: lipid profile, ALT, AST, fasting glucose, uric acid, creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, and scores from the STOP-Bang questionnaire, Berlin Questionnaire, and Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS).

Evaluation method

For women admitted to the labor and delivery unit, in-hospital PSG was used as the diagnostic gold standard for OSA. PSG was performed shortly before delivery, at a single, consistent time point during the admission preceding delivery, to ensure diagnostic stability and avoid variability in OSA status that may occur across different stages of pregnancy. All predictor variables, including gestational age, fundal height, abdominal circumference, neck circumference, blood pressure, gestational weight gain, laboratory parameters, pregnancy complications, Berlin Questionnaire scores, Epworth Sleepiness Scale scores, tongue size, and sleep position, were collected during the same admission, ensuring temporal consistency between predictor assessment and outcome ascertainment.

OSA was defined as an apnea-hypopnea index (AHI) ≥ 5 events/h, consistent with the International Classification of Sleep Disorders and clinical guidelines for the diagnosis of sleep-disordered breathing in obstetric populations, whereas AHI < 5 events/h was classified as non-OSA. All confirmed OSA cases were included regardless of severity, spanning mild (AHI 5–14.9 events/h), moderate (AHI 15–29.9 events/h), and severe (AHI ≥30 events/h) categories.

Mallampati classification

The Mallampati classification was used to assess oropharyngeal anatomy and upper airway risk. Patients were examined in the sitting position with the head in a neutral position, mouth fully open, and tongue protruded without phonation.

Grades were defined as follows:

Grade I: soft palate, uvula, tonsillar pillars, and pharyngeal walls fully visible;

Grade II: soft palate and uvula visible, with partial obscuration of tonsillar tissue;

Grade III: only the soft palate and base of the uvula visible;

Grade IV: only the hard palate visible.

STOP-Bang Questionnaire

The STOP-Bang Questionnaire was applied to assess the risk of OSA according to 8 items: snoring, daytime sleepiness, witnessed apnea, hypertension, BMI > 35 kg/m2, age > 50 years, neck circumference > 40 cm, and male sex. Each positive response had a score of 1 point. A total score of ≥ 3 was considered high risk for OSA.

Berlin questionnaire

The Berlin Questionnaire measured the risk of OSA in three areas related to snoring, daytime sleepiness and cardiometabolic risk (hypertension or BMI ≥ 30 kg/m2). Participants with positive responses in at least two domains were classified as high risk.

Epworth sleepiness scale

The ESS was used to assess daytime sleepiness. It includes eight items, with a total score ranging from 0 to 24. A score ≥ 10 indicated clinically significant excessive daytime sleepiness.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were performed using SPSS 25.0 and R 4.4.2. Normally distributed continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation and compared using the independent-samples t-test. Non-normally distributed variables were presented as median [interquartile range] and compared using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency (percentage) and compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate.

Binary logistic regression with backward stepwise selection based on likelihood ratio tests was used to identify independent predictors of OSA. These predictors were used to construct a nomogram in R. Model performance was evaluated in terms of discrimination using the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC), calibration using calibration plots and the Hosmer-Lemeshow test, and clinical utility using decision curve analysis (DCA). A two-sided P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Construction of the prediction model

The model was developed in three steps. First, univariate logistic regression was used to identify candidate predictors with P < 0.1. Second, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression was applied to reduce multicollinearity and select variables with non-zero coefficients. The optimal penalty parameter λ was determined by 10-fold cross-validation using the one-standard-error rule. Third, variables retained by LASSO were entered into a bidirectional stepwise multivariate logistic regression model, and predictors with P < 0.05 were considered independent risk factors.

A nomogram was then developed using the final regression coefficients to estimate the risk for OSA in individuals. Model performance was evaluated using calibration based on 1000 bootstrap resamples and Hosmer-Lemeshow test, discrimination based on area under the curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, positive and negative predictive value at the Youden optimal cutoff, and clinical utility using DCA. To test the stability and generalizability of the models, external validation was done in an independent validation cohort.

Missing data

Missing data rates for the five final model predictors were all below 2%, and the overall missing data rate across all candidate variables was below 10%, representing a low level of missingness unlikely to meaningfully affect the results. The missing data pattern was assessed and found to be missing at random, with no association between missingness and OSA status, indicating that selection bias due to missing data is not a concern. Complete case analysis was therefore applied.

Results

Model discrimination

The model demonstrated good discriminative ability. In the training cohort, the AUC was 0.811 (95% CI: 0.729–0.927). The Hosmer-Lemeshow test showed good fit (P = 0.275). At the optimum cutoff sensitivity was 0.776 (95% CI: 0.681–0.884) and specificity was 0.851 (95% CI: 0.806–0.901).

Calibration

The calibration analysis with 1000 bootstrap resamples was used for both calibration plot generation and optimism correction of model performance estimates. The optimism-corrected performance metrics were as follows: corrected AUC 0.800 (95% CI: 0.635–0.907), calibration slope 0.927, calibration intercept 0.046, and Brier score 0.145, indicating good discriminative ability and calibration with minimal overfitting. The average value of the calibration error was 0.017 which showed high accuracy. Discussion

Another risk factor that is not well understood or appreciated as a cause of OSA is gestational obesity, which is commonly ignored in the context of standard obstetric care because of vague symptoms [16]. Obesity and OSA in pregnancy are linked to the risks of maternal complications, such as gestational hypertension, preeclampsia, gestational diabetes mellitus, cesarean section, and postpartum cardiometabolic diseases, and poor fetal outcomes, including intrauterine growth restriction, preterm birth, and neonatal asphyxia [17], [18], [19]. Both mothers and offspring are at risk in the long term as well, with cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and neurodevelopmental impairments being some of the effects [20], [21]. Thus, it is clinically relevant to identify the risk of OSA in this population at an early stage.

An effective prediction model is necessary in enhancing early detection and prevention. Despite the PSG being the gold standard of OSA diagnosis, it is very expensive and not readily available to be used to screen a large population. The model created in this paper utilizes non-invasive clinical variables that are routinely available to facilitate quick risk stratification in the prenatal care. This justifies a two-step strategy, whereby high-risk patients identified through the model are sent to PSG, hence enhancing the efficiency in diagnosis and the allocation of resources.

The current research focuses on clinical applicability through the choice of variables that are readily available and using a multistage modeling approach. The univariate analysis revealed eight possible predictors and the following LASSO and multivariate regression analysis identified five independent predictors, Mallampati grade, sleep position, neck circumference, Berlin Questionnaire score, and ESS score. These variables indicate the important variables of upper airway anatomy, behavioral variables, and functional sleep disturbance.

The model showed excellent performance. It demonstrated high discrimination (AUC = 0.811), good calibration (Hosmer-Lemeshow P = 0.275; mean calibration error = 0.017) and good clinical utility as indicated by DCA. These results suggest that the model is statistically stable and useful in clinical use to predict the risk of OSA among obese pregnant women.

The risk factors identified are in line with the known pathophysiological mechanisms. Tongue hypertrophy also leads to upper airway constriction and collapse especially in sleep where muscle tone is lowered [22], [23]. This is further worsened by the fact that fat deposition and retention of fluid during obesity and pregnancy increases [24], [25]. The supine position of sleep enhances the airway collapsibility as a result of gravity, but the lateral positioning is more effective in enhancing airway openness and decreasing the occurrence of apnea [26], [27]. Neck circumference is an indicator of upper airway adiposity and has been extensively identified as an independent predictor of OSA [28]. The Berlin Questionnaire and ESS offer standard measures of symptoms of OSA and sleepiness during the day, which makes it possible to identify high-risk individuals at an early stage [29], [30].

There are a number of limitations to be taken into account. First, the model relies on cross sectional data and fails to include dynamic physiological changes in pregnancy. Second, the clinical variables were considered only, excluding biological and imaging markers that can enhance predictive accuracy. Third, the research is not prospectively validated to find out whether model-guided interventions enhance clinical outcomes. Fourth, the study is single-center and has a homogeneous population, which restricts external validity. The restriction to term deliveries (≥37 weeks of gestation) may limit the generalizability of the model to preterm populations, as women who deliver preterm, who may carry a higher OSA risk burden due to associated adverse pregnancy outcomes, were not represented in this cohort. Fifth, there was no inclusion of environmental and lifestyle factors. Lastly, it has to be externally validated in independent populations prior to clinical use.

Conclusion

This paper created a simple and correct nomogram using logistic regression to estimate the OSA risk among obese pregnant women. The model based on regularly accessible clinical variables had excellent predictive quality and useful applicability in Han Chinese populations. It allows the timely detection of people at risk, assists in clinical decision-making, and allows targeted interventions. Also, the model presents the opportunity to conduct the risk assessment individually, which could optimize the prenatal management and follow-up strategies, enhancing maternal and perinatal safety.
