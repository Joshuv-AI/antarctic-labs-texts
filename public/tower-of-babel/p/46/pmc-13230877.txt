A gelatin-stabilized nanosilver particle for visual freshness monitoring of braised chicken meat
Authors: Ya-lin Peng, Yong-zhan Wang, Yu-cong Li, Li-ting Zeng, Xin-yi Song, Xue-qing Li, Ao-jing Lv, Rui-ling Dong, Wen-hao Gao, Lu Feng, He-shuai Li, Jun Qi, Guo-yuan Xiong, Chun-hui Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis study developed a novel gelatin-stabilized nano-silver particle/agar hydrogel (AgNP hydrogel) and investigated its color rendering mechanism for monitoring the freshness of braised chicken. The results showed that the color of AgNP hydrogel shifted from brown to gray-white within 3 days of storage of braised chicken, and its application extended the shelf life of unpackaged braised chicken meat from 4 d to 6 d at 4 °C (P < 0.05). The results of circular dichroism (CD), particle size, zeta potential, and UV indicated that Cl- on the surface of braised chicken could react with Ag+ on the surface of AgNP, resulting in the formation of AgCl particles during storage. These AgCl particles were subsequently transformed into black Ag2S particles, causing the visual color shift of AgNP to gray-white. Overall, the AgNP hydrogel could provide a new method for the freshness assessment of cooked meat products. Subject terms: Biotechnology, Chemistry, Materials science, Nanoscience and technology

Introduction

In China, sauce-braised meat products have been consumed for more than 2000 years and currently hold the largest market share, with an annual consumption approaching $200 billion1. These products are typically prepared by stewing meat or by-products in a sauce-braised soup composed of salt, soy sauce, spices, and water2. A distinctive feature of sauce-braised soup is its repeated reuse during processing, which requires the supplementation of salt, spices, and other seasonings at the end of each stewing period. As a result, fresh meat can obtain similar levels of seasoning from the reused soup, while meat-derived flavor compounds are enriched in the soup, enhancing the overall flavor of sauce-braised meat1. This rich flavor profile largely contributes to the popularity of sauce-braised meat among consumers. Unpackaged sauce-braised meat products, which are not subjected to thermal sterilization, maintain a high level of flavor quality and enjoy the greatest market share. However, their shelf life—especially in summer—is significantly limited, making freshness a crucial concern.

There are several factors that affect the growth of microorganisms in cooked meat, including sterilization, packaging, storage temperature, and use of preservatives. In response to consumer demand for high eating quality and clean-label products, research has focused on extending the shelf life of processed meat by using refrigerator temperatures, modified atmosphere packaging, and natural spices such as rosemary and ginger3. According to hurdle technology, the combination of these preservation strategies can synergistically improve the shelf life of meat products3,4. Recently, edible films have gained attention as a method to extend the shelf life of unpackaged meat products. Yuan et al.5 have reported that chitosan-based films significantly extend the shelf life of fish. In the meat industry, chitosan coatings are considered active packaging that can control microbial contamination after processing, thereby improving the microbial safety. Currently, edible packaging is primarily applied in the field of ready-to-eat meat products and has not been reported in the field of sauce-braised meat products.

Visual freshness perception is another hot topic in the field of meat quality and safety evaluation. Anthocyanins are commonly utilized as pH-sensitive compounds to track spoilage-related color changes in gel films6. As active packaging technologies advance, there is growing demand for multifunctional food packaging materials capable of both extending shelf life and enabling real-time freshness monitoring. Zhang et al.7 have reported a novel antibacterial visualized intelligent film for monitoring pork quality by using chitosan as a pH-responsive and antimicrobial component. Similarly, Li et al.8 have prepared a smart food-packaging material that releases antimicrobials continuously and allows for real-time freshness monitoring. The core of this food film contains a carbon quantum dot derived from citric acid, which responds to pH changes during shrimp storage, while the encapsulated Artemisia argyi emission oil extends shelf life. However, these studies focused exclusively on the freshness of raw meat, where pH increases significantly from 5.0 to 8.0 during spoilage. In contrast, cooked meat exhibits minimal pH variation throughout its shelf life, making pH-based indicators less effective. As a result, it is necessary to develop novel intelligent antibacterial films that function independently of pH response.

A key distinction between sauce-braised and other boiled meat products is the relatively high salt content (3%–5%) in the sauce-braising soup. It has been reported that silver nanoparticles (AgNPs) undergo morphological changes—from triangular to spherical—upon reacting with chloride ions (Cl⁻), accompanied by a blue shift in their surface plasmon resonance (SPR) absorption peaks9. This interaction alters the visual appearance of AgNP solutions. Moreover, AgNPs exhibit broad-spectrum antibacterial activity and have been approved for certain food-related applications by the US Food and Drug Administration10. However, the potential for using AgNP SPR spectral changes to evaluate the freshness of sauce-braised meat products and extend their shelf life remains largely unexplored.

In this experiment, AgNPs were synthesized via a one-pot method using gelatin and subsequently immobilized within agar to form AgNP hydrogels. These hydrogels were then applied to visually monitor the freshness of unpackaged sauce-braised chicken meat stored at 4 °C. The color development mechanism and antibacterial effect of AgNP hydrogels were systematically analyzed. The findings provide a potential strategy for assessing the freshness of meat products independent of pH response, offering a promising approach for the visual freshness monitoring of unpackaged cooked meat products.

Results

Synthesis of AgNPs

Surface plasmon resonance absorption occurs when incident light interacts with metal nanoparticles at a frequency that matches the natural oscillation frequency of free electrons of the metal particles11. This resonance leads to the absorption of light, and the characteristics of the resulting absorption curve—particularly the peak wavelength—are closely related to the shape and size of the nanoparticles12. As shown in Fig. 1a, the synthesized solution displayed a reddish-brown color, and its UV-absorption spectrum showed a strong absorption peak at 420–430 nm. In contrast, the blank control was colorless and exhibited no absorption peak at 300–800 nm. These results indicated the presence of a single SPR band, indicating the formation of silver nanoparticles with uniform morphology. Gelatin can reduce Ag+ ions to Ag0 under high temperature and pressure, and then Ag0 aggregates to form AgNP13. It is well established that spherical AgNPs exhibit a characteristic single SPR absorption peak around 420 nm14. Therefore, based on the observed UV–Vis spectra, it can be concluded that spherical AgNPs were successfully synthesized in this study.

Antibacterial effect of AgNP

The Oxford cup method was used to evaluate the antibacterial activity of AgNPs. Generally, the larger the diameter of the inhibition zone, the stronger the antibacterial effect of AgNPs. As illustrated in Fig. 1b, AgNPs exhibited inhibitory effects against Escherichia coli, Staphylococcus aureus, and Bacillus subtilis, with inhibition zone diameters of 13.52 mm, 12.68 mm, and 12.48 mm, respectively.

The antibacterial effect of AgNP was more pronounced against the Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli) compared to the Gram-positive bacteria (Staphylococcus aureus and Bacillus subtilis). This difference was attributed to the fact that Gram-positive bacteria have thicker cell walls than Gram-negative bacteria, making it easier for AgNPs to penetrate the cell membrane of Gram-negative bacteria15. Additionally, Gram-negative bacteria typically have a higher negative charge than Gram-positive bacteria16, and the AgNP prepared in this experiment had a positive charge. As a result, AgNP combined easier with Gram-negative bacteria, increasing bacterial membrane permeability and causing their death.

Effect of storage time of braised chicken meat on the color of AgNP hydrogels

The effects of storage time on the visual appearance and CIE chromaticity coordinates of AgNP hydrogels in contact with braised chicken meat are shown in Fig. S1 and Table 1, respectively. As shown in Fig. S1, the AgNP hydrogels (AH) exposed to air remained reddish-brown color over 7 days of storage at 4 °C, exhibiting no obvious visual color change. This outcome indicated that gelatin-stabilized AgNP exhibited greater storage stability. Similarly, the AgNP hydrogels (AH-0%) in contact with boiled chicken also maintained a reddish-brown color throughout the storage period, consistent with those stored in air. In contrast, AgNP hydrogels in contact with braised chicken prepared using 3%, 4%, and 5% salt solutions underwent a clear color transition from reddish-brown to gray-white. These results indicated that the color transformation of AgNP hydrogels during storage was closely associated with the salt concentration in the sauce-braising soup used for stewing chicken meat.

The results from Table 1 showed that the L* value of the AgNP hydrogels first decreased significantly (P < 0.05), then increased significantly (P < 0.05), and finally remained constant for the last 5 days. In general, the L* value of samples is positively correlated with their surface moisture. Therefore, the initial decrease in the L* value might be attributed to surface water evaporation, while the subsequent increase in the L* value might be attributed to an increase in the surface moisture caused by the rebalancing of moisture within the hydrogels. However, the overall variation in the L* value of these hydrogels throughout the 7-day storage period was within 10%, aligning with the absence of significant visual color changes. AgNP hydrogels in contact with boiled or braised chicken meat (3–5% salt) exhibited similar trends in the L* values to those stored in air. As the salt concentration of boiled chicken solution increased, the L* value of the AgNP hydrogels in contact with braised chicken meat at each storage period first decreased significantly and then increased significantly within the first 5 days of storage, whereas the L* value of these hydrogels at each storage time increased significantly within the last 2 days of storage. Overall, these L* values varied between 5% and 10%.

The a* value of the AgNP hydrogels exposed to air increased significantly (P < 0.05) after 3 days of storage, then remained constant for the next 3 days, and increased significantly (P < 0.05) on day 7. This trend may be attributed to gradual oxidation of silver nanoparticles17. The AgNP hydrogels in contact with boiled chicken meat exhibited a similar a* trend, with a variation of ~approximately 15%, corresponding to no significant visible color change. However, the a* value of the AgNP hydrogels in contact with braised chicken meat made by cooking with 3%, 4%, and 5% salt solutions decreased significantly (P < 0.05) by about 80% during the first 3 days of storage and then remained constant. These outcomes were contrary to the trend of redness of the AgNP hydrogels exposed to air or in contact with boiled chicken meat but were consistent with the visible discoloration of these hydrogels from reddish-brown to gray-white (Fig. S1). These results suggested that salt present in braised chicken meat could affect the morphology or aggregation state of AgNPs through direct contact, resulting in a significant change in the redness18.

The b* value of the AgNP hydrogels exposed to air and in contact with boiled chicken meat decreased significantly (P < 0.05) during storage, especially within the first 3 days. The decrease in yellowness should be due to the oxidation of nanosilver particles17. The b* values of the AgNP hydrogels exposed to air and in contact with boiled chicken meat decreased by about 20% during the storage period. In contrast, the b* values of the AgNP hydrogels in contact with braised chicken meat made by cooking with 3%, 4%, and 5% salt solutions decreased significantly by about 65% during the first 3 days of storage and then remained constant. The results were consistent with the observed color transition of the hydrogels from brown to gray-white. Therefore, the results of the yellowness values implied that salt in braised chicken meat had a substantial effect on the structure of nanosilver particles in the hydrogels through surface contact18.

Together with the results of the L*, a*, and b* values, as well as the visual observations, the AgNP hydrogels in contact with braised chicken meat made by cooking with 3%, 4%, and 5% salt solutions showed significant color changes during the first 3 days of storage. Generally, the recommended selling period of fresh, unpackaged braised meat products should not exceed 3 days at 4 °C, beyond which flavor deteriorates markedly. As a result, the AgNP hydrogels developed in this study demonstrated potential as effective visual indicators for monitoring the freshness of braised chicken meat under refrigerated conditions.

Effect of AgNP hydrogels on the total aerobic microbial counts of braised meat

The effect of the AgNP hydrogels on the total aerobic microbial counts of braised chicken meat is shown in Fig. 2a. As the storage time increased, the total aerobic microbial counts of braised chicken meat in all treatment groups increased significantly (P < 0.05), particularly during the first 4 days of storage. Notably, after 2 days of storage, the total aerobic microbial counts of braised chicken meat in contact with the AgNP hydrogels were significantly lower than those in the control group without AgNP hydrogel treatment (P < 0.05). However, there were no significant differences in the total aerobic microbial counts of braised chicken meat made by cooking with different salt content solutions (P > 0.05). According to the Chinese National Standard GB 2726-2016, the total aerobic microbial counts of braised chicken meat should be less than 50,000 CFU/g (4.70 log CFU/g). In the control group, this threshold was reached on the 4th day. In contrast, samples treated with AgNP hydrogels only exceeded the limit on the 6th day, indicating a delayed microbial proliferation. These findings suggested that AgNP hydrogels exhibited effective antimicrobial activity, consistent with previous reports by Mathew et al.19, who found that silver nanoparticle-infused nanocomposite pouches significantly delayed microbial growth of chicken sausage during the storage period.

Effect of AgNP hydrogels on the total volatile basic nitrogen (TVB-N) of braised meat

Total volatile basic nitrogen is used to evaluate the accumulation of basic nitrogen substances-such as cadaverine, putrescine, tyramine, spermine, and ammonia- generated via microbial-induced decarboxylation and deamination of proteins during storage20. Figure 2b shows the effect of AgNP hydrogels on TVB-N levels in braised chicken meat. At the beginning of storage, the TVB-N values of both boiled and braised chicken meat ranged from 12.6 mg/100 mg to 13.3 mg/100 mg. As the storage time increased, the TVB-N values increased significantly in all treatment groups (P < 0.05). After 7 days of storage, the TVB-N value of boiled chicken meat from the control group reached 24.73 mg/100 g. However, chicken samples in contact with AgNP hydrogels exhibited significantly lower TVB-N values compared to the control, indicating a suppression of protein degradation due to decreased microbial activity. Interestingly, the TVB-N value of boiled chicken meat (AH-0%) was significantly higher than that of braised chicken meat (AH-3%, AH-4%, and AH-5%), which might be due to the antibacterial effect provided by the higher osmotic pressure of sodium chloride21. Together with the results of the total aerobic microbial counts, it suggested that AgNP hydrogels inhibited the microbial growth of braised meat products, even though surface contact alone.

Selective response of AgNP hydrogels to inorganic anions

The pH values of chicken meat in different treatment groups during storage are shown in Fig. 2c. With the increased storage time, the pH values increased significantly in all groups (P < 0.05), likely due to the accumulation of basic amines produced by microbial activity on the meat surface22. After 3 days of storage, the pH of boiled chicken meat from the control group was significantly higher than that of braised chicken meat in contact with AgNP hydrogels (P < 0.05). Among the AgNP-treated samples, the pH values of boiled chicken meats (AH-0%) were significantly higher than those of braised chicken meat (AH-3%, AH-4%, and AH-5%), though no significant differences were observed among chicken meat made by cooking with 3%, 4%, and 5% salt solutions. These results were similar to those of TVB-N and total aerobic microbial counts.

Currently, there have been a lot of studies focusing on visually monitoring the freshness of meat based on pH variations during storage. In these studies, anthocyanins are typically used to achieve visual color development based on the increasing pH of meat from acidic to basic23. However, in this experiment, the pH of braised chicken meat remained relatively stable, ranging only from 6.25 to 6.43 over the entire storage period. Thus, pH is not a reliable indicator of spoilage for this product type. A key distinction in the processing of braised chicken meat lies in the use of 3–5% salt in the sauce-braising soup. As shown in Table 1 and Fig. S1, AgNP hydrogels in contact with braised chicken prepared with 3%-5% salt solution underwent obvious visual color changes within 3 days of storage at 4 °C, suggesting that the presence of salt—particularly chloride ions—plays a critical role in the color-responsive behavior of AgNPs.

Figure 3a shows the UV–Vis spectral response of AgNPs to various common anions found in meat. At an equal molar concentration, AgNP exhibited a significantly stronger response to Cl- compared to CH3COO-, OH-, NO3-, and SO42-. The response intensity of AgNP to Cl- was approximately 8-50 times higher than that observed for the other ions. This discrepancy is likely related to the solubility product constants (Ksp) of the corresponding silver salts24. As shown in Table S1, AgCl has a much higher Ksp than other silver-based compounds, indicating a higher tendency to precipitate and react with Ag⁺. These findings suggested that Cl⁻ should be the primary anion responsible for the observed color changes in AgNP hydrogels. It is hypothesized that during the braising process, Cl⁻ ions penetrate the meat tissues and later migrate back to the meat surface during storage. Upon contact with the AgNP hydrogel, these chloride ions interact with silver nanoparticles, altering their structure and inducing the visible color change observed in the hydrogels.

Color-responsive freshness behavior of AgNP hydrogels

To elucidate the role of gelatin, PVP, and silver nitrate in the formation of silver nanoparticles, Table S2 shows the changes in particle size and zeta-potential of each component following heat treatment at 121 °C for 20 min—conditions consistent with those used for AgNP synthesis. The table also presents the changes in particle size and zeta potential after the reaction of these components with NaCl. Following heat treatment, gelatin and PVP exhibited negative zeta potentials, while silver nitrate remained positively charged. However, the heat-treated silver nitrate did not exhibit the characteristic surface plasmon resonance (SPR) peak of silver nanoparticles in the UV–Vis spectrum (data not shown), indicating that silver nitrate alone could not form AgNPs in the absence of a reducing agent. The average particle size of the synthesized AgNPs was 23.80 nm (Fig. 4c), which was comparable to the size reported by Yu et al.25. The corresponding zeta potential was 13.38 mV (Fig. 4d), indicating that the positive surface charge likely originated from residual Ag⁺ ions adsorbed onto the nanoparticle surface. Notably, the zeta potential of heat-treated silver nitrate was ~2.3 times higher than that of the synthesized AgNPs, implying that a portion of Ag⁺ was reduced to Ag⁰ in the presence of gelatin26.

PVP, a commonly used stabilizer for hydrophilic nanoparticles, contains numerous polar groups along its polyethylene backbone27. After heat treatment, the particle size of PVP was approximately 58% smaller than that of gelatin. The particle size of the PVP–gelatin mixture after heating was significantly larger than that of heat-treated PVP alone, yet remained markedly smaller than that of heat-treated gelatin. This outcome may be attributed to hydrogen bonding interactions between the polar groups of PVP and the proline residues in gelatin28, leading to the incorporation of PVP within the three-dimensional structure of gelatin and forming a more compact gelatin-PVP complex. Furthermore, the zeta potential of the gelatin-PVP complex was significantly lower than that of gelatin alone, supporting the hypothesis that PVP modifies the structure of gelatin when encapsulated within it.

Salt can induce conformational changes in proteins by altering electrostatic interactions and acting as negatively charged residues to form salt bridges between carboxyl groups29. Upon addition of NaCl, the particle size of heat-treated gelatin increased significantly (P < 0.05), while its absolute zeta potential value decreased significantly (P < 0.05). These changes suggested that NaCl shielded electrostatic repulsion among ionizable groups along the gelatin chain, promoting molecular aggregation. This outcome was consistent with the findings of Sow and Yang30, who reported that 1.5% NaCl induced a transition in fish gelatin from an ordered to a disordered structure, decreasing α-helical content and increasing random coil level. Similarly, the particle size of PVP increased significantly from 15.87 nm to 25.63 nm (P < 0.05) after the addition of NaCl, likely due to a salting-out effect31. For AgNO3, the addition of NaCl caused Ag+ to react readily with Cl-, forming AgCl precipitation and significantly increasing the particle size (P < 0.05). Under identical NaCl treatment, the particle size of these individual components increased by approximately 25%-60%. However, the particle size of silver nanoparticles increased by 172% (Fig. 4c). This substantial increase can be attributed to the formation of AgCl via surface reactions between Cl⁻ and residual Ag⁺ ions on the surface of AgNPs, as well as enhanced aggregation of the gelatin–PVP network induced by salt.

In order to reveal the color-responsive freshness behavior of AgNP hydrogels in contact with braised chicken meat during cold storage, the effects of Cl− and H2S on the structure of gelatin-stabilized silver nanoparticles were investigated, given that H2S is also an important factor in the color development of silver nanoparticles. Figure 4 shows the effect of NaCl concentrations on the visual appearance, UV–Vis absorption spectra, particle size, zeta potential, and circular dichroism (CD) spectra of AgNP solutions.

As shown in Fig. 4a, the UV absorption increased with the increase in the Cl- content. This enhancement is attributed to the reaction between Cl- and Ag+ on the surface of AgNP, leading to the formation of AgCl precipitation32. These precipitates adhered to the AgNP surface, creating a “black hole” effect that enhanced the UV absorbance. Besides, the half-peak width of UV-absorption spectra, indicative of particle size uniformity33, broadened with increasing Cl⁻ content (Fig. 4a), suggesting a decreased uniformity of AgNPs. This observation was consistent with the result that the original spatial structure of AgNPs was destroyed by the reaction between Cl- and Ag+ on the surface of AgNP.

The AgNPs synthesized via the one-pot method had an average particle size of 23.80 ± 3.53 nm (Fig. 4c). In this process, PVP could interact with hydrophilic groups of gelatin and was embedded within the gelatin matrix, providing steric hindrance, while hydrophobic residues of gelatin could interact with the hydrophobic AgNP surface. These interactions contributed to the colloidal stability of the AgNPs. As the NaCl concentration increased, the particle size of AgNP increased significantly (P < 0.05), from 23.80 nm to 64.86 nm (Fig. 4c). As shown in Fig. 4d, the zeta potential of AgNPs decreased significantly from 13.83 mV to 0.55 mV with the increased NaCl concentration (P < 0.05). This decrease confirmed that Cl⁻ neutralized the surface charge of AgNPs by reacting with Ag⁺, thereby weakening electrostatic repulsion and promoting aggregation. Since electrostatic repulsion plays a critical role in maintaining nanoparticle dispersion34, the observed decrease in zeta potential was consistent with the increased particle size (Fig. 4c) and decreased stability (Fig. 4b).

UV-CD spectroscopy was used to analyze the effect of NaCl on the secondary structure of gelatin in AgNPs, as shown in Fig. 4e. Two characteristic peaks were observed: a positive peak at 220 nm and a negative peak at 199 nm, which was in line with the typical CD spectrum characteristics of gelatin35. As the NaCl concentration increased, the absolute value of the negative peak decreased significantly (P < 0.05), while there was no significant difference in the absolute value of the positive peak at 220 nm. A decrease in the absolute value of the negative peak of the CD spectrum of gelatin indicates an enhanced aggregation of gelatin molecules. As a result, this finding suggested that gelatin molecules exhibited a higher degree of aggregation as the NaCl concentration increased, which was consistent with the results of the UV spectrum (Fig. 4a), particle size (Fig. 4c), and zeta potential (Fig. 4d).

To further explore the color response mechanism, the impact of H₂S on the NaCl-treated AgNP solution was investigated (Fig. 5). This investigation was motivated by previous reports indicating that spoilage microorganisms commonly found in sauce-braised meat products, such as Pseudomonas fragi, are capable of producing H₂S36. Moreover, H₂S is recognized as a key indicator of spoilage during the deterioration of meat products37. Upon exposure to gaseous H₂S, the absorbance at 420 nm increased significantly with the increased NaCl concentration (Fig. 5a), and the visual appearance of the AgNP solution gradually transformed from transparent yellow to gray (Fig. 5b). This transformation could be explained by the solubility product constants (Ksp) of silver salts: Ag₂S (Ksp = 6.3 × 10⁻⁵⁰) and AgCl (Ksp = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰). Because Ag₂S is far less soluble than AgCl, AgCl formed on the AgNP surface was replaced by Ag₂S in the presence of H₂S, HS⁻, and S²⁻ ions38. In previous studies, H₂S generated during the spoilage of fresh chicken reacted with gas-sensitive AgNP hydrogels to form Ag₂S, resulting in a visible color change of the hydrogel from yellow to colorless37. However, as shown in Fig. 5b, when H₂S gas was introduced into the AgNP solution in the absence of sodium chloride, no significant color change was observed, and the solution did not turn colorless. This discrepancy was likely attributable to differences in the structural characteristics of AgNPs used in this study.

The formation of the Ag2S layer on the AgNP surface significantly altered the surface plasmon resonance (SPR) characteristics of AgNPs, resulting in a visual color shift (Fig. 5b). Moreover, the extent of this transformation was modulated by the NaCl concentration, which influenced the amount of pre-formed AgCl available for conversion, consistent with observations by Estrada-Mendoza, et al.39. Following H₂S exposure, the particle size of AgNP increased further from 64.87 nm to 116.63 nm (P < 0.05, Fig. 5c), and the Zeta potential decreased significantly (P < 0.05, Fig. 5d), indicating further deterioration of colloidal stability.

Upon the addition of NaCl, the absolute value of the negative peak value of the CD spectrum of gelatin decreased significantly, but there was no significant difference in the absolute value of the negative peak value when the NaCl concentration increased (Fig. 5e). The subsequent introduction of H₂S led to a further decrease in the negative peak intensity, confirming that gelatin aggregation was exacerbated under combined salt and H2S. In brief, by constructing a reaction system involving NaCl and H₂S with the AgNP solution, a distinct color change from reddish-brown to gray-white was achieved. This color transition was consistent with the color change trend observed in the AgNP hydrogel shown in Fig. S1. Based on the color changes of the hydrogel (Table 1 and Fig. S1), it can be concluded that during the first three days of storage, the color change of the hydrogel—used to monitor the freshness of sauce-braised chicken—was primarily attributed to the migration of NaCl to the meat surface. In contrast, during the subsequent four days, the color change of AgNP hydrogels was mainly driven by the presence of H₂S, a product of microbial metabolism, which was consistent with the results of total aerobic microbial counts (Fig. 2a) and TVB-N (Fig. 2b).

In summary, the one-pot synthesis of AgNPs involved the reduction of Ag⁺ to Ag⁰ by gelatin, with residual Ag⁺ adsorbed onto the nanoparticle surface. Ag⁰ interacted with hydrophobic residues of gelatin, while PVP embedded into the three-dimensional structure of gelatin through hydrogen bonding between the carboxyl group of PVP and proline residues of gelatin. This gelatin–PVP complex functioned as a structural scaffold, stabilizing the AgNPs through hydrophobic interactions. During storage, NaCl from braised chicken meat migrated to the surface, where Cl⁻ reacted with surface Ag⁺ to form AgCl particles attached to the AgNP surface. Simultaneously, NaCl shielded electrostatic interactions between ionizable groups of gelatin, promoting gelatin aggregation and reducing the stability of the gelatin–PVP matrix. With the extension of storage time, H₂S produced by microbial metabolism converted surface-bound AgCl into black Ag₂S, further destabilizing the gelatin–PVP complex. These structural and chemical transformations collectively led to a visible color shift of the AgNP hydrogels from reddish-brown to gray-white, thereby explaining the color-responsive freshness mechanism of gelatin-stabilized AgNP hydrogels in contact with braised chicken meat.

This study presented an AgNP hydrogel capable of monitoring the freshness of sauce-braised chicken products. Unlike conventional systems that rely on pH sensitivity, this hydrogel film responded specifically to NaCl and H₂S, offering a novel and pH-independent approach for the freshness evaluation of cooked meat. However, it is important to acknowledge the limitations of this work. In authentic sauce-braising systems, the presence of other components in the braising soup—such as nucleotides, amino acids, and various soluble substances—may potentially interfere with the colorimetric response of the hydrogel. Assessing the impact of these compounds on the performance and reliability of the hydrogel will be the focus of future investigations.

Discussion

In this study, AgNPs were successfully synthesized via a one-pot method using gelatin as both a reducing and stabilizing agent. PVP was incorporated into the three-dimensional gelatin matrix through hydrogen bonding between its carboxyl groups and the proline residues of gelatin, thereby enhancing the structural stability of the resulting AgNPs. Due to the inherent antimicrobial properties of AgNPs, the shelf life of unpackaged sauce-braised chicken meat was effectively extended from 4 to 6 days under cold storage conditions.

Traditional pH-based freshness indicators are not suitable for sauce-braised chicken, as its pH only increased slightly from 6.25 to 6.43 throughout the entire storage period. To address this limitation, this study developed an AgNP hydrogel capable of evaluating the freshness of sauce-braised meat products based on two key factors during storage: the migration of Cl− and the production of H₂S by spoilage microorganisms. During storage, Cl− migrated from the interior of chicken meat to the surface, where Cl⁻ reacted with Ag⁺ on the surface of AgNPs, forming AgCl precipitates. In the presence of H₂S, these AgCl particles were subsequently transformed into black Ag₂S, while the gelatin–PVP complex underwent further aggregation. These physicochemical transformations resulted in a distinct color change of the AgNP hydrogel from reddish-brown to gray-white, providing a clear visual indicator of the freshness of sauce-braised chicken meat.

Overall, this study offers both experimental evidence and theoretical insight into the color-responsive behavior of gelatin-stabilized AgNP hydrogels and supports their potential application in real-time freshness monitoring of sauce-braised meat products.

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

Gelatin (type B, ~240 bloom, ≥ 95%) was supplied by Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., while polyvinyl pyrrolidone (PVP) was purchased from Shanghai Maclean Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. All other chemicals and solvents used in this study were of analytical grade.

Chicken breasts were sourced from 40-day-old chickens and purchased post-slaughter from a local supermarket (RT-Mart, Hefei, China).

Preparation of AgNP

Gelatin particles (1 g) were dissolved in 90 mL of pure water and stirred for 30 min at 60 °C to ensure complete dissolution. After cooling the gelatin solution to room temperature, 1 g of PVP and 10 mL of 0.1 mol/L silver nitrate solution were added. The mixture was stirred at 1200 rpm for 10 min in the dark. The resulting solution was then transferred to a 150 mL conical flask and subjected to thermal treatment in an autoclave (LX-B100L, Kangmai Instrument, Guangdong, China) at 121 °C for 20 min. The sample solution acquired after the thermal reaction exhibited a reddish-brown color, and these samples were stored in brown bottles for future use.

Determination of UV–Vis spectroscopy

The UV-Vis spectrum of AgNP was recorded in the range of 300–800 nm using a scanning multi-well spectrophotometer (SpectraMax M2e, Molecular Devices, Sunnyvale, CA, USA).

The antimicrobial activity of AgNP

The antibacterial activity of AgNP was evaluated using the Oxford Cup Method with modifications, as described by Feng et al.40. Gram-positive bacteria (S. aureus and B. subtilis) and Gram-negative bacteria (E. coli) were selected and incubated in Luria-Bertani (LB) liquid medium at 37 °C for 24 h. Each bacterial suspension (0.1 mL, ~10⁶ CFU/mL) was evenly spread onto solidified LB agar plates. After the plates were prepared, sterile Oxford cups (8 mm in diameter) were carefully placed on the agar surface. Subsequently, 150 μL of the AgNP solution was added to each cup, while distilled water served as the negative control. The plates were left at room temperature for 1 h to allow diffusion of the samples, followed by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h. The antibacterial activity of AgNP was evaluated by measuring the average diameter of the circular antibacterial zone using a Vernier caliper.

Preparation of AgNP hydrogel

A 2 g aliquot of agar powder was added to 100 mL of AgNP solution and heated at 80 °C for 10 min on a magnetic stirrer at 1200 r/min to ensure complete dissolution. After ultrasonic degassing, 30 mL of the mixed solution was poured into a polystyrene petri dish with a diameter of 90 mm, and the AgNP hydrogel was obtained after solidification at ambient temperature.

Application of AgNP hydrogels on the surface of chicken

Generally, the salt content of braised soup that is used for stewing meat in the processing of sauce-braised meat products is between 3% and 5%. Therefore, in order to simulate the stewing process of braised soup, a series of solutions containing 3%, 4%, and 5% NaCl were used in the experiment to stew fresh chicken breast meat (RT-Mart, Hefei, China). Fresh chicken breast meat was cut into uniform pieces measuring 2.5 cm in length, 2.5 cm in width, and 0.5 cm in height. In order to simulate the stewing process of sauce-braised chicken products, the meat pieces were placed in NaCl solutions containing 0%, 3%, 4%, and 5%, respectively. Meanwhile, the meat pieces cooked in distilled water were set as a blank group. Following cooking, on a sterile operating table, the cooked meat was taken out with sterilized tweezers, and its surface moisture was absorbed using filter paper. Each piece was then placed onto a 4-cm square of AgNP hydrogel within a disposable Petri dish. Based on the NaCl concentration used during cooking, the samples were designated as AH-0%, AH-3%, AH-4%, and AH-5%, respectively. All dishes were stored at 4 °C for 7 d for further analysis.

Color evaluation of AgNP hydrogels

The color parameters L* (brightness), a* (+, red; -, green), and b* (+, yellow; -, blue) of AgNP hydrogels were measured using a chromameter (CR-400, Konica Minolta Inc., Tokyo, Japan). A standard whiteboard (L*0 = 92.91; a*0 = -0.51; b*0 = 5.52) was used for calibrating the colorimeter and served as the background for measurements. Five points were randomly selected within the hydrogel region located directly beneath the areas in contact with the braised chicken pieces, and the CIE L*a*b* color coordinates were recorded as the average of these 5 measurements. Meanwhile, the appearance of AgNP hydrogels was observed with a digital camera.

Total aerobic microbial count measurement

Total aerobic microbial counts of chicken samples were conducted according to Katiyo et al.22. Briefly, 1 g of chicken meat was placed in a sterile bag (Bkman Biological, Hunan, China) and homogenized with 9 mL of 0.1% sterilized peptone water (Basal Media, Shanghai, China). Subsequently, 0.1 mL of serial decimal dilutions of homogenates was spread on Plate Count Agar (PCA, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) for incubation at 37 °C for 48 h. The results were expressed as log CFU/g.

Total volatile basic nitrogen test (TVB-N)

TVB-N was conducted according to the micro-diffusion method of Chinese National Standard Method GB 5009.228—2016. Briefly, 5 g of ground chicken meat was mixed with 100 mL of distilled water in a beaker and shaken for 30 min at room temperature. The mixture was then filtered through filter paper. Subsequently, 1 mL of the filtrate and 1 mL of saturated K2CO3 were added to the outer chamber of a diffusion plate, and 1 mL of 20 g/L H3BO3 solution and 1 drop of mixed indicator (1 part of 1 g/L C15H15N3O2 solution and 5 parts of 1 g/L C21H14Br4O5S solution) were added in the central chamber of the plate. The diffusion plates were incubated at 37 °C for 2 h, then cooled to room temperature. Finally, the boric acid solution was titrated with 0.01 mol/L HCl. TVB-N values were expressed in mg/100 g of sample.

pH analysis

The pH of chicken meat was measured according to Liu et al.41 with some modifications. A 5 g portion of chicken meat was homogenized with 50 mL of distilled water at 8000 r/min for 2 min. The homogenate was then filtered, and the pH of the resulting filtrate was measured using a calibrated pH meter (PHS-3C, Shanghai INESA Scientific Instrument Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China).

Anion selectivity analysis

Anion selectivity analysis was performed according to Sangaonkar et al.42 with modifications. A 10 mL aliquot of the AgNP solution (10 mL) was mixed with 100 μL of pure water or 1 M solutions of various anions, including Cl⁻, CH₃COO⁻, OH⁻, NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, and NO₂⁻. Afterwards, the mixture was allowed to stand for 10 minutes before its UV spectra were measured at 520 nm.

Preparation and structure analysis of the AgNP simulation system

Separate solutions of 1% gelatin, 1% PVP, 0.01 M AgNO3, and a mixture of 1% gelatin and 1% PVP were prepared. These solutions were subjected to thermal treatment in an autoclave at 121 °C and 0.12 MPa for 20 min. Subsequently, 2 portions of 10 mL solutions were taken from each solution and mixed separately with distilled water and 100 μL of 1 M NaCl solution. These groups were labeled as the NaCl-free gelatin group, the NaCl-containing gelatin group, the NaCl-free PVP group, the NaCl-containing PVP group, the NaCl-free AgNO3 group, the NaCl-containing AgNO3 group, the NaCl-free gelatin and PVP group, and the NaCl-containing gelatin and PVP group. Similarly, a 2% gelatin solution and a 2% PVP solution were prepared separately and subjected to the same autoclave conditions. After the thermal reaction, the 2% gelatin solution and 2% PVP solution were mixed in a ratio of 1:1 to obtain two portions of 10 mL solutions, and these solutions were separately mixed with distilled water and 100 μL of 1 M NaCl solution. These groups were labeled as the NaCl-free mixed group of gelatin and PVP and the NaCl-containing mixed group of gelatin and PVP. The effect of NaCl on the particle size and zeta potential of components in the AgNP system was observed.

Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was employed to investigate the secondary structure of gelatin within the wavelength range of 190–260 nm. A gelatin solution was diluted to a final concentration of 0.125 mg/mL. Subsequently, 100 μL of the diluted solution was introduced into a 0.1 cm quartz cuvette (Hellma, Muellheim, Baden, Germany). A spectrophotometer (Applied Photophysics Ltd., London, England) was utilized with the following settings: a step size of 1 nm, an acquisition time of 0.5 s per data point, and a bandwidth of 1 nm. The obtained spectra were averaged over 6 scans, and the mean residue ellipticity (θ) was calculated based on an average value of 115 amino acid residues.

The samples were diluted 100-fold with distilled water prior to analysis. Particle size and zeta potential measurements were conducted using a nanoparticle size and zeta potential analyzer.

Effects of NaCl and H2S on the structure of AgNP

In order to determine the effect of NaCl on the structure of AgNP, a series of NaCl solutions with different concentrations (0, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 mM) were added individually in 10 μL portions to 10 mL of AgNP solution. After shaking for equilibrium, the particle size, zeta potential, UV spectrum, circular dichroism, and visual appearance of the samples were measured.

The effect of H2S on the structure of AgNP was analyzed according to Zhai et al.37. At ambient temperature, a certain mass of Na2S solid was weighed and placed in a gas-collecting bottle. Simultaneously, an HCl solution with a molar concentration three times higher than that of the Na2S solid was prepared. The excess HCl was included to prevent the re-dissolution of H2S gas into the solution. Subsequently, a 100-mL aliquot of the prepared HCl solution was added to the gas-collecting bottle containing the Na2S solid, and the bottle was sealed immediately. The H2S gas generated from the reaction between Na2S and HCl was introduced into the AgNP solution, which had varying NaCl concentrations, through nitrogen gas bubbling for thorough absorption. The entire experiment reached equilibrium after 1 h of reaction, following which the particle size, zeta potential, UV spectrum, circular dichroism, and visual appearance were measured.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD (standard deviation) values of four independent experiments. The analyses of variances were determined by one-way analysis using SAS software (SAS Institute Inc., North Carolina, USA), followed by Duncan’s multiple range test at a significance level of P < 0.05.
