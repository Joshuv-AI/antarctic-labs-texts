Association of disease-modifying antirheumatic drug selection with hospitalised infection among youth with childhood-onset systemic lupus erythematosus
Authors: Jordan E Roberts, Anna V Faino, Marshall Brown, Gabrielle Alonzi, Mersine A Bryan, Cordelia Burn, Joyce C Chang, Jonathan D Cogen, Nidhi Naik, Kareena Patel, Emily Zhang, Esi M Morgan, MaryBeth Son
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveYouth with childhood-onset SLE (cSLE) have increased risk of serious infection. It is unknown how much of this risk is due to modifiable factors such as choice of immunosuppressant. We aimed to compare hospitalised infection rates in youth with cSLE on different disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs).MethodsWe included youth ≤18 years with cSLE treated from 2009 to 2022 at two centres. Clinical data were extracted from electronic health records and the Paediatric Health Information System. Hospitalised infection frequency was calculated over the first year of treatment in youth included in each DMARD exposure group, stratified by lupus nephritis (LN) status. Cox proportional hazard regression with inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was used to compare infection rates across DMARD groups, adjusting for corticosteroid dose.ResultsAmong 257 youths with cSLE, 5% had ≥1 hospitalised infection within 1 year of DMARD initiation. 8% of those with LN had ≥1 hospitalised infection compared with 2.5% without LN. In IPTW-adjusted models, children with LN treated with mycophenolate had lower risk of infection compared with those treated with cyclophosphamide (HR 0.12; 95% CI 0.019 to 0.88). Among those without LN, mycophenolate did not differ from azathioprine in infection risk (HR 1.67, 95% CI 0.56 to 4.99). Higher oral corticosteroid dosing (per 1 mg/day of prednisone) was associated with increased risk of infection (HR 1.1, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.15).ConclusionsWe observed higher hospitalised infection rates in children with cSLE and LN compared with those without LN. Among children with LN, those who received cyclophosphamide had more infections than those who received mycophenolate. Methotrexate was associated with lower infection rates than mycophenolate or azathioprine among youth without LN. Higher daily oral steroid dose was significantly associated with increased hospitalised infection risk in youth with non-renal SLE.

Introduction

Youth with childhood-onset SLE (cSLE) have increased risk of serious infection.1 2 Like adults with SLE, the risk of infection in cSLE is multifactorial and includes immune system dysfunction associated with lupus as well as the immunosuppressive therapies used for treatment.35 This problem is particularly complex in children with cSLE given the overlap with immunodeficiencies such as complement deficiency.6 7 Youth with cSLE also tend to have more severe disease compared with adult-onset lupus, leading to higher dosing of glucocorticoids and more frequent use of immunosuppressants as part of multidrug regimens.810 The risk of infection is higher still in those with lupus nephritis (LN), who in addition to having more severe disease, may have further risk factors including proteinuria and more intensive immunosuppressive treatment.2 However, it is unknown how much of the risk of infection in cSLE is due to modifiable factors, including choice of immunosuppressant medication and corticosteroid dosing. Currently, there is limited understanding of how specific disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs (DMARDs) contribute to the risk of serious infection, and even fewer studies in children, limiting efforts to mitigate these risks in clinical practice.

In this study, we aimed to compare hospitalised infection rates in youth with cSLE treated with different DMARDs, while adjusting for detailed disease-related and demographic factors and coadministered medications including corticosteroids to isolate the specific impact of immunosuppressant choice. We hypothesised that use of cyclophosphamide and higher dose intravenous and oral steroid would be associated with increased serious infection risk.

Patients and methods

Study design and population

We conducted a retrospective longitudinal cohort study to assess the association between DMARD selection and hospitalised infection over the first year of cSLE treatment. The study population included youth diagnosed with cSLE based on American College of Rheumatology (ACR)/European League Against Rheumatism (EULAR) 2019 criteria11 at age 18 or younger who were treated from time of lupus diagnosis at two large paediatric rheumatology centres. Youth were eligible if they initiated a non-biological DMARD other than hydroxychloroquine for treatment of their cSLE between 2009 and 2022. Youth were excluded if care was started at another centre and disease characteristics or medications received including corticosteroid dosing could not be confirmed, or if they received a biological DMARD for cSLE prior to initiating a non-biological DMARD. Youth who had previously received an immunosuppressive or chemotherapeutic agent for another condition prior to cSLE diagnosis were also excluded in order to ensure that immunosuppressive effects of another medication were not incorrectly attributed to the first DMARD prescribed for cSLE.

Clinical and demographic data were extracted using manual chart review and structured data from electronic health records (EHR), and from inpatient encounters in the Paediatric Health Information System (PHIS) data set. The PHIS is a national data set administered by the Children’s Hospital Association (Lenexa, Kansas, USA) with contributions from over 50 children’s hospitals in the USA, including the two paediatric rheumatology centres included in this study. It includes billing codes for diagnoses assigned, procedures performed and medications received during emergency department, surgical, observation and inpatient encounters.

Exposure and outcome definitions

The primary exposure was the first DMARD received for cSLE after initial diagnosis other than hydroxychloroquine (ie, cyclophosphamide, mycophenolate mofetil, azathioprine or methotrexate). These DMARDs were selected based on their inclusion in treatment guidelines for LN induction therapy (cyclophosphamide and mycophenolate mofetil), and non-renal lupus (mycophenolate, azathioprine and methotrexate), as well as data showing that these were the most commonly used DMARDs for cSLE.1214 Dates of initiation and discontinuation were extracted manually by chart review to include both hospital-administered and outpatient prescription doses. The observation period was truncated at 1 year or discontinuation of the first DMARD received.

Our primary outcome was occurrence of a hospitalised infection within 1 year of starting first DMARD for cSLE. Infections occurring during the initial hospitalisation for cSLE diagnosis were excluded. Hospitalised infections were defined by ICD-9 and ICD-10 codes for infection using PHIS data. As previously published,2 15 a list of codes for serious infection was generated via literature review and based on clinical experience, and then reviewed to remove any codes that could overlap with non-infectious SLE manifestations (ie, pyuria, fever), were unlikely to be severe enough to be the reason for hospitalisation or described postinfectious complications rather than an acute infection. For infections classified as bacterial, receiving an antibiotic during the same hospital encounter as recorded in PHIS was also required.

Covariate assessment

Cohort demographics were extracted from structured data fields in the EHR. Manual chart review was used to collect diagnostic and clinical characteristics including: (1) Date of physician cSLE diagnosis, (2) confirmation of meeting ACR/EULAR SLE criteria, (3) retrospectively tabulated SLE Disease Activity Index 2000 (SLEDAI-2K) score,16 (4) LN status and date of diagnosis, (5) renal biopsy class, (6) central nervous system (CNS) involvement and date of diagnosis and (7) daily oral corticosteroid dose. To account for oral prednisone tapering, clinic chart notes, inpatient notes and phone communication notes or patient messages were reviewed for changes in prednisone dose recommended by their physician and reportedly taken. Where available, patient-reported discontinuation or taper was used to calculate dose received rather than dose recommended. A weighted average of oral prednisone-equivalent dose was calculated for each 8-week period starting from date of first DMARD initiation and modelled as a time-varying covariate. Intravenous methylprednisolone was characterised as a ‘pulse dose’ if 500 mg/dose or greater. Data on pulse dose methylprednisolone and rituximab received during inpatient hospitalisation or in infusion centres was extracted from EHR pharmacy data. Pulse dose methylprednisolone and rituximab were modelled as binary, saturating time-varying covariates, with anyone who received one or more doses considered to be exposed at all subsequent time points.

Statistical analysis

Summary statistics were performed for youth with cSLE with and without LN at the time of first DMARD initiation, and for those with no infection and ≥1 hospitalised infection. Frequency of hospitalisation with infection was calculated over the first year of treatment for youth who received each DMARD, stratified by LN status. Timing of first hospitalised infection was compared between those with and without LN.

Among those with LN at time of first DMARD initiation, the rate of infection in youth exposed to cyclophosphamide versus mycophenolate was compared using Cox proportional hazard regression, adjusting for concomitant rituximab exposure, intravenous pulse dose methylprednisolone and oral corticosteroid dose. In those without LN, infection rates were compared between azathioprine, mycophenolate and methotrexate using Cox proportional hazard regression with the same adjustment for coadministered medications.

Inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) was used in all regression modelling to account for confounding by indication. We constructed propensity score models to estimate the probability of receiving the first DMARD based on clinical and demographic characteristics at time of diagnosis. Separate models were developed for youth with and without LN. Covariates included age at cSLE diagnosis, year of lupus diagnosis, SLEDAI-2K score, CNS involvement, race, ethnicity, insurance type and for those with LN, renal biopsy classification (Class III, IV and/or V for LN models, with each class as a separate binary variable). For youth with LN, logistic regression was used to model the probability of receiving cyclophosphamide versus mycophenolate (reference). For youth without LN, a multinomial regression model was constructed to estimate the probabilities of receiving methotrexate or mycophenolate versus azathioprine (reference). R V.4.2.2 was used for statistical analysis and p values below 0.05 were considered to be significant.

The most commonly prescribed DMARDs were mycophenolate mofetil (42%), azathioprine (23%), cyclophosphamide (16%) and methotrexate (15%). A total of 98 youth stopped their first DMARD within 1 year of start date, at a median (IQR) of 88 (44–175) days. Frequency of hospitalised infections

12 youths (5%) had one or more hospitalised infections within the first year of DMARD initiation. The rate of hospitalised infection was higher in those with LN (8%), compared with 2.5% of those without LN. Characteristics of youth with hospitalised infections

Youth with cSLE who had one or more hospitalised infections differed demographically and in disease characteristics and treatment (table 3). They tended to be younger at diagnosis (median age 11.5 vs 14), were less likely to be white and were more likely to be publicly insured. Youth with hospitalised infections also had numerically higher rates of LN (67% vs 38%) and CNS lupus (17% vs 4%) and higher median SLEDAI-2K scores (17 vs 12). They also had greater corticosteroid exposure, with median oral daily prednisone dose over the first 8 weeks of treatment of 39 mg vs 30 mg in the group with no hospitalised infections and were more likely to have received pulse dose methylprednisolone (42% vs 23%).

Discussion

Our study demonstrated higher rates of hospitalised infection among youth with cSLE who received cyclophosphamide for initial treatment of LN compared with those who received mycophenolate. This difference persisted after propensity score adjustment for demographic and clinical characteristics at time of diagnosis, as well as adjustment for coadministered medications including adjunctive biologics, intravenous pulse dose steroids and oral prednisone dose. Among youth with cSLE who did not have LN, infection rates were equivalent between those who received mycophenolate and azathioprine, and no infections were observed in youth who received methotrexate. Higher oral prednisone dose was significantly associated with higher risk of hospitalised infection.

We observed higher hospitalised infection rates in youth with cSLE and LN compared with those without LN. This finding was mostly driven by high infection rates among those who received cyclophosphamide, with similar overall infection rates observed between those who received mycophenolate whether or not they had LN. While comparative infectious risk studies are lacking in cSLE, our finding is consistent with prior series reporting very high rates of infection among children with cSLE who received cyclophosphamide.17 18 Our finding of higher hospitalised infection risk with cyclophosphamide compared with mycophenolate may support the preferential use of mycophenolate over cyclophosphamide for treatment of cSLE complicated by LN, given evidence from several adult trials showing similar efficacy in renal response with mycophenolate and cyclophosphamide1921 and a small retrospective study in children supporting equivalent or better renal response with mycophenolate,22 in addition to the risk of premature gonadal failure with cyclophosphamide.23 24

While few studies have directly compared infection risk between cyclophosphamide and mycophenolate, particularly with detailed risk adjustment to account for confounding by indication, one large, propensity-score adjusted study of adults with severe LN using a Medicaid cohort showed equivalent risk of serious infection between cyclophosphamide and mycophenolate.25 It is possible that our findings differed because children and young adults are particularly susceptible to the leucodepleting effects of cyclophosphamide and other concomitantly administered medications, as a prior study of children with autoimmune disease has demonstrated an association of younger age at time of treatment with hypogammaglobinaemia and infection.26

Another possible explanation, though not directly assessed in our study, is the more frequent use of higher-dose cyclophosphamide regimens in children compared with adults with SLE,27 which may lead to greater leucodepletion and susceptibility to infection. Paediatric practice has been slower to shift from older, high-dose regimens such as the National Institutes of Health (NIH) clinical trial dosing28 to lower fixed-dosing strategies such as the EuroLupus regimen,29 which is now recommended for most people with SLE by both EULAR and ACR guidelines.12 30 Recent retrospective data showing equivalent renal response among youth with LN treated with NIH and EuroLupus dosing may support broader use of lower-dose regimens in the paediatric LN population.31 Finally, the study showing equivalent infection risk with mycophenolate and cyclophosphamide included only Medicaid enrollees, while our study included all payor types and was more socioeconomically diverse. Poverty and race are known to be associated with more severe lupus in both adults and children.3234 It is possible that despite adjusting for demographic characteristics, there was unmeasured confounding by demographic factors associated with both cyclophosphamide use and infectious adverse effects. Adherence concerns are important for physician decision-making and may be influenced by real or perceived social barriers to care3537 and may factor into decisions to give intravenous cyclophosphamide over oral mycophenolate.

While rituximab was not significantly associated with increased risk of hospitalised infection among youth with LN in our study, we did observe a numerically higher HR for hospitalised infection in those who received rituximab. It is possible that the failure to find an association was due to our analysis being underpowered and that such a relationship would be significant in larger cohorts. Our prior analysis of rituximab use for cSLE in the PHIS data set15 found an incidence of hospitalised infection within the first year of treatment of 14%,15 higher than the overall rate (5%) and the rate among youth with LN (8%) in our study. Concomitant use of B cell depleting biologics is significantly more common in cSLE than adult SLE, where they are the most frequently used adjunctive therapy for LN.13 14 Therefore, the infection risk of B cell depleting agents in combination with other conventional DMARDs for cSLE and LN warrants further research.

Our finding of higher risk of hospitalised infection with higher dose oral corticosteroid dose adds to the large body of literature implicating higher doses of corticosteroids in infection risk.3840 Notably, many youth in our cohort with non-renal lupus received high oral prednisone doses, with a mean dose over the first 8 weeks after DMARD initiation of 20 mg/day and a quarter of the cohort receiving >38 mg/daily. These doses substantially exceed current adult recommendations for steroid use in SLE without LN12 and reflect the use of higher and variable dosing of corticosteroids in paediatric rheumatology practice.13 41 42 Notably, there is a lack of evidence for steroid dosing in both LN and lupus without LN in children, which may contribute to variability in steroid dosing. Additionally, children may have more frequent haematological and CNS manifestations driving higher steroid dosing compared with adult cohorts, and there is a dearth of evidence for steroid dosing for these organ manifestations. However, given the well-established body of evidence showing harms of higher steroid doses and cumulative steroid exposure for both adults and children,4346 our study supports efforts to limit oral steroid exposure even as additional studies to optimise steroid taper schedules and updated guidelines are in progress. While we did not find a similar association of oral corticosteroid dose with infection among those with LN, those with LN were more likely to also receive intravenous pulse dose steroids of variable duration. It is possible that an inverse relationship between pulse dose steroid and oral dose complicated assessment of the infectious risk of corticosteroid exposure. Future studies with larger cohorts may allow for more nuanced modelling of the trade-offs of intravenous pulse dose methylprednisolone versus oral prednisone for children with cSLE.

Strengths of this study include the manual collection of highly detailed treatment information, including actual oral prednisone dose received over time. The use of IPTW to minimise confounding by indication addressed the frequent limitation of non-randomised studies in that youth with different disease severity receive different treatments. Our use of real-world data from all youth treated at two centres may represent a more generalisable rate of infection outcomes among this cSLE population compared with more highly controlled clinical trial settings.

This study also had several limitations. Although our two-centre study included a large number of youth with cSLE given the rarity of this disease, the limited sample size, small number of youth in each DMARD subgroup and low overall rates of hospitalised infection events limited our ability to detect smaller effects and make precise estimates of risk. The small number of hospitalised infection outcomes may have contributed to imprecision in the estimated HRs comparing DMARD groups. In particular, the low number of youth receiving methotrexate and lack of any events may underestimate risk in this population. It is possible that our results may not be generalisable to all practice settings, as data came from two quaternary care centres with large paediatric rheumatology programmes. In order to define infections in a uniform manner and focus on the most serious adverse events, we included only hospitalised infections. However, milder infections treated by primary care physicians, or at urgent care or even emergency departments not requiring admission, may also be highly disruptive and impact quality of life and would reasonably impact treatment choices, but these events were not addressed in our study. Finally, it is possible that ascertainment bias impacted our study if some hospitalised infection events occurred outside of PHIS hospitals and were not captured as outcomes.

Future studies of infection risk in children with cSLE should incorporate larger cohorts from multiple centres to better isolate the risks of individual immunosuppressants and corticosteroid dosing strategies. Larger cohorts will be especially important as multidrug regimens including upfront adjunctive therapies become more common for treatment of LN and other severe end-organ manifestations. While prospectively collected data and randomised studies offer advantages, attention to inclusion of diverse populations, including those of different racial, ethnic, socioeconomic backgrounds and those treated in different care settings, will be essential to ensure generalisability of findings. Finally, the relatively high rates of infection seen in our study, though lower than published elsewhere, also reinforce the importance of preventative care including vaccination to lower risk.47 48 Research priorities in cSLE preventative care may include assessment of preventive strategies such as prophylactic antibiotics and risk-stratification to better understand which children and youth with cSLE are at highest risk in order to guide individualised treatment.

In summary, we found that cyclophosphamide was associated with higher hospitalised infection risk compared with mycophenolate among youth with LN, which persisted after adjusting for baseline differences between groups, while oral corticosteroid dose contributed significantly to hospitalised infection risk regardless of DMARD choice in those without nephritis. While further research in larger cohorts is needed to replicate the findings in our study, our findings support changes in recent guidelines to emphasise steroid-sparing regimens, particularly in those without serious end-organ involvement, and to favour mycophenolate over cyclophosphamide when possible to mitigate infection risks in the high-risk population of youth with cSLE, particularly those with LN.
