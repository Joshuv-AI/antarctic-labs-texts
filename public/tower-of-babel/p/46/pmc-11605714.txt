Association between dental diseases and stroke
Authors: Haocheng Zhang, Qian Yu, Caidi Ying, Yibo Liu, Xiaoyu Wang, Yinghan Guo, Liang Xu, Yuanjian Fang, Xiaoyue Liao, Sheng Chen
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThis study aimed to analyze the existing evidence on the association between dental diseases and stroke. Various pathogenic mechanisms, including bacteraemia and systemic inflammation, were investigated to explore their impact on stroke occurrence and severity.MethodsA comprehensive literature search was conducted through the online databases PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Scopus. The initial screening process resulted in a total of 138 articles being gathered. Upon subsequent refinement, 52 articles were discarded due to duplication or non-English language, leaving 86 articles for further assessment.ResultsOral bacteria can travel to the brain’s blood vessels and cause bacteraemia, leading to local inflammation and the release of toxins. This process can result in the blockage or rupture of blood vessels, leading to stroke. Oral infections consistently trigger immune responses and systemic inflammation, with inflammation-related proteins playing a crucial role in stroke development. Notably, periodontal treatment could reduce the risk of stroke.ConclusionThis review emphasizes the role of dental diseases in promoting stroke occurrence through various mechanisms. Dental diseases contribute to the development of stroke by facilitating bacteraemia and systemic inflammation. Furthermore, through effectively treating and preventing dental diseases, the risk of strokes can be significantly reduced.Keywords: Dental diseases, Stroke, Periodontal treatment, Bacteraemia

Introduction

Dental diseases encompass a variety of conditions that impact the teeth, gums, and supporting structures, such as caries, gingivitis, periodontitis, oral cancer, and malocclusion (Benzian et al., 2022). These conditions can cause significant discomfort and potential complications if left untreated. Tooth loss, the ultimate consequence of dental diseases, signifies the absence of one or more teeth from the dental arch. Tooth loss is the result of various factors, with some of the most significant contributors being severe periodontitis, severe apical disease, vertical root fracture, trauma, and tumors (Peres et al., 2019). Among the factors mentioned above, dental caries and periodontal disease are considered relatively important drivers of tooth loss (Haworth et al., 2018). A global epidemiological study revealed that both dental caries and periodontitis exhibited high prevalence across all age groups (Frencken et al., 2017).

The impact of poor dental health on systemic diseases is well documented, prompting renewed interest in understanding the significance of dental diseases for overall systemic health. Various case-control and cohort studies have indicated an association between periodontal disease and the comprehensive cancer risk, highlighting the pivotal role of systemic inflammation in the biological plausibility of this connection (Michaud et al., 2017). Additionally, interactions between dental diseases and other systemic conditions like osteoporosis, cardiovascular disease (CVD), and diabetes have been reported (Nazir et al., 2018). Oral infections often lead to chronic inflammations connected to CVD through several proposed pathogenic mechanisms (Aarabi et al., 2018).

As the world’s population increases and ages, stroke has become the second significant contributor to death and a primary cause of disability globally (Katan and Luft 2018). The impact of dental diseases on atherosclerosis suggests dental diseases have a potential association with stroke occurrence (Cheng et al., 2018). Indeed, a recent study has established a connection between dental diseases and ischemic stroke (Guo et al., 2022). A number of epidemiologic studies explored the links between these conditions (Diouf et al., 2015, Alhadainy et al., 2021), with evidence suggesting that dental diseases, including periodontitis, dental caries, and tooth loss, increase the risk of stroke (Sanz et al., 2020). Meta-analyses have revealed a correlation between tooth loss and a 3 % elevation in the risk of suffering from a stroke per two missing teeth (Lafon et al., 2014). Moreover, a cohort study indicated that individuals with periodontitis are at a heightened risk of ischemic stroke (Lin et al., 2019).

Based on existing research, there seems to be a certain link between dental diseases and stroke. In this review, we aim to elucidate the relationship between dental diseases and stroke, shedding light on the potential connections and implications for public health.

Materials and methods

Literature searches were proceeded based on the databases PubMed, Google Scholar, Web of Science, and Scopus utilizing the following keywords: “stroke” “dental diseases” OR “periodontal disease,” OR “dental caries” OR “periodontal treatment”. The initial search yielded 138 articles. Fifty-two articles were excluded after filtering out duplicates and non-English articles. The remaining 86 articles were evaluated for their eligibility.

Results

The effect of bacteraemia

Existence of periodontal pathogens in carotid plaques

Increasing evidence suggests that oral pathogens can enter the host’s bloodstream and cause bacteraemia. Among the bacteria most commonly encountered were Viridans streptococci, Aggregatibacter actinomycetemcomitans, Porphyromonas gingivalis, Micromonas micros and Actinomyces species (Marín et al., 2016). In addition, dental procedure could facilitate bacteraemia caused by oral pathogens. One study indicated that routine dental procedures could place a patient at risk of the development of bacteraemia (Livingston and Perez-Colon 2014). Multiple investigations have indicated that bacteremia occurs with greater frequency directly following various preventive and therapeutic oral procedures (Reyes et al., 2013).

Significant associations have been reported between tooth loss, severe periodontal disease, extensive gingivitis and atherosclerotic stenosis (Sen et al., 2024). Periodontal pathogens, such as Porphyromonas gingivalis, have been identified in highly calcified atherothrombotic human plaques using nested polymerase chain reaction (Brun et al., 2020). Recently, researchers investigated the presence of peri-pathogens in atheromatous plaques extracted from carotid arteries of patients with stroke (Pavlic et al., 2021). A recent comprehensive meta-analysis, encompassing 63 studies and 1791 patients, has validated the presence of 23 distinct oral bacterial species within atherosclerotic plaque samples (Chhibber-Goel et al., 2016).

Given the ability of dental bacteria to enter the bloodstream and the discovery of dental pathogens in atherosclerotic plaques, we believe that dental bacteria can promote the occurrence stroke by causing bacteraemia. Furthermore, bacteraemia of oral pathogens can induce the rupture of carotid plaques (Engelmann and Massberg 2013). Increased intraplaque hemorrhage volume has been correlated with higher levels of bacteraemia (Brun et al., 2021). These findings highlight the potential implications of oral bacterial species in stroke pathogenesis and plaque destabilisation. Next, we try to present a comprehensive overview of the potential mechanisms linking bacteraemia caused by dental bacteria to stroke.

Mechanisms of Streptococcus mutans-induced cerebral hemorrhage

Dental caries represents a chronic condition marked by the demineralization of tooth structures, ultimately resulting in the development of cavities. And the associated complications of which have the potential to worsen or trigger systemic diseases, such as stroke (Cheng et al., 2022). A significant reason why dental caries can lead to stroke is due to bacterial infections. These bacteria can enter the bloodstream and spread throughout the body via bacteraemia (Bordagaray et al., 2021). Streptococcus mutans (S.mutans), through its carbohydrate metabolism, is widely acknowledged as the primary cariogenic pathogen responsible for human dental caries (Lin et al., 2021), leading to the excessive acidification of the oral environment (Abranches et al., 2018). In addition, S. mutans can result in bacteraemia, which can contribute to the development of systemic conditions such as stroke (Chamat-Hedemand et al., 2020).

S. mutans-induced bacteraemia has been reported as a possible contributing factor to development of intracerebral hemorrhage (LaValley et al., 2024). Notably, certain collagen binding proteins (CBPs), such as the Cnm protein, have been implicated in inhibiting platelet aggregation and causing bleeding (Watanabe et al., 2016). Furthermore, transverse investigations have demonstrated a correlation between oral infection caused by Cnm-positive S. mutans and an elevated incidence of cerebral microbleeds. A cohort study compared patients harboring Cnm-positive S. mutans to those without, revealing a significantly higher occurrence of cerebral microbleeds, particularly in deeper region (Tonomura et al., 2016). S. mutans can disrupt blood vessels barriers through the activation of matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP9) (Gursoy-Ozdemir et al., 2004). Studies have confirmed that S. mutans can induce larger hemorrhage areas in mouse models compared to control groups, which might be attributed to the higher activity of surface CBPs in these strains (Nakano et al., 2011). The binding ability of the Cnm protein to constituents of the vascular basement membrane (BM), for instance, collagen-IV and laminin, plays a critical role in this process (Sato et al., 2004). The CBP selectively binds to the diseased tissue rather than soft tissue, such as vessel walls. When Cnm-positive S. mutans adheres to the BM, it can provoke the influx of neutrophils, initiating localized inflammation. This, in turn, may heighten the permeability of the blood–brain barrier and stimulate the production of enzymes, ultimately causing intracerebral hemorrhage (Nomura et al., 2013) (Fig. 1). Overall, S. mutans appears to increase the incidence of hemorrhagic stroke, and targeting this pathogen could represent a novel therapeutic approach for stroke management.

The mechanism of cerebral hemorrhage due to bacteraemia caused by Cnm-positive Streptococcus mutans (S.mutans).Cnm-positive S. mutans can enter the bloodstream and can subsequently attach to the exposed basement membrane (BM) of cerebral blood vessels. Once the Cnm-positive S. mutans adhere to the BM, they could potentially trigger neutrophil activation, resulting in the onset of local inflammation. This inflammation would then lead to an increase in blood–brain barrier permeability and the secretion of enzymes, ultimately leading to cerebral hemorrhage. Furthermore, Cnm-positive S. mutans possess the capability to disrupt the normal clotting process by inhibiting collagen-induced platelet aggregation, which could eventually lead to uncontrolled bleeding and potentially cause cerebral hemorrhage. Systemic inflammation and immunity

Oral infections, such as gingivitis, periodontitis, and endodontic lesions, consistently elicit immune responses and systemic inflammation. Among these diseases, chronic periodontitis is considered as a major disease. This disease affects a significant portion of the global population, estimated to be between 20 and 50 percent (Nazir 2017). Periodontitis is associated with increased systemic inflammation markers, which have been implicated in the etiology of stroke (Romagna et al., 2012). For example, periodontitis has been linked to elevated levels of C-reactive protein (CRP), an increase in white blood cell counts, albumin, and heightened concentrations of interleukin 6 and other acute-phase reactants (Beck and Offenbacher, 2005, Elkind and Cole, 2006, You et al., 2009). A study showed that nod-like receptor protein 3 and interleukin-1β expressions were significantly upregulated in mice with experimental periodontitis-induced ischemic stroke (Chi et al., 2019). Given the abundant blood supply to periodontal tissue, the chronic inflammatory molecules increased by periodontitis have the potential to spread through the bloodstream to the brain, therefore playing a role in stroke pathogenesis (Stewart and West 2016). This suggests that chronic periodontitis can directly contribute to stroke by activating multiple signal pathways (Brun et al., 2016).

Studies have shown that a higher burden of oral infection is separately correlated with increased levels of carotid atherosclerotic burden in stroke patients (Leão et al., 2022). Several epidemiological studies have also demonstrated the relationship between oral infection and stroke (Sfyroeras et al., 2012, Söder et al., 2015). A systemic review investigating the link between dental diseases and chronic conditions indicated that the most frequently reported correlations were found between periodontitis and CVD (Seitz et al., 2019). Another cross-sectional analysis concluded that periodontitis was associated with CVD and may be a risk factor for it (Byun et al., 2020). In this section, we will examine the inflammation-related protein activated by oral infection, which plays an essential role in stroke (Fig. 2).

The mechanisms linking dental disease-induced elevation of inflammation-related proteins to stroke. The translocation of subgingival plaque bacteria into the bloodstream triggers an immune response, leading to the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-1 (IL-1), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α). These cytokines contribute to systemic inflammation and stimulate the production of C-reactive protein (CRP) by the liver. CRP binds to oxidized low-density lipoproteins, promoting foam cell formation and the development of atherosclerotic plaques. CRP also activates immune cells and exacerbates inflammatory reactions, leading to ischemic injury. Additionally, CRP is associated with endothelial dysfunction by reducing nitric oxide (NO) synthesis. IL-1, IL-6, and TNF-α increase the secretion of soluble ST2 (sST2) in endothelial cells. Elevated sST2 acts as a decoy receptor for interleukin-33 (IL-33), impairing the beneficial effects of IL-33 on tissue preservation, cell survival, and cytokine secretion. This resulted in the inhibition of the interaction between IL and 33 with the ST2 gene-like (ST2L) membrane receptor, further increasing the risk of stroke. Potential role of CRP (Fig. 2, upper panel)

CRP is an acute phase protein known for its non-specific and high sensitivity in detecting various forms of body injury. Oral infections have been consistently linked to elevated systemic levels of CRP (Gomes-Filho et al., 2011). A research investigation affirmed that individuals exhibiting variations in CRP levels exhibited a greater incidence of periodontitis compared to those with normal CRP levels (Esteves-Lima et al., 2020). In the context of chronic periodontitis, rats subjected to periodontal interventions showed an increase in high-sensitivity CRP (hs-CRP) levels over time, followed by a decrease after the second intervention (Wang et al., 2019). Furthermore, CRP has been identified as a notable predisposing factor for numerous systemic ailments, including stroke (Bansal et al., 2014). Elevated CRP levels during the very early phase stroke have been linked to poor clinical outcomes and prognosis (Mazaheri et al., 2018). Moreover, higher levels of CRP are correlated with greater depressive symptoms following a stroke (Kowalska et al., 2020).

A previous study revealed that periodontitis is a common finding among patients with acute stroke, and elevated serum hs-CRP levels can serve as a strong indicator of systemic inflammation, as nearly all stroke patients with elevated serum hs-CRP had periodontitis (Tuominen et al., 2020). In addition, another cross-sectional study demonstrated that the periodontal profile classification is linked to both stroke occurrences and systemic indicators of CRP. Specifically, the severe periodontal profile class was significantly linked to prevalent stroke, while individuals classified as having high oral infection were significantly more likely to have elevated serum CRP levels (Beck et al., 2018). It is worth noting that hs-CRP not only acts as a biomarker of inflammation but also serves as a serum biomarker for the stability of atherosclerotic plaque (Pearson et al., 2003). Given this context, oral infection may promote stroke by potentially playing a crucial role in the stability of atherosclerotic plaque through its impact on hs-CRP levels (Chen et al., 2021).

Potential role of soluble suppression of tumorigenicity-2

Experimental studies offered compelling evidence in favor of the view that the expression of soluble suppression of tumorigenicity-2 protein (sST2) is triggered when the heart muscle experiences stress and inflammation (Mildner et al., 2010). Moreover, scientific studies have also reported that excessive sST2 expression is also induced in cases of chronic local infection, particularly periodontitis (Torrungruang et al., 2019). Some studies have provided explanations for the reason behind the increase of sST2 due to periodontal disease. Gram-negative bacteria which forms a considerable proportion of the subgingival plaque, causes a transient rise in interleukin-6 and tumor necrosis factor-alpha levels (Mildner et al., 2010, Wang et al., 2013). This initial increase in cytokines is then followed by a notable surge in serum sST2 concentrations. Several studies have explored the association between periodontitis and sST2, indicating that periodontitis is linked to increased serum levels of sST2 (Torrungruang et al., 2019). Additionally, another clinical study assessed serum levels of Interleukin 33 (IL-33) and sST2 in adult patients with and without periodontal disease, demonstrating a significant difference in sST2 protein levels between healthy volunteers and patients with periodontal diseases (Singh et al., 2022).

sST2 has been extensively investigated in recent years as a biomarker with clinical significance. Circulating sST2 concentrations have been found to correlate with worse disease phenotypes, including unfavorable changes in tissue structure and fibrosis, cardiac malfunction, compromised hemodynamic flow, and an elevated likelihood of disease advancement (Pascual-Figal et al., 2016). Notably, sST2 stands as a clinically significant biomarker, mirroring the pathophysiological alterations across a range of cardiovascular diseases,including stroke (Lu et al., 2021). Understanding the associations between periodontitis and sST2 protein levels could provide valuable insights into the pathophysiological mechanisms that link periodontitis to stroke, warranting a review on this specific mechanism.

IL-33, a cytokine of the IL-1 superfamily, upon release, functions in an autocrine or paracrine fashion to stimulate the ST2 gene-like (ST2L) membrane receptor on neighboring cells. This interaction facilitates cardiac healing, mitigates detrimental cardiovascular remodeling, and impedes the progression of atherosclerotic plaques (Altara et al., 2018). Therefore, the IL-33/ST2L axis plays a vital role in stroke recovery. Studies in ST2 knockout mice have reported reduced the integrity of white matter and the compromised long-term neurological functions post-stroke are accompanied by augmented oligodendrocyte and their precursor cell mortality (Xie et al., 2021). Engagement of IL-33/ST2L stimulates the production of IL-10 from microglia and enhances neuronal survival after stroke (Yang et al., 2017).

However, sST2, which increases after periodontitis, acts as a decoy receptor for IL‐33, thus preventing IL‐33 from binding to ST2L. Consequently, when sST2 levels are elevated, the cardioprotective effect of IL‐33 is abrogated (Fig. 2, lower panel). Clinical studies have independently associated sST2 with poor outcomes and mortality after stroke (Wolcott et al., 2017). Additionally, prospective observational studies have linked elevated plasma sST2 levels to post-stroke cognitive impairment (Zhu et al., 2021). In conclusion, the connection between periodontitis and stroke may be attributed to the increased secretion of sST2 and its inhibitory effects on the IL-33/ST2L axis.

Potential role of antibody titres

A recent review has highlighted that patients with dental diseases express a number of antibodies that can trigger systemic inflammatory responses (Schenkein and Loos 2013). Notably, increased serum antibody titres against Fusobacterium nucleatum, a bacterium commonly found in dental plaque, have been associated with poor outcomes after stroke (Nishi et al., 2020). Specific pathogen related to oral infection have been linked to the incidence of stroke, as serum IgG antibody titres against several oral pathogens have shown associations with cerebral small-vessel disease in patients with acute stroke. For example, a clinical study found high serum IgG antibody titres against Campylobacter rectus in acute stroke patients with cerebral microbleeds (Shiga et al., 2020). The presence of systemic periodontal pathogens seems to influence stroke patients, as increased serum IgG levels of A. actinomycetemcomitans are linked to hemorrhage expansion, while those of F. nucleatum are predictive of an unfavorable outcome in stroke patients (Nakamori et al., 2020).

One reason these antibodies generated by dental diseases can influence systemic inflammatory responses and stroke progress is their ability to react with non-bacterial antigens in cerebrovascular diseases. A significant peptide commonality between dental and human cardiovascular autoantigens suggests that antibodies against dental antigens may react with non-bacterial antigens in cerebrovascular pathology (Lucchese 2017). Human heat-shock proteins are among the non-bacterial antigens targeted. Pathogens from dental diseases expressing heat-shock proteins, such as F. nucleatum, can induce antibodies reactive with human heat-shock proteins, leading to enhanced inflammation (Lee et al., 2012, Rizzo et al., 2012). Natural antibodies, such as P. gingivalis-derived natural IgM, may also contribute to related inflammatory diseases by reacting with malondialdehyde modified LDL and inducing inflammatory responses (Turunen et al., 2012). Notably, serum concentrations of anti-cardiolipin antibodies are elevated in 15–20 % of patients with periodontitis. Excessive anti cardiolipin antibodies can induce anti phospholipid syndrome, which increases the risk of stroke (Gunupati et al., 2011). Additionally, anti-apolipoprotein A-1 IgG, a potential marker of cerebrovascular diseases, is more prevalent in patients with periodontitis in comparison to age- and sex-matched controls (Wick et al., 2013). In conclusion, it is crucial to recognize that antibodies against oral pathogens may contribute to stroke onset by promoting systemic inflammation.

Treatment of dental diseases

Periodontal treatment encompasses various interventions, such as eliminating causative factors, mechanical cleaning of the teeth's supragingival and subgingival surfaces and performing periodontal surgery (Orlandi et al., 2020). Maintaining periodontal health through periodontal treatment can effectively reduce the occurrence of stroke (Lee et al., 2013). The available evidence suggests that periodontal therapy can impact cerebrovascular events by reducing multiple risk factors, such as inflammation, blood pressure, and carotid thickness (Orlandi et al., 2020). A recent study demonstrated that periodontal treatment is particularly effective in minimizing the likelihood of recurrent vascular episodes among patients with ischemic stroke is a crucial aspect of management and transient ischemic attack survivors compared to standard periodontal care (Redd et al., 2019). However, evidence from randomised controlled trials has indicated that intensive periodontal treatment was not superior to standard treatment in prevention of stroke (Tonetti et al., 2007, Sen et al., 2023). Observations suggest that invasive dental treatments can temporarily disrupt blood flow, which might momentarily elevate the risk of stroke (Choi et al., 2020).

In summary, the type of periodontal treatment provided can have varying effects on the risk of stroke. Following integrative dental care, the risk of stroke was reduced in patients. Discussion

This review has emphasized the significant role that dental diseases play in exacerbating the incidence of stroke. The presented findings strongly support the argument that dental diseases are likely to impact multiple pathways involved in stroke.

Bacteraemia and systemic inflammation are notably important in this context. The dissemination of bacteria and bacterial toxins via bacteraemia can contribute to the formation of cerebral emboli and vascular inflammation. Additionally, the systemic inflammation associated with dental diseases can promote cytokine release and endothelial dysfunction, further increasing the risk of stroke.

Several clinical trials have provided compelling evidence that effective treatment and prevention of dental diseases could serve as a preventive measure against stroke. By addressing and managing periodontal treatment, such as through professional cleanings, scaling and root planing, can help reduce multiple cerebrovascular factors.

Nevertheless, further research is needed to ascertain the involvement of viruses and fungi in the association between dental diseases and stroke. Overall, addressing dental diseases and implementing appropriate oral healthcare measures are crucial in the comprehensive management and prevention of stroke. By recognizing the interplay between dental health and stroke, healthcare professionals can take proactive steps to improve oral health outcomes and reduce the burden of stroke in affected individuals.
