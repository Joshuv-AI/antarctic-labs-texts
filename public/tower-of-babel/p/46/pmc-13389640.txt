Analysis of Age, Period and Cohort Trends in Cervical In Situ and Invasive Carcinomas for Assessing the Utility of Screening Approaches—Results From the Saarland Cancer Registry
Authors: Natalie Rath, Klaus Neis, Bernd Holleczek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundCervical cancer continues to pose a significant public health burden. In Germany, prevention relies heavily on regular screening and HPV vaccination.ObjectiveTo analyze trends in cervical cancer incidence over a 50‐year period in Saarland, Germany, with a focus on cohort effects and the impact of screening programs.MethodsData from the Saarland Cancer Registry were evaluated for the period 1970–2023. Trends in cervical carcinoma (ICD‐10: C53, ICD‐9: 180) and carcinoma in situ (ICD‐10: D06, ICD‐9: 233.1) were analyzed, stratified by age and birth cohort. Additionally, model‐based age‐period‐cohort analyses were derived. The D06/C53 ratio was calculated by age group and calendar period.ResultsSince the introduction of Pap smear screening in 1971, the age‐standardized incidence of cervical cancer declined by 74%. Subsequently, sharp decreases were observed in women born in 1933 or earlier. Women who grew into screening never reached the high pre‐screening rates. Concurrently, in situ carcinoma incidence rose more than tenfold, with detection concentrated in women aged 30–39, indicating a shift toward earlier detection. The D06/C53 ratio increased across all age groups, most strongly in women ≤ 39 years. Overall mortality fell by 71%, although no significant further decline was observed over the last two to three decades.ConclusionThis analysis underscores the substantial impact of cervical cancer screening in reducing both incidence and mortality, particularly among women of screening age. The increasing D06/C53 ratios point to a shift toward earlier detection and treatment of lesions, although cancer registry‐based data cannot fully distinguish prevented progression from overdiagnosis. While the effects and benefits of screening are already well established, the first effects of HPV vaccination are only beginning to emerge. To build on these achievements, sustained efforts to enhance both screening uptake and HPV vaccine coverage are imperative.

Introduction

Cervical cancer is the fourth most common cancer in women worldwide, with an estimated 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths in 2022, and remains one of the most common cancers in women of reproductive age [1, 2, 3]. In Germany, it ranked fourth among cancers in women in 2022, with a mortality‐to‐incidence ratio of 52.7% [2]. Nearly all cervical cancers worldwide are attributable to persistent infection with one or more oncogenic HPV types, especially HPV 16 and 18 [4, 5]. Infection with HPV is also responsible for 4% of all new cancer cases worldwide, including those of the head and neck and other anogenital sites [5]. Additional risk factors of cervical neoplasms include oral contraceptive use, high parity, immunosuppression, other genital infections (e.g., chlamydia, herpes simplex), and smoking [6, 7, 8, 9].

Cervical cancer prevention relies on widespread screening and vaccination against HPV. Since 1971, women in Germany aged 20 and older had been offered annual Pap smears [10], and since 2020, co‐testing (HPV and cytology) every three years is provided for women aged 35 and above, while younger women continue with annual cytology [10]. If abnormalities are detected, diagnostic follow‐up is guided by the national algorithm for cervical cancer, which includes differential colposcopy with biopsies of all visible lesions and endocervical curettage when CIN III or adenocarcinoma in situ is suspected [10, 11]. As a measure of primary prevention, HPV vaccination has been available since 2007 for girls (initially ages 12–17, lowered to 9–14 in 2015) and since 2018 also for boys, though uptake remains uneven across regions and demographics [11].

This paper analyzes population‐wide data on in situ and invasive cervical tumors and related mortality over a period of 50 years from the Saarland Cancer Registry. It focuses on the long‐term impact of Pap smear screening and comprehensively examines recent incidence trends, particularly in light of the recent update of the cervical cancer screening that introduced organized co‐testing and was implemented in 2020.

Materials and Methods

The analysis included 12,760 women diagnosed with either carcinoma in situ (n = 8388, ICD‐10: D06, ICD‐9: 233.1) or cervical cancer (n = 4372, ICD‐10: C53, ICD‐9: 180), as well as women who died from cervical cancer (n = 1504) between 1970 and 2023. In situ lesions were included in the analyses if reported and classified as such. Based on the International Classification of Diseases for Oncology (ICD‐O) [12, 13], carcinomata in situ included cervical intraepithelial neoplasia, grade III (CIN III) and high grade lesions of severe dysplasia (2nd edition, until 1999) and squamous intraepithelial neoplasia, grade III including CIN III (3rd edition, since 2000). Low grade lesions are not considered synonymous with carcinoma in situ and thus were not included.

For the analysis of secular trends in incidence and mortality by socio‐demographic and tumor characteristics between 1974 and 2023, cases were categorized by age at diagnosis into five broad groups (≤ 39, 40–49, 50–59, 60–69, and ≥ 70 years) for the tabular and stage‐specific analyses (Table 1). Invasive cancers were further classified by T‐stage according to the 6th to 8th editions of the TNM classification. Additional distinctions were made between cases notified by death certificate only (DCO) and those that were histologically verified (HV). To reduce random variation in secular trends, observations were grouped into five calendar intervals of 10 years each (1974–1983, …, 2014–2023). For the age‐period‐cohort and cohort‐specific analyses described below, five‐year age groups (0–4 to 85+) were used instead.

To analyze the incidence of cervical neoplasms in successive birth cohorts, a Lexis diagram‐like analysis was performed: incidence and mortality rates per 100,000 women per year were calculated by 5‐year age groups (0–4 to 85+) and 5‐year calendar periods (1974–1978 to 2019–2023). Observations by birth cohort were derived from observations within subsequent calendar periods and age groups (cohort = period − age), yielding observations of cohorts of five subsequent birth years spanning central years of birth from 1914 to 1999 (according to the definition of the boundaries of the contributing age groups and calendar periods of observation, the birth years of these cohorts partly overlapped). In the remaining text, the central year of birth is used as a label and referred to when presenting results according to birth cohort.

This approach allowed us to comprehensively examine (i) age‐specific incidence for successive birth cohorts, and (ii) cohort trends in age‐specific rates up to 2023. For clarity, results of these trends shown in Figure 3 are depicted in different colors according to birth cohort to better reflect potential effects of changes in screening in recent years. Rates were age‐standardized using the 1976 European Standard Population [14].

Age, period and birth‐cohort patterns of the incidence of in situ and invasive tumors were additionally displayed as age‐period‐cohort plots following the graphical approach of Bray et al. [15]. Age‐specific incidence rates and rate ratios (RRs) were estimated from Poisson regression models with the logarithm of person‐years as offset, fitted by 5‐year age group, 5‐year calendar period and thus birth cohort. Age effects (shown as incidence rates per 100,000 person‐years) and cohort effects were obtained from an age–cohort model, and period effects from an age–period model (Clayton & Schifflers [16, 17]). To facilitate direct comparison between invasive and in situ cancer, both models used as reference the age of 42 years (midpoint of the five‐year age group containing the pooled median age at diagnosis of both cancer types). As reference years of birth and diagnosis, 1954 and 1996 were chosen, respectively. The reference years only anchor the derived RRs and do not affect the shape of the estimated age, period and cohort trends. In the derived figures, the “age component” represents the age‐specific incidence rates (per 100,000 person‐years in 1996), and the “period” and “cohort” components represent relative changes in terms of RR compared to the calendar year 1996 and the year of birth 1954. For ease of comparison, logarithmic y‐axes were used throughout these plots. Birth cohorts at the margins that did not meet prespecified minimum requirements for stable estimation (at least five age–period cells and 10 cases) were excluded from the model‐based age‐period‐cohort plots.

All analyses were performed with the R software environment for statistical computing and graphics (version 4.0.3) [18].

Results

Overall incidence rates of cervical cancer declined sharply between 1970 and the mid‐1980s, and continued to decrease thereafter across all age groups (Figure 1a,b). Since the introduction of Pap smear screening in 1971, age‐standardized incidence overall dropped by 74%, from 41.1 to 10.7 cases per 100,000 women by 2023. Subsequently, mortality fell by 71%, from 7.6 to 2.2 per 100,000 (Figure 1a).

(b) Age‐specific incidence rates of cervical cancer (15–49, 50–69, and ≥ 70 years). (c) Age‐specific incidence rates of in situ cervical cancer (15–49, 50–69, and ≥ 70 years).

Considering 10‐year calendar periods between 1974 and 2023, the median age at diagnosis of cervical cancer decreased from 58 years in 1974–1983 to 51 years in 2004–2013, before rising again to 55 years in 2014–2023 (Table 1). Since 1984, the proportion of T1 staged tumors (confined to the uterus) remained virtually stable and ranged between 53% and 61%, whereas the proportions of T2 staged tumors (extending beyond the uterus but not reaching the pelvic wall or lower vagina) increased from 21% to 31% and of T3/4 staged tumors (locally advanced) declined from 26% to 16%, respectively (Table 1). In the observed period, the amount of DCO notified cases remained negligible (less than 3%), whereas the proportion of HV cases consistently ranged between 93% and 98%.

In contrast, after an initial decline after introduction of the Pap smear screening, the age‐standardized incidence rate of in situ carcinomas of the cervix started to rise steadily since the mid‐1980s, with a sharp increase observed between 2019 and 2021 (Figure 1a), representing a more than 10‐fold increase from 8.2 to 90.9 between 1970 and 2023.

Considering again 10‐year calendar periods, the median age at first detection remained almost constant over time (Table 1). The diverging trends of incidence of invasive tumors and in situ lesions led to a marked shift in the D06/C53 ratio (Table 1). Among women ≤ 39 years, the ratio rose from 2.42 (1974–1983) to 17.76 (2014–2023). In women aged 50–59, it increased from 0.46 to 2.69, and in those ≥ 70, from 0.12 to 0.47. Figure 2 illustrates the decline in invasive tumors and rise in in situ lesions, highlighting both the changes of risk according to tumor type and age groups (while the overall number of in situ lesions increased threefold, the number of invasive tumors dropped by 50%, Table 1). Age‐specific incidence rates by 5‐year age group were relatively evenly distributed across age groups in 1974–1983. By 1994–2003, in situ carcinoma incidence had shifted toward younger age groups. In 2014–2023, in situ carcinoma incidence peaked among women aged 30–39 years, while invasive carcinoma remained comparatively more prevalent in older age groups.

Following initial decline of age‐specific incidence of in situ lesions after introduction of Pap screening, the incidence of in situ lesions increased gradually in cohorts born from the mid‐1950s onward, with a marked acceleration in cohorts with central year of birth from 1984 onward (Figure 3b) and those reaching age 35 in the most recent calendar period (Figure 3d). Figure 3d further showed that incidence of in situ lesions leveled off in the cohorts of women with central year of birth ≥ 1990 or even started to decline in this part of the population, alongside persistently low rates of cervical cancer (Figure 3c). This rise in in situ incidence was not observed in the youngest age groups (20–24 and 25–29 years), whose central birth years fall in the 1980s or later.

The age‐period‐cohort plots are shown in Figure 4. For cervical cancer, the modeled age‐specific incidence was highest at age 42 (30.8 per 100,000 person‐years for the 1954 reference cohort). The RR by birth‐cohort fell from 2.85 in the 1914 cohort to 0.73 in the 1984 cohort, and the RR by calendar period declined from 1.73 in 1974–1978 to 0.79 in 2019–2023. For in situ carcinoma, the modeled age‐specific incidence was highest at age 37 (53.8 per 100,000 person‐years). In contrast to cervical cancer, the incidence rose markedly by cohort and calendar period: the RR by birth‐cohort increased from 0.63 in the 1934 cohort to 10.85 in the 1999 cohort, and the RR by calendar period increased from 0.77 in 1974–1978 to 4.10 in 2019–2023.

Discussion

This trend study investigated the changes in incidence, mortality and stage distribution of cervical cancer in Germany over the past five decades, which were largely driven by implementation and enhancement of population‐wide offered screening examinations. The analyses of the incidence included both observed trends by age‐group and central year of birth and a modeled age‐period‐cohort analysis deriving relative changes among subsequent birth cohorts and over time. Although HPV is still the causative pathogen, improved test sensitivity—especially in HPV‐based screening with lower specificity but high sensitivity—and optimized screening structures have most likely contributed to the detection and increased identification of precancerous lesions particularly in recent years [19].

Pap smear screening, introduced in the early 1970s, led to a sharp decline in cervical cancer, especially in women born in 1933 or earlier. Women born after 1933, who grew up with access to screening throughout their reproductive years, consistently showed lower age‐specific risks and never reached the high pre‐screening rates of earlier cohorts (Figure 3c).

Over the past 50 years, early detection has led to a marked rise in in situ lesions and a decline in locally advanced tumors (Table 1). Cohort trends in in situ carcinoma showed a continuous increase across successive birth cohorts: rates were lowest in the oldest cohorts and rose gradually before accelerating markedly in the most recent cohorts (born from 1984 onward, Figure 3b), especially at ages 35–44 years (Figure 3d). Across the screened generations, regular participation allowed pre‐invasive lesions to be detected and treated before progression to invasive cancer, which typically develops over about 10 years [20]. In contrast, the incidence rise in women aged 60+ was modest, suggesting overdiagnosis due to lower screening uptake and possible selection effects in older or less‐educated groups [21]. The lower incidence in younger women and higher D06/C53 ratios reflect the impact of screening: more early‐stage detections, fewer advanced tumors (T3/T4).

The implemented changes in the screening with HPV co‐testing in 2020 were followed by a steep increase of detected in situ lesions in the screened female population above the age of 35 years. This suggests that offered co‐testing may be more effective than traditional Pap smear examinations in identifying precancerous lesions. The question is, however, to what extent the early diagnosis and treatment of prevalent in situ lesions might have prevented the progression to invasive tumors, and to what extent precursor lesions have been overdiagnosed [22]. The diverging incidence trends of in situ and invasive lesions raise this issue, as the observations had been made in a population which consists of women with and without usage of screening examinations.

When examining long‐term trends in cervical cancer incidence, factors such as HPV prevalence and lifestyle changes must also be considered [23]. Some studies suggest rising HPV infections, though European data indicate stable prevalence [24]. However, increasing vulvar cancer cases in Germany and the USA since the 1970s/1990s suggest changing infection patterns, with only a proportion of women affected [2, 5, 6, 25, 26]. The so‐called “sexual revolution” of the 1960s–1970s, marked by earlier sexual activity and more partners, may have raised cervical cancer risk in women born since the 1940s [27, 28], which is supported by this analysis. Given HPV's long latency, many cases in these cohorts likely stem from earlier infections [29]. In contrast, younger cohorts benefit from vaccination and improved screening, lowering their long‐term risk [30]. Among women born from 1994 onward, a decline in in situ lesions and consistently low invasive tumor rates was already observed below age 30 (Figure 3d). These cohorts were the first to benefit from HPV vaccination starting in 2007, suggesting an early vaccination effect—although most have not yet reached the ages with historically rising cancer risk.

Up to now, HPV vaccination rates in Germany remain low. Only 54.6% of 15‐year‐old girls and 34% of boys were fully vaccinated by 2023, well below the WHO target of 90% by 2030 and far behind countries with school‐based programs like Australia and Scandinavia [31, 32, 33]. Programs such as SPI:Ke and PERCH aim to improve uptake, but gaps remain [32]. Participation in opportunistic cytology‐based cervical cancer screening in Germany remains moderate, with an estimated annual uptake of approximately 50% and approximately 70% of women participating at least once within a three‐year interval [34]. A model‐based projection for the Saarland population estimated an expected magnitude of a decline of CIN III incidence of about 35% following vaccination and co‐testing combined [35]. Demonstrated effects on disease endpoints have meanwhile emerged from larger vaccinated populations elsewhere. In Sweden, quadrivalent HPV vaccination was associated with a substantially reduced risk of cervical cancer, most pronounced for vaccination before age 17 [36], and a Danish nationwide study found markedly lower cervical cancer incidence in vaccinated women [37]. In Scotland, no cervical cancer has been registered to date in a population in which vaccination with the bivalent vaccine at age 12–13 has been implemented [38]. Recent evidence suggests that a single dose may offer comparable protection to multi‐dose schedules, potentially enabling broader coverage [39]. Smoking, a known risk factor for a large number of cancer diseases, peaked in women born 1930–1934 and 1955–1959 and has since declined, and may contribute to higher incidence of cervical cancer in older cohorts [40].

This study has several strengths and limitations. It draws on high‐quality, long‐term data from the population‐based Saarland Cancer Registry, which, though covering a small population, is considered representative of Germany regarding both incidence and mortality of neoplasms and the provision of cancer care and preventive measures. With data spanning more than half a century, the Saarland Cancer Registry allows monitoring of long‐term trends and generational changes of cancer burden and outcomes.

Based on nearly complete case ascertainment in the past [41, 42, 43], this study therefore could provide robust, unselected estimates of cervical cancer burden in a population with established and recently updated prevention measures. Furthermore, the Saarland Cancer Registry registered in situ precancerous lesions from the outset, whereas the registrations of these lesions started in some other population‐based cancer registries very recently only. The combination of analyses of observed trends of incidence by age‐group and year of birth (Figure 3) and the modeled age‐period‐cohort analysis (Figure 4) allowed detailed insights into trends by age, period, and birth cohort—helping to disentangle the effects of risk factors and prevention strategies that often remain hidden in cross‐sectional analyses. Based on the chosen analysis approach, the study could not yet reveal first observable effects of HPV vaccination, as the cohorts of women with offered vaccination have not yet reached ages with significant morbidity observed in the past. Low proportions of DCO notified cases (≤ 3%), high rates of histological verification (≥ 93%), and pathology‐based data minimized the risk of misclassification. Nevertheless, based on its observational design, this trend study presented results of a female population with varying amounts of usage of screening examinations and recently HPV vaccinations as implemented public health interventions. Therefore rather temporal associations than causal associations could be analyzed.

The early observation of the sharp rise in detected in situ carcinomas since 2020 may highlight the value of population‐based cancer registries in monitoring cancer burden and evaluating public health interventions. In the context of cervical cancer, the timely reporting of the impact of updated screening procedures, including the personal information of eligible women, remains a key challenge. This is particularly pertinent when considered in conjunction with the tracking of the long‐term effects of HPV vaccination [33]. Population‐based cancer registries are key tools to assess the effectiveness of population‐wide offered screening examinations and vaccination programs to reduce HPV‐caused burden of cervical neoplasms in female populations.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study, which combined observed trends with a model‐based age‐period‐cohort analysis, underscores the substantial impact of cervical cancer screening in significantly reducing both incidence of and mortality from invasive tumors—particularly among women of screening age. The increasing D06/C53 ratio and the divergent trends observed both for subsequent cohorts of women and over time point to a decisive shift toward earlier detection and timely treatment of pre‐invasive lesions. Although older women have also benefited, boosting screening participation in this age group remains a critical priority. The marked decline in advanced‐stage tumors further validates the success of early detection strategies. The chosen analysis approach focused trends after implementation of screening but could not yet reveal first observable effects of HPV vaccination in the youngest cohorts, as these women have not yet reached the ages at which cervical cancer morbidity has historically been highest. To preserve and build on these public health achievements, sustained efforts to enhance both screening uptake and HPV vaccine coverage along with collection of population‐wide data are imperative.
