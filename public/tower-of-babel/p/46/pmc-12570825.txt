A panoramic review of transcranial focused ultrasound neuromodulation: from basic research to clinical applications
Authors: Yu Shi, Guiyuan Cai, Wen Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS) is an emerging non-invasive brain stimulation technique that uses acoustic energy to achieve precise modulation of specific brain regions, showing significant potential in the treatment of neurological disorders. Building upon extensive foundational work in clinical safety and mechanistic understanding, this review examines temporal aspects of tFUS mechanisms and clinical applications, with particular attention to the distinction between immediate online effects and sustained offline aftereffects. We examine the mechanisms of action spanning molecular to network levels, regulatory effects on key brain functional systems (sensory, motor, cognitive, and emotional), and evidence for applications in neurological and psychiatric disorders, including neurodegenerative diseases, epilepsy, depression, and anxiety. Studies have demonstrated that tFUS can selectively regulate brain functions by influencing ion channels, synaptic transmission, neural oscillations, and brain networks at multiple levels, with effects showing distinct temporal profiles. Clinical trials provide evidence for potential efficacy and safety within established parameter guidelines. However, current understanding of temporal dynamics, long-term effects, and optimal parameter schemes requires further elucidation. The temporal mechanistic framework examined here may contribute to understanding therapeutic protocol optimization and sustained clinical benefits. Future research priorities include systematic parameter optimization, standardization of protocols across applications, and development of personalized approaches to address individual variability in treatment response.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12984-025-01753-2.

Introduction

The growing needs in neuroscience research and clinical practice have highlighted the importance of developing safe, precise, and reproducible brain modulation techniques for neurological disease treatment [1, 2]. Transcranial focused ultrasound (tFUS), as an emerging non-invasive neuromodulation technology, has gained significant attention due to its unique physical properties and biological effects [3, 4].

Compared to traditional brain stimulation methods such as transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) and transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), tFUS offers superior spatial precision. By utilizing focused acoustic waves, tFUS achieves precise stimulation of targeted brain regions with millimeter-scale resolution (1–5 mm), which significantly surpasses the centimeter-scale resolution of TMS (3–5 cm) and tDCS (5–7 cm) (Fig. 1). Although recent advances in Focused TMS and High-definition tDCS (HD-tDCS) have improved spatial precision (to 1–2 cm and 2–3 cm respectively), they still operate at the centimeter scale [3, 5–11]. A key advantage of tFUS is its ability to penetrate the skull and concentrate energy on deep brain structures, potentially reaching subcortical targets that remain challenging for conventional TMS and tDCS, even though Focused TMS can also target some deeper structures [3, 5–11]. Furthermore, tFUS modulates brain function through both acoustomechanical and thermal effects, which fundamentally differ from the electromagnetic mechanisms of traditional stimulation methods. This distinction may provide insights for exploring novel mechanisms of brain function regulation [12].

tFUS technology has been developed over several decades, initially used for non-invasive brain surgeries such as brain tumor thermal ablation [13]. In the past decade, low-intensity tFUS has emerged as a novel neuromodulation tool [14]. This review primarily focuses on nonthermal, low-intensity tFUS applications for neuromodulation, which typically operate at acoustic intensities below the threshold for significant tissue heating. Numerous animal and human studies have shown that tFUS affects brain function through multi-level mechanisms involving ion channels, synaptic transmission, neural oscillations, and brain networks, exhibiting significant neuromodulatory effects across major functional domains, including sensory, motor, cognitive, and emotional processes [15, 16]. Concurrently, clinical research on tFUS applications has been continuously expanding, showing promise in the treatment of multiple neurological disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease (AD), Parkinson’s disease (PD), and neuropsychiatric disorders [14]. The precise spatial targeting, deep brain penetration capabilities, and demonstrated multi-level regulatory mechanisms suggest that tFUS offers unique advantages over existing neuromodulation techniques for treating neurological diseases.

However, despite the substantial potential of tFUS technology in neuroscience research and clinical applications, several critical challenges remain unresolved. The current understanding of its mechanisms of action is still incomplete, and key issues such as long-term effects, optimal parameters, and safety assessments urgently require systematic elucidation [17]. Moreover, the auditory-related accompanying effects that have received much attention in recent years represent an important controversy in the field that remains to be resolved [18]. Although focused ultrasound applications have demonstrated significant clinical success [19], the existence of these specific issues in tFUS neuromodulation requires systematic elucidation to fully realize its broad therapeutic potential across neurological and psychiatric disorders.

As the field of tFUS rapidly evolves, there is an increasing need for a comprehensive framework that integrates the growing body of mechanistic knowledge with clinical application insights. The complexity of tFUS effects, spanning from molecular interactions to system-wide network changes, requires a systematic approach to understand how these different levels of biological organization interact to produce therapeutic outcomes. Furthermore, the translation from promising preclinical results to effective clinical interventions faces unique challenges due to substantial differences in skull properties, brain anatomy, and neural architecture between animal models and humans.

Building upon extensive foundational work—including systematic safety assessments [20, 21], comprehensive clinical evaluations [22, 23], mechanistic frameworks [24], neuropsychiatric applications [25], and technical developments [26, 27]—this work examines temporal mechanistic aspects of tFUS technology. We present a synthesis of molecular mechanisms, neural circuit modulations, clinical applications, and safety considerations with particular attention to the temporal distinction between immediate online effects and sustained offline aftereffects—a perspective that may contribute to understanding therapeutic protocol optimization. Additionally, we explore emerging applications such as brain-brain interfaces (BBIs) and closed-loop systems, while addressing key controversies regarding safety and auditory effects [28, 29]. Our work aims to examine temporal aspects of tFUS effects across multiple biological scales, with potential implications for understanding mechanisms and clinical applications. The review examines current knowledge regarding temporal dynamics of tFUS effects, focusing on the distinction between immediate and sustained responses. We discuss mechanistic findings, clinical observations, and methodological considerations related to temporal aspects of tFUS neuromodulation.

Stimulation parameters and protocols of tFUS

For neuromodulation applications, tFUS primarily operates at low intensities, clearly distinguishing it from high-intensity focused ultrasound (HIFU) used in ablative procedures [30]. While HIFU typically employs acoustic intensities greater than 200 W/cm², low-intensity focused ultrasound for neuromodulation generally operates at intensities below 100 W/cm² [31, 32]. In practice, most neuromodulatory tFUS studies adhere to FDA safety limits, with intensities carefully controlled to prevent thermal effects in neural tissue. Specifically, the FDA guidelines distinguish between the spatial-peak temporal-average intensity (ISPTA) limit of 0.72 W/cm² and the spatial-peak pulse-average intensity (ISPPA) limit of 190 W/cm², where ISPTA represents time-averaged intensity and ISPPA represents pulse-on intensity [31, 33–37]. The neuromodulatory effects are determined by several key parameters, including the fundamental frequency of acoustic waves (typically 0.25–0.65 MHz for optimal skull penetration), acoustic pressure (typically 0.1-1.0 MPa for human neuromodulation studies), pulse duration, pulse repetition frequency, duty cycle, and pulse train duration [4, 38].

Both penetration depth and spatial resolution are directly influenced by the fundamental frequency, with lower frequencies penetrating the skull more effectively while higher frequencies provide superior focal precision [39]. A critical consideration is skull composition, as human skulls cause substantially greater attenuation and aberration compared to the thinner skulls of animal models. At typical neuromodulation frequencies (0.25–0.65 MHz), human skulls can cause 65–90% reduction in acoustic intensity. This frequency-dependent attenuation necessitates distinct parameter considerations when translating from preclinical to clinical applications [39, 40]. To ensure mechanical safety, the International Consortium for Transcranial Ultrasonic Stimulation Safety and Standards (ITRUSST) consensus recommends using either mechanical index (MI) or transcranial mechanical index (MItc) not exceeding 1.9, with the appropriate index selected based on specific experimental configurations. The pulse repetition frequency fundamentally shapes the temporal pattern of neural stimulation, thereby influencing cellular response mechanisms and neurotransmitter release dynamics [39, 40].

Both preclinical and human studies have demonstrated that these parameters can be optimized to achieve either excitatory or inhibitory effects. For instance, higher pulse repetition frequencies (>100 Hz) combined with lower acoustic pressures typically facilitate neural excitation, whereas lower frequencies (< 100 Hz) with higher pressures generally produce inhibitory effects [16, 41]. This bidirectional modulation capability (both excitatory and inhibitory effects) has been validated across diverse clinical applications, ranging from motor enhancement in PD to anxiety reduction in psychiatric disorders [36, 42].

The relationship between ultrasound parameters and biological effects demonstrates complex, non-linear characteristics [12]. Recent clinical trials have reported variable treatment outcomes with different parameter combinations, suggesting that optimal protocols may need to be tailored to individual diseases or patient characteristics [43, 44]. For example, studies targeting the motor cortex in PD patients have demonstrated that theta-burst patterns can enhance cortical excitability [42, 45], whereas continuous protocols appear more effective for pain management when targeting the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) [35, 46]. Emerging research has revealed that low pulse repetition frequencies (2.5 Hz) combined with higher duty cycles (20%) and shorter durations (5s) produce excitatory effects, whereas higher pulse repetition frequencies (20 Hz) with lower duty cycles (2.5%) and longer durations (40s) tend to induce inhibitory effects across various neuronal populations [47]. These findings underscore the importance of systematic parameter optimization for specific neural targets and therapeutic outcomes. Parameter optimization strategies necessarily differ between animal and human applications. Animal studies allow more controlled exploration of dose-response relationships, while human applications must address greater inter-individual variability in skull properties. However, our current understanding of parameter-dependent effects remains preliminary, as most clinical trials have explored only a limited range of parameter combinations. This gap underscores the urgent need for systematic investigation of dose-response relationships and standardization of stimulation protocols [17]. Recent theoretical frameworks have proposed comprehensive approaches to conceptualizing tFUS dose based on biophysical effects, providing guidance for parameter optimization [48]. The clinical implementation of these parameters is significantly influenced by technical platform characteristics. Contemporary tFUS systems encompass single-element configurations offering operational simplicity, multi-element phased arrays providing enhanced focusing precision and aberration correction, and specialized pulse stimulation systems utilizing ultra-short pressure pulses [27, 39]. These technical variations necessitate platform-specific parameter optimization strategies and contribute to the observed variability in reported therapeutic outcomes across studies.

Safety considerations are paramount in parameter selection for clinical applications [17]. The ITRUSST consensus recommends minimizing thermal effects through an integrated approach that either: maintains temperature rise below 2℃, limits thermal dose to less than 0.25 cumulative equivalent minutes at 43℃ (CEM43), or restricts exposure times based on thermal index (TI) values (e.g., 80 min for TI ≤ 2.0; 40 min for TI ≤ 2.5; 10 min for TI ≤ 3.0) [49, 50]. In practice, these safety criteria are achieved by maintaining low duty cycles (typically < 10%), selecting appropriate pulse durations and peak pressures, and ensuring adequate inter-stimulus intervals for thermal dissipation [51]. Additionally, recent studies have emphasized the importance of controlling potential auditory confounds through careful parameter selection and acoustic shielding [18]. As the field advances, the development of closed-loop systems and personalized stimulation protocols promises to enable more precise and effective parameter optimization strategies [29].

Mechanisms of action of tFUS: a multi-scale analysis from molecular to system levels

The mechanisms of action underlying tFUS, an emerging non-invasive neuromodulation technology, span multiple levels of biological organization, from molecular interactions to system-wide effects. Understanding these mechanisms requires a systematic analysis that progresses hierarchically through different scales of neural organization while distinguishing between immediate and sustained effects at each level.

Molecular-level mechanisms

At the molecular level, tFUS modulates neuronal activity through diverse mechanisms, primarily involving mechanosensitive ion channels and altered cell membrane properties. Studies have demonstrated that tFUS activates various mechanosensitive ion channels—including transient receptor potential polycystin 1/2 (TRPP1/2), transient receptor potential canonical 1 (TRPC1), piezoelectric protein 1 (Piezo1), and TWIK-related arachidonic acid-activated K+ (TRAAK) potassium channels [52, 53] —through induced changes in cell membrane tension, though other cellular pathways also contribute to its effects. The activation of these channels triggers ion flux and alters neuronal membrane potentials. For example, TRAAK channel activation results in potassium efflux and subsequent neuronal hyperpolarization [53]. Additionally, tFUS regulates anion channels such as cystic fibrosis transmembrane conductance regulator (CFTR), mediating chloride ion flow [54].

The immediate molecular responses to tFUS occur within milliseconds to seconds, involving direct mechanotransduction through membrane-associated proteins. These acute changes in ion channel conductance create the foundation for all subsequent cellular responses. Beyond direct ion channel activation, tFUS influences cellular mechanisms through acoustic radiation force effects on cytoskeletal structures, alterations in membrane fluidity, and modulation of intracellular signaling cascades [55]. These molecular alterations establish a cascade of effects that propagate to cellular-level responses, with some changes persisting for minutes to hours after stimulation cessation.

Cellular-level mechanisms

The molecular effects of tFUS directly manifest in the electrophysiological properties of neurons, creating measurable changes in cellular excitability and firing patterns. Research has demonstrated that tFUS induces rapid sodium influx, followed by activation of voltage-gated calcium channels and subsequent calcium influx, ultimately leading to neuronal depolarization and action potential generation [56]. These cellular responses build upon the molecular foundation established by mechanosensitive channel activation, translating ion flux changes into coordinated patterns of neural activity.

Notably, tFUS also alters action potential waveforms and firing characteristics by regulating voltage-gated potassium channels [57]. In hippocampal Cornu Ammonis 1 (CA1) pyramidal neurons, for example, tFUS compresses the action potential waveform, reduces its amplitude and width, and prolongs the latency of the first action potential evoked by depolarizing current steps. These effects appear to involve sustained increases in potassium conductance induced by tFUS [58]. The temporal dynamics of these cellular changes span from immediate alterations during stimulation to prolonged modifications that can persist for tens of minutes, reflecting different underlying molecular processes.

Beyond individual neuronal responses, tFUS induces coordinated cellular effects across multiple cell types within neural tissue [59]. Recent evidence indicates that tFUS induces calcium responses not only in neurons but also in astrocytes, endothelial cells, and pericytes, primarily through mechanosensitive ion channels including transient receptor potential (TRP) channels [59]. This multi-cellular activation pattern suggests that tFUS effects involve coordinated responses across different cell types within neural tissue. The integration of responses across multiple cell types creates a complex cellular environment that influences subsequent synaptic and network-level effects, highlighting the interconnected nature of neural tissue responses to acoustic stimulation.

Synaptic-level mechanisms

At the synaptic level, tFUS demonstrates the ability to modulate synaptic transmission and plasticity through both direct neuronal effects and complex cellular interactions. The cellular changes described above translate into altered patterns of synaptic communication, fundamentally affecting how neurons communicate with each other. Animal studies have shown that tFUS can increase the frequency of spontaneous excitatory postsynaptic currents and enhance the probability of synaptic vesicle release. This effect may be related to the influence of tFUS on presynaptic calcium dynamics [56], building directly upon the cellular-level calcium responses previously described.

Furthermore, tFUS may also affect synaptic function by activating specific signaling pathways, such as extracellular signal-regulated kinase 1/2-cAMP response element-binding protein (ERK1/2-CREB), and upregulating the expression of neurotrophic factors (e.g., brain-derived neurotrophic factor [BDNF]) and cellular factors (e.g., calmodulin-dependent kinase [CaMK]) [60]. These molecular cascades represent sustained effects that can modify synaptic strength and connectivity for hours to days following stimulation.

The coordinated responses of different cell types collectively regulate synaptic plasticity-related proteins, such as postsynaptic density proteins and cytoskeletal proteins, thereby exerting long-term effects on synaptic structure and function. Recent studies have demonstrated that theta-burst ultrasound stimulation induces bidirectional long-term potentiation (LTP) or depression (LTD)-like plasticity, significantly enhancing motor skill learning in mice through direct modifications of synaptic efficacy [61, 62]. Additionally, pulse repetition frequency-dependent protocols can achieve selective targeting of excitatory and inhibitory neurons in specific neural circuits [63].

The temporal characteristics of synaptic-level effects demonstrate a clear distinction between immediate changes in neurotransmitter release probability and longer-term structural and functional modifications. While acute effects on synaptic transmission occur within seconds of stimulation onset, the activation of plasticity-related signaling pathways leads to sustained changes that can alter neural circuit function long after the cessation of acoustic stimulation. This temporal progression from immediate functional changes to sustained structural modifications underlies the therapeutic potential of tFUS in various neurological conditions.

Network-level mechanisms

At the neural network level, tFUS modulates functional connectivity within and between brain regions primarily by influencing neural oscillations and neurotransmitter levels. The synaptic changes described above aggregate to produce coordinated patterns of neural activity across populations of neurons. Animal studies have revealed that tFUS significantly affects local field potentials (LFPs) and various frequency band oscillations, particularly θ and γ oscillations, in regions such as the hippocampus and cortex [64–66]. These neural oscillation patterns serve crucial roles in higher-order cognitive functions: θ oscillations (4–8 Hz) are intimately associated with memory encoding, working memory maintenance, and spatial navigation, functioning as a key regulatory mechanism for information transfer and integration across multiple brain regions.

Meanwhile, γ oscillations (30–100 Hz) relate to perceptual awareness, attention allocation, and information binding, representing a fundamental mechanism through which local neural networks process information. tFUS not only alters the power of these oscillations but also modulates the phase-amplitude coupling between them [61, 66]. In the hippocampal CA1 region, for instance, tFUS enhances the modulation of γ oscillation amplitude by θ rhythm—a cross-frequency coupling mechanism that supports memory formation and cognitive processing [61]. This theta-gamma coupling represents a critical network-level mechanism that emerges from the coordinated synaptic activities across neural populations.

The influence of tFUS on neurotransmitter systems further extends its network-level effects, creating sustained changes in neural circuit dynamics. Studies have demonstrated that tFUS stimulation of the thalamus leads to approximately a 20% reduction in prefrontal gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) levels, and this attenuation of GABAergic inhibition may enhance cognitive flexibility through thalamo-cortical circuits [67]. Concurrently, thalamic tFUS can elevate prefrontal dopamine levels by modulating the thalamo-cortical-striatal circuit, specifically through disinhibition of dopaminergic systems via reduced GABAergic tone—changes closely associated with motor control and reward processing [68]. Furthermore, tFUS can increase prefrontal 5-hydroxytryptamine (5-HT) levels by affecting projections from midbrain nuclei to the prefrontal cortex and feedback pathways through the dorsal thalamic nuclei [68], potentially contributing to emotional regulation and amelioration of depressive-like behaviors.

Importantly, these modulatory effects vary across behavioral states (e.g., anesthesia, wakefulness, and locomotion), highlighting the state-dependent nature of tFUS effects [61, 62]. The effects of tFUS exhibit significant brain region specificity, reflecting differences in the inherent electrophysiological properties of neurons and local microcircuit structures in different brain regions. For example, in a comparative study of the hippocampus and motor cortex, the spatiotemporal characteristics of tFUS-evoked neuronal responses differed between the two regions, with hippocampal neurons showing shorter response duration (4.97s vs. 7.39s) and smaller response amplitude (2.17% vs. 5.73% calcium signal change) compared to motor cortex [69].

System-level mechanisms

At the peripheral and systemic levels, the mechanisms of action of tFUS involve the coordination of multiple physiological systems, extending beyond direct neural effects to encompass broader biological responses. In recent years, researchers have begun to focus on the impact of tFUS on neurovascular coupling and neuroimmune interactions, providing new perspectives for understanding the comprehensive mechanisms of action of tFUS. Regarding neurovascular coupling, studies have found that tFUS can affect local cerebral blood flow and vascular reactivity [70, 71]. For instance, tFUS stimulation can lead to a transient increase in blood flow in the stimulated region, which may be due to the increased local metabolic demand and vasodilation caused by enhanced neural activity. This effect may involve the release of vasoactive substances such as nitric oxide (NO) [72].

Additionally, tFUS may also modulate the function of the neurovascular unit by altering the permeability of the blood-brain barrier, though the specific mechanisms require further elucidation [73]. In terms of neuroimmune interactions, tFUS shows potential in modulating neuroinflammatory responses. Studies have indicated that tFUS can influence the activation state of astrocytes and microglia and the secretion of pro-inflammatory/anti-inflammatory factors [74]. For example, tFUS may downregulate the production of inflammatory mediators by inhibiting the activity of transcription factors such as kappa-light-chain-enhancer of activated B cells (NF-κB) [75]. Moreover, tFUS may also affect local immune microenvironments by modulating neuron-glia interactions, such as synaptic pruning, phagocytosis, and the release of neurotrophic factors [59, 75].

Research suggests that tFUS may modulate heart rate and blood pressure by influencing the autonomic nervous system, with this effect potentially caused by tFUS stimulation of specific brainstem nuclei [76]. Additionally, tFUS may also affect the endocrine system, such as by regulating the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis to influence stress responses [77, 78]. These systemic effects reflect the broad impact of tFUS on the regulatory functions of the central nervous system, demonstrating how localized acoustic stimulation can produce widespread physiological changes through interconnected neural and hormonal pathways.

Cross-scale integration and Temporal dynamics

In summary, tFUS appears to influence nervous system function through mechanisms spanning multiple biological levels from ion channels to brain networks (Fig. 2). Crucially, these mechanistic effects are not static but are dynamically modulated by stimulation parameters and demonstrate distinct temporal profiles across different organizational levels. The effects of tFUS are profoundly influenced by technical parameters including acoustic pressure, frequency, pulse duration, and sonication protocols. Different acoustic pressures, for instance, induce varying degrees of neuronal excitation or inhibition, with lower intensities typically facilitating neural activity while higher intensities produce inhibitory effects [79]. Moreover, the choice of stimulation frequency selectively affects specific neural oscillations, with different frequency bands targeting distinct neural network dynamics [80].

Recent experimental evidence reveals that even subtle parameter variations can produce dramatically different neuromodulatory outcomes, underscoring the critical importance of parameter optimization for specific applications [47]. This parameter-dependent response provides a flexible framework for tailoring tFUS interventions to specific therapeutic needs and neural targets. The temporal dynamics of tFUS effects demonstrate clear distinctions between immediate responses occurring during stimulation and sustained changes that persist after acoustic exposure ceases. At the molecular level, ion channel activation occurs within milliseconds, while gene expression changes may require hours to manifest. Cellular responses span from immediate changes in membrane potential to prolonged alterations in excitability lasting tens of minutes. Synaptic effects range from acute changes in neurotransmitter release to sustained modifications in synaptic strength and connectivity. Network-level changes include both immediate alterations in oscillatory patterns and lasting modifications in functional connectivity.

Optimizing these parameters based on mechanistic understanding offers the possibility of achieving precise, individualized neuromodulation that can be adapted for diverse clinical applications. The integration of effects across multiple scales creates complex, non-linear relationships between stimulation parameters and biological outcomes, highlighting the need for systematic investigation of parameter-effect relationships. Understanding these cross-scale interactions and temporal dynamics provides the foundation for developing more effective and targeted therapeutic applications of tFUS technology, while also revealing the complexity inherent in translating basic mechanistic knowledge into clinical practice. This multi-scale temporal framework extends existing mechanistic models by systematically integrating immediate and sustained effects across biological scales. While previous comprehensive analyses have addressed parameter-effect relationships and bidirectional modulation capabilities [24, 81], our temporal distinction provides mechanistic insights for understanding therapeutic efficacy and optimizing sustained clinical benefits beyond immediate stimulation effects.

Neuromodulatory effects of tFUS on specific brain functions

The rapid development of tFUS technology has provided new tools for studying the neuromodulation of specific brain systems. Growing evidence suggests that tFUS can selectively modulate multiple brain functional systems, including sensory, motor, cognitive, and emotional systems, showing broad application prospects.

Motor functions

tFUS demonstrates measurable modulatory effects on motor systems across species, with evidence revealing important differences between immediate responses during stimulation and sustained changes that persist after stimulation cessation. Understanding these temporal dynamics and species-specific patterns is crucial for translating findings from animal models to human applications.

Immediate motor responses during stimulation

Animal models: high-probability direct evocation

Animal models: high-probability direct evocation

Animal models: high-probability direct evocation

In rodent studies, tFUS can reliably elicit motor responses at frequencies below 1 MHz, with success rates reaching 70–100% under optimal conditions [38, 82–85]. The spatial precision is remarkable, with high-resolution tFUS capable of evoking distinct limb movements in adjacent brain regions separated by only 1 mm [83–85]. These immediate responses demonstrate positive correlations between acoustic intensity, stimulation duration, and response success rates, indicating dose-dependent effects within safe parameter ranges.

Large animal studies reveal more complex patterns that better approximate human responses. In awake sheep, excitatory stimulation parameters targeting M1 or thalamic motor nuclei can increase corticospinal tract excitability and evoke contralateral limb movements, but with notably lower success rates of 5–7% during resting conditions [86, 87]. Crucially, these effects are state-dependent rather than simply bidirectional: excitatory protocols show minimal efficacy (5–7% success) in resting animals, while inhibitory protocols effectively suppress motor output during active locomotion [86, 87]. This state dependency reveals that identical parameters produce opposite outcomes depending on baseline motor activity levels, challenging simple interpretations of bidirectional modulation.

Human studies: limited direct evocation

In humans, immediate motor evocation during tFUS is uncommon, with most studies focusing on excitability changes rather than direct motor responses. When motor effects do occur during stimulation, they typically manifest as subtle modifications in muscle activation patterns rather than overt movements. This species difference likely reflects the increased complexity of human motor control networks and higher activation thresholds required for motor output.

Sustained motor system changes

Neuroplasticity induction in humans

Neuroplasticity induction in humans

Neuroplasticity induction in humans

The most clinically relevant effects of tFUS on human motor systems involve sustained changes in cortical excitability that persist well beyond the stimulation period. Theta-burst tFUS protocols consistently demonstrate the ability to induce lasting increases in motor evoked potential (MEP) amplitudes, with effects persisting for 30–60 min after stimulation cessation [84, 88, 89]. These sustained changes suggest the induction of long-term potentiation-like plasticity mechanisms within motor cortical circuits, though individual response variability remains considerable (30–70% response rates).

The temporal characteristics of these sustained effects reveal important therapeutic implications. Studies using various stimulation protocols show that motor cortex excitability enhancement can be achieved with different parameter combinations, but the duration and magnitude of effects vary considerably. For instance, continuous theta-burst protocols produce sustained inhibitory effects lasting 30 min or more, while intermittent protocols can enhance excitability for similar durations [85].

Mechanistic basis of sustained changes

Sustained motor system changes are characterized by measurable alterations in cortical excitability patterns. Effective plasticity-inducing protocols demonstrate reduced short-interval intracortical inhibition and increased intracortical facilitation [88–90], which correlate with the observed behavioral improvements in motor function.

State dependency and individual variability

Critical role of baseline state

Critical role of baseline state

Critical role of baseline state

One of the most important findings from cross-species research is the profound influence of baseline brain state on tFUS effects. The sheep studies provide particularly clear evidence that identical stimulation parameters can produce opposite effects depending on the animal’s behavioral state: excitatory effects during rest versus inhibitory effects during active movement [86]. This state dependency has crucial implications for protocol design and timing of therapeutic interventions.

(2)Individual response variability

(2)Individual response variability

Individual response variability

Human studies consistently report substantial individual differences in response to tFUS, with response rates varying from 35% to 70% depending on the specific protocol and outcome measure. This variability appears to relate to factors including skull thickness, individual brain anatomy, and baseline cortical excitability levels. Some studies report that approximately 30–35% of participants show no measurable response to standard protocols [91], highlighting the need for personalized parameter selection approaches.

Clinical relevance and translation considerations

Despite the substantial differences in response patterns across species, tFUS has demonstrated therapeutic potential in motor system disorders. In Parkinson’s disease, preliminary studies suggest that repeated tFUS can enhance motor cortex excitability and may improve motor symptoms, particularly when combined with dopaminergic medication [45]. Essential tremor research shows more pronounced effects, with tFUS targeting the ventral intermediate nucleus achieving tremor suppression lasting up to 30 min [92].

The translation from animal models to human applications reveals important considerations for clinical implementation. The substantial differences in response probability—near 100% success rates in rodents versus 5–10% in humans for direct motor evocation—reflect fundamental differences in brain organization and acoustic transmission properties. Human skulls cause 65–90% attenuation of acoustic intensity compared to minimal attenuation in small animal models, necessitating careful parameter adjustment for effective human applications [93]. Additionally, individual variability in response (35–70% response rates depending on protocol) suggests the need for personalized approaches in clinical settings. Methodological considerations including auditory confounding effects require careful control in motor system studies, as failure to replicate some offline effects may relate to inadequate control of these confounding factors.

Cognitive system modulation

tFUS has demonstrated modulatory effects on multiple cognitive functions in animal models and human studies, including learning, memory, decision-making, and response inhibition. The cognitive effects show distinct temporal patterns that provide insights into underlying neural mechanisms and therapeutic potential.

Immediate cognitive effects during stimulation

Real-time cognitive modulation has been clearly demonstrated in decision-making tasks. Studies in macaque monkeys show that tFUS can strongly and controllably alter visual decision-making behavior during stimulation periods [94, 95]. When applied to the frontal eye field, tFUS creates immediate biases in spatial attention and choice behavior, with effects reaching 2:1 or even 4:1 choice ratios depending on stimulation side [95]. These immediate effects demonstrate precise, online control of cognitive processes.

Human studies reveal similar immediate effects in executive control. tFUS targeting the right inferior frontal gyrus produces immediate improvements in response inhibition, reducing stop-signal failure rates and shortening stop-signal reaction times, but only when stimulation is delivered concurrently with the cognitive demand [96]. This temporal specificity indicates that immediate cognitive effects require precise timing relative to task demands.

Sustained cognitive enhancement

Memory system improvements

Memory system improvements

Memory system improvements

Sustained cognitive effects are most robust in memory systems. In rodent studies, tFUS targeting the hippocampus produces lasting improvements in spatial memory performance that persist beyond the stimulation period [97, 98]. Closed-loop stimulation approaches, where tFUS delivery is synchronized to specific phases of ongoing theta oscillations, can significantly enhance memory encoding [98].

Aging research provides compelling evidence for sustained cognitive restoration. In senescent mice, repeated tFUS treatments can restore both synaptic plasticity and cognitive function. Weekly treatments over six weeks improve spatial learning performance and restore long-term potentiation induction capability that had been lost with aging, with these improvements involving enhanced neurogenesis and restoration of NMDA receptor signaling [99].

Executive function and cognitive control

Sustained improvements in executive functions have been documented in response inhibition capabilities. tFUS targeting inhibitory control networks produces lasting enhancement, with improvements persisting for 30 min or more after stimulation cessation. These sustained effects involve modifications in neural processing dynamics, as evidenced by changes in P300 event-related potential onset latencies that correlate with behavioral improvements [96].

The anterior prefrontal-putamen circuit demonstrates importance for sustained cognitive effects. Research reveals its causal role in response inhibition, with targeted interventions producing lasting improvements in cognitive control capabilities [99].

Motivational integration

Long-term primate studies reveal that cognitive effects depend on motivational context. tFUS targeting the dorsal striatum produces different outcomes depending on reward magnitude and expectation, demonstrating complex interactions between motivation and executive control systems. These context-dependent effects persist across multiple sessions, indicating sustained modifications in motivational-cognitive integration [94].

Individual variability and optimization

Cognitive effects show substantial individual variability, with response rates varying across different protocols and outcome measures. Closed-loop approaches that adapt stimulation parameters based on real-time neural states show promise for optimizing individual responses. For example, stimulation synchronized to theta peak phases enhances memory encoding, while stimulation at other phases may have neutral effects [98].

Clinical relevance

Aging and cognitive decline

The demonstrated ability to restore cognitive function in aging models suggests therapeutic potential for age-related cognitive decline. The multi-modal benefits observed—including restored synaptic plasticity, enhanced neurogenesis, and improved behavioral performance—indicate that tFUS may address multiple aspects of cognitive aging simultaneously [100].

Executive dysfunction

The capacity for enhancing executive control functions suggests potential applications in attention deficit disorders and other conditions involving executive dysfunction. The specificity of effects on different cognitive domains allows for targeted intervention approaches [96, 100].

However, translation from animal results to human applications requires careful consideration of species differences in cognitive complexity and neural organization. While immediate effects can be demonstrated in humans, achieving the sustained cognitive enhancements observed in animal models may require protocol optimization and personalization approaches that account for individual variability in human cognitive systems [96, 101].

Emotional system modulation

tFUS has shown positive effects on emotion regulation in brain regions involved in emotional processing, especially in studies of frontal and limbic circuits. The emotional effects demonstrate both immediate responses during stimulation and sustained changes that persist beyond the treatment period.

Immediate emotional responses

Fear and defensive behaviors

Fear and defensive behaviors

Fear and defensive behaviors

tFUS targeting emotional processing centers can produce immediate behavioral responses. Stimulation of the periaqueductal gray induces defensive behaviors in mice, including avoidance, escape, and freezing responses that emerge during stimulation periods. In real-time place preference testing, animals show immediate avoidance of stimulation-paired locations, while open field testing reveals increased escape behaviors during stimulation [102].

Human studies demonstrate immediate effects on fear processing systems. tFUS targeting the amygdala produces immediate reductions in fear network activation during threat anticipation, with concurrent decreases in subjective anxiety ratings that correlate with reduced amygdala BOLD activation [103, 104].

Mood regulation

Clinical studies have found that tFUS stimulation of the right inferior frontal gyrus can significantly improve the emotional state of healthy volunteers, with effects lasting 20–30 min after stimulation [105]. Functional MRI results showed that after tFUS stimulation of the rIFG, its functional connectivity with emotion regulation-related brain regions was enhanced, while its functional connectivity with the default network was weakened [105].

Fear extinction and learning

tFUS stimulation of the infralimbic cortex can produce lasting improvements in fear extinction. Research demonstrates that tFUS targeting this region during extinction training facilitates fear extinction, with tFUS groups showing significantly lower freezing behavior during retention testing compared to sham groups. Importantly, tFUS can promote extinction even under immediate extinction deficit conditions, reducing freezing levels during retention phases [106].

(2)Depression-related behaviors

Depression-related behaviors

Animal studies using multiple depression models show sustained antidepressant-like effects following tFUS treatment. The same research demonstrated that tFUS can reverse stress-induced anhedonia (restored sucrose preference) and increase cortical DCX levels in restraint stress models, while also increasing cortical and callosal MBP and NG2 levels in cuprizone-induced demyelination models, suggesting neuroprotective effects through both neurogenesis and oligodendrocyte support [107].

(3)Anxiety and stress responses

Anxiety and stress responses

Studies focusing on the bilateral medial prefrontal cortex using different acoustic parameters have shown that excitatory tFUS protocols can reduce delta and alpha band power during stimulation and increase beta band power after stimulation, while inhibitory protocols reduce delta band power during stimulation [108]. Human studies demonstrate that tFUS can reduce participants’ subjective anxiety in negative emotion-inducing tasks, with enhanced functional connectivity between emotion regulation regions [103, 105].

Learned helplessness and control perception

tFUS applications in learned helplessness paradigms reveal complex effects on emotional resilience. Stimulation of the right lateral prefrontal cortex significantly affects midline theta activity, which correlates with emotional, cognitive, and performance variables. tFUS reduces theta power at frontal and parietal electrodes, with effects most pronounced during later game stages and dependent on participants’ depression scores [103].

Network-level changes

Beyond direct stimulation effects, tFUS produces modifications in emotion-related network connectivity. Stimulation targeting limbic regions such as the amygdala enhances functional connectivity with the medial prefrontal cortex while reducing anxiety-related activation patterns [103]. These network changes suggest that emotional benefits involve both direct regional effects and broader circuit-level modifications.

Clinical relevance

The demonstrated capacity for both immediate and sustained emotional modifications suggests potential therapeutic applications for mood and anxiety disorders. The ability to enhance fear extinction while simultaneously reducing anxiety responses provides a dual mechanism that could complement existing therapeutic approaches.

However, the translation from animal models to human applications requires careful consideration of the complexity of human emotional systems and individual variability in treatment response. The current evidence, while promising, primarily comes from healthy populations and animal models, indicating that clinical applications will require systematic validation in patient populations with emotional disorders.

Sensory system modulation

The application of tFUS in sensory systems has been most extensively studied in somatosensory processing, with emerging evidence in visual and auditory domains. These studies reveal tFUS’s capacity to modulate sensory perception, discrimination abilities, and neural processing patterns with spatial precision that distinguishes it from other non-invasive stimulation techniques.

Somatosensory processing

tFUS targeting the primary somatosensory cortex (S1) demonstrates consistent modulatory effects on tactile perception and sensory processing capabilities. Human studies show that tFUS can directly evoke tactile sensations, with 11 out of 18 participants reporting contralateral hand and finger sensations including tingling, pressure, and movement sensations when S1 hand representation areas are stimulated [109]. These evoked sensations exhibit clear somatotopic specificity, allowing differentiation between individual fingers, indicating precise spatial targeting capabilities [109].

Beyond direct sensory evocation, tFUS produces measurable improvements in sensory discrimination performance. Stimulation of S1 enhances both two-point discrimination and vibrotactile frequency discrimination thresholds, improving sensitivity (d’) while maintaining spatial specificity—effects that disappear when the ultrasound beam is displaced by 1 cm from the target [110]. These behavioral improvements are accompanied by systematic changes in somatosensory evoked potentials (SEPs), with tFUS reducing early SEP components (N20-P27, P27-N33) and modulating gamma-band (30–55 Hz) spectral power during sensory stimulation [110].

The temporal dynamics of somatosensory modulation reveal both immediate and sustained effects. Immediate responses include shortened latency of cortical responses by approximately 3 ms and more spatially focused, symmetrical calcium responses [111]. Sustained effects persist for 30 min or longer, suggesting induction of plasticity-like mechanisms similar to long-term potentiation or depression [112]. Importantly, different tFUS parameters produce distinct modulatory profiles: higher pulse repetition frequencies (≥ 75 Hz) generally increase neuronal correlations, while lower frequencies (≤ 50 Hz) tend to decrease correlations between somatosensory neurons [113].

Subcortical somatosensory targets

tFUS’s ability to penetrate deeper brain structures enables modulation of subcortical sensory processing centers. Stimulation of the ventral posterior lateral (VPL) nucleus of the thalamus produces measurable changes in cortical sensory responses, with effects that can be bidirectional depending on stimulation parameters [12, 114]. Human studies demonstrate that thalamic VPL targeting can suppress P14 SEP components while affecting downstream cortical areas, indicating successful subcortical neuromodulation with functional consequences for sensory processing networks [114].

Visual and auditory systems

Limited evidence suggests tFUS can modulate visual and auditory processing, though these applications remain less developed than somatosensory interventions. Visual cortex (V1) stimulation can induce phosphenes and visual field changes in humans, with concurrent fMRI revealing activation not only in the stimulated region but also in broader visual processing networks [115, 116]. In the auditory domain, tFUS targeting primary auditory cortex and subcortical centers like the inferior colliculus can suppress auditory evoked potentials, suggesting broad modulatory effects across auditory pathways [117].

The emerging evidence in visual and auditory systems indicates potential for cross-modal sensory modulation, though the mechanisms and optimal parameters for these applications require further investigation. The spatial precision demonstrated in somatosensory studies suggests similar specificity may be achievable in other sensory modalities with refined targeting approaches.

Overall, tFUS demonstrates reliable modulatory effects across sensory systems, with the most robust evidence supporting its application in somatosensory processing. The technology’s unique combination of spatial precision and ability to target both cortical and subcortical structures positions it as a valuable tool for both basic sensory neuroscience research and potential therapeutic applications in sensory processing disorders.

Sleep functions

tFUS technology is emerging in the study of sleep, consciousness, and other brain functional systems, providing new tools for exploring brain state regulation. Recent rodent studies demonstrate that tFUS effectively modulates both rapid eye movement (REM) and non-rapid eye movement (NREM) sleep in mouse models.

Sleep architecture modulation

Studies have found that tFUS can increase the proportion of NREM sleep, reduce the proportion of REM sleep, and alter the relative power and sample entropy of the delta and spindle frequency bands during NREM and the theta and gamma frequency bands during REM [118]. The regulatory effects of tFUS on sleep depend on the individual’s sleep state. In sleep-deprived mice, tFUS can reduce the proportion of REM sleep or the relative power of the θ band during REM [118].

Notably, contrasting effects have been reported depending on stimulation parameters and target regions. For instance, closed-loop tFUS targeting the prefrontal cortex during NREM sleep has shown increased REM sleep duration and enhanced REM maintenance probability, in contrast to the REM reduction observed in other protocols [119]. These parameter-dependent differences highlight the importance of stimulation conditions, including pulse repetition frequency, duty cycle, and timing relative to sleep states, in determining the direction and magnitude of sleep modulation effects.

State-dependent and phase-specific effects

An exploratory study found that tFUS locked to the in-phase/anti-phase of hippocampal slow-wave oscillations during NREM and the peak and trough phases of theta oscillations during wakefulness can increase the occurrence rate of ripples during NREM and affect θ-γ coupling during REM by modulating NMDA receptor function and inducing LTP [120]. These findings demonstrate that the effects of tFUS on sleep systems show complex phase-dependent characteristics.

Clinical applications in sleep disorders

In addition to studies in healthy individuals, the application of tFUS in sleep disorders has also attracted attention. Research has shown that tFUS can increase total sleep time and the proportion of NREM sleep, modulate the relative power and sample entropy of the δ and spindle frequency bands during NREM and the θ band during REM, and improve sleep disturbances in AD mice [118].

Regarding the mechanism of tFUS in regulating sleep, researchers have proposed that ultrasound may influence the activity patterns of neurons by affecting the ion channels of neurons and glial cells, thereby influencing the neural circuits that regulate sleep states [118]. The temporal characteristics of these sleep-related effects demonstrate both immediate changes during stimulation and sustained modifications that can persist for hours, reflecting the complex interaction between tFUS and endogenous sleep regulatory mechanisms.

Although current research on tFUS regulation of sleep mainly focuses on animal models, this emerging technology shows great potential for future clinical applications in treating insomnia, sleep disorders, and other conditions. The demonstrated neuromodulatory effects across motor, cognitive, emotional, sensory, and sleep systems provide compelling evidence for tFUS’s therapeutic versatility. Having established these fundamental neuromodulatory capabilities across major brain functional systems, the translation of these effects into clinical therapeutic applications becomes the next critical step in tFUS development.

Application progress of tFUS in neurological systems and related diseases

Neurodegenerative diseases

AD and PD are among the most common neurodegenerative diseases, with no effective treatments currently available. In recent years, tFUS technology has shown promising potential for treating neurodegenerative disorders. While this section focuses on AD, PD as a movement disorder will be discussed in detail in Sect. 5.2.1.

In AD animal model studies, tFUS treatment can reduce Aβ plaques and tau protein pathological burden, improve spatial learning and memory, and cause no apparent tissue damage [121–123]. Mechanistic studies suggest that tFUS may promote Aβ clearance through microglia-mediated phagocytosis via regulation of cholesterol metabolism-related genes (e.g., LRP1b) [124, 125]. Additionally, tFUS can upregulate the levels of neurotrophic factors such as BDNF and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), alleviate synaptic loss, and promote tau protein clearance by inducing autophagy, thereby exerting neuroprotective effects through multiple pathways [126–128].

Notably, the selection of tFUS parameters may influence its regulatory effects in AD. For example, one study found that γ-frequency (40 Hz) tFUS can not only reduce Aβ deposition but also enhance γ oscillation power, suggesting that it may improve learning and memory abilities in AD mice by modulating neural network activity [121]. Another study explored the effects of different tFUS intensities and found that even at intensities below the blood-brain barrier opening threshold, tFUS can still exert neuroprotective effects by upregulating molecules such as endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) [127].

In addition to animal studies, the application of tFUS in AD patients has also made preliminary progress. One study showed that compared to the placebo group, patients in the tFUS group had a slower deterioration in the AD assessment scale-cognitive subscale (ADAS-cog) scores at week 72, with good overall tolerability and no serious adverse events observed, suggesting that ultrasound therapy may have a potential delaying effect on cognitive function deterioration in early AD patients, with good safety [129]. Furthermore, some small-sample preliminary studies have found that transcranial ultrasound stimulation can improve local cerebral glucose metabolism and neural electrical activity in AD patients, which may be related to their cognitive function improvement [125, 130].

Movement disorders

Movement disorders represent a group of neurological conditions characterized by abnormal voluntary or involuntary movements. While these disorders have different underlying pathologies, they share common therapeutic targets in motor circuits. This section discusses two major movement disorders - PD and essential tremor (ET) - where tFUS has demonstrated therapeutic potential through modulation of motor pathways, particularly the thalamic circuits.

Parkinson’s disease

Preclinical and clinical studies have demonstrated promising therapeutic effects of tFUS in PD, targeting both motor and non-motor symptoms. In preclinical research, multiple PD animal models have validated tFUS efficacy. Studies using 1-methyl-4-phenyl-1,2,3,6-tetrahydropyridine (MPTP)- and 6-Hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA)-induced models show that tFUS alleviates dopaminergic neuron damage, inhibits neuroinflammation, improves motor function, and enhances antioxidant enzyme activity to relieve oxidative stress [131–134]. Notably, these neuroprotective effects are time-dependent, with efficacy emerging on the 4th day after stimulation and increasing with treatment duration [134].

Beyond motor symptoms, tFUS has shown efficacy in addressing non-motor manifestations of PD. In α-synuclein A53T transgenic mice, tFUS increases the power and coupling strength of θ and γ oscillations in the hippocampus, thereby improving working memory [135]. In a chronic MPTP mouse model, tFUS alleviates PD-related neuroinflammation and emotional disorders by inhibiting abnormal activation of microglia and astrocytes and reducing inflammatory factors in the substantia nigra and striatum [132]. Moreover, tFUS may improve PD cognitive impairment through increased dopamine release in the hippocampus [135].

Clinical studies have revealed mixed results in both motor and cognitive domains. Research has shown that theta-burst tFUS can increase motor cortex excitability in healthy subjects and PD patients during dopaminergic medication treatment, evidenced by increased MEP amplitudes at 30 min post-stimulation and reduced bradykinesia scores at 60 min. However, these effects were absent after medication withdrawal, suggesting that dopaminergic medications may enhance tFUS-induced LTP-like plasticity [42]. Recent studies targeting bilateral M1 with accelerated tFUS protocols have demonstrated increased MEP amplitudes following active stimulation, though without significant changes in motor function scores [45]. Regarding cognitive effects, while preliminary evidence suggests that weekly tFUS treatment for 8 weeks may improve both cognitive and motor functions [136], conclusive evidence is still needed.

Essential tremor

ET is a common movement disorder characterized by involuntary, rhythmic muscle contractions that often affect patients’ daily lives. Like PD, ET significantly impacts motor function, though through different pathological mechanisms. tFUS stimulation of relevant brain regions may provide new treatment options for ET patients.

Multiple animal and human studies have confirmed that tFUS stimulation of the ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) of the thalamus can effectively suppress ET symptoms [33, 137, 138]. In animal research, a study of harmaline-induced ET in rats showed that tFUS stimulation of the inferior olivary system significantly reduced tremor frequency and amplitude, with effects lasting from tens of seconds to several minutes [138].

Clinical studies have further validated these findings with promising results. A study of 10 ET patients demonstrated that tFUS treatment targeting the VIM significantly reduced tremor scores after eight 10-minute stimulation sessions, with no significant adverse events [33]. In another study, three ET patients showed immediate tremor suppression within seconds of receiving 15-second tFUS stimulation, with effects increasing with stimulation duration [137]. Most recently, clinical evidence revealed that low-power tFUS targeting both VIM and dentato-rubro-thalamic tract (DRT) could reduce tremor by up to 98%, with effects lasting up to 30 min and minimal temperature elevation (37.2 ± 1.4 ℃), supporting a non-thermal mechanism [92].

These results demonstrate that tFUS modulation of VIM has the advantages of being rapid, reversible, and highly spatially specific. Compared to existing deep brain stimulation (DBS) and HIFU ablation surgeries, tFUS, as a non-invasive method, is expected to reduce treatment risks and costs [33, 137].

Psychiatric disorders

Depression, anxiety disorders, addiction, and schizophrenia are psychiatric disorders that seriously affect patients’ quality of life, with a substantial subset of patients showing resistance to conventional pharmacological and psychological treatments [139–141]. Growing evidence suggests that tFUS may provide new treatment options for psychiatric disorders.

In animal models of depression, studies have found that tFUS stimulation of the dorsal raphe nucleus, prefrontal cortex, and other brain regions can significantly improve depressive and anxiety-like behaviors, which may be related to the regulation of monoaminergic neurotransmitters, increased BDNF expression, and inhibition of neuroinflammation [77, 142–144]. Clinical studies support the findings from animal experiments, indicating that tFUS targeting the prefrontal cortex, amygdala, and other emotion regulation-related brain regions can alleviate core symptoms in patients with depression, with good safety and tolerability [145, 146]. Studies have shown that tFUS to the right fronto-temporal cortex can reduce worry symptoms and improve global affect in mild to moderately depressed patients, with differential responses observed based on individual anxiety and rumination levels [147] .Notably, tFUS has also shown some efficacy in patients with treatment-resistant depression, providing a new treatment option for this subtype [146, 148, 149].

For anxiety disorders, research has found that the amygdala and other key brain regions for anxiety emotion processing are effective targets for tFUS treatment. A clinical study on patients with treatment-resistant generalized anxiety disorder showed that tFUS stimulation of the right amygdala could significantly reduce anxiety symptoms, suggesting its feasibility as a novel treatment for anxiety disorders [150].

Furthermore, tFUS has potential application value in the field of substance addiction. Animal studies have shown that tFUS stimulation of key nodes in the reward circuit, such as the nucleus accumbens and prefrontal cortex, can suppress drug-seeking behavior in addicted animals [151–153]. Preliminary clinical studies further support this finding, with tFUS stimulation of the bilateral nucleus accumbens reducing drug cravings in patients with substance use disorders, with good tolerability [154, 155]. However, given the small sample sizes, the above results still need validation from larger-scale randomized controlled trials.

The negative and cognitive symptoms of schizophrenia lack effective treatments, and tFUS brings new hope to this field. Animal studies have shown that tFUS can reverse schizophrenia-like behaviors induced by MK-801 (also named Dizocilpine), which may be related to the regulation of glutamatergic and dopaminergic systems and the function of GABAergic interneurons [156, 157]. An exploratory clinical study found that left dorsolateral prefrontal cortex tFUS stimulation can improve negative symptoms and some cognitive functions in patients with schizophrenia, further supporting its application potential in this disease [158].

Moreover, social avoidance is a core symptom of autism and various other psychiatric disorders. An animal study showed that prefrontal tFUS could reduce social avoidance behavior caused by social defeat stress, suggesting that it may also have some application prospects in the treatment of this symptom [159].

Pain

Pain is a complex experience involving multiple components, including sensory, cognitive, and emotional aspects, and pathological pain is often difficult to treat. tFUS, due to its unique spatial specificity and non-invasiveness, has attracted much attention in the field of pain research and treatment.

Multiple studies have shown that tFUS can specifically modulate key brain regions involved in pain processing, such as the thalamus, ACC, and insula, thereby affecting pain perception and autonomic nervous responses [160–162]. For example, tFUS applied to the right anterior thalamus in healthy volunteers can significantly increase heat pain thresholds [160]. Stimulation of the ACC can also attenuate the amplitude of heat pain-evoked potentials, reduce subjective pain ratings, and increase heart rate variability [161]. Furthermore, tFUS can differentially modulate different subregions of the insula, affecting different stages of pain processing [162].

In addition to healthy populations, tFUS has also achieved positive results in multiple pain-related disease models and clinical cases. Clinical studies have demonstrated that tFUS to the posterior frontal cortex or ACC can significantly improve pain and mood outcomes in chronic pain patients, with effects lasting up to one week [46, 163]. In patients with chronic neuropathic pain, navigation-guided tFUS treatment of the bilateral ACC can alleviate pain and improve quality of life, with no serious adverse reactions observed [164]. Animal studies have further revealed the potential mechanisms of tFUS, such as activating the periaqueductal gray (PAG) in the midbrain to inhibit nociceptive activity in spinal dorsal horn neurons [165], or reversing abnormal neuroplasticity in the ACC to alleviate mechanical hyperalgesia in neuropathic pain mice [166]. Moreover, tFUS has also been used in the study of other pain types, such as migraine. Preliminary evidence suggests that tFUS can alleviate pain behavior in a rat model of migraine by inhibiting the cerebral cortex [167].

Epilepsy

Epilepsy is a chronic brain dysfunction disorder, and some patients still experience recurrence after drug treatment, necessitating new treatment methods. Recent studies have shown that ultrasound brain stimulation technology has good prospects in the treatment of epilepsy.

In a penicillin-induced epilepsy model in rhesus monkeys, 30 min of tFUS stimulation significantly reduced the total number and hourly frequency of behavioral epileptic seizures [168]. In vitro experiments found that tFUS could suppress epileptiform discharges in human epileptic brain tissue, with an efficiency exceeding 65%, possibly by increasing the activity of local inhibitory neurons to modulate the excitation-inhibition balance [168]. Rodent experiments further confirmed that tFUS could reduce epileptiform discharges in acute epilepsy models induced by pentylenetetrazol, which may be related to influencing neuronal activity and the phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase-protein kinase B-mammalian target of rapamycin (PI3K-Akt-mTOR) pathway [169]. Notably, stimulation parameters significantly affect the bidirectional modulatory effects of focused ultrasound on epileptiform discharges, with long-term low-intensity stimulation having an inhibitory effect, while short-term high-intensity or overly frequent stimulation may aggravate discharges [170].

Clinical studies have preliminarily explored the safety and efficacy of tFUS in patients with refractory temporal lobe epilepsy. Studies involving epilepsy patients have demonstrated the feasibility of delivering tFUS to hippocampal and temporal lobe targets, with evidence showing temporal lobe blood oxygen level-dependent (BOLD) signal reduction following suppressive stimulation, though neural responses were variable across participants [171, 172]. A trial involving six patients found that ultrasound stimulation of the hippocampal region did not cause adverse events, and the average frequency of epileptic seizures decreased by approximately 50%, with some subjects showing improved default network functional connectivity [173]. Another study using stereoelectroencephalography monitoring showed that ultrasound stimulation of the epileptogenic zone could alter brain electrical spectra, and two patients had reduced epileptic seizure frequency within three days after stimulation, with only mild reversible adverse reactions [174]. Although the preliminary results are encouraging, current human studies have small sample sizes and lack control groups, still requiring further validation of long-term efficacy and safety with larger samples.

Multiple sclerosis

Multiple sclerosis is a chronic inflammatory demyelinating disease that can lead to various neurological deficits. Although there is currently no cure, research suggests that tFUS may contribute to the treatment of this disease.

In a rat model of local hippocampal demyelination induced by lysolecithin (LPC), tFUS significantly reduced the area of demyelinating lesions and increased the magnetization transfer ratio (MTR) value of the lesion site, indicating that tFUS can promote the remyelination process [175]. Immunohistochemical results further confirmed that tFUS treatment increased the expression of myelin basic protein (MBP) and mature oligodendrocytes (CC-1), decreased the expression of oligodendrocyte precursor cells (Olig2), and inhibited the activation of microglia and astrocytes [175]. Mechanistic studies suggest that tFUS may promote the differentiation of oligodendrocyte precursor cells (OPCs) into myelinating cells and enhance remyelination by upregulating the production of BDNF in astrocytes and activating the tropomyosin receptor kinase B-phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase-protein kinase B (TrkB-PI3K-Akt) and calcium-CaMK signaling pathways [175, 176]. These findings indicate that tFUS has the potential to become a new non-invasive neuromodulation technology for the treatment of multiple sclerosis.

Stroke

Stroke is one of the leading causes of disability and mortality worldwide, and survivors often experience various neurological dysfunctions. Recent research has explored the role of tFUS in promoting neurological function recovery after stroke.

tFUS stimulation of the lateral cerebellar nucleus can effectively promote sensorimotor function recovery and reduce cerebral edema in mice after stroke [177]. tFUS stimulation of the ischemic core can exert acute neuroprotective effects in a rat model of distal middle cerebral artery occlusion, possibly by increasing cerebral blood flow and reducing inflammation [178]. Clinical studies have found that tFUS combined with conventional cognitive rehabilitation training can improve executive function, naming, attention, language, and memory abilities in patients with post-stroke cognitive impairment, with better efficacy than rehabilitation training alone, and may be related to the upregulation of BDNF and P300 [179]. Overall, translational research of tFUS in the field of stroke has made preliminary progress, but optimizing stimulation protocols, elucidating mechanisms of action, and conducting large-sample long-term follow-up studies remain key areas for future research.

Disorders of consciousness

Disorders of consciousness resulting from severe brain injury are challenging in the field of neurological critical care, and the effects of existing pharmacological and neurostimulation therapies are limited. In recent years, tFUS has shown some potential in the rehabilitation treatment of patients with disorders of consciousness.

Monti et al. first reported a case of a patient with traumatic disorder of consciousness whose level of consciousness improved after receiving tFUS stimulation to the thalamus. The patient showed improvement within their minimally conscious state (MCS), with enhanced motor and oromotor functions, and later demonstrated abilities consistent with emergence from MCS [180]. Subsequent case series studies further confirmed the feasibility and safety of tFUS application in patients with chronic disorders of consciousness, with some patients showing significant increases in responsiveness and levels of consciousness [34, 181]. Mechanistic studies suggest that tFUS may exert its effects by resetting the functional connectivity patterns of the cortical-basal ganglia-thalamic-cortical circuit, but more evidence is needed to support this [182].

Other rare diseases

Certain rare neurological diseases, such as mucopolysaccharidosis and Rett syndrome, place a heavy burden on patients and their families due to their complex pathogenesis and lack of treatment options. Although research on the application of tFUS in these special diseases is still in its infancy, it has shown some therapeutic potential.

Recent studies indicate that tFUS combined with microbubbles can temporarily and reversibly open the blood-brain barrier over a large area, increasing the amount of intravenously administered enzyme replacement therapy drugs entering the brain tissue, providing a new treatment approach for mucopolysaccharidosis type I and other inherited metabolic diseases [183]. tFUS may also have beneficial effects on Rett syndrome by increasing BDNF levels and stimulating hippocampal neurogenesis [184]. Moreover, in an animal model of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), tFUS stimulation can modulate neuronal activity in the prefrontal cortex and improve cognitive and behavioral performance [185]. Although current research is still in its early stages, tFUS shows broad application prospects in the field of special neurological diseases.

In summary, tFUS has shown therapeutic potential across various neurological and psychiatric disorders (Fig. 3; Table 1). The clinical applications reviewed demonstrate tFUS’s evolution from experimental tool to promising therapeutic intervention. Having established therapeutic potential across diverse conditions, systematic safety evaluation becomes essential for widespread clinical adoption.

Safety considerations and assessment of auditory accompanying effects

Having demonstrated therapeutic potential across diverse neurological and psychiatric conditions, the widespread clinical adoption of tFUS requires comprehensive safety evaluation and resolution of methodological controversies that have emerged during clinical translation. These findings are supported by extensive safety databases spanning diverse clinical populations and technical platforms. Systematic analyses have consistently demonstrated excellent tolerability profiles across multiple studies, with comprehensive reviews confirming that clinical parameters operate well within established safety margins [20–22]. Current evidence supports the safety of low-intensity protocols while highlighting the need for standardized approaches to address auditory artifacts that may confound interpretation of direct neural effects.

Safety profile and tissue effects

Clinical and preclinical studies consistently demonstrate the safety of tFUS when applied within established parameter guidelines. A retrospective analysis of 120 healthy volunteers participating in tFUS research found no serious adverse events, with only 7 cases reporting mild to moderate symptoms possibly related to stimulation—rates comparable to other non-invasive brain stimulation modalities [51]. Pathological assessments following tFUS treatment in epilepsy patients showed no significant tissue damage, and high-dose ultrasound application to autopsy brain tissue revealed no abnormal histological changes [186]. Extensive animal studies across multiple species further support safety profiles, with histological examinations consistently showing no evidence of tissue damage, hemorrhage, or inflammatory responses when appropriate parameters are used.

Parameter-dependent safety thresholds have emerged from systematic investigations. While high-pressure tFUS (>1.6 MPa) can cause brain tissue damage with local depolarization and epileptiform activity in rodents [187], clinically relevant parameters operating below FDA safety limits (0.72 W/cm²) show excellent safety profiles. Recent clinical studies using precise MR thermometry demonstrate that therapeutic low-power tFUS induces no significant temperature elevation during applications [92], supporting non-thermal mechanisms of action at clinically relevant intensities.

Auditory confounding effects and mitigation strategies

A significant methodological challenge in tFUS research involves distinguishing direct neural effects from indirect auditory pathway activation. Studies indicate that conventional tFUS pulse protocols may activate auditory cortex through cochlear stimulation, with activation patterns spatially and temporally similar to acoustic stimulation [188, 189]. This cochlear-dependent activation challenges interpretations of direct neuromodulatory effects, particularly for studies targeting regions with auditory connectivity. Importantly, transcranial ultrasound produces audible sounds detectable by most human participants, with conventional pulse protocols being clearly audible to all subjects in controlled studies [190].

Several approaches have been developed to address both auditory and somatosensory confounds [191]. Masking noise effectively eliminates ultrasound-induced auditory perception and auditory evoked potentials [192]. Smoothly enveloped ultrasound waveforms avoid auditory brainstem responses while preserving therapeutic effects, indicating that neuromodulatory outcomes do not depend on auditory activation [18]. Validation studies using chemically deafened animal models confirm that ultrasound-induced responses occur independently of peripheral auditory pathway integrity [187]. These findings support the existence of direct neural effects while emphasizing the importance of appropriate controls and parameter selection to minimize auditory confounds.

Methodological considerations for clinical applications

The resolution of safety and methodological concerns requires systematic approaches to protocol design and evaluation. Future studies should incorporate comprehensive auditory control conditions, optimize ultrasound parameters to minimize acoustic artifacts, and employ refined neuroimaging methods to distinguish direct from indirect effects. The development of patient-specific parameter optimization, based on individual skull properties and target anatomy, may further enhance both safety and efficacy profiles.

Long-term safety assessment remains a priority, particularly for repeated treatment protocols in clinical populations. While current evidence supports the safety of established parameters, systematic evaluation across diverse patient populations and extended treatment regimens will be essential for clinical translation. The establishment of standardized safety monitoring protocols will facilitate the continued development of tFUS as a mainstream therapeutic modality [193]. With safety foundations established, the field can now explore advanced applications that build upon these validated clinical and safety frameworks.

Emerging applications of tFUS in the field of neuromodulation

With established clinical applications and validated safety profiles, tFUS technology is expanding into novel research domains. Recent advances demonstrate its potential in BBI, brain-computer interface (BCI) enhancement, anesthesia management, and advanced neuroimaging applications.

In the field of BBI, researchers have pioneered the establishment of cross-species brain functional connectivity using tFUS and EEG technology. By decoding human brain motor intentions and transmitting them to rodents, and then using tFUS to stimulate their motor cortex, researchers have successfully established real-time control of animal movements by the human brain [28]. Subsequent research has further expanded the application of tFUS in transmitting tactile information between humans [28]. These groundbreaking studies highlight the potential of tFUS-mediated BBI for enabling cross-individual information transmission and facilitating collaborative tasks.

The combination of tFUS with brain functional imaging techniques is another important advancement in neuromodulation research. By integrating tFUS with EEG, fMRI, and other modalities, researchers can monitor the dynamic changes of brain networks while modulating brain activity. For example, tFUS stimulation of the visual motion cortex (V5) can improve the performance of visual motion-based BCI and is accompanied by enhanced attentional modulation of the dorsal pathway [194]. Using EEG source imaging techniques, researchers have also found that tFUS can induce unique neural oscillation patterns in specific brain regions, providing a new approach for non-invasive monitoring of deep brain activity [194]. The combination of tFUS with fMRI also helps to reveal the brain network mechanisms of ultrasound neuromodulation and blood-brain barrier opening. The application of these multimodal research paradigms greatly expands the depth and breadth of tFUS in basic and applied research.

In the field of clinical translation, the modulation of brain function by tFUS under anesthesia is a new breakthrough. Relevant studies have found that tFUS stimulation of key brain regions such as the dorsal raphe nucleus and thalamus can significantly shorten the time for subjects to recover from general anesthesia and improve postoperative cognitive function [195, 196]. Mechanistic studies suggest that tFUS may accelerate the anesthesia recovery process by modulating neurotransmitter levels (e.g., 5-HT) and brain network connectivity characteristics [195, 196]. This finding not only provides a new tool for perioperative brain state management but also offers new clues for the application of tFUS in disorders of consciousness and other fields.

In animal behavioral research, tFUS has also made significant progress. Researchers have developed miniaturized, multi-channel tFUS stimulation devices that can achieve precise stimulation of multiple brain regions in awake, freely moving rodents [197]. Using this system, researchers found that tFUS stimulation of the medial thalamus can dose-dependently enhance locomotor activity in rats, while stimulation of control brain regions had no such effect [197]. This technological breakthrough provides a more flexible and efficient tool for studying the behavioral and cognitive effects of tFUS, and is expected to accelerate the application and translation of tFUS in animal models.

Overall, these emerging applications highlight the rapidly expanding possibilities for tFUS technology, from basic research to sophisticated clinical interventions and advanced brain interface paradigms. From cross-individual brain functional connectivity to precise modulation of brain network activity, and from perioperative brain state management to animal behavior manipulation, tFUS technology has shown considerable promise in various aspects. These emerging applications represent the future trajectory of tFUS technology, building upon the established mechanistic understanding, validated clinical applications, and confirmed safety profiles reviewed in this comprehensive analysis.

Summary and outlook

This comprehensive review reveals that tFUS has evolved from an experimental tool to a promising therapeutic modality with demonstrated mechanisms spanning molecular to network levels, as confirmed by extensive clinical databases and safety analyses [21, 22, 26]. Our temporal mechanistic framework highlights the critical distinction between immediate online effects during stimulation and sustained offline changes persisting 30–60 min beyond stimulation cessation. This temporal distinction, combined with parameter-dependent bidirectional modulation and the technology’s unique ability to achieve precise modulation of both cortical and subcortical structures, provides the foundation for rational therapeutic protocol design and distinguishes tFUS from existing neuromodulation approaches.

Key scientific advances

Building upon comprehensive foundational reviews addressing clinical safety, systematic applications, and mechanistic understanding [20, 24, 198], this work examined temporal aspects of tFUS mechanisms involving mechanosensitive ion channels, with effects cascading through cellular excitability changes to network-level modifications. The observed distinction between immediate online effects during stimulation and sustained offline changes persisting 30–60 min beyond stimulation cessation, combined with parameter-dependent bidirectional modulation, may inform considerations for therapeutic protocol design.

Functionally, tFUS demonstrates reliable effects across motor, cognitive, emotional, and sensory systems, with the strongest evidence in motor and somatosensory domains. Clinical applications show particular promise in movement disorders, where essential tremor studies achieve up to 98% tremor reduction, demonstrating clear therapeutic efficacy.

Critical challenges and solutions

Significant translational challenges exist between animal models and human applications. While rodent studies achieve 70–100% response rates, human applications show more variable 35–70% success rates, reflecting fundamental differences in brain organization and acoustic transmission properties. Human skulls attenuate acoustic intensity by 65–90%, necessitating species-specific parameter optimization.

Individual variability remains a key limitation, with substantial differences in treatment response highlighting the need for personalized approaches. Methodological concerns regarding auditory confounding effects require systematic implementation of control measures, though established solutions including masking and waveform optimization are available.

Safety profiles are encouraging, with extensive studies demonstrating excellent tolerability within established guidelines. However, most clinical evidence derives from small-scale studies, emphasizing the need for larger randomized controlled trials and standardized protocols across applications.

Future directions

Technological innovations including closed-loop systems and AI-guided parameter optimization offer solutions to individual variability through real-time adaptation. Critical research priorities include systematic parameter optimization, development of predictive biomarkers, and standardization of protocols across applications.

tFUS demonstrates spatial precision and subcortical accessibility that may complement existing neuromodulation techniques. The temporal aspects of tFUS effects examined here, along with developing closed-loop technologies and parameter optimization approaches, suggest potential for refined therapeutic applications. Further investigation of temporal dynamics may contribute to protocol development for clinical applications where current treatments show limitations.
