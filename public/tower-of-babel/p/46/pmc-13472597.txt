Genetic Perspective on the Relationship between Female Reproductive Traits, Exercise, and Bone Health Outcomes: Evidence from a National Database
Authors: Bo-Kai Chen, Jansen Marcos Cambia, Chien-An Shih, Meng-Che Tsai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionFemale reproductive traits influence bone mineral density (BMD) and fracture risks through estrogen exposure. However, these causal relationships remain unclear due to confounding. Understanding these relationships is crucial for osteoporosis prevention strategies. This study aimed to investigate the causal impact of age at natural menopause (ANM) and age at menarche (AAM) on BMD and osteoporosis risk, and to explore gene-exercise interactions influencing bone health in East Asian women.MethodsWe conducted two-sample Mendelian randomization (MR) using 15 genetic variants for AAM and 36 variants for ANM in 51,049 women from the Taiwan Biobank, split into discovery (n = 25,549) and validation (n = 25,500) cohorts. Outcomes were BMD Z scores, T scores, and clinical osteoporosis. We performed univariable and multivariable MR, followed by regression analyses, focusing on exercise effects on BMD and genetic predisposition scores for AAM and ANM.ResultsIn multivariable MR, genetically determined later ANM was associated with higher BMD Z scores (β = 0.045, 95% confidence interval [0.021, 0.069]), higher T scores (β = 0.046, [0.020, 0.072]), and lower osteoporosis risk (β = −0.106, [−0.154, −0.058]). No consistent causal effect was observed for AAM due to genetic pleiotropy. ANM polygenic risk score and regular exercise were independently associated with improved BMD Z- and T scores and reduced risk of osteoporosis. Specific gene-exercise interactions were identified in which the AAM polygenic risk score interacted significantly with gymnastics, while the ANM polygenic risk score interacted with lower-limb exercise and swimming.ConclusionLate ANM provides causal protection against osteoporosis in East Asian women, while the effects of AAM remain insignificant. Exercise benefits bone health with activity-specific genetic interactions. These findings support personalized osteoporosis prevention strategies incorporating both genetic risk assessment and targeted exercise recommendations, particularly for women with early menopause predisposition.

Introduction

Puberty is a critical period for bone growth and mineral accumulation, during which most of a woman’s peak bone mass (PBM) is attained shortly after menarche, typically within 2–4 years post menarche [1, 2]. In puberty, sex hormones, especially estrogen in females, have an essential influence on bone growth [3], mainly by activating osteoblasts and inhibiting osteoclasts through multiple remodeling pathways, including suppression of interleukin (IL)-1, tumor necrosis factor, and IL-6, promotion of osteoclast apoptosis, and regulation of RANK/RANKL signaling [4]. PBM is therefore considered an important determinant of lifelong bone strength and fracture risk, as higher PBM provides greater skeletal reserve to offset postmenopausal and age-related bone loss, delaying osteoporosis [1, 5, 6].

Age at menarche (AAM) is an important milestone in sexual maturation and social maturity status [7]. In mid-to-late puberty, once sufficiently high and sustained estrogen exposure builds an unstable uterus endometrium, which leads to the first menstrual bleeding without luteal progesterone [8]. After this transition, females may experience several decades of cyclic menstrual periods until the age of natural menopause (ANM), at which estrogen levels undergo a significant decline [9]. The interval between AAM and ANM, known as the reproductive lifespan, varies across individuals and serves as an indicator of cumulative endogenous estrogen exposure. Estrogen is widely recognized as protective for bone health because longer exposure reduces bone resorption and helps preserve bone mass, making reproductive lifespan an important determinant of osteoporosis risk [10]. In contrast, the postmenopausal estrogen drop accelerates bone loss [3, 6, 11, 12], and early ANM is therefore considered a critical risk factor for lower bone mineral density (BMD) and osteoporosis [13, 14]. Late AAM has also been associated with reduced BMD and increased fracture risk [1, 8, 10, 15–20], although results are inconsistent, as some observational studies report no significant relationship [21–23]. These discrepancies may reflect residual confounding due to lifestyle, socioeconomic factors, or population stratification. Thus, careful consideration of confounding is essential when investigating how reproductive traits, such as AAM and ANM, influence lifelong bone health.

Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) are widely regarded as the gold standard for causal inference because random allocation balances both measured and unmeasured confounders, leaving any remaining differences largely due to chance. However, the implementation of RCTs in epidemiological research is often constrained by ethical concerns, logistical challenges, and high costs. As an alternative, Mendelian randomization (MR) has gained increasing attention [24]. MR exploits the random assortment of alleles during meiosis as a natural experiment, which mimics the randomization process of RCTs and reduces the risk of confounding and reverse causation [24, 25]. In this framework, genetic variants, commonly single-nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), are selected as instrumental variables (IVs) because they influence outcomes only through the exposure of interest and are assumed not to affect other traits with downstream consequences [26]. This approach has been applied to investigate bone health, with previous MR studies demonstrating that late AAM [10, 19] and early ANM [19, 27] are causally associated with greater osteoporosis risk. Nonetheless, most studies have focused on individual traits, without addressing how multiple reproductive factors might act jointly. More recently, multivariable MR (MVMR) has been proposed as an extension that incorporates genetic variants for several related exposures simultaneously, thereby allowing researchers to disentangle the independent effects of each trait on a given outcome [28, 29].

Several environmental factors play a crucial role in preserving BMD and preventing osteoporosis and fractures. Before pharmacotherapy, such as bisphosphonates [30] or calcium plus vitamin D supplementation [31], maintaining physical activity [32] is often recommended, and optimizing PBM during puberty is a key strategy to mitigate age-related BMD loss and fracture risk [1]. Although genetic factors account for 60–80% of PBM variation, environmental and behavioral factors (20–40%) [1] remain important as modifiable targets for precision medicine. Exercise interventions, particularly weight-bearing activities during peripubertal bone development, are essential for attaining optimal PBM [1] and delaying osteoporosis onset [33], partly through Wnt/β-catenin signaling, which promotes osteoblast activity [34], while exercise-induced mechanical loading downregulates sclerostin and enhances BMD [35]. Resistance training is especially effective [35–37], though activities with insufficient mechanical loading (e.g., swimming [38, 39], casual walking [40, 41], and most yoga [42]) show limited benefit. Studies on gene-environment (G × E) interactions further suggest that exercise can modify genetic risk for obesity [36, 43], and potentially bone health, but findings on gene–exercise effects on BMD are mixed: one US cohort found associations at the variant level but not at the polygenic score level [44], while a UK Biobank analysis linked leisure physical activity to osteoporosis protection without G × E effects [45]. Since exercise effects may differ by race and research is limited in East Asian populations, identifying optimal exercise strategies for women of East Asian descent is essential for improving bone health.

Comprising healthy individuals from the general Taiwanese population [46], the Taiwan Biobank (TWB) database offers a wealth of genomic data and lifestyle information specifically for East Asian populations. The adoption of both univariable and multivariable MR methods enables the assessment of individual and combined effects of reproductive traits on bone health. This approach, which uses IVs, mitigates confounding through random allocation, given that the G × E interactions between female reproductive genes and physical activities are a fundamental aspect of precision medicine for osteoporosis. Consequently, we aimed to recapitulate this G × E interaction on the nationwide data in the Taiwanese population. This study aims to determine whether regular exercise habits or specific types of sports can attenuate or amplify the effects of genetically determined AAM or ANM on bone-related phenotypes, thereby providing insights for optimizing personalized approaches to bone health.

Materials and Methods

Data Source

TWB collects genomic profiles, lifestyle patterns, and dietary habits of Taiwan residents. The database of TWB has an ongoing cohort of nearly 200,000 individuals from the general Taiwanese population with no history of cancer [46]. In this project, we received 648,611 autosomal SNPs from TWB chips on the Axiom Genome-Wide Array Plate System (Affymetrix, Santa Clara, CA, USA), and lifestyle information from self-report questionnaires of 88,347 individuals. Since TWB only collected individuals with East Asian ancestry [46], all 88,347 individuals were highly overlapped with Han Chinese in Beijing, Han Chinese in South China, and Japanese in Tokyo in the 1000 Genomes Project [47]. Therefore, the cohort data can provide a unique and representative look at the genetic and health traits, allowing for a deep understanding of health patterns and both protective and risk factors for disease within East Asian populations.

Participants self-reported their AAM and ANM. As for exercise habits, structured interviews assessed participants’ exercise habits by asking whether they engaged in regular physical activity (defined as at least three times per week for >30 min each session), with responses recorded in the questionnaire. Those with a positive answer were then asked to list their three most frequently performed exercises, along with the frequency and duration of each [36]. Using this information, we calculated each participant’s weekly metabolic equivalent of tasks (METs/week) by applying standard conversion formulas corresponding to each exercise type [48]. The conversion formula we used to align prior research on TWB has been described elsewhere [49]. For analytical purposes, we also regrouped exercise types based on intensity, such as clustering jogging, power walking, rope jumping, stair climbing, and hiking into a single category characterized by activities in which both feet potentially leave the ground simultaneously, resulting in a forceful impact upon landing [50]. BMD was collected by calcaneal quantitative ultrasound (QUS) of the heel and then transformed into T and Z scores. Although dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry is the gold standard clinical diagnosis of osteoporosis, its results in older women are susceptible to degenerative changes. In particular, lumbar spine degeneration may artificially elevate BMD, leading to misinterpretation and potential underdiagnosis of osteoporosis [51]. Given that this study primarily evaluates BMD in postmenopausal women, such bias should be carefully considered. On the contrary, calcaneal QUS is a valuable alternative, especially for large-scale screenings, as it assesses peripheral sites unaffected by spinal degeneration, thereby circumventing this confounding effect. In terms of diagnostic performance, calcaneal QUS demonstrates 100% specificity and positive predictive value for detecting abnormal BMD, with overall accuracy exceeding 90% [52]. Accordingly, QUS devices are more portable, less expensive, and do not use ionizing radiation; therefore, they offer a safer and more practical way to assess bone health in a broader population [53, 54]. BMD Z scores are sex-, ethnicity-, and age-matched BMD references appropriate for younger individuals and premenopausal adults. At the same time, BMD T scores compare a person’s BMD to the PBM of a healthy young adult (around 30 years old), and BMD T scores are primarily a tool used for osteoporosis diagnosis in postmenopausal women and men over the age of 50 years [55]. Therefore, we defined osteoporosis as having a T score of <−2.5 [50].

Quality Control

First, we excluded SNPs and individuals with missingness greater than 0.02. Sex discrepancies were assessed using the X-chromosome inbreeding coefficient (F value), with individuals excluded if F > 0.2 in females or F < 0.8 in males. Only the autosomal SNPs were kept, and SNPs with a low minor allele frequency < 0.05 were deleted. To deal with the cryptic relatedness, individuals with identity by descent greater than 0.2 were filtered out. In the paired set, one with a higher missing rate was deleted, and the other was retained, prioritizing data quality and minimizing information loss [56]. A detailed process has been described elsewhere [57]. After quality control, 75,786 individuals and 278,366 SNPs remained. The 75,786 individuals were then randomly split into two groups. Since female-related traits were our main variables of interest, male individuals in the two groups were excluded. We employed the multidimensional scaling (MDS) approach, anchored by the 1000 Genomes Project data. The threshold for non-East Asian ethnic outliers was set for MDS 1 of >−0.05 and MDS 2 of >−0.025 after visualization of the MDS plot, as this is the criterion separating East Asian from other ethnic groups (online suppl. Fig. 1; for all online suppl. material, see https://doi.org/10.1159/000553353). Given the lack of comparable population data and the population-specific nature of genetic signals, such as SNP minor allele frequencies that may not be shared even among closely related ethnic groups, we randomly split the entire TWB population into two equally sized subsets for analysis, a method previously applied in MR research using TWB data [58]. Hence, we obtained 25,549 females in subset 1 for exploring genetic variants associated with female-related traits and 25,500 in subset 2 for examining the causal relationship based on a two-sample framework.

MR Analytic Strategy

To test the causality between female reproductive phenotypes and bone health outcomes, we selected AAM and ANM as exposures and three BMD-related traits, including T scores, Z scores, and osteoporosis, as outcomes. Initially, genome-wide association studies (GWASs) were performed with subset 1, with the top ten latent dimensions of MDS incorporated as covariates to identify significant SNPs associated with AAM and ANM, respectively. To ensure the validity of the IVs, only SNPs yielding F-statistics greater than 10 were imported into the MR analysis and used to build polygenic risk scores (PRSs) for AAM (PRSAAM) and ANM (PRSANM). Considering that a single SNP may have small effects and limited predictive power, we introduced PRS to aggregate the effects of all the selective risk alleles, weighted by the effect size derived from the GWAS summary statistics [59, 60]. For each SNPi, we utilized the posterior probability ωij of observing genotype j(j∈{0, 1, 2}) to calculate an expected dosage. This dosage was then weighted by the corresponding effect size βi from the discovery GWAS summary statistics from subset1 [60]. PRSice-2 was introduced to construct individual PRS of subset2, and the formula used in the software follows:PRS=∑imβi∑j=02ωij×j

Since MR studies require at least 10 IVs to perform a reliable analysis [61, 62], we chose a more relaxed p value (5 × 10−6) [63], along with 10 kb for window size and 0.001 for the R2 of the linkage disequilibrium with PRSice-2 [60], as the clumping threshold to keep the IVs independent from each other. The inverse variance weighted (IVW) method was primarily used to analyze the causal relationship between female-related traits as exposures and BMD-related outcomes. Because the IVW method assumes all genetic instruments are valid or that average pleiotropy is zero, it may be biased in the presence of horizontal pleiotropy [64]. To test robustness, we additionally applied weighted median, MR-Egger, and maximum likelihood methods. Weighted median provides consistent estimates if at least 50% of the weight derives from valid instruments and improves type I error control, though it is less efficient than IVW and sensitive to outliers [65]. MR-Egger accounts for directional pleiotropy but has lower power and is sensitive to assumption violations [64]. Maximum likelihood was included for robustness to weak instruments and pleiotropy, but given its reliance on parametric assumptions and susceptibility to model misspecification, it was not treated as the primary result [66]. Furthermore, heterogeneity was appraised by Cochran’s Q and I2 statistics. The intercept test of MR-Egger was used to examine directional horizontal pleiotropy, while more MR pleiotropy residual sum and outlier (MR-PRESSO) [67] and MR least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (MR-LASSO) [68] were performed to control overall horizontal pleiotropy. In MR-PRESSO, instruments with p < 0.05 were treated as outliers and removed to address pleiotropy. In MR-LASSO, the tuning parameter was set as the largest value yielding a non-significant Cochran’s Q statistic (p ≥ 0.05) for residual heterogeneity. Additionally, a leave-one-out analysis was employed and visualized to determine whether a particular SNP impacted the overall MR estimate [69]. Subsequently, we employed the multivariable MR analysis to analyze the joint effects of these female reproductive traits on BMD-related outcomes. Detailed reporting items and specific study characteristics required by the guidelines are elaborated in the STROBE-MR checklist [70] (online suppl. Table 1). R (version 4.4.1) and the R packages MendelianRandomization (version 0.10.0), MRPRESSO (version 1.0), and MVMR (version 0.4) were used to perform the MR statistical studies mentioned above [71].

Individual-Level Causal Relationship and Gene-Exercise Interaction Analysis

In addition to the summary statistics-based MR methods, we conducted the two-stage least squares (2SLS) regression analysis to estimate the causal effects using individual data, which allowed for further examination of the moderating role of exercise in the causal relationship. To evaluate the moderating effect of physical activity, we defined the gene-exercise interactions as the PRSAAM or PRSANM multiplied by each participant’s weekly metabolic equivalent of tasks (METs/week) [36, 43, 48]. In regression models, we incorporated PRSAAM, PRSANM, exercise, and gene-exercise interactions as independent variables. At the same time, bone phenotypes were defined as dependent variables. Continuous outcomes, such as BMD Z and T scores, were analyzed using linear regression (ordinary least squares, OLS), whereas osteoporosis (defined as case = 1 and control = 0), a binary outcome, was examined using logistic regression within a generalized linear model with a logit link function. As such, a positive interaction term in a regression model means that the effect of exercise on the outcome becomes more positive (or less negative) among individuals who are genetically predisposed to a later AAM or ANM. Lastly, we conducted stratified analyses by exercise type to identify specific activities that may offer protective effects against osteoporosis. Considering the confounding effects from the literature, all regression analyses were adjusted for the following covariates: age, height, weight, body fat percentage, fasting glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, low-density cholesterol, high-density cholesterol, cigarette smoking, alcohol consumption, tea drinking, coffee drinking, diabetes, type 2 diabetes, pregnancy times, number of childbirths, natural abortion times, and breast milk times [4, 72]. An alpha value was set at 0.05 as the threshold for statistical significance in the regression analyses.

Results

IV Selection and Study Population Characteristics

In subset 1, used for searching for IVs, we identified 15 SNPs associated with AAM and 36 SNPs associated with ANM, which were subsequently used as IVs in the univariable and multivariable MR analyses (online suppl. Table 2). These selected IVs explained 0.16% of AAM and 2.2% of ANM, respectively. In subset 2, used to explore the causal relationships, the average age of the participants was 51.1 years, with an average AAM of 13.3 years and ANM of 59.8 years, which were not different from those in subset 1 (online suppl. Table 3). Except for 10 individuals with unavailable data on exercise type, 9,983 (39.15%) participants reported engaging in at least one type of regular exercise.

Univariable MR Analysis

In the univariable MR analysis (online suppl. Table 4), genetically determined AAM was not associated with bone health outcomes, except for a positive association with BMD T scores (β = 0.434, 95% confidence interval [0.069, 0.799]) based on MR-Egger, suggesting that a later AAM is associated with higher BMD T scores in adulthood. Worth of attention, weak horizontal pleiotropy might exist in the effects of AAM on T scores (MR-Egger intercept; p = 0.005, MR-PRESSO global test; p = 0.065) even after we performed MR-LASSO analysis to penalize SNPs with possible horizontal pleiotropy effect (online suppl. Table 5). On the contrary, the MR analysis consistently showed a significant association between genetically determined ANM and bone health outcomes across different methods, except MR-Egger and 2SLS.

Multivariable MR Analysis

In the multivariable MR analysis integrating both AAM and ANM (Table 1), genetically determined ANM remained associated with BMD Z scores (β = 0.045, 95% confidence interval [0.021, 0.069]), BMD T scores (β = 0.046, 95% confidence interval [0.020, 0.072]), and reduced risk of osteoporosis (β = −0.106, 95% confidence interval [−0.154, −0.058]), but not the genetically determined AAM.

Gene-Exercise Interaction Analysis

Regarding gene-exercise interaction, no significant association was observed between the PRS of female-related traits and overall regular exercise habits for any of the three BMD-related traits, even after we stratified exercise habits by intensity according to the 2020 guideline of the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) [73] (Tables 2, 3). Despite this, when stratifying the analysis on osteoporosis by exercise type (Table 4), significant gene-exercise interactions were identified between PRSAAM and gymnastics (β = 0.119, S.E. = 0.057, p = 3.74E−02), as well as between PRSANM and both lower-limb exercise (including jogging, power walking, rope jumping, stair climbing, and hiking) (β = 0.054, S.E. = 0.024, p = 2.42E−02) and swimming (β = −0.166, S.E. = 0.064, p = 9.54E−03).

Discussion

Using the MR analytic framework on a nationwide cohort, we re-evaluated the relationship between female reproductive traits and bone health outcomes. This study found that genetically determined late ANM was consistently associated with increased BMD and reduced osteoporosis risk, while evidence for AAM effects was limited. As for G × E interactions, three kinds of gene-exercise interactions between female-reproductive-related PRS and physical activity were identified, including gymnastics (with AAM), lower-limb exercise (with ANM), and swimming (with ANM).

Our findings align with previous MR studies showing the protective effects of late menopause on bone health [19, 27]. However, unlike some observational studies [15–18], our MR analysis found limited evidence for AAM effects on BMD. Nevertheless, the causal relationship between AAM and BMD must be examined with caution. In univariable MR analysis, the MR-Egger intercept test for directional horizontal pleiotropy [74] and the MR-PRESSO global test for overall horizontal pleiotropic variants [67, 75]. Our univariate MR analysis of AAM on BMD T score found directional horizontal pleiotropy but no overall horizontal pleiotropic variants. This evidence warrants cautious interpretation of the causal estimate, consistent with methodological warnings that unbalanced pleiotropy can bias MR results [67, 76].

Estrogen has been known to possess a protective effect on the bone, including suppressing the production of IL-1 and tumor necrosis factor, indirectly inhibiting the expression of interleukin-6 (IL-6) [77], directly promoting the apoptosis of mature osteoclasts, activating osteoprotegerin, and inhibiting the gene activity of RANK and RANKL [4]. Because late AAM and early ANM will lead to shorter estrogen exposure in postmenopausal women, the two female-related traits are usually considered to be risk factors for lower BMD. In other studies [8, 10, 15–19], late AAM, which might lead to a shorter reproductive lifespan and, therefore, less estrogen exposure, was associated with lower BMD and may eventually increase the risk of osteoporosis. In our MVMR analysis, a non-significant protective causal relationship between early AAM and BMD-related traits, but a significant one between late ANM and BMD-related traits was revealed. Our MVMR result was similar to other studies to perform MR analysis of multiple female-related traits, including AAM, ANM, age at first live birth, and age at last live birth on BMD, and only to found late ANM was the most dominant female reproductive phenotypes to benefits bone health, while the effect of early AAM on BMD was not significantly after using MVMR to adjust potential interaction among female reproductive traits [19]. Since estrogen loss at menopause is a primary driver of accelerated bone loss in women [9], the result of MVMR analysis, which includes female reproductive traits, may imply that the causal effect of genetically determined AAM on BMD is more subtle than genetically determined ANM or even overridden by it. However, whether ANM is more proximal to bone loss and therefore physiologically potent for adult BMD than puberty timing remains to be investigated further.

Our study found that regular exercise independently improved BMD, with specific gene-exercise interactions varying by activity type. In our OLS regression model, we observed that regular exercise habits significantly increased BMD T score and BMD Z score; however, the negative association between regular exercise habits and osteoporosis was not statistically significant. One of the well-known mechanisms by which regular exercise improves BMD is that mechanical loading during exercise stimulates secondary bone healing. When osteochondral progenitor cells receive a messenger from the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, they transform into osteoblasts, which are responsible for the synthesis and mineralization of bone during bone formation. In the Wnt/β-catenin signaling pathway, β-catenin enters the nucleus and stimulates mesenchymal stem cells to differentiate into cartilage stem/progenitor cells. Sclerostin, secreted by osteocytes, is a Wnt antagonist that inhibits osteoblast differentiation and activates osteoclasts. In mechanical loading, the downregulation of Sclerostin led to an increase in osteoprogenitor cells, and osteoblast apoptosis was inhibited. Therefore, it can prevent and reverse age-related skeletal structural deterioration [34, 35]. Several types of exercise exert a mechanical loading effect. For example, resistance exercise training has been found to improve BMD [35–37].

While no overall gene-exercise interaction was found for general regular exercise habits, similar to the previous US cohort study [44], our OLS regression model revealed specific gene-exercise interactions between female-related PRS and certain types of regular exercise concerning osteoporosis, which is not aligned with the UK Biobank study [45]. Specifically, PRSAAM showed an interaction with gymnastics in the Taiwanese population, whereas PRSANM was found to interact with lower-limb exercise and swimming. Considering the directionality of the PRS-exercise type interactions, a negative interaction coefficient between the PRSANM and swimming suggested that swimming has a stronger protective effect against osteoporosis among individuals genetically prone to a later ANM. On the contrary, the protective effects of lower-limb exercise and gymnastics appeared to be attenuated among individuals with a genetic predisposition to later ANM and AAM, respectively. Given the existence of gene-exercise interaction effects between specific types of regular exercises and PRS of female-related traits on osteoporosis, we can tentatively conclude that genes determining AAM or ANM in the Taiwanese population are sensitive to exercise-related environmental influences. We believe that this result may further support the potential value of individualizing regular physical activity according to their genetic predisposition of AAM and ANM during their early life stages to improve their maximum BMD and benefit their bone structure in later life [33, 78, 79].

While the SNPs used to generate PRSANM were sporadic and dispersed across different genomic regions, 6 out of 15 SNPs used to construct PRSAAM were located near LIN28B, a pubertal timing-associated gene [80–83]. In humans, LIN28B is highly expressed in the hypothalamus and pituitary, key components of the hypothalamic-pituitary axis that regulate growth and puberty [80]. A prior study of genotype-tissue expression project data found that later AAM was associated with higher transcript levels of LIN28B in the pituitary [84]. In zebrafish, LIN28B expression correlated positively with hypothalamic ESR1, which encodes the estrogen receptor alpha (ERα) protein, and pituitary POMC expression [80]. Moreover, ERα knockout in the medial basal hypothalamus increases BMD in female mice via Kiss1 neurons [85]. Taken together, LIN28B and ESR1 expression in the hypothalamus provide a plausible mechanistic link between LIN28B variation and its associations with puberty and BMD through a central estrogen pathway [80]. Future investigations are needed to elucidate how exercise may interact with and modulate this pathway in relation to bone health.

There might be some potential limitations in this study. In the GWAS analysis, we randomly split the data into the exploration and validation subsets. This approach reduced the effective sample size, potentially leading to lower statistical power and greater uncertainty in the estimates, as compared to prior research [19]. Translating an individual's PRS from a percentile of a normal distribution to a lifetime disease risk predictor is still challenging [86]. Notably, our PRSAAM can only explain 0.16% of genetically determined AAM. The low proportion of variance explained in the exposure represents a limitation, suggesting that inferences about observed AAM based on genetically determined AAM may lack precision. The F-statistics were well over 20, so weak instrument bias was less likely to be an issue affecting effect estimates. However, low explained variance may reduce the statistical power to detect a true association. Therefore, the null finding may reflect either the absence of a causal effect or insufficient sensitivity to identify a modest but real effect. Exercise enhances BMD with a more pronounced effect in pre-puberty compared to during or after puberty [1, 87]. Therefore, if the personalized exercise intervention advice is given after menarche, we may miss the optimal time for obtaining a substantial benefit from it. Still, there might be some limitations to this study. Since TWB collects data on the Taiwan population, the effects derived from our gene-exercise interaction results may not be suitable for other races. Moreover, data on female reproductive phenotypes and physical activity from the TWB were mainly self-reported. No medical records or biological measures were available to verify the exact timing of AAM or ANM, and participants' recall of their exercise habits may not accurately reflect their actual behaviors. Finally, we use QUS, instead of dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, which is the gold standard for clinical diagnosis of BMD, to assess BMD, so there might be a deviation from the real bone state.

Conclusion

Using MR on TWB data, this study demonstrates that genetically determined late ANM significantly protects against osteoporosis in East Asian women, while the effects of AAM remain inconclusive due to pleiotropy. Regular exercise independently improves bone mineral density, with specific gene-exercise interactions identified for gymnastics with AAM and lower-limb exercise and swimming with ANM. These findings support personalized osteoporosis prevention strategies that integrate genetic risk assessment with targeted exercise recommendations, particularly during bone-building years.
