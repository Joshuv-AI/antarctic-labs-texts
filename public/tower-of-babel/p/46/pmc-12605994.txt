Introduction

Obturator hernia (OH) is a rare type of pelvic hernia caused by the protrusion of abdominal contents through the obturator canal. Known as the “little old lady's hernia,” it predominantly affects elderly, emaciated females, with an incidence of <1 % of all hernias and 0.05–1.4 % of small bowel obstructions [1]. Because the obturator foramen and canal are narrow, OH's have a high tendency to become incarcerated and strangulated [2].

Delayed diagnosis is common because clinical signs are often non-specific, which can lead to bowel ischemia requiring resection in up to 40 % of cases and mortality rates as high as 70 % when complicated by strangulation and bowel ischemia [1,3]. CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis is the most sensitive modality for detecting OH and any associated bowel compromise [4,5]. Management of this condition is primarily surgical.

The difficulty of preoperative diagnosis, combined with high surgical complication and mortality rates, continues to make obturator hernia a major challenge for surgeons. Here we present you a case of left sided OH in an elderly female diagnosed preoperatively on CT, managed with reduction, appendiceal decompression and preperitoneal mesh repair. This case report has been reported in line with the SCARE 2025 checklist [6].

Case presentation

Preoperative course

An 80 year old female with a BMI of 18 kg/m2 presented to the emergency department with a five days history of progressive abdominal distension and pain, multiple episodes of non-bilious vomiting and inability to pass stool or flatus. There was no history of fever, dizziness, or shortness of breath. There was no history of chronic constipation. She had no known history of hypertension, diabetes mellitus, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, cardiac illness, or prior abdominal surgeries. She was not on any regular medications and had no history of allergies to food or drugs. There was no history of smoking, alcohol, or substance use. Family history was non-contributory for hernias, bowel obstruction, or other significant gastrointestinal conditions. Socially, she lived with family, was independent in her activities of daily living prior to illness, and had no recent travel or infectious exposures.

On examination, the patient appeared ill, with a Glasgow Coma Scale of E4V5M6. Signs of dehydration were present. Her vital signs were stable: pulse 96 bpm, blood pressure 100/60 mmHg, respiratory rate 18/min, temperature 98 °F, and GRBS 70 mg/dL. Abdominal examination revealed distended tympanic abdomen with no bowel sounds, no tenderness and no clinically detectable external hernia (inguinal, femoral, umbilical). No signs of fluid thrill or shifting dullness were appreciated. Per rectal and per vaginal examinations were unremarkable.

Initial management included nil per oral (NPO) status, nasogastric tube placement on free drainage for decompression, IV fluids (DNS), and 16F foley's catheterization for input-output monitoring. Laboratory investigations revealed: Leukocytosis: WBC 17,300/mm3 with 94 % neutrophils, Hemoglobin: 10.2 g/dL, Platelets: 244,000/mm3, RFT and LFT were within normal limits. PT/INR: 15.4 s/1.35, ECG: Normal sinus rhythm. Plain X -ray abdomen showed dilated small bowel loops with valvulae conniventes and multiple air fluid levels. Ultrasound abdomen and pelvis showed dilated small bowel loops (3 cm diameter) and reduced IVC caliber, suggestive of dehydration.

On presentation the patient exhibited clinical features consistent with small bowel obstruction, including abdominal distension, vomiting, and obstipation. Based on initial evaluation, several differential diagnoses were considered; inguinal or femoral hernia (most common external hernias causing obstruction in elderly females, though no external bulge or tenderness was present), adhesive small bowel obstruction (less likely given the absence of prior abdominal surgery), volvulus (possible in elderly patients, though imaging did not show classic whirl signs), pelvic or gynecological masses (e.g., ovarian cyst/tumor; can mimic obstruction but no pelvic mass was detected on exam. The absence of obvious external hernias, negative Howship–Romberg and Hannington-Kiff signs, and nonspecific radiographic findings made clinical diagnosis challenging. Then a contrast-enhanced CT (CECT) scan of the abdomen and pelvis was done that provided definitive evidence of a left-sided obturator hernia with small bowel herniation with preserved enhancement. It revealed a defect measuring 11.7 × 10.9 × 7.2 mm in the left obturator foramen, with herniation of small bowel loops in between obturator externus and pectineus muscle (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). Proximal to the herniation, bowel loops were dilated with multiple air fluid levels with prominent valvulae conniventes and collapsed large bowel distally (Fig. 2). The herniated segment had normal enhancement, with no signs of strangulation. Given the imaging findings and clinical status, the patient was planned for emergency exploratory laparotomy and proceed.

Intraoperative findings and management

The patient underwent emergency laparotomy under epidural anesthesia, which was chosen over general anesthesia due to her advanced age, low BMI, and absence of sepsis or strangulation, to reduce respiratory complications and allow better pain control post operatively. A midline infra-umbilical incision was made, and the anterior abdominal wall was opened in layers. Approximately 500 mL of reactive peritoneal fluid was noted. A 10 × 6 mm defect over the left obturator foramen was identified, containing omentum and a segment of proximal ileum. Proximal bowel loops were dilated and contralateral side was normal. The herniated contents were then reduced. Upon reduction, herniated bowel loops appeared viable, confirmed by normal colour, sheen, peristalsis, and visible pulsating mesenteric vessels. To decompress the dilated bowel, an appendectomy was performed, and the small bowel was decompressed via the appendiceal orifice, avoiding enterotomy and its associated risk of contamination. Although not widely reported, appendiceal decompression can serve as a safe and effective method in cases where a large enterotomy is undesirable, especially in elderly patients. Then a left preperitoneal flap was created, and a 10 × 10 cm polypropylene mesh was secured covering all the hernial orifices on the left side with Prolene 2–0 (Fig. 3). Hemostasis was achieved. Closure was performed in layers using Vicryl 2–0 and PDS 1–0 for fascia, and the skin was stapled. A sterile dressing was applied.

Postoperative course and follow-up

The patient was shifted to the surgical ICU (SICU) for observation. She was kept nil per oral initially, maintained on intravenous fluids, and started on IV antibiotics (ceftriaxone and metronidazole). Epidural analgesia was continued for better post operative pain control. Incentive spirometry was initiated every 2 h. On postoperative day (POD) 2, following the return of bowel sounds and passage of flatus, oral intake was started gradually with sips of water, followed by liquid diet. Laboratory tests repeated on POD 2 showed declining leukocytosis.

She remained in the SICU for two days and was transferred to the general surgical ward thereafter, where she stayed for four days. Her epidural catheter was removed on POD 3 before shifting to ward. Her postoperative recovery was uneventful, and no postoperative complications were observed (Clavien–Dindo grade 0). She was discharged on POD 7 with instructions for regular wound dressing, outpatient follow up and suture removal on POD 10. At 6 month follow up, the patient remained asymptomatic with no evidence of recurrence or late complications. She had regained baseline functional status, expressed satisfaction with her recovery, and reported no restrictions in daily activities. Postoperative imaging was not performed, as the patient demonstrated stable recovery and complete resolution of symptoms during follow-up.

Discussion

Obturator hernia occurs when abdominal contents push through the obturator foramen and enter the obturator canal, a narrow passage about 2–3 cm long and 1 cm wide, located between the pubic bone and obturator membrane, through which obturator nerve and vessels pass [7]. The pathogenesis of formation of this hernia is classically described in 3 stages:

•Organ stage- where abdominal contents herniate into the sac causing signs and symptoms in this stage [8,9].

Obturator hernia, often referred as the “little old lady's hernia,” predominantly affects elderly, thin, multiparous women. This is attributed to age-related and pregnancy-related loss of the protective fat and lymphatic tissue that normally cushions the obturator foramen and canal, creating extra space around the obturator nerve and vessels, which facilitates herniation. Other predisposing factors include advanced age (mean age 78.8 years), low BMI or emaciation, a wider pelvic anatomy, and conditions that increase intra-abdominal pressure such as chronic constipation, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, ascites, and kyphoscoliosis [3,10,11].

Obturator hernias are more commonly found on the right side, as the sigmoid colon on the left is thought to offer some protection against hernia formation [5]. In a study by Schizas et al. (2021), where 146 patients were included, 40.1 %(n = 67), 29.9 %(n = 50) and 25.2 % (n = 21) suffered from a right, a left and bilateral OH, respectively. Occasionally OH may be occult, discovered incidentally during imaging or laparotomy or present subtly delaying diagnosis [3]. Therefore, they should be searched actively during imaging and intraoperatively in such cases. In our case, the OH was left-sided, an uncommon finding that reflects the variability of anatomical protection.

Obturator hernia often causes non-specific symptoms, making it hard to diagnose clinically. A high level of suspicion is needed, especially in patients with known risk factors. Based on the review by Schizas et al. and others, the most common presenting symptom was acute mechanical bowel obstruction, followed by abdominal pain; whereas Howship-Romberg sign (pain radiating along the medial thigh due to obturator nerve compression upon extension, adduction and internal rotation) which is pathognomic for OH was seen in ~56 % of cases concluding that the absence of this sign cannot safely exclude OH3. Another clinical sign which is more specific than Howship-Romberg sign is the Hannington-Kiff sign where the patellar reflex remains normal, but the thigh adductor reflex is absent due to compression of the obturator nerve, leading to weakness of the adductor muscles. Although this sign is more specific for obturator hernia, it is rarely observed [12,13]. No such signs could be elicited in our patient. Since the diagnosis of this condition cannot be completely relied upon clinical examination because of this non-specific presentation, imaging modality plays a critical role. CT scan of the abdomen and pelvis has been demonstrated as the most sensitive and specific imaging modality for OH detection [4]. CT typically reveals a herniated bowel loop passing through the obturator foramen, often seen between the pectineus and obturator muscles, and is particularly valuable in identifying strangulated or bilateral hernias [14,15]. Early studies by Megibow et al. demonstrated that CT enables accurate identification of incarcerated loops and reduces the risk of delayed intervention [16]. More recent reviews, including the systematic analysis by Schizas et al., reaffirm that preoperative CT is associated with lower mortality and improved surgical outcomes [3].Ultrasound, though occasionally used, has limited utility due to its inability to consistently visualize deep pelvic structures, and it may misinterpret obturator hernia as femoral hernia in thin elderly women [14]. Given CT diagnostic accuracy, rapid availability, and ability to detect associated complications such as bowel ischemia or bilateral hernias, CT imaging should be promptly employed in elderly, emaciated females presenting with unexplained signs of small bowel obstruction.

Recently, Kobayashi et al. (2024), in a retrospective cohort and systematic review involving 112 patients, evaluated non-invasive reduction techniques for incarcerated OH. They reported a markedly higher success rate with ultrasonography-assisted or guided reduction (76–80 %) compared to blind manual reduction (33 %). Furthermore, reductions attempted within 72 h of symptom onset were significantly more successful, with delays beyond this window correlating with higher emergency surgery and bowel resection rates. Although our patient presented beyond this optimal window, their findings underscore the importance of early recognition and timely non-invasive attempts, where feasible to reduce morbidity and avoid bowel ischemia [17].

Definitive management of OH is surgical and several approaches and techniques have been described in literature. Laparotomy remains the most frequently employed approach (73.3 %), particularly in emergency cases or where bowel compromise is suspected [3]. Our decision to proceed with an infraumbilical midline laparotomy aligns with this trend. Intraoperatively, we identified herniation of the ileum through the left obturator foramen with no signs of strangulation.

A unique aspect of our case is the use of appendectomy and decompression via the appendiceal orifice. While Schizas et al. documented enterotomy in a minority of cases requiring bowel decompression or resection [3], no prior report to our knowledge describes the use of appendiceal decompression in the absence of appendiceal pathology. This makes our approach potentially novel. It allowed safe decompression of distended proximal loops without full enterotomy, thus avoiding fecal contamination, a particularly advantageous strategy in elderly, frail patient where even minor complications can be serious. However, this method is not without concerns. There is always a small chance of stump leak or infection, and the decompression may not work well if the appendix lumen is too narrow. It is also not suitable in patients with appendicitis, suspected appendicular neoplasm, fibrosis or those who had a prior appendectomy. This means the technique can only be applied in very selective cases with healthy appendix and viable small bowel. While our experience was positive, one case alone is not enough to recommend it widely. More reports and studies are to require to assess its safety, reproducibility and long term outcomes before this can be considered a standard option. We also performed preperitoneal mesh repair, consistent with studies cited in the review by Schizas et al., showing mesh use leads to lower recurrence rates without increasing postoperative complications [3]. Postoperative care, including close SICU monitoring, early ambulation, and attention to nutrition and pulmonary hygiene, further contributed to positive outcomes and aligns with enhanced recovery protocols discussed by authors such as Rodriguez-Hermosa et al. [18]

This surgery was performed in a tertiary care teaching hospital where complex abdominal and pelvic hernias are routinely managed. The operating surgeon had prior experience handling similar rare hernias, and the procedure was supervised within a multidisciplinary team, ensuring adherence to institutional safety and ethical standards.

Strengths and limitations

This report describes a rare left-sided obturator hernia in an elderly female, managed successfully with a novel decompression technique via the appendiceal orifice. The main strength of this case is the detailed description of the operative approach and the introduction of an alternative method to avoid enterotomy in selected patients. A limitation is that this is a single case with relatively short follow-up, which restricts the generalizability of our findings. Future reports and studies are required to validate the safety and reproducibility of this technique.

Conclussion

Obturator hernia, though rare, should be suspected in elderly, thin females presenting with signs of bowel obstruction. Prompt CT imaging is critical for diagnosis. This case demonstrates the successful management of a left-sided obturator hernia using an innovative decompression approach via the appendiceal orifice, avoiding full enterotomy and reducing risk in a high-risk surgical candidate. Given the rarity of this hernia, further multi-center collaboration with data pooling and more study and reports are encouraged to refine diagnostic pathways and standardize this technique. This report also highlights the importance of early diagnosis and individualized surgical strategies in improving patient prognosis.
