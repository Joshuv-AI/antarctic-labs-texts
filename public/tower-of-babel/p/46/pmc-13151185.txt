Can experiential learning boost dental ergonomics? evaluating a microscope-based curriculum through Kirkpatrick’s model
Authors: Zimeng Li, Yanlin Xia, Yujie Chen, Cui Huang, Shanshan Liang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMusculoskeletal disorders are a major occupational health issue for dental professionals. Traditional ergonomics training is ineffective for achieving mastery of its principles. This study evaluated the effectiveness of the experiential ergonomics course incorporating dental operating microscopes, using Kirkpatrick’s levels 1 and 2.MethodsThis study conducted a pretest-posttest study with a single group of 103 second-year dental undergraduates from Wuhan University, who completed an experiential ergonomic learning program. The course was designed based on Kolb’s cycle, using dental operating microscope as a key tool. Participants completed researcher-developed pre- and post-course questionnaires featuring 5-point Likert scales, multiple-choice, and binary-response items to assess changes in knowledge, attitudes, and satisfaction. Practical skills were evaluated using the Dental Ergonomics Assessment (DEA) rubric. Data were analyzed using paired-sample t-tests, one-sample t-tests, McNemar’s test, and Spearman’s correlation, with a significance level of p < 0.05.ResultsOf the 103 enrolled students, 89 completed matched pre- and post-course assessments. The curriculum led to significant improvements in students’ ergonomic knowledge (p < 0.001) and their attitudes toward the importance of ergonomics for pain prevention. Post-course ergonomic performance was favorable (overall DEA score: 1.45), and this objective performance showed a strong negative correlation with the belief in ergonomics’ effectiveness (r = -0.624, p < 0.001). Participant feedback was exceptionally positive, with 100% satisfaction regarding course content.ConclusionsThe microscope-based experiential learning course improved dental students’ ergonomic knowledge, attitudes, and postural performance. The strong correlation between competency and conviction supports the view that this approach effectively synchronizes theory with practice.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12903-026-08173-1.Keywords: Ergonomics, Experiential learning, Kirkpatrick model, Dental operating microscopes, Dental education

Introduction

Musculoskeletal disorders (MSDs) are a group of conditions that affect muscles, nerves, tendons, and joints, often resulting in pain, discomfort, or functional impairment [1]. MSDs are highly prevalent among dental professionals, with a significant majority experiencing work-related symptoms that not only compromise their health and well-being but may also lead to reduced productivity, career disruption, and early retirement [1, 2]. Additionally, the onset of these disorders can occur during the initial years of clinical practice or as early as during undergraduate dental training [3, 4]. Implementing appropriate ergonomic practices at an early stage is regarded as an important approach to reducing the risk of MSDs, making the integration of ergonomic training into dental education essential [5, 6].

Currently, ergonomics training has been incorporated into some dental schools as part of undergraduate education, typically through methods such as traditional didactic lectures [7, 8]. While these methods establish a foundational understanding, students often find that ergonomic principles are easily forgotten and difficult to internalize, ultimately hindering the translation of acquired knowledge into sustained ergonomic practices during clinical practice [9–11]. To bridge this gap between knowledge and habitual practice, a more immersive and tangible teaching method is required [12].

Experiential learning provides an effective approach for translating abstract theoretical knowledge into concrete skills and deeper understanding [13, 14]. The efficacy of this approach is well-established in the preclinical training of both medical and dental education, notably in anesthesia management and evidence-based health technology assessment [15–17]. This method engages learners through active practice and critical reflection, allowing them to construct knowledge and develop competence rather than passively absorb information [18, 19]. This approach aligns closely with the objectives of ergonomics education, which emphasizes the translation of theoretical principles into clinical practice [20, 21]. Dental operating microscopes (DOM), which promote neutral working postures as an effective ergonomic intervention [22, 23], are particularly well-suited for experiential learning due to their capacity to render ergonomic principles physically tangible. The use of DOM helps reduce poor postures, including excessive neck flexion, elevated shoulders, arm abduction, and spinal deviations [24, 25]. Incorporating DOM into training has been shown to positively influence students’ ergonomic behaviors [22]. However, despite these advantages, few studies have evaluated the effectiveness of ergonomics training that incorporates DOM as a key tool [26].

For any training program, evaluation is a critical component for assessing its effectiveness and impact. The Kirkpatrick Model stands as a well-established framework for measuring training outcomes, comprising four sequential levels: Reaction (level 1), Learning (level 2), Behavior (level 3), and Results (level 4) [27]. Level 1 gauges participants’ satisfaction and engagement with the training, while level 2 assesses the acquisition of knowledge, skills, and attitudes [28]. Level 3 evaluates the application of learned competencies in practice, and level 4 measures the ultimate organizational or clinical outcomes achieved [29]. The Kirkpatrick Model holds particular value for evaluating straightforward, short-term educational interventions with clearly defined objectives [30]. This focus is highly relevant to the goals of ergonomics education, wherein long-term behavioral change is the ultimate aim [31]. Since such sustained change depends on positive initial reactions and successful knowledge acquisition [32], assessing levels 1 and 2 offers crucial early evidence of an intervention’s potential to ultimately influence higher-level outcomes, a principle that aligns with the foundational role of formative assessment in fostering competence and guiding learner progress [33].

The purpose of this study was to evaluate the efficacy of an experiential learning course in dental ergonomics that utilized DOM, employing the Kirkpatrick Model (levels 1 and 2) as the evaluation framework. It was posited that students would, upon completion, report high levels of satisfaction and demonstrate a significant improvement in their ergonomics-related knowledge and attitudes, and exhibit favorable postural outcomes.

Methods

Study design & study participants

All 103 participants in this single-group pretest-posttest study were second-year undergraduate dental students enrolled in the Ergonomics Training in Dentistry course at the School of Stomatology, Wuhan University. The investigation took place during March and April 2022. All participants had finished their first-year curriculum, which provided them with the essential foundational knowledge and served as the primary inclusion criterion. Crucially, no student had undergone prior ergonomics training. All individuals gave their informed consent, resulting in no exclusions. The study was approved by the local ethics committee.

Implementation of teaching methods

This 4-session course on Ergonomics Training in Dentistry targets second-year undergraduates in the dentistry program at Wuhan University (WHU). All learning activities are distributed across a two-month timeline. The instruction was delivered by faculty from the Wuhan University School of Stomatology. All instructors possessed over a decade of teaching experience and were also experienced clinicians.

The curriculum integrates ergonomic principles into four practical operative sessions, using DOM as the primary tool to make abstract concepts tangible and actionable. Each session focuses on a fundamental operative task: (1) oral examination and probing, (2) penetrating targets, (3) endodontic access opening, and (4) suturing.

Data collection

Data were collected to evaluate the course’s effectiveness based on Kirkpatrick’s model, focusing on level 1 (Reaction) and level 2 (Learning). A pre-course questionnaire (Additional file 1) assessed students’ baseline knowledge and attitudes. A matched post-course questionnaire (Additional file 2) evaluated the changes in knowledge and attitudes, along with student satisfaction. Additionally, practical skills were assessed post-course using a separate instrument (Additional file 3). To assess initial levels of ergonomic knowledge and attitudes, the pre-course questionnaire was delivered to participants prior to the course start. The post-course assessment was conducted immediately after the final session and comprised two parts: (1) the post-course questionnaire, which repeated the knowledge and attitude items and introduced new items to measure satisfaction, (2) a practical skills evaluation. The questionnaires were developed based on relevant literature and adapted from previously published studies [32, 34, 35]. Both researcher-developed questionnaires employed a combination of 5-point Likert scales (where 1 represented strong disagreement and 5 strong agreement), multiple-choice items, and binary items (coded 1 for yes; 0 for no). All questionnaire components were examined for content validity by four subject matter experts not involved in the study and subsequently revised based on their feedback. A pilot test was conducted using a sample of five randomly chosen dental students in their third year to ensure clarity and appropriateness.

For level 1 (Reaction), participants’ satisfaction with the course content and teaching methods was measured in the post-course questionnaire using a 5-point Likert scale, and this satisfaction scale demonstrated acceptable reliability (Cronbach’s α = 0.70). For level 2 (Learning), knowledge was assessed via pre- and post-course tests containing two Likert-type items and one multiple-choice question (scored from 0 to 8 points). Attitudes were evaluated using seven binary questions, which demonstrated good reliability (pre-course KR-20 = 0.747; post-course KR-20 = 0.768).

Ergonomic skills were assessed as a component of level 2 (Learning) during the final course session. The Dental Ergonomics Assessment (DEA) served as the instrument for measuring the posture of participants [22]. Two pre-trained teachers observed the students’ operations from various angles and score their performance on neck flexion, focal distance range, trunk flexion-side, trunk flexion-back, trunk rotation, arm abduction, and shoulder abduction based on the tool’s scoring criteria [22]. On this scale, 0 denotes the ideal neutral posture, and any score of 1 or above marks a departure from neutrality. The final score is the sum of the individual scores from these seven categories.

To enable matched-pairs analysis across all data sets while ensuring participant confidentiality, all questionnaires collected identical demographic identifiers (gender, age, student ID). Questionnaire data were collected electronically via the SurveyStar platform (Changsha, Hunan, China), while skills assessment data were recorded manually on standardized sheets and later digitized.

Statistical analysis

IBM SPSS Statistics (version 26, Armonk, NY, USA) performed the analysis of all data, which had been previously exported to Microsoft Excel (Microsoft Corp, Redmond, WA). Statistical analyses were performed using paired samples t-tests for continuous data (reported as mean ± standard deviation, SD) collected before and after the course. A one-sample t-test was used to evaluate participant satisfaction through a comparison with a neutral value. For categorical data (reported as frequencies and percentages), changes in pre- and post-course responses, including those from multiple-choice questions, were assessed using McNemar’ s test. Postural data from the DEA were summarized using descriptive statistics (mean ± standard deviation). The analysis of the correlation between students’ DEA scores and their attitudes towards applied ergonomics was assessed using Spearman’s test. A p-value below 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Prior to matching, 102 students (99.0%) finished the pre-course questionnaire and 98 (95.1%) completed the post-course assessment. Matching via anonymized IDs yielded 89 complete paired datasets for the final analysis, representing a valid match rate of 86.4%. This final cohort had a mean age of 19.47 years (SD = 0.74) and consisted of 55 females (61.8%) and 34 males (38.2%).

Baseline evaluation indicated a notable deficiency in students’ grasp of fundamental ergonomic principles (Table 2). The post-course score for subjective knowledge of ergonomic postures was 4.28 ± 0.64, which was higher than the pre-course score of 2.72 ± 0.97 (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.87). Similarly, understanding of the relationship between inappropriate postures and MSDs increased from 3.26 ± 1.01 to 4.58 ± 0.56 (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 1.61). For the identification of inappropriate postures, a post-course score of 6.34 ± 2.12 was recorded, showing a significant increase from the pre-course average of 4.56 ± 2.29 (p < 0.001, Cohen’s d = 0.81). The recognition rates for all types of inappropriate postures also improved, as detailed in Fig. 2a.

Discussion

The results of this study support the initial hypothesis, indicating that the experiential learning course utilizing DOM was associated with positive outcomes. The course led to significant enhancements in students’ understanding of ergonomic principles and recognition of the benefits of applying ergonomics in clinical practice. Furthermore, the low post-course DEA scores were strongly correlated with the high cognition scores regarding the role of ergonomics in pain reduction. Concurrently, participant satisfaction with the course was high. These findings collectively suggest that the course met its objective of providing an effective educational intervention in dental ergonomics for undergraduate students.

The structured progression of Kolb’s experiential learning cycle, which guides learners from concrete experience to abstract conceptualization, appears to be a key factor contributing to the significant improvement in students’ knowledge levels [13, 19]. This pedagogical approach, as implemented in this course, effectively translated abstract ergonomic principles into tangible understanding by incorporating the DOM as a pivotal tool. It enabled students to visually identify their involuntary and inappropriate postures during the concrete experience and reflective observation phases, leading to a significant gain in ergonomic knowledge. This direct visual evidence then formed a solid foundation for the abstract conceptualization phase, facilitating a deeper internalization of why specific postures are detrimental [20]. The early recognition of these postures is critical for preventing MSDs [2]. Therefore, by mastering this skill early in their training, students gain essential knowledge that is fundamental to safeguarding their health throughout their professional careers [36].

The experiential learning cycle progresses to the phase of active experimentation, enabling participants to personally experience and observe the advantages of the technique [15, 35]. In this course, the comparison between initial unguided practice and the subsequent application of ergonomic principles under DOM guidance allowed students to experience the comfort of correct postures. This contrast facilitated a shift from knowing the importance of ergonomics to personally believing in its necessity, resulting in the observed positive attitudes toward its role in mitigating MSDs [37]. Whereas lectures have proven inadequate for maintaining ergonomic habits [7], the internal drive cultivated through this experiential course is more likely to support long-term adherence in future practice.

The post-course DEA assessment revealed a mean overall score of 1.45 ± 1.765, indicating that students maintained neutral postures in the majority of body segments, with ergonomic risk present in only 1–2 areas on average. These generally low scores are consistent with previously reported improvements associated with microscope use [22]. A comparison with prior literature indicates that the DEA scores across all body segments in this study were consistently lower than those reported in contexts where magnification was employed merely as a technical tool [22]. This comparative outcome suggests that integrating magnification within a structured experiential learning course, rather than introducing it only as an aid, may lead to more comprehensive postural improvements. This pedagogical method, akin to approaches proven to develop professional habits through reflection [38], likely fostered the observed ergonomic improvements by prompting students to continuously reflect on and adjust their whole-body positioning.

Furthermore, the strong negative correlation observed between DEA scores and students’ perceived effectiveness of ergonomics in reducing musculoskeletal pain points to a close integration of practical competency and attitudinal endorsement. Whereas prior research has highlighted instances where ergonomic awareness does not readily translate into practice [9, 10], the current findings demonstrate a coherent alignment between students’ postural performance and their belief in the value of ergonomics for pain prevention. This relationship suggests that the course design not only facilitated skill acquisition but also cultivated a contextualized understanding of how ergonomic postures contribute to personal well-being. Thus, an experiential pedagogy that intertwines tool use with reflective learning shows promise in synchronizing ergonomic competence with corresponding attitudes in dental education [13].

The high level of satisfaction with this course can be directly linked to the successful implementation of Kolb’s experiential learning cycle [15]. The four-stage process provided a comprehensive learning experience by enabling students to first encounter ergonomic challenges directly, then reflect on their performance with the aid of DOM, further conceptualize underlying principles through instructor guidance, and finally apply these principles in practice with immediate feedback. Consequently, the majority of students reported that this teaching method motivated them to actively apply what they had learned. These findings are consistent with existing literature, further confirming the necessity of integrating ergonomics training early in undergraduate education [32, 35].

Despite the positive findings, there are some limitations in this study. The single-group pretest-posttest design did not include a control group, as this study served as an initial exploration of a new teaching method and dividing the same cohort of students would raise ethical concerns. Additionally, the post-test occurred immediately after the intervention, and long-term retention is an area that could be explored in future research. As DOM was integral to the experiential learning cycle, the design does not allow for isolating the effects of the tool from the teaching method. Future research could compare this approach with other ergonomic aids, such as loupes. Furthermore, baseline DEA scores were not available, so the extent of behavioral change remains unclear. Incorporating such measurements would strengthen future studies.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that the experiential, microscope-based curriculum significantly enhanced dental students’ ergonomic knowledge and fostered highly positive attitudes toward its importance. Post-course assessment revealed good postural performance among students, and the strong negative correlation between these posture scores and their belief in ergonomics for pain reduction reveals a critical link between competency and conviction. These findings validate the course as an effective educational intervention that synchronizes ergonomic understanding with practical application, justifying its early integration into dental curricula to promote long-term professional health. Successful implementation requires consideration of equipment and facility costs, access to microscopes, ongoing device maintenance, and faculty training to guide experiential learning effectively.
