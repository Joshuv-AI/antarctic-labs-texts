Investigation into the Ideal Concoction for Performance and Emissions Enhancement of Jatropha Biodiesel-Diesel with CuO Nanoparticles Using Response Surface Methodology
Authors: Elumalai Ramachandran, Ravi Krishnaiah, Elumalai Perumal Venkatesan, Saboor Shaik, Chanduveetil Ahamed Saleel, Fayaz Hussain
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
The present work covers the preparation of biodiesel from jatropha oil through the transesterification process followed by its characterization, and furthermore, performance and emission analyses were done in terms of blending biodiesel with fossil diesel and CuO nanoparticles. Jatropha biodiesel blends (B10, B20, and B30) were chosen for this preliminary investigation based on the observation that B20 outperformed other blends. Next stage B20 with copper oxide (CuO) nanoparticle concentrations of 25, 50, 75, and 50 ppm are used to examine the performance and emission characteristics of a constant speed single cylinder, 4-stroke, 3.5 kW compression ignition (CI) engine. Finally, The response surface methodology (RSM) was utilized to determine the optimal nanoparticle concentration for B20. The results revealed that the blend of B20 with 80 ppm nanoparticles had the highest desirability (0.9732), and the developed RSM model was able to predict engine responses with a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 3.113%. A confirmation test with an error in prediction of less than 5% verified the model’s adequacy. When comparing optimized B20CuO80 to diesel, brake specific energy consumption (BSEC) increased by 8.49% and brake thermal efficiency (BTE) was lowered by 3.34%. Hydrocarbon (HC), carbon monoxide (CO), carbon dioxide (CO2), nitrogen oxide (NOx), and smoke emissions were reduced by 3.66% and 2.88%, 4.78%, 22.9%, and 20.54%, respectively, at 80% load. As a result, the B20 blend with nanoparticle concentrations of 80 ppm may be used in current diesel engines without engine modification.

Introduction

Diesel consumption among fossil fuels is substantial due to the widespread use of CI engines. Diesel engines are preferred because of their power production, availability of current technology, low operating costs, and dependability.1 Diesel engine exhaust in the atmosphere wreaks havoc on the ecosphere and creates a variety of health problems.2 Environmental degradation and global warming are brought on by inefficient combustion of fossil fuel combustion. Environmental deterioration is mostly caused by harmful emissions from diesel engines, including HC, CO, NOx, and sulfur oxide.3 Acid rain is significantly influenced by these chemical pollutants, which also spreads a number of infectious illnesses to other living things. As a result, widespread diesel engine use contributes to major environmental issues like ozone layer degradation, global warming, and unnatural climate change.4 The governing authorities have established strict emission standards and are taking active measures to limit emissions, such as using other fuels and engine modifications.5 Utilizing alternative energy sources diminishes reliance on petroleum-based products and has less effect on fluctuations in fuel costs in global markets.6 Biodiesel is a possible alternative fuel with benefits over diesel fuel, such as being easily available, renewable, and transferable.7−9 First generation feedstocks of biodiesel were animal fat and vegetable oil, while second generation feedstocks of biodiesel were forest and agricultural leftovers that could be used to make biodiesel. However, edible oils are in great demand as food sources and may become scarce, which further contributes to economic inequality. The possibility of replacing petroleum on a wide scale is being constrained by the lack of second-generation feedstock in many nations. Due to the enormous environmental and financial advantages of third generation feedstock for biodiesel production from nonagricultural and nonedible sources, various researchers are focusing on them as a way to overcome challenges associated with developing biodiesel from first- and second-generation feedstock.10 Numerous studies on the use of different biodiesels in CI engines have been published. The CI engine was tested by Nabi et al.11 on various mixtures of jatropha biodiesel. They came to the conclusion that B25 had remarkable BTE and fuel economy statistics that were comparable to those of neat diesel. The NOx emissions from the Jatropha blends were lower. Additionally, the HC and CO were lowered by 14–18% compared to diesel. Reddy et al.12 studied microalgae biodiesel as a partial diesel fuel substitute in CI engine and found that thermal efficiency remained nearly identical with that of diesel. High NOx emissions and lower HC and CO emissions than diesel are additional disadvantages.

Recent developments in nanoscience and technology have made it possible to create energetic materials that are nanoscale in size and have several benefits over materials.13 Since these nanoparticles are so small, they have a greater surface area, which speeds up the oxidation and combustion of fuel. According to the fuel and fuel blend used, using the right range of metal additives will yield better results. In an investigation, nanoparticles were incorporated into diesel to enhance the fuel’s ability to ignite. It was found that in every instance, the diesel nanoparticle concoction had a higher ignition probability than pure diesel.14 Selvan et al.15 studied nanoparticles-emulsified fuel in a CI engine, When nanoparticles were used as catalysts in ethanol blends. They came to the conclusion that the nanoparticle additive functions as an oxygenating catalyst and acts as a source of oxygen for the oxidation of CO to CO2. They showed that the nanoparticle inclusion greatly decreased exhaust pollutants and enhanced fuel combustion. Due to the huge surface area under volume and strong catalytic action, fuel efficiency and emission levels are enhanced. In another investigation, biodiesel and its blends with nanoparticles were used in diesel engines. The findings reveal a rise in thermal efficiency and a fall in NOx emissions.16 An experiment using 20% Mahua biodiesel blended with nanoparticles tested the CI engine performance and emissions. The result showed improvements in thermal efficiency and reduced emissions over diesel.14 According to research by Hoseini et al.,17 GO nanoadditives with different biodiesel blends in CI engine showed reductions in CO, HC, and BSEC while showing a marginal increase in thermal efficiency and NOx emissions.

Considering all of the engine parameters, several tests may be required to determine whether the fuel is a viable alternative to fossil fuels. When both cost and time are considered, an inefficient condition arises. Many software solutions have been developed to increase the efficiency of this scenario. RSM is a statistical approach and is one of the multivariate techniques for optimization and engine response simulation. In an investigation, the influence of speed and the concentration of nanoparticles in biodiesel blended fuels on engine performance and emissions was studied using the RSM approach. At 2150 rpm, the B30+20 ppm biodiesel blend had a minimum BSFC of 0.34 kg/kWh and a maximum BTE of 25.9%. CO and HC emissions decreased to 25.61 kg/kWh and 0.053 kg/kWh, respectively.18 Mostafa et al.19 used RSM to study the influence of load and concentration of nanoadditives in a CI engine. According to the analysis, Diesel+50 ppm nanoparticle concentration decreases NOx, HC, and CO emissions by 32.3%, 21.7%, and 20%, respectively. Furthermore, the BTE improved by 4.9% at 2.7 kW compared to pure diesel. The experimental outputs were found to be in excellent agreement with those predicted. In an investigation, the RSM was used to optimize the performance and emission characteristics of a CI engine by varying the load and compression ratios. From both optimization observations, it is found that the engine load is 1.87 kW, and a compression ratio of 17 is found to be the optimal operating parameter. The optimum responses corresponding to these factors are found to be 19.4%, 758 ppm, 2.1 vol %, and 184 ppm for BTE, HC, CO, and NOx emissions, respectively, with an overall desirability of 0.96.20 Research was carried out using RSM, and the findings revealed that there is a high degree of agreement between the optimization and experiment results, with the error rate ranging from 0.63% to 4.57%.21

The use of jatropha biodiesel in CI engines has a significant impact on engine-out emissions. In contrast, jatropha biodiesel has a lower thermal efficiency; therefore, suggesting it as an alternative fuel to fossil fuels solely based on engine-out attributes is insufficient. As a result, in order to overcome the shortcomings of jatropha biodiesel performance due to its superior qualities, it is important to assess the performance behavior of CuO nanoparticle emulsification.

This kind of investigation would aid in determining how biodiesel and nanoadditives are burned in CI engines and how emissions are produced. Another novel component of this study was the use of numerical techniques to optimize the output and predict the input parameters. When these technologies are used, the time and cost of experimenting are significantly reduced, and the system’s efficiency is increased. The working function of RSM is a type of statistically based inquiry that successfully establishes the interaction between the many components to identify the optimal engine factor levels. In this study, RSM was used to create a regression model and find the optimal nanoparticle concentration for the outperforming biodiesel blend.

Materials and Methods

Esterification Method

When an alkaline catalyst is utilized, the free fatty acids (FFAs) and moisture levels adversely affect the transesterification of glycerides with alcohol. In an alkaline-catalyst transesterification method, a high FFA concentration is undesirable, because the FFA will react with the catalyst to form soap, making it difficult to separate the biodiesel products and lowering the production of biodiesel. The amount of catalyst needed for the transesterification process depends on the FFA content of the oil, since oils with higher FFA concentrations require more catalyst to prevent the production of soap. If the oil’s FFA level is greater than 3%, transesterification does not take place. Normally, more than 6–20% (wt) of jatropha oil includes free fatty acids. As a result, the FFA content of raw jatropha oil was assessed in this work using the neutralization titration method, and it was discovered that it was around 7.2%. The most widely used technique is the esterification of FFAs with CH3OH in the presence of acidic catalysts because it is a straightforward procedure, and the acid catalysts convert the FFA into oil to produce biodiesel. For esterification, the methanol/oil ratio is 6:1 molar ratio, the H2SO4 catalyst is 1% (v/v), and the reaction condition is 60 °C at 90 min with a stirring speed of 8000 rpm. After esterification was done, FFA was calculated, and it was discovered to be 0.7%.

Transesterification Method

The most widely used crop oil derivatives for fuel are methyl esters. This was produced from the transesterification method of an 8:1 CH3OH to bio-oil molar ratio in the presence of KOH (1 wt %) catalyst for a reaction condition of 60 °C at 60 min with a stirring speed of 8000 rpm.22 The process’s yield is determined to be 92.13%. FTIR Analysis

Organic compounds, covalent bonds, and the kinds of vibrations that were present in jatropha biodiesel were all identified using an FTIR analyzer. FTIR spectra were captured in transmission mode with a conventional wavenumber measuring range of 4000 to 500 cm–1 (see Figure 1). For the purpose of distinguishing between organic and inorganic components in the sample, the FTIR also contains a single optical beam system, a ZnSe crystal, and a DLaTGS type detector. The detector relies on changes in the temperature and infrared radiation intensity. The interaction of a conventional infrared source with jatropha biodiesel to measure the infrared absorption at a particular wavelength reveals several components present in jatropha biodiesel.

Alkanes may be inferred from the observation of C–H stretching at wavelengths of 2923.31 and 2853.73 cm–1, and aromatic compounds can be confirmed by C=C stretching at the peak of 1435.91 cm–1. The chemical groups O–H, C–O, and C=O most likely controlled the oxygen molecules’ availability in microalgae. An alkane group was seen at the peak for =C–H at 722.10 cm–1. The presence of several hydrocarbon groups (C–H) in jatropha biodiesel supports its use as a reliable alternative energy source to diesel.

Nanoadditive Blend Preparation

Jatropha biodiesel was produced from raw oil using the transesterification technique. Commercially available diesel and CuO nanoparticles were purchased from the market to prepare nanofuel blends. The fuel used in this study was a B20 blend made up of 20% biodiesel and 80% diesel. The nanofuel blends were made with nanoparticle concentrations ranging from 25 to 100 ppm. The stability of the nanofuel blends at 25, 50, 75, and 100 ppm was observed for 4 weeks. The nanoparticles were stably mixed with biodiesel fuel molecules in fuel blends of 40, 80, and 120 ppm. In general, biodiesel fuel blends are stable over time. It can be stored safely for 2 to 3 years.23 A magnetic stirrer serves as an agitator for blending the biodiesel blends for around 20 min at room temperature. The nanoparticle average size range varies from 30 to 45 nm. The surface charge of nanoparticles is typically high due to their large surface area per unit volume. A micro molecule was formed when a group of nanoparticles began to settle. Hence, particle sedimentation must be managed. Nanoparticles are required to have their surfaces modified in order to remain stable in a base fluid.14 The CuO nanoadditive was disseminated in the B20 mix at varied dosage levels of 25, 50, 75, and 100 ppm using ultrasonicator equipment at a temperature of 30 °C and a frequency of 40 kHz for 30 min to create the nano additive fuel samples. To ensure adequate mixing and to prevent nanoparticle agglomeration in the biodiesel blend, 2% cationic surfactant is added to the nanoadditive biodiesel blend. The manufactured fuel samples were labeled B20CuO25, B20CuO50, B20CuO75, and B20CuO100 based on the amount of CuO3 added to the B20 fuel sample.

Test Procedure

The present study is focused on the performance (BTE, BSEC) and emission (HC, CO, CO2, NOx, and smoke) characteristics of the CI engine over the entire load range at a constant 1500 rpm. Initially, the test engine was operated in diesel mode for a baseline reading. Then, the fuel was switched to jatropha biodiesel blends (B10, B20, and B30), and the optimal blend was determined based on the experimental results. Next, the nanoparticles were emulsified at concentrations of 25, 50, 75, and 100 ppm across the entire load range using the optimal biodiesel blend. Finally, the RSM method was utilized to determine the optimal nanoparticle concentration for an outperforming blend.

Experimental Setup

The engine utilized for testing is a Kirloskar single-cylinder, four-stroke, water-cooled, naturally aspirated direct injection engine. The engine arrangement described above was utilized to test the performance and emission characteristics. Table 2 accurately represents the engine specs, and Figure 2. shows a schematic depiction of the test arrangement. The noncontact speed sensor detects speed, and the load cell measures load. An adjustable water-cooling arrangement with a flow meter is provided to maintain the dyno and engine at the appropriate temperature. To monitor the temperature of the cooling water, K-type thermocouple sensors are appropriately placed at the relevant junctures. To acquire pressure data, a water-cooled Kistler piezoelectric pressure transducer with a charge amplifier is mounted on the cylinder head. Using an AVL crank angle encoder, pressure data from 100 stable consecutive cycles was collected for each operational point at a precision of 0.2 CAD. The anemometer and calorimetric fuel flow sensor record the engine’s intake air and fuel consumption rates at each cycle for performance studies. To acquire real-time data from all sensors, we used the NI Instrument USA data acquisition system with a data collection rate of 250 kHz is used. The engine software package makes use of the obtained data and computes performance indices. The AVL 444 Digas gas emission analyzer is used to test HC, CO, and NOx emissions, while the AVL 437 smoke meter is used to evaluate smoke opacity. To prevent moisture condensation and particles from entering the gas emission analyzer, a moisture trap and filter setup are supplied. To ensure the accuracy of the exhaust gas data, the gas analyzer was calibrated before the experiments. The zero-calibration using fresh air was adjusted after each test. The tests were carried out in steady-state condition to ensure the reliability and accuracy of the measurement results. Measurements were taken 10 min after starting the engine. 60 min are allowed for each test for engine stabilization and subsequent fuel changes. Response Surface Methodology

RSM is an established method for designing experiments and determining the optimal values of dependent variables (responses) by using a statistical model between independent variables (experimental factors). RSM drastically reduces the number of necessary experimental trials and, depending on the chosen model, can also identify the effect of factor interactions. Implementing Design-Expert V12 and RSM for statistical analysis to evaluate the effect of the independent variables on the performance and emission parameters, the central composite design (CCD) was utilized. The fuel blend (nanoadditive concentration) and engine load at five levels were independent variables.

Results and Discussion

Performance Characteristics

Brake Thermal Efficiency

The performance of an engine is determined by its brake thermal efficiency, which effectively converts fuel chemical energy into a usable power output. The observed BTE values were 36.53%, 34.18%, 34.87%, and 33.66% for Diesel, B10, B20, and B30, respectively. Due to the adverse fuel properties of biodiesel, BTE for biodiesel blends was found to be 6.43%, 4.53%, and 7.86% lower than diesel. Figure 3 depicts how the BTE of diesel and its blends fluctuates with load. Diesel’s improved BTE is due to its enhanced physicochemical features, such as its higher heating value and less dense fuel, which lead to more efficient combustion.24 From the result, moderate BTE for B20 might be attributed to sufficient oxygen present in the biodiesel to complete combustion, and combustion reaction effectively uses all of the oxygen.25,26 Based on suitable performance and emission, B20 was chosen as a better fuel, and it may be utilized as a fuel for future experimental investigation. BTE for the emulsified blends of B20CuO25, B20CuO50, B20CuO75, and B20CuO100 was found to be 35.22%, 35.9%, 37.23%, and 36.77%, respectively. B20CuO75 showed 6.75% greater BTE than B20 without nanoadditive and 1.91% greater than diesel because of the catalytic activity of nanoparticles due to the higher surface area/volume ratio, which promoted combustion through a superior oxidation process.27 Over 75 ppm of nanoadditive BTE does not significantly improve. In both cases, BTE increases as engine load increases, reaching its maximum at full load, due to an increase in fuel consumption and a rise in cylinder temperature.28Figure 4 depicts the changing trend of BTE in relation to engine load and nanoparticle concentration in B20.

Brake Specific Energy Consumption

The important measure to reflect the engine’s performance is brake-specific energy consumption. Figure 5 illustrates how the BSEC varies with load for diesel and its blends. It gradually decreases when the load gradually decreases. During peak load, Diesel, B10, B20, and B30 had average BSECs of 9.78, 11.20, 10.97, and 11.48 MJ/kWh, respectively. The BSEC for biodiesel blends was increased in relation to diesel by 14.47%, 12.16%, and 17.38%, respectively. It was found that biodiesels inferior combustion to diesel is caused by its reduced heating value.29,30 The BSEC grew when biodiesel’s ratio to diesel was raised. It is possible that the engine can adjust for the identical power, indicating that additional fuel is needed.31 In comparison to other algae blends, B20 demonstrated a lower BSEC. Further, BSEC for the emulsified blends of B20CuO25, B20CuO50, B20CuO75, and B20CuO100 was found to be 10.96, 9.94, 9.75, and 9.79 MJ/kWh, respectively. B20CuO75 exemplifies reduction in BSEC 11.12% compared to B20 without nanoadditive and 0.31% than diesel. The effects of engine load and nanoparticle concentration on the BSEC values are shown in Figure 6. The decreased energy consumption rate was obtained for blends with dispersed CuO. B20 with CuO 75 ppm exhibits a lower BSEC than other tested fuels; this might be attributed to improving the catalytic process of nanoadditive, which would allow for the use of additional air to boost the combustion properties.32,33

Emission Characteristics

Unburned Hydrocarbon Emission

The findings of unburned HC emissions are shown in Figure 7 with respect to different fuels. The observed HC emission values were 82, 93, 88, and 90 ppm for Diesel, B10, B20, and B30, respectively. The HC for biodiesel blends was increased in relation to diesel by 12.89%, 7.7%, and 10.29%, respectively. It may be because of the properties of the biodiesel fuel. The A/F ratio and engine load circumstances have an impact on HC. When more fuel was allowed into the combustion chamber at full load, the quench zone increased and fuel was deposited in crevice areas, leading to excessive HC production.13,34 Further, HC for the emulsified blends of B20CuO25, B20CuO50, B20CuO75, and B20CuO100 was found to be 84, 81, 74, and 80 ppm, respectively. B20CuO75 was revealed to have 16.21% lower HC emissions than B20 without nanoadditive and 9.76% lower than diesel. The effects of engine load and nanoparticle concentration on HC emissions are shown in Figure 8. The inclusion of a nanoadditive, which increases the amount of oxygen available for burning and speeds up combustion, may be the cause of this. By increasing the amount of nanoadditive in fuel, the ignition delay (ID) is shortened, resulting in a greater premixed combustion stage and less HC production.35 Afterward, there was no appreciable reduction in HC emissions when the CuO content was increased above 75 ppm with B20, possibly as a result of the increased blend viscosity and subpar atomization properties.36,37

Carbon Monoxide Emission

The CO emissions demonstrate a poor combustion efficiency. The worst chemical reaction in the fuel is also indicated by the CO release. The CO and load contrast is shown in Figure 9. Fuel heterogeneity, insufficient oxygen, a short residence time for burning, and inefficient CO2 formation are the main causes of CO emissions. Among other things, the A/F ratio has a significant role in the formation of carbon monoxide.38,39 At low and full load states, the CO emissions were high. This could be explained by a higher A/F mixture, which slowed the CO oxidation. Diesel, B10, B20, and B30 had noted CO values of 0.104, 0.129, 0.119, and 0.132 vol %, respectively. The CO for biodiesel blends was increased in relation to diesel by 23.62%, 14.42%, and 26.92%, respectively. All of the biodiesel blends were shown to have higher CO emissions than diesel. This may be because the biodiesels higher viscosity, which causes improper A/F mixing and atomizing, reduced the amount of CO2 conversion.40,41 CO for the emulsified blends of B20CuO25, B20CuO50, B20CuO75, and B20CuO100 was found to be 0.114, 0.103, 0.094 and 0.098 vol %., respectively. B20CuO75 was revealed to have 21% lowest CO emissions than B20 without nanoadditive and 9.61% lower than diesel. It was discovered that B20CuO75 exemplifies the lowest level of CO emissions. The high viscosity of biodiesel caused by improper air-fuel mixing is rectified by emulsifying oxidized CuO nanoparticles with biodiesel.42,43

Carbon Dioxide Emission

Carbon dioxide emissions exemplify complete combustion of the fuel. Figure 11 depicts the disparity between the CO2 and load. The key factors that contribute to CO2 release include a homogeneous fuel state, sufficient oxygen, and residence time for burning. The A/F ratio is a key factor in the production of carbon dioxide, among other things.44 Diesel, B10, B20, and B30 showed CO2 emissions that were 3.14, 2.97, 3.04, and 2.95 vol %, respectively. The CO2 for biodiesel blends decreased in relation to diesel by 5.27%, 3.18%, and 5.94%, respectively. It was established that all biodiesel mixes produced less CO2 than diesel. Due to biodiesel’s high viscosity and low volatility, it influence the fuel’s atomization properties, which impair combustion.45 Further, the concentration of CO2 for the emulsified blends of B20CuO25, B20CuO50, B20CuO75, and B20CuO100 was found to be 3.07, 3.11, 3.19, and 3.15 vol %, respectively. B20CuO75 was revealed to have a 4.93% highest CO2 emission compared to B20 without a nanoadditive and 1.59% higher than diesel. Figure 12 depicts the relationship between the engine load and nanoparticle concentration in biodiesel fuel and its impact on CO2 emissions. This may be as a result of complete combustion, which reduces CO formation. A greater rate of CO2 generation was caused by the addition of nanoadditives, which increase the O2 content of the fuel blend.46,47

NOx Emission

When the cylinder temperature is high, nitrogen and oxygen combine efficiently to generate nitrogen oxide. The burning of air-fuel under stoichiometric conditions is another major factor in the generation of NOx. Near the peak load region, the NOx emissions were significantly greater. Figure 13 shows the difference in the NOx emissions for the various test fuels. The NOx emissions were found to be 559, 499, 518, and 495 ppm for diesel, B10, B20, and B30, respectively. The NOx for biodiesel blends decreased in relation to diesel by 10.57%, 7.21%, and 11.29%, respectively. It was observed that NOx decreased as the amount of biodiesel increased in the mixture. This outcome might be the result of increased biodiesel viscosity, which causes poor A/F mixing and incomplete combustion.48 Further, the NOx values for the emulsified blends of B20CuO25, B20CuO50, B20CuO75, and B20CuO100 were found to be 538, 553, 570, and 564 ppm. B20CuO75 was revealed to have 10.09% higher NOx emissions than B20 without nanoadditive and 1.97% higher than diesel. Figure 14 depicts the NOx emission variation trend as a function of the engine load and nanoparticle concentration in B20. It was discovered that B20CuO75 exemplifies the highest rate of NOx emissions. This may be due to full combustion, which results in an increase in NOx production. It could be ascribed to the development of the catalytic action owing to nanoadditive, which would enable the usage of more oxygen to enhance the combustion.49,50

Smoke Emission

Figure 15 reveals a variation in smoke for the test fuel. The conclusion was that when engine load increased from idle to maximum, smoke emissions also increased. Diesel, B10, B20, and B30 had noted smoke opacity values of 11.2, 12.6, 12.3, and 13.2 N, respectively. Admonition of Current Study with a Trace of Attained Experimental Results

The environmental and human health impacts of pollutants mandate the setup of carbon-free energy sources. Biodiesel is a renewable energy source that may aid in the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions and the “carbon footprint” of agriculture. Furthermore, biodiesel emits less air pollution than fossil fuels. For a variety of reasons, India and other oil-importing nations want alternative fuels to replace petroleum-based fossil fuels. The major causes for this include growing energy consumption in the vehicle industry, frightening oil import prices, and severe pollution standards. Biodiesel from jatropha is popular in India because it can be grown on waste land. Jatropha biodiesel, produced from jatropha oil through the transesterification process, has qualities comparable to diesel and passes the criteria. Although jatropha biodiesel has immense potential, it has failed to gain commercial traction in India. Experimentation with jatropha and the implementation of jatropha-related biofuel policies are restricted to research and development in laboratories and a few big vehicle firms. The high cost of production and the absence of a solid government policy governing the use of biodiesel are the two most significant impediments to its effective deployment in the fuel business. Third-generation biofuels, notably algal biodiesel, have the potential to compete with jatropha-based biodiesel. Because India is an agrarian nation, if jatropha production is done more methodically, it may prove to be more inexpensive than algae. The formation and appropriate execution of a clear biodiesel policy by the Indian government may help this situation in the near future.

According to the tests, CuO nanoparticles added to biodiesel blends enhanced emission characteristics, as well as engine performance values. As a result, as compared to biodiesel devoid of CuO nanoparticles, the BTE and BSEC parameters improved significantly. Catalytic oxidation, microexplosions, high oxygen content, and excellent atomization of CuO-added biodiesel all contributed to the beneficial findings presented in the paper. CuO nanoparticles added to biodiesel enhanced emission characteristics as well. CuO nanoparticles have also been utilized as oxidation agents to promote fuel oxidation, resulting in significant reductions in CO and HC emissions. CuO nanoparticles’ promising properties of CuO nanoparticles have shown their advantages when used in biodiesel-fueled diesel engines. On the other hand, more study on the potential detrimental impacts of CuO nanoparticles on human health and natural ecosystems should be conducted.

Furthermore, the importance of Artificial Intelligence (AI) techniques in engine response control, decision-making, simulation, and optimization is summarized. According to the present state of the art, most study research studies suggest that the successful implementation of AI approaches has a lot of promise. There is a need for their correct use in future research for innovative sources of engine responses, particularly in hybrid systems. The use of unique and hybrid optimization methodologies such as artificial neural networks, deep neural networks, genetic algorithms, and multicriterion decision making tools will provide an additional performance enhancement for engine responses, which will benefit global prosperity.

To scientifically support the adoption of B20CuO80 in vehicular engines, more extensive research must be conducted on the following topics: (a) the use of several fuel additives; (b) engine modification techniques; (c) engine wear and durability.

Conclusion

In order to choose the best mixture of various jatropha blends and CuO nanoparticles in CI mode at varied loads with an identical rpm of 1500 and an injection timing of 27° bTFDC, the trials were done, and the outcomes of the experiment were summarized as follows:The observed BTE values are 36.53%, 34.18%, 34.87%, and 33.66% for diesel, B10, B20, and B30, respectively. Based on the performance results, B20 was taken up for further studies due to its moderate performance and enhanced emission over other blends.Blend B20 with CuO nanoparticle concentrations of 25, 50, 75, and 100 ppm were carried out; according to the results, B20CuO75 demonstrates improved BTE and reduced BSEC of 37.23% and 9.75 MJ/kWh, showing 6.75% and 11.12% enhancement compared to B20 without nanoadditive and 1.91% and 0.31% enhancement compared to diesel. Similarly, HC, CO, and smoke emissions were decreased by 16.21%, 21%, and 17.58% compared to B20 without nanoadditive and 9.76%, 9.61%, and 9.82% compared to diesel, while CO2 and NOx emissions increased by 4.93% and 10.09% compared to B20 without nanoadditive and 1.59% and 1.97% compared to diesel.Finally, the RSM results revealed that the blend of B20 with 80 ppm nanoparticles had the highest desirability (0.9732), and the developed RSM model was able to predict engine responses with a MAPE of 3.113%. When comparing B20CuO80 to diesel, BSEC increased by 8.49% and BTE was lowered by 3.34%.
