Anatomical and physiological traits to identify low-vigor Persian walnut accessions as candidate dwarfing rootstocks
Authors: Rasoul Sadeghi-Majd, Mahmoud Reza Roozban, Saadat Sarikhani, Maryam Norouzi, Mozhdeh Osku, Kourosh Vahdati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Size-controlling planting materials are essential for improving orchard sustainability, enabling high-density systems, and reducing management costs in nut tree production. However, reliable sources of growth-restricting planting materials remain scarce in walnut improvement programs. This study quantified differences in growth performance among diverse Juglans regia germplasm using an integrative evaluation of morphological, physiological, and anatomical characteristics. Fifteen accessions with varying growth vigor collected from eight regions of Iran were propagated as half-sib families and grown under uniform field conditions. Growth attributes were recorded over two consecutive seasons, after which contrasting groups were subjected to detailed wood structure and water-relation analyses. Based on morphological evaluations, four of the fifteen accessions—representing high, low, and intermediate growth vigor—were selected for anatomical and physiological analyses to explore their differential characteristics. The less vigorous accessions, ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’, showed significantly smaller vessel diameters, lower hydraulic conductance, reduced water potential (WP), decreased relative water content (RWC), and shorter stomatal length compared with the high-vigor genotypes, ‘Urmia’ and ‘Damavand’. Moreover, ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ showed a greater frequency of small-diameter vessels. Collectively, these features are consistent with mechanisms associated with compact tree architecture and improved resource-use efficiency. The results highlight two accessions (‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’) as promising candidates for breeding and propagation programs aimed at developing size-controlling planting materials for modern walnut orchards.

Introduction

Rootstocks are critical for enhancing the efficiency and sustainability of orchard systems. Through grafting, the combination of rootstock and scion traits influences tree growth, productivity, and overall performance1–4. Rootstocks affect fruit quality by modifying both physical attributes and chemical composition, as well as influencing traits such as precocity, yield, tree size, stress tolerance, fruit respiration, crop load, and canopy architecture5. Consequently, rootstock selection plays a pivotal role in orchard profitability, exerting effects on vigor and yield comparable to those of the scion6.

Breeding rootstocks for fruit and nut trees is a complex and time-intensive process due to their long reproductive cycles. This complexity, coupled with the extended testing periods required for rootstock evaluation, limits opportunities for early-stage screening. The challenge is further compounded by an increasing number of selection criteria for new rootstocks7–10. As the demand for resource-efficient horticulture rises, interest in dwarfing rootstocks is growing11,12. Dwarfing rootstocks restrict vegetative growth of the scion while enhancing yield efficiency and reducing management costs associated with pruning, spraying, and harvesting, making them increasingly favored by breeders and growers13,14.

Numerous studies have identified distinct morphological, biochemical, physiological and genetic differences between dwarf and vigorous trees, which have been effectively employed as screening criteria for selecting dwarfing rootstocks. Four key traits associated with dwarfing—internode length, branch angle and branching position (basitonic, mesotonic and acrotonic), and growth rate—are known to be under genetic control15,16. Additional morphological indicators such as canopy spread and height, trunk cross-sectional area, and bark-to-wood ratio have also been identified as reliable markers for distinguishing dwarf phenotypes17. The availability of numerous mutants with defects in hormone biosynthesis or signaling pathways has greatly facilitated in-depth investigations into the mechanisms underlying dwarfism in model plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana18. Studies have demonstrated that gibberellins, as key growth-promoting hormones, exert their effects by inducing the degradation of growth-repressing DELLA proteins, thereby facilitating plant growth19. Internode length is regulated by gibberellins; however, rootstocks can modulate tree growth by converting active gibberellin forms into inactive ones, thereby altering hormonal balance and growth responses20,21. The ratio between auxin and cytokinin has been recognized as a key determinant in the regulation of dwarfism. Natural auxin, IAA, synthesized in the buds and young leaves and transported basipetally via the phloem, plays a crucial role in promoting root development and expansion. In contrast, cytokinins are synthesized in the root apical meristem and, following their translocation to aerial tissues, act to stimulate shoot growth and promote branching22,23.

Vigorous rootstocks typically exhibit higher hydraulic conductivity than their dwarf counterparts. Axial stem hydraulic conductivity is largely determined by xylem anatomical traits, especially the number and diameter of xylem vessels24,25. Dwarfing rootstocks of sweet cherry, peach, pear and apple are characterized by smaller xylem vessel diameters and surface areas, resulting in reduced stem hydraulic conductivity and vegetative growth. In apple and peach trees, the rootstock can influence shoot growth rate by altering stem water potential (WP), which is closely linked to hydraulic conductivity26–28. Evaluations of dwarfing rootstocks further reveal that their smaller vessel lumens lead to lower hydraulic conductivity, reduced leaf water potential and diminished stomatal conductance29,30 These reductions in conductivity ultimately restrict photosynthetic assimilation31. According to Tyree and Ewers (32), the number and diameter of xylem vessels are the most critical factors influencing theoretical hydraulic conductivity. Variability in hydraulic conductivity across plant species, genera and families, can be explained in part by the Hagen-Poiseuille law, which states that flow through a capillary tube is proportional to the fourth power of the radius32,33. As a result, even minor increases in vessel diameter can lead to exponential increases in hydraulic conductivity.

In addition to xylem traits, stomatal structure and function play a key role in regulating water uptake, transport and overall plant growth. Stomata—microscopic pores located on the epidermis—govern both water loss via transpiration and CO₂ uptake for photosynthesis34. The regulation of stomatal aperture is essential for balancing water conservation and carbon assimilation. Although high stomatal conductance can decrease water use efficiency, it supports faster plant growth under optimal conditions due to enhanced CO₂ uptake34.

Although the Persian walnut (Juglans regia L.) is a globally important nut crop, a reliable dwarfing rootstock has yet to be developed. However, there is strong evidence linking low-vigor walnut genotypes to early maturity or precocity35. This correlation suggests that breeding programs focused on selecting cluster-bearing or precocious genotypes may offer a promising approach for developing dwarfing walnut rootstocks10,12,30,35–40. Given the protracted and labor-intensive nature of rootstock development—an effort few breeders undertake—any strategy that shortens the breeding cycle is of considerable value. In this regard, morphological and anatomical markers are particularly advantageous due to their accessibility, ease of observation and measurement.

Given the availability of early-bearing walnut germplasm in Iran39,41, which is closely associated with low vigor, and the utility of morphological and anatomical traits as markers for selecting dwarfing rootstocks, this study was conducted. The research aimed to evaluate anatomical and physiological characteristics in walnut genotypes collected from eight regions of Iran, with the ultimate goal of identifying promising candidates for the development of dwarfing walnut rootstocks.

Materials and methods

The research was conducted in three distinct phases: selection, cultivation, and anatomical and physiological analysis, as outlined below:

Selection of candidate dwarfing genotypes

Based on previous studies identifying early-bearing, low-vigor genotypes in certain regions of Iran, eleven mature Persian walnut (Juglans regia L.) trees with varying growth vigor, grown in traditional privately owned orchards across eight regions of Iran, were initially identified and labeled (Table 1). These genotypes, characterized by cluster-bearing habits, low vigor, and a basitonic growth pattern, were selected from a larger population of 2,000 seedling trees. Half-sib (HS) seeds of the selected genotypes were harvested and purchased at harvest time.

These genotypes are named ‘Caspian 1, ‘Caspian 2’, ‘Qazvin 1’, ‘Qazvin 2’, ‘Arya’, ‘Aryan’, ‘Semnan’, ‘Zagros’, ‘Naghadeh’, ‘Marand’, and ‘Maragheh’. As control, two vigorous genotypes with terminal bearing habits and acrotonic growth pattern—‘Damavand’ and ‘Iran’—along with two semi-vigorous, laterally bearing genotypes with mesotonic growth pattern—‘Urmia’ and ‘Qazvin 3’—were also included. A total of 15 mother trees were selected.

Cultivation of phenotype-contrasting genotypes

Altogether, HS seeds from the fifteen genotypes were planted under open-air conditions at the Department of Horticulture, College of Aburaihan, University of Tehran. All maintenance practices, including irrigation (drop irrigation) and fertilization, were carried out uniformly across treatments, and their morphological traits were systematically evaluated across two years. Due to high heterozygosity, the obtained seedlings were carefully evaluated for phenotyping characteristics and off-type and highly divergent seedlings for each mother plants were removed in order to retain individuals that uniform represented the phenotypic characteristics. The experimental layout followed a randomized complete block design (RCBD) with three replicates, using a spacing of 40 × 150 cm. After a two-year evaluation of morphological traits (data not shown), ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ were identified as the least vigorous, bearing fruits in their first year, while ‘Damavand’ showed the highest vigor. For further anatomical and physiological analyses, four genotypes were selected: ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ (low vigor), ‘Urmia’ (semi-vigorous) and Damavand (vigorous).

Anatomical and physiological analysis

The late-selected genotypes were subjected to detailed anatomical evaluations to examine xylem vessel structure and calculate theoretical hydraulic conductance. Water status parameters and stomatal features were also assessed to complement the anatomical data.

Anatomical analysis

In March 2022, two years post-planting, three seedlings from each of the four selected genotypes (‘Qazvin 1’, ‘Qazvin 2’, ‘Urmia’ and ‘Damavand’) were harvested for anatomical analysis. Trunk samples were processed using a microtome to obtain cross-sections approximately 200 μm thick. Sections were stained with 2% Fuchsin to enhance contrast and mounted on microscope slides for observation under a Nikon ECLIPSE E200 light microscope equipped with a Nikon Digital Sight DS-Fi2 digital camera.

One seedling per block was chosen for detailed anatomical analysis. From each, two wood segments were sampled from one-year-old wood and three from two-year-old wood. These segments were taken from different angular positions around the trunk, each representing one-fifth of the total cross-sectional area. For each replicate, 15 photographs (three per segment) were randomly selected at 10× magnification to count and measure vessel diameters using ImageJ software (version v.1.52). Vessels were classified into four diameter categories: <30 μm, 30–60 μm, 60–90 μm, and > 90 μm. This classification was based on the collective observation of vessel types across all genotypes and does not imply that each genotype exhibited all categories. Average diameters were calculated for each category and overall by dividing the total vessel diameter by the number of vessels measured per replicate.

Total vessel area

The total vessel area (µm²) was calculated using ImageJ software (version v.1.52).

Plant water status

The water status of the plants was assessed through measurements of leaf water potential (WP) and relative water content (RWC), with the terminal leaflet at the canopy’s midpoint serving as the sampling unit. Measurements were conducted on fully expanded young leaflets in mid-July. Immediately after collection, the leaves were placed in a dark bag inside a polystyrene container and promptly transported to the laboratory to minimize physiological changes. Leaf water potential was systematically assessed at seven time points during the day (06:00, 08:00, 10:00, 12:00, 14:00, 16:00, and 18:00) across a series of post-irrigation intervals. Specifically, measurements taken at 06:00, 08:00, 10:00, 12:00, and 14:00 were performed on the 2nd, 4th, 6th, 8th, and 10th days after the final irrigation event, respectively.

Relative water content (RWC) was measured at three time points during the day (09:00, 12:00, and 15:00) on the 2nd, 6th, and 10th days after the final irrigation event. In addition, WP measurements were repeated at 16:00 and 18:00 on the 2nd and 4th days following a light re-irrigation, respectively.

WP was determined using a pressure chamber (Model 3005, Soil Moisture Equipment Corp., Santa Barbara, CA, USA). Stomatal morphology

Stomatal traits on the lower epidermis were assessed in late May. A thin layer of clear nail polish was applied to leaf surfaces, allowed to dry, and peeled using adhesive tape. The impressions were mounted on slides and examined under a Nikon ECLIPSE E200 light microscope with a DS-Fi2 digital camera. Stomatal length was measured by sampling 100 stomata per replicate, while stomatal density (number per mm²) was calculated from six leaves per replicate using ImageJ software (version v.1.52).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SAS software (version 1.6). The assumptions of normality and homogeneity of variances were assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk and Bartlett’s tests, respectively. Data that satisfied these assumptions were subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), and mean comparisons were performed using Duncan’s Multiple Range Test (DMRT) at a significance level of P ≤ 0.05.

A correlation plot based on Pearson coefficients was constructed using ggraph package under R program43.

Anatomical analysis

Vessel number

The low-vigor genotypes, ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’, exhibited the highest number of vessels in the small to medium diameter classes (< 30 μm and 30–60 μm). In contrast, the semi-vigorous genotype, ‘Urmia’, and the vigorous genotype, ‘Damavand’, displayed the lowest vessel numbers in these categories. However, both ‘Urmia’ and ‘Damavand’ showed significantly more vessels in the larger diameter classes (60–90 μm). Notably, no vessels were found in the > 90 μm category for ‘Qazvin 1’, ‘Qazvin 2’, and ‘Urmia’ (Fig. 1).

Water status

Leaf water potential (WP)

WP measurements revealed a significant influence of sampling time (Fig. 5). At early time points (6:00 AM, 8:00 AM, and 10:00 AM at 2th, 4th, and 6th days after the last irrigation), no significant differences in WP were found among the genotypes (Fig. 5A–C). However, by 12:00 PM, as temperatures rose (eight days after the last irrigation), significant differences emerged, ‘Qazvin 1’and ‘Qazvin 2’ displayed more negative WP values than ‘Urmia’ and ‘Damavand’ (Fig. 5D). The lowest WP values were recorded at 2:00 PM (10-day after last irrigation), with ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ reaching − 1.23 MPa and − 1.16 MPa, respectively, while ‘Urmia’ and ‘Damavand’ exhibited higher WP values of -1.00 MPa and − 0.95 MPa, respectively (Fig. 5E). At 4:00 PM and 6:00 PM (At two and four days following light re-irrigation, respectively), ‘Damavand’ maintained the highest WP, while ‘Qazvin 1’and ‘Qazvin 2’ consistently showed the lowest values (Fig. 5F, G).

Relative water content (RWC)

At 9:00 AM (Two days after irrigation), RWC values indicated favorable water conditions across all genotypes, with no significant differences observed (Fig. 6A). By 12:00 PM (Six days after irrigation), the highest RWC was recorded in ‘Urmia’, and the lowest in ‘Qazvin 1’ (Fig. 6B). At 3:00 PM (Ten days after irrigation), although ‘Damavand’ maintained an RWC approximately 4% higher than ‘Qazvin 1’, differences among genotypes were not statistically significant (Fig. 6C).

Stomatal morphology

No statistically significant differences were detected in stomatal density among the genotypes. However, ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ exhibited the highest values, with densities of 281 and 284.3 stomata/mm², respectively, while the lowest density was observed in ‘Damavand’ (274.6 stomata/mm²). Regarding stomatal size, the low-vigor genotypes ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ had the shortest stomata, measuring 25.67 μm and 25.85 μm, respectively. In contrast, the vigorous genotype ‘Damavand’ had the largest stomatal length at 27.45 μm (Table 3).

Correlation analysis

Significant correlations were observed between the index of the average diameter of total xylem vessels and several anatomical and physiological parameters. Specifically, this index showed strong positive correlations with the average diameter of vessels in the 60–90 μm size class (r = 0.92***), total hydraulic conductivity (r = 0.96***), and the number of vessels within the 60–90 μm range (r = 0.94***) (Fig. 7). In contrast, negative correlations were found between the same index and both the total number of vessels (r = -0.94***) and the number of vessels in the 30–60 μm group (r = -0.97***). The total vessel count exhibited a strong positive correlation with the number of vessels in the 30–60 μm category (r = 0.98***), while showing high negative correlations with the average diameter of total vessels (r = -0.94***) and the average diameter of vessels in the 60–90 μm group (r = -0.93***). Furthermore, analysis of water potential dynamics revealed that the midday water potential (12 o’clock WP) was negatively correlated with the average diameter of vessels smaller than 30 μm (r = -0.86***) (Fig. 7).

Discussion

Our findings confirm that early-bearing walnut genotypes, which exhibit slower growth, possess a higher number of xylem vessels compared to their more vigorous counterparts (Figs. 1 and 2A). However, this increase in vessel count is primarily attributed to a greater proportion of smaller-diameter vessels, with most vessels falling into the low to medium diameter categories (Fig. 1). This suggests that although the overall vessel number is higher, the size distribution is skewed towards smaller vessels, rather than being uniformly spread across all size classes (Fig. 1).

These results are consistent with numerous studies reporting that weaker genotypes typically exhibit a higher number of vessels, albeit with smaller diameters. In contrast, vigorous rootstocks and cultivars often display fewer vessels but with larger diameters. Such anatomical patterns have been observed across various tree species, including peach, apple, pear, and cherry31,40,44–48. For example, in dwarf pear trees, the average vessel diameter was reported as 58.2 μm, compared to 67.47 μm in standard-type trees40. These observations align with our findings, where less vigorous walnut genotypes exhibit smaller average vessel diameters relative to more vigorous ones (Figs. 2B and 3).

Tree height, a trait generally associated with vigor, often correlates with decreased vessel density and increased vessel diameter31, further supporting our results. However, some exceptions exist, such as in pear trees, where standard types have approximately 2.4 times greater vessel density than dwarf types40, which contrasts with our observations.

The importance of vessel characteristics cannot be overstated, as vessel elements are the primary conduits for water transport in angiosperms49–51. In trees, vessel traits are widely used to estimate hydraulic conductivity, predict size and vigor, and assess growth potential and water transport efficiency31,52,53. As water and nutrient transport depend on vessel hydraulics (Table 2), vessel diameter plays a more critical role than vessel number alone. Hydraulic conductivity is primarily determined by the size of larger vessels, and hence, traits such as tree height and growth rate are more closely linked to large-diameter vessels31,52,54,55.

According to the Hagen–Poiseuille law, vessel function is exponentially related to vessel diameter, with conductivity increasing to the fourth power of the radius32. Consequently, genotypes with a high number of small vessels exhibit significantly lower hydraulic conductivity than those with fewer, larger vessels.

In this study, we observed that the slower-growing genotypes ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ had more vessels but with smaller average diameters compared to the vigorous genotype ‘Damavand’ (Figs. 1 and 2A and B, and 3). Variations in anatomical structure resulted in a marked decrease in hydraulic conductivity, as ‘Qazvin 1’ demonstrated nearly 50% lower conductivity compared to ‘Damavand’ (1.47 × 10− 6 vs. 2.73 × 10− 6 kg·m·MPa⁻¹·s⁻¹) (Table 2). The limited presence of large-diameter vessels highlights the critical role of vessel anatomy in determining water transport efficiency (Table 2; Figs. 1 and 3).

An important question arises: how closely does theoretical hydraulic conductance, calculated using Poiseuille’s equation, align with actual hydraulic conductance? Poiseuille’s law assumes ideal cylindrical conduits, but actual xylem vessels deviate due to wall irregularities, perforation plates, and pits that increase resistance56. One underexplored factor affecting this discrepancy is vessel shape, particularly the degree of cross-sectional circularity50. According to microchannel fluid dynamics, conduits with more circular cross-sections are more efficient in fluid transport than those with irregular shapes57. Based on our observations, the more circular vessel shapes found in ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ (Fig. 4) likely result in theoretical values that more closely approximate actual hydraulic conductance. This geometric efficiency may reduce deviation from the assumptions of Poiseuille’s equation.

In this experiment, both water potential (WP) and relative water content (RWC) varied among genotypes and across the days. While WP declined (became more negative) from 6:00 AM to 10:00 AM (Two-six days after irrigation), no significant differences were observed between genotypes during this period. However, by 12:00 PM (eight days after irrigation), the low-vigor genotypes ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ exhibited significantly more negative WP values compared to the vigorous genotype. The most negative leaf water potential (WP) values were recorded at 2:00 PM, ten days after irrigation. Following a light irrigation, a relative recovery in WP was found (Fig. 5A–G). Diurnal and seasonal variations in stem WP have been well documented in both temperate and tropical tree species26,53,58–60.

For instance, it showed that peach trees grafted onto low-vigor rootstocks had significantly lower WP values compared to those grafted onto vigorous rootstocks58. Similarly, it found that changes in stem WP were closely correlated with shoot elongation rates59. These studies emphasize the critical role of water status in growth regulation.

Hydraulic conductivity has been shown to be higher in vigorous trees than in dwarf ones26–28,55,60. Since stem WP is influenced by hydraulic conductance31, low conductivity can limit stomatal conductance and photosynthetic capacity. Afternoon WP patterns, especially during the early growing season, are strong predictors of shoot growth58. Throughout the experiment, low-vigor genotypes characterized by reduced hydraulic conductivity maintained lower WP, which may have contributed to earlier stomatal closure, generally observed around midday.

In terms of RWC, no significant differences were detected at 9:00 AM (Two days after irrigation) or 3:00 PM (Twelve days after irrigation). However, at 12:00 PM (Six days after irrigation) the vigorous genotype ‘Damavand’ exhibited significantly higher RWC than ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ (Fig. 6). These patterns suggest that more vigorous genotypes maintain higher transpiration and photosynthetic activity throughout the day due to superior water status. Since transpiration reflects a plant’s capacity for water and nutrient uptake, it has a direct impact on photosynthesis and growth61. Low-vigor genotypes experienced greater water stress, as evidenced by their reduced WP and RWC (Figs. 5 and 6). Reduced water uptake triggers early physiological responses, such as stomatal closure, to minimize water loss, but this also disrupts carbon assimilation and photosynthesis62.

Plants with different stomatal sizes and densities exhibit varying responses to environmental changes. While stomatal aperture can be regulated within minutes, changes in stomatal density occur over longer periods and are genetically predetermined34. In our study, ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ showed higher stomatal density but smaller stomatal size, whereas ‘Damavand’ had fewer but larger stomata (Table 3). Previous research indicates that smaller stomata, often associated with higher density, exhibit faster responsiveness to environmental fluctuations, enhancing water-use efficiency63–66. This faster response may be attributed to the higher surface area-to-volume ratio of smaller stomata, enabling more rapid solute fluxes67.

Under increasing temperatures, stomata open to facilitate evaporative cooling but close once vapor pressure deficit (VPD) becomes too high, in order to prevent excessive water loss68. Small stomata thus provide an advantage in rapidly changing environments by enabling faster regulation of gas exchange and transpiration65–67,69.

Precocity, defined as early plant maturity, is a valuable trait in fruit breeding as it shortens the breeding cycle70,71. An inverse relationship between the length of the juvenile phase and tree size or vigor is well documented. The use of dwarfing rootstocks and early-maturing parents is now common in many fruit crops, including apple, pear, citrus, olive, cherry, and walnut35,72–74. Although precocious walnut genotypes have been identified, true dwarf rootstocks are still lacking. This study aims to identify and characterize potential dwarf rootstocks to increase planting density, productivity, and harvest efficiency.

Despite the potential of dwarf rootstocks, breeding them is generally slower than scion breeding due to the extensive evaluation required. The increasing number of selection criteria for rootstocks adds to this complexity. Therefore, the use of anatomical markers, such as vessel characteristics, in early-stage selection is of considerable value. Vessel traits are directly linked to hydraulic conductivity, which in turn affects tree size and growth rate. Our results support the hypothesis that low-vigor genotypes tend to have more, smaller-diameter vessels and lower hydraulic conductivity. In contrast, vigorous genotypes have fewer, larger vessels and higher hydraulic conductance. This pattern, observed in other tree species such as peach, apple, pear, and cherry, is also evident in walnut. Based on these findings, we propose the precocious genotypes ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ as promising candidates for walnut rootstock development aimed at regulating tree size and improving orchard management.

This study provides mechanistic insights into the anatomical and physiological determinants of dwarfism in Persian walnut highlighting the interplay between hormonal regulation, vascular architecture, and stomatal traits in shaping plant vigor. Low-vigor genotypes such as ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ exhibited reduced plant height and shortened internode length, consistent with a basitonic branching pattern that promotes compact canopy formation15,16. These morphological traits are tightly regulated by phytohormones, particularly gibberellins, auxins, and cytokinins.

Gibberellins, known for promoting stem elongation through the degradation of DELLA proteins19, are likely inactivated in dwarfing genotypes via rootstock-mediated conversion to inactive forms20,21. This hormonal suppression reduces internode elongation and overall vegetative growth. Mutations in GA biosynthesis genes often result in dwarf phenotypes across species such as Arabidopsis, maize, and wheat75,76. In fruit trees like apple, peach, citrus, banana, and sweet cherry, inhibition of GA synthesis—especially GA20ox—leads to reduced internode length and plant height, effects reversible by exogenous GA77–79. Additionally, the auxin-to-cytokinin ratio plays a pivotal role in growth modulation: auxin, synthesized in the shoot apex and transported basipetally, promotes root development, while cytokinin, produced in the root apex and transported acropetally, stimulates shoot proliferation and branching37,62. A shift in this hormonal balance may underlie the reduced shoot vigor observed in low-vigor genotypes.

Xylem vessel anatomy further differentiates dwarf and vigorous genotypes. ‘Qazvin’ genotypes had a higher number of small-diameter vessels (< 60 μm) and lacked vessels > 90 μm, whereas ‘Damavand’ exhibited fewer but significantly wider vessels, with an average diameter of 54.38 μm. According to the Hagen-Poiseuille law, hydraulic conductivity is proportional to the fourth power of vessel radius32,33, explaining the higher theoretical hydraulic conductance in ‘Damavand’ (2.73 × 10⁻⁶ kg·m·MPa⁻¹·s⁻¹) compared to ‘Qazvin 1’ (1.47 × 10⁻⁶ kg·m·MPa⁻¹·s⁻¹). These differences in xylem architecture directly influence stem water potential, leaf water status, and ultimately photosynthetic capacity26,28–31.

Stomatal traits also contributed to growth differentiation. Although stomatal density did not vary significantly, ‘Qazvin’ genotypes had shorter stomata, which may limit maximum aperture size and CO₂ uptake, thereby constraining photosynthesis and growth34. Furthermore, the more negative midday leaf water potential in ‘Qazvin’ genotypes suggests a slower rate of stomatal arm closure and reduced hydraulic responsiveness under water stress, further limiting carbon assimilation and biomass accumulation.

In summary, growth vigor in Persian walnut is governed by a complex network of hormonal signaling, vascular structure, and stomatal function. The integration of these traits—shorter internodes, altered hormone ratios, reduced vessel diameter, and smaller stomata—collectively restricts hydraulic conductivity and photosynthetic efficiency, culminating in reduced vegetative growth. These findings underscore the utility of anatomical and physiological markers for the early identification of dwarfing genotypes, offering a strategic advantage in the development of efficient, compact rootstocks for sustainable walnut orchard systems.

Conclusion

This study identified clear anatomical and physiological differences among walnut genotypes exhibiting contrasting levels of vigor. Low-vigor genotypes were characterized by smaller xylem vessel diameters and lower theoretical hydraulic conductivity compared with vigorous genotypes. These anatomical traits are consistent with reduced water transport capacity and may contribute to differences in growth behavior among genotypes.

Although hydraulic conductivity was estimated using the Hagen–Poiseuille equation, it should be noted that actual xylem vessels do not fully meet the assumptions of this model due to structural irregularities and surface roughness. In this context, the relatively higher circularity of vessels observed in the low-vigor genotypes ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’ may reduce deviations between theoretical and actual conductance. In contrast, the vigorous genotype ‘Damavand’ exhibited less circular vessels, which may further contribute to discrepancies between modeled and functional conductivity.

Low-vigor genotypes also showed more negative water potential and lower relative water content, indicating a greater degree of daytime water stress and a tendency toward earlier stomatal closure. These physiological responses are commonly associated with reduced transpiration and photosynthetic activity. Conversely, vigorous genotypes maintained higher water potential and relative water content, suggesting a greater capacity for water uptake and transport under the experimental conditions.

In accordance with the Hagen–Poiseuille law, narrower vessels are expected to limit hydraulic conductivity. Additionally, smaller stomata, as found in the low-vigor genotypes, are generally associated with faster stomatal responses to environmental changes, potentially contributing to improved water-use efficiency. While stomatal density did not differ significantly among genotypes, variation in stomatal size indicates differences in stomatal regulation rather than stomatal abundance. It should be noted that this experiment was conducted under controlled stress-irrigation conditions, and prolonged or varying environmental stresses may influence stomatal development and function through systemic signaling pathways, as reported in previous studies63,80,81.

Overall, the combination of anatomical and physiological traits found in ‘Qazvin 1’ and ‘Qazvin 2’—including smaller vessel diameter, reduced theoretical hydraulic conductivity, and lower water status parameters—suggests an association with reduced vegetative vigor. These genotypes may therefore be considered suitable candidates for further evaluation in breeding or grafting studies aimed at developing dwarfing rootstocks for Persian walnut, although additional long-term and field-based assessments are required.
