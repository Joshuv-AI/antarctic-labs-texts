Comparing the Utility of Cannabidiol Quantitation Methods for Use in High-Throughput In Vitro Assays
Authors: Gokce Alp, Behnoush Kermanshahi, Andrea Olaizola, Frantz Le Devedec, Jessica Kalra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years, isolated phytocannabinoids have received significant attention for their therapeutic potential, showing diverse pharmacological effects in vitro and in preclinical studies. However, in vitro drug efficacy and drug development studies are challenging to perform as accurate quantification of phytocannabinoids is required to generate dose response curves and pharmacokinetic profiles. While assessment of free cannabinoids is straightforward, assessing small quantities in cell culture media or as part of a formulation is difficult due to the low solubility of cannabinoids in aqueous medium and high propensity for adsorbing to plastics. Therefore, to pursue a phytocannabinoid drug development program, one of the major obstacles to overcome is selecting an appropriate analytical method that provides both accuracy and efficiency for cannabinoid quantitation. To address this challenge, three methods for quantifying cannabinoids are compared: high performance liquid chromatography (HPLC), UV/vis Spectrophotometry, and colorimetric analysis using Fast Blue B Salt (FBBS). Each method demonstrates advantages and limitations, and a comprehensive understanding of their utility in different experimental workflows is necessary for advancing the study of cannabinoids as therapeutic agents.

Introduction

The cannabis plant contains over 100 distinct phytocannabinoids, among which tetrahydrocannabinol (Δ9-THC) and cannabidiol (CBD) are the most extensively studied. These bioactive compounds interact with the endocannabinoid system in the human body, influencing a variety of physiological processes. Growing interest in the therapeutic potential of the nonpsychoactive cannabinoids: cannabidiol (CBD), cannabigerol (CBG), cannabichromene (CBC), cannabidivarin (CBDV), cannabigerovarin (CBGV), cannabichromevarin (CBCV), has instigated a surge in research and the development of diverse cannabis-derived pharmaceutical products. Of particular interest to our group is CBD as there is increasing evidence supporting antineoplastic, anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective activity of this particular cannabinoid in vitro and in vivo. − Furthermore, FDA approved CBD formulations such as Epidiolex are already being used clinically to treat intractable childhood seizure disorders. − However, despite the availability of approved drug products, current cannabinoid formulations are limited by poor and uneven bioavailability. For this reason, new dosage forms are needed.

One of the biggest challenges to developing cannabinoids as pharmaceuticals is that they are notoriously difficult to quantify. This difficulty is due in part to the physicochemical characteristics of CBD, a Biopharmaceutics Classification System (BCS) Class II compound characterized by low aqueous solubility and high membrane permeability. − These properties, combined with its high propensity for adsorbing plastics and strong protein-binding affinity, − interfere with accurate quantitation of free and formulated cannabinoids in bioassays profiling safety and efficacy.

Drug development research into cannabinoid pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics depends on analytical accuracy. For example, developing nanoformulations requires accurate cannabinoid quantitation for both encapsulation efficiency and release kinetics. However, poor cannabinoid solubility within the test system as well as use of plastic tubes and pipet tips are known to interfere with recovery and accurate measurements. − In vitro studies that employ plasticware for tissue culture are also susceptible to the same challenges. Additionally, bioassays often necessitate measurements of small quantities of cannabinoids within buffers or media, which requires extraction of cannabinoids from these matrices. Moreover, high-throughput screening relies on microplates often composed of incompatible plastics. Characterization of cannabinoid adsorption to polymeric materials and binding to media proteins, is important to accurately determine the bioactive concentration. This understanding is necessary to inform best practices for generating and interpreting dose–response relationships derived from efficacy studies. Furthermore, some cell culture assays require quantitation of drugs as part of the workflow. For example, to test permeability using standard cell-based assays (e.g., Caco-2 permeability assays) involves extracting cannabinoids from cell culture media at different time-points to generate a kinetic profile. This is challenging because of the low concentrations of cannabinoids used in these assays, the poor solubility of the drug, the adsorption of cannabinoids to the plasticware and high propensity of protein binding. For these reasons, a validated method for extraction and quantitation of cannabinoids is essential to continue drug development efforts. It should also be noted that precise quantitation in preclinical and clinical studies is necessary to identify drug concentrations that elicit maximal therapeutic efficacy while minimizing adverse effects. Finally, for regulatory compliance, reliable quantification is necessary to verify product labeling and control formulation composition. −

High-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) remains a widely accepted gold standard method for cannabinoid quantitation due to its precision, sensitivity, and ability to separate individual analytes. Previous studies have validated the reproducibility and accuracy of HPLC, establishing this method as a benchmark for comparison. , However, HPLC has several weaknesses as the methods often require extensive sample preparation, including extraction and cleanup, particularly when analyzing complex biological matrices. Moreover, while HPLC effectively quantifies parent cannabinoids, it lacks the structural resolution to differentiate metabolites or degradation products without being coupled to a mass spectrometer. , This makes it less suitable for comprehensive pharmacokinetic or stability studies in drug development.

Colorimetric assays, such as those employing Fast Blue B Salt (FBBS), have gained attention for their simplicity, speed, possible high-throughput scale and use of common instrumentation without the requirement for specific expertise. , These methods rely on the formation of a chromophore upon reaction between phenolic cannabinoids and FBBS, enabling quantification by plate reader or spectrophotometry or identification using thin layer chromatography. Mechanistically, the reaction follows a pathway in which the phenolic hydroxyl group of CBD undergoes alkaline oxidation to form a quinone intermediate, which subsequently undergoes azo coupling with the diazonium moiety of FBBS (Figure ).

Molecular structures of FBBS (A) and CBD (B) and their representative reaction under alkaline conditions to form a colored azo chromophore (CBD–FBBS complex) (C).

This reaction produces a stable azo–quinone complex characterized by an intense coloration in the visible range, the absorption wavelength of which depends on concentration and reaction conditions. − However, the FBBS assay lacks specificity, as multiple cannabinoids and phenolic compounds can produce similar chromophores, leading to potential overestimation or false positives in mixed samples. Additionally, FBBS reactions are time- and concentration-dependent, with shifting absorbance peaks and precipitation issues at higher concentrations, complicating standardization. , These shortcomings limit its utility for regulatory applications where high specificity and reproducibility are required.

UV–visible (UV–vis) spectroscopy represents another attractive method for routine CBD analysis due to its low cost, ease of use, and nondestructive nature. Nevertheless, UV–vis suffers from poor selectivity in complex matrices, where proteins, lipids, and other endogenous components can interfere with absorbance measurements. The method also lacks the sensitivity needed to detect cannabinoids at the low concentrations typically encountered in pharmacokinetic studies unless derivatization or enrichment steps are performed. −

Liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry (LC–MS) has emerged as the most powerful method for cannabinoid analysis, providing unparalleled sensitivity and structural resolution. LC–MS not only distinguishes CBD from other cannabinoids with similar UV absorbance but also identifies metabolites and degradation products, making it the preferred tool for advanced pharmacokinetic and stability studies. LC–MS enables simultaneous detection of multiple cannabinoids at low concentrations in complex biological matrices. Numerous studies have demonstrated effective chromatographic separation of lipophilic cannabinoids in buffers and biological fluids using appropriate sample preparation strategies. − The present study does not aim to replace LC–MS/MS methodologies, but rather to evaluate the performance, limitations, and practical constraints of more accessible analytical techniques that are commonly employed in formulation development and in vitro screening laboratories where mass spectrometry may not be routinely available. We focused our comparison on HPLC, FBBS-based colorimetric assays, and UV–vis spectroscopy, which allowed us to evaluate performance trade-offs among accessible techniques for CBD quantification.

There is a paucity of information in the literature describing the challenges related to cannabinoid quantitation in the context of drug discovery and development. For instance, it is widely accepted that poor solubility of CBD interferes with accurate quantitation in commonly used buffers. Furthermore, anecdotally, investigators are aware of the issues regarding adsorption of cannabinoids to plasticware, however, to our knowledge these issues are not described in the literature, particularly as it pertains to preclinical assessment of cannabinoids. Therefore, the studies described herein were undertaken to further characterize and document these challenges while also comparing the efficacy of four quantification methods for cannabinoids, and specifically, CBD: 1. HPLC; 2. UV–vis spectroscopy; 3. Fast Blue B Salt-based colorimetric assay; and 4. UV–vis spectroscopy following derivatization with Fast Blue B Salt.

While this study focuses specifically on CBD, its selection is intentional. CBD is among the most widely studied nonpsychoactive cannabinoids in formulation development and in vitro efficacy assays, and it exhibits physicochemical propertieshigh lipophilicity, low aqueous solubility, strong protein binding, and adsorption to plasticsthat pose substantial analytical challenges. Although other cannabinoids may differ in lipophilicity or polarity, the methodological considerations evaluated here are most directly applicable to CBD. Extension of these findings to other cannabinoids will require additional validation and is beyond the scope of the present study. By evaluating the performance of these techniques under standardized conditions, this study aims to translate these methods into viable options for cannabinoid quantitation in the context of drug development. To ensure comparability, all methods were implemented using standardized protocols and calibrated with reference standards of known CBD concentration. Sample preparation techniques were optimized to maximize recovery and reduce matrix effects. This is the first study to directly compare these three methods for CBD quantification. The findings presented herein are intended to establish best practices in cannabinoid analysis across both research and industry settings.

Methods

Materials

Cannabidiol was generously provided by Avicanna (Toronto, Canada). Hexane, acetonitrile, ethanol, sodium hydroxide, Fast Blue B salt (FBBS), Tween 80, dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) and bovine serum albumin (BSA) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Phosphate buffered saline (PBS) was purchased from Corning. All the reagents were of analytical grade. Deionized (DI) water was used throughout the experiments and all chemicals were used as received.

Quantification of CBD

Quantification of CBD Using HPLC

CBD quantification was carried out using HPLC (Agilent Technologies 1260 Infinity II) equipped with a Variable Wavelength Detector (VWS) with the software Agilent OpenLab CDS (Chromatography Data System). A 20 μL injection volume was used for all samples, which were separated on Agilent Poreshell 120; EC-18 2.7 μm; 3.0 × 150 mm column maintained at 30 °C. The mobile phase A consisted of 70% (v/v) acetonitrile in HPLC grade water containing 0.1% (v/v) formic acid and 5 mM ammonium formate and the mobile phase B consisted of 0.1% (v/v) formic acid in methanol delivered at a flow rate of 0.8 mL/min. Detection was carried out at 212 nm using a UV detector. Calibration curves were constructed by plotting peak area versus known CBD concentrations. Each sample was analyzed in triplicate.

Quantification of CBD Using UV–vis Spectroscopy

UV–vis Spectroscopy: A UV–vis Spectrophotometer (Cary 60, Agilent) was used to measure solutions composed of CBD in the region from 190 to 900 nm and quantification was performed at 270 nm, corresponding to the characteristic absorption maximum of CBD. Measurements were conducted individually in quartz cuvettes. The CBD concentration ranged from 0.0005 to 1 mg/mL (0.00159 mM to 3.18 mM) in ACN, with HPLC-grade ACN used as the blank. Each measurement was performed in triplicate, and mean absorbance values were plotted against theoretical concentrations to generate a calibration curve. Linear regression analysis was used to determine the calibration equation and assess the method’s linearity.

Quantification of Free CBD Using the FBBS Method

Preparation of the Solutions

Stock solutions of CBD (50, 10, 5, and 1 mg/mL) were prepared by dissolving the CBD isolate powder in acetonitrile. Working solutions (1–500 μg/mL) were prepared by diluting the 1 mg/mL stock solution with acetonitrile. All solutions were freshly prepared prior to each experiment and stored at –20 °C until use. Solutions of FBBS (25 μg/mL) and NaOH (0.025 M) were prepared by dissolving the reagents in DI water.

Sample Preparation

CBD working solutions were prepared in acetonitrile (ACN) and aliquoted into individual wells of a polystyrene 96-well plate (VWR). Subsequently, FBBS and NaOH solutions were added in a 1:1:1 (v/v/v) ratio (CBD/FBBS/NaOH), following established protocols. − The CBD concentration range was kept at 0.001 to 0.1 mg/mL (0.00318 mM to 0.318 mM) to maintain solubility. The same sample preparation procedure was followed for all subsequent measurements performed on the multimode plate reader.

Multimode Reader

A multimode microplate reader (Cytation 5, BioTek Instruments, USA) operated with the Gen5Microplate Reader and Imager Software was employed for absorbance measurements. The instrument offers multiple detection modes, including the Spectral Scanning Mode, which was first used to identify the maximum absorbance wavelength of the CBD–FBBS complex. Measurements were conducted in 96-well plates over a wavelength range of 350 to 700 nm in Absorbance mode. Plates were read from the bottom with lid on. Following spectral analysis, the wavelength corresponding to the maximum absorbance of the CBD–FBBS complex was selected for further quantification using End Point Mode.

All absorbance-based measurements were performed using clear, flat-bottom 96-well polystyrene plates to ensure accurate transmission of incident light through the sample, as required for UV–Vis and colorimetric absorbance detection. Clear plates are the standard format for absorbance-based assays because opaque-walled plates, while commonly used for fluorescence or luminescence measurements, impede transmitted light and can distort absorbance readings. To minimize potential optical cross-talk between adjacent wells, samples and standards were arranged with buffer-only wells separating concentration series, and measurements were acquired under uniform path length and read-height conditions. Under these conditions, no systematic edge or adjacency effects were observed.

Quantification of Free CBD Using the FBBS Method via UV/Vis

Samples were prepared as described in Section , Preparation of the Solutions.

UV–vis Spectroscopy: A UV–vis Spectrophotometer (Cary 60, Agilent) was used to measure solutions composed of CBD/FBBS/NaOH (1:1:1 v/v) in the region from 350 to 650 nm. Measurements were conducted individually in quartz cuvettes. The CBD concentration ranged from 0.001 to 0.1 mg/mL and a mixture of ACN/FBBS/NaOH (1:1:1 v/v) was used as the blank. A time-course study was completed to monitor the spectral changes of the CBD–FBBS complex over 10 min using UV–Vis spectroscopy, with absorbance spectra recorded at 2 min intervals between 350 and 700 nm to assess the reaction kinetics and peak stability.

Solubility Assessment of CBD

To evaluate the solubility of CBD in PBS containing 0.5% (v/v) Tween 80, CBD solutions were prepared in glass beakers at concentrations of 50, 100, 200, 400, 500, and 600 μg/mL (0.159 mM to 1.91 mM). To compare the solubility of CBD in other aqueous mediums, 100 μg/mL CBD solutions were prepared in pure water and in PBS (as controls), in 0.2% Tween 80-PBS solution and in 1% Tween 80-PBS solution. The solutions were incubated overnight at 37 °C in a shaking incubator at 300 rpm, to ensure adequate mixing and equilibration. Following incubation, 1 mL aliquots were immediately withdrawn from each sample, filtered through 0.22 μm syringe filters to remove undissolved CBD, and subsequently diluted 1:10 with the HPLC mobile phase (70:30, v/v, ACN/water). The resulting samples were then analyzed by HPLC to quantify the solubilized CBD. Each solution was prepared and analyzed in triplicate.

Extraction of Free CBD from PBS and Cell Culture Media

Enhanced Matrix Removal (EMR) Cartridges

Briefly, CBD was extracted from acetonitrile or from aqueous media (PBS or water) using Captiva EMR-Lipid Cartridges (Agilent). A mixture of acetonitrile and water (80:20, v/v) was added to each sample, followed by vigorous shaking and 15 min of sonication to disrupt the nanoparticles in formulation. A 2.5 mL aliquot of the sample supernatant was passed through an unconditioned 300 mg/3 mL Captiva EMR-Lipid cartridge followed by a secondary elution step with 0.6 mL of the same acetonitrile and water mixture (80:20, v/v). CBD quantification was carried out using HPLC.

Solid-phase Extraction (SPE) Columns

Solid-phase extraction was performed using Agilent Bond Elut C8 cartridges (1 mL capacity) to isolate CBD from cell culture media prior to HPLC quantification. A 0.01 mg/mL (0.0318 mM) CBD working solution in complete cell culture medium was prepared by diluting a 0.1 mg/mL CBD stock solution in ethanol. A 500 μL aliquot of this CBD-spiked medium (containing approximately 0.005 mg CBD) was filtered through a 0.2 μm syringe filter to remove particulates and then loaded onto the SPE column by gravity flow. Following sample loading, the column was washed with 1 mL deionized water to remove residual salts, sugars, and proteins from the matrix. CBD was subsequently eluted using ACN in two successive 250 μL portions, allowing each solvent addition to pass through by gravity. During the first ACN elution, a faint pink coloration was observed in the effluent, suggesting partial coelution of matrix components; the collection tube was changed before the second elution to ensure clear separation of the CBD-rich fraction. Combined ACN eluates were collected and further analyzed via HPLC.

Hexane Liquid–Liquid Extraction (LLE)

For extraction from PBS, PBS + BSA (1%, 5%, 10%, 20% and 40% (w/w)) and cell culture media, CBD was first fully dissolved in DMSO, and the final sample was prepared in the relevant aqueous phase to achieve a final DMSO concentration of 1%. To extract CBD, 100 μL of the sample was transferred into a 16 mm × 50 mm glass tube. Lipid precipitation was initiated by adding 400 μL of cold acetonitrile (prechilled at −20 °C for 30 min), followed by vortex mixing for 1 min. Next, 400 μL of distilled water was added, and the mixture was vortexed for an additional minute. Subsequently, 2000 μL of n-hexane was added to extract CBD into the organic phase, and the sample was vortexed for 5 min. The mixture was then centrifuged at 1200g for 15 min at 10 °C. The upper organic layer was carefully transferred to a clean glass tube using a glass pipet. The solvent was evaporated under a stream of air at 35 °C and the resulting residue was reconstituted in 300 μL of acetonitrile. The sample was further prepared for quantification with HPLC or with FBBS as described previously.

Comparing Glass and Plastic

To assess the adsorption of free CBD on plastic materials and to compare the recovery, the extraction method described in Section was employed using polystyrene falcon tubes (Corning) or 2 mL eppendorf centrifuge tubes (Eppendorf) instead of glass tubes throughout the process.

CBD Quantification from CBD Loaded Lipid-Based Nanoparticle Formulations

CBD-SEDDS A was prepared by dissolving CBD isolate in a mixture containing 50% MCT oil (Nature’s Way Canada, Nova Scotia, Canada) and 50% Tween 80 (Sigma-Aldrich) at 70 °C. Once CBD was fully dissolved in the oil phase, ultrapure water was added at a 1:100 oil-to-water ratio, and the resulting dispersion vortexed vigorously for 30s. CBD-Liposomes were prepared using the NanoAssemblr Benchtop microfluidic instrument (PrecisionNanoSystems, Vancouver, Canada). POPC (1-palmitoyl-2-oleoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine), cholesterol, and DSPE-PEG (1,2-Distearoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphoethanolamine-Poly­(ethylene glycol)) (Avanti Polar Lipids) were dissolved in absolute ethanol at a molar ratio of 72:25:3, along with CBD which was solubilized in the organic phase for a final concentration of 1.4 mg/mL in the liposome. 1X PBS (Fisher Scientific) was used as the aqueous phase. To form monodisperse liposomes, the organic and aqueous phases were mixed at 1.5:1 (aqueous: ethanol) flow rate ratio at a total flow rate of 12 mL/min. CBD-SEDDS A and CBD-Liposomes were diluted in water and PBS, respectively for a final CBD concentration of 0.3 μg/mL. After dilution, sample preparation was utilized for extraction as described above.

Results & Discussion

Quantification of Free CBD

Quantification of Free CBD Using HPLC

HPLC remains the gold standard for CBD quantification due to its sensitivity, specificity, and reproducibility. , The method used in this study demonstrated excellent linearity in the range of 0.2–20 μg/mL (Figure ), with an R 2 value of 0.99 when quantifying free CBD.

Experimental vs theoretical free CBD concentrations measured via HPLC.

However, a major challenge in using HPLC to quantify CBD in formulation samples or bioassays is that the injected sample must be devoid of lipids, surfactants, and salts, as these components can interfere with chromatographic separation, damage the column, or suppress the detector response. Because formulations and test samples often contain excipients such as proteins, oils and surfactants (e.g., Tween 80), direct injection is not possible without prior sample cleanup. For this reason, methods of CBD extraction and sample cleanup followed by HPLC quantitation are compared as described in Section .

Quantification of Free CBD Using UV–vis Spectroscopy

To evaluate the feasibility of direct UV–Vis analysis for CBD quantification, calibration standards were prepared in acetonitrile within a concentration range of 0.5–100 μg/mL (0.00159 mM - 0.318 mM) and analyzed at 270 nm, corresponding to CBD’s characteristic absorbance maximum in the UV region. , As shown in Figure , a linear relationship was observed between absorbance and concentration, with an R 2 value of 0.974. While these results indicate acceptable linearity across a wide concentration range, the correlation coefficient and baseline offset suggest limited sensitivity at lower concentrations and potential background interference from solvent or instrument noise. Furthermore, UV–Vis spectroscopy lacks molecular selectivity, and in complex matrices containing other UV-absorbing species (e.g., lipids, proteins, or excipients), spectral overlap can significantly compromise accuracy. Therefore, although UV–Vis analysis provides a rapid, reagent-free approach for estimating CBD concentration in simple solutions, its utility for quantitative applications in formulation or biological samples is restricted by low specificity and relatively high detection limits.

Calibration curve for free CBD concentrations measured via UV–vis Spectroscopy.

This preliminary UV–Vis calibration served as a baseline assessment of CBD’s direct quantifiability without derivatization or complex sample preparation. Compared to the FBBS-based colorimetric assay and HPLC methods, the UV–Vis approach offered the advantages of simplicity, minimal reagent use, and rapid analysis. However, the relatively low molar absorptivity of CBD at 270 nm and the limited sensitivity at concentrations below 1 μg/mL restrict its applicability to relatively concentrated or purified samples. As such, while UV–Vis spectroscopy provides a convenient tool for quick estimation of CBD content in simple matrices, it is insufficient as a standalone quantitative technique for formulations or biological media where interference and low analyte concentrations are expected.

Quantification of Free CBD Using the FBBS Method

The FBBS colorimetric method was investigated as a potential alternative to chromatographic and spectrophotometric techniques for the quantification of CBD. The rationale for pursuing a colorimetric method lies in its compatibility with high-throughput screening (HTS) platforms that are important for drug development and lead optimization in pharmaceutical research. Colorimetric assays are also amenable to miniaturization and parallel processing, which enable HTS. The reaction end points can be easily quantified by a standard microplate reader which has the potential to eliminate the need for complex, time-consuming separation steps or specialized, expensive instrumentation. Additionally, an assay such as this would increase screening capacity, enable automation and reduce expenditure of reagents, enzymes, and compounds, making the screening process more cost-effective and sustainable at scale. Quantification of free CBD via FBBS Method was carried out in clear, flat-bottom 96-well polystyrene plates. Although opaque-walled plates are frequently used in high-throughput screening formats to reduce optical bleed-through in fluorescence-based assays, they are not compatible with absorbance-based measurements that rely on transmitted light. In the present study, the use of clear plates was therefore necessary for UV–Vis and FBBS colorimetric detection. While minor optical cross-talk can theoretically occur in absorbance assays, its impact is generally negligible compared to matrix effects and chromophore stability. Future adaptation of FBBS chemistry to automated or high-throughput platforms may benefit from alternative plate designs (e.g., clear wells with opaque walls) to further minimize interwell optical interference.

Prior to evaluating CBD–FBBS absorbance, spectral scans of FBBS solutions alone (0.005–10 mg/mL) confirmed that the reagent exhibited no absorbance in the 350–700 nm range (Figure A). Similarly, CBD solutions in NaOH showed negligible absorbance across this range (Figure B), indicating that any measurable signal would arise specifically from the reaction between CBD and FBBS. It should be emphasized that this set of studies were conducted using polystyrene cell culture plates.

UV–vis spectra of FBBS solutions (0.005 mg/mL - 10 mg/mL) in water (A). UV–vis spectra of CBD solutions after the addition of NaOH (0.001–1 mg/mL) (B). All measurements were obtained with spectral scanning mode (n = 3).

CBD working solutions were mixed with FBBS and NaOH in a 1:1:1 (v/v/v) ratio and allowed to react. Spectral scans were performed using the multimode reader’s “Scanning Mode”. The resulting UV–vis spectra (Figure A) revealed that the CBD–FBBS complex produced a distinct absorbance peak in the visible region, but the wavelength of maximum absorbance varied with concentration. For midrange concentrations (0.05–0.25 mg/mL/0.159–0.795 mM), the peak absorbance appeared around 500 nm, consistent with the formation of a highly conjugated azo chromophore resulting from the electrophilic aromatic substitution between the phenolic moiety of CBD and the diazonium group of FBBS. However, at lower concentrations (≤0.01 mg/mL), the absorbance maximum shifted toward shorter wavelengths (∼450 nm), a phenomenon known as hypsochromic (blue) shift. This behavior likely reflects a change in the electronic environment of the CBD–FBBS complex, possibly due to altered coupling stoichiometry, dilution effects, or reduced molecular aggregation at lower CBD concentrations. The spectral shift obtained for lower concentrations can be attributed to several factors, including: (i) changes in reaction stoichiometry, where excess FBBS at lower CBD concentrations may favor formation of less-conjugated or incomplete azo adducts; (ii) reduced chromophore aggregation, which minimizes π–π stacking interactions that typically enhance bathochromic behavior; and (iii) solvatochromic effects, wherein increased solvent interaction at dilute concentrations stabilizes ground-state species, leading to absorption at higher energy. Additionally, slower reaction kinetics at low analyte concentrations may result in transient intermediates contributing to blue-shifted absorbance. ,

UV–vis spectra of CBD solutions after the addition of FBBS and NaOH (0.001–50 mg/mL) (A), UV–vis spectra for solutions with concentrations between 0.001 and 0.25 mg/mL (B). Measurements were obtained with spectral scanning mode (n = 3). CBD solutions (0.001–1 mg/mL) after addition of FBBS and NaOH showing precipitation (blue arrows) at concentration greater than 0.5 mg/mL (C). Image source: Authors’ own work.

At the higher end of the tested range, CBD concentrations of 10 and 50 mg/mL produced saturated absorbance values (>1 AU), leading to unreliable quantification and distorted peak profiles. Although 5 mg/mL did not exceed the absorbance threshold, it was excluded from further analysis because the spectral peak was poorly resolved and lacked a defined maximum, suggesting incomplete solubilization or early stage precipitation. In fact, visible precipitation of the CBD–FBBS complex was observed in samples with concentrations above 0.1 mg/mL (Figure C), which is consistent with CBD’s limited aqueous solubility in the FBBS/NaOH reaction environment. These findings highlight the need to select an appropriate concentration range that ensures complete solubilization, reproducible chromophore formation, and stable absorbance characteristics. The spectral view of the lower concentration range (Figure B) shows the distinct peak shifts and well-formed absorbance maxima.

The results clearly demonstrate that both absorbance intensity and peak wavelength are influenced by CBD concentration and highlight the importance of selecting an appropriate and solubility-compliant range to avoid errors due to saturation, precipitation, or spectral variability. These findings also emphasize the importance of selecting a fixed wavelength within the linear and stable absorbance range for quantitative end point measurements and highlight the need for rigorous concentration control when applying FBBS-based colorimetric assays for CBD quantification.

Based on spectral scanning results, 500 nm was selected as the optimal wavelength for generating a calibration curve, as it corresponded to the maximum absorbance (λmax) of the CBD–FBBS complex at moderate concentrations (0.05–0.25 mg/mL/0.159–0.795 mM), offering the highest sensitivity and linearity while minimizing spectral variability and background interference from the reaction matrix. Using these parameters, a calibration curve was generated and the R 2 value was 0.96 (Figure A), which is comparable to previous studies using similar chromogenic reactions.

Calibration curve for CBD solutions (0.001–0.1 mg/mL) obtained with the spectral scanning mode where R 2 = 0.9793 (A). Comparison of the curves obtained with end point mode immediately and 30 min after the addition of NaOH (B). Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (n = 3 independent replicates).

Following the identification of the wavelength corresponding to maximum absorbance, subsequent measurements were conducted using “Endpoint Mode”, which enables fixed-wavelength detection in a microplate format. Sample preparation was identical to the spectral scan experiments; however, absorbance was recorded at three discrete wavelengths: 450, 500, and 550 nm, to account for the previously observed concentration-dependent spectral shifts. Data was collected using clear, nonprecipitated CBD solutions in the range of 0.001–0.1 mg/mL (0.00318–0.318 mM), based on absorbance values measured at 500 nm, where the most stable and maximum absorbance was observed across concentrations. While the overall response across the tested concentration range displays a quadratic trend for the end point mode at higher concentrations, a linear relationship is maintained up to 0.025 mg/mL (Figure B).

Time-dependency was evident, as absorbance values changed within the first 30 min after reaction initiation (Figure B), necessitating kinetic analysis to identify the optimal reaction time for reliable quantification as described below (Figure ).

Quantification with FBBS via UV–vis & Time-Resolved Absorption of the Solutions

To investigate the stability and kinetics of the chromogenic reaction between CBD and FBBS, UV–vis spectral scans were performed at 2 min intervals for CBD concentrations ranging from 0.001 to 0.1 mg/mL (0.00318–0.318 mM). As shown in Figure A–F, the absorbance profiles of the CBD-FBBS-NaOH mixtures changed over time, with notable shifts in both peak intensity and wavelength. Specifically, the absorbance peaks gradually shifted toward longer wavelengths (i.e., a red shift) over the first 10 min of the reaction, particularly at higher CBD concentrations (≥0.05 mg/mL). For example, at 0.1 mg/mL, the maximum absorbance shifted from approximately 470 nm to around 500 nm over the course of 10 min (Figure A), while the shift was less pronounced or absent at lower concentrations such as 0.001 mg/mL (Figure F).

This time- and concentration-dependent bathochromic shift is indicative of continued formation and stabilization of the CBD–FBBS azo chromophore. The reaction between CBD (a phenolic compound) and FBBS (a diazonium salt) proceeds via electrophilic aromatic substitution, forming an azo bond (−NN−) between the aromatic ring of CBD and the diazonium group of FBBS in an alkaline environment. This coupling results in an extended conjugated system that absorbs visible light, producing a measurable color change. −

The red shift observed in Figure reflects either continued reaction (i.e., incomplete coupling at early time points) or gradual structural reorganization of the conjugated chromophore in solution. This effect has been observed in similar diazo-coupling reactions with phenolic cannabinoids and other aromatic analytes. Additionally, peak broadening and shifts may be influenced by solvent polarity and ionic strength, both of which can alter the electronic environment of the chromophore. , In addition to the bathochromic shift of the primary absorbance peak, a secondary peak initially observed around 350 nm progressively diminished over time, indicating either the consumption of intermediate species or a rearrangement of the chromophore as the CBD–FBBS complex stabilized.

Notably, the peak wavelength appeared to be stabilized after approximately 6 min for all concentrations tested, indicating the reaction reaches a pseudoequilibrium within that time frame. Based on this observation, subsequent UV–vis end point measurements were standardized to 10 min postreaction to ensure reproducibility and minimize spectral drift (Figure A). The calibration curve constructed at this time point (Figure B) showed the relationship between CBD concentration and absorbance, reinforcing the suitability of this approach for routine quantification within solubility-compliant ranges (≤0.1 mg/mL).

UV–vis spectra for CBD solutions (0.001–0.1 mg/mL) obtained 10 min after the addition of FBBS and NaOH (A). Calibration curve for CBD solutions obtained with UV–vis (B). (n = 3 independent replicates).

These findings emphasize the importance of timing and wavelength selection when employing the FBBS method for quantification of cannabinoids. Failure to account for the evolving absorbance spectrum can lead to inaccurate quantification, particularly in settings where precise CBD measurements are critical (e.g., pharmaceutical formulation). Further investigation into the kinetics and mechanistic aspects of the CBD-FBBS interaction may enable the development of more robust, time-independent protocols.

Solubility Assessment of CBD

CBD is a highly lipophilic compound with inherently low aqueous solubility, which presents a major challenge for consistent analytical quantification in biological or buffered systems. − To address this, we first evaluated CBD solubility in different aqueous systems with and without surfactants. Preliminary trials demonstrated that CBD readily precipitated in PBS and water alone, while addition of nonionic surfactants such as Tween 80 markedly improved dispersion and solubility. , Based on these observations, PBS supplemented with 0.5% (v/v) Tween 80 was selected as the optimal medium, as this concentration of Tween 80 was sufficient to maintain CBD in solution without introducing excess surfactant that could interfere with downstream analyses. As shown in Figure A, up to 100 μg/mL (0.318 mM) of CBD can be readily solubilized in PBS when Tween 80 is present. At higher concentrations, visible precipitation was observed (Figure B). As shown in Figure B, two different phase separations of CBD were observed. The yellowish phase (blue arrows) floating on top of the solution are CBD-rich oil droplets. It can be seen that, as the CBD concentration in the solution increases, the volume of the oil droplet increases. CBD also precipitates and settles at the bottom (magenta arrows). Subsequent HPLC analysis of filtered samples confirmed a plateau or decrease in measurable CBD content, indicating that the system had reached supersaturation, where the dissolved CBD concentration temporarily exceeds its equilibrium solubility limit. Under these conditions, the excess CBD becomes thermodynamically unstable and begins to separate from solution as a distinct solid or oil phase, as in Figure B. This behavior marks the upper solubility threshold of CBD in the PBS Tween 80 system.

The rationale for using PBS containing Tween 80 extends beyond improving CBD solubility. PBS provides a physiologically relevant buffered environment that maintains pH stability, while Tween 80 serves to mimic the solubilizing effect of biological surfactants. This system is directly applicable for downstream assays such as determination of encapsulation efficiency and release kinetics of CBD-loaded formulations, where aqueous buffer conditions are necessary to simulate in vitro environments and ensure accurate performance evaluation of drug delivery systems. ,

The solubility constraints directly informed the selection of CBD concentrations used in downstream analyses with both the FBBS colorimetric method and UV–vis spectroscopy. Specifically, to ensure complete solubilization and eliminate variability due to precipitation, a maximum CBD concentration of 0.1 mg/mL (100 μg/mL) was used for all further spectrophotometric assays, including endpoint plate reader and time-resolved UV–vis studies. This concentration is well below the solubility threshold in PBS-Tween 80, ensuring solution clarity and analytical reproducibility. Furthermore, previous results show that at 100 μg/mL precipitation is not observed, and staying below this concentration minimizes signal interference and improves the accuracy of calibration curves. These results highlight the importance of incorporating solubility profiling early in method development to define reliable concentration ranges for quantitative assays.

Comparing Methods of Sample Cleanup and CBD Extraction

Accurate quantification of CBD in complex matrices requires an effective extraction step to separate the analyte from interfering components. This is particularly important in the context of drug efficacy testing, where CBD concentrations must often be measured in biological assays using buffers or cell culture media. In such systems, proteins, salts, and other media components can mask or interfere with CBD detection if not adequately removed prior to analysis. Similarly, in drug development, CBD may be incorporated into lipid-based formulations, such as liposomes or self-emulsifying drug delivery systems (SEDDS), where lipids and surfactants must be eliminated to reliably assess parameters such as encapsulation efficiency and release kinetics. Without efficient extraction, both over- and underestimation of CBD concentrations may occur, compromising the validity of analytical results. To address these challenges, we evaluated three different extraction strategies to remove interfering matrix components before assessing CBD recovery using HPLC: 1. EMR cartridges; 2. SPE columns; and 3. n-hexane-based liquid–liquid extraction (LLE). Each method was selected based on its potential to remove specific interfering components associated with either formulation matrices (lipids, surfactants) or biological media (proteins, salts).

EMR-Lipid Cartridges were tested primarily for lipid-based formulations (CBD-SEDDS A and CBD-liposomes), where lipid removal is essential to prevent chromatographic interference. Studies report that EMR-Lipid cleanup can provide high recoveries for cannabinoids in fat-rich food matrices (typically ∼82–115% depending on matrix and analyte) and that Captiva/EMR cartridges effectively remove phospholipids and lipids to improve LC–MS performance in biological samples. In this study, when free CBD in solvent was extracted using EMR cartridges, recovery was 82%, indicating good retention and elution efficiency under clean conditions. However, recoveries decreased in more complex samples. Recovery was 63% for CBD-SEDDS A and 79% for CBD-liposomes, suggesting partial retention of CBD within the sorbent phase or coelution losses due to interactions between CBD and formulation excipients. Comparable trends have been reported in the literature, where extraction recoveries for cannabinoids or other highly lipophilic compounds using dispersive or cartridge-based cleanup systems typically range from 60–85%, depending on matrix composition and solvent strength. While EMR cartridges effectively reduce lipid carryover, their performance for highly lipophilic compounds like CBD may be limited by nonspecific adsorption, particularly when complex formulation components compete for binding sites. Also, extraction efficiency will vary with matrix composition and extraction workflow (e.g., extraction solvent, evaporation strategy and use of modifiers). These observations are consistent with previously reported EMR-lipid applications − and vendor evaluations.

SPE columns were evaluated to remove proteins and salts from biological matrices. When tested with cell culture media, SPE yielded a low recovery of 25%, indicating substantial loss of CBD during loading or washing steps, likely due to strong hydrophobic binding to the stationary phase or matrix-induced retention. In contrast, when tested with ethanol, recovery improved markedly increasing to 81%, confirming that the low recovery in media was attributable to protein and macromolecular interference rather than poor elution efficiency. Although SPE offers high selectivity and cleanliness of eluates, it appears less suited for direct extraction of CBD from complex biological fluids without additional optimization of elution solvents or pretreatment steps to release protein-bound drugs.

As presented in the following section, LLE proved to be effective and fastest, producing clear samples that could be directly injected into the HPLC system without clogging the column. Importantly, hexane extraction showed consistent recovery of CBD, enabling accurate quantification across the tested range. Thus, the combination of n-hexane LLE followed by HPLC analysis provided a robust workflow for reliable CBD quantification in complex matrices.

As summarized in Table , the hexane-based LLE protocol was first evaluated in simple matrices to establish baseline recovery. In water and PBS, recovery was consistently high: 83.77% and 97.22%, respectively, confirming that the extraction method was effective when matrix interference was minimal. Interestingly, extraction of free CBD from acetonitrile yielded a lower recovery (78.11%) compared to water or PBS. This reduced efficiency is likely due to the lower polarity difference between acetonitrile and hexane, which diminishes CBD’s partitioning into the organic phase. In contrast, when CBD is present in highly polar aqueous matrices (water or PBS), the stronger polarity gradient favors its transfer into hexane, resulting in higher recovery.

The values given in Table are for the LLE implemented using glass tubes and glass pipettes. When the same extraction procedure was performed using polystyrene Falcon tubes instead of glassware, recovery decreased substantially across all matrices, with values of 70.21% for water, 73.46% for PBS, and 65.35% for acetonitrile (Table & Figure ). This reduction is most likely due to adsorption of CBD onto the plastic surfaces, consistent with its strong lipophilicity and hydrophobic interactions with polymeric materials.

Adsorptive loss of cannabinoids to plastics has been reported, with studies showing binding of THC and CBD to polymer surfaces under storage or handling conditions. , As binding can lead to underestimation of drug concentration, these findings emphasize the importance of using glassware for sample handling and extraction steps in order to minimize nonspecific losses and ensure accurate quantification of CBD.

To better reflect conditions encountered in biological systems, and determine the influence of protein binding on recovery, we next tested PBS supplemented with bovine serum albumin (BSA) at increasing concentrations (1–40%) as a model for protein-rich environments, as well as complete cell culture media. Under these conditions, CBD recovery decreased substantially, with values falling to 43.33% at 40% BSA and 47.22% in cell culture media. These observations are consistent with CBD’s high protein-binding capacity, which has been reported to exceed 95% in human plasma. The reduced recovery obtained in protein-rich matrices aligns with findings from pharmacokinetic extraction studies, where CBD recoveries from plasma ranged between approximately 66–93%, depending on the internal standard and extraction conditions. Together, these results suggest that the moderate recovery values obtained in our study reflect the strong affinity of CBD for albumin and other serum proteins, which limits its partitioning into the organic phase during extraction, an important factor to consider when using cannabinoids in biological or cell-based systems.

This trend in the results demonstrates that CBD strongly associates with proteins, consistent with its high lipophilicity and known affinity for albumin binding. , The reduced recovery observed in protein-rich matrices using hexane-based LLE reflects the strong association of CBD with albumin under nondenaturing conditions rather than an inherent failure of liquid–liquid extraction. The reduced extractability is therefore a reflection of the underlying drug–protein interactions that sequester CBD in the aqueous phase. Importantly, this experiment highlights a key consideration for drug development studies: when quantifying CBD in biological matrices such as plasma, serum, or cell-based systems, direct recovery of the free drug may significantly underestimate total CBD content unless protein binding is accounted for. Similarly, in formulation studies, apparent recovery can be confounded by excipient interactions, highlighting the need to optimize extraction methods for each matrix. By systematically testing across matrices with increasing complexity, this work establishes the strengths and limitations of hexane-based LLE for CBD analysis and points toward the necessity of complementary approaches when studying protein-bound or formulation-associated CBD. It is well established that alternative solvents such as acetonitrile or methanol, particularly when combined with protein precipitation strategies, can disrupt protein–drug interactions and yield higher total CBD recovery from biological matrices. − However, when compared to sample cleaning with EMR cartridges or SPE columns, hexane extraction showed more consistency on the recovery of CBD from formulations and it enabled accurate quantification across the tested range.

Hexane LLE of CBD from lipid-based formulations (CBD-liposome and CBD-SEDDS A) was also successful. Recovery from the liposomal formulation was notably high (90.33%), while recovery from SEDDS A was slightly lower (79.78%). These differences may be attributed to the differing release dynamics and CBD localization within the lipid structures. In line with this hypothesis, studies from our group have shown that these formulations show different CBD-release dynamics in in vitro cell viability assays, which directly supports the different recovery results obtained after hexane LLE from the formulations. Liposomes, which encapsulate CBD in bilayers, may release the compound more readily into the extraction solvent, while SEDDS formulations may retain CBD in internal oil phases, impeding partitioning into hexane. Nevertheless, both results affirm the suitability of the LLE method for formulation characterization. These results also confirm the suitability of HPLC for routine CBD analysis in both simple and complex matrices.

Quantification using the FBBS-UV–vis method yielded similar but slightly lower recovery values across matrices (Table ). Even though both tests were implemented using glassware throughout the process, comparison of FBBS-based quantification with HPLC revealed important differences in method performance across matrices. In simple systems such as PBS and water, FBBS provided reasonable quantitation values with high recoveries (79.7% and 75.2%, respectively), in line with HPLC data, indicating that the method is reliable when matrix interference is minimal. However, in more complex environments, quantitation recovery decreased markedly. For example, in cell culture media and PBS supplemented with increasing concentrations of BSA, quantitation recoveries dropped to 41.2% and as low as 38.2% at 20% BSA, reflecting the strong protein-binding affinity of CBD that limits its partitioning into the organic phase and reduces chromophore formation. Similarly, in lipid-based formulations, FBBS underestimated CBD content compared with HPLC, yielding quantitation recoveries of 72.5% for liposomes and 65.4% for SEDDS. When compared with quantitation via HPLC, lower apparent recovery in the FBBS assay can be attributed to the sensitivity of chromophore formation to reaction conditions and residual matrix components. Even after identical LLE, subtle variations in pH, FBBS–CBD stoichiometry, or the presence of trace proteins, lipids, or salts can inhibit complete azo coupling, meaning that only a fraction of the extracted CBD is converted into the detectable chromophore.

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that while FBBS can be applied as a rapid and low-cost method for CBD quantification in simple aqueous matrices, its sensitivity to protein and excipient effects limits its accuracy in biologically or pharmaceutically relevant samples, where HPLC remains the more reliable method.

It is important to note that the starting CBD concentrations used for method evaluation were different for HPLC (0.3 μg/mL) and FBBS (5 μg/mL). This difference reflects the intrinsic sensitivity of the methods: HPLC can reliably detect CBD at subμg/mL levels, whereas the FBBS assay requires higher concentrations to generate a measurable chromophore signal. While this prevents a direct one-to-one comparison of absolute recovery values, the relative trends observed across matrices remain valid. In both cases, recovery was high in simple aqueous systems (PBS, water) and decreased markedly in protein- and lipid-rich matrices (BSA, culture media, nanoformulations). Thus, despite differences in detection limits, the results are consistent with the physicochemical behavior of CBD, namely, its lipophilicity, protein-binding, and interaction with excipients, and support the conclusion that FBBS is adequate for simple systems but HPLC is required for complex matrices. Conclusion

This study presents a comparative evaluation of different analytical techniques for CBD quantification: HPLC, UV–vis spectroscopy, FBBS-based colorimetric assay using a plate reader, and FBBS using UV–vis spectroscopy. Through a systematic analysis, we demonstrated that while HPLC remains the gold standard due to its precision, selectivity, and robustness in complex matrices, the FBBS-based methods offer practical, cost-effective alternatives for rapid CBD detection, especially in resource-limited settings. However, several methodological limitations were also identified that should be addressed prior to routine implementation. Significant adsorption of CBD to plastic materials such as polystyrene tubes resulted in reduced recoveries during extraction, emphasizing the need to use glassware throughout sample handling to minimize loss. Additionally, solubility testing revealed that CBD exhibits supersaturation and phase separation at concentrations above 100 μg/mL in aqueous systems, even with surfactant stabilization. This highlights the importance of determining the solubility limit in each analytical matrix before method development to avoid precipitation and inconsistent quantification. Application of a hexane-based liquid–liquid extraction protocol enabled the assessment of method performance across various media, including PBS, water, cell culture media, and complex protein matrices (1–40% BSA), as well as when quantifying CBD within lipid-based formulations (CBD-liposomes and CBD-SEDDS A). The HPLC method consistently produced higher recovery, particularly in protein-rich and formulation-containing samples, while UV–vis and plate-based FBBS methods were more sensitive to matrix interference and reduced specificity in complex environments. Future research should extend comparative validation against LC–MS/MS or qTOF, particularly for low-concentration CBD quantification in pharmacokinetic or clinical settings. Further studies should also focus on developing and evaluating automated liquid-handling systems or miniaturized analytical platforms (e.g., Opentrons or Hamilton platforms, lab-on-chip, or portable devices) incorporating FBBS chemistry for high-throughput and on-site cannabinoid screening. Together the data presented provides a robust comparison of CBD quantification approaches and highlights both the practical advantages and methodological challenges associated with each technique and the trade-offs between sensitivity, complexity, and practicality providing guidance for optimizing solubility, sample handling, and extraction prior to analytical implementation.
