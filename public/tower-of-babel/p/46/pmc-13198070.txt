Aspiration Thrombectomy in High-Risk Pulmonary Embolism: Mechanical Reperfusion for Thrombosed Arteries Proven Again
Authors: Maya Serhal, James A Goldstein
Published: 2026

Decades of lessons from acute myocardial infarction and stroke have firmly established the primacy of the “open-artery hypothesis”—the principle and practice proving mechanical reperfusion of occluded vessels achieves clinical benefits strikingly superior to “standard therapies” and systemic thrombolysis. In acute pulmonary embolism (PE), the advent of increasingly sophisticated percutaneous aspiration thrombectomy technology and technique now offers the benefits of rapid reperfusion at low risk.

Observations from the STRIKE-PE study1 support the concept that urgent mechanical reperfusion improves outcomes in hemodynamically unstable PE cases. In this subgroup analysis of 48 high-risk, hypotensive PE patients, computer–assisted vacuum thrombectomy resulted in rapid recovery of right ventricular (RV) function (30% reduction in RV/left ventricular [LV] ratio), which was associated with immediate hemodynamic improvement, indicated by significant reductions in systolic and mean pulmonary arterial (PA) pressures. In this severely ill cohort (41% required vasopressors and 10% experienced CPR or cardiac arrest), procedure-related adverse events were rare, and overall survival was excellent (30-day mortality = 2.1%), with significant functional recovery at 90 days measured by dyspnea score and 6-minute walk test.

Hemodynamic compromise in acute PE: the role of RV dysfunction

Acute PE may induce hemodynamic compromise characterized by low cardiac output (CO) hypotension resulting from reduced LV preload, a consequence of thrombus-induced PA obstruction that directly diminishes transpulmonary flow from RV to LV. LV preload deprivation is exacerbated by acute RV systolic dysfunction attributable to afterload strain imposed by elevated pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR), which further impairs transpulmonary flow.2

RV “strain” (dilation and systolic dysfunction) is the key parameter upon which PE risk stratification, clinical decision-making, and prognosis are based. Patients with acute PE have been recently reclassified into 5 categories—subclinical, symptomatic (low clinical severity score), symptomatic (elevated clinical severity score), incipient cardiopulmonary failure, and cardiopulmonary failure. Patients historically classified as high-risk manifest RV dilation and frank hypotension, a cohort with mortality rates nearly 60% managed by standard anticoagulation alone. Intermediate high-risk patients manifest RV strain with elevated biomarkers (troponin, B-type natriuretic peptide), and while they are clinically ill with dyspnea and tachycardia, they do not exhibit persistent hypotension.

Acute RV failure plays a crucial pathophysiological role in PE,3 with rapid increases in PVR, inducing RV systolic dysfunction. Under these conditions, RV failure is exacerbated by RV ischemia, even in the presence of normal coronary anatomy. The afterload-strained failing RV is exquisitely dependent on optimal aortic perfusion; therefore, it follows that hypotension precipitates further hemodynamic decline as the RV progressively deteriorates in a spiraling “doom loop,” triggering a vicious cascade of low CO hypotension. Accordingly, rapid restoration of aortic perfusion pressure is paramount, initially with vasopressors and ultimately by urgent definitive amelioration of PA thrombus burden.

Benefits of mechanical reperfusion of thrombotically occluded arteries

Reperfusion decision-making must balance the efficacy to achieve rapid definitive reperfusion against the fewest adverse events. Compared with thrombolysis, mechanical reperfusion of occluded arteries in ST-elevation myocardial infarction and stroke is superior because it achieves rapid and definitive relief of obstruction and restoration of flow at lower bleeding risk. Although systemic thrombolysis is guideline endorsed for PE complicated by severe hemodynamic compromise, serious bleeding concerns temper enthusiasm for this therapy, especially now considering the efficacy of catheter-mediated reperfusion.

The present observations support the concept that in high-risk PE, percutaneous aspiration thrombectomy rapidly ameliorates thrombus burden, thereby reversing the “RV shock spiral” associated with excellent hemodynamic and clinical outcomes. The present results support previous findings affirming the salutary effects of relief of thrombotic obstruction by mechanical reperfusion, in both high-risk and intermediate high-risk cases. Prior reports of high-risk PE have similarly demonstrated that aspiration thrombectomy achieves immediate hemodynamic improvement associated with excellent outcomes.4,5 It is important also to emphasize the efficacy of this intervention in intermediate high-risk patients who, although not in frank shock, are still typically clinically quite ill (dyspneic and tachycardic). Whereas mortality in intermediate high-risk is low, many suffer from prolonged hospitalizations, persistent dyspnea, limited exercise tolerance, and reduced quality of life. Gratifyingly, mechanical reperfusion has been consistently demonstrated to benefit such patients, achieving reductions in RV/LV ratio associated with salutary hemodynamic and clinical benefits.6

Considerations pertinent to the study

Methodologic limitations warrant consideration in the interpretation of the present results. The observational single-arm design precludes definitive comparisons with systemic thrombolysis or anticoagulation alone. Further, extrapolation to generalizable recommendations for widespread utilization of the present techniques must consider that the present results were obtained by highly experienced operators in high-volume centers. Further randomized controlled trials in high-risk PE patients would be valuable; however, given the high mortality without reperfusion and excellent results with it, it may be difficult (and unwise) to pursue or enroll patients in such investigations. Clearly, further study is necessary to establish whether aspiration thrombectomy should be more routinely applied to intermediate high-risk cases. Finally, the present report did not specifically address patients with PE-induced profound hemodynamic collapse, in whom extracorporeal membrane oxygenation may serve as a lifesaving “bridge” to aspiration thrombectomy and hemodynamic recovery.

Conclusion

In high-risk PE, the shock state and associated high mortality rates warrant rapid reperfusion. The present observations, taken together with those of prior studies, support the concept that catheter-based reperfusion confers early benefits with respect to early hemodynamics and survival and also exerts salutary effects on clinical recovery trajectories. Given that this intervention is now technically straightforward and associated with very low procedural risk, it is reasonable to conclude that this therapy should now be considered “mainstream” for these critically ill cases. It is both promising and likely that this intervention will soon become more widely applied in intermediate high-risk cases as well.
