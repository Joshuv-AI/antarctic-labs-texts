Efficacy of dual-task training on motor performance and cognitive function in patients with stroke disease: an umbrella review comprising meta-analyses of randomized controlled trials
Authors: Yezheng Wang, Congcong Li, Lijuan Wang, Jingyi Zhong, Xu Yan, Wei Zhao, Kaihang Liu, Ying Tian, Hongbiao Wang, Liyan Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStroke poses a major threat to human health, often causing motor, balance, and cognitive impairments that profoundly disrupt patients’ daily lives and markedly reduce their quality of life.ObjectiveThe objective was to summarize current study findings, evaluate the reliability of the evidence, and provide a thorough summary of the results concerning dual-task training in improving motor performance and cognitive function for stroke patients.MethodsFour electronic databases were searched for studies published in English from inception to June 2025. The Assessment of the quality of the evidence was assessed using the AMSTAR2 scoring system and GRADE system. For the meta-analysis, we recalculated the effect sizes for dual-task training using DerSimonian and Laird (DL) random effects models.ResultsFive meta-analysis with eighteen outcomes included in the umbrella review were recalculated for effect size. For motor performance, pooled results found dual-task training can improve the step speed (SMD = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.13,0.59), step frequency (SMD = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.23, 0.83), stride length (SMD = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.14, 1.24), walking endurance (SMD = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.12, 0.81), 10MWT (SMD = -0.80, 95% CI: -1.33, -0.28), and BBS (SMD = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.15, 0.64). For cognitive function, dual-task training can improve total execution function (SMD = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.16, 0.80), total cognitive function (SMD = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.25, 0.91), working memory (SMD = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.26, 1.33), and cognitive flexibility (SMD = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.51, 1.17).ConclusionIn this umbrella review, we found a statistically significant effect between dual-task training and step speed, step frequency, stride length, walking endurance, 10MWT, BBS, total execution function, total cognitive function, working memory, and cognitive flexibility. Overall, despite the moderate to very low quality of evidence, the findings support dual-task training as a viable rehabilitation approach for stroke patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04805-9.Keywords: Stroke, Dual-task training, Motor performance, Cognitive function

Introduction

Stroke is a significant risk to human health [1]. In 2021, stroke ranked as the fourth primary Level 3 contributor to disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [2], leading to long-term disability in the majority of survivors [3]. In more economically developed nations, up to 20% of the population will experience a stroke in their lifetime, whereas in less economically advantaged countries, the number may reach 50% [4]. Stroke can result in multifaceted functional deficits across motor, balance, and cognitive function, significantly detrimentally affecting patients’ everyday lives and diminishing their quality of life [5, 6]. At the same time, the family and social support system endure considerable financial strain and mental distress [7]. The restoration of balance and gait is critical for stroke patients, since the impairment of these abilities limits their movement and dramatically impacts their independence and social engagement in everyday life. For stroke patients, the restoration of balance and gait is essential for enhancing their quality of life and is the primary emphasis of rehabilitation training programs [8]. Rehabilitation of cognitive function is equally important to the recovery of body function after stroke [9]. One of the common consequences of stroke is cognitive impairment with direct implications for poststroke functional recovery and quality of life, including the ability to return to work, live independently, maintain meaningful social relationships, and drive a vehicle safely [10].

Dual-task training is a rehabilitation method applied for stroke patients, necessitating the execution of a fundamental action concurrently with an additional task that may challenge cognitive or motor skills, including motor-cognitive dual-task training and motor-motor dual-task training [11, 12]. This training technique seeks to enhance the patient’s capacity for attention allocation and multitasking by augmenting task complexity, therefore promoting the restoration of their body total functionality [13]. In rehabilitation practice, dual-tasking encompasses both cognitive and motor tasks [14]. Common cognitive tasks involve mental arithmetic, such as addition and subtraction [15], memorizing numbers in reverse [16], or executing the Stroop test [17], while motor tasks include walking while carrying water [18] or patting a ball during walking [19]. A randomized controlled pilot trial conducted by Liu et al. [20] compared the effects of different types of dual-task gait training on dual-task gait performance in patients with stroke. Specifically, the cognitive dual-task gait training group significantly reduced the dual-task cost of gait speed (DTC-speed) and increased stride length during cognitive motor dual-task gait performance. In contrast, the motor dual-task gait training group exhibited significant improvements in gait speed, stride length, and DTC-speed during motor dual-task gait performance. A study by Kim et al. [21] shows that motor and cognitive dual-task gait training exhibits superior efficacy in improving balance function and gait performance among patients with chronic stroke compared to either motor dual-task gait training or cognitive dual-task gait training alone. Several studies have shown that dual-task training can also improve cognitive function in stroke patients and bring about significant and lasting neurological benefits [22, 23].Through dual-task training methods, stroke patients can not only make progress in motor and cognitive function, but also improve their daily life skills, enhance social participation, and ultimately realize the quality of daily life [24–26].

The assessment of dual-task training’s efficacy is ongoing, and additional empirical research is required to ascertain its success in enhancing stroke patient recovery. An umbrella review is necessary to evaluate the rehabilitative benefit of dual-task training on strengthening motor performance and cognitive function. This review will consolidate the data of many studies and offer a more thorough viewpoint on the application of dual-task training in stroke treatment.

Systematic reviews and meta-analyses of many published randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have shown that dual-task training improves gait, balance, quality of life, and cognitive function in stroke patients across different modalities compared to traditional physical therapy. Several meta-analyses have focused on certain health outcomes. Nevertheless, differing measures provide diverse findings. The quality of this evidence has not yet been established. This research sought to systematically uncover relevant meta-analyses concerning the effectiveness of dual-task training on motor performance and cognitive function in stroke patients. Summarize the existing study findings, assess the quality of the evidence, and give a comprehensive overview of the results of dual-task training’s efficacy in enhancing motor performance and cognitive function for stroke patients. Our objective was to evaluate the efficacy of dual-task training by establishing and analyzing measures for motor performance and cognitive function, therefore creating a robust scientific foundation for future rehabilitation training programs. This study carefully analyzed multiple types of dual-task training to ensure the comprehensiveness and reliability of the results.

Method

Search strategy and selection criteria

A systematic literature search was conducted in PubMed, Web of Science, Ebsco and Embase databases from the beginning of database construction to June 2025. Our search strategy is associated with dual-task training, stroke, motor performance, cognitive function, and meta-analyses. Comprehensive search keywords are delineated in Table 1 and eTable2. Only articles published in English were considered. Only those quantitative meta-analyses that provided effect sizes and a 95% confidence interval (CI) were included. Meta-analyses where the results were less than two RCTs were excluded. According to the literature inclusion principle of the umbrella review, when the RCTs included in two meta-analyses overlapped, selected the meta-analysis that included the significant number of RCTs for analysis [27]. There are detailed meta-analyses inclusion and exclusion criteria (eTable3). The preferred reporting items for systematic reviews and meta-analyses (PRISMA) guidelines were followed in reporting this review [28]. The review protocol was registered at the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (registration number: CRD420250607928).

Data extraction

Data extraction was conducted separately by two reviewers (Y.W and C.L), with any discrepancies addressed by a third reviewer (J.Z). The retrieved data included the number of original studies, sample size, sample characteristics (e.g., mean age and population), intervention tasks, control group, frequency of intervention, length of intervention, observed group standard deviation (SD), and control group SD for quadratic calculation.

Outcome

Motor performance in stroke patients (e.g., step speed, cadence, step length, stride length, 10 m Walk Test (10MWT), Timed Up and Go test (TUGT) and Berg Balance Scale (BBS).

Cognitive function in stroke patients (e.g., total execution function, total cognitive function, working memory, memory, attention, language, spatial cognition, cognitive flexibility). Cognitive function was assessed using the following: Stroop Test, Mini Mental Status Examination, Addenbrooke Cognitive Examination, Trail Making Test, Digital Span Test, Montreal Cognitive Assessment, Cancelation Tests, Cogstate Subtests, Addenbrooke’s Cognitive Examination-Revised, Correct Response Rate Test.

Assessment of the quality of the evidence

All meta-analyses that fulfilled the criteria were assessed for methodological quality using the AMSTAR2 [29] and were independently categorized as high, moderate, low, and critically low according to the score results by two reviewers (Y.W and C.L). The detailed evaluation criteria are presented in eTable4 [29]. AMSTAR2 consists of 16 entries, with Entries 2, 4, 7, 9, 11, 13, and 15 being the critical domains. The systematic reviews are considered high quality when there is no or only one noncritical weakness, moderate quality when there is more than one noncritical weakness, low quality when there is only one critical few with or without noncritical deficiencies, and critically low quality when there is more than one crucial few with or without noncritical weakness.

Each relationship in the meta-analyses was evaluated for evidence credibility using the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) technique (eTable5) by two reviewers (Y.W. and C.L.), which can be categorized into four levels on the basis of the evaluation system: high, moderate, low, and very low [30, 31]. The GRADE method consists of five parts: risk of bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication bias.

Data analysis

For each outcome associated with dual-task training affecting motor performance and cognitive function in stroke patients, we extracted the mean, sample size (n), and standard deviation (SD) from the individual studies included in each meta-analysis based on the study design and performed replicated RCT meta-analyses to calculate the effect sizes, the 95% confidence interval (CI), and 95% predictive interval (PI). The range of Hedges’g values was interpreted as small (< 0.2), moderate (0.5), or large (> 0.8) effect sizes. For the calculation of effect sizes for each meta-analysis, we used the DerSimonian and Laird (DL) random-effects model that capable of addressing significant heterogeneity [32]. When there was more than one meta-analysis for the same association outcome, we selected the meta-analysis with the largest number of included RCTs for data analysis in the umbrella review [27]. The statistical analysis of each association was completed using R version 4.4.2. Continuous findings were evaluated by computing the standardised mean difference (SMD) across sets of outcomes obtained with the same instrument. The chi-square test and the I² statistic were employed to evaluate heterogeneity among studies. p-values < 0.05 indicated statistically significant differences.

Results

Search results

The initial search included 175 articles. After removing 58 duplicates, 68 non–meta-analyses (including network meta-analysis) were excluded by reading the titles and abstracts, and 40 meta-analyses that did not meet the inclusion and exclusion criteria were excluded after further reading of the full text (eTable3). Ten meta-analysis [33–42] included in the umbrella review and five meta-analysis were [33, 34, 37, 40, 41] included in the umbrella review that recalculated effect size.18 association results from five meta- analyses were ultimately included in the umbrella analysis (Fig. 1).

Assessment of the quality of the evidence

According to the AMSTAR2 scoring system, of the ten meta-analyses, three were [37, 40, 41] considered moderate quality, five [33–36, 39] were considered low quality, and two [38, 42] were considered critically low quality (eTable4). None of the eighteen outcomes received a high rating in the GRADE system. two outcomes received a moderate classification from GRADE, thirteen were low and three were very low (eTables7 and eTables8).

The effects of dual-task training for stroke patients

The results found dual-task training can largely improve the 10MWT (SMD = -0.80, 95% CI: -1.33, -0.28) in stroke patients, with low heterogeneity (I² = 0). Stride length (SMD = 0.69, 95% CI: 0.14, 1.24) and step frequency (SMD = 0.53, 95% CI: 0.23, 0.83) are also moderately improved, but the heterogeneity of correlations is high (stride length: I²=75%). Step speed (SMD = 0.36, 95% CI: 0.13,0.59), walking endurance (SMD = 0.47, 95% CI: 0.12, 0.81), and BBS (SMD = 0.39, 95% CI: 0.15, 0.64) exhibited a small effect size of improvement. Besides, for cognitive function, it suggested that dual-task training can largely improve working memory (SMD = 0.80, 95% CI: 0.26, 1.33) and cognitive flexibility (SMD = 0.84, 95% CI: 0.51, 1.17). A moderate improvement in total cognitive function (SMD = 0.58, 95% CI: 0.25, 0.91) was also revealed, but the heterogeneity is high (total cognitive function: I² = 56%). Total execution function (SMD = 0.48, 95% CI: 0.16, 0.80) showed a small effect size of improvement.

In addition, no statistically (p > 0.05) clear evidence was found in step length (SMD = 0.31, 95% CI: -0.04, 0.65), dual-task gait speed (SMD = 0.03, 95% CI: -0.40, 0.47), single-task gait speed (SMD = 0.05, 95% CI: -0.38, 0.49), TUGT (SMD = -0.58, 95% CI: -1.40, 0.23), memory (SMD = 0.00, 95% CI: -0.50, 0.50), attention (SMD = 0.24, 95% CI: -0.09, 0.58), language (SMD = 0.15, 95% CI: -0.28, 0.58) and spatial cognition (SMD = -0.32, 95% CI: -0.76, 0.12) during dual-task training. Correlation results for all significant and nonsignificant findings are presented in Table 3, eTables7 and eTables8.

Sensitivity analyses and risk of bias assessment

To further evaluate the reliability of our results, we performed a sensitivity analysis. We removed RCTs with sample sizes below 25% of the total and conducted the analysis accordingly (eTable9). The results showed that attention was altered after exclusion (SMD = 0.34, 95% CI: -0.17, -0.84) while all other outcomes remained unchanged. We are using an HKSJ random effects model for sensitivity analysis of outcomes with fewer than five RCTs (eTable9). Eight outcomes with fewer than five RCTs maintained the same rank in quality after transforming the random effects model. Six outcomes of the range of 95% CI crossed the null prior and dual-task gait speed (SMD = 0.03, 95% CI: -0.35, 0.41) and single-task gait speed (SMD = 0.05, 95% CI: -0.00, 0.11) were narrowed.

Discussion

This umbrella review sought to comprehensively assess the impact of dual-task training on motor performance and cognitive function in stroke patients, establishing a scientific basis for rehabilitation strategies using a meta-analysis of published, randomized controlled trials. This study used the DL random-effects model and SMD to recalculate the effect sizes of each meta-analysis, ensuring the accuracy and reliability of the results. The results showed that dual-task training had a statistically significant effect on step speed, step frequency, stride length, walking endurance, 10MWT, BBS, total execution function, total cognitive function, working memory, and cognitive flexibility, but no statistically significant effect on step length, TUGT, dual-task gait speed, single-task gait speed, memory, attention, language, and spatial cognition. The absence of statistically significant effects is more likely due to substantial between-study heterogeneity, statistical imprecision, limited study numbers, and small sample sizes in the included studies. Stride length (I2% = 75), TUGT (I2% = 89), Total cognitive function(I2% = 56) exhibited high heterogeneity, which may be associated with the calculation method for SMD being sensitive to differences between studies, the standardization process of SMD altering the weight allocation among studies rather than reflecting inherent high heterogeneity in clinical effects themselves, and may also be related to the disproportionate weight contribution of certain studies included in the analysis.

The restoration of gait function is essential in the rehabilitation of stroke patients, significantly influencing their quality of life and social engagement. Enhancing gait ability not only dramatically lowers the chance of falls but also improves stroke patients’ capacity to independently execute chores of daily life, which is critical for minimizing subsequent injuries and enhancing patient confidence [43]. This umbrella review, by assembling and analyzing data from five meta-analyses, showed that dual-task training significantly improves step speed, step frequency, stride length, 10MWT and Walking endurance in stroke patients. A careful review of the included reviews through meta-analysis revealed that the results of Zhou et al. [38] meta-analysis based on a randomized controlled trial (RCT) showed that the experimental group, which used cognitive-motor dual-task as an intervention, showed a certain degree of improvement in gait, cadence, and stride length compared to the control group. The meta-analysis published by Zhang et al. [36] showed that dual-task training is an effective training method for improving step length and cadence in stroke patients. The dual-task training group improved step length and significantly improved cadence compared to the control group. Nonetheless, the dual-task group showed ineffectiveness on the TUG test, and the findings suggest that the efficacy of dual-task training in enhancing balance function requires more examination. Seven studies with 139 stroke patients were included in a meta-analysis [33] to examine how exercise and gait training, particularly dual-task workouts, affect stroke patients’ dual-task gait speed. Comparing dual-task gait training to single-task gait training increased gait speed by 0.08 m/s. There is evidence that training for dual-task gait may be more successful than training for single task gait at improving the speed of dual-task gait. However, the interpretation of these results may be flawed due to the limited number of existing studies. In an updated version of the meta-analysis published by Shu et al. [37], it was noted that dual-task training markedly enhanced step length, step speed, stride length, and performance on a 10-meter walk test in stroke patients, but nevertheless, the potential benefits of dual-task training in improving balance function need to be further explored. Scholars have conducted in-depth research on the neural basis of dual-task execution, and neuroimaging studies have shown that during walking, numerous regions in the brain are activated, which can be categorized into direct and indirect motor pathways [44], and dual-task walking is associated with changes in activation of the indirect motor pathway and frontoparietal network [45]. Dual-task training enhances gait ability in stroke patients, likely due to its capacity to promote brain remodeling and neuroplasticity by improving neurological movement control and augmenting activation of indirect motor pathways and frontoparietal networks. Through dual-task training, stroke patients enhance cognitive resource allocation strategies, appropriately assign primary and secondary tasks, and strengthen the coordination between physical activities and brain function [46].

Balance is the capacity of the body to sustain equilibrium in a specific position, typically including automatic adaptations to voluntary movements or externally exerted pressures [47]. The decline in balance function is recognized as a significant factor in fall risk among stroke patients. The deterioration of balance function in stroke patients is characterized by an unstable gait and reduced walking speed, which, in severe cases, may lead to deficits in postural control and an increased risk of falls [48]. The danger of falling not only poses possible physical damage, such as fractures and further disabilities, but may also induce psychological despair, significantly impacting patients’ quality of life. The results of this umbrella review suggest that dual-task training is effective in improving test scores on the BBS in stroke patients. In a meta-analysis published by Wang et al. [35] showed that Cognitive-motor interference, arising from the concurrent execution of cognitive and motor activities, effectively enhances balance function in stroke patients. Nevertheless, there is limited evidence to substantiate the theory of the long-term advantages of motor cognitive interference. Dual-task training not only improves balance in stroke patients but also improves cognitive function, perhaps due to its improvement of postural control and cognitive function [49–51]. By activating postural muscles, dual-task training effectively enhances neural function, shortens reaction time, and improves the processing of sensory information [52]. Dual-task training stimulates high excitability in the prefrontal cortex and enhances joint responses to motor and cognitive dual tasks by combining motor and cognitive training [53]. Radiologic studies indicate that dual-task training enhances hemodynamics in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, a feature linked to increased balancing performance. Concurrently, certain brain regions linked to dual-task processing exhibited reduced activity post-training, suggesting that training alleviates the brain’s processing requirements [54]. According to the capacity sharing theory [55–57], the reduced brain processing demand facilitates improved task performance in stroke patients and reduces dual-task interference that occurs when performing cognitive and motor tasks simultaneously.

Improvements in cognitive function are crucial for the motor rehabilitation, mental health, quality of life, and social participation of stroke patients. Post-stroke cognitive impairment (PSCI) is highly prevalent after stroke, stroke can adversely affect information processing speed, executive function, memory, language, and visuospatial abilities, thereby impacting the health status of stroke survivors [10, 58, 59]. Dual-task training, particularly motor cognitive dual-task training, which involves conducting cognitive exercises while performing motor tasks, enhances stroke patients’ ability to multitask. This approach promotes the efficient allocation of attentional resources, increases cognitive flexibility and recovery potential within the brain, and effectively improves cognitive function [60, 61]. Meanwhile, motor cognitive dual-task training stimulated heightened excitability in the prefrontal cortex, enhancing integrated responses to combined motor and cognitive tasks while reducing dual-task interference [62].

Appropriate and effective training modalities are crucial in stroke patients’ rehabilitation. In an experimental study conducted by An et al. [63], it was found that cognitive motor dual-task gait training was more effective than either motor dual-task or cognitive dual-task gait training alone in improving balance and gait abilities in chronic stroke patients. In a randomized controlled trial, Chen et al. [64] found that cognitive dual-task and motor dual-task gait training effectively improved lower limb coordination and reduced walking-related fall risk in subacute stroke patients. We recommend that cognitive-motor dual-task and motor-motor dual-task gait training be incorporated into routine stroke rehabilitation based on individual patient characteristics and available resources. Both training methods improve gait and balance function. Cognitive-motor dual-task training enhances attention, executive function, and memory in stroke patients. Cognitive-motor dual-task training may offer greater rehabilitation value for improving cognitive function [65].

Contemporary study evidence substantiates the benefits of dual-task training compared to single-task training in enhancing motor performance and cognitive function in stroke patients. Nonetheless, dual-task training elevates task complexity, potentially increasing the risk of falls among stroke patients during training and posing obstacles in the first phases of training development. Furthermore, establishing a standardization for the intensity and advancement of dual-task training has some challenges. To enhance the beneficial effects of dual-task training, a more standardized intervention design is advised for rehabilitation practices and future research. This involves modifying task complexity while carefully evaluating the quality of task completion to ensure training safety and effectiveness.

Limitations

The study has several limitations. The study exclusively examined existing meta-analyses of randomized, controlled trials. Secondly, this study did not explicitly evaluate the quality of all pertinent studies in each meta-analysis but concentrated solely on the quality of the included relevant studies. The constraints of the source data hindered the detection of effects associated with dual-task training on motor performance and cognitive function. Fourth, we did not evaluate whether the efficacy of dual-task training therapies for stroke patients was affected by additional factors, such as follow-up duration, various types of control groups, and social support.

Conclusions

The present review identified a certain level of enhancement in gait, balance and cognitive function among stroke patients following dual-task training. The findings indicate that dual-task training might serve as an effective intervention for patients, enhancing motor performance and cognitive function, potentially improving the quality of life for stroke survivors. Nonetheless, further long-term clinical trials with large cohorts are required to elucidate the impact of dual-task training on additional outcomes, including psychological state.
