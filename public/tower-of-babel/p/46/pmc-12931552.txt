Secondary overtriage: impact of trauma center designation and trauma system integration
Authors: Darrell L Hunt, Harun Mazumder, Jordan S Rahm, Alessandro Orlando, Carol Jones, Bryenne Hutton, Jeneva M Garland, Nina Y Wilson, Andrea Palmer, Samir M Fakhry
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSecondary overtriage in trauma refers to the interfacility transfer of minimally and moderately injured patients who do not require major interventions, resulting in short stays at tertiary receiving facilities. Secondary overtriage rates are commonly used as a measure of trauma system efficiency, focused on the optimal allocation and use of healthcare resources. However, limited data exist examining the impact of Level III trauma centers (L3TC) on secondary overtriage rates. Therefore, this study aimed to determine whether transfer from L3TCs, compared with non-trauma facilities, was associated with a lower rate of secondary overtriage among patients subsequently admitted to a Level I trauma center.MethodsThis was a single-center, retrospective cohort study of trauma patients transferred to a tertiary trauma center between 1 August 2021 and 31 July 2023. The primary exposure was the American College of Surgeons-verified or state-designated trauma center level of the referring facilities. Multiple variable regression analysis was applied to three models: (1) unadjusted, (2) adjusted for patient characteristics and (3) adjusted for patient and hospital characteristics.ResultsA total of 2309 patients were transferred to our tertiary trauma center from 20 referring facilities, including 15 non-L3TCs and 5 L3TCs. Secondary overtriage rates were significantly lower at L3TCs (23.5% vs 28.4%, p=0.009). On multivariable regression analysis, even after adjusting for patient and hospital characteristics, the odds of secondary overtriage from non-L3TCs remained 31% higher compared with L3TCs (OR: 1.31 (1.02, 1.67)). Non-L3TC transfer patients had a 12% shorter average hospital length of stay (LOS), a 21% shorter average intensive care unit LOS, and a 39% shorter average ventilator LOS (all p<0.05).ConclusionsIn this study, secondary overtriage rates were significantly lower for L3TCs compared with non-L3TCs, even after adjusting for patient and hospital characteristics. These findings suggest that L3TCs benefit from state-mandated healthcare resource investment and trauma system integration.Level of evidenceLevel III, Therapeutic/Care Management.Keywords: triage, wounds and injuries

Background

Secondary overtriage in trauma describes interfacility transfer patients with minor and moderate injuries whose definitive treatments do not require major intervention, resulting in large healthcare resource consumption but brief hospital stays at tertiary trauma centers.1 2 The rate of secondary overtriage has been used as a measure of trauma system efficiency, focused on the most appropriate distribution and use of healthcare resources. In an efficient trauma system, minimally injured patients would be definitively treated at nontertiary facilities, while more severely injured patients would be stabilized and triaged for interfacility transfer to a higher level of care.

Tertiary referral trauma centers receive transfers from regional facilities with widely varying resources for the evaluation and treatment of injured patients. The rates of secondary overtriage have been reported to vary, from as low as 6.9% to as high as 48.2%, and have been shown to be associated with multiple injury- and non-injury-related factors.3 Injury-related factors often involve subspecialty management of brain, spinal cord, craniofacial, and complex soft tissue injuries.4,6 Non-injury-related factors include payor status, geography (urban vs rural), and the number of regional tertiary trauma centers.1 Independent of the reasons for secondary overtriage, a reduction of secondary overtriage would save costs and reduce overutilization of scarce healthcare resources.7 8

Due to the large potential for cost and resource savings, efforts have been made to investigate factors affecting secondary overtriage. Regionalization of practices and protocols has been posited to reduce secondary overtriage, but the effect of non-tertiary trauma centers (Levels III–V) has been mixed. Iyer and colleagues showed increased levels of secondary overtriage when injured patients were evaluated at non-tertiary trauma centers.9 On the other hand, Parikh and colleagues demonstrated a reduction in secondary overtriage.2 As healthcare systems face increasing demands, particularly in high-acuity environments like trauma centers, the ability to identify and mitigate avoidable transfers has both clinical and operational implications. Understanding the patterns and drivers of these transfers is important to optimizing trauma system performance, improving patient flow, and preserving resources for those with life-threatening injuries.

Our regional trauma system consists of a tertiary state-designated Level I trauma center with four Level III trauma centers (L3TCs) sharing protocols for injury management. A fifth L3TC, not part of our regional system, was considered in the study due to its geographic proximity and role in regional trauma care. However, our Level I trauma center also receives injured patients from over 100 non-trauma facilities, with whom we have less coordination of injury management guidelines. Because of these differing levels of coordination, we hypothesized that among patients transferred to our Level I trauma center, those coming from L3TCs would have a lower secondary overtriage rate compared with those transferred from non-trauma facilities.

Methods

Study design and participants

This was a single-center, retrospective cohort study of trauma patients transferred to a tertiary trauma center between 1 August 2021 and 31 July 2023. Only patients transferred from non-tertiary centers that referred at least 30 patients during the study period were included to ensure stable estimates, reduce sparse-data bias, and minimize potential selection bias from low-volume centers, which were uniformly geographically very distant from the tertiary center. Among the referring facilities, five were L3TCs. Four of these L3TCs are part of the same system as the tertiary center and adhere to the American College of Surgeons (ACS) verification criteria for L3TCs. The remaining L3TC is designated by the state of Tennessee.10 State designation is a regulatory process administered by each state’s department of health, granting legal authority to operate as a trauma center. Each state sets its own criteria, which may vary in rigor, structure, and frequency of review. In contrast, ACS verification is a voluntary, nationally standardized review process that provides external validation of quality and adherence to national benchmarks.

Prior to the commencement of the data collection, this study was determined to be exempt from Institutional Review Board oversight in accordance with federal regulations and institutional policy. Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines were applied to ensure proper reporting of methods, results, and discussion (online supplemental file 1).11

Data source and data collection

Patient data were electronically extracted from an enterprise-wide trauma registry, including patient demographics, injury-related details, emergency department (ED) vital signs, the International Classification of Diseases, Tenth Revision, Clinical Modification (ICD-10-CM) diagnosis codes, ICD-10 Procedure Coding System (ICD-10-PCS) procedure codes and timestamps, discharge disposition, and other outcomes. Data on admission service, referring facility identifications (IDs), and names were obtained from the local trauma registry. A data honest broker ensured the de-identification of the data by assigning a random study subject number to each patient and removing any patient protected health information.

Variable definition

The primary exposure was the ACS verified or state designated trauma center level of the referring facilities. Patients were categorized based on whether they were transferred to the tertiary trauma center from either (1) a L3TC, or (2) a non-L3TC (eg, Level IV trauma centers, non-trauma centers). A separate analysis aimed to address the varied patient transfer volume from non-L3TCs. Patients transferred from non-L3TCs were categorized according to the total volume of transfer patients from their trauma center: <100 patients (ie, 30–99 patients) and ≥100 patients. This cut-off of 100 patients was selected to define clinically meaningful low- and high-volume non-L3TCs and to examine whether higher volume non-L3TCs demonstrated transfer patterns similar to those of L3TCs. This threshold appeared to provide a meaningful separation between low- and high-volume centers in the exploratory analysis.

The primary outcome was secondary overtriage, defined as transfers who on arrival at the tertiary center had no major surgical intervention under general anesthesia and were: (1) discharged home directly from the ED within 2 days or (2) after admission, discharged alive from the hospital without transfer to hospice care within 2 days. Secondary outcomes were hospital disposition, total mortality, mechanical ventilation within 24 hours of admission (ICD-10-PCS 5A1935Z, 5A1945Z, or 5A1955Z), hospital length of stay (LOS), intensive care unit (ICU) LOS, and ventilator days. Hospital disposition was defined according to the National Trauma Data Standards and included in-hospital death and discharge to hospice.12 Total mortality was defined as the sum of in-hospital deaths and discharges to hospice care.

Additionally, body mass index (BMI) was computed by using weight in kilograms divided by the square of height in meters. Using the comorbidity package in R,13 Elixhauser score was calculated based on ICD-10-CM diagnosis codes and weights set by van Walraven et al.14 The shock index was defined as the ED heart rate divided by ED systolic blood pressure, with both measures being the first recorded one within 30 min of arrival.

Statistical analysis

We compared L3TCs and non-L3TCs across patient demographics, injury characteristics, and patient outcomes. For categorical variables, comparisons between the Trauma Center groups were performed using Fisher’s exact test when the expected cell counts were less than 5, and the χ2 test otherwise. Continuous variables were analyzed non-parametrically using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test for two group comparisons. When comparing L3TCs with the two subgroups of non-L3TCs, categorical variables were analyzed using the same approach, while continuous variables were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test to account for more than two group comparisons.

Multivariable regression analyses were conducted to evaluate the association between the primary exposure trauma center level (L3TC vs non-L3TC) and patient outcomes to address potential bias due to confounding variables. Binary outcomes, including secondary overtriage, in-hospital mortality, hospice discharge, total mortality, ICU use, and ventilator use within 24 hours, were analyzed using logistic regression models and ORs were calculated. Count outcomes, including hospital LOS, ICU LOS, and ventilator LOS, were analyzed using negative binomial regression models to account for overdispersion (ie, variance greater than mean) in the count data, and incidence rate ratios (IRRs) were calculated. Similar to the OR in logistic regression, the IRR in negative binomial regression represents the relative rate of an event occurring in the exposed group (non-L3TCs in this case) compared with the reference group (L3TCs). An IRR >1 indicates a higher rate of the event in the exposed group, while an IRR <1 indicates a lower rate.

For each outcome, three sequential models were developed. Model 1 was unadjusted. Model 2 adjusted for patient-level characteristics including age, race, primary payor, Elixhauser comorbidity index, Injury Severity Score (ISS), Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS), trauma activation type, and injury type. Model 3 further adjusted for hospital characteristics including distance from referring center to tertiary center (in miles), number of patients transferred, and whether the referring center was part of the same hospital system as the tertiary center. Of the adjusting variables, only GCS had missing values (15 patients; 0.6%); because of this small number, imputation for missing values was not performed, and these patients were excluded from the multivariable analysis. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (V.4.4.1), were two-tailed, and p values <0.05 were considered statistically significant.15

Results

Sample characteristics

A total of 2892 patients were transferred from 125 Level III and non-L3TCs during the study period (figure 1). After excluding 105 centers that referred fewer than 30 patients (most transferring only one to five patients), the final analytic sample comprised 2309 patients from 20 referring facilities, including 15 non-L3TCs and 5 L3TCs (table 1). Of these patients, 1355 (58.7%) were transferred from non-L3TCs and 954 (41.3%) from L3TCs. Four L3TCs (80%) and seven non-L3TCs (46.7%) were part of the same hospital system. The median (IQR) distance from the referring facility to tertiary center was greater for non-L3TC, 41.0 (21.7–57.7) miles, compared with L3TCs, 25.5 (15.1–45.2) miles (p<0.001).

The median (IQR) age of patients was 58.0 (36.0–74.0) years, with similar age distributions across both the L3TC and non-L3TC groups. There were more White patients from L3TCs compared with non-L3TCs (82.9% vs 75.2%), while non-L3TCs had higher proportions of Black and other racial groups (p<0.001). There were no significant differences in sex distribution, BMI, or Elixhauser comorbidity index between groups. Overall, Medicare was the most common payment method (40.7%). Compared with non-L3TC patients, a higher proportion of L3TC patients had private/commercial insurance (27.3% vs 22.7%, p=0.01) and were less likely to have Medicaid insurance (8.8% vs 12.8%, p=0.003). Ground ambulance was the predominant transport mode overall (85.5%), though helicopter ambulance use was more frequent among non-L3TC transfers (18.1% vs 8.1%, p<0.001). The median (IQR) ISS was 9.0 (5.0–14.0), and this was similar between groups. However, L3TC transfers had a slightly higher proportion of patients with a GCS of 3–8 (6.3% vs 4.0%, p=0.02). Blunt trauma accounted for the majority of injuries (90.5%), while penetrating injuries were less common (7.5%), and both occurred at similar rates across groups. Falls were the leading mechanism of injury. Motor vehicle collisions were more frequent in L3TC transfers (23.1% vs 17.5%, p=0.001), while assaults were more common in the non-L3TC group (9.0% vs 6.0%, p=0.01).

To further explore heterogeneity among referring centers, we conducted a secondary analysis stratifying non-L3TCs based on patient transfer volume (<100 vs ≥100 patients) (SDC 2). While overall demographics and injury severity were similar across groups, several significant differences were observed. Patients from low-volume non-L3TCs were older (median age 62 vs 57 years), more likely to be White (85.3% vs 71.4%), Medicare-insured (46.8% vs 37.6%), and had a higher rate of helicopter transport (19.8% vs 17.4%) compared with those from higher-volume non-L3TCs. These low-volume centers were also located farther from the tertiary center (median 52.7 vs 22.2 miles) and had a lower proportion affiliated with the same hospital system as the tertiary center (33.2% vs 71.4%).

Compared with L3TCs, patients from high-volume (≥100 transfers) non-L3TCs had similar trauma activation rates (38.7% in high-volume non-L3TCs vs 39.7% in L3TCs), similar rates of severe injury (ISS ≥25: 6.9% vs 7.0%), and a comparable proportion of falls injuries (54.6% vs 55.5%). Additionally, the distance from the tertiary center was similar (median 22.2 miles for high-volume non-L3TCs vs 25.5 miles for L3TCs). High-volume non-L3TCs showed a higher proportion of shared system affiliation compared with low-volume non-L3TCs (71.4% vs 33.2%), indicating closer institutional proximity to the tertiary center.

To further examine whether trauma center designation or shared system protocols influenced patient outcomes, we compared four L3TCs with the seven non-L3TCs within the same hospital system as L1TC (SDC 3). Despite being part of the same system, patients treated at non-L3TCs had significantly higher rates of secondary overtriage and lower ICU and ventilator use within 24 hours compared with L3TCs. In-hospital mortality and total mortality were also lower in non-L3TCs. LOS metrics (hospital LOS and ICU LOS) were shorter in non-L3TCs, with statistically significant differences.

Patient outcomes

Patient outcomes between L3TCs and non-L3TCs showed several differences (table 2). Compared with L3TCs, secondary overtriage was significantly higher at non-L3TCs (28.4% vs 23.5%, p=0.01). In-hospital mortality rates were comparable between the two groups (4.9% for L3TCs vs 3.8% for non-L3TCs, p=0.25), as were hospice discharges (2.7% vs 3.6%, p=0.33). Total mortality, which combined both in-hospital deaths and hospice discharges, was similarly distributed between groups (7.0% vs 6.6%, p=0.78). ICU utilization was slightly higher in L3TC transfers (37.8% vs 34.9%, p=0.16). The proportion of patients requiring ventilation within the first 24 hours of transfer was significantly greater for L3TCs (9.2% vs 6.6%, p=0.02). Hospital LOS was significantly longer for L3TC transfers, with a median (IQR) hospital LOS of 4.0 (2.0–7.0) days compared with 3.0 (2.0–7.0) days for non-L3TC transfers (p=0.03).

Secondary overtriage

In model 1, patients from non-L3TCs had significantly higher odds of secondary overtriage compared with those from L3TCs (OR (95% CI): 1.29 (1.07 to 1.57)). This association with secondary overtriage persisted after adjusting for patient characteristics (adjusted OR (aOR:) 1.31 (1.06 to 1.61)) and after further controlling for hospital-level factors (aOR: 1.31 (1.02 to 1.67)).

Mortality, discharge to hospice, and ICU/vent use

No significant associations were found between referring trauma center level and in-hospital mortality, hospice discharge, total mortality, ICU use, or ventilator use within 24 hours of arrival in any of the three adjustment models in table 3.

Duration of resource utilization

Multivariable negative binomial regression models suggested that patients referred from non-L3TCs had significantly shorter hospital, ICU, and ventilator LOS days compared with those referred from L3TCs. In the fully adjusted model (model 3), non-L3TC referrals had a 12% shorter average hospital LOS (adjusted IRR (aIRR)=0.88 (0.81–0.96), a 21% shorter average ICU LOS (aIRR=0.79 (0.69–0.91), and a 39% shorter average ventilator LOS (aIRR=0.61 (0.43–0.86).

Discussion

In this retrospective analysis of over 2300 interfacility trauma patient transfers to a tertiary-level trauma center, the level of the referring trauma center (ie, L3TC vs non-designated) was significantly associated with patterns of transfer, patient demographics, and resource utilization, but not with mortality outcomes. Notably, patients transferred from non-L3TCs experienced significantly higher rates of secondary overtriage, even after adjusting for patient- and hospital-level factors. These findings suggest potential inefficiencies in triage decision-making at non-designated centers, which may reflect differences in trauma system integration, provider experience, or access to diagnostic resources. Despite these differences, in-hospital mortality and hospice discharge rates were comparable between groups, indicating that once patients reached the tertiary center, outcomes were not adversely affected by the trauma designation status of the referring facility. However, the shorter hospital, ICU, and ventilator LOS observed among non-L3TC transfers raises important questions about the appropriateness and timing of these transfers, and whether some may have been avoidable with adequate local decision-making and/or regional system integration.

Using a nationwide inpatient sample from the early 2000s, Osen et al identified a secondary overtriage rate of 6.9% with a higher likelihood of these interfacility transfers associated with younger age, uninsured status, and being transferred to a teaching hospital.16 One of the highest rates of secondary overtriage, 43%, was reported by Osher and colleagues at a tertiary, rural level I trauma center.3 They correlated secondary overtriage with younger ages and head and neck injuries. The overall secondary overtriage rate for the 20 hospitals in our study was 26.4%. The five L3TCs had a significantly lower secondary overtriage rate when compared with the 15 non-L3TCs (23.5% vs 28.4%, p=0.009). When reviewing patient injury patterns, the difference in secondary overtriage rate may be related to the significantly higher percentage of patients with GCS 3–8 transferred from L3TCs (6.3% vs 4.0% p=0.016). With a presenting GCS score of 3–8, these patients likely contributed to the higher ventilator utilization in L3TC interfacility transfers (9.2% vs 6.6%, p=0.022) with resultant longer ICU and hospital stays that exceeded the 48-hour discharge threshold within our secondary overtriage definition. These unadjusted results were corroborated by adjusted regression analyses which demonstrated a 31% significantly higher odds of secondary overtriage in patients transferred from a non-L3TC, compared with those transferred from a L3TC (table 3). Moreover, non-L3TC transfer patients had a 12% shorter average hospital LOS, a 21% shorter average ICU LOS, and a 39% shorter average ventilator LOS. These robust and consistent findings suggested that L3TCs were associated with a significant reduction in secondary overtriage, independent of patient profiles, and transfer and receiving hospital characteristics. When comparing L3TCs and non-L3TCs within the same hospital system, differences in patient outcomes persisted despite shared protocols and infrastructure. Patients treated at non-L3TCs had significantly higher rates of secondary overtriage, lower ICU and ventilator use, and shorter hospital and ICU LOS (SDC 3). When comparing the 4 L3TCs that are part of the same hospital system as the L1TC to the one L3TC that is not, there were no statistically significant differences in patient outcomes between these two groups of patients (SDC 4). These findings suggest that trauma center level remains an important factor in patient outcomes, potentially reflecting differences in resource availability, staffing, or clinical decision-making practices.

The effect of Level III designation and/or verification on secondary overtriage rates has not been well documented. State designation as an L3TC requires hospitals to fulfill three main requirements. The first pertains to outlining the minimum necessary equipment to appropriately evaluate, stabilize, treat, and monitor patients. Second are the personnel requirements focusing on surgeon participation, oversight, and review of the care provided to injured patients. The final requirement outlines compulsory processes and includes the establishment of trauma team activation criteria and review of the conduct of care through performance improvement programs. These three main requirements are designed to improve the care of injured patients, including patient triage for interfacility transfer. Designation and/or verification as a L3TC may therefore inherently result in more efficient care including more appropriate transfers to higher levels of care and less secondary overtriage.

In addition to the personnel, equipment, and processes required for state trauma center designation, four of the five L3TCs and the tertiary receiving trauma center share leadership in the form of a system trauma surgeon and nurse director responsible for secondary oversight of injured patient care, and the creation and implementation of shared practice management guidelines. All trauma centers in this system meet monthly to discuss and adopt best practices in patient care and to review patients transferred within the system. There is direct access between system trauma medical directors and trauma program directors and managers, allowing for real-time patient care discussions. Facile contemporaneous and retrospective review of injured patient care is promoted by an interconnected electronic medical record, including radiographic images. The state requirements for L3TC designation and the organizational attributes of participation in a unified regional trauma system contributed to the lower secondary overtriage rate at the L3TCs, versus non-L3TCs, in the current study. We believe a strength of the L3TCs in this study is integration into a trauma system with shared EMR and unified Quality Improvement/Performance Improvement leadership; however, we recognize this level of integration may not be widely available.

A potential limitation of this study was that secondary overtriage rates were determined from the perspective of the receiving trauma center. As described by Con et al, overtriage rates can also be determined from the perspective of the nontertiary hospitals. However, we did not have access to the necessary information from the referring hospitals in this study to calculate the Con et al version of overtriage.17 Other study limitations are inherent to the retrospective observational cohort design. We did not have documentation of first responder medical decision-making or geographic resource allocation leading to facility choice. This study was also vulnerable to confounding bias, in that first responders (and private transport) may have chosen to transport more critically injured patients to L3TCs versus non-L3TCs. This potential bias was limited through the use of multivariable regression analyses, controlling for important injury, transport, and hospital-related factors; nevertheless, residual confounding factors might remain. Finally, this study was conducted in the central southeastern USA, with many patients transferred from rural areas to a single trauma system. Therefore, these results may not be generalizable to other regions of the USA with differing population densities, healthcare resources, and organized trauma systems. Finally, because four of the five L3TCs were a part of the same trauma as the tertiary center, we are unable to separate the individual effects of the L3TCs from the individual effects of being in the same trauma system. However, we are designing a larger, more representative study to address the issues of generalizability and to determine what system attributes are likely to optimize interfacility transfers.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study demonstrated that patient transfers from state-designated L3TCs in the Southern USA, particularly those embedded within an integrated trauma system, were associated with significantly lower rates of secondary overtriage compared with non-L3TCs. These findings underscore the value of structured trauma designation requirements augmented by system-level integration. While mortality outcomes were similar across groups, the reduced resource utilization and improved triage precision observed among L3TCs highlight their potential to enhance both system efficiency and patient-centered care. Designation of a hospital as a L3TC may not only provide organized trauma care to its local community but also contribute to more efficient regional trauma care. Based on these findings, we encourage tertiary TCs to increase their locoregional outreach efforts and, in particular, establish shared protocols, open lines of communication, and collaborative PI processes. Future research should explore the patient-level consequences of unnecessary transfers and evaluate strategies to extend trauma system integration to non-designated centers to reduce avoidable transfers and optimize trauma care delivery.
